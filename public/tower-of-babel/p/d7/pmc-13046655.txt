Letermovir improves the outcome of allogenic stem cell transplantation with anti-thymocyte globulin against acute leukemia and myelodysplastic syndromes
Authors: Shuto Negishi, Kotaro Miyao, Fumiya Ohara, Kenta Motegi, Hiroya Wakabayashi, Hitomi Sawa, Yuichiro Inagaki, Masashi Sawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graft versus host disease (GVHD) is a serious side effect of allogenic stem cell transplantation (allo-SCT). Anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG) reduces GVHD but raises the risk of infections following allo-SCT. Preventive therapy is recommended for cytomegalovirus (CMV), which is a major pathogen. Prophylactic administration of letermovir has shown a significant reduction in CMV reactivation. We hypothesized letermovir could reduce CMV reactivation and affect the outcome of allo-SCT. We retrospectively studied 88 patients who received allo-SCT with ATG for acute leukemia or myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) from 2014 to 2022. The patients were divided into letermovir (n = 34) and non-letermovir (n = 54) groups. 3-year overall survival rates were 85% (95%Confidence interval [CI]: 67%-93%) and 47% (95%CI: 33%–60%) in the letermovir and non-letermovir groups, respectively (p < 0.01). Non-relapse mortality rate was equivalent, and the relapse rate was 21% (95%CI: 9.0%-36%) vs. 47% (95%CI: 33%-60%) and significantly lower in the letermovir group (p = 0.015). The cumulative CMV reactivation incidence rates at day 100 were 5.9% (95%CI: 1.0%-17%) and 43% (29%-55%) in the letermovir and non-letermovir groups (p = 0.012). These findings suggest letermovir helped the suppression of CMV reactivation and improved the outcome of allo-SCT with ATG by lowering relapse.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-026-06964-8.

Introduction

After an allogenic stem cell transplantation (allo-SCT), graft versus host disease (GVHD), a severe complication deemed an important prognostic factor, may occur [1–3]. To prevent GVHD, various prophylactic therapies have been used in practice. The administration of anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG) is one of these therapies, which has been shown in several clinical studies to reduce GVHD [4–7].

However, ATG raises the risk of infection, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) is a common pathogen [8]. Severe CMV infections can be fatal, so prophylaxis or early treatments is critical. To manage CMV infections precisely after allo-SCT, it is necessary to monitor CMV regularly, and when CMV reactivation is detected, antiviral drugs are administered as pre-emptive therapy [9–11]. These medications frequently cause renal dysfunction, myelosuppression, and fungal infection [12]. The adverse events frequently disrupt early treatment of CMV and management after SCT, including the adjustment of immunosuppressive therapy, infection control, and supportive care, thereby increasing non-relapse morbidity. This problem has increased demand for a new CMV prophylaxis drug with fewer side effects. Letermovir is an inhibitor of CMV-specific DNA-terminase complex encoded by UL56. It prevents the cleaving of the long concatemer DNA into unit-length DNA and regulates the proliferation of CMV. In 2017, letermovir demonstrated its suppression effect against CMV reactivation in a phase III study [13] and was prescribed to patients undergoing allo-SCT as a prophylactic agent for CMV reactivation. Because it has fewer side effects and is more effective, letermovir has become a more popular prophylactic drug.

Letermovir also improved patients’ overall survival (OS) after allo-SCT in the pivotal study, and several other studies have found similar results [13–16]. However, some studies have found that letermovir did not affect OS after SCT [17–19], and it is still unknown whether letermovir can improve SCT outcome. We hypothesized that because ATG increased the risk of infection, and, ultimately, the non-relapse mortality rate (NRM), letermovir would improve the outcome of SCT with ATG. We investigated whether letermovir suppressed CMV reactivation in patients undergoing allo-SCT with ATG, as well as the impact of letermovir on allo-SCT outcomes.

Materials and methods

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of patients who underwent allo-SCT with ATG for the first time to treat acute leukemia or myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) at the Department of Hematology and Oncology of Anjo Kosei Hospital between January 2014 and December 2022. All of the patients were over the age of 18. Patients who received letermovir were labeled as being in the letermovir group, while those who did not receive the drug were labeled as being in the non-letermovir group. The refined Disease Risk Index (rDRI) was used to stratify disease-related risk at transplantation [20], and pre-transplant comorbidities were evaluated using the Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation–Specific Comorbidity Index (HCT-CI) [21]. We designated the day of SCT as day 0. Rabbit ATG was used for all patients as part of the GVHD prophylaxis. ATG was given intravenously on day − 2 and day − 1 at a dose of 1.25 mg/kg/day. For GVHD prophylaxis, patients were given 0.015 mg/kg/day of tacrolimus from day − 1 intravenously. Following neutrophil engraftment, intravenous tacrolimus was transitioned to oral administration when the patient’s clinical condition was stable and oral intake was possible. In addition, 15 mg/m2 methotrexate was administered on day 1, and 10 mg/m2 methotrexate was administered on days 3 and 6. CMV serology was examined by measuring immunoglobulin G against CMV. In case when both recipients and donors have negative CMV serology, those patients were excluded. CMV antigenemia was assessed using the pp65 antigenemia assay with C10/C11 monoclonal antibodies. Results were expressed as the number of pp65-positive cells per 5 × 10⁴ peripheral blood leukocytes. CMV reactivation was determined when the attending doctor started preemptive therapies according to antigenemia test results. Letermovir was administered if patients and/or donors had CMV serology positive. Letermovir was not administered to patients in whom both the recipient and donor were seronegative for CMV, or in those who were considered unable to tolerate the drug due to gastrointestinal symptoms. Letermovir was prescribed on day 5 at a dose of 480 mg daily and was continued until day 100 as far as possible. Letermovir administration was halted when CMV reactivation occurred or attending doctors determined drug intolerance due to side effects. The OS was the primary endpoint, and the secondary endpoints were progression free survival (PFS), NRM, relapse rate, GVHD incidence, GVHD-relapse-free survival (GRFS), and the incidence of CMV reactivation. We defined the neutrophil engraftment day as the first three consecutive days with a peripheral blood neutrophil count greater than 0.5 × 109/L. Acute GVHD (aGVHD) was graded as 0–IV according to the criteria reported by Glucksberg et al. [22]. Chronic GVHD (cGVHD) was classified as mild-severe according to the National Institutes of Health [23]. GRFS events were classified as grade III–IV aGVHD, cGVHD with systemic treatments, relapse, and death. The number of CD4+ and CD8+ lymphocytes was counted using the results of a bone marrow aspiration on day 30. Mononuclear cells were isolated and analyzed by multiparametric flow cytometry to determine the percentages of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T lymphocytes among the total CD45⁺ bone marrow cell population, in accordance with the previous study reported [24]. For each sample, the absolute numbers of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells per mL of marrow were estimated by multiplying the flow cytometric frequency of each subset by the total number of bone marrow nucleated cells determined on the same aspirate. The total lymphocyte counts at days 30, 60, and 100 were calculated using the peripheral blood test value. In our statistical analyses, we used a t-test for unrelated continuous variables and Fisher’s exact tests for nominal variables. The log-rank test was used for survival rate, and the gray test was used for cumulative incidence. For multivariate analyses, Cox regression analysis was used. Multivariate analyses were performed using endpoint-specific covariates. For OS, the model included letermovir administration, HCT-CI, transplantation year, disease status at transplantation, and conditioning intensity. For PFS, covariates included letermovir administration, transplantation year, disease status, stem cell source, conditioning intensity, and sex compatibility. For relapse incidence, letermovir administration, HCT-CI, and disease status were included. For GRFS, the model included letermovir administration, transplantation year, and sex compatibility. Donor type (matched related donor (MRD), matched unrelated donor (MUD), and mismatched unrelated donor (MMUD)) was additionally incorporated into the models for PFS and relapse incidence. All the tests were two-sided, with p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Data were assessed using Easy R version 1.54 (EZR, Saitama, Japan) [25]. Anjo Kosei Hospital’s institutional review board (Anjo, Japan) authorized the study protocol. The Helsinki Declaration was followed. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included this study in the written form.

Results

Patients

Of the 88 patients, 34 were given letermovir, while the other 54 were not (Table 1). The median age was 50 (range: 19–70) in the letermovir group and 54 (range: 18–67) in the non-letermovir group (p = 0.92). Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) was the most common disease in both groups. Disease status at transplantation was similar between groups. In patients with MDS, disease status was not assessable using conventional remission criteria and was classified as not applicable (NA). The median year of SCT was 2020 (range: 2018–2022) compared to 2016 (range: 2014–2021), and the letermovir group had SCT in more recent years (p < 0.01). Myeloablative conditioning (MAC) was experienced more in the letermovir group (p < 0.01). As stem cell sources, relative peripheral blood cells (R-PB), unrelated bone marrow (UR-BM), and unrelated PB (UR-PB) were used in both groups (p = 0.62). The ratio of MRD, MUD, and MMUD was similar (p = 0.22). The median duration of letermovir administration was 93 days (range: 14–110) (Table 1). OS and PFS

The 3-year OS was 85% (95%CI: 67%−93%) and 47% (95%CI: 33%−60%) in the letermovir and non-letermovir groups, respectively, and considerably higher in the letermovir group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1A). The 3-year PFS was 73% (95%CI: 55%−85%) vs. 38% (95%CI: 25%−51%) and also higher in the letermovir group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1B). In the multivariate analysis for OS, the administration of letermovir showed the tendency of lower hazard ratio (hazard ratio [HR]:0.24, 95%CI: 0.049–1.2.049.2, p = 0.081). The analysis for PFS, the administration of letermovir (HR:0.30, 95%CI: 0.094–0.98, p = 0.046) was identified as significant elements (Table 2). NRM and relapse rate

The NRM at 3 years was 5.9% (95%CI: 1.0%−17%) vs. 15% (95%CI: 6.9%−26%) (p = 0.20) (Fig. 1C), and the relapse rate was 21% (95%CI: 9%−36%) vs. 47% (95%CI: 33%−60%) (p = 0.015) in the letermovir and non-letermovir groups, respectively (Fig. 1D). In the multivariate analysis, there were no significant NRM factors found. For the relapse rate, the administration of letermovir (HR: 0.28, 95%CI: 0.097–0.83, p = 0.021) was found to be significant (Table 2). A multivariable analysis incorporating donor type (MRD, MUD, and MMUD) was conducted (Table S1). Even after adjustment for donor type, letermovir administration was identified as a significant factor associated with a reduced risk of relapse.

Stratified analysis of the relapse rate

Patients’ conditioning regimens intensity and SCT year differed significantly between the two groups. We divided the patients based on these characteristics and compared the relapse rates between the letermovir and non-letermovir groups. The 3-year relapse rate was 22% (95%CI: 9.3%−37%) vs. 44% (95%CI: 27%−60%) in MAC patients (p = 0.043) (Figure S1A) and 21% (95%CI: 9.0%−36%) vs. 48% (95%CI: 25%−68%) in patients received SCT after 2017 (p = 0.037) (Figure S1B) in the letermovir and non-letermovir groups, respectively. An additional analysis was performed to evaluate the impact of CMV donor/recipient serostatus on relapse rates. No statistically significant differences were identified, but a trend toward a lower relapse rate was observed in the Positive/Negative group, whereas a trend toward a higher relapse rate was suggested in the Positive/Positive group (Figure S1C).

GVHD and GRFS

The cumulative incidence of grade II–IV aGVHD at day 100 was 18% (95%CI: 7.2%−33%) in the letermovir group and 26% (95%CI: 15%−38%) in the non-letermovir group (p = 0.39) (Fig. 2A). The incidence of grade III–IV aGVHD was comparable in both groups (p = 0.35) (Fig. 2B). The rate of moderate-severe cGVHD at day 365 was 6.1% (95%CI: 1.0%−18%) vs. 19% (95%CI: 9.4%−30%) (p = 0.063) (Fig. 2C), and the rate of severe cGVHD was also comparable (p = 0.21) (Fig. 2D). In univariate analysis, the allele mismatch between the donor and patient was the only significant factor (HR: 2.8, 95%CI: 1.2–6.5, p < 0.05) for grade II-IV aGVHD (Table not shown). There were no significant factors for cGVHD in univariate analysis. The 1-year GRFS was 71% (95%CI: 52%–83%) in the letermovir group and 35% (23%–48%) in the non-letermovir group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2E). Multivariate analysis revealed no significant factors for GRFS. Number of lymphocyte count after SCT

A delay in lymphocyte recovery after SCT has been reported in SCT with ATG [26], and we investigated the lymphocyte count after SCT with letermovir. The number of CD4+ lymphocytes in the bone marrow at day 30 was 208/µL (range: 24–1403/µL) in the letermovir group and 322/µL (range: 32–1181/µL) in the non-letermovir group (p = 0.12) (Fig. 3A). For CD8+ lymphocytes, the result was 175/µL (range: 2–2326/µL) vs. 681/µL (range: 23–5134/µL), respectively, and significantly lower in the letermovir group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3B). The total lymphocyte count in peripheral blood at day 30 was 378/µL (range: 60–1728/µL) in the letermovir group and 588/µL (range: 51–3960/µL) in the non-letermovir group, and significantly low in the letermovir group (p < 0.05). Besides, the number at day 60 was 660/µL (range: 89–2166/µL) vs. 1705/µL (range: 18–9512/µL) (p < 0.01) and at day 100, it was 650/µL (range: 216–2064/µL) vs. 1213/µL (range: 5–4900/µL) (p < 0.01), indicating significantly lowered lymphocyte counts after day 30 in the letermovir group (Fig. 3C).

Reactivation and infection with CMV and cause of death

The cumulative incidence of CMV reactivation at day 100 was 5.9% (95%CI: 1.0%−17%) in the letermovir group and 43% (95%CI: 29%−55%) in the non-letermovir group (p = 0.012) (Fig. 4). CMV infection was observed in one case (3.0%) in the letermovir group and three cases (5.6%) in the non-letermovir group, but the difference was not significant between the two groups (p = 1.0) (Table 3). In the non-letermovir group, two patients with CMV infection had enteritis and one had pneumonia (Table 3). The median period of CMV reactivation from the transplantation was 137 days (range: 17–152) in the letermovir group and 40 days (range: 17–110) in the non-letermovir group (p < 0.01). There were five fatal cases (15%) in the letermovir group and 29 (45%) in the non-letermovir group. Discussion

There has not been enough research done on the effects of letermovir on allo-SCT with T cell depletion. This study performed retrospective analyses of letermovir effects for allo-SCT with ATG. In this study, the OS and PFS of SCT with ATG was optimized by letermovir administration. Surprisingly, the relapse rate was significantly lower in the letermovir group, and the NRM was comparable between the two groups. The GRFS was also significantly higher in the letermovir group. Because the two groups had the same NRM and cumulative incidence of acute and chronic GVHD, the reduction in relapse made a difference in GRFS. Although moderate-to-severe chronic GVHD tended to be less frequent in the letermovir group, this difference was not statistically significant. Given previous reports suggesting an association between CMV reactivation and acute GVHD, suppression of CMV by letermovir may have indirectly influenced chronic GVHD incidence. However, the relationship between GVHD and graft-versus-leukemia effects is complex and not entirely overlapping, and the mechanisms underlying the observed relapse reduction remain to be elucidated [27]. It is reasonable that all patients received ATG and had low NRM and GVHD incidence rates, regardless of letermovir use. Although several studies have shown that letermovir improves OS after allo-SCT [13–16], they have also suggested that the reduction in NRM plays a role. This study found a lower relapse rate, which could be due to a mechanism different from that reported in previous studies. Previous research has linked CMV reactivation to relapse rates. CMV reactivation has been linked to an increased relapse rate following allo-SCT with ATG in patients with AML [28]. Considering letermovir decreased the reactivation of CMV, the suppression of CMV activity may have brought relapse reduction. On the other hand, some studies have reported CMV reactivation decreased the relapse rate in allo-SCT without ATG administration [29–32]. According to these facts, it is hard to explain the relationship between letermovir and relapse only by suppression of CMV reactivation. Although the sample size was limited and no statistically significant differences were detected, the observed trends suggest that CMV donor/recipient serostatus could potentially impact relapse risk. We need further analysis and considering other mechanisms.

Letermovir may have prevented the reduction of graft versus leukemia (GVL) effect caused by T cell depletion. ATG causes a delay in the reconstitution of helper and naïve T cells [26, 33] as well as a decrease in the repertoire of CD8+ T cell receptors [34, 35], which leads to an increased relapse. Letermovir may have altered T cell reconstitution destructed by ATG and reduced relapse [36]. The impact of antibiotics on relapse after SCT is stated in previous research. According to Vallet et al., azithromycin disrupted the GVL effect by inhibiting glycolysis and synthesis of mitochondrial RNA in T cells and increasing the expression of genes related to immunosuppression, leading to an increase in relapse [37]. Although it is unknown whether letermovir is related to energy metabolism, the reaction of azithromycin has been shown the possibility to directly affect immune cells such as T cells, and influence the GVL effect after allo-SCT. Interestingly, letermovir did not enhance OS in allo-SCT with post-transplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy) in a prior study [18]. This suggests that T cell depletion caused by ATG or PTCy is based on different mechanisms and this difference is linked to the letermovir effect of improved OS. Given the differences in the biological mechanisms of PTCy and ATG, Zhao et al. found that PTCy caused delayed reconstitution of cellular immunity, decreased regulatory T cells, and increased naïve T cells, resulting in GVHD inhibition [38]. On the other hand, ATG causes the depletion and dysfunction of not only T cells but also B cells, NK cells, and macrophages [39], indicating that it has a broader impact on the immune system than PTCy. Based on our findings and those of PTCy, the letermovir effect is linked to various types of immune cells.

The number of CD8+ cells was significantly low in the letemrovir group. The total number of lymphocytes at days 30, 60 and 100 was also lower in the letermovir group. According to a previous study, the number of CD4+ and CD8+ lymphocytes after allo-SCT was considerably lower in patients receiving letermovir than those receiving preemptive therapy [40]. This result could be attributed to the suppression of CMV reactivation or letermovir’s direct interaction with immune cells. However, another study found that the number of total lymphocytes had little effect on the prognosis of patients undergoing allo-SCT with ATG [41]. Letermovir indeed reduced the number of lymphocytes, but other mechanisms probably underlie the suppression of relapse.

This study had some limitations. This was a retrospective study, and the number of patients was relatively insufficient. Although we evaluated the data to reduce the bias, we need to state there was a difference in SCT year and conditioning regimens in patient characteristics. Given the relatively small sample size and the imbalance in conditioning intensity between groups, our findings should be interpreted with caution. Larger, prospective studies are required to confirm whether letermovir independently contributes to relapse reduction.

This study suggests that giving letermovir after allo-SCT with ATG may improve the outcome of allo-SCT by lowering relapse. The mechanisms are still need to be investigated, however, this finding contributes to satisfy the clinical needs to improve the outcome of allo-SCT with ATG.
