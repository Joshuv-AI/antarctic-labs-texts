Validation of the CLIF-SIG score in patients with HBV-related acutely decompensated cirrhosis
Authors: Jiyang Chen, Xianbin Xu, Sisi Yang, Xia Yu, Ferran Aguilar, Joan Claria, Jonel Trebicka, Juan José Lozano, Julia Sidorova, Paolo Angeli, Alberto Queiroz Farias, Richard Moreau, Wenyi Gu, Zelu Meng, Ying Nie, Yining Zhang, Xiaohan Qian, Yu Shi, Yida Yang
Published: 2025

We read with great interest about the recent publication by Trebicka et al in Gut,1 in which the authors developed and validated the Chronic Liver Failure-Systemic Inflammation Gene score (CLIF-SIG) score to predict short-term outcomes in patients with acutely decompensated cirrhosis (AD). The study demonstrated the robustness of CLIF-SIG across multiple international cohorts, predominantly consisting of alcohol-related cirrhosis.

However, the accuracy of this score in the hepatitis B virus (HBV) population remains unclear, in particular considering HBV-acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) displays a distinct clinical phenotype from ACLF of other aetiologies.2 To validate the generalisability of the CLIF-SIG score in the context of HBV-related cirrhosis, we analysed the peripheral blood mononuclear cells transcriptomic data from 220 patients with HBV-related AD at admission, as well as from 29 healthy controls (HCs). Among these patients, 48 met the EASL-CLIF criteria for ACLF at admission.3,6 The baseline characteristics of the study subjects are shown in online supplemental table 1.

First, we compared the expression levels of the 28 genes individually across the HC, HBV-AD and HBV-ACLF groups (figure 1A). Consistent with the prior study, 24 out of 28 genes were significantly upregulated in the AD/ACLF groups with a stepwise increase from AD to ACLF, while 4 NK cell-related genes were downregulated.

We then calculated the CLIF-SIG score for each patient based on the 28-gene panel using the proposed algorithm. As shown in figure 1B, there was a stepwise increase of CLIF-SIG score across HC, AD and ACLF, supporting a worsening of systemic inflammation as the disease progressed.

By a more detailed subgroup analysis of AD phenotypes,7 we further found that even those with stable decompensated cirrhosis (SDC) had significantly higher CLIF-SIG score than HC (figure 1C), consistent with prior studies showing the presence of a low-grade systemic inflammation even in an early stage of the natural history of cirrhosis.8 More importantly, the CLIF-SIG score clearly distinguished between the SDC, unstable decompensated cirrhosis (UDC), pre-ACLF and patients with ACLF at baseline, underscoring the role of systemic inflammation in driving divergent trajectories of AD patients.

Using a cut-off value of 0.386, AD patients were categorised into high-score and low-score groups. We compared the 90-day transplant-free mortality between the two groups. The cumulative transplant-free mortality rates were sharply higher in patients with a CLIF-SIG score above 0.386 than those below the threshold (figure 1D). A very low mortality rate was noted in the low-score group, which implied a good ‘ruling out’ performance of the CLIF-SIG score.

We also compared the performance of the CLIF-SIG score with that of traditional prognostic models.9 10 The area under the receiver operating characteristic (auROC) of CLIF-SIG score for predicting 90-day outcomes of patients with AD and ACLF was 0.821 (95% CI 0.747 to 0.895) and 0.73 (95 CI% 0.558 to 0.901), respectively, numerically higher than MELDs (AD: 0.785, 95% CI 0.691 to 0.885; ACLF: 0.663, 95% CI 0.469 to 0.857) and MELD-Nas (AD: 0.775, 95% CI 0.670 to 0.857; ACLF: 0.691, 95% CI 0.513 to 0.870) (figure 1E,F). CLIF-SIG score also had a good performance by classifying patients with low severity (SDC and UDC) and high severity (pre-ACLF and ACLF) (auROC: 0.843, 95% CI 0.791 to 0.895) (figure 1G). Besides, CLIF-SIGs was superior to MELD-Nas in predicting the progression from AD to ACLF (CLIF-SIGs: 0.794, 95% CI 0.719 to 0.870, MELD-Nas: 0.756, 95% CI 0.671 to 0.841) (figure 1H). A combined use of CLIF-SIGs and MELDs further improved the predictive capability (auROC: 0.824, 95% CI 0.759 to 0.889).

The CLIF-SIG score effectively stratified 90-day transplant-free mortality risk in AD patients across clinical subgroups (online supplemental figure S1A–E, all p<0.01). Compared with conventional surrogate markers of systemic inflammation such as white cell count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and C reactive protein, it more accurately predicted disease severity, mortality and AD-ACLF progression (online supplemental figure S1F–H), highlighting its superiority as a marker of systemic inflammation.

In summary, our study externally validated the CLIF-SIG score in a distinct HBV population. The CLIF-SIG score had a good performance in both predicting outcome and clinical trajectory of patients with AD cirrhosis. Our findings confirmed the generalisability and robustness of CLIF-SIGs as a quantifying tool to measure systemic inflammation, across different aetiologies of AD.
