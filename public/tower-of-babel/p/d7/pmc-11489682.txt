The effect of platelet-rich fibrin on the biological properties of urothelial cells
Authors: Shaohua Hu, Zhenli Zhao, Zhisheng Wan, Weizhen Bu, Songqiang Chen, Tianhong Han, Yiqun Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrethral reconstruction presents a challenging issue in urology, primarily due to the limited availability of alternative materials for repair. The advancement of bioengineering technology has brought new hope to researchers, with a focus on the selection of appropriate biological scaffolds and seed cells. In order to find an ideal alternative material, we used platelet-rich fibrin as the bioscaffold and urothelial cells as the seed cells, meanwhile, we intended to investigate the effect of platelet-rich fibrin on the biological properties of urothelial cells. We transformed and characterised induced pluripotent stem cells into urothelial cells and prepared platelet-rich fibrin. Platelet-rich fibrin was cultured in a complex with urothelial cells to observe the effect of platelet-rich fibrin on the proliferation and migration ability of urothelial cells. The results showed that the induced pluripotent stem cells were successfully transformed into urothelial cells, platelet-rich fibrin was regularly arranged in cords, with platelets and other structures distributed between them, and the proliferation and migration of urothelial cells were significantly increased. These results suggested that platelet-rich fibrin is biocompatible with urothelial cells and it promotes the proliferation and migration of urothelial cells, which lays a good foundation for its use as an alternative material for urethral repair.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75699-1.Keywords: Platelet-rich fibrin, Induced pluripotent stem cells, Urothelial cells, Urethral repair, Tissue engineeringSubject terms: Stem cells, Urology

Introduction

Hypospadias and urethral defects or urethral strictures due to trauma, and inflammation, are common diseases of the urinary system, which seriously affect the patient’s urinary function and quality of life. Hence, early repair and reconstruction of the urethra remains significant for urologists1. Due to the lack of autologous tissue, obtaining sufficient replacement material for repair has long been a major problem for urologists. Local subcutaneous penile tissue, tunica vaginalis, dartos, and extragenital tissues, including bladder mucosa, fascia lata, and free skin grafts, have been described to repair the urethra. However, these alternative materials are often limited in availability, or may increase patient discomfort, and are susceptible to various complications such as restenosis, urethral fistulae, fibrosis, and urethral calculi, leading to suboptimal postoperative outcomes2,3. buccal mucosa, the most common graft for reconstruction, also have defects, buccal mucosa harvesting is painful and not complication-free (bleeding, postoperative infection, pain, swelling, salivary duct disorders)3. The current research challenge in the lower urinary tract lies in the selection of appropriate and efficacious alternative materials for urethral repair and reconstruction.

The advancement of bioengineering techniques and regenerative medicine has demonstrated significant potential in the field of urethral repair. The application of bioengineering techniques for urethral repair primarily depends on the preparation of biological scaffolds and the selection of seed cells. Platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), extracted from autologous peripheral blood, was first introduced by Choukroun4 in 2000. It possesses a well-structured three-dimensional fibrin space that effectively facilitates cell migration and proliferation, providing seed cells with an optimal cellular scaffold and suitable growth environment. Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) can be derived from autologous fibroblasts, possessing the ability to differentiate into all germ layers simultaneously while avoiding ethical and immune rejection. As a result, it has garnered widespread attention, with relevant studies demonstrating successful transformation into urothelial cells5.

In this study, we initially differentiated iPSCs into urothelial cells and concurrently prepared PRF. Subsequently, we combined the PRF with the iPSCs-derived urothelial cells to investigate the impact of PRF on the biological characteristics of urothelial cells to provide a new idea and methodology for the source of alternative materials for urethral repair.

Results

Alkaline phosphatase stain

As alkaline phosphatase is highly expressed in stem cells, so we performed alkaline phosphatase staining on the iPSCs, the results showed that alkaline phosphatase staining was expressed in iPSCs, suggesting that the cells have stem cell characteristics, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Alkaline phosphatase staining of iPSC observed under fluorescence microscope, suggesting that the cells have stem cell characteristics.

Observation of cell morphology

We examined the morphology of iPSCs using a light microscope and observed the presence of cell clusters with prominent nuclei and well-defined nucleoli, which are indicative features of stem cells, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Cell morphology of iPSC under light microscope, cell clusters with prominent nuclei and well-defined nucleoli were observed, which are indicative features of stem cells.

Cell immunofluorescence

We used immunofluorescence to examine the expression of three stem cell surface markers, Oct4, Sox2, and SSEA-1 in iPSCs, and the results showed that Oct4 and Sox2 were expressed in the nucleus, and SSEA-1 was expressed in the cytoplasm, suggesting that they possessed stem cell characteristics, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Expression of Oct4, Sox2 and SSEA-1 in iPSC, Oct4 and Sox2 were expressed in the nucleus, and SSEA-1 was expressed in the cytoplasm, suggesting that they possessed stem cell characteristics.

Western blot

The protein expression levels of pluripotent genes in each group of cells were assessed using Western Blot. The results revealed that compared with iPSCs, the protein expression levels of Oct4 and Klf4 in embryonic stem cells (ESCs) did not change significantly, and the protein expression levels of Sox2 and c-Myc were decreased; the protein expression levels of Oct4, Sox2, c-Myc, and Klf4 in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) levels were reduced to different degrees. Compared with ESCs, there was no significant difference in the protein expression levels of Klf4, and the protein expression levels of Oct4, Sox2, and c-Myc were significantly reduced in MEFs, as detailed in Fig. 4.

Protein expression levels of multipotent genes (Oct4, Sox2, c-Myc, Klf4) in each group of cells were identified by Western blotting assay. Data were presented as mean ± SD, n = 3; *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01.

PCR

PCR analysis showed that compared with iPSCs, the mRNA levels of Oct4 in ESCs had no significant change, but the mRNA levels of Sox2, c-Myc, and Klf4 decreased. The mRNA levels of Oct4, Sox2, c-Myc, and Klf4 in MEFs decreased to varying degrees. Compared with ESCs, the mRNA levels of Oct4, Sox2, c-Myc, and Klf4 in MEFs were decreased to varying degrees, as detailed in Fig. 5.

mRNA expression levels of multipotent genes (Oct4, Sox2, c-Myc, Klf4) in each group of cells were identified by qPCR. Data were presented as mean ± SD, n = 3; *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01.

Transformation of iPSCs into urothelial cells

A key point in the differentiation of the urinary epithelium lies in the intermediate state of transition, that is, the directed endoderm, FOXA2, and SOX17 are markers of endodermal differentiation. We examined their expression using flow cytometry and showed that the percentage of FOXA2 and SOX17-positive cells was significantly higher in endoderm cells compared with iPSCs5. Morphological changes of the iPSC, directed endoderm (DE), and Urothelial cells were observed under a light microscope, and the results showed that the final differentiated cells showed a polygonal-like cellular morphology in the form of urothelial cells. Immunofluorescence and flow cytometry revealed that the expression levels of UP1a, UP1b, and UP2 were significantly higher in the urothelial cells group, as detailed in Figs. 6, 7, 8 and 9.

Discussion

Hypospadias, urethral defects, and urethral strictures require reconstruction of the urethra, and the key to repair is the availability of adequate replacement materials. Although there have been many studies on autologous replacement materials, initial results have been unsatisfactory due to higher recurrence rates as well as complications. The buccal mucosa is considered to be the preferred free graft material for urethral reconstruction surgery6, but some patients may require a large number of grafts, meanwhile, others may not have access to buccal mucosa or have a limited amount of oral mucosa, This method may also lead to complications such as donor-site hemorrhage, infection, pain, parotid ductal injury, graft contracture, and numbness6,7. Therefore, finding suitable alternative materials is a critical issue that needs to be urgently addressed in urethral repair. The rapid development of tissue engineering (TE) technology as well as regenerative medicine provides a new way for urethral reconstruction. Various explorations have been carried out in the field of TE, and a large number of preclinical studies have been conducted and significant progress has been made in the past few decades. In contrast, the progress of urethral TE in clinical practice has been slow so far, and the optimal biologic replacement material has not yet been found for urethral repair8.

The first aim of TE is to provide the ideal scaffold, which has to be biocompatible, biodegradable, and non-immunogenic. It has to promote adequate blood supply, enhance cell adhesion and native-like tissue organization, and provide a substrate with similar mechanical features to those of native tissue, thereby accelerating the repair process and increasing the success rate9. Although many efforts in the selection of scaffolds have been made, suitable scaffolds have not yet been developed. Cell-free matrices do not guarantee complete removal of cellular components, which may lead to severe immune reactions. The degradation products of synthetic polymers can affect the microenvironment of the urethral tissue, thereby compromising the effectiveness of the repair10. PRF is a second-generation platelet concentrate that is simple to prepare, does not require the addition of any anticoagulant, and can be obtained by centrifugation in a single pass. It has a unique three-dimensional mesh structure, which is enriched with platelets and a variety of growth factors, such as TGF-β1, VEGF, IGF-1, and is capable of being released slowly, playing an important role in tissue repair mechanisms4. Autologous PRF increases the local concentration of growth factors and cytokines, promotes the healing process, and reduces the incidence of postoperative complications. Over the years, PRF has opened up an exciting new field of clinical research with its expanding possibilities for the future11,12. Based on the promising results of PRF in tissue repair, some researchers have attempted to incorporate PRF into urethral repair to improve the success rate. Guinot A13 first evaluated the use of PRF as an intermediate layer during the repair of hypospadias and demonstrated the feasibility and safety of using PRF as a covering layer in hypospadias surgery by showing a quick and simple procedure with a low risk of postoperative complications. Soyer T14,15 suggested that PRF could be used as a novel alternative to urethral fistula repair and could be used as an adjunct to other fistula prophylactic surgeries by incorporating PRF in urethral fistula repair. At the same time, they found the addition of a significant increase in the expression of TGF-β-R and VEGF, suggesting that PRF could be considered as an alternative measure to improve the success rate of urethral repair.

Cells are the source of engineered tissues, and scaffolds provide space for cell proliferation, differentiation, and migration, as well as structural support for tissue formation. Grafted cells promote rapid vascularisation and re-epithelialization of the scaffold material at the site of graft repair, reducing local inflammation and scarring, and resulting in better repair outcomes, thus, cell involvement is essential for urethral reconstruction16. Several studies have shown that cell-loaded grafts can significantly improve the repair outcome compared to grafts alone during the repair process17–20. A systematic evaluation showed that cell-scaffold material composite grafts inoculated with cells had a 5.67 times higher long-term success rate than scaffold material alone when performing urethral repair21. To date, a variety of cell types have been explored and applied in the field of urethral regeneration. However, there is no optimal strategy for the source, selection, and application conditions of the cells. Differentiated cells were first explored for application in the field of urethral regeneration, and many researchers have attempted to apply them in clinical studies. The most popular of these are epithelial cells, as a continuous epithelial layer plays an important role in resisting urine and can be effective in avoiding wound erosion and urethral fistulae22. With the research on the extensive differentiation and transdifferentiation abilities of stem cells as well as their wide range of bioregulatory potentials, researchers are now attempting to obtain seed cells from stem cells23. Takahashi 24 successfully transformed mouse fibroblasts into iPSCs by genetic reprogramming technique in 2006, and the research on iPSCs has gradually intensified since then. Several studies have found that iPSCs can be transformed into urothelial cells through targeted induced transformation5,25–27. Based on the good characteristics and differentiation potential of iPSCs, we selected them as the source of seed cells in this experiment.

Based on the previous research results, we proposed whether PRF could be compounded with urothelial cells and applied to the urethral repair, so we conducted this preliminary experiment. In this experiment, we selected PRF as the scaffold and iPSCs-derived urothelial cells as seed cells, and for the first time, we combined them in culture. The results showed that iPSCs could be successfully transformed into urothelial cells, PRF had a good three-dimensional spatial structure, and it could also promote the proliferation and migration of urothelial cells. The PRF and urothelial cells had good biocompatibility. This lays a good foundation for us to use as an alternative material for urethral repair in further study.

Admittedly, there are some limitations to this experiment. Firstly, we transformed the iPSCs into urothelial cells by induced transformation, we only studied the proliferation and migration of urothelial cells, we did not study the maturation and differentiation of urothelial cells in a more in-depth manner, which requires us to follow up with further research. Secondly, considering the cost and the difficulty of the experimental process, we chose rats to prepare PRF membranes, and there have been several cases of rat PRF membranes being applied to different organisms, whether it will be affected by the difference in species can’t be analysed systematically at present because of the small sample size, which is also an issue we need to further study in the next step.

Conclusions

PRF is biocompatible with urothelial cells and it promotes the proliferation and migration of urothelial cells, which lays a good foundation for its use as an alternative material for urethral repair.

Methods

Institutional review board statement

The animal study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hainan Medical College (No. HYLL-2022-403, 16 November 2022). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The study was carried out in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Cultivation of iPSCs

In this study, mouse iPSCs were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Mouse iPSCs were cultured in a high-glucose DMEM medium. When the cells were fused to 90%, the medium was discarded, washed with PBS, and added with trypsin-EDTA digestion solution, and observed under the microscope for about 30 s. When the cells became rounded, a complete medium was added to terminate the di-gestion quickly, and the cells were collected. The cells were resuspended with a complete medium and cultured in separate flasks, and the solution was changed every other day.

Identification of the iPSCs

Alkaline phosphatase stain

Cells are discarded from the old medium and washed with pre-warmed DMEM/F-12 medium for 2–3 min, the medium is aspirated discarded, and repeated once. Prepare 1× AP live staining working solution by diluting 500× stock solution in DMEM/F-12. Appropriate amount of 1× AP live staining solution was applied directly onto the adherent cell cultures and incubated for 20–30 min. The DMEM/F-12 medium is washed again and then the fluorescent-labeled colonies are displayed on a fluorescence microscope (200×) using a standard FITC filter. Images can be captured within 30–90 min after staining, and after visualization, the DMEM/F-12 medium is replaced with fresh medium and the cells can be returned to normal culture.

Observation of cell morphology

The morphology of iPSCs was observed under a light microscope (100×).

Cell immunofluorescence

Cells were inoculated into 6-well plates at 5 × 105 cells/well respectively, observed under an inverted microscope, and the supernatant was aspirated and discarded when the cell proliferation reached 80%. The cells were fixed with 500 µL 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, washed with PBS added with 0.1% Triton X-100, and incubated at room temperature for 10 min, washed with PBS. The cells were blocked with a blocking solution for 30 min at room temperature, the blocking solution was discarded, and a primary antibody (Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488)) was added, and the cells were incubated at 4 °C overnight. At the end of incubation, PBS solution was washed, and then the corresponding Alexa Fluor 488 labeled secondary antibody (Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488)) was added and incubated for 1 h in the dark, and washed three times with PBS solution, each time for 5 min. The plate was sealed by adding DAPI dropwise, and then the excess liquid was drained off, the plates were observed under a laser confocal microscope. The expression of Oct4, Sox2, and SSEA-1 was observed under a laser confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM), and the field of view was randomly selected at 200× magnification for photographing (Antibodies and dilution are shown in Table 1).

Western blot

Extraction of total cellular proteins: Cell samples were lysed with RIPA containing PMSF, and the supernatant was collected in a pre-cooled Eppendorf tube after centrifu-gation. The cellular proteins obtained from the extraction were quantified using the BCA method. Gel electrophoresis and membrane transfer: To measure proteins with different molecular weights, 10% separating gel and 5% concentrating gel were prepared for SDS-PAGE. The gel was filled, and 1× electrophoresis buffer was added. Sampling began with the volume of samples adjusted to 60 µL. Proteins on the membrane were observed after the completion of membrane transfer. Developing: The membrane was wetted and transferred to a dish with a sealing solution, and closed by shaking on a decolourising shaker for 1 h at room temperature to seal the immunoglobulin binding site of the PVDF membrane. The primary antibody was added overnight. The membrane was washed with TBST, placed in a closed pouch, and a secondary antibody was added (the primary antibody, secondary antibody and dilution ratio are as shown in Table 1). The pouch was closed and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The membrane was washed with TBST, and equal volumes of chemiluminescent reagents, liquid A and liquid B, were mixed. The mem-brane proteins were placed face down in full contact with this mixture for 5 min, then detected by Tanon-6600 Luminescence Imaging Workstation. Protein expression was analyzed using Image Pro Plus 6.0 software for optical density values.

PCR

mRNA extraction: cell suspension was inoculated in a 6-well plate with 5 × 105 cells per well, when the cell confluence reached 90%, the cells were collected and centrifuged. Trizol, chloroform, and isopropanol were added in sequence for shaking, and centrifugation respectively to precipitate the RNA. Mixing the dissolved RNA to get the total solution, and 2 µL of the sample was taken, the concentration and purity of cDNA were determined by Nanodrop-2000. cDNA synthesis: take 2 µL of total RNA as a template, configure the reverse transcription reaction system according to the kit manual, place the reaction system in the PCR instrument, select the set program, and complete the cDNA. Real-time quantitative PCR: Carry out the reaction system configuration according to the kit manual, add the premixed reagents into the 8-link tube, and set the reaction parameters to amplification. The data were processed by the 2-ΔΔCt formula, and the expression level of the target mRNA was obtained using the internal reference gene β-actin as a reference (PCR reaction condition and the primers used are shown in Tables 2 and 3).

Preparation of PRF and HE staining

10 mL of healthy rat whole blood was collected into an anticoagulant-free test tube and immediately centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 10 min. After centrifugation, the blood in the centrifuge tube was divided into three layers, with a yellowish platelet-poor plasma (PPP) layer on the top, a red blood cell debris layer on the bottom, and a platelet-rich fibrin gel layer in between, the PRF gel was removed and left to stand for 10 min to be slowly dehydrated to get the PRF.

Fresh PRF was prepared, fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, and dehydrated. The tissue wax blocks were loaded on the clamping table of the slicer and the thickness adjuster was adjusted to 5 microns for slicing. The prepared paraffin sections of PRF membranes were baked in an electrically heated oven for 3 h, and the dried paraffin sections were subjected to conventional xylene dewaxing, ethanol hydration, distilled water washing, hematoxy-lin-stained nuclei, and eosin-stained solution staining and then sealed. A field of view was randomly selected using a phase contrast microscope to be photographed using a 1000× magnification field of view.

Transmission electron microscopy observation of PRF

The prepared PRF was placed into a 6-well plate, take 1 mL of cell suspension and drop it on 1 piece of PRF membrane (each 1 mL of PRF prepared from arterial blood is 1 piece of PRF) for culture, the PRF membrane inoculated with cells was fixed after the culture was completed. The simple PRF membrane and the PRF membrane containing cells were fixed and dehydrated after osmium acid, and the dehydrated specimens were infiltrated and embedded. The embedded specimens were cut into sections with a thickness of 70 nm on an ultrathin sectioning machine. The sections were stained with lead citrate and uranyl acetate, after the ultra-thin sections were dried, they were observed by transmission electron microscopy and photographed.

Effect of PRF on the proliferation and migration of urothelial cells

Urothelial cells derived from iPSC in the logarithmic growth phase were taken and made into cell suspension and inoculated into 96-well plates at a concentration of 3 × 103/mL, 100 µL per well. DMEM and PRF medium were added 24 h later, respectively, and incubated for 24, 48, and 72 h, respectively. 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and incubation was continued for 1 h. The absorbance was detected at 450 nm by an enzyme counter-clockwise.

Cells were routinely digested and centrifuged, then resuspended using serum-free medium and counted, the cell density was adjusted to 2.5 × 105 cells/mL. Incubated chambers were removed, and cell suspension was added to the upper chamber for in-cubation, after incubation, the chambers were removed for staining. After staining, cells passing through the chambers were counted using an inverted microscope with randomly selected counting fields.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed and graphed using GraphPad Prism 9 (Version 9.4.0) and collated using Adobe Illustrator 2022 (Version 2022). All data were expressed as mean ± SD, and statistical differences between groups were determined by 2-way ANOVA, one-way ANOVA, and Tukey’s test. P values < 0.05 were considered significant.
