First spectral dataset of Parthenium hysterophorus (Famine Weed) to support multi-sensor species classification and modelling
Authors: Mahlatse Kganyago
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Parthenium hysterophorus (Famine weed) is considered one of the top seven most problematic and devastating weeds in the world. It compromises the integrity of ecosystems, human health, agricultural production and biodiversity. For example, the species releases toxic chemicals such phenolics and lactones which inhibit germination and growth of co-occurring species, leading to declines in pasture production, dry grass biomass and natural habitats and biodiversity. It may also lead to health complications in human populations, declined quality of milk and meat products from cattle and degenerative changes in liver and kidney of sheep and buffalo. Therefore, its early detection and discrimination are critical for facilitating site-specific weed management to avert its adverse impacts. The spectral library datasets that characterize P. hysterophorus spectral properties, critical for its detection, are not available in the public domain. Through this article, we aim to make accessible the first spectral dataset for P. hysterophorus and its co-occurring plant species to the research community to facilitate the development of techniques for effectively identifying and mapping its distribution at various scales, facilitate the development of more robust dimensionality reduction and classification models, and support mapping efforts aiming to scale up to operational analysis with airborne and satellite sensors. The data was collected using the Spectral Evolution spectroradiometer at Ndumo Game Reserve in KwaZulu-Natal province, South Africa. It is made available as Tables (.csv format) containing averaged Raw spectra (per Elementary Sampling Unit [ESU]), pre-processed and multi-sensor resampled data. Moreover, codes are provided in R-Statistical language to replicate the preprocessing and resampling steps. This spectral library dataset for P. hysterophorus is invaluable for aiding early detection and discrimination and evaluating the effectiveness of eradication measures, thus can potentially aid resource allocation and mitigation of severe impacts by the species.

Background

Parthenium hysterophorus L. (Famine weed), a globally invasive species, severely impacts agriculture, ecosystems, and human/livestock health. It exhibits significant allelopathic characteristics, which are a critical factor in its aggressive invasiveness and detrimental impact on agricultural systems, natural ecosystems, and biodiversity [2,3]. Allelopathy refers to the release of biochemicals from one plant that can influence the growth and development of other plants in its vicinity, either by inhibiting or, in some cases, stimulating them [4]. P. hysterophorus produces a variety of allelopathic chemicals, primarily sesquiterpene lactones such as parthenin, hysterin, and ambrosin, along with other phytotoxic flavonoids [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. These phytotoxins are released into the environment through several mechanisms, including through shoots, roots, inflorescences, and seeds [6]. The pollen grains of P. hysterophorus can also fall onto the floral structures of various crops, leading to poor seed set and indirect yield losses. Moreover, Water-soluble germination inhibitors, including parthenin and certain phenolic acids, are present in the layers surrounding the seed [2]. These inhibitors must be leached out before maximum germination can occur. The allelopathic properties of P. hysterophorus enable it to outcompete and suppress a wide array of native vegetation and cultivated crops, often leading to the formation of dense monospecific stands [3]. Climate projection studies [2,10,11] show that climate change, particularly increasing temperatures, elevated atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) levels, and altered precipitation patterns leading to droughts, is predicted to significantly exacerbate the spread and impact of P. hysterophorus.

Early detection is, therefore, critical for effective management [12,13], as established infestations are costly and labor-intensive. Existing methods (physical, chemical, biological) face limitations, such as herbicide resistance, health risks, or incomplete eradication [14,15]. Remote sensing, particularly hyperspectral data, offers a rapid, scalable alternative for early detection by exploiting species-specific spectral signatures linked to biophysical/biochemical traits. However, the lack of published spectral libraries for P. hysterophorus hinders the development of spectral discrimination tools. The narrow, contiguous bands in hyperspectral data enable fine-scale discrimination of plant species, supporting identification, mapping and monitoring across various scales, i.e., from field to landscape levels, offering advantages in terms of speed and cost compared to conventional surveying methods. This dataset addresses the gap in spectral reference data for P. hysterophorus, essential for training machine learning algorithms. By capturing its spectral reflectance properties, the dataset facilitates species identification, multi-sensor evaluation, and feature optimization (e.g., dimensionality reduction) for early detection.

This data article complements our original research paper [1] by providing foundational spectral information necessary for operationalizing remote sensing-based detection. It enables reproducibility, supports algorithm development, and fills a critical gap in spectral libraries for invasive species management, directly aiding efforts to prioritize timely eradication in vulnerable ecosystems.

Data Description

This article describes Parthenium hysterophorus L. (Famine weed) spectral library dataset repository. The dataset was collected using PSR-3500 field spectrometer under field conditions in Ndumo Game Reserve (26°54′43′' S, 32°15′48′' E) in KwaZulu-Natal province, South Africa (Fig. 1). The area experiences a summer rainfall regime, with annual precipitation ranging between 500 mm and 2000 mm. Seasonal temperature variations are marked by cooler winter averages of 13.9 °C and warmer summer averages of 21.7 °C [16]. Ecologically, the area is classified within the Savanna biome, typified by open Lowveld savanna vegetation interspersed with shrubs and grasses. Dominant tree species include Acacia tortilis (umbrella thorn), Acacia karroo (sweet thorn), and Spirostachys africana (tamboti). Grass species composition varies with disturbance levels: Aristida congesta subsp. barbicollis (spreading prickle grass) and Bothriochloa insculpta (pinhole grass) thrive in highly disturbed areas, whereas Themeda triandra (red grass) and Heteropogon contortus (spear grass) dominate less disturbed landscapes. The proliferation of invasive alien plants (IAPs), particularly Parthenium hysterophorus, has emerged as a significant ecological concern. Its infestations are prevalent along roadsides, croplands, fencelines, residential areas, disturbed grasslands, and protected zones. P. hysterophorus was initially documented in KwaZulu-Natal in 1880, with substantial post-establishment spread reported following the 1984 floods associated with Cyclone Demoina [11,17]. Ndumo Game Reserve is one of the province’s most severely infested protected areas. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Sampling frame

A field survey was conducted in December 2014 (during summer, rainy season) across multiple Parthenium hysterophorus (Famine weed) infestation sites within Ndumo Game Reserve to understand its spatial distribution. Prior to hyperspectral data acquisition, particular attention was given to documenting the ecological conditions associated with Parthenium hysterophorus presence, particularly the identification of co-occurring plant species. Based on field observations, 1 × 1 m plots, i.e., Elementary Sampling Unit (ESU), were demarcated to represent areas of homogeneous juvenile P. hysterophorus with greater than 90 % canopy cover and adjacent ESUs were created to represent co-occurring species, i.e., Acacia Trees (AT), Grasses (GS) and Other Plant Species (OPS) (Fig. 4). A total of 149 Elementary Sampling Units (ESUs) encompassing both homogeneous stands of P. hysterophorus (65) and co-occurring vegetation species, i.e., grasses (19), Acacia sp. (33) and other plants (32), were randomly selected across the study area. This sampling strategy aimed to capture intra-species variability while ensuring adequate representation of both P. hysterophorus and its co-occurring species. Within each ESU, field spectroscopy measurements were obtained from a minimum of three to five randomized sampling positions to account for within-class spectral variability, spatial heterogeneity and enhance data robustness. Therefore, an average of three to five spectral measurements constituted each ESU.Fig. 4Illustration of differences in canopy and leaf structures of Parthenium hysterophorus (a), Acacia sp. Field hyperspectral measurements

Hyperspectral measurements were obtained in December 2014 using Spectral Evolution PSR-3500 spectroradiometer. The Spectral Evolution PSR-3500 spectroradiometer is designed to make detailed spectral measurements across the full spectral range of 350 nm to 2500 nm. The PSR-3500 spectroradiometer is equipped with multiple detectors, offering varying spectral resolution across different parts of the electromagnetic spectrum: approximately 3.5 nm at regions between 350 nm and 1000 nm, 10 nm at 1500 nm, and 7 nm at 2100 nm. Therefore, the spectral bands in these regions also had different spectral sampling intervals of 1.5, 3.8, and 2.5 nm, respectively, resulting into a total of 1024 spectral bands. The spectroradiometer was equipped with a 1.5 m Fiber optic cable with a 25° field of view (FOV), attached to a pistol grip. We consistently held the fiber optic cable at a distance of 50 cm from the canopy, at nadir direction, and away from their operator’s body and shadows. As a result, each spectral measurement had a circular Instantaneous FOV (IFOV) with a radius of about 11.08 cm. Internally, the spectroradiometer measured and averaged 10 scans (i.e., spectral signatures) per measurement. To calibrate the spectroradiometer, irradiance measurements were taken from a white reference panel, i.e., Spectralon, prior to each ESU spectral measurements and whenever wind conditions and sun direction changed. Moreover, to reduce the impact of sun angle variability, excessive canopy shadows, atmospheric perturbations and bidirectional reflectance distribution function (BDRF) effects, field hyperspectral measurements were obtained under cloudless conditions between 10:00 am and 14:00 pm Central African Time [[19], [20], [21]]. Each measurement was displayed, in real-time, on the handheld Spectral Evolution Personal Digital Assistant (PDA), equipped with Darwin SP software and a Windows operating system. Pre-processing

To achieve finer and consistent sampling intervals, instead of the original of 1.5 nm, 3.8 nm, and 2.5 nm, the spectral data was interpolated to 1 nm using Cubic spline interpolation, yielding 2151 bands. This step ensured that the errors potentially arising from varying spectral sampling interval and detector steps of the spectrometer could be minimized [22,23]. Next, the spectral dataset was subject to various preprocessing steps, including noisy regions removal and smoothing. Thenkabail et al. [24] identified the spectral bands located at 350 nm – 399 nm, 1350 nm – 1465 nm, and 1790 nm – 1960 nm as irrelevant for vegetation analysis due to their susceptivity to noise. Moreover, the region between 2350 nm – 2500 nm was also masked in this dataset due to obvious noise. Spectral reflectance library of Parthenium hysterophorus and co-occurring plant species before (i.e., Raw spectra) and after noisy regions (350–399 nm; 1350–1465 nm, 1790–1960 nm, and 2350–2500 nm) are masked (i.e., Masked spectra).

As separate treatments, the Raw spectral data were also subject to Savitzky Golay filtering (Savitzky and Golay, 1964) with quadratic polynomial (i.e., p = 2) and a window size (w) of 11, and a Moving average filter with a window size (w) of 11. These parameters resulted in relatively smooth spectral curves, which closely maintaining the absorption features across all wavelengths in Savitzky Golay filtered data only. The use of filtered spectral dataset with these spectral filtering or smoothing techniques for quantitative is entirely up to the user; however, our previous study [1] found that Savitzky Golay filtering causes a loss of essential absorption features, which are essential for achieving greater separability and reducing generalization errors. The effect of the filter approaches is displayed in Fig. 7. As shown, Savitsky-Golay filter with p = 2 and window size (w) of 11 maintains the general shape of the absorption features, while the Moving average filter a window size (w) of 11, provided a smooth spectral curve, causing absorption feature to diminish.Fig. 7Raw and Pre-processed Parthenium hysterophorus (pH) spectral reflectance using Moving Average with a window size (w) of 11 and Savitzky-Golay with p = 3 and w = 11.Fig 7

Raw and Pre-processed Parthenium hysterophorus (pH) spectral reflectance using Moving Average with a window size (w) of 11 and Savitzky-Golay with p = 3 and w = 11.

Multi-sensor spectral library

Finally, the spectral libraries were resampled to the spectral settings of hyperspectral and multispectral sensors, i.e., EnMAP, Hyperion, Sentinel-2 and Worldview-2 (Fig. 8).
