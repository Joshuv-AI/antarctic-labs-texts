Dual induction of tissue-resident T and B cell immunity for broad influenza protection with a nanofiber-VLP vaccine
Authors: Xiuyu Wang, Haoran Luo, Zhenyu Fan, Ran Xie, Shiqiang Liu, Qicong Pan, Zhihao Zhang, Fangnan Yan, Jiaxin Li, Chengjie Yang, Bibo Zhu, Jing Ye, Shengbo Cao, Anita S. Chong, Chen Tan, Youhui Si
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Respiratory pathogens pose significant global health challenges, with current vaccines often failing to prevent viral replication at mucosal entry points. To address this, we developed an adjuvant-free intranasal nanovaccine (NP@HA-VLP-Q11) by integrating SpyTag/SpyCatcher-mediated antigen multimerization on virus-like particles (VLPs) with self-assembling Q11 nanofibers. This vaccine leverages the ability of Q11 to induce both humoral and cellular immunity without requiring supplemental adjuvants or eliciting local inflammation to co-deliver a conserved nucleoprotein (NP) CD8+ T cell epitope and influenza hemagglutinin stem domain (miniHA) VLPs. In mouse models, we show that NP@HA-VLP-Q11 elicits systemic neutralizing antibodies and lung-resident memory T cells (TRMs) and B cells (BRMs) that are maintained for at least 12 weeks after immunization and expand rapidly upon heterologous H9N2 challenge. Importantly, intranasal immunization with NP@HA-VLP-Q11 conferred immunity and cross-protection against heterologous influenza strains that were preserved for at least 12 weeks. The dual induction of TRMs and BRMs offers immediate humoral protection and sustained cellular immunity at the infection site, mimicking natural viral exposure while avoiding replication risks. These findings demonstrate the potential of self-assembling nanomaterials combined with stabilized multimerized antigens in VLPs to create next-generation vaccines for broad, broad and effective protection against respiratory pathogens.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04349-7.

Introduction

Respiratory pathogens such as influenza viruses and SARS-CoV-2 pose significant global health burden. Vaccines are essential for disease mitigation, but their efficacy is often limited. Influenza vaccines, for instance, face challenges from antigenic drift and strain variability, leading to suboptimal protection particularly in high-risk populations [1]. Similarly, although mRNA vaccines have been highly effective against COVID-19 [2–4], breakthrough infections and immune evasion by emerging variants highlight the limitations of systemic immunity in preventing viral infection and replication at mucosal entry points [5]. A key issue is that many current vaccines fail to establish robust defenses at the lung mucosa, which allows pathogens to replicate locally before systemic immune activation occurs. This deficiency underscores the urgent need for immunization strategies that induce potent mucosal and tissue-resident immunity at the site of infection [6–9]. However, vaccines that elicit durable, protective immune responses within the respiratory mucosa while eliciting minimal inflammation at the vaccination site remains a significant challenge [10].

The lung represents a complex immunological interface, continuously exposed to environmental insults and pathogens. Its defense relies on tightly regulated interactions between structural cells, innate immune sentinels, and adaptive effectors. Tissue-resident memory T cells (TRMs) and B cells (BRMs) are crucial for local protection [11]. TRMs rapidly recognize and eliminate infected cells [12–15], while BRM-derived antibodies neutralize pathogens at mucosal surfaces [16, 17]. Indeed, influenza-specific TRMs in murine models provide heterosubtypic protection [18], and BRMs populations in human lungs are associated with reduced viral shedding [19, 20]. Despite their importance, conventional subunit vaccines that are delivered intramuscularly are inefficient at inducing lung TRMs and BRMs, as these cells typically require antigens within the lung to provide localization cues [13, 18]. Live attenuated vaccines, while effective, are associated with safety concerns, and adjuvants that balance mucosal efficacy with inflammatory tolerance remain clinically elusive. Thus, the development of non-replicating vaccine platforms that elicit durable tissue-resident immunity is an unmet need.

The SpyTag/SpyCatcher system is a highly efficient and specific protein-protein interaction tool, where the peptide SpyTag forms a rapid, irreversible covalent bond with its partner protein SpyCatcher [21, 22]. This system is versatile, stable under diverse conditions, and resistant to harsh environments, offering a robust method for creating stable protein complexes. In this study, we leverage the SpyTag003/SpyCatcher003 system [23], an optimized version enabling faster and more stable protein-protein interactions, to stably display influenza hemagglutinin stem domain (miniHA) antigens on virus-like particles (VLPs). These miniHA antigens are designed to elicit broadly protective, heterologous antibody responses against influenza viruses [24–26]. In our studies, VLPs are further combined with Q11 self-assembling nanofibers, which co-present the conserved nucleoprotein (NP) CD8+ T cell epitope (QIASNENMETMESSTL) from influenza A/PR/8/34 (H1N1 PR8), to form a NP@HA-VLP-Q11 vaccine. The Q11 scaffold, a synthetic peptide platform capable of autonomous assembly into nanofibers, enables adjuvant-free induction of both T cell and B cell epitope-specific immune responses [27, 28]. Building on previous work demonstrating the ability of Q11 nanofiber vaccines to elicit circulating and lung-resident memory CD8+ T cells via intranasal delivery [27, 29], we engineered a dual-component vaccine that spatially organizes neutralizing miniHA trimers alongside NP-specific T cell epitopes. This design mimics natural viral exposure at the respiratory mucosa in engaging both humoral and cellular arms of immunity while bypassing the risks of replication. In murine models, needle-free intranasal immunization with NP@HA-VLP-Q11 without the need for supplemental adjuvants induced systemic neutralizing antibodies and durable TRMs and BRMs. These resident effectors persisted for months and mounted rapid recall responses against heterologous influenza strains, conferring cross-protection. By precisely controlling the presentation of a conserved CD8+ T cell epitope and an antibody-neutralizing antigen domain, our platform established a dual-layered defense of both mucosal and systemic immunity at the pathogen’s portal of entry. This modular approach demonstrates the potential of integrating self-assembling nanomaterials with structurally stabilized antigens to engineer next-generation vaccines that elicit robust protective immunity without the need for adjuvants. This novel approach offers a promising vaccine platform for combating antigenically variable respiratory pathogens.

Materials and methods

Ethics statements

All experimental procedures were approved by the Scientific Ethics Committee of Huazhong Agricultural University (approval number: HZAUMO-2024-0027). Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility at the university’s Laboratory Animal Center under controlled temperature and light cycles.

Viruses and cells

The A/Puerto Rico/8/34 (H1N1) (PR8) and A/chicken/China/2018 (H9N2) strain were stored in our laboratory. Expi 293 F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were maintained in 293 cell culture (Union Biotechnology) at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. MDCK (Madin-Darby canine kidney) cells were maintained in minimum essential medium (MEM; Gibco) supplement with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

Protein expression and purification

The miniHA-SpyTag003 gene was cloned into the pFUSEss vector with 6×His-tag in the C terminal. MiniHA-SpyTag003 was expressed in Expi 293 F cells cultured in Union-293 medium by transient transfection using branched polyethylenimine transfection reagent. Supernatants were harvested after 4 days followed by filtration through a 0.45 μm filter. The protein was enriched using Ni-NTA agarose, and the bound protein was eluted by gradient imidazole in buffer. Elution fractions were pooled and the buffer was exchanged to TBS buffer (pH 8.0).

SpyCatcher003-mi3 was a gift from Mark Howarth [21] (Addgene plasmid # 159995 ; http://n2t.net/addgene:159995 ; RRID: Addgene_159995) and expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) as previously described [22, 30]. Protein expression was induced with IPTG and incubated at 22 °C, 200 rpm for 16 h. Cell suspension was sonicated on ice four times for 60 s using an Ultrasonic Processor. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 17,000 × g for 45 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter and precipitated with ammonium sulfate. SpyCatcher003-mi3 were pelleted by centrifugation at 17,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The sediment was resuspended in TBS (pH 8.5; 25 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl), and residual ammonium sulfate was removed by dialysis for 16 h against 500-fold excess TBS. Dialyzed SpyCatcher003-mi3 was concentrated to 4 mg/mL using a 100 kDa spin concentrator (Millipore) and centrifuged at 17,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter. The purified SpyCatcher003-mi3 was further purified using size-exclusion chromatography (SEC). In brief, 2 mL was loaded into a molecular-exclusion chromatography column equilibrated with TBS using an ÄKTA Pure 25 system. Proteins were separated at 1 mL/min while collecting 1 mL elution factions. The fractions containing purified particles were identified by SDS-PAGE, pooled, and concentrated using a Millipore 100 kDa MWCO centrifugal concentrator.

SpyTag003-Q11 (RGVPHIVMVDAYKRYKGS-GGAAY-QQKFQFQFEQQ) and NP-Q11 (QIASNENMETMESSTL-GGAAY-QQKFQFQFEQQ), both with an N-terminal acetylation group and a C-terminal amidation, were synthesized by Genscript with a purity of > 90%. The peptides were chemically synthesized using standard Fmoc solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) methodology and purified by reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) to a final purity of > 90%. Their molecular weights and identities were confirmed by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HR-MS).

Optimizing the preparation of NP@HA VLP-Q11 conjugation in vitro

To optimize the combination conditions of miniHA-SpyVLP, miniHA-SpyTag003 and SpyCatcher003-mi3 was diluted to 2mM in TBS buffer (pH 8.0). And then they were mixed to conjugate at 4 °C for 16 h with molar ratio 1:0.5, 1:0.75, 1:1, 1:1.25, 1:1.5 and 1:2. Possible aggregates were then removed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. To prepare nanofibers, lyophilized SpyTag003-Q11 and NP-Q11 were dissolved in ultrapure water at 2:1 molar ratio and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The following day, ultrapure water and 10× PBS were added to achieve a 2 mM total peptide concentration in 1× PBS, and the peptide solution was stored at room temperature for 3 h prior to use. Finally, to form NP@HA-VLP-Q11 conjugates, HA-VLP was mixed with nanofibers and incubated over night at 4 °C before immunization.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

NP@HA-VLP-Q11 were prepared as described for immunizations and diluted to 0.2 mM in 1 × PBS. Then, 5 µL of diluted nanofiber solution was spotted onto Formvar/carbon-coated 400-mesh copper grids. The sample was allowed to incubate for 1 min, washed three times with 0.2 μm filtered ultrapure water, and negatively stained for 1 min with 5 uL of 2% (w/v) uranyl acetate, followed by wicking of the staining solution with filter paper. Samples were imaged using Hitachi TEM.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS)

Prior to measurement, samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove aggregates. Then the total protein concentration of the sample was diluted to 125–250 µg/mL. A 1 mL sample was analyzed using Zetasizer Nano ZS at 25 °C, performing 20 scans of 10 s each.

Administration to mice by intranasal routes

8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Research Center of Huazhong Agricultural University (Wuhan, China) and approved by the Ethical and Welfare Committee. Mice received intranasal immunizations (three times unless specified otherwise) with 10 µg NP@HA-VLP-Q11 (Based on miniHA-SpyTag003 mass) on day 0. Two and four weeks after primary immunization, mice were boosted with the same immunogen. Blood was collected weekly via the submandibular vein. PBMCs were collected at 10 days after the primary immunization, 7 days after the booster, and 5 days after the 2nd boost for NP-specific CD8⁺ T cell analyses.

Influenza virus challenge

The mice were anesthetized and intranasally challenged with a lethal dose (5 × LD50) of Influenza A virus A/PR/8/34 (H1N1) or H9N2 at 2 weeks after the second immunization boost. Weight loss was monitored daily for 15 days and the mice that lost more than 25% of their initial body weight were euthanized according to ethical guidelines.

Histology analysis

Lung tissues from mice of different immunization groups were collected 7 days post-challenge, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde buffer (Biosharp) for 48 h, then embedded in paraffin and sectioned longitudinally. Finally, pathological sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological analysis.

Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) of antibody titers

96-well plates were coated with 0.5 µg/mL solution of the miniHA-ST at 4 °C overnight (100 µL per well). Plates were washed with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBS-T) and subsequently blocked with block buffer (PBS-T containing 2% BSA) for 2 h at RT. After washing, serum was added to the plate in duplicate, serially diluted in block buffer and incubated for 2 h at 37 ℃. Following a wash with PBS-T, a 1:5000 dilution of Goat-anti-Mouse IgG-HRP was added to the plate for mouse samples. After incubation for 40 min at 37 °C, plates were washed and developed using substrate. The reaction was stopped after 10 min by adding 1M H2SO4. The optical density was measured at 630 nm with an ELISA reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). ELISA titers were expressed as log10 ELISA Units.

Virus neutralization assay (VNT)

MDCK cells were inoculated into 96-well plates and cultured into monolayers. Serum samples that had been inactivated (56 °C, 30 min) were 2-fold gradient diluted using MEM medium and incubated with 100 TCID50 PR8 or H9N2 virus at 37 °C for 1 h. The serum and virus mixture were then added to the MDCK monolayers along with 1 µg/mL TPCK-treated trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich) and incubated for 1 h (37 °C, 5% CO2), then the medium was removed from each well and replaced with fresh MEM containing 1 µg/mL TPCK-treated trypsin. The cells were allowed to incubate for additional 5 days at 37 °C, 5% CO2. After 5 days, the titer of the IAV-specific neutralizing antibody was calculated and expressed as the log2 of the reciprocal of the highest dilution of when MDCK cells infection was inhibited cytopathic effect (CPE).

Antigen uptake in vitro

Bone marrow cells from C57BL/6 mice were isolated and prepared as single-cell suspensions. Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1 µg/mL Flt3L. On day 6, BMDCs were seeded into 24-well plates and stimulated for 12 h with equivalent concentrations (10 µg/mL) of NP@HA-VLP-Q11-FITC, Q11-FITC, or RPMI medium as a mock control. Cells were then harvested for flow cytometry analysis to assess FITC uptake and the expression levels of CD80, CD86, and CD40 on BMDCs.

Cell preparation and flow cytometry

To identify circulating NP specific cells in CD8+ T cells, freshly isolated PBMCs were stained with cocktails of T cell surface markers for 30 min. Zombie Nir Fixable Viability Kit (1:1000) were used to gate live cells. The following antibodies were used: Brilliant Violet 605 anti-mouse CD8 (1:200), Brilliant Violet 510 anti-mouse CD3 (1:200), FITC anti-mouse CD4 (1:200), APC anti-mouse CD44 (1:200), Brilliant Violet 421 anti-mouse CD19 (1:200). For NP-specific T cells identification, cells were incubated with NP: H-2Db tetramers for 1 h at RT before adding other antibodies. Cells were washed three times and then resuspended in flow buffer for FCM analysis.

To identify total GC B cells, freshly isolated LN and spleen single cell suspensions were stained with the surface cocktail containing the following antibodies, the Zombie NIR Fixable Viability Kit were used to gate live cells, APC/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD19 (1:200), PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD95 (Fas) (1:200), PE/Dazzle 594 anti-mouse CD38 (1:200), Brilliant Violet 510 anti-mouse CD3 (1:200).

To identify lung-resident memory cells, mice were injected intravenously with 3 µg PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD45 antibody 10 min before sacrifice to distinguish circulating cells from tissue-localized cells. Lungs were collected and perfused with 20 mL PBS through the right ventricle. Each lung was cut into small pieces with scissors to homogenize the tissues followed by digestion using Collagenase and Deoxyribonuclease I for 1 h in 10mL PBS with 5% FBS at 37 °C with slow tilt rotation. After enzymatic digestion, the tissue was further dissociated by repeated pipetting of digested lung tissue adequately and filtered through a 70 μm filter strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. Red blood cells (RBCs) were removed with ACK lysis buffer followed by washing with PBS twice. All samples were then counted and stained with the Zombie NIR Fixable Viability Kit, and blocked with TruStain FcX PLUS (anti-mouse CD16/32) to exclude dead cells and to prevent unspecific binding. For memory B cells surface staining, cells were then stained with APC/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD19 (1:200), Brilliant Violet 421 anti-mouse CD138 (1:200), Brilliant Violet 711 anti-mouse IgD (1:200), Brilliant Violet 510 anti-mouse CD3 (1:200), Brilliant Violet 605 anti-mouse IgM (1:100), FITC anti-mouse IgA (1:50), PE/Dazzle 594 anti-mouse CD38 (1:100 dilution), Alexa Fluor 647 anti-mouse IgG (1:200), APC anti-mouse CD95 (Fas) (1:200) at 4 °C for 30 min. For HA-specific BRM quantitation, recombinant miniHA-ST protein was conjugated to PE by using PE/R-Phycoerythrin Conjugation Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were incubated with 10 µg/mL miniHA-ST-PE (50 µL) for 1 h prior to FCM analysis. For the identification of tissue resident NP-specific memory CD8+ T cells, cells were incubated with NP: H-2Db tetramers for 60 min and flowed stained with Brilliant Violet 605 anti-mouse CD8 (1:200), Brilliant Violet 510 anti-mouse CD3 (1:200), FITC anti-mouse CD4 (1:200), APC anti-mouse CD44 (1:200), Brilliant Violet 421 anti-mouse CD19 (1:200), PE/Dazzle594 anti-mouse CD69 (1:200), APC-R700 anti-mouse CD103 (1:200). Cells were washed three times and then resuspended in flow buffer for FCM analysis.

Flow cytometry was performed with Cytoflex LX flow cytometer (Beckman) and Aurora flow cytometer (Cytek), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software V_10.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Two-way ANOVA was used for variance analysis. Error bars for all experiments represent standard error of mean (SEM). The significance of the differences in all analyses was expressed as: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 or ***P < 0.001.

Results

Design and characterization of an influenza nanofiber-VLP vaccine

To develop a modular vaccine platform eliciting both humoral and cellular immune responses, we engineered a dual-component system combining epitope-displaying nanofibers with antigen-presenting virus-like particles (VLPs). The Q11 self-assembling peptide displayed a conserved influenza nucleoprotein (NP) CD8+ T cell epitope (QIASNENMETMESSTL), while VLPs presented structurally stabilized influenza hemagglutinin stem domain (miniHA). To integrate these components, NP-Q11 and SpyTag003-Q11 (Fig. 1A) were co-assembled at a 2:1 molar ratio, resulting in nanofibers displaying NP and SpyTag003 (RGVPHIVMVDAYKRYKGS). MiniHA, fused with a C-terminal SpyTag003 (miniHA-SpyTag003), was conjugated to SpyCatcher003-mi3 VLPs (SpyVLP) via spontaneous covalent bonding between SpyTag003 and SpyCatcher003 at an optimized molar ratio (1:2, miniHA: SpyVLP) (Fig. 1B and C, Figure S1). Gel electrophoresis confirmed that the SpyVLP were completely free of nucleic acid material (Figure S2). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) confirmed successful conjugation, with miniHA-SpyVLP exhibiting a hydrodynamic diameter of 38.4 nm, compared to 28.2 nm for unmodified SpyVLP (Fig. 1D). Residual SpyCatcher003 domains on miniHA-SpyVLP surfaces enabled covalent attachment of SpyTag003-displaying nanofibers, creating a composite vaccine (NP@HA-VLP-Q11) presenting both the CD8+ T cell epitope and B cell antigen (Fig. 1B). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed the structural integrity of this hybrid nanostructure, demonstrating successful integration of Q11 nanofibers with miniHA-VLPs (Fig. 1E). This design offers precise control over NP and miniHA presentation, using the SpyTag/SpyCatcher interaction for antigen orientation and Q11’s properties for adjuvant-free immune activation.

In comparisons of the different vaccine components, mice intranasally immunized with NP@HA-VLP-Q11 displayed significantly higher anti-miniHA IgG titers than those receiving HA-VLP or NP-Q11 alone, while maintaining NP-specific CD8⁺ T cell responses comparable to the NP-Q11 group. Two weeks after the third immunization, the NP@HA-VLP-Q11 group showed higher serum neutralizing antibody titers and superior protective efficacy against viral challenge relative to either single-component vaccine (Fig. 1I and K). Notably, although both the alum-adjuvanted formulation and the NP@HA-VLP-Q11 vaccine elicited comparable levels of serum anti-miniHA IgG, the NP@HA-VLP-Q11 group conferred significantly enhanced protection against PR8 influenza challenge. Collectively, these results demonstrate that integrating nanofiber and VLP architectures synergistically enhances both humoral and cellular immune responses, achieving protective efficacy that exceeds that of individual vaccine components or a conventional alum-adjuvanted formulation, without the need for external adjuvants.

To assess the biological activity of the NP@HA-VLP-Q11, in vitro assays were performed using bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that incubation with FITC-labeled NP@HA-VLP-Q11 markedly increased cellular fluorescence compared with Q11-FITC, indicating efficient uptake of the composite vaccine (Figure S4A-B). Furthermore, stimulation with NP@HA-VLP-Q11 significantly upregulated the expression of BMDCs activation markers CD80, CD86, and CD40 (Figure S4A, S4C-E). Collectively, the modular architecture allows the co-delivery of conformationally stabilized B cell antigens and T cell epitopes, together with potent innate immune activation, providing a foundation for adjuvant-free vaccine development.

NP@HA-VLP-Q11 vaccination induces NP-specific CD8+ T cells responses

Previous studies have shown that intranasal delivery of Q11-conjugated vaccines elicits NP antigen-specific CD8+ T cell responses, but inducing neutralizing anti-HA antibody responses with this vaccine platform has been challenging [31, 32]. To evaluate whether NP@HA-VLP-Q11 is able to induce systemic immunity, C57BL/6 mice received primary either intranasal or subcutaneous immunization (Day 0) followed by intranasal boosts at weeks 2 and 4 (Fig. 2A). Strikingly, NP@HA-VLP-Q11 induced a significant expansion of circulating NP-specific CD8+ T cells compared to naïve controls, detectable as early as 10 days post-primary immunization. This response was further amplified after two booster doses on day 14 and 28, with a pronounced increase in NP-tetramer+ CD8+ T cell frequencies in circulation (Fig. 2B and D, Figure S3). The subcutaneous. immunization elicited a higher frequency of circulating NP-specific CD8⁺ T cells than the intranasal route at each time point, indicating strong systemic T cell priming (Fig. 2C and D). Functional analysis revealed significant interferon-γ (IFN-γ) production by PBMC stimulated with NP peptides from both intranasal and subcutaneous immunized mice 5 days after 2nd boost (Fig. 2E-F). Together, these data demonstrate that whether administered intranasally or subcutaneously NP@HA-VLP-Q11 vaccination can elicit robust functional NP-specific CD8+ T cell responses in systemic circulation.

NP@HA-VLP-Q11 drives robust humoral immune responses via enhanced germinal center activity

To evaluate the humoral immunogenicity of NP@HA-VLP-Q11, we analyzed the serum from C57BL/6 mice intranasally or subcutaneously immunized with the same prime-boost regimen (prime at week 0, boosters at 2-week intervals; Fig. 3A). Both NP@HA-VLP-Q11 immunization routes elicited progressively increasing serum anti-miniHA IgG titers, with the S.C. group showing slightly higher titers than the I.N. group, while both were significantly elevated compared with naïve controls (Fig. 3B). In addition, robust miniHA-specific IgG and IgA responses at 2 weeks after 2nd boost immunization were detected in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid, suggesting the vaccine’s capacity to induce mucosal immunity (Fig. 3C).

Intranasal NP@HA-VLP-Q11 confers cross-protection against homologous and heterologous influenza challenges

To evaluate the protective efficacy of NP@HA-VLP-Q11, mice were intranasally or subcutaneously immunized and challenged with homologous (H1N1 PR8) or heterologous (H9N2) influenza viruses two weeks post-2nd boost (Fig. 4A). Pre-challenge serum analysis revealed significantly elevated viral neutralizing antibody titers in both NP@HA-VLP-Q11-vaccinated mice compared with naïve controls, with comparable titers observed between the I.N. and S.C. routes (Fig. 4B). Following viral challenge, NP@HA-VLP-Q11 conferred complete protection in the I.N. group, as evidenced by minimal body weight loss and 100% survival against H1N1 PR8 infection (Fig. 4C). The S.C. NP@HA-VLP-Q11 mice also exhibited substantial protection, although mild transient weight loss was observed before full recovery. H9N2, an avian-origin influenza A virus, shares 50.71% sequence identity in full-length HA and 63.13% in miniHA with H1N1 PR8. Notably, the vaccine also conferred cross-protection against heterosubtypic H9N2, with the I.N. group achieved 60% survival and reduced morbidity compared to naïve controls (Fig. 4D). Whereas all mice in the S.C. and naïve groups succumbed to infection, with no evident protective effect observed in the S.C. group. Histopathological assessment of lung tissues further validated the vaccine’s efficacy, demonstrating complete protection against infection-induced pulmonary damage in vaccinated mice (Fig. 4E and F). These findings demonstrate the capacity of NP@HA-VLP-Q11 intranasal immunization to elicit broad-spectrum immunity, providing robust protection against both homologous and heterologous influenza viruses while mitigating infection-associated pathology. The intranasal route confers superior efficacy, likely mediated by both antibody- and CD8+ T cell-mediated immunity. The significantly elevated viral neutralizing antibodies in serum suggests a critical role for humoral immunity, particularly in protection against homologous H1N1 PR8. However, the observed cross-protection against heterosubtypic H9N2, despite limited sequence identity between the HA proteins of H9N2 and H1N1 PR8, supports the involvement of cellular immunity.

Prolonged protective efficacy of NP@HA-VLP-Q11 against viral challenge

To evaluate the persistence of vaccine-induced protection, mice immunized with NP@HA-VLP-Q11 were challenged with homologous H1N1 PR8 influenza virus 12 weeks after the final boost (Figure S6A). Prior to viral challenge, serum neutralizing antibody titers were measured to assess the persistence of humoral immunity. As shown in Figure S6B, mice vaccinated with NP@HA-VLP-Q11 retained robust neutralizing antibody responses at 12 weeks post-immunization. Notably, the subcutaneously immunized group exhibited slightly higher serum neutralizing titers than the intranasally immunized group at this time point. Despite these maintained systemic antibody responses, clear differences in protective outcomes were observed following viral challenge. Compared with naïve controls, NP@HA-VLP-Q11–immunized mice displayed markedly attenuated body-weight loss and significantly improved survival, demonstrating that vaccine-induced immunity remained functionally protective at 12 weeks post-immunization (Figure S6C-D). Importantly, mice immunized via the intranasal route exhibited complete protection, characterized by minimal morbidity and 100% survival, whereas protection in the subcutaneously immunized group was substantially reduced, with a survival rate of approximately 40%. Lung viral titers and histological analysis were quantified at 7 days post-challenge. Although both NP@HA-VLP-Q11-immunized mice showed significantly reduced pulmonary viral titers compared with naïve mice, intranasally immunized mice exhibited significantly lower PR8 titers and mild pathological damage than subcutaneously immunized mice (Figure S6E-F), indicating more effective viral control following mucosal administration of NP@HA-VLP-Q11. Collectively, these results demonstrate that while subcutaneous administration of NP@HA-VLP-Q11 induces systemic neutralizing antibody responses maintained for at least 12 weeks, intranasal immunization confers superior protection against influenza challenge at this time point.

Intranasal vaccination with NP@HA-VLP-Q11 establishes lung tissue-resident memory CD8+ T cells with rapid antiviral recall capacity

Intranasal vaccination with NP@HA-VLP-Q11 establishes lung tissue-resident memory CD8+ T cells with rapid antiviral recall capacity

To evaluate the induction of TRMs, mice were immunized with NP@HA-VLP-Q11 followed by boosts at weeks 2 and 4. Twelve weeks post-2nd boost, TRMs were analyzed from mice following intravascular anti-CD45 to discriminate circulating (anti-CD45+) from tissue-resident (anti-CD45−) leukocytes (Fig. 5A). NP@HA-VLP-Q11- intranasally immunized mice exhibited a significant increase in anti-CD45−CD8+ T cell frequencies and absolute counts in the lung compared to naïve controls, confirming robust tissue residency (Fig. 5B and D, Figure S7). In contrast, the subcutaneously immunized NP@HA-VLP-Q11 group showed only a slight elevation in the frequency of anti-CD45⁻CD8⁺ T cells relative to naïve mice; however, the absolute numbers of these cells in the lung remained comparable to naïve controls and were markedly lower than those induced by intranasal immunization (Fig. 5B and D). Phenotypic analysis revealed that these cells co-expressed canonical TRM markers CD69 and CD103, with CD44hiCD69+CD103+ CD8+ TRM populations markedly elevated in intranasally immunized mice (Fig. 5E and G). In contrast, the subcutaneous group displayed minimal induction of CD69⁺ or CD103⁺ CD8⁺ T cells, indicating that parenteral delivery was insufficient to promote TRM differentiation in the lung (Fig. 5E and G). Notably, elevated frequencies and numbers of lung NP-specific CD8+ T cells were observed in vaccinated mice, and ~ 50% displayed a CD69+CD103+ TRM phenotype (Fig. 5H and L). Importantly, NP-specific CD8⁺ T cells were not detected in the lung of subcutaneously immunized mice (Fig. 5H and L), further demonstrating that mucosal delivery is required to establish antigen-specific TRM responses at the site of viral entry.

Discussion

The persistent threat posed by respiratory pathogens, particularly influenza viruses and SARS-CoV-2, highlights the need for vaccines capable of establishing robust frontline defenses at mucosal entry sites that leads to protection from infection and ultimately, control of transmission. While traditional parenteral vaccines effectively reduce disease severity, they often fail to prevent viral replication within the respiratory tract, leaving a critical gap in pandemic preparedness [33]. In this study, we demonstrated that intranasal immunization with NP@HA-VLP-Q11 simultaneously elicited mucosal and systemic immunity, and the coordinated induction of BRMs and TRMs. We showed that the HA-specific IgA+ and IgG+ BRMs persisted in the lung and expanded within 24 h of heterologous H9N2 challenge, thus these BRMs were poised to provide immediate humoral protection at the primary site of viral entry. This ability of NP@HA-VLP-Q11 to establish localized protective humoral memory is a feature of intranasal NP@HA-VLP-Q11 vaccination that is often lacking in traditional intramuscular vaccines. Additionally, intranasal NP@HA-VLP-Q11 vaccination induced circulating NP-specific CD8+ cells and antigen-specific CD8+ TRMs that were characterized by the expression of CD69 and CD103. These TRMs persisted for over 12 weeks post-immunization and were capable of rapid expansion within 24 h of heterologous H9N2 challenge. Importantly, our comparative analysis revealed that subcutaneous immunization with NP@HA-VLP-Q11 failed to generate meaningful mucosal memory responses. Although subcutaneous vaccination induced higher serum neutralizing antibody titers and detectable circulating NP-specific CD8⁺ T cells, it resulted in minimal induction of lung-resident BRMs, lacked IgA⁺ class-switched BRMs entirely, and exhibited only low levels of HA-specific IgG⁺ B cells that did not expand following heterologous challenge. Similarly, NP-specific CD8⁺ T cells and TRMs were virtually undetectable in the lung after s.c. immunization, underscoring that systemic immune responses alone are insufficient to establish durable mucosal protection in the respiratory tract. Functionally, these immunological differences translated into protection. 12 weeks post-immunization, intranasally NP@HA-VLP-Q11 mice exhibited attenuated weight loss and improved survival following H1N1 PR8 challenge, whereas subcutaneously vaccinated mice showed only limited protection. The dual induction of BRMs and TRMs provide both immediate humoral defense and sustained cellular immunity at the site of infection, offering a promising strategy for improved respiratory virus vaccination.

Respiratory pathogens often exhibit a wide range of strains and subtypes, with influenza viruses having 18 hemagglutinin (HA) and 11 neuraminidase (NA) subtypes, and SARS-CoV-2 generating rapidly evolving variants (e.g., from Wuhan to Omicron). These strain variants underscore the need for universal vaccine strategies that can overcome strain-specific limitations [34–36]. Current efforts to achieve broad-spectrum immunity focus on two complementary approaches: (1) targeting conserved viral regions to elicit cross-reactive antibodies, and (2) harnessing T cell responses against invariant epitopes. For influenza, structural stabilization of the HA stem domain has emerged as a promising approach, enabling antibodies to neutralize diverse subtypes by avoiding hypervariable head epitopes [24, 37, 38]. Building on this paradigm, our NP@HA-VLP-Q11 platform incorporates a stabilized “headless” miniHA immunogen, which induced potent cross-neutralizing antibodies in mice, as evidenced by significant inhibition of heterologous H9N2 infection in vitro. Notably, HA-specific BRMs in the lung mucosa rapidly expanded upon H9N2 challenge, demonstrating that the conserved HA stem drives cross-reactive humoral immunity capable of overcoming subtype-restricted protection. These findings align with recent advances in HA stem stabilization, which show that engineered miniHA constructs elicit broad protection in naïve animal models [24], and extend this principle to mucosal immunity by leveraging BRMs as frontline defenders.

Similarly, T cell-based vaccines that prioritize conserved internal proteins offer resilience against different strains. This strategy has gained additional urgency following recent COVID-19 studies, where early CD8+ T cell activation was associated with reduced disease severity, even in the absence of detectable neutralizing antibodies, highlighting their role in controlling viral escape [39–41]. While antibodies remain essential for sterilizing immunity, T cells provide a critical fail-safe by targeting epitopes less prone to mutation. In our prior work, CD8+ T cell epitope-bearing Q11 nanofibers elicited antigen-specific CD8+ T cell responses in vivo and reduced pulmonary viral loads [27]. Here, the NP@HA-VLP-Q11 vaccine induced robust circulating and tissue-resident NP-specific CD8+ T cell responses, characterized by IFN-γ production upon antigenic challenge. Strikingly, nearly half of the lung NP-specific CD8+ T cells in immunized mice exhibited a CD44hiCD69+CD103+ TRM phenotype, persisting for at least 12 weeks post-immunization. Upon heterologous influenza challenge, these TRM cells underwent rapid clonal expansion, demonstrating functional longevity and the capacity for broad-spectrum protection. These TRM-driven responses were markedly stronger in intranasally immunized mice than in those vaccinated subcutaneously, aligning with the observed differences in protective efficacy.

By combining SpyTag003/SpyCatcher003-mediated miniHA antigen display on virus-like particles (VLPs) with Q11 nanofibers for NP-specific CD8+ T cell priming, we were able to engage both arms of adaptive immunity. This strategy mirrors recent successes in broadly neutralizing antibody design but extends protection to include cellular immunity. The cross-protection observed against antigenically distinct strains (H1N1 PR8 and H9N2) further underscores the platform’s potential as a universal influenza vaccine candidate. Although this platform is designed to function without exogenous adjuvants, questions regarding the intrinsic immunogenicity of the nanofiber and the potential for anti-nanofiber immune responses upon repeated dosing merit consideration. Our prior studies have demonstrated that Q11 nanofibers alone do not elicit detectable antigen-specific antibody responses or overt local inflammation following mucosal administration, supporting their role primarily as a structural carrier rather than a dominant immunogen [27, 42]. Consistent with these findings, repeated intranasal administration of NP@HA-VLP-Q11 in the present study resulted in sustained antigen-specific humoral and cellular immune responses without evidence of attenuation following booster immunization, and protective efficacy against viral challenge was maintained. Together, these observations suggest that any immune recognition of the Q11 scaffold does not measurably compromise vaccine efficacy or safety under the conditions tested. Moreover, the adjuvant-free delivery that is enabled by the immunogenicity and low inflammatory properties of Q11 achieves the desired balance of efficacy and safety necessary for intranasal vaccines [27, 42]. Additionally, Q11 has the unique capability to simultaneously present multiple T cell epitopes by linking different types of epitopes to the nanofiber scaffold [32]. By Q11 co-assembly, we may be able to deliver universal MHC class II epitopes such as PADRE, which elicit CD4+ T cell help for B cell responses, to further enhance vaccine efficacy [32]. Recent studies suggest that broadening T cell help significantly improved responses across influenza strains. This “borrowing” of T cell help not only amplifies antibody responses but also broadens the spectrum of protective immunity [43]. Thus, this modular platform could be easily optimized to co-deliver a broader array of T cell epitopes thereby enhancing its efficacy and cross-reactivity against diverse respiratory pathogens.

While the design principle underlying this platform has broad applicability, several key questions remain that must be addressed to fully optimize its potential. First, the durability of mucosal TRM and BRM immunity in pulmonary tissues beyond 12 weeks requires further investigation. Second, the relative contribution of BRMs versus TRMs to heterologous protection need to be explored in greater detail. Third, systematic optimization of epitope combinations to include both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell epitopes, adjusting for epitope density and diversity to modulating the responses of CD4+ versus CD8+ T cell responses. Finally, vaccinations should be pursued in naïve as well as heterologously infected or outbred mice. Achieving the optimal balance between these immune components will be critical for broadening cross-reactivity without compromising safety.

In conclusion, NP@HA-VLP-Q11 represents a transformative approach to mucosal vaccination by combining structural vaccinology with biomaterials engineering to achieve an adjuvant-free vaccine capable of inducing broad-spectrum immunity. By engaging tissue-resident memory cells, this modular platform offers robust mucosal-systemic synergy without the inflammatory trade-offs. This approach opens up new possibilities for mucosal vaccine design not only for influenza but also for combating other respiratory pathogens.
