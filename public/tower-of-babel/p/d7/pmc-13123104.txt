Geographical variation of chemical signals and odor discrimination in the great Himalayan leaf-nosed bat (Hipposideros armiger)
Authors: Congnan Sun, Ziqi Zheng, Jeffrey R. Lucas, Aoqiang Li, Jiang Feng, Chunmian Zhang, Tinglei Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSignal divergence and sensory preferences may cause reproductive isolation and eventually speciation between animal populations. These patterns are particularly well known in systems with acoustic or visual signals. However, relatively few studies have quantified the patterns, causes and consequences of geographical variation of chemical signals, especially in mammalian populations.ResultsWe examined Hipposideros armiger (the great Himalayan leaf-nosed bat) and collected secretions from the forehead glands of males. We selected nine colonies across a large geographic area and investigated both the potential causes and patterns of variation in chemical signals from that gland between these colonies. We also examined whether or not males could tell the difference between gland scents from a foreign colony and their own colony using habituation–discrimination tests. To determine whether females preferred the scent of local versus foreign males, we performed two-choice tests. Overall, we found significant colony-level differences in the concentrations of compounds and in general compound categories. We show that symbiotic microbes inhabiting odor glands may explain the observed chemical variation between colonies. Moreover, behavioral studies indicated that males were able to discriminate between gland scents from their own colony and those from a distant colony. Finally, females were more attracted to scents of local males from their own colony compared to scents from foreign colonies.ConclusionsOur results demonstrate significant geographical variation in colony chemical signals linked to gland symbiotic microbes, with males discriminating colony-specific scents and females preferring the gland scents of local males. This study expands our limited knowledge of geographical variation of mammalian chemical signals and highlights the importance of bacterial communities in shaping chemical divergence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-026-02576-w.

Background

The study of geographical variation in traits has become an important means of revealing population differentiation, diffusion, niche differentiation, and speciation [1, 2]. Geographical variation in communicative signals has been shown to be particularly important for processes such as speciation, mate recognition and mate selection [3]. However, most studies on geographical variation in communicative signaling systems concentrate on acoustic signals[4–6]. Unfortunately, the geographic variation of chemical signals in mammals has not received as much attention.

Chemical signals are among the dominant signal traits in many mammals and play a vital role in territory defense [7], individual recognition [8], and mate attraction and choice [9]. Geographical variation in chemical signals may arise from genetic drift, ecological selection, symbiotic microbes or a combination of these factors. Genetic drift may have an impact on geographic variation in chemical signals as a result of random changes in enzyme-encoding genes [10]. Another significant driving force of differences in chemical signals is ecological selection. Local environmental factors, including humidity and temperature, should influence the efficacy of chemical signal transmission [11]. Furthermore, symbiotic microbes may play a prominent role in generating these patterns. The fermentation hypothesis for chemical recognition postulates that symbiotic microbes generate metabolites that shape the odor of their host [12]. As the composition of these microbial communities varies between individuals and populations, so too does the host’s distinctive odor. Supporting this idea, differences in the scent-gland odors of social groups of spotted hyenas (Crocuta crocuta) have been linked to variation in the structure of bacterial communities within their scent glands [13]. Similarly, in male meerkats (Suricata suricatta), the volatile compounds found in anal paste mixtures showed strong covariation with the bacterial assemblages present in these secretions [14].

Two fundamental issues in determining the consequences of geographical variation in chemical signals are whether animals can discriminate population differences in conspecific signals and whether females prefer local conspecific signals. These issues are relevant because geographic divergence in chemical signals can make it difficult for individuals from different populations within a species to recognize one another [15]. This can in turn result in reproductive isolation, assortative mating, and speciation [15]. This phenomenon has been observed in insects[16, 17] and reptiles [18, 19]. For example, geographical variation in the scent from the femoral pores of male Podarcis gaigeae (Skyros wall lizards), along with female preferences for the scent of local males, ultimately contributed to reproductive isolation and speciation [20]. However, the ability of mammals to distinguish geographical differences in chemical signals remains poorly understood. Our study was designed to address this issue with bats as our model system.

Echolocating bats are a group of highly social, nocturnal mammals that are found virtually everywhere except in Arctic or Antarctic regions. They commonly rely on acoustic signals (e.g., social and echolocation calls) for communication and orientation [21] and also employ complex chemical signals in a dark environment for social activities [22]. Therefore, echolocating bats offer an ideal model system with which to study the causes, consequences, and patterns of geographic variation in chemical signals. Nevertheless, only two investigations have addressed the question of chemical signal divergence of bat odors between populations and their underlying driving forces [23, 24]. Safi and Kerth [24] examined the interaural gland secretions of female Myotis bechsteinii (Bechstein’s bats) and determined that the olfactory signals were different across four colonies that were 5–15 km apart. Schneeberger et al. [23] studied the wing-sac chemical "fingerprints" of male Saccopteryx bilineata (greater sac-winged bats) and found that they were different across six colonies that were 100–3,300 m apart. Additionally, bats’ behavioral preference in regard to geographic variation in chemical signals has been examined by only one study. Schneeberger et al. [25] found that female S. bilineata prefer the scent of foreign males over local males. Notably, there is female-biased dispersal in this species. However, how chemical signals differ across a large geographic area remains unknown, and little is known about the roles of symbiotic microbes in driving chemical divergence. Specifically, it is unclear whether other species of bats can identify colony differences in chemical signals and whether or not female bats with male-biased dispersal prefer foreign or local male odors.

We addressed these knowledge gaps in Hipposideros armiger (the great Himalayan leaf-nosed bats; mean forearm length: 94.99 mm), which are spread out across Southeast Asia, including southern China [26]. Hipposideros armiger has a harem-polygynous mating system and is a gregarious species [27]. They have a forehead gland that is sexually dimorphic (Fig. 1A, B). During the mating season, males flap their wings toward females, accompanied by the protrusion of the forehead gland and the release of its characteristic scent (personal observations by Congnan Sun). Male H. armiger also release an odoriferous substance from a thick black secretion produced by actively protruding the forehead gland during territorial defense [28]. In this study, we characterized the patterns of geographic variation in chemical signals of H. armiger, and explored potential correlates and associated implications of this variation. We first collected secretions from the forehead gland from nine H. armiger colonies and identified geographical differences in the chemical profiles. We next investigated the factors that contributed to this variation in scent profiles assuming that any one of the following factors plays a role: (1) symbiotic microbes, (2) genetic drift, or (3) ecological selection. (1) Following the fermentation hypothesis, colony differences in chemical signals should reflect underlying differences between colonies in the composition of gland symbiotic microbial communities. (2) Because similarities in chemical traits may indicate common ancestry, genetic distance based on neutral genetic markers would have a positive correlation with the chemical divergence among colonies. (3) Because humidity and temperature can affect the transmission efficiency of chemical signals, selection pressures related to these environmental factors may drive the evolution of divergent chemical signal profiles among colonies. Therefore, variation across colonies in chemical signals could be tied to variation in humidity and temperature across colonies.

Finally, we determined whether males from one colony could distinguish between odors from bats in their own colony compared to odors from bats in a foreign colony. We also determined whether females had a preference for male odors from a foreign colony or from their own colony.

Variation in chemical signals

Scent profiles

In total, we detected 343 volatile compounds from 87 individuals across nine colonies (mean ± SE = 7.46 ± 0.18 mg/sample; range 5.20–13.80 mg; Fig. 1C; Additional file 1: Table S1 and Additional file 2: Fig. S1). After filtering, we used the 102 volatile compounds remaining in the dataset for further analysis (Additional file 3: Table S2). We classified the detected compounds into 10 categories, as follows (Additional file 4: Table S3): aromatic compounds (28.43%), aldehydes (16.67%), alkanes (14.71%), ketones (9.80%), alcohols (8.82%), esters (8.82%), carboxylic acids (5.88%), alkenes (3.92%), furans (1.96%) and a thiol (0.98%).

Factors affecting variation in chemical signals

According to the Mantel tests, we did not observe a significant correlation between pairwise and chemical distances for environmental and genetic factors across the colonies (r = −0.133–0.272, P = 0.189–0.838; Additional file 8: Table S6). Also, spatial autocorrelations were not detected in measures of chemical compounds (Principal component 1, PC1, Moran’s I = −0.055, P = 0.804; PC2, Moran’s I = −0.039, P = 0.91; PC3, Moran’s I = −0.166, P = 0.417; PC4, Moran’s I = −0.207, P = 0.261; PC5, Moran’s I = 0.024, P = 0.915; PC6, Moran’s I = −0.21, P = 0.291; PC7, Moran’s I = −0.192, P = 0.312; PC8, Moran’s I = −0.155, P = 0.533), suggesting that the obtained results were not biased by spatial autocorrelation.

Relationships between the gland microbiota and chemical composition

According to the Procrustes randomization test (PROTEST), the bat gland bacterial community had a significant correlation with the chemical composition among colonies (PROTEST: r = 0.41, P = 0.034; Fig. 4E). The Mantel test further showed that the pairwise gland microbial distance was significantly positively associated with pairwise gland chemical composition distance (Spearman’s r = 0.117, P = 0.039; Fig. 4F). These results suggested that bacterial communities could have an impact on the chemical signaling profiles of the various colonies.

Discussion

We found that the chemical signals from the secretions from the forehead gland of male H. armiger demonstrated significant geographic variation across nine colonies in southern China. We also found that chemical divergence did not have a significant association with climatic variables or genetic distance. Forehead gland microbiota were characterized from 3 colonies (HZ, SM and SG) and the microbiota differed significantly between each colony, suggesting symbiotic microbes may drive chemical divergence. In addition, females showed a preference for the scent of local males over foreign males in two-choice tests. The male H. armiger could also tell the difference between the chemical signals of its colony and those of a foreign colony. We believe this provides significant experimental evidence that wild, free-ranging bats can discriminate between geographically varying chemical signals.

Variation in chemical signals

We observed differences in the categories and concentrations of chemical compounds among different bat colonies. These results indicated that the odors of secretions from the forehead gland of H. armiger could convey information about colony identity. Other than aldehyde and Furan, the remaining eight compound categories showed greater variance and varied more within regions (9–46% of the total variation) compared with variance between regions (0–15% of the total variation). Moreover, although we observed no significant difference in the compound categories between colonies within the South Yunnan region, significant differences were detected in the compound categories between colonies within the Eastern China region and between colonies within the Western China region. The significant variation in chemical signals among the regional colonies demonstrated the existence of "odor dialects", which are similar to vocal dialects [30]. These odor dialects provide colony identity and make it easier to identify intruders from other colonies. In this study, Lin et al. [31] described a high rate of gene flow among regional colonies, which suggested that interactions between conspecifics from various colonies were frequent. As a result, the ability to precisely recognize foreign intruders could promote divergence in chemical signals among regional colonies.

Although we identified geographic variation in the chemical signals of H. armiger across the geographic range, most of this variation resulted from individual differences within the colonies (i.e., different individuals of the same colonies; 54–91% of the total variation). This high variation among individuals in categories of chemical compounds revealed that the scent of the secretions from the forehead gland of H. armiger was a significant contributor to individual recognition. This finding was supported by a previous study that also found that the olfactory signals of male H. armiger could encode individual signatures and that the males could identify differences among individuals based on these scents [28].

What drives variation in chemical signals?

We observed significant geographic variation in the chemical signals derived from the forehead gland secretions of male H. armiger across nine colonies. These differences in chemical profiles in H. armiger may be attributed to the local variation of bacterial diversity. According to the fermentation hypothesis, symbiotic microbes in odor glands produce metabolites that have an impact on the host scent profile. Thus, colony and group variations in the composition of these microbes drive colony and group variations in scent [12]. We also observed significant differences in the chemical profiles and gland bacterial community structure among the SM, SG, and HZ colonies. The Procrustes analysis and Mantel test demonstrated that bat gland microbiota and gland chemical composition were significantly correlated. These results suggest that bat gland microbiota may contribute to gland chemical cues. A similar phenomenon has been found in song sparrows Melospiza melodia where both preen oil chemistry and the community composition of preen gland microbes differ across populations [32].

The microbial community in the forehead glands of H. armiger is dominated by Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas, Thermoanaerobacterium and Bacillus. These microbes are known to be enriched with genes encoding lipid metabolic enzymes that participate in fermentation processes to produce signal molecules involved in mammalian chemical communication [33–36]. Metabolites of symbiotic bacteria, being closely linked to the health and physiological status of animals, can also serve as honest individual signals that convey reliable information about an animal’s physical condition, physiology and reproductive status [37]. Indeed, scent gland microbiota have been proposed to mediate mate choice in the greater sac-winged bat (S. bilineata) [38].

The observed colony differences in chemical signals are likely not associated with seasonal hormonal differences because our sampling period (July 10–September 1, 2022; see Additional file 1: Table S1) fell mainly during the mating season of H. armiger, and males over this time period likely had similar hormonal levels. In addition, a previous study reported differences in gland composition between harem and bachelor males in Phyllostomus hastatus, a large Neotropical harem‑polygynous bat species [39]. In our study, although we did not distinguish male social status (harem holder vs. bachelor) during field sampling, all individuals were healthy adult males during the breeding season, and individuals from the same colony shared similar roosting conditions, age structure, and reproductive status. Therefore, potential variation in male social status would not confound or mask the colony-level differences in chemical signals that were the focus of this study. Future studies should explore the effect of social status on the colony differences in chemical signals of H. armiger.

The three colonies selected for analysis of gland microbiota (HZ, SM, SG) exhibited inherent differences in chemical signals, genetic background and geographical distribution. These results are highly consistent with the research objective of exploring the correlation between the forehead gland bacterial communities of H. armiger and its chemical signals. However, we acknowledge a limitation of our study. The selected three colonies do not cover the entire geographical distribution range and habitat types of H. armiger, which may lead to the omission of colonies with unique microbial community characteristics and an incomplete understanding of the overall geographical variation in the forehead gland microbiota of H. armiger. Additional colony sampling is needed to determine the covariation between forehead gland chemical and microbial composition. Also further experiments using a broad-spectrum antibiotic in the forehead gland should be performed to confirm the causal relationship between odor gland bacterial community structure and host odor profiles.

Ecological selection frequently drives divergence in the chemical signals of animals through the effects of factors such as temperature and humidity on chemical signal transmission [40]. In Podarcis hispanicus (the Iberian wall lizard), the chemical signal characteristics varied between two populations that inhabited environments with different humidity and temperatures [41]. In our study, we did not observe any humidity and temperature effects on chemical profiles, which suggested that ecological selection may play a small role in chemical divergence. Similarly, we found no any evidence of a relationship between bat genetic profiles and chemical distance, which indicated that differences in chemical signals in H. armiger were not caused by genetic drift. This result further supports the conclusion that the chemical signals have a microbial origin. Colony-level differences in chemical signals that lacked any genetic correlations were pivotal in shaping geographic differences in the odor profiles from the anal glands of Lutra lutra (Eurasian otters [42]) and the odor profiles from interaural glands of Bechstein’s bats Myotis bechsteinii [24]. However, several other ecological and physiological factors that are likely to influence chemical composition, such as diet, roost microclimate, and hormone profiles, should be explored in future studies.

Discrimination of chemical signals

The ability to recognize and discriminate colony members from non-colony members is particularly crucial for social species as it permits individuals within the same colony to safeguard resources such as food, mates, or roosting sites against invaders while maintaining social cohesion within the colony [43]. Although there is evidence for chemical signatures of colony membership in many mammals [24, 42, 44], we know of no experimental investigations that examine whether mammals can use odor to discriminate colony members from non-colony members. We observed that male H. armiger could tell the difference between colony-specific and foreign forehead-gland scents. The ability of bats to use chemical signals to discriminate between local and foreign colony members could offer several advantages. Because the male forehead-gland scent of H. armiger encodes information about fighting ability, it is essential in conflict resolution [28, 29]. In addition, this colony-specific recognition along with individual recognition limits energy expenditure because it prevents agonistic interactions from escalating during physical confrontations. Hipposideros armiger has a complex social system in which single males defend a harem of females; therefore, agonistic interactions among males are frequent [27]. Because individuals with nonlocal signals may pose a greater relative threat to resources (e.g., mates), colony-mate males may cooperate to exclude foreign males from settling (e.g., greater sac-winged bats [45]). Male H. armiger may participate in the cooperative defense of their colony to prevent nonresident males from mating with the females in the colony. Taken together, our behavioral tests preliminarily suggest that chemical signatures of colony membership are not just an epiphenomenon but instead have a social function.

Preference for local chemical signals

We showed that female H. armiger selected the scent of local males over the scent of geographically distant males. This result contradicts a recent study that showed that female S. bilineata (greater sac-winged bats) had a preference for the scent of geographically distant males over the scent of local males [25]. Because S. bilineata has female-biased dispersal, these females may use the odor of the males to determine geographic location and thus prevent inbreeding [25]. In contrast, H. armiger exhibits a male-biased dispersal pattern [46], suggesting that females are at less risk of inbreeding. Females’ preference for the scent of local males over the scent of foreign males is supported by the fact that females preferentially select mates who produce local signals, which have adapted to conditions in the local environment [47, 48]. Indeed, females selecting local males may be ensuring that their offspring inherit genetic traits suited to their local habitat (e.g., resistance to local diseases, better adaptation to climate, etc.). Similar findings can be found in Vlei rat Otomys irroratus [47], striped mouse Rhabdomys pumilio [48, 49] and the house mouse Mus musculus [50]. Moreover, we found that in HZ vs. SM (20 trials) and HZ vs. SG (20 trials), 4 (20%) and 6 (30%) trials resulted in no choice, respectively. One possible interpretation is that this may relate to female reproductive state (some individuals were non-estrous, reducing motivational drive for odor choice) or odor detection thresholds (volatile concentrations of foreign odors did not reach individual response thresholds).

Conclusions

In this study, we showed that, across nine colonies, forehead gland scents in the forehead of male H. armiger exhibit notable geographic variation. The differences in scents among these colonies were masked by the significant variation in scents among the individuals within these same colonies. The symbiotic microbes inhabiting the forehead gland may be the potential force driving the chemical variation between colonies. We also found that male H. armiger could tell the difference among scents from their colony and scents from an allopatric colony, and females demonstrated a preference for local scents over foreign scents. Future work will improve the present study by conducting reciprocal odor experiments on both males and females from multiple geographically distinct colonies, which may generalize the discrimination ability across the species.

Methods

Odor experiment: variation in chemical signals

Field sampling

In the July–September 2022 mating season, 87 adult male H. armiger were caught from nine colonies in southern China with inter-colony distances between around 98 and 1980 km (mean 924 km). All sampled males exhibited enlarged scrotal testes. As shown in Fig. 1C and Additional file 1: Table S1, the colonies sampled were: Anlong (AL), Beichuan (BC), Chongyi (CY), Fanchang (FC), Hanzhong (HZ), Hekou (HK), Jiangkou (JK), Simao (SM), and Shaoguan (SG). We assigned a name to each colony based on its geographical region. The colonies we examined are the same as the colonies reported by Lin et al. [31]. Our sample colonies belonged to the same three genetic lineages that were previously determined by Lin et al. [31], i.e., Eastern China (EC), Western China (WC), and South Yunnan (SY) region.

Forehead gland secretion collection

Adult males from each colony were captured after sunset at the cave entrance using mist nets. A black gland substance is released from the forehead gland of the male. We gently squeezed the area around this gland to extract the black gland secretion. We used presterilized forceps to move the sample into a 20-mL headspace glass vial with a polytetrafluoroethylene-lined septum. We immediately stored the samples on dry ice and returned them to the laboratory where they were stored at − 80 °C until needed for analysis. To test the effect of potential contaminants, we collected one blank sample by waving the vial three times in air to limit the effect of potential contaminants. Since olfactory signals of male H. armiger from forehead gland secretions encode strong individual signatures [28], only one sample was obtained from each individual. Ten adult males were sampled for each colony, excluding the CY colony, from which seven males were collected.

Chemical compounds analysis

On the basis of a study by Zhang et al. [28], we conducted an analysis using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) to identify compounds. Prior to analysis, 10 µL of 2-octanol (internal standard) was added to each sample. Samples were heated at 60 °C for 15 min, then extracted via headspace solid-phase microextraction (SPME) (50/30 µm Divinylbenzene/Carboxen/Polydimethylsiloxane fiber) for 30 min; volatiles were desorbed and injected into a 250 °C injector. GC–MS (Agilent 7890/5977) used a Durabond-Wax column (30 m × 250 µm × 0.25 µm; Agilent) and helium (1 mL/min). A 1-µL sample was injected in 1:1 split mode (septum purge: 3 ml/min). Column temperature was held at 40 °C for 4 min, then increased to 245 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min and held for 5 min. Temperatures of the ion source, injector, and transfer line were 230 °C, 250 °C, and 260 °C, respectively. Mass spectrometry was run in full-scan mode from m/z 20 to 500 with no solvent delay. Data preprocessing used Chroma Time of Flight 4.3X and National Institute of Standards and Technology database (NIST) [51]. Compounds were excluded if: < 80% NIST similarity, relative peak area < 0.01%, present as blanks (contaminants), or extremely rare in natural products. See supplementary materials for a detailed analysis of the process used.

Geographical distance measurement

We used a Global Positioning System (eTrex Vista, Beijing, China) to record the latitude and longitude of each sampling site. For each of the nine sites, we calculated the pairwise geographic distance matrix between all sampling sites using the VincentyEllipsoid formula (to compute distances between latitude/longitude coordinates) via R package ‘geosphere’.

Genetic data collection and analysis

For the nine colonies, we used genetic data from Lin et al. [31], including the complete cytochrome b (1140 bp) sequences, a partial mitochondrial control region (358 bp), and seven nuclear microsatellite loci (nuclear Simple Sequence Repeat (nSSR): BAM09, TE2, CHANG242, P6D12, P541, PT5B2, and PE4). We used pairwise FST/(1-FST) separately for concatenated mitochondrial deoxyribonucleic acid (mtDNA) sequences and nSSR in Arlequin version 3.5 [52] to calculate genetic distance among colonies (Additional file 12: Table S7).

Climatic data collection

We obtained the mean annual temperature (MAT) and mean annual relative humidity (MARH) from the China Meteorological Data Service Center (CMDSC, https://data.cma.cn/). We used annual average grid data (1980–2022) covering the sampling region, with a spatial resolution of 0.01° × 0.01°. For each sampling site, we assigned the grid data corresponding to the grid cell that contained the site’s geographic coordinates to ensure spatial relevance.

Bacterial DNA extraction and sequencing

We selected the HZ, SM and SG colonies to investigate whether there were significant differences in gland bacterial communities between colonies. The choice of these colonies was based on three main criteria. First, we observed notable variations in the compound categories and chemical composition among the three colonies (Additional file 12: Fig. S5). Second, we found significant genetic differences among the SM, SG, and HZ colonies from Southern Yunnan, Eastern China, and Western China, respectively [31]. Third, these three colonies are geographically far apart with distances between around 1100 and 1330 km.

After collecting forehead gland secretions, we collected gland microbiota samples from the same adult males as described in the Methods section ‘Forehead gland secretion collection’ (8 males per colony) by inserting a sterile polyester swab into the forehead gland pocket and rotating the swab five times. The swab tip was then stored in individually labeled sterile tubes containing 500 μL of preservation solution (Yuanxin, Shanghai, China). We immediately placed the samples on dry ice and then returned them to the laboratory where they were stored at − 80 °C until needed for analysis. Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted from swab samples using the MagPure Soil DNA KF Kit and validated by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, after which the V3-V4 region of the 16S ribosomal ribonucleic acid (rRNA) gene was amplified via triplicate polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with primers 338 F and 806R [53]. These primers may underrepresent certain groups such as some Crenarchaeota, while providing excellent coverage for the dominant bacterial taxa typically found in animal-associated microbiomes [54]. The 338F/806R primer set has been successfully used to characterize bacterial assemblages in bat odor glands [55]. The resulting amplicons were gel-purified, and Illumina-compatible sequencing libraries were constructed and quality-assessed prior to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 PE250 platform. Raw reads from the HZ, SG and SM colonies were preprocessed by trimming adapters, assembling contigs and filtering for high quality, then clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% sequence similarity with the UPARSE method (excluding singletons and chimeras). OTUs were taxonomically annotated against the Greengenes database, and phylogenetic trees were generated. Low-abundance, mitochondrial and chloroplastic OTUs were removed for data reliability, and the OTU table was rarefied to 40,087 reads per sample to standardize sampling depth across all samples (Additional file 13: Fig. S6 and Additional file 14: Table S8). See supplementary materials for detailed description of bacterial DNA extraction, processing and sequencing procedures.

Behavioral experiment: colony odor discrimination

We selected the SM, SG, and HZ colonies to examine whether male H. armiger from the HZ colony could tell the difference between odors from their own colony and odors from the SM and SG colonies. Notably, each of these three selected colonies was representative of a distinct genetic lineage, with one colony selected per lineage. Because the odor secreted from the forehead gland of male H. armiger is essential in territorial conflict resolution, which is tied to reproductive success, we tested only male discrimination [28]. We used a scentless plastic box to conduct the habituation–discrimination tests (Fig. 5A, B). Bats underwent a 3-day habituation phase with daily exposure to a colony-specific odor swab ("habituation odor") and an odourless control swab. Bats were deemed habituated to the odor when they spent significantly longer detecting the odorous swab than the odorless one in the first trial, with detection duration declining significantly in subsequent trials. On the 4th day, we conducted the discrimination phase: bats were exposed to both the habituation odor and a novel odor from another colony. We concluded that discrimination occurred if bats spent significantly more time near the novel odor. Each male bat was tested for HZ vs. SM and HZ vs. SG colony odors with random sample placement. Odor detection was defined as a bat keeping its head ≤ 1 cm from a swab for ≥ 1 s, with detection duration referring to total interaction time with the odor. The sample information of forehead gland secretion from each individual can be found in Additional file 15: Table S9. The information of "habituation odor" and "novel odor" can be found in Additional file 16: Table S10 and Additional file 17: Table S11. See supplementary materials for a detailed experimental process.

Behavioral experiment: female choice of colony odor

We selected the SM, SG, and HZ colonies to test whether female H. armiger from the HZ colony had a preference for the male odors from their own colony or for odors from the SM and SG colonies. Only female preference was tested here because mate choice decisions are largely made by female mammals [56], including bats [57, 58]. Two-choice tests were performed in a scentless plastic bucket (Fig. 6A; Additional file 18: Video S1). A tested female bat was placed at the center of a neutral zone and allowed a minimum 5-min acclimation until she remained stationary. Before the experiment, vials with male gland secretions were thawed on ice for 10–20 min. Forehead gland secretions from three random individuals of the same colony were mixed to prepare local and foreign odor samples, which were then attached to two separate swabs respectively. Once the bat remained motionless, the two swabs were fixed with foam and slowly moved at a constant speed above air fans simultaneously, with each trial lasting 10 min. We recorded the time each female bat spent in the two response zones of the bucket during the trial. The sample information of forehead gland secretion from each individual can be found in Additional file 15: Table S9. The information of "local odor" and "foreign odor" can be found in Additional file 19: Table S12 and Additional file 20: Table S13. See supplementary materials for a detailed experimental process.

The behavioral tests were restricted to individuals from the HZ colony primarily due to the substantial sample size required for the odor experiments. Specifically, scent secretions were collected from 41 males in the HZ colony, with an additional 20 males used for the odor playback assays and 40 females for two-choice tests—critically, individuals involved in scent collection and behavioral testing were non-overlapping to avoid confounding effects. Only the HZ colony could meet these rigorous sample demands: it is a large colony of approximately 580 individuals, is highly accessible, and easy to capture. This allowed us to efficiently acquire the sufficient sample size needed and complete the experiments within the study’s time constraints.

The individuals used in behavioral experiments were captured during the mating season [27], and the individuals originated from the three colonies mentioned (i.e., SM, SG, and HZ colonies). To minimize the effects of individual variation in age, body size, reproductive condition, and health status on the experimental results, we strictly selected experimental individuals according to the following criteria: 1) All individuals were adult with sealed epiphyseal gaps, brown fur, and similar degrees of tooth wear in the upper canines and premolars; 2) All individuals were in breeding condition: males had enlarged testes, and females possessed well-developed false nipples; 3) There was minor variation in forearm length among individuals (males: 2.15 ± 1.56 mm; females: 2.38 ± 1.65 mm); 4) All individuals were in good health.

Behavioral analysis

To assess the video recordings, we used IQiYi Player version 5.6.0.5495 (Beijing IQiYi Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). To reduce observer bias for the habituation–discrimination and two-choice tests, we followed an experimental blinded method (the blinded observer was Ziqi Zheng). After we completed the behavioral tests, we released all of the tested bats in good health at their original site of capture.

Statistical analysis

We used the peak area of each compound divided by the peak area of the internal standard to calculate the relative GC–MS peak areas. On the basis of the relative peak area between each pair of samples, we calculated the Bray–Curtis similarity index. To perform a NMDS ordination, we used the similarity matrix. To ensure that the relative distance between samples matched their chemical similarities, we placed the NMDS plot for each sample in a two-dimensional space. To measure goodness of fit, we used stress and then evaluated the ability of a specific configuration to reproduce the observed distance matrix relative to the data. We adopted the following standards for stress results in a two-dimensional space were adopted: stress > 0.2 was a poor model, 0.1 < stress < 0.2 was a good model, 0.05 < stress < 0.1 was a very good model, and stress < 0.05 was an excellent model [59].

We performed a nonparametric analysis of similarity (ANOSIM) with 10,000 permutations according to the Bray–Curtis similarity distance matrix, using a colony as the fixed factor, to identify any differences in the chemical compounds of gland secretions across the nine colonies. The ANOSIM tests are random Mantel-type permutations that do not make data distribution assumptions [60]. We defined global R as the difference in mean rank dissimilarity within versus between groups. When global R was close to 1, samples from the same colony showed greater similarity among themselves, and more pronounced differences from samples of other colonies. We calculated the Bray–Curtis similarity index according to the mean relative peak area of each category of compounds with 10,000 permutations in the ANOSIM test procedure to identify differences in the compound categories of gland secretions among the nine colonies. To assess variations among individuals within a colony, between colonies within a region, and between the three regions, we used univariate nested ANOVAs on the basis of the proportion of each of the ten compound categories. The proportional data of the ten compound categories were normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk tests: all P > 0.05). We used ANOVA to estimate the percentage of total variance in each compound category attributed to individual, colony, and regional scales. Because alcohols showed one of the strongest and most significant levels of differentiation among colonies within regions, we performed a univariate one-way ANOVA on their mean relative peak areas, followed by Tukey’s HSD test, to test if their relative amounts differed across the nine colonies. The mean relative peak areas of alcohols from each colony were normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk: All P > 0.05).

We performed the above-mentioned ANOSIM tests to determine whether the compound categories of gland secretions differed among colonies within the South Yunnan region, Eastern China region and Western China region. We also performed ANOSIM tests to determine whether chemical compounds and categories of compounds of gland secretions differed between the HZ, SM and SG colonies.

To determine whether there was a spatial autocorrelation of chemical compounds, we first performed a principal component (PC) analysis on the colony mean values of the olfactory parameters. We extracted eight PCs (with eigenvalues > 1) that explained 100% of the total variance (Additional file 21: Table S14). Subsequently, we measured the spatial autocorrelation of each PC factor score with Moran’s I using a Monte Carlo permutation test (199 permutations) to evaluate statistical significance in SAM 4.0 [61].

We used the colony mean values of relative peak area to calculate a dissimilarity matrix of chemical Euclidean distances between colonies to assess whether variations in chemical compounds were related to climate distance and genetic distance among the nine colonies. We calculated a genetic distance matrix according to mtDNA or nSSR distances. Climate distance matrices were calculated based on the MAT and MARH distances. We then tested the correlations between chemical divergence and two independent variables (genetic distance and climatic distance) using nonparametric Mantel tests in PASSaGE version 2 [62].

We used the function "diversity" in the R package "vegan" [63] and calculated the Shannon diversity as a measure of alpha diversity to identify variations in gland bacterial communities among colonies. Because the Shannon diversity data were normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk tests: P > 0.05), we performed a Tukey multiple-comparison test and a univariate one-way ANOVA to assess the difference in Shannon diversity among the colonies. We next calculated beta diversity using the Bray–Curtis distance matrices with the function "metaMDS" in the R package "vegan", and we visualized results in k = 2 dimensions with NMDS. To determine the significance of beta diversity among colonies, we used the function "adonis" in the R package "vegan" and then conducted a permutational multivariate ANOVA [64]. Because the relative abundance data were not normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk tests: P < 0.05), we used nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis tests to compare variations in the relative abundances of phylum levels and genus levels among the colonies. We used Bonferroni correction to correct the P-values for multiple comparisons [65].

We conducted Procrustes analysis with the "procrustes" function of the "vegan" package in R to determine the relationship between bat gland microbiota and gland chemical composition, and we assessed the impact of the gland microbiota on chemical composition. We conducted NMDS on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrices for the gland chemical composition and microbiota, and we used 10,000 permutations to test significance. We assessed the correlation between gland chemical composition distance and gland microbial community distance using a Mantel test with Spearman’s rank correlation in the R package "vegan", based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and 10,000 permutations.

We used Shapiro–Wilk tests to test the normality of duration of detection to assess whether males responded differently to forehead gland odors from different colonies. Other than the duration of detection of the novel odor, we found the distribution of variables to be normal (P > 0.05). For the habituation phase, we tested differences in the detection duration when the tested bats were offered the habituation odor repeatedly over 3 days using one-way repeated-measures ANOVA. For the detection duration for the HZ versus SM trials (χ2 = 13.44, P = 0.001), Mauchly’s tests indicated that sphericity was not violated. However, the sphericity assumption was violated for the HZ versus SG trials (χ2 = 4.162, P = 0.125). Therefore, we used a Greenhouse–Geisser correction to adjust the degrees of freedom for HZ versus SM colonies (ɛ = 0.769 for the duration of detection). We also used Bonferroni post hoc tests to determine variations in the detection duration across days. We used paired-sample t-tests to test differences in the detection duration in all habituation trials between the fragrant and odorless swabs. For the discrimination phase, we used Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to compare variations in the detection durations between the novel and habituation odors.

We next tested whether females preferred foreign or local male scent. Because the time spent with the SM, SG, and HZ colony odors was not distributed normally (Shapiro–Wilk tests: P < 0.001), we used Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to compare variations in time spent close to the foreign and local male scent zones.

We also provide in the Supplemental Materials a more in-depth analysis of female responses to male scents using heatmaps to visually display the distribution of the time bats spent in different areas of the bucket (see Additional file 9: Fig. S3 and Additional file 10: Fig. S4). Warmer colors (such as red and yellow) signify a longer time spent, while cooler colors (such as blue) indicate a shorter time.

Unless stated otherwise, we conducted all statistical analyses in SPSS v. 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R v. 4.1.2 [66]. We set the level of statistical significance at P < 0.05. Because of multiple tests, we used a Bonferroni correction for P-values [67].
