Peripheral blood immune cell subsets as non-invasive biomarkers of colorectal cancer stage, laterality, metastasis and survival
Authors: Julia del Prado-Montero, Ramón Cantero-Cid, Jenny Guevara-Martínez, Roberto Lozano-Rodríguez, Francisco J. Cueto, Gonzalo Sáenz de Santa María-Diez, Verónica Terrón-Arcos, Rebeca Abad-Moret, Esteban Díaz-Serrano, Laura Córdoba-García, Jesús Fernández-Felipe, Karla Montalbán-Hernández, Carlos del Fresno, Laura Hurtado-Navarro, Eduardo López-Collazo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeColorectal cancer (CRC) is a heterogeneous disease in which immune dynamics critically influence tumour progression and patient outcomes; however, the relationship between circulating immune cell subsets, immune checkpoints (ICs) expression, and clinical parameters remains poorly defined. MethodsIn this study, we employed a high-dimensional spectral flow cytometry approach to analyse peripheral whole blood samples from 16 CRC patients, profiling 918 immune populations across both innate and adaptive compartments. Immune profiles were stratified by tumour stage (I vs. II–III), anatomical location (colon vs. rectum; right- vs. left-sided), and three-year metastasis and survival outcomes. Data analysis included unsupervised clustering, multiple t-tests, volcano plots, ROC curve analysis and logistic regression models.ResultsPreliminary patterns suggested associations between specific immune populations and clinical features: locally advanced-stage disease (stages II and III) tended to show higher frequencies of CD134+CD4+ conventional T cells and CCR6- CD161+CD57-CD8+natural killer T cells; rectal tumours were appeared enriched in regulatory T cells and basophils, and right- and left-sided colon cancers exhibited divergent CD4+CD8+ T cell distributions. Metastatic patients displayed an abundance of CD152+ CD14+ monocytes. Mortality correlated with the presence of CD141- CD1c+ myeloid dendritic cells and CD4-CD8- Conventional T cells.ConclusionAll immune signatures demonstrated perfect classification, with an area under the curve (AUC) value of 1.000. Despite the limited size of the cohort, these findings provide preliminary evidence for the potential of peripheral immune profiling as a non-invasive approach for prognostication and patient stratification in CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13402-026-01174-w.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) constitutes a major global health burden, ranking as the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. In 2020, CRC was responsible for an estimated 9.4% of all cancer deaths equating to approximately 900.000 cases [2, 3]. Alarmingly, its global incidence is projected to double by 2040, underscoring the urgent need for improved strategies in early detection and the identification of robust prognostic biomarkers [4, 5].

Despite progress in screening programmes, particularly in high-income countries, approximately 20% of CRC patients are diagnosed at stage IV, and an additional 25% of those initially diagnosed with early-stage disease will eventually develop metastasis. Prognosis remains closely linked to disease stage, with five-year survival rates declining from 90% in localised tumours to just a 10% in metastatic cases [6–8].

It is increasingly recognised that CRC is not a uniform disease but rather a heterogeneous group of tumours, whose biological behaviour and pathogenesis differ markedly depending on anatomical location. Significant molecular, histological, and clinical distinctions have been described between right-sided (proximal) and left-sided (distal) colon cancers, as well as between colonic and rectal tumours [9, 10]. These differences, rooted in variations in embryological origin, anatomy, function, mutational burden and treatment response, justify the need for distinct clinical management strategies [11, 12]. Traditionally, CRC classification has relied on clinicopathological features such as TNM staging and histology [13]. However, these parameters often fail to accurately predict patient outcomes or responses to therapy. As such, molecular markers, including microsatellite instability and BRAF/KRAS mutational status, are increasingly used to improve prognostic precision [14, 15].

The immune system’s role in cancer development and progression has garnered increasing attention given its dual capacity to suppress or promote tumour progression, influencing processes ranging from tumorigenesis to metastasis [16]. In CRC, tumour-associated immune infiltration within the tumour microenvironment (TME) has been strongly linked to both disease progression and clinical prognosis [17–19]. This has stimulated renewed interest not only as a prognostic tool but also as a basis for therapeutic intervention [20]. Notably, the peripheral immune system has emerged as a valuable window into systemic immunological alterations in cancer patients, enabling the detection of dysregulated innate and adaptive immune subsets [21, 22]. In parallel, the study of circulating immune checkpoints (ICs) is gaining interest as a promising approach for early diagnosis, prognostication, and the prediction of response to immunotherapy in several cancer types, including CRC [23, 24]. Yet, comprehensive characterisation of circulating immune phenotypes and ICs expression patterns in CRC patients remains limited.

In the present study, we applied two high-dimensional spectral cytometry panels to analyse the frequency, phenotype and functional status of circulating innate and adaptive immune cells in peripheral whole blood samples from CRC patients. The first panel enabled an in-depth characterisation of immune subpopulations, while the second focused on the expression of 21 markers, including ICs and Immunoglobulin (Ig)-like V-type receptors, within predominant immune populations. Collectively, our findings improve the understanding of CRC heterogeneity and disease progression and underscore the value of peripheral immunophenotyping as a non-invasive tool for stratifying patients and guiding clinical decision-making.

Material and methods

Human clinical samples and eligibility criteria

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from 16 patients diagnosed with colorectal cancer (CRC) prior to surgery at the Colorectal Surgery Department of La Paz University Hospital (Madrid, Spain), following written informed consent in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines (HULP-PI-1958). All participants had a confirmed CRC diagnosis prior to enrolment and were evaluated in the outpatient clinic prior to inclusion. Each case was subsequently reviewed by the hospital’s Colorectal Tumour Committee. Clinical data were used to stratify patients into distinct groups for comparative analysis based on tumour stage (early: stage I; locally advanced stage: stage II-III), anatomical site (colon vs. rectum), tumour laterality (right- vs. left-sided), the presence or absence of distant metastases within a three-year follow-up period (metastatic vs. non-metastatic) and three-year survival status (survivors vs. non-survivors). Details in Supplementary Table 1.

Sample processing and full-spectrum flow cytometry

Whole blood samples were collected in EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid anticoagulated) tubes (Vacuette®, Greiner Bio One, Kremsmünster, Austria). Red blood cell lysis was performed using BD PharmLyse™ Lysing Buffer (BD Biosciences), yielding a leukocyte cell suspension, which was washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Cell viability was assessed using LIVE/DEAD™ Fixable Blue dye (Invitrogen). Subsequently, cells were incubated with True-Stain Monocyte Blocker solution (BioLegend) and then split into two aliquots for staining with two distinct antibody panels. Cells were stained for 25 minutes at room temperature (RT) in the dark with fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies. The staining panels, based on standardized spectral flow cytometry panel previously established in our laboratory [25, 26], were designed to characterise immune cell populations and ICs expression, respectively (detailed in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Data acquisition was carried out using an Aurora full-spectrum flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences), which was calibrated daily using SpectroFlo® cytometer QC beads (Cytek Biosciences).

Data analysis, statistics and bioinformatics processing

Flow cytometry data were analysed using FlowJo v10.6.2 (TreeStar). Using a predefined analysis template, we generated descriptors that included the percentage of cells within parental gates, the proportion of CD45+ cells and, for specific subsets, the percentages of major cell populations. For data UMAP visualisation and hierarchical clustering, we used the software Graphext (Madrid, Spain), which applies Euclidean distance to quantify the similarity between data points in multidimensional space. The parameters were set as follows: n_neighbours = 30, defining the number of nearest neighbours considered for each point; n_components = 10, specifying the number of dimensions used for the embedding; and min_dist = 0.01, which controls the minimum distance between points in the low-dimensional representation, thereby influencing cluster compactness. For radar plot visualisation we used the software RAWGraphs (Milan, Italy) [27].

Statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc.). Normal distribution of data samples was determined with the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between groups within each clinical condition, multiple unpaired t-tests were applied, using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test when data did not follow a Gaussian distribution. This test was also applied to assess individual differences in subsets highlighted by volcano plots, where cell populations showing statistical significance are delineated with dashed lines based on log10 (p-value) on the Y-axis, while the magnitude of change is represented as log2 (fold-change) on the X-axis. The p values are indicated with asterisks, note that p > 0.05 was considered not significant (ns).

Multiple unpaired t-tests were performed as an exploratory approach to identify and prioritise immune cell populations exhibiting the most relevant differences between clinical groups. The p-values obtained from these analyses were visualised in volcano plots, including all 918 immune cell populations, thereby facilitating the most significant variables for subsequent modelling. No formal correction for multiple testing (e.g., FDR or Benjamini-Hochberg) was applied, as the analysis in nature and intended to identify candidate populations for further evaluation.

Binary logistic regression analyses were performed using the backward stepwise Wald method in IBM SPSS Statistics v0.23 (Armonk, NY). The outcomes variables were explicitly defined as three-year survival status (non-survivors = 0, survivors = 1), presence of metastasis within a three-year follow-up period (non-metastatic = 0, metastatic = 1), anatomical site (colon = 0, rectum = 1), tumour laterality (right = 0, left = 1) and tumour stage (early stage = 0, locally advanced stage = 1). The analysis included the last four immune cell populations with the highest area under the curve (AUC) values in the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. These numbers were chosen to match the maximum number of patients in the smallest comparison group, ensuring robustness and avoiding overfitting in subsequent modelling steps. This method begins with all candidate predictors and progressively eliminates or reintroduces variables based on their statistical relevance. For each variable retained in the final model, a regression coefficient (B) was calculated, indicating its contribution to group separation. Final scores were obtained using the formula: Score = ([Parameter A] x B-factor A) + ([Parameter B] x B-factor B) + … + ([Parameter X] x B-factor X), as previously described [28, 29]. These scores were then used to generate ROC curves, from which the area under the curve (AUC) and optimal thresholds were determined using the Youden index. The selection of the optimal regression model aimed to include as few variables as possible, retaining only those with the strongest statistical significance based on multiple t-tests, volcano plot analysis, and ROC curve analysis.

In view of the limited cohort size (n = 16), all logistic regression analyses were performed as exploratory, hypothesis-generating evaluations. To further mitigate the possibility of overfitting and to assess the stability of the models, a non-parametric bootstrap procedure with 10,000 iterations was applied consistently across all logistic regression analyses previously described. The bootstrap resampling was conducted using the boot package in R software.

Results

Patients’ characteristics and statistical workflow

Between 17 January 2022 and 14 March 2022, a total of 16 CRC patients were recruited at the Colorectal Surgery Department of La Paz University Hospital (Madrid, Spain). Patients were classified according to key clinical parameters, including TNM staging, anatomical site, tumour laterality, the presence or absence of metastasis at three-year follow-up and survival status. All these clinical and demographical characteristics of the study cohort are available in Supplementary Table 1.

Following sample processing, a unified analytical workflow was applied across all clinical subgroups (Fig. 1). Spectral flow cytometry enabled high-dimensional profiling of the peripheral immune landscape, including blood cell composition and immunophenotyping, identifying 918 immune cell subsets (Fig. 1A). Unsupervised clustering (Fig. 1B) and multiple unpaired t-tests were performed to detect significant intergroup differences (Fig. 1C), with results visualised via a volcano plot to prioritise relevant populations. Specifically, immune populations were considered significant if they met both statistical and biological relevance thresholds. Statistical significance was defined as –log₁₀ (p-value) greater than 1.30 (equivalent to p < 0.05) on the Y-axis, while biological relevance was assessed based on log₂ (fold change) on the X-axis within the range of − 6 to 6. The latter threshold was applied with the objective of excluding extreme or potentially outlying frequency differences, thereby ensuring the retention of immune subsets exhibiting biologically interpretable changes in expression frequencies (corresponding to a 64-fold decrease to a 64-fold increase).The four subsets with the highest discriminatory capacity (meaning those able to discriminate patients according to clinical variables), determined by ROC AUC values, were selected for binary logistic regression, generating an immune score (Fig. 1D) whose predictive performance was further evaluated through ROC analysis (Fig. 1E). To confirm the robustness of this score, clustering was repeated using only the immune score, revealing strong concordance with the original full dataset (Fig. 1F). Consequently, the single patient with stage IV CRC was excluded from this stage-based comparative analysis to avoid introducing bias or disproportionate heterogeneity due to the absence of comparable cases. Subsequent multiple t-tests analysis revealed statistically significant differences in several immune cell subsets, including monocytes, Natural Killer T (NKT) cells, conventional T cells and Natural Killer (NK) cells (Supplementary Table 4). To refine the selection of most relevant populations, a volcano plot was generated based on p-values and fold-change values, highlighting subsets with both statistical significance and biologically meaningful differences (Fig. 2B). From these, the four immune populations with the highest discriminative performance, according to ROC analysis, were selected for further evaluation (Fig. 2C). These include CD161-CD38+ (% on CD45RA- CCR7+CD4-CD8- conventional T cells), PD-1+CD38- (% on CD56lowCD16low NK cells), CD134+ (% on CD4+ conventional T cells) and CCR6-CD161+ (% on CD57-CD8+ NKT cells). The first two populations were found to be increased in early-stage disease, whereas the latter two showed higher frequencies in locally advanced-stage (Fig. 2D).

To explore whether an immune signature could discriminate between early- and locally advanced-stage disease, the four selected populations were incorporated into a binary logistic regression model using backward stepwise selection. This exploratory analysis identified CD161-CD38+ (% on CD45RA-CCR7+CD4-CD8- conventional T cells) and CCR6- CD161+(% on CD57-CD8+ NKT cells) as the most promising stage-specific discriminators. ROC analysis yielded an optimal threshold of −4.48, determined by the Youden index, achieving 100% sensitivity and specificity (Supplementary Table 5). Notably, application of this threshold separated early- from locally advanced-stage patients, with higher frequencies observed in the latter (Fig. 2E).

In the same line, unsupervised clustering based on the immune signature score alone was able to stratify patients according to clinical stage, further supporting the discriminative power of the model (Fig. 2F). Additionally, the other immune subsets identified through the volcano plot were evaluated individually. Among them, only CD57+ CD95- (% on CD45RA+CCR7-CD4-CD8- conventional T cells) and CD16+ (% on NKT cells) showed an increased frequency in locally advanced-stage patients, while the three remaining variables shared the increased expression in early-stage patients (Supplementary Figure 1).

We acknowledge that using a small cohort size may limit statistical power and increase the risk of overfitting, particularly in complex models such as those applied in this study. To mitigate this issue, we employed bootstrap resampling with 10,000 iterations to evaluate the stability of our binary logistic regression model, which demonstrated robust performance (AUC = 1).

Impact of tumour anatomical site and colon laterality on the immune profile of colorectal cancer

Given the well-established clinical heterogeneity among CRC anatomical subtypes, particularly between colon and rectal cancers, as well as between right- and left-sided colon cancers, we sought to determine whether such differences are reflected in the circulating immune cell composition and ICs expression. To this end, CRC patients were stratified by tumour site (colon, n = 12; rectum, n = 4) and among those with colon cancer, further subdivided by tumour laterality (right-sided, n = 8; left-sided, n = 4).

Consistent with findings from the stage-based analysis, unsupervised clustering of the full immune dataset revealed one cluster associated with tumour laterality and two clusters associated with tumour site; however, these were not consistent with the clinical data across patients (Figs. 3A-B). Group-wise comparisons using multiple t-tests identified several differentially represented immune cell subsets across anatomical locations, particularly among T cells, NKT cells, NK cells, innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), basophils, dendritic cells (DCs), and B cells (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). Discussion

The role of the immune system in colorectal cancer (CRC) remains incompletely understood, posing a major challenge to the effective integration of immunotherapy in clinical management. While tumour-infiltrating immune populations have been extensively characterised, far less attention has been given to immune alterations in the peripheral blood. To address this gap, our study provides a comprehensive characterisation of circulating immune cell subsets and their membrane immune checkpoints (ICs) expression in CRC patients, exploring their clinical significance and prognostic value.

Our analysis comparing early- versus locally advanced disease revealed a systemic immunological remodelling in advanced CRC, with two immune populations significantly increased in each group of individuals. In the case of locally advanced disease, an increased prevalence of CD134+ on CD4+ conventional T cells was observed. CD134+, also known as OX-40, is a co-stimulatory receptor primarily located on activated CD4+ T cells [30]. It has been demonstrated that this provides a survival and proliferation signal for, principally, CD4+ lymphocytes to persist and respond against tumour cells [30]. In accordance with other reports published, OX-40 expression increases in locally advanced tumours, which has been associated with better prognosis and efficacy of immunotherapy [31]. Additionally, the enrichment of CCR6− CD161+ CD57− CD8+ NKT cells suggests an expansion of innate-like T cells responsive to sustained antigen exposure and tumour-driven inflammation. CD161, also known as NKR-P1A, marks cytokine-responsive T cell subsets, including NKT cells, capable of rapid effector responses even without TCR engagement [32, 33]. The absence of CCR6 may indicate an altered migratory behaviour to epithelial and mucosal tissues or a systemic retention phenotype in late-stage disease [34]. Conversely, the expansion of PD-1+CD38-CD56lowCD16low NK cells in the early stage suggests the presence of an exhausted or regulatory phenotype. This is consistent with an impaired cytotoxic function and a poor prognosis, as has been described in CRC and other solid tumours [35–38]. Finally, the elevated frequency of CD161− CD38− CD45RA− CCR7+ CD4− CD8− conventional T cells, which correspond phenotypically to central memory T cells (TCM) as they lack CD45RA expression but express CCR7, point to an accumulation of double-negative T cells that have transitioned to a memory phenotype but may be in a latent state of activation [39]. Further investigation is needed to establish whether these cells are transitioning from one memory state to another, and to determine their specific role in the development of CRC. Collectively, these findings highlight a complex remodelling of peripheral immune cell subsets during the different stages of the disease, marked by features of activation, exhaustion, and senescence.

A similar comparative analysis based on tumour location, colon versus rectum, identified four immune subsets significantly increased in patients with rectal cancer. Notably, two regulatory T cell subsets (CD27− CD45RA+ and CD27− CD45RA−) exhibited enhanced expression of PD-1− CD38+. These populations correspond to terminally differentiated effector memory cells (called TEMRA) and, effector memory T cells (TEM), respectively [39, 40]. The lack of PD-1 expression may reflect recent activation, while CD38 expression indicates enhanced suppressive function and expansion potential [41–43]. Likewise, the enrichment of CD45RA− CCR7− (markers of TEM cells) CD8+ conventional T cells [44, 45], has been associated with improved prognosis in CRC patients, including better progression-free and overall survival [46]. Lastly, we also found an increase in CD38+ HLA-DR− basophils in rectal tumours. CD38 is a robust marker of innate immune cells like basophils, whose lower expression has been considered an indicator of atypical cells in myeloid neoplasms and systemic lupus erythematosus [47–49]. Together, these findings highlight that tumour anatomical site shapes a distinct systemic immune signature profile, with rectal cancers exhibiting simultaneous upregulation of both effector and regulatory immune signals.

Within the colon cancer subgroup, we observed distinct systemic immune patterns based on tumour laterality. Right-sided tumours showed elevated frequencies of PD-1− CD38+ Tregs (CD27− CD45RA−), a phenotype associated with highly suppressive function and poor outcomes, likely contributing to the inhibition of anti-tumour responses [50]. Mechanistically, the expression of CD38 in/on Tregs has been linked to enhanced adenosine production via the CD38–CD203a–CD73 enzymatic pathway. This leads to a local accumulation of adenosine, which inhibits T-cell activation and proliferation and promotes an immunosuppressive environment [51, 52]. Furthermore, recent evidence from multiple myeloma studies has demonstrated that targeting CD38 with anti-CD38 monoclonal antibodies, such as daratumumab, depletes CD38+ regulatory T cells, restoring the proliferation and cytotoxicity of effector T cells [43]. This phenotype also appeared enriched in rectal tumours, reinforcing its clinical significance. Furthermore, right-sided tumours displayed increased frequencies of CD27+ CD28+ double-positive TEM cells (CD45RA− CCR7− CD4+ CD8+), indicative of heightened antigen processing and co-stimulatory signalling, consistent with their larger size and greater mutational burden. Conversely, left-sided tumours were enriched for CD27− CD28− terminally differentiated or senescent TEM cells (CD45RA− CCR7− CD4+ CD8+), which display reduced plasticity and limited proliferative potential, likely a consequence of chronic immune activation.

In addition, left-sided tumours exhibited increased CD57− CD95+ CD45RA+ CCR7+ CD4+ CD8+ T cells, a population described as stem cell-like memory T cells (TSCM) [53, 54]. These cells possess self-renewal capacity, a long lifespan, and rapid effector differentiation, and have been associated with favourable CRC prognosis and early detection [55, 56]. To our knowledge, this is the first study providing evidence of TSCM enrichment in left-sided tumours, suggesting that while right-sided colon cancers harbour immunosuppressive and co-stimulatory memory T cell profiles, left-sided tumours preserve multipotent-like T cell with signs of immune exhaustion. Given the well-established dichotomy in treatment responses, greater immunotherapy efficacy in right-sided tumours and better chemotherapy outcomes in left-sided disease [10, 11], our results offer immunological insights into this clinical divergence and suggest potential for site-specific immunomodulatory strategies.

According to metastatic patients, the present an enhanced frequency of CD152+ (% on CD14+ monocytes). CD152+, also known as CTLA-4, is considered the major negative regulator of T cell responses [57]. A plausible mechanism involves the ability of CTLA-4 on the surface of the monocytes to bind competitively to CD80/CD86 on antigen presenting cells (APCs) with higher affinity than CD28. This results in the blocking of co-stimulatory signalling, required for full T cell activation. Within the tumour microenvironment, such competitive binding may impair effector T-cell priming and favour an immunosuppressive milieu, ultimately facilitating tumour immune escape [58]. On the contrary, CD38-CD24-(% on CD27+ IgD- B cells), CD57-CD95+ (% on CD45RA+ CCR7- CD8+ conventional T cells) and CD3+CD56+ NKT cells (% on CD45+ cells) were elevated in non-metastatic patients. CD3+CD56+ NKT cells, also referred as NKT-like cells, have been identified as playing a pivotal role in tumour immunity, functioning as a conduit between innate and adaptive immune responses [59, 60]. Functionally, these cells secrete IFN-γ and TNF-α upon activation, thereby enhancing dendritic cell maturation and promoting cytotoxic CD8+ T cells response [61]. Conversely, they may also acquire regulatory features, releasing IL-10 or/and TGF-β and contributing to local immunosuppression. As their functional profile depends on the microenvironment, their role in colorectal cancer remains controversial, with evidence supporting both pro-tumorigenic and anti-tumorigenic effects [62–64]. The present study supports the hypothesis that an augmented presence of NKT cells in CRC may be indicative of a more favourable prognosis. Regarding the CD38-CD24- (% on CD27+IgD- B cells) population, they have not been specifically identified or studied in colorectal cancer. This specific population, designated memory B cells, has been linked to an elevated inflammatory status [65]. In specific circumstances, an inflammatory TME may instigate immune responses (activating cytotoxic cells, such as natural killer cells or CD8+ T cells) which have the capacity to impede the process of metastasis. In parallel, CD57− CD95+ CD45RA+ CCR7− CD8+ TEMRA cells were also elevated in non-metastatic patients. These terminally differentiated effectors are associated with immune-senescence and rapid cytotoxic responses upon antigen exposure [66–68]. Their expansion may reflect effective immune pressure preventing metastatic dissemination, positioning them as candidate biomarkers of immunocompetence in localised disease and potential targets for immunotherapy.

Our immunophenotyping analysis comparing CRC survivors and non-survivors revealed three immune populations significantly associated with mortality: CD57+ CD95+ cells (within CD45RA+ CCR7+ double-negative T cells), CD141− CD1c+ myeloid DCs, and CD4− CD62L− ILCs. The former might be considered a terminally differentiated (CD57+) TSCM cell, an immune subset with high cytotoxic potential but limited proliferative capacity. Their accumulation in survivors may indicate a sustained cytotoxic immune profile, potentially beneficial in controlling low-level persistent antigens or inflammation, even in the context of limited proliferative potential [53, 54]. Similarly, the high frequency of CD62L− ILCs in the survivors group may reflect a functionally active ILC subset, possibly contributing to mucosal integrity, epithelial repair, or residual immune control via IL-22 or IL-17 production [69, 70]. In contrast, the decreased frequency of CD141− CD1c+ myeloid dendritic cells in survivors may point to a reduced Th2 immune response, favouring effective antitumor immunity [71, 72]. In contrast, survivors were enriched for double-negative conventional T cells and CD141− CD1c+ cells DCs.

In addition to these findings, it is important to contextualize our results within the broader framework of tissue-based immune profiling in CRC. Tumour-infiltrating immune cells, particularly CD3+, CD8+ and memory T lymphocytes, are well established prognostic markers, with higher densities consistently associated with improved survival, reduced recurrence, and better responses to therapy [73, 74]. Although our study focuses on circulating immune populations, the enrichment of activated, memory-like, and checkpoint-modulated lymphocyte subsets in circulation parallels features described within tumour tissue, suggesting that systemic immune signatures may mirror, at least in part, local immune dynamics. Conversely, certain peripheral populations identified in our study, such as exhausted NK cells or circulating TSCM-like subsets, may capture aspects of immune dysfunction or immunosurveillance escape that are under-represented in tumour samples, underscoring the complementary nature of blood-based immunophenotyping. Together, these observations support the concept that integrating peripheral and tissue-derived immune information may provide a more comprehensive understanding of CRC immunobiology and enhance the development of clinically actionable biomarkers.

In summary, our study provides an integrative immunophenotypic landscape of CRC, suggesting that distinct systemic immune signatures in peripheral blood may be associated with tumour stage, anatomical site, metastasis development, and patient survival. The identification of specific immune subsets, including exhausted NK cells, activated Tregs, effector memory and stem-like T cells, raises the possibility that these populations could serve as candidate blood-based biomarkers for non-invasive patient stratification. In line with our findings, computational approaches have attempted to model tumour-immune interactions in CRC using systems biology frameworks. Notably, Li et al.,proposed a quantitative cancer-immunity cycle (QCIC) model incorporating CD8+CD4+ Th1 and Treg populations, showing that the balance among these subsets predicts tumour progression and overall survival in metastatic patients [75].

While our findings are encouraging, they must be interpreted considering the limited cohort size, which warrants further validation in larger independent cohorts. Future studies integrating functional assays and longitudinal sampling will be necessary to determine whether these immune populations represent robust biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Nonetheless, this work contributes to the growing field of blood-based immune biomarkers in CRC, supporting the improved patients’ stratification and informing tailored immunotherapies. It also provides valuable insights for computational approaches seeking to model tumour-immune interactions and reinforces the concept that circulating immunity mirrors tumour biology in clinically meaningful ways.
