A Kinetic Study of the Inhibition Mechanism of Hexafluoropropylene (HFP) on Hydrogen Combustion
Authors: Wei He, Cheng Wang, Qichun Zhang, Yin Sijing, Yan Li, Kang Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hexafluoropropylene (HFP) is used as an advanced fire extinguishing agent with high thermal stability, excellent fire suppression performance, and environmentally friendly characteristics. In this study, the inhibition mechanism of HFP during H2 combustion was analyzed, focusing on its reaction behavior with H and OH radicals to verify its interruption of the combustion chain reaction by scavenging key reactive radicals. The unimolecular pyrolysis process of HFP was investigated to characterize the heat absorption effect when it dissociates into small molecular fragments. The gas-phase kinetic properties of the proposed reaction pathway were analyzed by combining high-level quantum chemical calculations with the RRKM/master equation. The results show that the unimolecular dissociation reaction of HFP has a significant rate difference at low temperatures, but this difference gradually decreases with increasing temperature. In the substitution reaction pathway, the reaction rates of HFP with H and OH were much higher than those of H-abstraction, suggesting that it has an important influence in inhibiting H2 combustion. In addition, the addition reaction of HFP with H and OH also plays a key role in inhibiting combustion at low temperatures due to the presence of CC double bonds in the molecule. It is particularly noteworthy that the rate of the addition reaction of HFP with H increases significantly with increasing temperature, while the rate of the addition reaction with OH is lower than that of the former at high temperatures. The present study lays a data foundation for the establishment of a more accurate kinetic mechanism of combustion inhibition, which is of great significance for the design of new flame retardants.

Introduction

Reducing carbon emissions and reliance on fossil fuels has become a pressing global task, necessitating a shift to carbon-free energy carriers. , Against this backdrop, hydrogen (H2) shows great potential as an alternative energy source with its significant environmental benefits, high energy density and wide range of applications. However, H2 faces challenges in storage and transportation, and its high flammability and explosiveness require strict safety management. Therefore, it is particularly important to have an in-depth understanding of the inhibition mechanisms of existing and potential flame retardants to ensure the safe application of H2 energy.

The inhibition mechanism is closely related to the molecular structure of chemical inhibitors, and the flame inhibition effect of HFC inhibitors mainly originates from three aspects: slowing down or interrupting the chain reaction by scavenging the active radicals, lowering the local temperature by chemical heat dissipation generated by the dissociation of the inhibitors, and physical heat dissipation by the phase transition or sensible heat release. − In-depth understanding of the decomposition behavior and the competitive reaction with H/OH radicals is of great significance in elucidating the inhibition mechanism in hydrogen combustion and provides a theoretical basis for the design of new high-efficiency fire extinguishing agents.

Hynes et al. investigated the inhibition mechanism of 2-H Heptafluoropropane (CF3CHFCF3) during H2 combustion using a combination of experimental and numerical simulations. The results show that the inhibition effect is mainly through the reaction of H radicals with fluorine-containing species to generate HF, which effectively cuts down the concentration of reactive radicals, while the inhibition effect on OH radicals is relatively weak. Liu et al. investigated the hydrogen abstraction mechanism of the reaction of the tricarbon fluorinated alkane CF3CHFCF3 with hydroxyl radicals (OH) by using density-functional theory (DFT) in combination with the variational transition state theory and the small curvature tunnelling effect correction. It is found that the reaction mainly proceeds through an indirect mechanism of transition state formation by hydrogen-bonded complexes, and the variational effect has a significant influence on the reaction rate constant, while the tunneling effect is small. Copeland et al. systematically explored the pyrolytic behavior of 2-H-heptafluoropropane (CF3CHFCF3) under low-pressure argon dilution conditions by combining ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy and ab initio molecular orbital calculations. It was found that in the low-temperature range of 600–900 °C, the main pyrolysis reaction was CF3CHFCF3 → CF3CFCF2 + HF; and in the medium-high-temperature range of 900–1200 °C, CF3CHFCF3 → C2F4 + CF3H reaction becomes important. Bian et al. investigated the reaction mechanism of 2-bromo-3,3,3-trifluoropropene (2-BTP) with OH and H radicals using quantum chemical calculations in order to reveal its inhibition and combustion-supporting behaviors in the flame. The results show that at low temperatures, both types of reactions are dominated by radical addition, while with increasing temperature and pressure, the reaction of 2-BTP with OH is dominated by H extraction and Br substitution pathways. Zhang et al. explored the pyrolysis mechanism of trans-1,1,1,4,4,4-hexafluoro-2-butene and its fire extinguishing performance by experimental and theoretical methods. It was found that trans-1,1,1,4,4,4-hexafluoro-2-butene has good thermal stability at room temperature and exhibits excellent fire extinguishing properties, and its outstanding fire extinguishing properties are mainly attributed to the effect of its pyrolysis products on the trapping effect of free radicals in the flame. Wang et al. systematically investigated the inhibition mechanism of HFC-227ea on hydrogen combustion by high-level quantum chemical calculations combined with the RRKM master equation method. Based on the rate constants of these reaction pathways, a new chemical kinetic model was constructed and simulated in combination with C1–C3 fluorinated hydrocarbons and hydrogen combustion mechanism, and the results showed that HFC-227ea plays an inhibitory role mainly through the depletion of H and OH radicals, and thermal cracking is only a secondary factor. He et al. systematically analyzed the inhibition mechanism of HFC-236fa (1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoropropane) during H2 combustion based on the master equation method of transition state theory solution. The results show that the fire suppression effect of HFC-236fa is mainly realized through the hydroxyl abstraction reaction on the carbon atom at the center of its molecule; in contrast, the related reaction involving the trifluoromethyl group contributes little to the overall fire suppression effect due to its low kinetic and thermodynamic activity.

Hexafluoropropylene (HFP, CF2CFCF2) is a colorless, odorless, and nonflammable gas that is stable at room temperature and possesses excellent physical properties. It is commonly used as a raw material in the production of various fluorinated fine chemicals, fluorosulfonic acid ion-exchange membranes, pharmaceutical intermediates, and fire extinguishing agents. HFP demonstrates strong environmental compatibility, with an ODP (Ozone Depletion Potential) of 0, a GWP (Global Warming Potential) of 2, and an ALT (Atmospheric Lifetime) value of 0.016, making it nearly harmless to humans. Lv et al. determined the flammability limits of 1,1,2,3,3,3-hexafluoro-1-propene (R1216) with two flammable workmates, R32 and R152a, by the standard method of ASTM E681, and found that R1216 has a significant fire suppression ability, with critical flammable volume fractions of about 0.15 and 1.1, respectively. It was shown that the higher the F/H atomic ratio in the mixture, the lower the flammability; at the same time, it is easier for R1216 to generate in the thermal decomposition of free radicals during thermal decomposition, effectively inhibiting flame propagation. Li et al. systematically explored the thermal decomposition behavior and fire extinguishing performance of R1216 through theoretical calculations and experimental means. The results show that R1216 has excellent thermal stability, almost does not decompose below 600 °C, and can generate CF3 during its decomposition, which can effectively capture H and OH to interrupt the combustion chain reaction, and at the same time realize synergistic fire-extinguishing effects through the mechanisms of heat-absorbing cooling and product dilution. Krishnan et al. investigated the kinetics and mechanism of the addition reaction of ozone with tetrafluoroethylene (TFE) and hexafluoropropylene (HFP) at low temperatures. Activation energies and rate constants for the reaction of both with ozone were obtained by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). Quantum chemical calculations showed that the reaction of TFE proceeds mainly through asynchronous addition (DeMore mechanism), while the reaction of HFP involves both synchronous addition (Criegee mechanism) and asynchronous addition.

The main objective of this study is to establish the reaction mechanism of the inhibition of HFP during H2 combustion. The study consists of three key components: (1) an advanced ab initio computational approach to study the decomposition process of HFC-227ea and its potential energy surfaces for the reaction with hydrogen atoms (H) and hydroxyl radicals (OH); (2) calculation of rate constants for the relevant reaction pathways; and (3) in-depth analyses of the possible reaction mechanisms to provide a comprehensive understanding of the inhibition of H2 combustion by HFP.

Theoretical Methodology

Electronic Structure Methods

The potential energy surfaces for HFP unimolecular dissociation and its reactions with OH/H radicals were computed at the M062X/6-311++g (d, p) level, including structural optimization, frequency analysis, transition state identification, and zero-point energy corrections. , The lowest energy configurations were identified via flexible scanning of dihedral angles in 10° increments. Transition state configurations were identified by the presence of a single imaginary frequency and further validated by intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) calculations to ensure connectivity between the designated reactants and products. Frequency and zero-point energy corrections were applied using scaling factors of 0.985 and 0.971, respectively. To enhance the accuracy of single-point energy calculations, the CCSD­(T) method was employed in conjunction with the cc-pVXZ (X = D, T) basis sets, followed by extrapolation to the complete basis set (CBS) limit. E[CCSDT(T)/CBS]DZ→TZ=E[CCSD(T)/cc−pVTZ]+{E[CCSD(T)/cc−pVTZ]−E[CCSD(T)/cc−pVDZ]}×0.4629

In order to verify the accuracy of the CBS extrapolation formula, further benchmark comparisons with experimental thermochemical data and high-precision CCSD­(T)/QZ reference calculations were carried out for representative molecular systems. The results of these comparisons show that the difference between the calculated relative energy values and the extrapolated formula is 0.38 kcal·mol–1, indicating that our CBS extrapolation method is in good agreement with the experimental data. The DFT calculations were conducted using the Gaussian 09 software package.

Reaction Kinetics

Calculations of temperature- and pressure-dependent rate constants for correlated reactions on the HFP potential energy surface were performed using transition state theory (TST), Rice-Ramsperger-Kassel-Marcus (RRKM) theory, and the Eckart tunneling effect correction method. These calculations were accomplished by solving the master equation using the MESS program package. , The Lennard-Jones (L-J) model was utilized to estimate the collision frequencies between the dilute gas (argon, Ar) and the reactants. The parameters employed were as follows: for Ar, σ = 3.47 Å and ε = 79.20 cm–1; for the reactants, σ = 3.47 Å and ε = 79.20 cm–1. The parameters for argon were obtained from previous studies, while those for HFP were derived using Joback’s group contribution method. , The collision energy transfer was modeled using a single exponential down model, expressed as <ΔE>down = 300 × (T/300)0.85. Additionally, the low-frequency torsional mode associated with the internal rotation at stationary points on the potential energy surface was treated as a one-dimensional hindered rotor. The hindered potential for this mode was determined through relaxed scanning at 10° intervals, performed at the M062X/6-311++g­(d, p) level of theory.

Due to the formation of a weak van der Waals (vdW) complex between HFP and OH, the transition state of this barrierless reaction is highly loose, making it impossible to calculate the rate constant using conventional transition state theory. Therefore, the rate constant for this process is determined by using phase space theory. The interaction between the reactants and the weak complex is described by the potential (R) = C 6/R 6, where R represents the distance between the reactants. The coefficient C 6 is estimated by the equation C 6 = α1 × α2 × E 1 × E 2/(E 1 + E 2), where αi and E i(i = 1, 2) denote the polarizability and ionization energy of the two reactants, respectively. Based on calculations at the M062X/6-311++g (d, p) level of theory, the value of C 6 in the current system is 2.01 × 105 cm–1 × Å6.

Unimolecular Decomposition of HFP

The effectiveness of inhibitors is significantly affected by their chemical heat sink. In this study, our focus is on the tendency of the inhibitors to undergo heat-absorbing reactions when HFP is involved in the initial stages of H2 combustion. The initial decomposition of HFP can take place mainly through the unimolecular dissociation reaction channel, in which four simple bond-cleavage reactions (R1-R4) are recognized, as shown in Figure .

Relative energy of the decomposition reaction of HFP at 0 K includes the zero-point energy. The unit is in kcal·mol–1.

The dissociation potential energy surface analysis of HFP shown in Figure reveals a remarkable selective feature of its bond breaking: the C5–C1 bond breaking channel (R1) generating 1,2,2,2-tetrafluoroethene (F2CFC*) and CF3 exhibits the lowest dissociation energy barrier of 109.4 kcal·mol–1, indicating that this dissociation pathway is the most thermodynamically favorable. Notably, the fracture energy barriers of the three C–F bonds show a clear site-dependence C1–F:119.7 kcal·mol–1 (R2), C5–F:119.4 kcal·mol–1 (R3), and C6–F:126.3 kcal·mol–1 (R4), with the C6–F bond possessing the highest dissociation energy due to conjugate stabilizing effect of the fracture product radicals and significant spatial site-dependent interaction. The 8.0–16.9 kcal·mol–1 energy barrier difference between the dissociation channels predicts a clear temperature-dependent reaction selectivity. C–C bond breaking dominates at low temperatures, while the C–F bond activation process will be gradually revealed with increasing temperature.

Bimolecular Reaction of HFP + H

In this study, the two most representative reactive radicals (H and OH) in the H2 combustion system were selected as objects of study, and the detailed reaction kinetic mechanism of the interaction of HFP as a chemical scavenger with these radicals was revealed. Figure shows the PES characteristics of the bimolecular reaction of HFP with H, which clearly presents the structural evolution of the key intermediates and transition states and their corresponding energy changes during the reaction.

Relative energies of the reaction between HFP and H at 0 K include the zero-point energy. The unit is in kcal·mol–1.

Since the HFP molecule contains a CC double bond, we constructed PESs for all abstraction (R5-R7), substitution (R11-R12), and addition (R9-R10) reaction channels of the reaction of HFP with H atoms.

In the abstraction pathway, H-abstraction occurs at the lowest energy barrier of 35.5 kcal·mol–1 (TS2) and forms 1,1,3,3,3-pentafluoroprop-1-en-2-yl + HF in an exothermic reaction with an energy of −16.5 kcal·mol–1. In addition, when HFP undergoes the abstraction reaction with H atoms, the reaction energy barrier is 43.1 kcal/mol via the transition state TS3 (R7), which generates the products 1,2,3,3,3-pentafluoroprop-1-en-1-yl (FC*CFCF3) and HF, and 38.0 kcal·mol–1 via the transition state TS1 (R5), which generates the products 1,1,2,3,3-pentafluoroprop-2-en-1-yl (F2CCFC*F2) and HF. Due to the high bonding energies and intrinsic stability of the C–F bonds, the reactivity is low, so the abstraction process of fluorine atoms needs to overcome the higher energy barriers corresponding to TS1 and TS3, thus limiting the occurrence of this type of reaction.

The substitution reaction of HFP with H atoms shows remarkable reaction channel selectivity involving three competing pathways: via TS5 (energy barrier 3.6 kcal·mol–1, R9) to produce 1,1-difluoro-3λ1-prop-1-ene and F, via TS6 (energy barrier 15.6 kcal·mol–1, R10) to form (Z)-1,2-difluoro-3λ1-prop-1-ene and F, and via TS4 (energy barrier 62.7 kcal·mol–1, R8) to produce 1,1,2,3,3-pentafluoroprop-1-ene and F. Kinetic analyses showed that the TS5 pathway was the thermodynamically most favorable major reaction channel due to its lowest activation energy barrier (3.6 kcal·mol–1). It is particularly noteworthy that the energy barrier of the TS4 pathway involving C–F bond breaking is significantly higher than that of the other pathways (about 17–20 times higher), a phenomenon that directly reflects the kinetic inertness characterized by the high dissociation energy of the C–F bond and the strong electronegativity of the fluorine atom.

The addition reaction of HFP with H atoms involves two competing pathways: generation of 1,1,2-trifluoro-3λ1-propan-2-yl via TS8 (5.8 kcal·mol–1, R12) or 1,1,2-trifluoro-3λ1-propan-1-yl via TS7 (5.5 kcal·mol–1, R11). The proximity of these two energy barriers suggests that 1,1,2-trifluoro-3λ1-propan-2-yl and 1,1,2-trifluoro-3λ1-propan-1-yl may be generated in parallel and that their product distributions are regulated by kinetic and thermodynamic factors.

Bimolecular Reaction of HFP + OH

The potential energy surfaces for the abstraction and substitution reactions of HFP with OH are shown in Figure . Like the reaction of HFP with H atoms, the reaction also includes three abstraction channels, two substitution channels, and two addition channels.

Relative energies of the reaction between HFP and OH at 0 K include the zero-point energy. The unit is in kcal·mol–1.

In the OH-abstraction reaction of HFP, the calculation of transition state energy barriers showed that there were significant differences in the activation energies of different reaction paths. Among them, TS9 corresponds to the lowest energy barrier (79.8 kcal·mol–1, R13), which mainly generates 1,1,2,3,3-pentafluoroprop-2-en-1-yl + HOF, while TS10 has a slightly higher energy barrier (79.9 kcal·mol–1, R14), which favors the formation of 1,1,3,3,3-pentafluoroprop-1-en-2-yl + HOF. In contrast, TS11 has the highest energy barrier (88.2 kcal·mol–1, R15), which corresponds to the 1,2,3,3,3-pentafluoroprop-1-en-1-yl + HOF pathway. This energy barrier distribution suggests that the formation of 1,1,2,3,3-pentafluoroprop-2-en-1-yl and 1,1,3,3,3-pentafluoroprop-1-en-2-yl is kinetically more favorable, whereas the generation of 1,2,3,3,3-pentafluoroprop-1-en-1-yl may be less competitive at ambient temperature and pressure due to the higher energy barrier.

The different pathways of the OH substitution reaction of HFP show significant energy barrier differences. The reaction proceeds mainly via two competing pathways: (1) via transition state TS13 (energy barrier 28.4 kcal·mol–1) to produce the products (E)-1,2,3,3,3-pentafluoroprop-1-en-1-ol and F. The reaction proceeds via the transition state TS12 (energy barrier 76.5 kcal·mol–1) to produce the products 1,1,2,3,3-pentafluoroprop-2-en-1-ol and F. It is noteworthy that the difference in energy barriers between the two pathways is as high as 48.1 kcal·mol–1, indicating that the reaction will be highly selective via the TS13 pathway under ambient conditions, whereas the contribution of the TS12 pathway can be neglected due to its higher energy barrier.

Among the reactions of HFP with OH, the addition reaction has the lowest energy barrier. HFP passes through the transition state TS14 with an energy barrier of 0.6 kcal·mol–1 (R18) with OH to produce 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoro-1-hydroxy-1,2,3-trifluoroethyl. In addition, HFP forms a weak van der Waals (vdW) complex (−1.0 kcal/mol) with OH, passing through transition state TS15 with an energy barrier of −0.7 kcal·mol–1 to produce 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoro-1-hydroxy-1,2,3-trifluoroethyl. The direct addition pathway (TS14) and the pathway via the van der Waals complex (TS15), the difference in energy barriers between the two is only 1.3 kcal·mol–1, suggesting that both pathways will occur simultaneously at room temperature. Among them, the direct addition pathway has a weak kinetic advantage, while the van der Waals interaction may have a moderating effect on the reaction process.

Unimolecular Dissociation Reactions of HFP

The trends of rate constants for the four HFP unimolecular dissociation reactions (R1-R4) at different temperatures and pressures are demonstrated in Figure . The rate constant of all the dissociation reaction paths increased significantly with increasing temperature, which indicated that high temperature conditions play a key role in promoting the pyrolysis of HFP. The most reactive one was R3, whose rate constant was higher than that of other pathways throughout the temperature interval, indicating that this pathway might be the main thermal cleavage channel. Second, the rate constants of R2 and R4 were relatively close and slightly lower than those of R3, while R1 was always the slowest rate pathway, indicating that its activation energy was higher and it might be a secondary pathway during the conventional thermal decomposition process.

Rate constants for unimolecular dissociation of HFP. (a) p = 1 atm; (b) p = 100 atm.

There are several possible cleavage pathways during thermal decomposition of HFCs, of which R3 is the most kinetically favorable, has the highest reaction rate, and thus may be the dominant product pathway. In addition, this type of cleavage reaction is extremely temperature sensitive, with a significant increase in reaction rate with increasing temperature.

H-Abstraction and H-Substitution Reaction Rate Constants for HFP

The trend of the rate constant with temperature for the six possible paths (R5-R10) for the reaction of HFP with H atoms is illustrated in Figure . Overall, the rate constant of all the reaction paths increases with increasing temperature, showing a typical positive temperature-dependent trend. Among them, R9 and R10 exhibited the highest rate constants throughout the temperature range, indicating that these two reaction paths have lower activation energies, are kinetically more favorable, and may be the dominant product-generating channels in this reaction system. In contrast, the remaining pathways (R5-R8) showed lower overall values, although the rate constant also increased with increasing temperature. Among them, R8 has the lowest rate and is the least reactive, especially in the low-temperature region, indicating that this pathway is kinetically inferior.

Rate constant for the H-abstraction and H-substitution reactions between HFP and H atoms.

In the substitution reaction of HFP with H atoms, the reaction rates of R9 and R10 are significantly higher than those of other pathways, and it is hypothesized that these two pathways may play a key role in dominating the direction of the reaction and the distribution of the products in the high-temperature reaction environment.

OH-Abstraction and OH-Substitution Reaction Rate Constants for HFP

The temperature-dependent trends of the rate constants of five reaction paths (R13-R17) in the range of 300–2000 K for different products formed by the reaction of HFP with OH are presented in Figure . The rate constant of all the reaction paths increases with increasing temperature, showing typical positive temperature dependent behavior. Among them, the rate constants of reaction R17 were significantly higher than those of other pathways in the whole temperature range, indicating that this pathway was a dominant channel at high temperature and had a strong reaction driving force. This is followed by reactions R14 and R15, whose products both contain HOF, and the rate constants are gradually close to each other in the high-temperature region, but still lower than those of R17. Comparatively, reactions R13 and R16 have lower rate constants, which are especially pronounced in the low-temperature region, suggesting that their reactivity is limited. Although the rates of these two pathways also increase with temperature at high temperatures, the increase is not as pronounced as that of R17.

Rate constant for the OH-abstraction and OH-substitution reactions between HFP and H atoms.

In conclusion, the reaction pathways of HFP with OH are significantly temperature-dependent, with R17 dominating, which may have a key influence on the product distribution at high temperatures.

H-Addition and OH-Addition Reaction Rate Constants for HFP

Figure illustrates the rate constant for the addition reaction of HFP with H and OH. The results show that the rate constants increase with increasing temperature, exhibiting a typical positive temperature-dependent trend. The rate constant for the OH-addition reaction (R19) was highest at temperatures below 800 K, indicating its high reactivity at low temperatures. The rates of the H-addition reactions (R11 and R12) increase significantly with increasing temperature and become the dominant reaction pathway at temperatures higher than 800 K, highlighting their dominant role in the reaction network at high temperatures. In contrast, the reactions involving the addition of OH (R18 and R19) have lower overall rates, but their reaction rates are significantly enhanced with increasing temperature and pressure, especially at high pressures, showing greater temperature sensitivity. At high pressures of 100 atm, the rate constants for all reactions are generally higher than those at low pressures of 1 atm, and the rate enhancement is especially significant in the low temperature region.

Rate constants for the addition reactions of HFP with H atoms and OH.

In the unimolecular dissociation reaction of HFP, the substitution reaction of HFP with hydrogen atoms and hydroxyl groups, and the addition reaction of HFP with hydrogen atoms and hydroxyl groups, the addition reaction pathway has the highest rate constant and shows the strongest reactivity, indicating that this pathway is the most dominant in the reaction system. Especially in the lower temperature range, the dominance of the addition reaction is particularly significant; with the increase of temperature, the rate constant of this reaction pathway still dominates, reflecting obvious temperature sensitivity, and becomes the main reaction pathway in the high temperature region.

Conclusions

In this study, the inhibition mechanism of HFP on H2 combustion reactions is deeply analyzed based on the transition state theory and combined with the solution of the master equation. This work investigates the unimolecular decomposition behavior of HFP, highlighting its propensity for endothermic pathways during fragmentation into smaller molecular species. To elucidate the chemical kinetics of HFP in radical scavenging processes, the study concentrates on two primary reactive species H and OH. The study shows that there is a significant difference in the rate constants of the single-molecule dissociation reactions (R1-R4) of HFP at low temperatures, and the difference gradually decreases with increasing temperature. The rate constant of the substitution reactions of HFP with H and OH (R9-R10, R16-R17) are much higher than those of the corresponding H-abstraction channels, suggesting that this type of reaction has a dominant role in combustion inhibition. Since the HFP molecule contains CC, its addition reactions with H and OH also play an important role in combustion inhibition, especially at low temperatures. The rate constant of the addition reactions of HFP with H (R11-R12) increased significantly with increasing temperature, while the reactions with OH (R18-R19) were faster at low temperatures but slowed down at high temperatures and were eventually lower than R11-R12. By fitting the rate constants of each reaction to the Arrhenius expression and expressing them in the CHEMKIN-compatible PLOG format, the present study provides a key kinetic database for the subsequent in-depth revelation of the reaction mechanism between HFP and reactive radicals.
