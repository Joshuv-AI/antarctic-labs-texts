Sex-specific associations between grip strength and metabolic syndrome in Korean adults: a nationally representative cross-sectional study
Authors: Hyejin Chun, Miae Doo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study examined whether the association between grip strength and metabolic syndrome differs by sex and how it is influenced by body mass index (BMI).MethodsWe analyzed nationally representative cross-sectional data from Korean adults aged ≥40 years who participated in the 2022–2024 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. Grip strength was categorized into sex-specific tertiles. Metabolic syndrome was defined according to modified ATP III criteria using Korean-specific waist circumference thresholds. Survey-weighted logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using sequential adjustment models.ResultsIn men, the association between grip strength and metabolic syndrome observed in minimally adjusted models was markedly attenuated after BMI adjustment. In women, low grip strength was associated with higher odds of metabolic syndrome, and this association became stronger after BMI adjustment. Compared with women in the highest tertile, those in the lowest tertile had higher odds of metabolic syndrome (OR 1.54, 95% CI 1.21, 1.96). A significant interaction between grip strength and sex was observed.ConclusionsBMI substantially influenced the association between grip strength and metabolic syndrome, particularly in men. Grip strength may provide additional information on metabolic risk beyond BMI, especially among women.

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome is a major public health concern characterized by the clustering of central obesity, dyslipidemia, elevated blood pressure, and impaired glucose regulation, and is associated with increased risks of cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes (Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration et al., 2010; Dhondge et al., 2024). The increasing burden of metabolic syndrome underscores the need for simple and feasible indicators that can help identify individuals at elevated metabolic risk (Li et al., 2025).

Muscle strength, commonly assessed using handgrip dynamometry, has emerged as an important indicator of overall health status and has been associated with mortality and adverse metabolic outcomes in population-based studies (Cooper et al., 2010; Volaklis et al., 2015). These findings suggest that muscle strength reflects physiological reserve and may serve as a useful marker of metabolic health.

However, the relationship between muscle strength and metabolic syndrome is not fully understood, particularly with respect to sex differences. Biological sex may modify metabolic risk through differences in hormonal regulation, fat distribution, and metabolic physiology (Lovejoy and Sainsbury, 2009; Karastergiou et al., 2012). In addition, because absolute grip strength is strongly correlated with body size, its association with metabolic syndrome may be influenced by adiposity.

Previous studies of Korean adults have reported associations between grip strength and cardiometabolic risk indicators or metabolic syndrome, often using relative grip strength as the main exposure (Yi et al., 2018; Cho et al., 2019; Chong et al., 2020; Shim and Yoo, 2020; Hong et al., 2022; Park and Kim, 2025). However, less attention has been paid to how absolute grip strength, a clinically intuitive measure, should be interpreted when body size is explicitly considered. In particular, whether sex differences are supported by formal interaction testing and how the association changes before and after BMI adjustment remain insufficiently examined. Therefore, this study examined sex-specific associations between absolute grip strength and metabolic syndrome in Korean adults, formally evaluated effect modification by sex, assessed the influence of BMI using sequentially adjusted models, and explored the role of body size through supplementary analyses.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was based on data from the 2022–2024 KNHANES, a nationally representative, cross-sectional survey conducted by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency. KNHANES employs a stratified, multistage probability sampling design to represent the non-institutionalized Korean population (Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency, 2024). The analysis was restricted to the 2022–2024 KNHANES cycles to estimate associations in contemporary Korean adults using a consistent analytic framework while minimizing calendar-period heterogeneity. Among the initial participants (n = 20,191), individuals aged <40 years were excluded (n = 6856). Participants with missing data on grip strength (n = 3948), metabolic syndrome components (n = 276), or covariates including BMI and lifestyle variables (n = 516) were further excluded. After these exclusions, a total of 8595 participants were included in the final analytic sample. The final sample consisted of 3750 men and 4845 women. The present study used publicly available anonymized KNHANES data, for which all participants provided written informed consent, and was reviewed and determined to be exempt by the Institutional Review Board of Kunsan National University (IRB No. 1040117-202603-HR-005-01).

Measures

Grip strength was measured using a standardized hand dynamometer according to KNHANES protocols. Each participant performed two measurements for each hand, and the maximum value from the four measurements was used in the analysis. Because muscle strength differs substantially by sex, grip strength was categorized into sex-specific tertiles (T1: lowest; T2: middle; T3: highest) to compare metabolic risk across low, middle, and high levels within each sex. The highest tertile (T3) was used as the reference group in regression analyses. The tertile cut-off values were 34.7 and 41.3 kg for men and 21.7 and 25.8 kg for women. In sensitivity analyses, relative grip strength was calculated as maximum grip strength divided by BMI and categorized into sex-specific tertiles.

Metabolic syndrome was defined according to the modified National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III criteria. The five components were elevated blood pressure, elevated fasting glucose, elevated triglycerides, reduced HDL cholesterol, and abdominal obesity. Abdominal obesity was defined using Korean-specific waist circumference thresholds (≥90 cm for men and ≥85 cm for women). Participants meeting three or more components were classified as having metabolic syndrome.

Covariates

Potential confounders were selected a priori based on previous epidemiological studies examining muscle strength and cardiometabolic risk (Srikanthan and Karlamangla, 2014; Leong et al., 2015; Celis-Morales et al., 2018; García-Hermoso et al., 2018) as well as data availability in the KNHANES. Sociodemographic variables included age (continuous), educational level, and household income. Health behavior variables included current smoking status, alcohol drinking, and aerobic physical activity. Smoking status was categorized as current smoker or non−/ex-smoker. Alcohol drinking was categorized as <1 drink per month or ≥1 drink per month based on self-reported monthly drinking frequency. Aerobic physical activity was defined as meeting the KNHANES criterion of at least 150 min per week of moderate-intensity activity, at least 75 min per week of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination, with 1 min of vigorous activity counted as 2 min of moderate activityShim and Yoo. Educational level was classified as ≤elementary school, middle school, high school, or ≥college. Household income was categorized into quartiles: low, mid-low, mid-high, and high. Dietary intake variables were derived from the 24-h dietary recall and included total energy intake (kcal/day), sodium intake (mg/day), and dietary fiber intake (g/day). These nutrient variables were included to account for key aspects of dietary intake potentially related to cardiometabolic risk and metabolic syndrome in previous epidemiological studies (Mozaffarian, 2016; Reynolds et al., 2019; He et al., 2013). Composite diet quality indices were not used because the primary aim of this study was not to evaluate dietary patterns but to adjust for major nutritional factors associated with metabolic risk. BMI was calculated from height and weight and included as an overall body-size and adiposity-related variable because absolute grip strength is strongly related to body size and adiposity is closely associated with metabolic risk. Because metabolic syndrome includes waist circumference as a diagnostic component, BMI adjustment was interpreted cautiously as an additional model to examine the influence of overall adiposity rather than as a simple confounder adjustment.

Statistical analysis

All analyses accounted for the complex KNHANES sampling design using sampling weights, stratification variables, and primary sampling units. Integrated weights were applied for pooled survey years. Baseline characteristics were compared across sex-specific grip strength tertiles using survey-weighted linear regression for continuous variables and Rao–Scott chi-square tests for categorical variables. Survey-weighted logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for metabolic syndrome. Sequential models were used to examine changes in the association after covariate adjustment. Model 1 included age and survey year; Model 2 additionally included education, household income, smoking, alcohol drinking, and aerobic physical activity; Model 3 further included total energy intake, sodium intake, and dietary fiber intake; and Model 4 additionally included BMI. Linear trends were assessed by modeling grip strength tertiles as an ordinal variable, and sex interaction was tested using a cross-product term between grip strength tertiles and sex. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using relative grip strength, calculated as maximum grip strength divided by BMI, and using sex-specific quartiles of absolute grip strength to examine whether the findings were robust to body-size adjustment and alternative categorization. Exploratory mediation analyses examined BMI as a potential mediator between grip strength and metabolic syndrome, separately by sex, using bootstrapping. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for variables in the fully adjusted model, with values <10 indicating no problematic multicollinearity. All analyses were conducted using the survey package in R software (version 4.4.1), and a two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Association between grip strength tertiles and prevalence of metabolic syndrome

The association between grip strength tertiles and metabolic syndrome was examined using sequentially adjusted models (Table 3). In men, lower absolute grip strength was associated with lower odds of metabolic syndrome in Models 1–3, but this association was markedly attenuated after additional adjustment for BMI. Compared with the highest tertile, the OR for metabolic syndrome in the lowest tertile changed from 0.52 (95% CI 0.42, 0.65) in Model 1 to 0.93 (95% CI 0.72, 1.20) in Model 4. In women, low grip strength was associated with higher odds of metabolic syndrome, and this association became stronger after BMI adjustment (T1 vs. T3: Model 1, OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.10, 1.67; Model 4, OR 1.54, 95% CI 1.21, 1.96). A significant interaction between grip strength and sex was observed for the lowest tertile (P for interaction = 0.002). In sensitivity analyses using relative grip strength divided by BMI, lower relative grip strength was consistently associated with higher odds of metabolic syndrome in both men and women (Supplementary Table S2). Compared with the highest tertile, the lowest tertile showed higher odds of metabolic syndrome in men (OR 3.12, 95% CI 2.50, 3.89) and women (OR 6.39, 95% CI 5.00, 8.16). Additional analyses using sex-specific quartiles of absolute grip strength showed patterns generally consistent with the main tertile-based analyses; associations in men were attenuated after BMI adjustment, whereas lower grip strength quartiles were associated with higher odds of metabolic syndrome in women (Supplementary Table S3).

Exploratory mediation analysis

Exploratory mediation analyses were conducted to examine whether BMI mediated the association between grip strength and metabolic syndrome (Supplementary Table S4). In men, the average causal mediation effect (ACME) showed a marginal trend but did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.070), whereas the direct and total effects were not significant. In women, no significant indirect, direct, or total effects were observed.

Discussion

In this nationally representative study of Korean adults aged ≥40 years, we observed sex-specific associations between grip strength and metabolic syndrome. In men, lower absolute grip strength was associated with lower odds of metabolic syndrome in minimally adjusted models, but this association was markedly attenuated after BMI adjustment. In contrast, in women, low grip strength remained associated with higher odds of metabolic syndrome and became more pronounced after BMI adjustment. The significant interaction between grip strength and sex suggests that the association between muscle strength and metabolic risk clustering differs by sex.

Previous studies have shown that lower grip strength is associated with mortality and adverse cardiometabolic outcomes, supporting its role as a marker of physiological reserve and metabolic health (Cooper et al., 2010; Volaklis et al., 2015). However, sex-specific findings have been inconsistent, possibly reflecting differences in hormonal regulation, fat distribution, body composition, and analytical approaches (Lovejoy and Sainsbury, 2009; Karastergiou et al., 2012). In the Korean population, previous analyses using KNHANES have reported associations between relative grip strength and cardiometabolic risk indicators, including metabolic syndrome. However, variations in outcome definitions, covariate adjustment strategies, and analytical approaches limit direct comparison across studies. Building on these studies, the present study formally evaluated sex interaction and showed that the association persisted in women after BMI adjustment, whereas the association in men was substantially attenuated.

An important observation in the present study was that the association between grip strength and metabolic syndrome changed substantially after adjustment for BMI, particularly in men. In men, lower absolute grip strength was associated with lower odds of metabolic syndrome in minimally adjusted models, but this association was largely attenuated after BMI adjustment, suggesting that BMI plays a critical role in shaping this relationship. This pattern indicates that BMI may not function solely as a conventional confounder. BMI was included in the sequential regression models as an overall body-size and adiposity-related variable; therefore, attenuation after BMI adjustment should be interpreted as reflecting the influence of body size and adiposity on the association between absolute grip strength and metabolic syndrome, rather than as definitive evidence of confounding. Because absolute grip strength is strongly correlated with body size, adjustment for BMI may partially account for shared variance between muscle strength and adiposity and may also represent adjustment for a body-size-related factor or potential overadjustment. Consistent with this interpretation, sensitivity analyses using relative grip strength divided by BMI showed that lower relative grip strength was associated with higher odds of metabolic syndrome in both sexes. These findings suggest that absolute grip strength may reflect both muscle strength and body size, whereas relative grip strength may better capture muscle strength in relation to metabolic risk after accounting for adiposity. In women, the association between low grip strength and metabolic syndrome remained significant after BMI adjustment, suggesting that grip strength may reflect muscle function less dependent on overall body size.

BMI is widely used as an indicator of body mass relative to height, but it does not distinguish fat mass from lean mass or capture regional fat distribution (Nuttall, 2015). This limitation may be particularly relevant when sex differences in body composition are considered. Women generally have higher body fat percentages and different adipose tissue distribution than men, which may influence the relationship between muscle function and metabolic abnormalities (Lovejoy and Sainsbury, 2009; Karastergiou et al., 2012). In addition, the lack of direct measures of body composition, such as fat mass, lean mass, or muscle quality, may have contributed to residual confounding.

Skeletal muscle plays a central role in glucose uptake and lipid metabolism. Reduced muscle strength may reflect impaired muscle function or reduced metabolic reserve, which have been linked to insulin resistance and broader metabolic dysregulation (Pedersen and Febbraio, 2012; DeFronzo et al., 2015). These mechanisms may partly explain why low grip strength remained associated with metabolic syndrome among women after BMI adjustment. However, unmeasured lifestyle factors, including detailed dietary patterns, occupational physical activity, and sedentary behavior, may also have influenced the observed associations.

Exploratory mediation analyses did not provide conclusive evidence that BMI statistically mediated the association in either sex. However, because this study was cross-sectional, the mediation findings should be interpreted cautiously, as temporal ordering among grip strength, BMI, and metabolic syndrome cannot be established. Nevertheless, the attenuation observed after BMI adjustment suggests that BMI remains important in interpreting the relationship between absolute grip strength and metabolic risk, particularly in men.

Assessment of grip strength is simple, inexpensive, and feasible in clinical and population-based settings. Identifying individuals with reduced muscle strength may help improve metabolic risk stratification, and strategies to maintain or improve muscle strength may contribute to cardiometabolic risk reduction (Strasser and Pesta, 2013). Our findings further suggest that body size should be considered when interpreting absolute grip strength.

This study has several strengths, including the use of nationally representative KNHANES data, survey-weighted analyses, formal sex interaction testing, sequential BMI adjustment, and adjustment for sociodemographic, behavioral, and selected dietary covariates. Several limitations should also be considered. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal interpretation. Second, metabolic syndrome was used as the outcome rather than incident cardiovascular events. Third, direct body composition measures, such as fat mass, lean mass, or muscle quality, were not available; therefore, residual confounding related to body composition may remain. Fourth, although exploratory mediation analyses were performed, they should be interpreted cautiously because of the observational design and lack of temporal information. Fifth, self-reported lifestyle variables may be subject to measurement error, and selection bias due to exclusion of participants with missing data cannot be excluded. Finally, unmeasured factors, including detailed dietary patterns, occupational physical activity, and sedentary behavior, may have influenced the observed associations, and the restriction to recent KNHANES cycles may limit interpretation of longer-term temporal variation. Despite these limitations, this study contributes to the literature by addressing methodological issues related to formal sex interaction testing and sequential BMI adjustment.

Conclusions

In this nationally representative sample of Korean adults aged ≥40 years, sex-specific associations between grip strength and metabolic syndrome were observed. In men, the association was markedly attenuated after BMI adjustment, whereas in women, low grip strength remained associated with higher odds of metabolic syndrome. These findings indicate that BMI substantially influences the interpretation of absolute grip strength, particularly in men, and suggest that grip strength may provide additional information on metabolic risk beyond body size, especially among women. Longitudinal studies with direct body composition measures are needed to clarify underlying mechanisms and temporal relationships.
