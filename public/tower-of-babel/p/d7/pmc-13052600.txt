Introduction

In evidence-based healthcare, effective decision-making relies on a trustworthy evidence ecosystem. The term ‘evidence ecosystem’ encompasses the entire evidence life cycle, which involves the processes from evidence generation, evidence synthesis, evidence utilisation and evidence-based healthcare practice, as well as updating evidence.1,3 Evidence generation, as the starting point of this cycle, supplies the primary evidence base. It forms the foundation and determines the value of the evidence ecosystem in shaping clinical practice guidelines (CPGs).

In recent decades, considerable efforts have been made within the research community to ensure and reinforce the validity and reliability of evidence generation.4,9 Here, randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are considered the highest standard for generating primary evidence, due to their ability to minimise bias from confounding.10 11 An evidence ecosystem driven by RCTs is thus well-endowed with the ability to inform trustworthy and effective decision-making.12 This paradigm has enabled evidence-based medicine to be widely accepted and adopted in contemporary healthcare practice.

However, the emergence of fraudulent research behaviour opens a Pandora’s box for evidence-based medicine. Increasing publication of fraudulent clinical trials poses a serious threat to the well-established evidence ecosystem.13,16 When such trials enter the evidence synthesis process, they contaminate higher-level evidence, undermining its trustworthiness and ultimately compromising CPGs that incorporate contaminated evidence.

In the VITALITY Study I, we demonstrated that contamination by retracted trials significantly distorts results from evidence syntheses.17 Specifically, it led to nearly 10% of meta-analyses changing direction of the pooled effect and 16% changing statistical interpretation of the significance test. When problematic evidence cascades into CPGs, its impact is amplified nearly nine-fold. The upcoming VITALITY Study II will take this a step further and investigate how such problematic evidence undermines the credibility of guidelines’ recommendations. The study will quantify the associated burden on patient health caused by distorted recommendations. The VITALITY Study II will be structured to answer the following three key questions:

Impact on CPGs: How many CPGs were informed by, and made specific recommendations based on, evidence from retracted trials or meta-analyses that included such trials?

Methods

Design and settings

In alignment with the VITALITY Study I,17 the VITALITY Study II will employ a retrospective cohort design. To start with, a cohort of CPGs that were contaminated by retracted trials and/or meta-analyses that included retracted trials will be established based on forward citation searching (work package (WP) 1). Then, recommendations from these CPGs that used evidence from retracted trials and/or meta-analyses that synthesised these retracted trials will be re-evaluated after removing such evidence, in terms of both their direction and strength of effect (WP2). Finally, the subsequent impact on patient outcomes on a hypothetical population attributable to distorted recommendations will be estimated (WP3). For convenience, we defined evidence from retracted trials and/or meta-analyses that synthesised retracted trials as problematic evidence. Figure 1 presents the design of the study.

WP 1: Impact on CPGs

Identification of CPGs

Citation searching, up to 31 December 2025, will be performed via Google Scholar and Scopus for the 1330 retracted trials as well as 847 systematic reviews that quantitatively synthesised these retracted trials, as identified in the VITALITY Study I.17 We are not planning to search the Guidelines International Network (https://g-i-n.net/) or TRIP (https://www.tripdatabase.com/) databases, because Our VITALITY Study I has verified that searches using Google Scholar and Scopus can successfully identify 92.5% of CPGs.17

From these citations, CPGs will be screened and identified through a standard two-step literature screening process. The initial screening will be based on titles and abstracts and will be performed using the following keywords: ‘clinical practice guideline’, ‘clinical guideline’, ‘guideline’, ‘consensus’, ‘statements’ and ‘recommendations’. Then, a more detailed review of the full texts will be conducted through two groups of team members independently to identify whether the documents are CPGs.

We will include CPGs published in English where one or more recommendations used evidence from retracted trials or meta-analyses that synthesised retracted trials. The CPGs should have focused on healthcare interventions for human beings, regardless of the intervention type, a definition which would include, but is not limited to, drugs, surgery, devices, nutrition, education and psychological interventions. We will adopt the definition of CPGs as ‘statements that include recommendations intended to optimise patient care. They are informed by systematic reviews of evidence and an assessment of the benefits and harms of alternative care options’, based on the statement from the US Institute of Medicine (IOM).18 We will not make a distinction between evidence-based and consensus-based guidelines as suggested by Djulbegovic et al.19 Some CPGs may have been updated several times, and these will be treated as different CPGs.

The judgement of whether a CPG meets the above criteria requires information about the evidence base used in each recommendation. Some CPGs with poor transparency may not provide a detailed summary of the evidence base (a summary table) used for each recommendation; if this occurs, the supporting explanation under the recommendation (if provided) will be used to assess whether problematic data may have been included as part of the supporting evidence. We will exclude CPGs that failed to provide such information as judgement will not be possible in this case. In addition, CPGs that cited but did not use problematic evidence (ie, evidence from retracted trials and/or meta-analyses that synthesised these retracted trials) will not be considered.

Data extraction

Since the development of CPGs involves two important steps, namely, evidence summary and recommendation formation based on existing evidence (ie, evidence to decision),20,22 two sources of data will be involved in this study: (1) baseline characteristics of the guidelines and (2) information on the evidence summary and specific guideline recommendations.

For baseline characteristics of the guideline, the following data will be collected: date of release, version number, organisation(s) that developed the guideline (eg, society), conflict of interest, source of funding, number of citations and clinical topics (conditions or diseases) defined by Medical Subject Headings (MeSH).

Regarding information on the evidence summary and specific guideline recommendations, the following data will be collected: (1) total number of recommendations and the number of recommendations that used problematic evidence; (2) total number of evidence sources used for each recommendation as well as the number of meta-analyses and RCTs among the evidence sources for each recommendation; (3) number of problematic evidence items among each recommendation and (4) detailed recommendation content, recommendation strength (if provided) and certainty of overall evidence (if provided) for those recommendations that used problematic evidence.

Data collection of baseline information will be conducted independently by two groups of well-trained student volunteers. These volunteers will be recruited via online social media and required to participate in at least two rounds of training. Only those who achieve 95% accuracy will be allowed to participate in formal data collection.

In addition, the large language model tool, Claude (V.4.5), will be tested in a small sample (20 CPGs randomly selected from the VITALITY Study I), for its accuracy of data extraction. An alternative data collection method will be adopted if the assessment of Claude shows non-inferiority in performance compared with humans (ie, reaching an accuracy of 95% and above), again, in an independent ‘double extraction’ manner, with Claude representing one group of reviewers and human volunteers as another group. We chose Claude because our recent empirical study showed that it outperformed several other tools that we tested in data extraction tasks.23 A structured data collection form based on an Excel Sheet (Microsoft, version 2016) for each data source will be developed in advance, with input from all core members of the VITALITY Study II team.

WP 2: Integrity of recommendations

Direction of the contaminated recommendations

For each recommendation that used problematic evidence, its direction will be judged by two groups of methodologists and clinicians separately based on the recommendation’s content extracted, as favourable or against for an intervention.24 25 Any disagreements will be discussed to reach a consensus.

Claude (V.4.5) will be tested on the same sample to assess its performance in identifying the direction of the recommendation and will be adopted if the assessment shows Claude’s performance is non-inferior to humans, with Claude acting as one group of reviewers and humans as another.

The direction of the recommendation will be merged with related information extracted in WP1, including the strength of the recommendations, the certainty of the evidence and a summary of the evidence base.

Repair the evidence base

In order to estimate the potential impact of problematic evidence on the recommendations, we will repair the evidence base for each recommendation by removing the problematic evidence. For retracted trials within the evidence base, we will remove such trials directly. For meta-analyses that synthesised retracted trials, the pooled effects will be repaired after removing these trials (as done in VITALITY Study I), and the certainty of the repaired meta-analyses will be evaluated using the same approach as the original guideline committee. This will be done by a group of experienced methodologists in evidence-based medicine.

As the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach is the most frequently used system (accounting for 52%) for grading the evidence in CPGs,26 we provide details on how to re-evaluate the certainty of the repaired meta-analysis. In terms of GRADE, the removal of retracted trials in a meta-analysis is expected to result in potential changes to inconsistency, imprecision, risk of bias and publication bias for the totality of the evidence. Therefore, the certainty of the evidence of the repaired meta-analysis will be estimated by the changes in these four domains, with the certainty of the original meta-analysis as the starting point. For example, suppose the certainty of the original meta-analysis is moderate, and the removal of retracted trials led to a substantial increase in the publication bias that should be downgraded by one level, then the certainty of the repaired meta-analysis is estimated as low.

We set the following rules to trigger a change of the level of certainty. For inconsistency, a change of the amount of between-study heterogeneity for one category (eg, mild to moderate) triggers a change in the certainty for one level, and two categories trigger two levels, based on the categorisation in the Cochrane Handbook.27 A similar rule will apply to publication bias, based on the LFK index, with a change that crosses the threshold of ±1 triggers a change in certainty of one level, and ±1.5, of two levels.28 For imprecision, we will focus on the changes of the boundary of the CI as well as the magnitude of the effect size, with a change of 50% triggering a change in certainty; see figure 2 for details.29 In the Core GRADE guideline, rating risk of bias for the totality of the evidence relies on whether exclusion of retracted trials impacts the effects30—exactly the rule for rating the imprecision—and therefore we merged these two domains (ie, impression, risk of bias) into one for consideration.31

Integrity of guideline recommendations

The direction and strength of the recommendation will be re-evaluated based on the updated evidence base, to see whether the recommendations were distorted or not. The extent of impact of problematic evidence on the recommendations will be categorised as follows: (1) Unlikely to be distorted, wherein the updated evidence base aligns with the original and does not support a change in the direction or strength of the recommendation. (2) Possibly to be distorted, wherein the updated evidence base shows some inconsistencies that introduce uncertainty, making it unclear whether a change in direction or strength is required; (3) Likely to be distorted, wherein the updated evidence base is fundamentally inconsistent with the original, compelling a change in the direction or the strength of the recommendation. The details of the criteria for a judgement are as follows:

Direction. The direction of a recommendation indicates whether the intervention is favoured or not. Determining whether this direction should change depends on the highest-level evidence in both the original and updated evidence bases. A change will be issued when the dominant evidence (ie, evidence with the highest level of certainty) from each base conflicts in direction; an uncertain status will be issued when the dominant evidence agrees, but inconsistencies exist in the lower-level evidence; a no-change status will be issued only when all evidence is consistent.

Direction. The direction of a recommendation indicates whether the intervention is favoured or not. Determining whether this direction should change depends on the highest-level evidence in both the original and updated evidence bases. A change will be issued when the dominant evidence (ie, evidence with the highest level of certainty) from each base conflicts in direction; an uncertain status will be issued when the dominant evidence agrees, but inconsistencies exist in the lower-level evidence; a no-change status will be issued only when all evidence is consistent.

Strength. The strength of a recommendation indicates confidence in the balance between desirable and undesirable effects. A strong recommendation implicates the guideline committee is confident that the desirable outweighs the undesirable consequences, and most individuals should receive the recommended course of action; while a weak/conditional recommendation implies less confidence in the balance, with the recommended course of action being applied to most individuals, while often engaging shared decision making with patient’ preferences and clinicians’ experience.32 Therefore, when the updated evidence base supports a 20% reduction in desirable consequences (or downgraded certainty) or a 20% increase in undesirable consequences (or upgraded certainty), we will rate down the strength; while when updated evidence base supports a 20% increase in desirable consequences (or upgraded certainty) or a 20% reduction in undesirable consequences (or downgraded certainty), we will rate up the strength. Again, the dominant evidence will be used for the evaluation.

Strength. The strength of a recommendation indicates confidence in the balance between desirable and undesirable effects. A strong recommendation implicates the guideline committee is confident that the desirable outweighs the undesirable consequences, and most individuals should receive the recommended course of action; while a weak/conditional recommendation implies less confidence in the balance, with the recommended course of action being applied to most individuals, while often engaging shared decision making with patient’ preferences and clinicians’ experience.32 Therefore, when the updated evidence base supports a 20% reduction in desirable consequences (or downgraded certainty) or a 20% increase in undesirable consequences (or upgraded certainty), we will rate down the strength; while when updated evidence base supports a 20% increase in desirable consequences (or upgraded certainty) or a 20% reduction in undesirable consequences (or downgraded certainty), we will rate up the strength. Again, the dominant evidence will be used for the evaluation.

One special case may occur that after removing problematic evidence, there would be no evidence existing for the certain recommendation. It is reasonable to assume the case as ‘Likely to be distorted’. The above process will be performed by the aforementioned panel of experienced methodologists, and to ensure the quality of the re-evaluation process, each evaluator will be required to record detailed reasons aforementioned.

WP 3: consequences for healthcare

Distorted recommendations

Recommendations categorised as ‘Likely to be distorted’ will be analysed under potential changes in both direction and strength. The direction has two options (change or no change), while the strength has three (upgrade, downgrade or unchanged). This yields five distinct scenarios for impact estimation:

Scenario 1: A different direction with upgraded strength;

Scenario 1: A different direction with upgraded strength;

Scenario 2: A different direction with downgraded strength;

Scenario 2: A different direction with downgraded strength;

Scenario 3: A different direction with the same strength;

Scenario 3: A different direction with the same strength;

Scenario 4: The same direction with upgraded strength;

Scenario 4: The same direction with upgraded strength;

Scenario 5: The same direction with downgraded strength.

Scenario 5: The same direction with downgraded strength.

The implications of modifying a recommendation’s direction are clear and significant, potentially leading to suboptimal patient management with an unfavourable benefit-harm profile. The impact of a change in recommendation strength, however, is more nuanced. We posit that a change in strength meaningfully affects patient health primarily when it reflects a shift in the balance of desirable and undesirable outcomes, for two key reasons: (1) Strength changes can originate from altered certainty of evidence or a revised evaluation of outcomes; (2) A change based solely on evidence certainty, while informative, may not materially alter the net benefit for most patients, as both strong and conditional recommendations remain broadly applicable. Therefore, this study will evaluate the health impact across the above five scenarios, considering a change in strength only when it is driven by a reassessment of patient-important outcomes (ie, fewer/more desirable vs undesirable consequences).

Health impact of distorted recommendations

The health impact on patients will be measured by two metrics: the number of expected benefit loss (NBL) and the number of expected harm increment (NHI) per 100 000 patients, resulting from adherence to distorted recommendations. The NBL will quantify the consequence of a distorted recommendation: it will reflect a net loss of benefit when a favoured course of action is applied, whereas a net gain of benefit is forfeited when a discouraging recommendation against the use of an intervention is made. The NHI will quantify the miscalculation in expected harms: it will reflect a net increase in harm when a mistakenly favoured intervention is applied, whereas a net reduction in harm is forfeited when a discouraging recommendation against the use of an intervention is made.

The NBL will be estimated as the risk difference (RD) from the updated evidence base, as RDbenifits×100 000. The NHI will be estimated using the same approach, as RDharms×100 000. For example, a recommendation from the guideline favours the intervention and is based on one meta-analysis with retracted trials, with a RD of 0.1 for the benefits effect, and the repaired meta-analysis by removing the retracted trials resulted in an RD of −0.05. The NBL per 100 000 patients will then be estimated as (−0.05) *100 000=−5000. Now suppose the favoured intervention is expected to have less harms by the guideline committee, with an RD of −0.02 for harm effects; however, the repaired meta-analysis after removing the retracted trials resulted in an RD of 0.01. The NHI will then be estimated as 0.01*100 000=1000. Again, we will estimate the RD based on the dominant evidence (ie, evidence with the highest level) within the updated evidence base of the recommendation.

Outcomes

Aligning with the three basic WPs, the primary outcomes of VITALITY Study II will be: (1) the frequency and proportion of recommendations that used problematic evidence within each eligible CPG; (2) the proportion of specific recommendations whose direction or strength was distorted among those that used problematic evidence and (3) the healthcare burden attributable to those recommendations distorted by problematic evidence. The secondary outcome of interest will be the potential relationship between the proportion of problematic evidence and the total number of evidence sources within each recommendation and the risk of direction or strength distortion.

Statistical plan

Baseline characteristics of the eligible CPGs will be summarised using descriptive statistics such as frequency, proportion and means with SDs or IQRs, depending on the nature of the data. To assess the data variability, we will consider reporting the descriptive statistics by grouping the data into possible explanatory groups. The proportion and CI of the primary outcomes will be computed on the logit-transformed scale after accounting for clustering within a CPG and then back-transformed for reporting.33

We will fit a generalised linear mixed model to investigate the association between the proportion of problematic evidence among each recommendation and the likelihood of direction or strength distortion.34 To account for the anticipated non-linear relationship, we will apply a restricted cubic spline with three knots, following Harrell’s recommended percentiles.35 36 Potential confounders, such as guideline characteristics, will be identified via a directed acyclic graph and will be adjusted in the regression model directly.37

Data analyses will be undertaken using the Stata SE/V.16 program (StataCorp), with a two-sided p value of 0.05 accepted as the threshold for the test of significance. Data visualisation will be undertaken using Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft, Washington).

Patient and public involvement

Since this study addresses an issue of direct importance to patient health, we will facilitate patient and public involvement (PPI). We will recruit individuals who participated in the development of the CPGs and officials of the public health department as PPI. The PPI, when recruited successfully, will contribute to the interpretation and dissemination of the findings.

Discussion

The conduct of the VITALITY Study series will help clinical trialists, systematic review authors, guideline developers, decision-makers and methodologists in evidence-based medicine better understand the harms of problematic trials on the evidence ecosystem and the adverse consequences on current healthcare practice. More importantly, the VITALITY Study series will also seek valid solutions to address the threats posed by problematic trials on these matters. We believe this series will have a positive impact on the way forward to improve the trustworthiness of the current and future medical evidence ecosystem.

The VITALITY Study II is distinct from VITALITY Study I because it focuses mainly on the impact of problematic evidence on CPGs and the associated healthcare burden when distorted recommendations are applied to patients. There is still a small overlap between VITALITY Study I and II, since in the third part of VITALITY Study I, we tracked 68 systematic reviews with meta-analyses that were substantially distorted by retracted trials for potential CPGs that used the evidence from them.17 The aim was to establish the contamination chains and to understand how the problematic evidence cascaded via the chains. In WP1 of the VITALITY Study II, we will continue to track all 847 systematic reviews with meta-analysis that included retracted trials, as well as the 1330 retracted trials, for all CPGs that used such problematic evidence. The aim is to figure out the total number of CPGs that might be affected by problematic evidence.

There are several limitations that could be expected in terms of the design of our study. First, retracted trials represent only the tip of the iceberg for problematic trials.38 As Studd et al pointed out, ‘retracting untrustworthy, problematic academic publications can take years, and most often never occurs’.39 It has been estimated that as many as 20%–30% of the clinical trials might be problematic.40 This means the impact of problematic trials on systematic reviews, CPGs and healthcare practice will be underestimated when only considering retracted trials and systematic reviews that synthesised such trials. Second, it should be very clear that the problematic trials that pose a threat to the evidence ecosystem are those that are fraudulent trials (ie, falsification and fabrication in research data). While not all retracted trials involve fraud, for example, some were due to fake peer review processes, ethical issues, etc. Therefore, the VITALITY Study makes a strong pragmatic assumption that all retracted trials are fraudulent, based on the fact that more than 70% of retracted trials involve fraudulent data issues.17 Even so, a sensitivity or subgroup analysis makes no sense. This is because the reasons for retraction were not always transparently or consistently reported, with the terminology being highly varied, and there may have been multiple factors behind retraction rather than a sole categorical judgement.41 Despite these limitations, there is currently no better solution to address this, until such time that fraudulent trials are all discovered, retracted and labelled clearly.

In summary, problematic trials pose a serious threat to the integrity of evidence-based practice, and a massive effort is needed to identify and address their potential adverse impact on the trustworthiness of the evidence ecosystem.

Ethics and dissemination

Formal ethical approval is not necessary for this study as it does not involve human or animal participants, nor does it involve the collection of primary data. We will disseminate the findings through publication in peer-reviewed journals and, whenever possible, presentations at academic conferences.
