Endoscopic mitral and tricuspid valve repair in a patient with dextrocardia, hypoplastic right lung, and scimitar syndrome: a case report
Authors: Victoria Ennen, Daniel Höfer, Felix Nägele, Markus Theurl, Nikolaos Bonaros, Andreas Metzner, Sebastian Rosch, Mohamed Metwally Abdelaal, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe treatment of primary mitral and secondary tricuspid regurgitation (MR, TR) in the setting of lung agenesis poses major technical challenges, and data on the feasibility of endoscopic tricuspid valve repair in adult congenital anatomy remain limited.Case summaryWe report the case of a 76-year-old male with severe primary MR due to Barlow’s disease, torrential secondary TR, biatrial dilatation, persistent atrial fibrillation, and post-capillary pulmonary hypertension. Anatomical anomalies included dextrocardia, hypoplastic right lung, and scimitar vein syndrome. The patient had a history of surgical correction of an aortic coarctation at the age of 32 with residual stenosis but no proof of residual gradients in invasive assessment. Transcatheter repair was deemed unsuitable due to the massive dextrocardia, extensive leaflet pathology, and annular dilatation. The patient underwent endoscopic mitral and tricuspid repair with dual arterial and venous cannulation to avoid malperfusion of the upper body during extracorporeal circulation. Mitral valve repair included a surgical edge-to-edge repair, along with an implantation of an annuloplasty ring; secondary TR was repaired by ring annuloplasty too. Postoperatively, the patient experienced prolonged weaning and renal support. He was ultimately transferred to rehabilitation for uneventful recovery. Discharge echocardiography revealed good surgical results for the mitral valve and a residual moderate-to severe TR.DiscussionThe present case highlights that an endoscopic approach for the treatment of mitral and tricuspid valve pathologies can be used in selected patients with dextrocardia. Tricuspid annuloplasty may reduce but not abolish tricuspid regurgitation in a combination of primary, congenital, and secondary aetiologies.Keywords: Case report, Dextrocardia, Scimitar syndrome, Functional tricuspid regurgitation, Minimally invasive valve surgery, Tricuspid annuloplasty

Introduction

Secondary tricuspid valve disease is often multifactorial; however, congenital anomalies in combination with primary mitral disease represent a rare but particularly challenging subset due to their impact on annular geometry, atrioventricular alignment, and surgical exposure. Although endoscopic valve surgery is well established for degenerative mitral and tricuspid pathology and has demonstrated excellent durability even in complex repair scenarios,1,2 experience with endoscopic tricuspid valve repair in adults with congenital disease remains limited.

Congenital variants such as dextrocardia, hypoplastic right lung, and anomalous pulmonary venous return, including scimitar syndrome, can significantly alter the orientation of the atrioventricular valves and the relationship between cardiac chambers.3,4 These abnormalities pose technical challenges for surgical approaches by limiting visualization of the mitral and tricuspid valve and modifying the anatomical prerequisites for successful repair.

Dextrocardia, a rare malposition, may coexist with scimitar syndrome, characterized by anomalous drainage of the right pulmonary veins into the inferior vena cava, less commonly into the hepatic or portal veins and is often associated with right-lung hypoplasia or additional congenital defects.3,4 The combination of massive dextrocardia and aortic coarctation is particularly uncommon5 and would likely not provide sufficient exposure via conventional median sternotomy. Published case reports have demonstrated that endoscopic repair of both mitral and tricuspid valves is feasible and safe in patients with dextrocardia, with low morbidity and satisfactory functional recovery.6,7 They emphasize meticulous preoperative planning and optimal exposure as key to achieving a successful repair.

Case presentation

We report the case of a 76-year-old male patient with massive dextroposition of the heart due to a hypoplastic right lung (upper- and mid-lobe agenesia) and a scimitar vein, who was referred to our centre due to severe primary mitral regurgitation (MR) caused by Barlow’s disease, as well as severe secondary tricuspid regurgitation (TR) and concomitant massive biatrial dilatation (Figure 1). The patient had remained oligosymptomatic for years and was referred after clinical deterioration (NYHA Class III) with progressive right ventricular dilatation. At admission, the patient presented with mildly reduced general condition, a holosystolic heart murmur and an elevated NT-proBNP at 4106 ng/L. Preoperative echocardiography demonstrated torrential TR (proximal isovelocity surface area (PISA)-derived effective regurgitant orifice area (EROA) 1.67 cm2; regurgitant volume 124 mL) with severe right atrial enlargement (71 mm), the quantification of which was technically challenging due to cardiac malposition and persistent atrial fibrillation (Figure 2). Right heart catheterization confirmed a post-capillary pulmonary hypertension (mPAP 29 mmHg, PCWP 20 mmHg) with elevated right atrial pressure (mean 15 mmHg) and a pulmonary vascular resistance of 1.9 Wood units. The patient had a previous history of surgical aortic coarctation repair via left thoracotomy 44 years ago. Current examinations showed a non-relevant residual coarctation (maximum gradient 7 mmHg, minimum diameter 15 mm) with post-stenotic dilatation of the descending aorta (51 mm) (Figure 3). Due to the agenesis of the right upper and middle pulmonary lobes and the hypoplasia of the lower lobe, a small scimitar vein, connected to the subdiaphragmatic part of the inferior vena cava with a minimal shunt volume, was revealed.

Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair was deemed unsuitable due to the inability to perform peri-procedural echocardiographic guiding in the setting of dextrocardia and right lung agenesis. Furthermore, the presence of severe primary degenerative mitral regurgitation with concomitant severe tricuspid regurgitation favoured a surgical approach, allowing a combined valve repair.

Surgical exposure via median sternotomy was excluded due to the massive dextrocardia, and an endoscopic right thoracotomy approach was chosen to allow exposition of the atrioventricular valves. No relevant mediastinal adhesions were encountered despite prior coarctation repair. Cardiopulmonary bypass was established via dual arterial cannulation (right carotid and femoral artery) and venous cannulation (right jugular and femoral vein) to avoid upper body hypoperfusion during the non-pulsatile flow of extracorporeal circulation.

Due to the massive right atrial enlargement and the absent right pulmonary veins, a right atriotomy was performed along with a transseptal approach to expose the mitral valve. The latter was presented with a typical Barlow’s pathology with a posterior leaflet prolapse due to chordal elongation and was treated by an edge-to-edge repair (A2–P2), closure of an indentation in P2, followed by the implantation of a 38-mm semi-rigid, open annuloplasty ring (Physio Flex, Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA, USA).

The tricuspid valve was addressed with a restrictive annuloplasty using a 34-mm multi-planar semi-rigid annuloplasty ring (Physio Tricuspid Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA, USA) (Figure 4). The fragile and thin ascending aorta sustained a tear during cardioplegia needle removal and was successfully repaired with a bovine pericardial patch. The patient was weaned from bypass and transferred to ICU with stable haemodynamics. Intraoperative TEE showed a moderate to severe residual TR, which was tolerated given the complexity of the procedure.

(A) Postoperative CT-scan showing the tricuspid valve annuloplasty (red arrow) and (B) thoracoscopic approach via a right mini-thoracotomy.

Postoperatively, after a prolonged weaning period, the patient was successfully extubated on postoperative day (POD) 6. Continuous hemofiltration was required between POD 3 and 9 due to acute renal injury. Inotrope support was gradually weaned, while episodes of tachycardic atrial fibrillation were effectively controlled with amiodarone.

The postoperative TTE showed a normal left ventricular function, a competent mitral valve without signs of residual regurgitation, and a mean diastolic gradient of 4 mmHg. The torrential TR was reduced to moderate-to severe. On POD 22, he was transferred to an external facility for continued rehabilitation; his subsequent stay was uneventful, and he was discharged home approximately 3 weeks later. At 9-month clinical follow-up, the patient was in NYHA functional Class I. Echocardiography demonstrated stable mitral valve function with a residual minor regurgitation and a mean diastolic gradient of 3 mmHg. Tricuspid regurgitation deteriorated to severe, with a regurgitant volume of 55 mL, a PISA radius of 10 mm, and an ERO area of 0.94 cm2 (Figure 5).

(A) postoperative transthoracic Doppler echocardiography of the tricuspid valve showing severe residual regurgitation, (B, C, D) postoperative echocardiography of the mitral valve showing minor residual regurgitation.

Discussion

This case demonstrates that concomitant mitral and tricuspid repair via mini-thoracotomy is feasible in adult patients with massive dextrocardia and inability for cardiac access through sternotomy or transcatheter techniques. Achieving adequate exposure and perfusion was particularly challenging: the abnormal cardiac orientation limited direct visualization, a conventional median sternotomy would not have provided sufficient access, and residual aortic coarctation necessitated individualized perfusion management.

An endoscopic right thoracotomy and 3D visualization were therefore chosen, providing optimal access to both atrioventricular valves. The literature supports this approach as a safe and effective alternative in complex anatomical settings, offering reduced surgical trauma and favourable postoperative recovery compared with conventional sternotomy.8–10 Dual arterial cannulation ensured balanced upper and lower body perfusion and mitigated the risk of regional hypoperfusion under cardiopulmonary bypass, in line with current STS/EACTS recommendations.11,12

Valvular management was equally complex. We opted for a leaflet-based treatment of the mitral valve, as due to the cardiac malposition, reliable assessment of artificial chordae length would not have been feasible. The pathology was related to a bileaflet prolapse on the basis of a myxomatous, degenerated mitral valve. The combination of edge-to-edge repair and annular stabilization by a large-sized ring was deemed to be appropriate for the treatment of bileaflet prolapse. As expected in myxomatous degeneration, the quality of the tissue was suitable for an edge-to-edge repair, which was performed in a typical way, connecting the mid-portion of A2 and P2, creating a double-orifice mitral valve. This technique has been shown to yield excellent long-term results in this patient subgroup.13

The tricuspid regurgitation was predominantly functional, driven by long-standing volume overload, pulmonary hypertension, and right atrial enlargement leading to annular dilatation and missing leaflet coaptation. Severe dextrocardia, massive right atrial enlargement, and haemodynamic stress from lung hypoplasia, anomalous venous return, and residual coarctation likely further increased right ventricular pressure load. Given this, restrictive annuloplasty was selected as the primary tricuspid repair strategy. Leading to a TR reduction from torrential to moderate to severe. Due to the anatomical complexity related to the malalignment between papillary muscles and the tricuspid annulus, the use of subvalvular techniques was deferred. Conversion to a bioprosthetic valve replacement was deemed inappropriate due to the extremely high risk of thrombosis in a patient with a massive right atrial dilatation. The persistence of TR postoperatively likely reflects the anatomical malposition, the irreversible status of right ventricular remodelling and sustained pressure overload, which limited the efficacy of annuloplasty despite annular stabilization. In this setting, maintaining annular geometry and preventing further right-heart dilation were realistic and appropriate surgical goals. Although successful tricuspid valve repair has been reported in patients with dextrocardia, reports involving similarly complex anatomical and pathophysiological constellations remain limited.7 Postoperative management focuses on optimized medical therapy to control symptoms and reduce volume overload. For high-risk patients, transcatheter tricuspid interventions may be considered in a staged approach, although in our case, such a procedure was not forced due to the imaging limitation.14

In summary, this case illustrates that endoscopic combined mitral and tricuspid valve surgery is technically feasible in selected patients with massive dextrocardia due to lung agenesis. Abolishment of MR and reduction of TR can be achieved, though the success of tricuspid repair is mainly subject to the extent of cardiac displacement and right ventricular remodelling.
