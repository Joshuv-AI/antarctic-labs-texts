The relationship between the chronological phases of diffuse alveolar damage and clinical outcomes in acute respiratory distress syndrome
Authors: Ko-Wei Chang, Chi-Hsien Huang, Shaw-Woei Leu, Tzu-Hsuan Chiu, Hsin-Yueh Fang, Huang-Pin Wu, Li-Pang Chuang, Wei-Hsun Chen, Chien-Hung Chiu, Ning-Hung Chen, Chung-Chi Huang, Han-Chung Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiffuse alveolar damage (DAD) represents the hallmark histopathological feature of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS); however, its prognostic significance remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the associations between the chronological phases of histologically confirmed DAD and clinical outcomes in patients with ARDS.MethodsIn this retrospective study, we included patients diagnosed with ARDS who underwent open lung biopsy between January 2002 and December 2025 or transbronchial lung cryobiopsy between January 2017 and December 2025. Rapid progression was defined as the presence of the proliferative phase within 7 days or the fibrotic phase within 21 days from ARDS diagnosis to lung biopsy.ResultsA total of 90 patients were included in the analysis. No significant differences were observed in 28-day mortality (36.5% vs. 43.5%, p = 0.556) or 60-day mortality (63.5% vs. 69.9%, p = 0.601) between patients in the proliferative and fibrotic phases. In contrast, patients with rapid progression demonstrated significantly higher 28-day mortality (47.9% vs. 23.8%, p = 0.018) and 60-day mortality (72.9% vs. 50.0%, p = 0.025) than those without rapid progression. Multivariable analysis identified rapid progression as an independent predictor of 60-day mortality (hazard ratio, 2.274; p = 0.014). Furthermore, patients with rapid progression had significantly lower PaCO2 levels prior to lung biopsy (43.4 ± 11.2 vs. 51.2 ± 21.1 mmHg, p = 0.013). Pneumothorax was the most common biopsy-related complication, occurring in 17.8% of patients.ConclusionRapid progression of DAD was associated with significantly poorer clinical outcomes, and patients with rapid progression had significantly lower PaCO2 before lung biopsy. Larger controlled studies and additional physiological investigations are warranted to validate these findings.

Background

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a form of non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema characterized by a severe inflammatory response that results in diffuse alveolar injury [1, 2]. The hallmark histopathological feature of ARDS is diffuse alveolar damage (DAD), which is characterized by the formation of hyaline membranes—dense, eosinophilic, amorphous deposits composed of cellular debris, plasma proteins, and surfactant components [3]. ARDS typically progresses through three distinct histopathological phases. The exudative phase, occurring within the first week, is characterized by interstitial and alveolar edema, necrosis of type I pneumocytes, and hyaline membrane formation. The proliferative phase generally develops between days 7 and 21 and is marked by hyperplasia of type II pneumocytes, proliferation of interstitial myofibroblasts, and early organization of intra-alveolar exudates. In some patients, a fibrotic phase may develop after 21 days, characterized by dense collagen deposition, microcystic remodeling, and varying degrees of interstitial thickening [2, 4].

Given the ongoing controversy regarding the histopathological definition of ARDS and concerns about procedural risks, lung biopsy is not required for diagnosis. Instead, ARDS is diagnosed exclusively on the basis of clinical criteria defined by the Berlin Definition [5, 6]. Although DAD is considered the pathological hallmark of ARDS, it is identified in only 13%–56% of patients who meet the clinical criteria for ARDS and undergo lung biopsy [7–11]. A meta-analysis reported a pooled sensitivity of 0.46 for DAD and concluded that histopathological evidence of DAD is not a reliable diagnostic marker for ARDS [12].

In a previous study conducted by our group, the presence of DAD on open lung biopsy was identified as an independent predictor of hospital mortality in patients with ARDS [10]. In the present study, we specifically included patients with histologically confirmed DAD and examined the associations between the chronological phases of DAD and corresponding clinical characteristics.

Methods

Study patients and data collection

This study was conducted at the Linkou branch of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, a tertiary referral center in Taiwan with 56 medical intensive care unit (ICU) beds. We included all patients who underwent open lung biopsy between January 2002 and December 2025 or transbronchial lung cryobiopsy (TBLC) between January 2017 and December 2025. All patients fulfilled the Berlin Definition criteria for ARDS [5] and required invasive mechanical ventilation at the time of biopsy. Patients were excluded if lung biopsy was performed more than 28 days after ARDS diagnosis or if the biopsy indication was unrelated to diffuse lung disease (e.g., evaluation of a lung mass or endobronchial lesion).

We extracted demographic characteristics, laboratory results, ventilator parameters, and clinical outcomes from electronic medical records. Laboratory data were collected at the time of ARDS diagnosis. Ventilator settings and arterial blood gas measurements were recorded at three time points: at ARDS diagnosis, on the day of biopsy, and one day after biopsy. Clinical outcomes included 28-day mortality, 60-day mortality, ventilator-free days within 28 days, ventilator-free days within 60 days, ICU length of stay, and hospital length of stay; all outcomes were measured from the day of ARDS diagnosis.

Lung biopsy

Informed consent was obtained from the patients’ family members prior to the procedure. Whether and when to perform the biopsy would be decided by the responsible intensivists. Lung biopsy was indicated in cases of ARDS with no identifiable etiology or failure to respond adequately to initial treatment, when previous tracheal aspiration or bronchoalveolar lavage cultures were negative, or when biopsy results could influence treatment decisions, such as the initiation of corticosteroid therapy, or in cases with suspicion of malignancy or graft-versus-host disease in hematology patients. The biopsy site was determined based on the presence of new or progressive lesions identified on chest computed tomography imaging.

Bronchoscopy-guided TBLC was initiated in January 2017. Since 2018, all biopsies have been performed using cryobiopsy, except when a definitive diagnosis could not be established, in which case open lung biopsy was performed.

Open lung biopsy

All open lung biopsies were performed by thoracic surgeons in consultation with the on-duty intensivists. The procedure was carried out via video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery or a 5-cm thoracotomy, depending on the surgeon’s preference or the patient’s clinical condition. In unstable patients—such as those requiring a fraction of inspired oxygen (FIO2) of 1.0 and positive end-expiratory pressure above 12 cmH2O—the biopsy was performed at the ICU bedside to minimize the risks associated with transfer to the operating room.

Transbronchial lung cryobiopsy

The detailed methodology of our bronchoscopy-guided TBLC has been described previously [13]. In brief, a flexible bronchoscope (BF-P240 or BF-40; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was introduced via the endotracheal tube to reach the targeted bronchopulmonary segment. A 1.9-mm flexible cryoprobe (Erbokryo CA, Erbe, Germany) was then advanced through the working channel, using carbon dioxide as the cryogen to achieve a tip temperature of − 70 °C. The cryoprobe was advanced until resistance was encountered and then withdrawn 1–1.5 cm. Cooling was applied for 4–6 s before the bronchoscope and cryoprobe were withdrawn simultaneously. All procedures were performed at the ICU bedside, with patients sedated using midazolam or propofol.

Pathology findings

All biopsy specimens were reviewed by pathologists at our hospital, and the corresponding written pathology reports were collected. Features of the exudative phase included alveolar edema, hyaline membranes, capillary congestion, inflammatory infiltration, and necrosis of type I pneumocytes. Features of the proliferative phase included organizing pneumonia, hyperplasia of type II pneumocytes, and fibroblast proliferation. Features of the fibrotic phase included interstitial and alveolar fibrosis.

Because the ARDS phase is highly dependent on the timing of lung biopsy, which could not be standardized across patients, we employed an alternative classification method. Patients were divided into those with rapid progression and those without rapid progression. Based on previous studies [4, 14], we defined patients with rapid progression as those exhibiting the proliferative phase within 7 days or the fibrotic phase within 21 days from ARDS diagnosis to lung biopsy.

Mechanical ventilator settings

Ventilator settings in the ICUs were adjusted by intensivists and respiratory therapists according to the ARDSnet lung-protective strategy. All patients were ventilated in pressure control mode. For each patient, ventilator settings with the longest daily duration of use were recorded, and arterial blood gas measurements were obtained under these specific conditions. Statistical analyses

Nominal variables are presented as percentages, and differences between groups were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test. Continuous variables are reported as means ± standard deviations. One-way analysis of variance was used to compare continuous variables across the three phases, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons between the proliferative and fibrotic phases. Depending on data distribution, either the independent Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test was applied to compare patients with rapid versus non-rapid progression. The paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to evaluate changes in ventilator parameters before and after lung biopsy. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method, with group differences assessed by the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted to identify predictors of mortality; variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. Similarly, univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of rapid progression in the DAD phase, with factors having p < 0.05 in univariate analysis entered into the multivariate model. We used receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the Youden index to determine optimal biopsy day cutoffs for predicting 28-day mortality. We used Cohen’s d to evaluate the effect size of the ventilatory variables. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

General characteristics

During the study period, 121 patients underwent open lung biopsy, of whom 81 (66.9%) were diagnosed with DAD. All patients underwent lung biopsy only once. In comparison, 52 patients underwent TBLC, and DAD was identified in 11 patients (21.2%). The proportion of DAD diagnoses was significantly higher in patients who underwent open lung biopsy than in those who underwent TBLC (p < 0.01). Two patients were excluded from further analysis because lung biopsy was performed more than 28 days after the diagnosis of ARDS. Patients with exudative, proliferative, and fibrotic phases

Histopathological analysis revealed the exudative phase in 4 patients, the proliferative phase in 63 patients, and the fibrotic phase in 23 patients. The interval from ARDS diagnosis to lung biopsy differed significantly among the three phases (1.5 ± 1.7 vs. 8.0 ± 5.2 vs. 9.7 ± 8.3 days; p = 0.026). Notably, half of the patients in the exudative phase had underlying hematological malignancy (Table 1).

No patients in the exudative phase died during hospital admission. Among patients in the proliferative phase, 28-day and 60-day mortality rates were 36.5% and 63.5%, respectively. Corresponding mortality rates in the fibrotic phase were 43.5% and 69.9%. Because the exudative phase contained very few patients, thereby limiting meaningful comparisons across phases, we conducted a post hoc analysis restricted to patients in the proliferative and fibrotic phases. Post hoc analysis showed no significant differences in mortality between the proliferative and fibrotic phases at 28 days (p = 0.556) or 60 days (p = 0.601) (Table 2). The Kaplan–Meier survival curve is shown in Fig. 2A. Patients in the exudative phase had higher ventilator-free days at days 28 and 60 (17.3 and 49.3 days, respectively). However, ICU and hospital lengths of stay did not differ significantly among the three groups. Similarly, no significant differences in ventilator-free days, ICU stay, or hospital stay were observed between patients in the proliferative and fibrotic phases (Table 2). Patients with rapid and non-rapid progression

Among patients in the proliferative phase, 27 (42.9%) underwent lung biopsy within 7 days of ARDS diagnosis. In the fibrotic phase, 21 (84.0%) patients underwent lung biopsy within 21 days of diagnosis. Patients with rapid progression had a significantly shorter duration from ARDS onset to biopsy than those without rapid progression (5.4 ± 5.0 vs. 11.3 ± 6.0 days, p < 0.001). However, there was no significant difference in the duration from ARDS onset to biopsy between 60-day survivors and non-survivors (8.1 ± 6.0 vs. 8.2 ± 6.4 days, p = 0.914). Patients with rapid progression had significantly higher mortality at day 28 (47.9% vs. 23.8%, p = 0.018) and day 60 (72.9% vs. 50.0%, p = 0.025) (Table 3). Kaplan–Meier survival curves are shown in Fig. 2B, with a log-rank test p value of 0.004. No significant differences were observed between patients with rapid and non-rapid progression in ventilator-free days, ICU length of stay among survivors, or hospital length of stay among survivors (Table 3). Among patients in the proliferative phase, those with rapid progression had higher mortality at day 28 (48.1% vs. 27.8%, p = 0.097) and day 60 (74.1% vs. 55.6%, p = 0.131), although the differences were not statistically significant. Univariate and multivariate analyses for 60-day mortality predictors

Univariate Cox regression analysis identified several significant predictors of 60-day mortality: platelet count (hazard ratio [HR]: 0.994, p < 0.001), serum creatinine level (HR: 1.138, p = 0.050), driving pressure on the day of ARDS diagnosis (HR: 0.932, p = 0.047), lung compliance on the day of ARDS diagnosis (HR: 1.037, p = 0.031), partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood (PaO2)/FIO2 ratio on the day of biopsy (HR: 0.995, p = 0.040), ventilatory ratio on the day of biopsy (HR: 1.478, p = 0.025), and rapid progression of DAD (HR: 2.059, p = 0.009) (Table 4). In multivariate Cox regression analysis, only platelet count (HR: 0.996, p = 0.017) and rapid progression of DAD (HR: 2.274, p = 0.014) remained independent predictors of 60-day mortality (Table 4).

Optimal biopsy day cutoffs for predicting 28-day mortality

We used ROC curves and the Youden index to determine optimal cutoffs for predicting 28-day mortality. Among patients in the proliferative phase, the optimal cutoff was 6 days (AUC = 0.621, p = 0.113; Youden index = 0.247). Twenty-three patients (36.5%) were classified as rapid progressors; they had higher 28-day mortality (52.2% vs. 27.5%, p = 0.050) and 60-day mortality (73.9% vs. 57.5%, p = 0.193).

In patients with the fibrotic phase, the optimal cutoff was 10 days (AUC = 0.788, p = 0.020; Youden index = 0.515). Fourteen patients (61%) were rapid progressors and showed significantly higher 28-day mortality (64.3% vs. 11.1%, p = 0.012), although the difference in 60-day mortality was not significant (71.4% vs. 66.7%, p = 0.809).

Overall, patients with rapid progression had significantly higher 28-day mortality (56.8% vs. 22.6%, p = 0.001) and a trend toward higher 60-day mortality (73.0% vs. 54.7%, p = 0.079).

Complications of lung biopsy

Among patients who underwent open lung biopsy, pneumothorax occurred in 13 patients (16.5%). Of these cases, five developed within 5 days after the procedure, five between 5 and 10 days, and three more than 10 days post-biopsy. Additional complications included subcutaneous emphysema in nine patients (11.4%), wound infection in two patients (2.5%), and hypotension in one patient (1.3%).

In the TBLC group, three patients (27.3%) developed pneumothorax within 5 days of the procedure. One patient (9.1%) experienced intraprocedural hemorrhage, which was successfully controlled using topical diluted epinephrine spray alone.

There were no significant differences in 28-day mortality (39.3% vs. 35.5%, p = 0.729) or 60-day mortality (64.3% vs. 61.3%, p = 0.786) between patients with and without complications.

Discussion

In this study of patients with histopathologically confirmed DAD on lung biopsy, mortality did not differ significantly between those in the proliferative and fibrotic phases. However, patients with rapid disease progression—defined as the presence of the proliferative phase within 7 days or the fibrotic phase within 21 days from ARDS onset to biopsy—exhibited significantly higher 28-day and 60-day mortality rates. These patients also had significantly lower PaCO2 levels before biopsy. Although biopsy-related complications were observed, they were not associated with increased mortality. Dynamic lung compliance decreased significantly after open lung biopsy, whereas no significant decline was observed following TBLC.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated poorer outcomes among patients with DAD. In a study by Kao et al., 101 patients underwent open lung biopsy, of whom 57 were diagnosed with DAD. Hospital mortality was markedly higher in patients with DAD than in those without DAD (71.9% vs. 45.5%) [10]. Similarly, Guerin et al. evaluated 83 patients, including 48 with DAD, and reported higher ICU mortality in the DAD group compared with the non-DAD group (67% vs. 57%) [9]. A meta-analysis of eight studies comprising 156 patients with DAD and 194 without DAD further confirmed significantly higher mortality in the DAD group (66.0% vs. 50.0%)[18]. In the present study, only patients with confirmed DAD were included. The 28-day, 60-day, and in-hospital mortality rates were 36.7%, 62.2%, and 67.8%, respectively. These findings are consistent with previously reported mortality rates in patients with DAD. Notably, even among patients with non-rapidly progressive DAD, in-hospital mortality remained high at 61.9%, exceeding the mortality rates reported for patients without DAD in earlier studies.

Based on a previous report [4], early progression was defined as the identification of the proliferative phase on lung biopsy within 1 week or the fibrotic phase within 3 weeks of ARDS diagnosis. The definition of rapid progression was an operational definition based on the timing of the biopsy, and this definition may have been affected by the timing of the procedure. Rapid progression occurred in 42.9% of patients in the proliferative phase and in 84.0% of those in the fibrotic phase. These findings raise the question of whether 3 weeks is an appropriate cutoff for defining early fibrotic-phase progression, given that most patients in the fibrotic phase developed this pathology before 3 weeks. An autopsy study involving 159 patients reported pulmonary fibrosis in 4% of patients with disease duration < 1 week, 24% of those with duration 1–3 weeks, and 61% of those with duration > 3 weeks [14]. However, because that study included only non-survivors, the temporal evolution of ARDS phases among survivors could not be adequately determined.

In the present study, patients with rapid disease progression exhibited significantly poorer clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, the observed association between rapid progression and mortality should be interpreted as an association rather than a causal relationship, and it may reflect the severity of the underlying disease rather than a true acceleration of pathological progression. Compared with those without rapid progression, these patients had significantly lower PaCO2 levels and received higher tidal volume normalized to predicted body weight on the biopsy day. Multivariable analysis identified PaCO2 as the only independent predictor of rapid progression. One possible explanation is that insufficient adherence to lung-protective ventilation—characterized by relatively higher tidal volumes and inadequate permissive hypercapnia—may contribute to accelerated progression of ARDS. Ventilator-induced lung injury is a well-recognized contributor to multiorgan dysfunction and mortality in patients with ARDS [19]. Accordingly, lung-protective ventilation using low tidal volumes has become a fundamental component of ARDS management [20], and permissive hypercapnia is generally accepted within this strategy [21]. Moreover, hypercapnic acidosis has been reported to attenuate pulmonary inflammatory responses [22]. However, PaCO₂ is a non-specific physiological marker that is influenced by multiple factors, including ventilator settings, lung mechanics, and disease severity. Therefore, a causal relationship cannot be established from the current data. This finding warrants confirmation in dedicated physiological studies.

With respect to procedural safety, a meta-analysis of 13 studies reported an overall complication rate of 19.9% for open lung biopsy, with pneumothorax or prolonged air leak being the most common events (approximately 14.3%) [12]. In our cohort of patients with DAD, pneumothorax occurred in 16.5% of patients who underwent open lung biopsy, a rate comparable to previously published data. Among patients who underwent TBLC, pneumothorax occurred in three patients (27.3%). In our prior study, pneumothorax developed in only one patient (10.0%) [13], whereas a previous review reported a rate of 16.0% [23]. Additionally, a study evaluating TBLC in patients with interstitial lung disease reported a pneumothorax rate of 1.9% [24]. Given the small sample sizes across these studies, it cannot be concluded that TBLC carries a higher procedural risk than open lung biopsy in patients with DAD. Furthermore, patients who underwent open lung biopsy demonstrated a significant reduction in dynamic lung compliance after the procedure, whereas no such change was observed in the TBLC group. Importantly, neither group showed significant alterations in the PaO2/FIO2 ratio or PaCO2 following biopsy. These findings indicate that both procedures may be performed in carefully selected patients with DAD without substantial deterioration in gas exchange.

In this study, we included two biopsy methods in patients with ARDS: open lung biopsy and TBLC. The DAD detection rate was significantly higher with open lung biopsy than with TBLC (66.9% vs. 21.2%, p < 0.01). The DAD rate observed with open lung biopsy in our study was comparable to those reported in previous studies [9–11]. However, data on TBLC remain limited, as they are derived mainly from small case series. A recent review of four case series reported DAD in 44.0% of 25 patients [25]. Chang et al. found DAD in 4 of 10 patients (40.0%) [26], while Wang et al. reported DAD in 6 of 25 patients (24.0%) who underwent TBLC [27]. To date, no study has directly compared open lung biopsy with TBLC. The observed difference in DAD detection rates may be partly attributable to differences in sample size [26]. Although sampling bias in TBLC may lead to underdiagnosis of DAD in this study, we restricted our analysis to patients with histologically confirmed DAD, allowing our results to be generalized to patients with DAD. Further studies are needed to refine the TBLC technique and improve its diagnostic yield.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, because lung biopsy is not routinely performed in patients with ARDS, the overall sample size was limited, although it remains larger than that reported in most previous studies. In addition, the small number of patients in the exudative phase may have influenced the statistical comparisons. Second, accurate determination of the timing of the initial pulmonary insult and the onset of DAD—central to our primary analysis—was inherently challenging and may have led to timing bias, as each patient underwent only a single biopsy. Therefore, disease onset was defined as the date of ARDS diagnosis according to the Berlin Definition. Third, the study period extended over two decades, during which diagnostic criteria and treatment strategies for ARDS evolved substantially. To mitigate this limitation, we systematically reviewed the original clinical data, reconfirmed all ARDS diagnoses, and reclassified disease severity according to the Berlin Definition. Fourth, because the study population was highly selected and limited to patients who underwent lung biopsy, selection bias is present and the generalizability of our findings is limited. Finally, unmeasured confounders, including differences in ARDS etiology and the availability of etiology-specific therapies, are inevitable and may have influenced outcomes. However, most patients underwent lung biopsy to identify the underlying cause of ARDS or to clarify persistent nonresponse to prior therapy, and only a small proportion had received effective targeted treatment before the procedure. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to identify predictors of 60-day mortality; nevertheless, some confounders were not accounted for. Further controlled trials are required to confirm our findings.

Conclusions

Among patients with ARDS and histopathologically confirmed DAD, rapid progression of pathological phases—defined as transition to the proliferative phase within 1 week or to the fibrotic phase within 3 weeks—was associated with significantly higher 28-day and 60-day mortality. Patients with rapid progression also exhibited significantly lower PaCO2 levels. Further physiological investigations and large-scale, controlled clinical studies are warranted to confirm these observations and clarify the underlying mechanisms.
