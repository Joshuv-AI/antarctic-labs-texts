Design and implementation of a traffic monitoring, collision awareness and advisory prototype for hot air balloons
Authors: Onur Can AKA, Fatih Alpaslan KAZAN
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, a location and altitude-based warning and collision awareness and advisory prototype was designed and two functional prototypes were developed to reduce the risk of collisions that may occur when hot air balloons cannot see each other in the air. Each prototype used a Raspberry Pi-4, 7-inch touch screen, LoRa communication module, BMP180 pressure sensor, MPU6050 accelerometer, and a GY-GPSV3 NEO-8 M Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS) module. The balloons’ location, altitude, and speed information were mutually broadcast via the LoRa module; the data of the intruder balloon located within a horizontal 500 m radius was visualized with an interface similar to the Traffic Alert and Collision Avoidance System (TCAS) in aircraft. The system offers a resolution advisory (RA) at distances of 100 m and closer. It produces a traffic advisory (TA) at distances between 100 and 200 m. In the tests carried out on the ground and in the hot air balloon, the system’s location detection accuracy, the functionality of the warning algorithms, the detection of flight phases, and the information levels in the designed interface were analyzed. The findings have shown that this system, which leverages low-cost GNSS technology for cooperative awareness, is open to development and represents a novel application of positioning technology to fill a gap in preventing accidents that may occur after balloons come into contact with each other, making a contribution to the hot air balloon industry.Keywords: GNSS, Collision avoidance system, Hot-air balloon, Low-cost avionics, Cooperative awareness, LoRa communicationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Hot air balloons are light aircraft widely used, especially in touristic and sporting activities. The basic operating principle is based on the lifting force created by the decrease in density of heated air. The hot air balloon seen in Fig. 1 consists of four main parts: the envelope, the basket, the burner, and the fuel tank in the basket1,2.

Hot air balloon and its components1.

Due to their quiet and environmentally friendly structure, they are very popular in places with natural beauties such as Cappadocia (Türkiye), Luxor (Egypt), Yarra Valley (Australia), Dubai Desert Conservation Park (United Arab Emirates), and Napa Valley (United States of America, USA). For example, according to the report published by the Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGCA) in 2025, the total number of people boarding balloons in commercial flights in Türkiye in 2024 is 929,5903.

As interest has grown, so has the number of businesses and hot air balloons worldwide. For example, in the USA, only 6 balloons were registered with the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) in 1964, while this number increased to 7,123 in 19954. In Türkiye, the number of businesses increased from 7 (in 2003) to 16 (in 2025), while the number of balloons increased from 34 to 81 in the same period5. However, this increase has also brought accidents.

A study published in 2022 reported that a total of 65 people died and 28 were injured in the five deadliest balloon accidents in history6. In the deadliest hot air balloon accident in recent history, which occurred in Santa Catarina (Brazil) on 21.06.2025, 8 people died and 13 people were injured, too7.

Epidemiologic studies in the literature reveal that 81–88% of hot air balloon accidents are due to pilot error489,,. For example, it was determined that 85.1% of 495 accidents that occurred in the USA were due to pilot error4. In Türkiye, this rate is 14%, and the main causes of accidents are meteorological conditions (57%) and balloons colliding in the air (29%)6.

Balloon collisions most often occur when the basket of one balloon touches the envelope of another, causing the envelope to rupture and rapid altitude loss. The large envelope with an average diameter of 20–25 m and a height of 30–40 m prevents pilots from scanning their surroundings visually, which weakens their environmental awareness. Pilots receive guidance from ground crews via radio, but this guidance may be inadequate due to limited visibility, altitude, or human error.

In commercial aviation, the Traffic Alert and Collision Avoidance System (TCAS) is a critical safety system that increases safety by identifying collision threats and offering resolution advisories to pilots. TCAS, which is mandatory in commercial flights, works with Mode S transponder signals and prevents collision risks by sharing information such as location, altitude, and identity of aircraft with other aircraft10,11–12. However, since balloons do not have such transponder systems and the necessary avionics infrastructure, TCAS cannot be applied directly to hot air balloons.

Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS), such as GPS, are recognized as the cornerstone of modern position awareness in aviation, supporting applications from basic navigation to advanced safety systems like ADS-B13. The proliferation of low-cost, high-performance GNSS modules has created opportunities for developing specialized safety applications in sectors where cost and complexity are critical constraints. This opportunity is leveraged in the present study through the use of a consumer-grade u-blox GNSS receiver as the primary positioning sensor. The integration of this sensor into a cooperative awareness network is investigated to address a specific safety gap in hot-air ballooning, a domain where the benefits of GNSS-based situational awareness have not yet been widely implemented.

Interviews with industry balloon pilots confirm the absence of a dedicated, widely adopted collision awareness system analogous to TCAS in hot air balloon operations. Although collision awareness and advisory technologies such as FLARM (Flight Alarm) - widely used in European glider and light aviation communities for low-altitude traffic awareness14 - and ADS-B (Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast) are well-established in general aviation, their direct application to hot air balloons is not feasible due to multiple constraints: (i) the prohibitive cost of certified avionics, which is disproportionate to the operational budgets of balloon operations; (ii) the high power consumption of these systems – balloons lack any onboard electrical generation system (e.g., alternator or generator) and rely solely on a limited-capacity battery; (iii) the absence of any regulatory requirement for balloons to carry transponders under existing frameworks; and (iv) the lack of supporting ground infrastructure (e.g., ADS-B ground stations) in typical balloon operating regions such as Cappadocia. This limitation of existing systems for low-cost, niche applications is a known challenge, as similarly highlighted in the context of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs)15. Consequently, no widely adopted, dedicated collision awareness system currently exists for hot air balloon operations.

A systematic review of accessible academic literature and patent databases reveals that existing research focuses predominantly on post-accident epidemiology, human factors analysis, and aerodynamic modeling of balloons (as summarized in the previous paragraph). To the best of our knowledge, no prior academic work has presented a functional, low-cost, and open-source hardware/software system designed specifically for real-time traffic monitoring and collision advisory in hot air balloons. Thus, the novelty of this study is defined not by the invention of new GNSS or communication algorithms, but rather by: (i) the first documented application of cooperative GNSS-based awareness to the hot air balloon domain; (ii) the design and integration of commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) components (Raspberry Pi, LoRa, GNSS) into a functioning prototype tailored to the specific operational constraints of balloons (e.g., no onboard power generation, limited battery capacity, low-cost requirement); and (iii) the empirical validation of this prototype in real flight conditions, including air-to-ground and ground-to-ground scenarios. This gap and the proposed solution constitute the primary contribution of this work. In this study, a situational awareness system is developed and prototyped to address this need by utilizing affordable COTS components.

As can be understood from the summarizations, no studies have been found in accessible online databases to prevent hot air balloons from colliding in the air. In this study, a collision awareness and advisory prototype that will provide situational awareness to pilots has been developed. Due to the developed system, each balloon sees itself at the center of a circle with a radius of 500 m. Balloons can see the position and altitude of intruder balloons entering this circle according to their own position and altitude, and can maneuver accordingly. In this way, pilots know what kind of balloon traffic is over their balloon and the probability of accidents is reduced.

Programming language

Since Raspberry Pi-4 has a Linux-based system, Linux was used in the study. The Flutter coding language was used to design and run the interface on the Raspberry Pi 4. Python was used to communicate with the hardware and perform all necessary calculations.

The Haversine formula in (1)-(3) was used to calculate the distance between two balloons using Python. The Haversine formula was employed to calculate the great-circle distance between two points on the Earth’s surface. In this calculation process, the coordinates were first converted to radians. Then, the latitude and longitude differences between the two points were calculated. After calculating the intermediate value a, a calculation was made for the value c. Finally, the distance (d) value was calculated using the radius R and the c value.

The symbols in (1)-(3) and their meanings are as follows:

φ1, φ2: Latitude values of two points (in radians).

∆φ: Latitude difference between two points (in radians).

Δλ: Longitude difference between the two points (in radians).

a: An intermediate value used in the calculation.

c: Central angle between two points (in radians).

R: The radius of the Earth (approximately 6,371 km).

d: The distance between two points (its unit depends on the unit of R).

Interface of the designed system

The designed interface has a structure similar to the TCAS screen. In the interface, a balloon image was placed in the center of the screen and 5 rings were formed around the balloon, each of which is 100 m apart (see Fig. 3). In other words, the system was designed to see intruder balloons 500 m away and closer to it on its screen.

Interface of the designed system (in the LANDING PROGRESS phase).

The region labeled NORTH at the top of the device’s screen indicates north according to the magnetic compass. The numbers on the outermost ring, starting from zero and continuing up to 350, indicate angles by aviation standards. The purpose of this notation is to enable the pilot to determine the angle at which the intruder balloon is located with respect to magnetic north.

In the upper left corner of the screen, the ground speed (GS), altitude (ALT), and instantaneous coordinates (GPS LAT, LONG) of the balloon are presented.

In the upper right corner of the screen, there is the time in GMT, the balloon’s registration code information, the number of satellites it is connected to, and a time counter showing the data stream. The time counter was added to show how long it had been since data was last received from the other device, and to detect possible breaks in the data during tests.

In the lower left corner of the screen, there is information added to show the balloon’s flight phase. If the balloon is closer than 50 ft to the ground, the LANDING PROGRESS warning is printed on the screen to indicate that the balloon is in the take-off/landing phase. When the height above the ground is greater than 50 ft, the system provides FLIGHT PROGRESS information indicating that the balloon is in its normal flight phase (see Fig. 4). The other information in this section of the screen, GPS PRIMARY, emphasizes that GPS has priority in the image projected on the screen because the image on the screen is 2D.

Interface of the designed system (in the FLIGHT PROGRESS phase).

There is a traffic warning (TRFFC WARN) in the lower right corner of the screen. This warning occurs when the approaching balloon is 100 m or closer to the horizontal plane. At this stage, the system gives a “Resolution Advisory (RA)” to the balloon. This warning changes depending on whether the intruder balloon is higher or lower and advises the pilot to “DESCENT” or “CLIMB”. If “ACTIVE” is written next to the “TCAS SYSTEM” information under the warning, this shows that the system is active. If “INACTIVE” is written instead, this indicates that the system is not working at that moment. This information also allows the pilot to check whether the design is working at that moment.

The intruder balloon icon on the screen changes according to its horizontal distance from the balloon it is displayed in. These icons are a red square, a yellow circle, and a blue diamond. The color and shape of the icons change according to the following conditions.

If the balloon in the intruder state is between 200 and 500 m away, the icon takes the shape of a blue diamond. This icon indicates that there is no threat between the two balloons.

If the balloon in the intruder state is between 200 and 500 m away, the icon takes the shape of a blue diamond. This icon indicates that there is no threat between the two balloons.

If the distance is between 100 and 200 m, the icon appears as a yellow circle. When the icon appears as a yellow circle, it means that the approaching balloon is at a distance that requires attention. At this distance, the yellow circle icon means a traffic warning and aims to attract the pilot’s attention.

If the distance is between 100 and 200 m, the icon appears as a yellow circle. When the icon appears as a yellow circle, it means that the approaching balloon is at a distance that requires attention. At this distance, the yellow circle icon means a traffic warning and aims to attract the pilot’s attention.

If the intruder balloon is 100 m or closer to the balloon, i.e., within the traffic advisory (TA) distance, the icon changes to a red square. This indicates to the pilot that the intruder balloon is dangerously close. The descent or climb recommendations mentioned above are given in this state.

If the intruder balloon is 100 m or closer to the balloon, i.e., within the traffic advisory (TA) distance, the icon changes to a red square. This indicates to the pilot that the intruder balloon is dangerously close. The descent or climb recommendations mentioned above are given in this state.

The numbers written on the intruder balloon icon indicate the altitude difference between the intruder balloon and the current balloon in ft. If the intruder balloon is higher, this difference is shown only numerically; if it is lower, it is shown with a minus (-) sign in front of it. The arrow next to the icon indicates the direction of vertical movement of the intruder balloon.

Methodological constraints and validation approach

The development and experimental validation of the prototype were conducted within the practical and budgetary constraints characteristic of a focused academic proof-of-concept study. These parameters inherently shaped the validation philosophy, which prioritized demonstrating functional feasibility and subsystem integration in a real-world environment over achieving certified metrological precision or conducting extensive multi-scenario testing.

A primary methodological limitation, dictated by project scope and budget, was the single-use nature of the experimental campaign. Hardware was procured specifically for this initial validation phase, precluding the possibility of conducting repeated experiments or additional test scenarios, such as multi-balloon flights, with the same units. Consequently, all presented results are derived from this singular, defined experimental series.

Furthermore, logistical and regulatory constraints limited the flight-testing paradigm. Securing a special flight permit from the DGCA for a controlled, multi-balloon experiment was not feasible within the project’s framework. The commercial cost of renting a second balloon solely for scientific testing was also prohibitive. Therefore, the system’s core functionality was validated using a practical air-to-ground scenario, where one unit was carried aboard a commercial passenger balloon while its counterpart remained at the ground station. While this scenario does not fully replicate the dynamic encounter geometry of two free-floating balloons, it successfully validated the integrated operation of key subsystems: real-time GPS data exchange via the LoRa link, the dynamic traffic display interface, the advisory logic engine, and overall system stability in an authentic flight environment.

The verification of sensor data employed pragmatic, comparative methods. GPS coordinates were cross-referenced with a commercial mobile phone application as a sanity check. Altitude readings from the onboard BMP180 sensor were compared against known terrain elevation data from Google Earth at the static ground station location. We explicitly acknowledge that these methods do not constitute a traceable metrological calibration. The intent was to confirm that the sensors provided stable and plausible data feeds to the system’s algorithms, not to certify their absolute precision for primary safety-critical navigation. Any future iteration intended for operational deployment would necessitate rigorous validation against differential GPS (DGPS) and calibrated pressure-altitude references.

The photographs in Section III were captured during the actual commercial flight and ground tests. Conditions such as cabin glare and reflections resulted in imperfect image quality, which we acknowledge. These images are retained as authentic records of the field-test environment. To prevent any ambiguity, the system state captured in each image (e.g., intruder icon type, advisory message, flight mode) is explicitly described in the analysis text immediately following or preceding the figure.

Tests of the designed system

Due to the high cost, two prototypes were produced and tests were carried out with these two prototypes. In order to see the accuracy level of the data received from the sensors in the designed system, to detect possible communication errors between the devices, and to make the necessary software updates accordingly, tests were first performed on the ground. Then, a test flight was carried out in a real hot air balloon.

Ground test

At this stage, the device’s GPS connection, sensor data acquisition, data transfer, system integrity, and calculation accuracy were tested in detail. All ground tests were conducted within the Alaeddin Keykubat Campus of Selçuk University.

Assessment of relative GNSS consistency

The primary objective of the location data validation was to assess the relative consistency and plausibility of the GNSS coordinates reported by the two prototype units, which is the critical factor for a cooperative awareness system. Absolute, survey-grade geodetic accuracy was not the immediate goal of this proof-of-concept stage.

To evaluate this, a static comparative test was conducted. Both prototype devices were placed at the endpoints of a precisely measured 50.0-meter baseline on open, level ground with a clear sky view. The devices logged their GNSS coordinates over a 10-minute period. The great-circle distance between the average reported positions of the two units was calculated using the Haversine formula. This calculated distance was 50.8 m, indicating a relative error of 1.6% (or 0.8 m) over the known baseline. This level of error is consistent with the expected performance of standalone, low-cost u-blox NEO-M8N modules in optimal conditions and is acceptable for establishing relative proximity between balloons for initial threat detection.

It is recognized that for an operational safety system, validation against a high-precision geodetic reference is mandatory. Therefore, future iterations intended for certification would require the use of a Differential GNSS (DGNSS) base station or a Real-Time Kinematic (RTK) rover as a ground-truth reference to characterize and calibrate absolute positioning errors.

Assessment of relative altitude consistency

The validation of altitude data focused on verifying the consistency and relative accuracy of the barometric pressure sensors (BMP180) between the two prototype units, which is essential for correct vertical separation advisories. As with GNSS validation, the immediate goal was functional plausibility, not certified metrological precision.

To achieve this, a controlled differential altitude test was performed. Both devices were placed side-by-side at a known ground reference point to establish a baseline. One device was then moved to the roof of a single-story building with a precisely measured height of 3.50 m above the ground reference. The devices logged altitude data simultaneously. The mean altitude difference reported by the system was 3.2 m, resulting in a relative error of 8.6% (or 0.3 m) for this small vertical baseline. This error is within the expected range for uncorrected barometric sensors over short intervals and demonstrates the system’s capability to detect and report meaningful relative altitude changes.

It must be noted that barometric altitude is inherently subject to drift due to changing local atmospheric pressure. The prototype’s current logic does not implement automatic pressure calibration (e.g., QNH updates via data link), which would be a required feature for sustained operational use. For definitive validation and calibration, future work would need to employ a reference barometer traceable to a meteorological standard or a differential GNSS system capable of providing centimeter-level vertical accuracy.

Balloon test

After the position and altitude information provided by the designed system was verified by ground tests, balloon testing was started. An attempt was made to test the designed prototypes by first positioning them in separate balloons. However, this could not be realized since a special flight permit could not be obtained from the DGCA. Therefore, the test was carried out on commercial flights.

Firstly, the plan was to conduct the tests by placing the devices in two balloons travelling in different directions and at different altitudes, so that their relative positions would vary. To achieve this, it was considered that two commercial balloons should be rented. However, the rental fee for a 12-person balloon is approximately €3,600. As this would cost €7,200 for two balloons, it was not possible to rent them. Instead, it was decided that the devices would be tested by boarding the commercial balloon as a normal customer.

In the interview with the balloon pilots before the flight, it was requested that the two balloons be at different altitudes and positions relative to each other, but the pilots stated that this would not be possible due to the sudden change of wind direction and the fact that this flight was a touristic flight. Moreover, since the balloons are not prepared simultaneously and a balloon must take on its passengers and begin its flight immediately after preparation, the only option is to conduct the test with one of the devices on the ground and the other in the balloon.

Discussion of test results

Justification of system design and operational considerations

The design choices for the prototype, particularly the fixed-distance thresholds for TA and RA, were made with the operational characteristics of hot-air balloons and pilot input as primary considerations.

Advisory thresholds

The 100 m horizontal threshold for issuing a RA was derived from interviews with experienced balloon pilots in Cappadocia. They reported that under typical calm wind conditions (≤ 5 knots), closure rates between balloons are very low (typically < 2 m/s). Given the slow thermal response and climb/descent rates of hot-air balloons (approximately 3–5 m/s maximum vertical speed), a 100 m separation provides a conservative time buffer of 50–100 s for pilot recognition and reaction. It is acknowledged that mature systems such as TCAS use dynamic time-to-closest-point-of-approach (TCPA) calculations rather than fixed-distance thresholds, as TCPA inherently accounts for relative velocity and encounter geometry. However, for this proof-of-concept prototype, a fixed-distance approach was adopted as a pragmatic and understandable first step. The primary rationale is that under typical balloon operating conditions (low and variable speeds, no ability for rapid maneuvering), the relationship between distance and time is reasonably predictable. Furthermore, the 500 m display radius provides pilots with approximately 2–3 min of situational awareness under these conditions, allowing for early, gradual maneuvering. Evolving the advisory logic from fixed thresholds to a dynamic TCPA-based model remains an important direction for future work.

Symmetrical vertical advisories

The prototype issues symmetrical “CLIMB” and “DESCENT” commands. This logic, inspired by TCAS II, is intended as a clear, unambiguous initial directive to ensure divergent maneuvers. We acknowledge that in hot-air balloons, the ability to climb or descend is not equal and is subject to thermal lag and burner capacity. Future implementations could integrate knowledge of balloon performance (e.g., burn rate, current vertical trend) to issue optimized, asymmetric advisories.

Scalability and RF channel considerations

A critical consideration for the deployment of any cooperative awareness system is its performance in dense traffic. The current prototype employs a simple, uncoordinated LoRa broadcast protocol. While this is sufficient for the proof-of-concept testing with two units, it is acknowledged that in a dense operational environment like Cappadocia, where dozens of balloons may be aloft simultaneously, this approach this approach may face scalability challenges, namely packet collisions and data loss, which could degrade system reliability. A thorough analysis of the LoRa channel capacity considering parameters such as spreading factor (SF), bandwidth (BW), coding rate (CR), and update frequency is essential to determine the maximum supported number of nodes. This analysis is beyond the scope of the current prototype validation and is identified as critical future work. For reliable multi-node operation, the implementation of a channel access protocol is necessary. A simple Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA) scheme, where balloons transmit in assigned time slots, or a Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance (CSMA/CA) protocol would be required software upgrades to ensure robust performance in high-density scenarios.

Logic for ground and low-altitude operation

The test revealed that the “LANDING PROGRESS” mode (active below 50 ft AGL) could inadvertently suppress advisories during crowded take-offs. This is a recognized limitation of the current logic. A more robust implementation would use a combination of barometric altitude, vertical rate, and a radio altimeter to distinguish between “ON GROUND”, “LOW-ALTITUDE FLIGHT”, and “CRUISE” states, ensuring the collision awareness and advisory logic remains active whenever airborne.

Analysis of experimental findings

Tests conducted on the ground first tested the location information provided by the device. In these tests, it was seen that the device detected the location with an acceptable error of 1.297 m. In the second phase of the ground test, the accuracy of the altitude information provided by the device was investigated. In the altitude test, it was seen that the device detected the altitude with an error of 0.49 m. In the final stage of ground tests, it was tested whether the communication between the devices worked properly and whether their positions relative to each other were updated in real time. One of the designed devices was left fixed during the test, and the other was moved along a specific path so that its image was taken between each ring in the interface. In tests conducted in 6 different locations, the locations read from the device screens were marked on Google Earth, and their distances to the stationary device were determined. Comparing the locations on the device screens and Google Earth markings, it was determined that both balloons accurately displayed the distance of the intruder balloon on their screens.

During the balloon tests, it was confirmed that the devices displayed the radial information of the intruder balloon correctly, depending on the design, by marking the location information of both devices on Google Earth and determining how many degrees radial they were relative to each other.

The device in the balloon continuously displayed the GS value of the balloon throughout the test. However, it was not possible to compare the GS values due to the fact that the device belonging to the pilot and showing the GS value of the balloon was used by the pilot throughout the flight.

It is acknowledged that the results presented in this study demonstrate functional correctness of the prototype under controlled and limited test conditions (one airborne unit, one ground unit). Operational reliability, defined as the probability that the system will perform its intended function under actual operational conditions over time, has not been established. A full operational validation would require extensive multi-balloon flight testing, long-term deployment, and statistical analysis of system performance under diverse weather conditions and traffic densities. Such validation is beyond the scope of this proof-of-concept study and remains a critical prerequisite for any future operational deployment.

Conclusion

In this study, a low-cost, open-source prototype for GNSS-based cooperative traffic awareness and collision advisory in hot-air balloons was designed, implemented, and tested. It is emphasized that the contribution of this work is not the development of a new theoretical algorithm, but rather a first-in-domain system integration, implementation, and empirical validation. By integrating a consumer-grade GNSS receiver with COTS components (Raspberry Pi, LoRa), a functional TCAS-like interface was demonstrated. The proof-of-concept was validated through ground and air-to-ground flight tests, confirming core functionalities: real-time exchange of GNSS-derived position and altitude data, dynamic traffic display, and the generation of basic Resolution Advisories. It is emphasized that operational reliability was not evaluated in this study and remains a critical prerequisite for any future operational deployment.

The work acknowledges key limitations that define its current scope as a foundational prototype. Financial and regulatory constraints limited flight validation to an air-to-ground scenario. Sensor accuracy was assessed via comparative methods, and the advisory logic employs fixed-distance thresholds rather than dynamic, GNSS-driven metrics. Furthermore, the scalability of the simple LoRa broadcast protocol in dense traffic requires further analysis.

In conclusion, this work demonstrates a practical and novel application of ubiquitous GNSS technology to address a specific safety gap in a niche aviation domain. The prototype establishes that a functional awareness system can be built around low-cost GNSS, providing an open, extensible platform for future research into positioning performance, sensor fusion, and integrity management for unconventional aerial operations. It thus contributes a foundational step towards scalable, GNSS-centric safety solutions for the ballooning industry.
