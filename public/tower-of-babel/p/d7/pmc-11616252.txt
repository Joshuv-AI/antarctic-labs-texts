Pre‐referral ultrasound for cryptorchidism: Still common, still not necessary
Authors: Grace E Boyd, Bhaveshkumar Patel, Craig A McBride
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AimEvidence‐based guidelines do not recommend imaging in cryptorchidism, but anecdotally most referrals include an ultrasound report. We aimed to assess the frequency, utility and burden of imaging in children referred with presumptive disorders of testicular descent, and to assess trends over a 7‐year period before and after local and international guidelines have been introduced.MethodsThis was a prospective cohort study of children referred to the Queensland Children's Hospital for anomalies of testicular descent between 2015–2017 and 2023–2024. Data were collected regarding demographics, referral details, imaging performed and surgical diagnosis.ResultsA total of 268 children were recruited. Ultrasound frequency has not significantly changed over time (72.8%, 2015–2017; 63.6% 2023–2024; P = 0.11). Currently, 17.6% of families are charged, and 31.9% need to take time off work, for the ultrasound. This is a significant increase from the 2015–2017 cohort. Parents report concern and anxiety, and find it traumatic for their child. Following review, the majority (65.7%) had physiologically normal testes, while 4.9% and 22.4% were diagnosed with bilateral or unilateral cryptorchidism, respectively, and 7.0% had other diagnoses. Ultrasound was concordant with the surgical diagnosis in 25.0% (2015–2017) and 30.7% (2023–2024). Ultrasound did not contribute to diagnosis nor management in any patient.ConclusionsDespite international and local guidelines, cryptorchid children continue to undergo ultrasound prior to referral. Such ultrasounds do not provide utility, or value for cost. We recommend avoiding their routine use for suspicion of testicular maldescent and working together to improve strategies for translating evidence‐based guidelines into clinical practice.

Undescended testis is one of the most common genitourinary anomalies in male infants, 1 with approximately 3% of those born at term having an undescended testis diagnosed. 2 , 3 This is therefore a common referral seen in paediatric surgical outpatient clinics, representing up to 17% of all referrals to our unit. 4

Evidence‐based guidelines exist regarding the assessment and management of undescended testes. Recommendations published in the Australian Journal of General Practice (AJGP) in 2019 5 and our own 2020 Queensland Health Paediatric Surgery referral guidelines 6 emphasise ultrasonography is not required prior to referral. Additionally, there are a number of published guidelines in which the use of imaging in routine pre‐operative assessment is not recommended. These include guidelines from the American Urological Association, British Association of Paediatric Surgery, Canadian Urological Association, European Association of Urology, European Society for Paediatric Urology and the Nordic Consensus. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Ultrasound scanning can be misleading, as contraction of the cremaster muscle during the scan can elevate a normal testis into an apparently undescended position. Additionally, further studies have explored the utility of ultrasound found low concordance in diagnostic agreement of ultrasound with laparoscopic findings 12 or findings following examination under anaesthesia. 13 Apart from the AJGP guidelines, these guidelines are written from the perspective of the specialist looking back rather than the general practitioner (GP) looking forward. In the UK, following an educational survey of regional GPs, Cho et al. 14 found 80% of GPs thought ultrasonography was routinely used in the assessment of undescended testes. Anecdotally, our local data approximate this figure, and we have noted an ongoing preponderance of ultrasound studies for children prior to their referral.

The present study aims to assess the frequency and utility of outpatient imaging performed for children referred with a presumptive disorder of testicular descent at a major Australian tertiary paediatric centre. We also aimed to compare the frequency and utility of such outpatient imaging over two cohorts separated by several years – one in 2015–2017 and one in 2023–2024 – as guidelines were published locally between these time frames. We hypothesised ultrasound prior to referral does not accurately inform clinical diagnosis or management, and that frequency of ultrasound use has remained static over the past 7 years. Our secondary aims included determining the burden of such imaging on the child/family unit. This study was ultimately performed with an aim to streamline the referral process for these children.

Methods

This was a prospective cohort study of children referred to the Department of Paediatric Surgery Outpatient Clinic for anomalies of testicular descent (undescended/retractile testis/es) between April 2015 and December 2017, and from February 2023 to January 2024, at the Queensland Children's Hospital in Brisbane, Australia. Only new referrals were included.

Data were collected regarding demographics, referrer details, referral diagnosis, use of clinical examination and diagnostic imaging prior to referral, cost/burden of imaging for the family unit if performed and clinical diagnosis/assessment following review in the clinic. Testes were categorised as undescended if they were described by members of the paediatric surgery team as ‘undescended’, ‘non‐palpable’ or ‘not identified’. These patients were subcategorised into bilateral undescended testes, unilateral undescended testes or undescended testis/es with unilaterality or bilaterality not specified. Testes were categorised as descended if they were described as ‘normal’, ‘descended’, ‘scrotal’ or ‘retractile’.

Written informed consent was obtained from parents during their initial review in the clinic. Approval for this study was granted prior to commencement by the Children's Health Queensland Human Research Ethics Committee (Reference number: HREC/13/QRCH/195).

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using Stata 15 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using histograms. The association between variables were examined using χ 2 and Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables and Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney tests for non‐normally distributed continuous variables. We adhered to the Strengthening of Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Table A1 in Appendix I).

Results

In total, 268 children (125 in 2015–2017, 143 in 2023–2024) were referred with a presumptive disorder of testicular descent and recruited for inclusion in the study following review. Referral details are outlined in Table 1. The overall median age at time of referral was 15.5 months. Comparison across the two cohorts, with respect to referral diagnoses, showed significant increases in the proportions of unilateral undescended (35.2% (44/125) in 2015–2017, 51.1% (73/143) in 2023–2024, P = 0.009) and retractile testes (4.0% (5/125) in 2015–2017, 14.7% (21/143) in 2023–2024, P = 0.003). There was a corresponding decrease in undescended testis/es not specified (22.4% (28/125) in 2015–2017, 2.8% (4/143) in 2023–2024, P < 0.005), but no significant change in referrals for bilateral undescended testes (38.4% (48/125) in 2015–2017, 28.0% (40/143) in 2023–2024, P = 0.70). The majority (88.4%, 237/268) of parents recalled their child being examined by their doctor prior to referral. On average, families attended their referring practitioner 1.85 and 1.64 times in 2015–2017 and 2023–2024, respectively (P = 0.46).

In 2015–2017, 72.8% (91/125) of children underwent ultrasound prior to referral, while 63.6% (91/143) of children had an ultrasound in the 2023–2024 cohort. In the most recent cohort, 17.6% (16/91) of families were directly charged for the ultrasound scan, with the highest cost being $230. Approximately one in three (31.9%, 29/91) families took time off from work or school to attend the ultrasound. These are significant increases from the 2015–2017 cohort, where 8.8% (8/91) of families were charged (P = 0.040) and 12.1% (11/91) took time off from work or school (P = 0.001). Indirect costs incurred (6.6% (6/91) in 2015–2017, 14.3% (13/91) in 2023–2024, P = 0.088) did not alter significantly. Comments from parents were documented during clinical review as part of this study. Several parents described concern and anxiety surrounding their ultrasound results while awaiting surgical review, while others noted procedural anxiety and trauma associated with the ultrasound, as well as distance to a sonographer and the requirement for multiple appointments causing stress. A number of families were told their child had bilateral undescended testes and would be infertile. In most cases these testes were subsequently assessed as normal. There was no evidence ultrasound findings on referral affected outpatient clinic triaging.

When seen in our clinic (Table 3), in the most recent cohort, 65.7% (94/143) of children referred were diagnosed with physiologically normal testes, while 4.9% (7/143) and 22.4% (32/143) were diagnosed with bilateral or unilateral cryptorchism, respectively, and one (0.7%, 1/143) patient was diagnosed with undescended testis/es, not specified. There were nine (6.3%, 9/143) patients who had other diagnoses, including seven with high‐riding or ascending testes for further review, one with a vanishing testis/testicular remnant and one with a peeping (palpable at the deep inguinal ring) testis. In the earlier cohort (2015–2017), there were significantly fewer patients who were subsequently diagnosed with physiologically normal testes (48.0%, 60/125, P = 0.004). When ultrasound was compared with physical examination in our clinic (Table 4), concordance was 25.0% in 2015–2017 and 30.7% in 2023–2024. The ultrasound scan did not contribute to diagnosis or management in any patient (0% utility for diagnosis or planning).

Discussion

A significant number of children undergo ultrasound prior to referral for presumptive disorders of testicular descent. Our results suggest ultrasound assessment is more likely to falsely diagnose a normal testis as undescended, given our clinical assessment agreed with the ultrasound report only 30.7% of the time. This is similar to the positive predictive value of 49.6% for scrotal ultrasounds reported by Annick et al. 15 Our study has identified unnecessary financial and indirect costs (including time off work and school). Often the ultrasound report suggests bilateral undescended testes. A simple search on the internet will reveal concerns of infertility with bilateral undescended testes. In our cohort, a number of parents presented with such concerns, and in some distress, to the paediatric surgical clinic.

Despite evidence‐based guidelines published between 2007 and 2017, 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 including local referral guidelines and criteria published in the years between our two cohorts, in 2019 5 and 2020, 6 which do not recommend the use of imaging in routine pre‐operative assessment, there has not been a statistically significant reduction in pre‐referral outpatient ultrasounds that are performed. The current guidelines state boys over 2 years with retractile testes should be referred for a specialist opinion. This may explain the observed significant increase in referrals for retractile testes in the later cohort. It would appear these same available guidelines are not having an impact on ultrasound practices.

This study demonstrates higher rates of ultrasound performed compared to those described in earlier studies. In 2020, Carpenter et al. 16 retrospectively evaluated children in the USA who had undergone surgery for undescended testes and found 25.3% of patients underwent pre‐referral imaging assessment. They emphasised utilisation of diagnostic imaging prior to referral remained inappropriately high despite the breadth of available evidence demonstrating its ineffectiveness. In 2021, Milford et al. 17 performed a retrospective cohort study of Canadian children who had undergone orchidopexy over a 9‐year period and found 53% of patients underwent pre‐referral ultrasound for the purpose of identifying undescended testes. A more recent study by Annick et al. 15 described 38.3% of patients in California referred with cryptorchidism underwent an ultrasound prior to referral. It appears in our state, compared to other regions internationally, rates of pre‐referral ultrasound are disproportionately higher. In some locations, including Canada, there have been increases in the proportion of patients with undescended testes referred with an ultrasound over a 4‐year period. 18 In a survey of 246 paediatricians, reasons for facilitating such imaging included the belief that ultrasound could identify nonpalpable testes and to reassure parents. 19 It is therefore important to note the considerable stress described by some parents who presented to our clinic following outpatient ultrasound.

Despite the extensive guidelines previously outlined, we have shown utilisation of ultrasound by referring clinicians remains high in our catchment, with no significant reduction over time following the introduction of local guidelines. Despite the time taken, the expense, and the parental concern that was sometimes generated, there was not a single child in whom ultrasound contributed in any measure at the outpatient clinical assessment. There appears to be an ongoing disparity between evidence‐based recommendations against the use of ultrasound in the evaluation of undescended testes, and what is occurring in clinical practice, as ultrasounds continue to be requested and performed. The present study demonstrates an apparent systemic problem, and an improvement on current strategies to translate guidelines into clinical practice is required. Tertiary centres need to improve their communication, given current guidelines are not changing practice, as well as their education for referring clinicians with clinical examination. An effective strategy has been reported by Aggarwal et al. 20 in 2014, where educational updates were provided to referring providers, and overall a significant reduction in the number of patients referred with an unnecessary ultrasound was observed (P < 0.01).

A slightly unexpected finding in our cohort was the higher‐than‐average mean age of referral being 32.26 months, with the median being 15.5 months. This is currently being further explored in another study that looks further back into our data sets; however, there are a number of speculative factors that may be contributing. This includes a potential change in GP practice, a post‐COVID effect and a possible growing recognition of secondary ascending testes or a higher referral proportion of retractile testes.

Limitations

Our data do not explain the reason for the ultrasound request. It may be perceived as a requirement for referral, or perhaps an expectation from caregivers, or to augment (rather than replace) clinical examination, as the majority of children have an ultrasound reported prior to their referral to outpatients. In only a small number of cases did parents not recall their child being examined but an ultrasound requested, suggesting ultrasound is not routinely being used as a proxy for clinical examination. It is presumed that an ultrasound is requested on the basis of a clinical diagnosis of unilateral or bilateral undescended testis/es; however, we do not have this information. It is possible referrers are using ultrasound scanning in cases where clinical examination is equivocal, and subsequently not referring those children where the ultrasound is normal. Such patients would not be captured in this cohort, as they do not present to paediatric surgical outpatient clinics. We note most guidelines referred to above are written from the perspective of the hospital, rather than the community specialist. Unfortunately, our data are also limited in that they are not granular enough to determine a palpable undescended testis from an impalpable undescended testis, from the perspective of the referring clinician. There were only two referrals in the entire cohort that documented ‘impalpable undescended testes’; however, it would be presumed that a proportion of the ‘undescended testes’ were also impalpable to the referrer.

Conclusions

Despite numerous international clinical practice guidelines and local referral criteria, the majority of children seen in our Paediatric Surgery Outpatient Department for undescended or retractile testes continue to undergo ultrasound prior to referral, and this has not significantly reduced over time. These scans frequently overcall testicular maldescent, do not help with clinical decision‐making, and can cause undue distress to patients and families. We perform investigations to provide support for decision‐making for clinicians, and information transparency for patients. However, we have shown that ultrasound for undescended testes does not provide utility, and in an era of value‐based care, does not provide value for cost. It appears the guidelines and referral criteria available have not changed ultrasound practice. We recommend avoiding the routine use of ultrasound for presumptive disorders of testicular descent, and working together to improve strategies for translating evidence‐based guidelines into clinical practice, as well as promoting collaborative education in examination technique for primary care providers and referring clinicians. Our team are working towards active feedback with our referring clinicians, and are intending on furthering this study in the next two to 3 years to assess the impact of these efforts. Given the additional associated costs we have identified with unnecessary ultrasounds, we feel this cost is greater than a referral with a subsequent normal diagnosis. Through working together with primary care providers, as well as our radiology colleagues, 21 and reducing the number of ultrasounds requested and performed, this will streamline the referral process by enabling faster surgical review without the concern of an incorrect ultrasound result.
