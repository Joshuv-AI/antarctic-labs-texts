A state-switchable TADF macrocycle for multi-analyte sensing and hydrogen gas-driven emission enhancement
Authors: Raktim Deka, Dinesh Singh, Manjeet Singh, Kshatresh Dutta Dubey, Debdas Ray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Photochemical sensing demands systems that adjust their optical properties without altering chromophore structure. Here, we report CPCQ, a thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF)-active macrocycle, as a responsive platform for optical sensing. CPCQ shows a photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) of 78%, a delayed lifetime of 243 ns, and an efficient reverse intersystem crossing rate of 2.68 × 10⁷ s⁻¹. Unlike conventional fluorescent and TADF emitters, CPCQ with cyclic array of four TADF active donor-acceptors enables reversible state switching of its emission mechanism. Electron-deficient guests form exciplexes with reducing fluorescence and shifting emission. In contrast, electron-rich guests enhance delayed fluorescence (248 ns) via the heavy atom effect. Oxygen quenches charge-transfer emission and restores blue-shifted locally excited (LE) emission. Strikingly, hydrogen flips the effect, triples LE emission (kr = 2.07 × 108 s-1, PLQY = 93%), and hints at superradiance from cooperative behavior of donor–acceptor units. These adaptive responses establish CPCQ as a highly sensitive and selective molecular tool for next-generation sensing and optoelectronic applications.

Introduction

In photochemistry, the prevailing strategy has long involved designing chromophores tailored to perform a single, predefined function1–3. These molecules are synthesized with precision, often guided by a specific emission profile or energy transfer characteristic. Yet nature operates with a strikingly different approach. In living organisms, a single chromophore can carry out multiple roles, switching behavior depending entirely on its environment4. Microbial rhodopsins serve as a compelling example. Despite all containing the same all-trans retinal chromophore, their emission spans a broad spectral window from 435 to 590 nm5–8. This natural adaptability is driven by steric and electrostatic interactions within their protein scaffolds, which modulate the photophysical properties without altering the chromophore itself. Inspired by this biological strategy, researchers have begun to explore systems where function arises from context rather than static molecular design9,10.

Supramolecular chemistry provides the ideal platform for this vision11. In particular, macrocyclic hosts such as calixarenes12–14, cyclodextrins15–17, cucurbiturils18–20, pillararenes21, and metallosupramolecular cages22–27 offer structurally robust, well-defined cavities capable of engaging in selective molecular recognition. These hosts are often praised for their chemical stability, modularity, and capacity for reversible interactions, all essential traits for constructing dynamic, responsive systems. Literature has long established its utility in diverse areas, including catalysis28–33, molecular sensing34,35, and targeted drug delivery36–38. More recently, their applications in light-driven processes have gained attention30,32. The ability of macrocyclic hosts to form host–guest complexes in solution enables researchers to probe excited-state behaviors with high fidelity. In contrast, solid-state systems often suffer from uncontrolled packing, unpredictable intermolecular interactions, and quenching due to impurities or long-range energy migration. A particularly rich area of exploration lies in the charge-transfer (CT) states that arise during host–guest interactions32,39. These states, formed in both the ground and excited electronic configurations, are central to tuning fluorescence and energy transfer processes. Foundational studies with cucurbiturils and cyclodextrins have shown how confinement within a rigid cavity can restrict vibrational relaxation and enhance radiative decay, increasing emission efficiency39–42. Guest-dependent behavior further allows for dynamic modulation- certain guests can activate fluorescence, while others suppress it, depending on their electronic nature. These properties have been exploited in the development of sensors for small molecules, ions, and gases. Literature reports demonstrate macrocyclic systems capable of detecting O2, trapping CO₂, and even capturing toxic gases such as CH2 = CH2, SO₂, SF₆, and NO₂43–47. However, reported systems are either non-emissive or rely solely on fluorescence, limiting them to short-lived singlet excited-state pathways, narrowing their functional range.

Thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) offers a solution48–53. TADF-active materials harvest longer-lived triplet excitons by thermally upconverting them to singlet states, producing delayed emission without relying on heavy metals. When incorporated into macrocycles, this mechanism enables highly sensitive, tunable optical responses. While emerging reports have demonstrated TADF in host–guest frameworks, few studies have explored how the cavity environment itself could be leveraged to modulate TADF behavior54–61. This presents a clear opportunity. By adapting nature’s model—where structure defines function through environmental tuning—we can design supramolecular systems that shift their optical output with molecular precision. The result is a new class of smart, light-responsive materials with broad utility in sensing and optoelectronics.

Results and discussion

The host compound CPCQ shows strong blue TADF, with a high PLQY of 78%, impressive kRISC of 2.68 × 107 s−1, with a low ΔEST of 0.13 eV and prompt (PF) and delayed fluorescence (DF) lasting 12.1 ns and 243 ns, respectively. This performance arises from an increased local triplet (LE) density near the lowest singlet charge-transfer (CT) state, generated by both through-bond and through-space CT, which enhances spin–orbit and vibronic couplings. Because of its cyclic structure, CPCQ forms a clear internal cavity around the quinoxaline acceptor - an ideal site for guest inclusion. Our approach in choosing guest molecules had three clear aims. We anticipated guests capable of disrupting CPCQ’s internal CT pathways by forming inclusion complexes. Then, we aimed to capture how these complexes altered CPCQ’s optical signals. Lastly, we explored CPCQ’s potential as a gas sensor by observing quenching and coherence effects. To support these aims, we used cyclic voltammetry to examine CPCQ’s redox profile (Supplementary Fig. S1). Based on energy alignments, we identified electron-poor guests like phthalonitrile (PN) and terephthalonitrile (TPN), and electron-rich ones like 1,4-diiodobenzene (1,4-DIB). We also chose gases such as oxygen (O2), hydrogen (H2), sulfur dioxide (SO2), sulfur hexafluoride (SF6), and methane (CH4) to test CPCQ’s gas-sensing abilities. These choices allowed comprehensive exploration of CPCQ’s sensing potential through measurable optical responses.

Analyzing host-guest interactions using NMR proved difficult, as aromatic signals from both host and guests overlapped significantly, appearing close together at δ 7.0–7.3 ppm. However, matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI) analysis successfully confirmed that complexes did form in every case tested. Each aromatic guest reliably produced a 1:1 complex with CPCQ, clearly demonstrated by specific mass-to-charge ratios, including CPCQ⊃TPN ([M + H]+, 2143.556), CPCQ⊃PN ([M + H]+, 2143.714), and CPCQ⊃1,4-DIB ([M + H]+, 2346.121), as Supplementary Fig. S2 illustrates. To further confirm these complexes, we conducted ultraviolet-visible absorption tests in toluene. Because CPCQ naturally shows broad absorption spectra, adding one equivalent of guest did not change its absorption features. This unchanged absorption also excludes the possibility of a CT state (ground state) forming upon complexation, as highlighted in Supplementary Fig. S3. Molecular dynamics simulations later provided definitive confirmation of host-guest binding, discussed further in a subsequent section.

PL spectroscopy provides valuable insights into how guest molecules influence the excited-state behavior of a host. It also clearly reveals the deactivation pathways of host⊃guest complexes. Because CPCQ exhibits broad absorption spectra, earlier absorption studies couldn’t detect changes upon adding guests. This absence of spectral change indicates that no CT phenomenon occurs in the ground state. However, host-guest binding interactions can still effectively be explored using PL spectroscopy. To investigate these interactions, we carried out fluorescence titration experiments with CPCQ as the host and electron-poor TPN and PN as guests (Fig. 2). The results, illustrated in Fig. 2a, b, showed notable changes. When we introduced TPN as the guest, the emission peak shifted significantly to longer wavelengths. Additionally, fluorescent intensity dropped considerably. While PN also caused a shift toward longer wavelengths, the effect appeared more moderate compared to TPN, accompanied by a smaller decrease in emission intensity. These distinct changes likely result from exciplex formation, driven by the strong electron-accepting ability of TPN and PN. The observed decline in emission intensity further supports the above argument, suggesting an increased role of non-radiative decay paths due to exciplex formation. Fluorescence lifetime analysis offers additional proof. Both guest complexes showed a clear bi-exponential decay with two short lifetimes- 6.6 ns and 10.5 ns for TPN, and 6.8 ns and 11.0 ns for PN (Fig. 2d, e, Table 1). PL measurements revealed PLQYs of 32% for CPCQ⊃TPN and 45% for CPCQ⊃PN under ambient conditions. Furthermore, slight redshifts in more polar solvents reinforce the exciplex formation by confirming the CT nature of the excited states (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S4). When we introduced electron-rich 1,4-diiodobenzene (1,4-DIB), we observed a steady rise in PLQY from 78% to 82% as the concentration increased (Fig. 2c). Importantly, neither the emission maximum nor the onset position shifted. This confirms that, unlike electron-poor guests, 1,4-DIB does not form a new CT state. Examining fluorescence lifetimes revealed something intriguing. The CPCQ⊃1,4-DIB complex showed a bi-exponential decay profile (Fig. 2f). While the delayed fluorescence lifetime (τDF) slightly increased from 240 ns to 248 ns, the DF component jumped significantly, from 12.41% to nearly 19% (Fig. 2f and Table 1). This represents a ~7% absolute increase and a striking 58% relative improvement. The heavy atom effect and precise alignment of 1,4-DIB inside the macrocycle likely boosted PL performance. Fluorescence titration experiments with different guests (CPCQ⊃TPN, CPCQ⊃PN, CPCQ⊃1,4-DIB) yielded binding constants ranging from 2.18 × 10⁴ to 5.38 × 10⁴. We tested CPCQ’s effectiveness as a gas sensor by observing its PL responses to five distinct gases: O2, H2, SF6, SO2, and CH4. Introducing O2 led to intriguing changes (Fig. 3). PL intensity gradually declined as we increased O2 pressure from 5 mbar to 30 mbar (Fig. 3a). While quenching was expected, typical for emitters with TADF, we also saw an unexpected shift in the spectral shape. The emission transformed from broad and featureless characteristic of the CT excited state to narrower and more structured (locally excited, LE). This unusual spectral shift suggests O2 might block or suppress the CT pathway as a quencher. Fluorescence lifetime measurements further clarified this point, showing the complete disappearance of DF under O2 exposure (Fig. 3b and Table 1). To check if these changes were reversible, we cycled the sample between O2 and argon environments. Repeated cycles confirmed the fluorescence could reliably return to its initial state after each O2 purge (Fig. 3c). H2, however, delivered surprising results. Purging the sample with H2 gas at 30 mbar boosted the emission intensity nearly 3-fold within 5 min (Fig. 4a). Interestingly, the emission profile after H2 exposure closely matched the spectrum observed with O2, suggesting similar suppression of DF. Yet, the emission decay was noticeably faster with H2, aligning well with the increased intensity observed (Fig. 4b and Table 1). We wanted deeper insights into this phenomenon, so we compared CPCQ’s emission after H2 exposure to the spectra of its separate donor and acceptor units (Supplementary Fig. S6). Strikingly, the finding strongly indicates that under H2 (and similarly under O2) conditions, CPCQ spectrum after H2 purging closely mirrored that of the CPCQ’s emission originates predominantly from its donor unit. Nevertheless, a clear red shift was evident compared to the donor-only spectrum, with CPCQ’s emission onset shifting ~20 nm towards longer wavelengths. CPCQ’s emission intensity consistently recovered after each cycle, demonstrating excellent reversibility and reliability as a sensor material. We also tested how effectively CPCQ senses other gases like SO2, SF6, and CH4. When exposed to these gases at an initial pressure of 5 mbar, PL immediately quenched, as Supplementary Fig. S7 clearly shows. Both sulfur-based gases produced this consistent “turn-off” response, confirming CPCQ’s high sensitivity in solutions. To check if the PL could return, we purged the solution with argon. However, even prolonged purging did not restore PL. This irreversibility likely stems from interactions between CPCQ and heavier gases that lighter argon cannot remove. Thus, these results highlight CPCQ’s powerful and permanent quenching effect in the presence of sulfur-containing gases and CH4.

We set out to understand how the CPCQ cage interacts with different aromatic molecules. To do this, we carried out molecular dynamics simulations of CPCQ with three distinct substrates: terephthalonitrile (TPN), phthalonitrile (PN), and 1,4-diiodobenzene (1,4-DIB). Each guest molecule was first docked into the CPCQ host, creating three separate host–guest complexes. Once docked, every complex underwent 500 ns of simulation to test whether the guest could remain stable inside the cage (Fig. 5a). Full details of these simulations are provided in supplementary information. The results revealed clear differences. TPN and PN stayed inside the cage throughout the simulation, suggesting strong and stable host–guest interactions. By contrast, 1,4-DIB proved less stable. It displayed noticeable fluctuations and failed to maintain the same level of structural consistency as TPN and PN. This early observation hinted that the binding strength of 1,4-DIB would be weaker than the other two substrates. To get quantitative insights into the binding affinity of the guest molecules inside the host cage, we performed Molecular Mechanical Generalized Boltzmann Surface Area (MMGBSA) analysis for each system. The binding free energy for CPCQ⊃TPN was calculated at –16.48 kcal/mol, while CPCQ⊃PN showed a value of –14.97 kcal/mol. Both results indicate favorable binding. However, CPCQ⊃1,4-DIB gave a much weaker value of –10.19 kcal/mol (Table 1). The difference underscored what the simulation had already suggested: TPN and PN bind tightly, whereas 1,4-DIB does not. A breakdown of the energy contributions, including electrostatics, van der Waals forces, and solvation effects, can be found in Supplementary Tables S4–S6. When we analyzed the simulation trajectories, we found that TPN showed stable π–π stacking interactions with the aromatic parts of the CPCQ cage. PN displayed weaker stacking, while 1,4-DIB barely engaged in such interactions at all. We then turned our attention to gas diffusion within the cage environment. Using molecular dynamics simulations, we studied how H2 and O2 behaved inside CPCQ. Each system was prepared with forty molecules of gas and one CPCQ cage using PACKMOL software, followed by 500 ns of simulation. The diffusion patterns revealed a striking contrast. H2 diffused quickly, with an average coefficient of 3.25 × 10−9 m2 s−1. O2 moved far more slowly, with a coefficient of 5.16 × 10−11 m2 s−1 (Table 1). The trajectory data confirmed these findings. Mean square displacement plots showed a near-linear growth for H2, while O2 displayed only a gradual slope. Images from the simulations (Fig. 5b, c) visually confirmed how hydrogen spread freely around the cage, while O2 moved in a far more restricted manner (Fig. 5b, c, bottom panel and Supplementary Fig. S9).

Discussion

Experiments in controlled gas environments reveal just how adaptable CPCQ is as a gas sensor. With a compact cyclic structure of four donor–acceptor units packed within an 8-10 Å region (8 Å wide, 14 Å tall), CPCQ responds sharply to changes in its chemical surroundings. When CPCQ is exposed to low-pressure O2 in solution, its emission fades slowly as the broad CT band gives way to a sharper, more distinct local emission, while delayed fluorescence vanishes and TADF, once active, disappears, proving that the CT route breaks down. This disruption is further confirmed by a drop in fluorescence lifetime from 12.1 ns before O2 purging to 7.4 ns afterward, which points to an increased dynamic quenching in the presence of O2. What’s more compelling is its reversibility. When argon is introduced, CPCQ regains its TADF feature. The emission profile returns to its earlier state, confirming that its O₂ sensitivity is not only specific but also fully reversible, an essential quality for practical gas sensors.

In contrast, H2 exposure leads to a very different transformation. After 5 minutes of H₂ purging at 30 mbar, CPCQ emits a much brighter LE band, nearly 3-times more intense. Its emission spectrum narrows significantly, with the full width at half maximum (FWHM) dropping from 72 to 50 nm. Although this change might resemble the post-O2 profile, it must reflect a key difference: the emission peak shifts ~20 nm to longer wavelengths as compared with that donor. This, combined with the spectral features, suggests that donor units now dominate the LE emission. Time-resolved fluorescence measurements reveal further insight. A single, short-lived decay component of 4.5 ns emerges with suppression of delayed component (Table 1). Although the emission after H2 exposure may resemble the donor’s, its slight red shift, boosted radiative rate, and stronger transition dipole moment must suggest that superradiance (SR) could be at play. Because all four D–A pairs sit within just 10 Å, far below the 450 nm emission wavelength, their tight cyclic arrangement in CPCQ must play a deeper role, possibly enabling the conditions needed for superradiance (SR)62–64. This phenomenon allows for coherent coupling among nearby dipoles, resulting in amplified emission. Theoretically, four coupled units (N = 4) should produce a 16-fold intensity (N2 = 16) increase. CPCQ after H2 exposure exhibits about 4.5-fold enhancement, consistent with two units (N = 2) emitting coherently. Even more striking is the contrast between CPCQ and its isolated donor and acceptor components. Donor and acceptor units alone produce radiative rates of ~4.76 × 106 and 2.86 × 106 s−1, respectively. CPCQ, after H2 exposure, reaches 2.07 × 108 s−1, several orders of magnitude higher. This cooperative behavior, which must arise from their close spacing, could explain the striking response to SR and reveal the strength of the cyclic design. Furthermore, we observe that both emission intensity and lifetime remain essentially unchanged upon cooling from 298 to 190 K, showing that nonradiative decay channels are already strongly suppressed at ambient temperature and that the H2-induced enhancement does not arise from temperature-driven conformational rigidification (Fig. S8 and Table S3). Although this result does not constitute direct evidence for superradiance, it favors an interpretation based on intrinsic modulation of the electronic excited states rather than trivial thermal or structural effects. In systems dominated by localized emission, cooling typically increases intensity through further suppression of nonradiative pathways, a trend that is absent for the H2-induced emission enhancement in CPCQ. Since we find no direct evidence for physisorption or preferential localization of hydrogen within the cavity beyond global diffusion analysis, the observed emission enhancement and emergence of a localized emissive feature most plausibly arise from an increased transition dipole of the excited states that emit coherently. By responding distinctively to both O2 and H2, and doing so with sharp reversibility and high sensitivity, CPCQ proves itself to be a formidable candidate for gas detection. These findings uncover not only a high-performing sensor but also a molecular platform capable of complex, tunable emission, an ideal foundation for future advances in sensing and energy-transfer technologies.

Conclusions

In summary, the optical properties of CPCQ were systematically studied in the presence of small molecular guests and under various gaseous environments, revealing its strong potential as a versatile platform for energy transfer and sensing applications. The formation of exciplexes with electron-deficient guests demonstrated its sensitivity to electronic interactions, while the incorporation of a heavy atom-containing guest led to a marked enhancement in thermally activated delayed fluorescence. Both oxygen and hydrogen purging caused a transition in emission profile from a broad charge transfer band to a more localized spectral feature, with hydrogen exposure producing a threefold increase in emission intensity. Additionally, hydrogen gas induced a superradiance-type behavior. Exposure to sulfur-based gases and methane triggered a complete “turn-off” response. These distinct responses to different analytes demonstrate CPCQ’s capacity to modulate its excited-state properties based on its chemical environment. The reversibility of these effects (for H2 and O2 gases), particularly with inert gas purging, further strengthens its practicality for real-world sensing systems. These findings establish CPCQ as a responsive and adaptable photophysical system, offering a promising foundation for the development of next-generation materials in photosensitization and gas sensing technologies.
