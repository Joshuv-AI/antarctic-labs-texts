An investigation of theoretical model of the relationship between discussion with families and the attitude toward organ donation in Iran: the mediating role of Spiritual Well-Being
Authors: Hamid Sharif-Nia, Noushin Mousazadeh, Mahnaz Rakhshan, Roghye Nazari, Maryam Dehghani, Hamideh Hakimi, Setayeshi Jafari BenahKhalkhal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is still a serious imbalance between the number of organ donors and the number of patients on the transplant list. It is therefore of great importance to assess and strengthen resources to improve people’s attitudes towards organ donation. This study examines the possible mediating role of Spiritual Well-Being (SWB) in relation to attitudes towards organ donation and family discussions in Iranian society.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional study conducted in Iran in 2022. Participants included 316 members of the Iranian general population aged 18 years and older who participated in the study using convenience sampling methods. Data were collected using a demographic profile form, and the standardized Spiritual Well-Being Scale (SWBS) by Paloutzian and Ellisons, and two researcher-made questionnaire including Attitudes towards organ donation and discuss with family. The researchers used path analysis to analyze the mediation model. All the statistical analyses were done using the SPSS_AMOS 27 software.ResultsThe majority of participants were single professional women with a bachelor’s degree. Pearson correlation test results showed a positive correlation between attitudes towards organ donation and family discussions (r = 0.522, p < 0.001). There was also a positive association between attitudes towards organ donation and SWB (r = 0.247, p < 0.001). Similarly, results indicate a partial association between family discussion and attitudes toward organ donation, which is influenced by the mediating variable SWB and family affected in a manner similar to that seen for the variable ‘discussing with’. And ‘attitudes toward organ donation’ are mediated by varying SWB (B = 0.227, p < 0.009). SWB and discuss with family explain 30% of the variance.ConclusionAttitudes toward organ donation were directly influenced by discuss with family members and improved by SWB accordingly. This study suggests that SWB is integral to attitudes towards organ donation in Iranian society.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Attitude, Organ donation, Spiritual Well-Being, Discussion with families

Introduction

Organ donation technology is one of the most effective treatments to help patients suffering from end-stage organ failure [1, 2]. Successful donations not only increase patient life expectancy and quality, but also significantly reduce treatment costs [3, 4]. However, many patients suffering from organ failure lose their lives due to the lack of organs for transplantation [5, 6]. Despite tremendous progress in the field, there is a large disparity between the number of organs donated and the number of patients on waiting lists for transplantation [2]. This widening disparity between the number of organ donors and organ transplants is seen as a key challenge for governments [7].

Organ donation is an act that cannot be carried out with just a positive attitude [8]. Various studies have shown that attitudes toward organ donation and the tendency to register as an donor are associated with economic and social status, Islamic beliefs, empathy, humanism, and trust and perceptions of the health care system is shown [3, 4, 9–13]. Religious beliefs also play an important role in changing people’s attitudes towards death and organ donation. One of the most common religious beliefs that can provoke opposition to donations is the preservation of the deceased’s whole body for the afterlife [10]. In this regard, Parsa. et al. (2019) reported that religious beliefs significantly influenced organ donation decision-making in Iran [14]. Another factor that influences organ donation is spirituality. Its impact is different in communities with different religious beliefs and cultures [9, 10, 15].

SWB can be defined not only by socio-psychological and religious factors, but also by propensity toward interpersonal relationships, life purpose, and belief in divine power. This is a multidimensional construct reflecting both an individual’s sense of meaning and purpose in life (existential well-being) and the quality of one’s relationship with God or a higher power (religious well-being). This conceptualization highlights that SWB is experienced in daily life, is shaped by social and cultural contexts, and provides the foundation for attitudes and behaviors, including those related to health and altruism [16]. Social psychology describes human emotions and answers questions such as: Who are they? What are they doing and why? Where do they belong While the religious aspect puts the individual on a path of connection with God, the existential aspect is what brings the human being beyond the ego and in harmony with others and the environment. Research shows that the socio-psychological aspects of spirituality can encourage people to participate in voluntary humanitarian activities such as organ donation [13, 17].

SWBdoes not develop independently of social and relational contexts. Evidences indicates that spiritual beliefs and values are largely shaped, interpreted, and reinforced within close interpersonal environments, particularly the family. In religious societies such as Iran, families serve as a primary context for transmitting religious meanings, nurturing SWB, and translating spiritual beliefs into concrete health-related attitudes and decisions. Consequently, the influence of religion on attitudes toward organ donation is often expressed indirectly through SWB and family-based discussions rather than through individual belief alone [18–20].

Among the factors affecting organ donation, one of the biggest obstacles to successful organ donation is family dissatisfaction. This decision is influenced by many factors, including the cultural or religious beliefs of family members and close friends. The issue is therefore of national and global concern [11, 21] Although organ donation is influenced primarily from a “life-giving” perspective, the preservation of the deceased’s whole body is also an important consideration when making family organ donation decisions. The main reasons for not registering as a donor are family disagreements, reluctance to give up the organ, and disappointment of friends and family [14, 22].

In a study by Shaw. et al. (2017), One of the easiest ways is to encourage people who want to register as donors to persuade their family and friends of the decision and emphasize the importance of this action [23]. families who knew a deceased member’s organ donation decision before their death were twice as likely to donate than families who were unaware of the deceased member’s decision [2]. Qualitative results of a study by Parsa et al. (2019) found that participants who consented or opposed organ donation said they consented to organ donation after brain death only if their family members did so. Many of these people mentioned the impact their interactions with family members have on family decision-making [14]. The results of another study showed that 46% of people received donation-related information from their families; therefore, family is a significant factor affecting people’s attitudes toward organ donation [24].

The family contains the strongest biological, psychological, and social bonds where individuals experience becoming real human beings [25]. A person’s character is primarily shaped by family, and the family’s performance determines the individual’s social and moral health. Thus, the formulation of behavioral patterns like humanitarian tendencies and spirituality is established in the family [26].

In most countries, it is the family that primarily decides whether a member should donate an organ. Therefore, you should consult your family when making this decision [2, 27]. Making such a decision is greatly influenced by many factors. Chief among these is recognizing people’s desires and needs for life after death. This realization is gained by talking and talking with family members while the person is alive [28]. Conformity to the family’s patterns and discussing with them are encouraging steps for organ donation. Discussions continue in a way that families motivate all family members willingly to discuss these issues. Conformity is the families’ attempt to facilitate the conditions for emphasizing attitude equations, values, and beliefs. Therefore, having discussions and talks with family members can have a significant role in creating humanitarian tendencies [29].

Given the numerous and long-standing barriers to organ donation worldwide, death and disability are inevitable for many patients requiring organ transplantation [15]. However, many people in society don’t have positive attitude toward organ donation [30]. Perhaps the main reason for this is the novelty and anonymity of many aspects of this law, apart from the factors affecting it. The first step in planning appropriate mediation to increase organ donation acceptance is to identify the factors that influence organ donation. Recognizing these factors can help medical teams, managers, and planners to support individuals and families in organ donation decision-making and provide a framework for practicing positive attitudes towards organ donation in the community [14, 31, 32]. While interest in the role of SWB and its relationship to discuss with family in promoting an attitude to organ donation is increasing, there remains limited research that has evaluated these relationships in the general population in society. Although some research in Islamic countries has examined attitudes toward organ donation and the influence of religious and social factors, there remains a notable gap in understanding how SWB functions as a mediator between family discussions and attitudes toward organ donation within these cultural contexts. A study in Turkey found that Islamic belief and SWB significantly influenced attitudes toward organ donation among adults, highlighting cultural and religious dimensions of donation decision-making [33]. In the United Arab Emirates, community knowledge and attitudes about organ donation were shaped by religious understanding and awareness levels, with many participants reporting limited knowledge despite willingness to accept or consider donation [34]. Similarly, studies from Saudi Arabia indicate that although general willingness to donate may be moderate to high, barriers such as lack of awareness, misconceptions about religion, and family concerns persist, suggesting complex socio-cultural determinants of donation behavior. However, no study has specifically examined the mediating role of SWB in the relationship between family discussion and attitude toward organ donation in Iran or other Islamic societies [35]. Therefore, investigating this mediating pathway can clarify culturally relevant mechanisms and inform tailored strategies to promote organ donation more effectively. So, the study was to investigate a theoretical model of the relationship between discussion with family and attitudes toward organ donation in Iran and to evaluate the mediating role of SWB, so that by using its results, people’s attitudes towards organ donation and the act of organ donation in society be increased.

Following hypotheses were presented for this study:

Hypothesis (1)

Discussion with the family positively affects the attitude toward organ donation.

Hypothesis (2)

There is a positive relationship between discussion with family and SWB.

Hypothesis (3)

There is a positive relationship between SWB and attitude toward organ donation.

Hypothesis (4)

The relationship between discussion with family and attitude toward organ donation is mediated by SWB.

Methods

Settings and participants

This is a descriptive study of the cross-sectional type conducted in 2022–2023. Inclusion criteria were (1) People age 18years and older, (2) being able to read and write in Persian, and Some exclusion criteria included; Individuals with severe mental health conditions, people with cognitive impairments and individual with lack of spiritual or religious beliefs.

They were all selected using convenience sampling method. Considering the effect size 0.2 in a significant level α < 0.05, the power of test %80, 39 items, and three latent variables, the estimated sample size was 296 people, and considering the missing data, the sample was determined to be 316 people. All participants voluntarily completed the survey via electronic sources such as social media, and smartphone. In this way, the link was placed in social networks that had more than ten thousand members and people from different groups were members. At first, the participants received explanations about the purpose of the study before completing it. They were also reminded they would remain anonymous in all the study phases and that the questionnaire would be given them to complete if they consciously consented to participate and they were18 years and above. Convenience sampling using online questionnaires distributed through social media platforms, may introduce selection bias, as individuals with greater interest in organ donation or better internet access may be overrepresented, limiting the generalizability of the findings.

Instrument

The researcher used a demographic information form and questionnaire to gather data. The first part of the demographic information form included age, sex, marital status, occupation, and educational status. The second part of the questionnaire was for “the attitude toward organ donation,” which consisted of thirteen questions on the five-point Likert scale, with scores from very low (1 point) to very much (5 points). The higher the point, the stronger the attitude toward organ donation. This questionnaire was developed by the research team based on a review of literature and adapted from items used in prior studies [32, 36–41]. For content validity, at first we used the opinions of ten expert faculty members, then approved the questionnaire with CVI (0.96) and CVI (0.84) calculation for " the attitude toward organ donation”. The reliability of the questionnaire was measured using Alpha Cronbach that was 0.90. The questions explained 62.1% of the variance in the attitude toward organ donation.

The third part of the questionnaire is about “discussion with families” that includes six questions on a five-point Likert scale with scores from very low (point 1) to very much (point 5). In this study, “discussion with family” is defined as informal and intentional conversations among family members specifically about organ donation, during which participants express their wishes, beliefs, and preferences regarding organ donation and negotiate or share their perspectives within the family context. The questionnaire was developed by the researcher team and is composed after reviewing different literatures [39–41]. For content validity, at first, we used the opinions of ten expert faculty members, then approved the questionnaire with CVI (0.92) and CVR (0.84) calculation for “discussion with families”. The reliability of the questionnaire was measured by Alpha Cronbach. It was 0.84. The questions in this questionnaire explained 51% of the variance for the variable “discussion with families.”

In the fourth part, the Paloutzian and Ellison SWBS is introduced. It includes twenty questions on a six-point Likert Scale from strongly disagree (1 point) to strongly agree (6 points). The scores are between 20 and 120. The SWB score for people divides into three categories: low (20–40), medium (41–99), and high (100–120). The reliability of the questionnaire was measured at 0.84 using Alpha Cronbach. The validity and reliability of the present scale have been examined in various studies in Iranian culture [42–44].

Data analysis

The data was analysed using descriptive-inferential statistics. Considering the significance level of 0.05 to examine the normality of the study, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used. In order to evaluate the hypotheses of the study, first the significance level and severity between the variables were examined using the Pearson correlation coefficient, then they were assessed using the path analysis approach proposed by Barron & Kenny. The first hypothesis was assessed initially to evaluate the relationships. Then, the relationship between the independent and mediating variables was examined. Finally, the mediating variable effect was evaluated with Barron & Kenny approach. All the statistical analyses were done using the SPSS_AMOS 27 software.

Discussion

This study evaluates the role of SWB in mediating the relationship between discussions with families about organ donation and the attitude toward organ donation on a large group among the people in Iranian society.

In this regard, the final model of the study illustrated that the variable “discussion with families” with the presence of the mediating variable “SWB” has the potential to explain a 30% variance for the variable “attitude toward organ donation”. This finding admits the positive role of spirituality in changing Iranian families’ attitudes toward the humanitarian act of organ donation. According to Islamic beliefs, one of the concerns that Muslims may have about death is the fear of the hereafter [45]. SWB increases mental health, thereby reducing the death anxiety. The inner peace that comes from understanding what life means to you creates self-esteem, adaptability in life, a sense of responsibility, and reduces the fear of death [46]. In the Iranian-Islamic culture, family is the most commonly desired human constitution; thus, its performance determines its members’ physical, social, and moral health and influences all these aspects [26]. On the other hand, spirituality can help people face crises in critical times comprehensively; it also affects the way people infer the events; hence the adapting process is facilitated [29]; Thus, mentally healthy people are capable of powerfully experiencing and grasping purpose and meaning through interpersonal and intrapersonal relationships [47]. However, the interactions among family members are highly affected by the level of intimacy and feedback they receive. The orientation of the talks in the family predicts SWB positively [29]. As a result, the more the family members discuss with each other, the better would be their evaluations of the events and incidents happening to them; thus, they will face it more congenially and have a higher level of SWB [48].

The human dimension of spirituality is how people seek and communicate meaning in their lives, and how they feel and value their connection to themselves, others, nature and the world in the present. It refers to something that feels sacred [43]. Addressing the topic of family and SWB together is significant because this is one of the main barriers to meeting organ transplanting needs in more than 50 countries of the world is family disagreement [31, 49]. In case the family opposes, the chances for organ donation decrease [31]. Whether or not organ donation runs counter to religious beliefs, one resistance may be related to family members’ religious beliefs. Some families believe that deceased donors are blessed and rewarded for following religious teachings, loving and helping others, and fulfilling specific missions on earth [31, 49].

A clear example of this belief in the teachings of Islam is verse 32 of Surah Ma’ida, which says, “Whoever saves a person from death, it is as if he has saved all people from death " [50]. Therefore, Muslims also consider the saving act of donation as an opportunity to donate “life” that provides meaning and a sense of peace for families [31]. Tarzi. et al. also evaluated various factors affecting the tendency for organ donation; they mentioned religious reasons as the second most common factor [51]. Another study in Saudi Arabia stated humanitarian and religious reasons as the most significant factors in accepting organ donation [52].

Initiating discussions in the family on the topic of organ donation has a positive influence on people’s attitudes toward this act. Family consent means one of the family member’s consent on behalf of a potential donor [53]. The family’s attitude toward organ donation is crucial in consenting [2].

In most cases of deceased organ donation, the deceased or dying patient has not made a prior decision about organ donation. Therefore, the family has to make this decision. More difficult were cases in which the patient had already registered as an organ donor, but the family abstained from this decision and stopped the transplant process [28]. Perhaps the reason is that most of the families who agree to donate the organs of their deceased members cite as one of the main reasons that they know that the majority of potential donors It is believed that he did not inform his family and friends of his decision. you will know the dead [54]. This realization can only be achieved by talking to family members. However, some studies in Western countries have shown positive effects of clearly communicating organ donation decisions within families [27, 41, 55, 56]. Families who were informed about the decision of the deceased in advance consented to this action twice as often as those who were unaware of the decision. This is because in the sad time of losing a family member, respecting the will of the deceased means condolences for them [54].

Although SWB partially mediates the relationship between family discussion and attitudes toward organ donation, other factors such as culture, cognition, and healthcare environments, especially in collectivist and religious societies, where expectations and social conformity play a central role, influence related decision-making [57]. Furthermore, diversity in religious knowledge and interpretation may lead to different understandings of the permissibility and meaning of organ donation, creating uncertainty or hesitation despite positive spiritual orientations [58]. Trust in the health care system and trust in medical professionals have also been identified as key determinants of willingness to donate, as concerns about fairness, transparency, or abuse can undermine positive attitudes even when spiritual beliefs are supportive [59].

As the majority of Iranians are Muslim, and spirituality is a notable component in Islamic teaching; in this regard family may have a strong influence on SWB among Iranian people. The results of this study showed that discussion with family toward Individual opinions, wishes and desires has a positive effect on the attitude towards organ donation. SWB plays an important role in the way that individuals perceive toward organ donation and potentially contributing to decide to donate or not.

Implication and limitation

While research on SWB and its’ role in promoting attitudes toward organ donation in people is growing, there are limited studies investigating strategies to improve SWB. Therefore, while the exploratory research adds to what is known, it also sets the stage for further inquiry for individuals, especially in the Iranian cultural context. Furthermore, despite the high need for organ donation, the rate of donors is also low globally. The present study showed that considering the religiousness of the Iranian society and the influence of religion on people’s attitudes, it is possible to increase their willingness to donate with religious education and changes in the cognitive structure of people, and this can be done through family discussions. This finding can aid in the design of effective interventions to promote organ donation, identify potential barriers, and provide appropriate spiritual care to donors and their families.

This study has some limitation; one of the main limitations of the study is the use of convenience sampling, which may have resulted in selection bias and limited the generalizability of the findings. To improve representativeness, future research should consider stratified sampling approaches. The questionnaires were completed using self-report method, there was a possibility of bias in the responses, which could affect the generalizability of the findings. The important thing is to raise awareness about the importance of examining constructs such as spirituality and the role of the family in people’s attitudes towards organ donation. However, spirituality is an inner resource that is subjective and personal, and surveys may not best reflect each individual’s specific experience. Despite the fact that 78.5% of Iranian citizens use social media, there is a small percentage of people who do not have access to smartphones and social networks, so it was not possible for these people to participate in the present study. Another important limitation of this study was the lack of standard model fit indices for the path analysis and it was not possible to perform confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) on the sample as a result, the precise assessment of how well the proposed model fits the data was not available. Future studies should include CFA on larger and more diverse samples to evaluate model fit and strengthen confidence in these findings. This issue can also be a limitation for the study.

Conclusion

This study aimed to investigate the role of SWB in mediating the relationship between discussing with family and attitude toward organ donation in Iranian society. Although more studies are needed, findings suggest that fostering SWB and continuing to discuss with family are positive aspects of supporting organ donation attitudes in the Iranian society. The information provided can help policymakers to establish future strategies to promote organ donation programs and overcome current barriers based on developing SWB and discussion in families regarding the willingness to donate organs.

Recommendation

The current study was quantitative and cross-sectional. It is suggested that future studies be conducted qualitatively through group discussions, interviews, and observations with the participation of families to explain the factors influencing the family’s decision to accept or reject the donation of their loved ones.
