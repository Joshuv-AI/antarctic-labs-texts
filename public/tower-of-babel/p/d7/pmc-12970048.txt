Examining social camouflaging and its cognitive, mental health and neurophysiological correlates in autistic adolescents through triangulation-based assessment using self, parent and daily diary reports: a study protocol
Authors: Jessica Klein, Rachel L Krahn, Stephanie J Howe, Allison Cormier, Jonathan Rush, Megan Ames, Carly A McMorris, Sarah Macoun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSocial camouflaging (SC; ie, the concealing of autistic traits to socially assimilate) is associated with poor mental health, self-identity and quality of life outcomes, yet its typology, consequences and contextual triggers remain unexplored in autistic adolescents. Further study is necessary to identify protective factors against the potentially negative outcomes associated with SC to promote long-term well-being.MethodsThe current project will investigate SC in youth and its mental health, cognitive and neurophysiological correlates. Camouflaging will be captured by triangulating self-reported and caregiver-reported SC behaviours, as well as SC behaviours in day-to-day contexts using intensive longitudinal methods (ie, daily diaries). Non-autistic, self-identifying autistic and formally diagnosed autistic adolescents aged 15–18 years (N=150) will be recruited. Adolescents and caregivers will complete rating scales to assess mental health, and adolescents will complete in-lab cognitive assessments of attention, executive function, intellectual ability and theory of mind. Brain neurophysiological function and cortisol concentration over time will be measured during the same visit using electroencephalography and hair samples, respectively. Over the following 7 days, adolescents will complete daily diaries using their smartphones. The daily diaries pose survey questions about the type and degree of SC behaviour used within their daily environment, including the social context, individuals who are present and current well-being. Adolescents will also complete brief performance-based cognitive assessments of attention and executive function integrated within the daily diary surveys. Finally, adolescents and their parents will complete a follow-up of SC behaviours and mental health at 3 months. Correlations and regression analyses will be conducted to explore the associations between SC and mental health/cognitive outcomes and how baseline measures of cognition, mental health and SC predict patterns seen on the daily diaries. Multilevel modelling will be used, nesting daily data to capture within-person and between-group differences in contextual predictors of camouflaging behaviour. Results will contribute to current understanding of the typology of camouflaging, as well as inform intervention to mitigate mental health challenges for autistic youth.Ethics and disseminationThis project is approved by the University of Victoria Human Research Ethics Board (#23–0013) and the University of Calgary Conjoint Faculties Research Ethics Board (#23–0641). Informed consent will be obtained from caregivers and adolescent participants, and safety procedures will be put in place to support the adolescent should mental health concerns arise. Results will be disseminated through academic publications and conferences, as well as summarised and communicated to interested participants and relevant stakeholders.

Introduction

Social camouflaging (SC) is defined as the masking or compensation of autistic traits to outwardly present in a socially normative way.1 SC has gained clinical relevance in recent years as a potential risk factor for adverse impacts, rather than as an adaptive social behaviour. Autistic people report being motivated to use SC behaviours as a means to connect with others and reduce social stress.1 However, SC is often associated with negative mental health outcomes,24 burnout,1 a loss of identity1 5 and lower quality of life.4

SC behaviours include a range of active strategies used by autistic people to hide or compensate for differences associated with autism in social contexts. These behaviours typically fall into one of three categories: (1) masking, (2) compensation and (3) assimilation.6 7 Masking behaviours are employed to conceal autistic traits, such as hiding special interests in social settings or forcing eye contact.6 7 Compensation is the use of skills to work around social differences, for example, memorising social scripts or studying/mimicking others’ social behaviour.6 7 Finally, assimilation is the process of playing a social role in social contexts that may feel disingenuous or inauthentic to the individual.6 7

Though research in SC has increased in recent years, the exclusion of autistic adolescents from SC research is a notable limitation. Research focused on the typology and consequences of SC in autistic adolescents is imperative to shed light on the trajectory of SC behaviours and its associated consequences through the developmental period, with the goal of mitigating negative outcomes. Further, some research indicates that autism diagnoses may be missed or delayed in part due to the use of SC behaviours.1812 Research suggests autistic adolescents likely show similar associations to adults between SC and depression, anxiety and stress,21315 but there remains a dearth of research in this area.

Social camouflaging and mental health

Preliminary research indicates an association between SC behaviours and mental health consequences in autistic adolescents. Bernardin and colleagues investigated the internalising symptoms associated with the amount of intended SC self-reported by 140 autistic adolescents between ages 13 and 18 years, and found that SC was associated with greater depression, anxiety and stress.13 In a study with a wider age range (4–17 years), Ross and colleagues found that SC contributed to internalising symptoms, with increases in SC behaviours with age.14 Similarly, much of the research published on autistic adults has shown strong correlations between SC and mental health consequences, including depression, anxiety, perceived social non-acceptance and suicidality.1 4 15 This may be partially explained by evidence that mental health difficulties stem from the intense effort SC requires; SC is often described as physically, emotionally and mentally exhausting, and feelings of distress have been linked to feeling unauthentic or not true to oneself.1 16 17

Social camouflaging and cognition

Research focused on functional cognitive correlates of SC has shown inconsistent results for autistic adolescents and adults. Livingston and colleagues found that higher levels of SC were associated with intellectual function (IQ), executive function (EF) and anxiety in autistic children and adolescents 10–15 years of age (N=136).18 In contrast, Hull and colleagues explored cognitive predictors of SC in autistic adolescents aged 13–18 years (N=58) and found no significant correlations between SC and age, IQ or Theory of Mind (ToM), and only a weak correlation between SC and EF.19 Lai and colleagues examined the association between SC and cognition in autistic adults and found no significant associations between SC and age, verbal performance or full-scale IQ.20 Schuck and colleagues determined that working memory was not associated with SC behaviour in autistic adults.21 Inconsistencies in this research likely relate, at least in part, to the different paradigms, varying measures and different age ranges across studies, in addition to distinctive ways of measuring SC.

Most existing research examining cognition and SC has been correlational and cannot determine whether certain cognitive abilities/traits predict or are a consequence of SC behaviours.7 For adolescents specifically, the association between SC and cognition is complex, given ongoing brain development and associated variability in cognitive abilities.22 Further, previous work on cognition has focused predominantly on EF, but not other cognitive domains. It is likely that burnout resulting from SC behaviour may negatively impact attention, focus and learning,8 9 with consequences for long-term development. Given the role of ToM23 in social function and decision-making, this domain is also worthy of further study. This highlights the importance of investigating the association of SC behaviours with a range of cognitive abilities, to understand the directionality of associations and potential impacts of cognition on SC and SC on learning-related and socially-related cognitive processes.

Neurophysiological factors associated with social camouflaging

To date, few studies have explored the neurological and physiological mechanisms underlying SC. There is limited understanding of how these mechanisms influence the likelihood of engaging in SC or contribute to maladaptive mental health outcomes. Autistic individuals often describe SC as deeply exhausting and linked to feelings of anxiety, stress, sadness and identity confusion.5 Long-term dysregulation of the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis is strongly associated with mental health problems in autistic and non-autistic adolescents, including depression and suicide.24 25 When one’s body experiences an emotionally demanding situation, cortisol is released through the HPA axis. Frequent and excessive cortisol secretion can lead to dysregulation of the HPA axis and over time the development of mental health problems or vice versa.24 Hair cortisol concentration (HCC) is a biomarker of cortisol levels over time and is linked to chronic stress.24 While it is well-established that autistic people may have an atypically functioning HPA, both at rest and within social and non-social circumstances,25 26 no study to our knowledge has examined the association of HPA to SC behaviours, or whether an excessive stress response is an underlying mechanism that links SC to mental health difficulties in autistic people. Limited research has explored neural patterns associated with SC in autistic adolescents, although there is some evidence for an association between brain regions and connectivity patterns associated with attention, EF, ToM and SC.19 27 Event-related potentials (ERPs) represent the brain’s evoked neural responses to cognitive stimuli, assessing the ability and efficiency of neural systems to perceive and integrate stimuli.28 29 ERPs provide a non-invasive and objective means of assessing cognitive processing to explore the association between SC and brain function. NeuroCatch2 is a well-established translational framework for assessing cognitive evoked potentials using rapidly deployable, portable electroencephalography (EEG).30 NeuroCatch2 records three key ERPs spanning from low to higher-level information processing: auditory sensation (the N100 response),31 basic attention (the P300 response)32 and complex cognitive processing (the N400 response).33 The P300 and N400 signals are of particular interest in the current study, given the anticipated associations between attention/EF and SC behaviours.

Contexts of social camouflaging

SC occurs more frequently in environments where adolescents feel less comfortable, compared with those that feel familiar or safe. For both adolescents and adults, less SC behaviour is reported with trusted conversation partners or conversation partners that are also neurodiverse.15 8 1113 17 34 Adolescents report less SC at home or in their school resource centres than in regular classrooms.8 9 11 16 35 One potential explanation is that perceived stigma against autism may contribute to SC behaviours.9 11 12 34 36 37 In other words, stigma and social pressures may be more evident in these environments where SC is more frequently reported.11 12 34 Exploring the association between SC and environmental contexts can inform not only the causes of SC but also strategies for creating inclusive social environments wherein autistic individuals feel more comfortable and less motivated to camouflage.

Measurement of social camouflaging

The assessment of SC is relatively new, especially in autistic adolescents; however, a few measures have been developed to measure SC in autistic adults. The most frequently used measure of SC behaviour is the Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire (CAT-Q), which measures SC in the categories of masking, compensation and assimilation, will be combined into an equally weighted composite to derive a total score for overall camouflaging behaviour.6 The other frequently used method for measuring SC is the discrepancy method, whereby a clinical practitioner assesses the discrepancy between self-reported autistic traits and observed autistic social-communication behaviours.3 19 Notably, both the CAT-Q and discrepancy methods have primarily been used with autistic adults3 6 19 and neither captures SC behaviours in a holistic, naturalistic or ecological manner. There is a pressing need for tailoring the measurement of SC to adolescents, as well as employing multiple approaches to more comprehensively research SC behaviours.

One method that may better capture SC in autistic adolescents is the triangulation approach, which has been used to holistically capture autistic traits.38 Triangulation is an assessment method that uses three distinct and concurrent information sources to evaluate a particular trait or behaviour. Suggested best practice for a clinical diagnosis of autism in children includes a self-report, parent/caregiver report and an observed measure of behaviour.38 With respect to SC, triangulation has only previously been used by combining the discrepancy method3 19 with the CAT-Q,6 to capture observed autistic behaviour, self-reported autistic traits and self-reported SC behaviours.6 Hull and colleagues suggest that all current methods to operationalise SC behaviour have strengths and limitations, and that a combination of methods may be the most accurate way to study SC.6 However, to our knowledge, there is not yet an agreed-upon combination of assessment procedures to best capture SC in autism.

The present study

Existing research highlights the associations between SC, mental health, cognition and neurophysiology in autistic adults. However, there remains a critical gap in understanding these associations during adolescence, a period of heightened social dynamics and self-identity formation.11 12 Most studies to date have focused on autistic adults using narrow SC methodologies (ie, self-report questionnaires, controlled settings), which do not capture the dynamic and context-dependent nature of SC behaviours. Additionally, existing research on the association between cognition and SC in autism has primarily focused on EFs,18 19 overlooking other potentially relevant domains such as attention and ToM. To date, no studies to our knowledge have examined the underlying neurophysiological mechanisms of SC in autistic adolescents (ERP analysis) or its association with the stress-response system (HPA activation through HCC analysis).

To address these research gaps, the current study will focus on autistic adolescents aged 15–18 years. This study will explore the association of SC with mental health difficulties, as well as identity and socio-adjustment-related concerns. Cognitive associations with SC will be explored across several domains, including attention, ToM, EF and IQ. Neurophysiological associations with SC will be explored using ERP and hair cortisol concentration as a proxy for chronic stress.

To address previous limitations in SC measurement, the current study adopts a novel multimethod approach that combines intensive longitudinal assessments through using daily diaries with self-reports and caregiver-reports, to triangulate the assessment of SC behaviour and its associations. Though not previously used to study SC, daily diaries have been used in research with autistic adolescents.39 Daily diary methods will allow for assessment of real-time thoughts and behaviours of autistic adolescents in their natural environments, potentially reducing the risk of recall bias and increasing ecological validity.40 Adolescent self-reports capture the adolescent’s insight into their own thoughts and behaviours in addition to attempts to camouflage that may not be successful.6 To mitigate the limitations of self-reports, caregiver proxy-reports will also be used. This novel combination of self-report, caregiver-report and daily diaries will provide a more holistic assessment of SC and permit detailed, context-sensitive examination of SC behaviours and their cognitive and mental health associations in real-world settings.

The overall aim of the current study is to explore how SC behaviour relates to mental health, cognitive and neurophysiological functioning in autistic adolescents. Our specific objectives are:

Method

This study will employ a multimethod design involving in-lab cognitive, neurophysiological and mental health assessments, a 7-day daily diary period and a 3-month follow-up survey of SC and mental health. Pilot data collection began in July 2024 and was conducted to assess the feasibility and acceptability of study procedures, including the feasibility of our measures, sequencing and timing of assessments, clarity of instructions and functionality of the daily diaries. Results from pilot testing informed minor procedural refinements. Full data collection commenced in August 2025, with an anticipated end date of March 2027.

Participants

Adolescents (aged 15–18 years; N=150) of all genders will be recruited for this study, using purposive sampling and convenience sampling across two study sites. The sample will be divided into three groups: (1) formally diagnosed autistic adolescents (n=50), (2) self-identified autistic adolescents (n=50) and (3) non-autistic adolescents (n=50). Adolescents will be recruited through social media platforms (ie, Instagram, Facebook, Reddit), flyers sent to local autistic adolescent service providers, the University of Victoria Psychology research participation system (ie, Sona Systems), local community agencies and word of mouth. Inclusion criteria for Group 1 require a formal autism diagnosis made by a qualified professional (eg, paediatrician, psychologist, psychiatrist) that follows British Columbia’s (BC) and Alberta’s (AB) standards for autism spectrum disorder diagnosis and meets the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR)42 criteria. Group 2 will include adolescents who self-identify or are caregiver-identified as autistic without a formal diagnosis or those who are waiting for an autism assessment. These adolescents are included due to our recognition that substantial barriers exist that often impede access to formal autism assessments,43 as well as the diagnostic challenges that exist for autistic girls and gender diverse adolescents. In addition, research investigating SC will likely benefit from the inclusion of adolescents that may be traditionally excluded from autism research. Group 3 will consist of adolescents who do not have a formal autism diagnosis and who do not self-identify or caregiver-identify as having autism or another neurodevelopmental condition (eg, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder [ADHD], fetal alcohol spectrum disorder or learning disorder). Co-occurring diagnoses commonly associated with autism (eg, ADHD, anxiety, learning disorders) will be permitted for Groups 1 and 2 to enhance recruitment and generalisability. Inclusion criteria across all groups are participants aged 15–18 years, located in BC and AB, Canada. Participants must have an IQ score of 70 or above, functional language abilities and be fluent in English to complete the self-report and cognitive assessment measures. They must also have regular access to a smartphone or tablet device to complete daily surveys and mobile cognitive assessments. Gender identity will be determined based on self-report.

Monte Carlo simulations were conducted to estimate the statistical power of the planned multilevel modelling analyses to inform our sample size. We anticipate approximately 80–85% compliance to the daily diary protocol, resulting in between 840 and 890 observations out of a maximum of 1050 (150 participants×7 days). Simulations indicated that over 800 observations across 150 participants would provide sufficient power (>0.80) at an alpha level of 0.05 to detect small-to-moderate within-person effects. For the proposed analyses, a sample size of 150 (50 per group) will permit us to detect small to moderate within-person effects (Cohen’s d 0.2–0.5), as well as cross-level interaction effects of group differences.

Procedures

A flowchart outlining the participant protocol is presented in figure 1. Recruitment will occur on a rolling basis until all groups are full. Following recruitment, interested participants will complete a brief phone screening with a trained research assistant (RA) to confirm study eligibility and review study information. During this call, the RA will review the consent form with the adolescents and send them a copy via email. If they choose to participate, the adolescents will sign the form, followed by their caregiver. The signed form can be returned to the study team by email or brought to the in-person appointment. After screening and consent, participants will be scheduled for an in-person session at the Child Neuropsychology Lab at the University of Victoria or the Enhance Lab at the University of Calgary, based on where they reside. On arrival, an RA will review the study procedures with the adolescents and, if present, the caregiver (caregiver attendance is optional) and reconfirm consent. Adolescents attending alone will be required to bring signed caregiver’s consent, and all adolescents will be required to verbally re-consent before proceeding.

Participant protocol flowchart.

Around the time of the adolescent’s testing appointment, caregivers will complete a set of questionnaires, either in person or online, to provide information on their child’s SC behaviours, mental health/behaviour and adolescent/family demographics. These forms will be completed on Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a secure web-based application for building and managing online surveys and data storage44 and will take approximately 30 min. Parents will be given a $C20.00 e-card for survey completion. An RA will be available to answer questions and provide technical support as needed.

Adolescent participants will complete a fixed-order battery of standardised cognitive measures in a quiet room with an RA. Adolescents will then complete the ERP assessment using Neurocatch2 and will provide a hair sample to assess HCC. Finally, adolescents will complete self-report questionnaires related to SC, EF, attention and identity, stigma and mental health symptoms. Altogether, completion of the cognitive tasks and self-report measures will require approximately 2½–3 hours. Adolescents will be compensated with a $C40.00 e-gift card for completing the test session.

At the end of their testing, adolescents will be introduced to the daily diary component of the study. A brief, individualised training (approximately 15 min) will instruct adolescents how to install and navigate the diary application on their device. Over the following 7 days, adolescents will receive one daily evening prompt (~18:00-20:00) consisting of a brief survey assessing that day’s SC behaviours, cognitive experiences, mood, fatigue and stress. Surveys will include Likert-style, yes/no and optional open-ended responses (approximately 10 min). At the end of each survey, adolescents will complete brief (5 min) assessments of attention, processing speed and working memory integrated into the diary app. RAs will continuously monitor participant responses throughout the daily diary period and will be available to answer questions, provide resources should the adolescents be in distress and/or troubleshoot problems that arise. Adolescents will be provided with a $C40.00 e-gift card for completion of the daily diaries, prorated based on the number of days completed. Finally, at a 3-month follow-up, adolescents and their parents will be invited to complete an abbreviated battery of the same survey measures administered at the time of the initial testing session to assess SC, mental health and autistic burnout at 3 months. Screening measures

Caregivers of all participants will complete the Social Responsiveness Scale-second edition (SRS-245), a 65-item self-report questionnaire assessing the presence of autistic characteristics that has been used previously to screen for the presence of autistic traits when sampling autistic youth.46 Participants with an established formal diagnosis and self-identified autistic youth will be required to meet a total T-score cut-off of >59 on the SRS-2,45 and proof of prior diagnosis will be additionally requested for the formally diagnosed group. For non-autistic youth, the SRS-245 will be used to screen for the absence of autistic traits by confirming that scores fall below this cut-off point. Finally, to assess study eligibility on the basis of IQ, adolescents will complete the Kaufman Brief Intelligence Test-second edition (KBIT-247), which provides estimates of verbal, non-verbal and overall cognitive ability. Scores of <70 on the KBIT-247 will be screened out of the participant sample.

Caregiver-report measures

Caregivers will complete several standardised caregiver-report questionnaires to provide information on the adolescent’s SC behaviour, attention, EF and demographics. Questionnaires will be distributed through REDCap. The demographic form requests information on family structure, socioeconomic status, co-occurring diagnoses, medication use, intervention history and gender identity. The CAT-Q-Caregiver Report (CAT-Q-PR)48 is a 25-item measure adapted from the original CAT-Q self-report, to capture caregiver perceptions of SC behaviours.6 The caregiver-report version of the CAT-Q has good internal consistency (α=0.90), is strongly correlated with the CAT-Q self-report and has been used when autistic adolescents are unable to report on their own SC strategies.48 The Conners 4 Short Form (Conners-4) caregiver report assesses behavioural, emotional and cognitive characteristics associated with attention-related concerns.49 The Behaviour Rating Inventory of Executive Function - second edition (BRIEF-2) caregiver report assesses EFs across the domains of cognitive, behavioural and emotional regulation.50 At the 3-month follow-up, caregivers will be sent surveys to complete through REDCap, asking about current SC behaviours (CAT-Q), attention (Conners-4) and executive functions (BRIEF-2). Caregivers will be provided with $C40.00 in e-gift for completing rating scales ($C20.00 around the initial testing appointment and $C20.00 at the 3 month follow-up).

Adolescent self-report measures

Adolescent participants will also complete a series of standardised self-report questionnaires via REDCap, which assess SC, attention, EF, metacognition, autistic identity, stigma, anxiety, depression, burnout and belonging. The CAT-Q self-report combines an individual’s camouflaging behaviours across compensation, masking and assimilation domains to provide a composite score of overall camouflaging.6 The Autism Spectrum Identity Scale assesses adolescents’ identification with autism.51 A modified version of the Stigma Scale, which has been adapted to reflect autism-related experiences, will be used to assess the adolescent’s experience with stigma.52 The Reynolds Children’s Anxiety and Depression Scale53 will assess anxiety and depressive symptoms, respectively. The Autistic Burnout Severity Items will assess exhaustion and mental fatigue,54 and the Interpersonal Needs Questionnaire will assess belonging and perceived burdensomeness.55 56 The Suicide Behaviour Questionnaire-Revised will assess suicidal ideation.57 Adolescents will also complete the Conners 458 and BRIEF-259 self-reports to assess their attention and EF.

Adolescent cognitive assessments

Adolescent participants will complete a limited battery of performance-based tasks assessing EF, working memory and ToM. Sustained attention will be assessed using the Sustained Attention to Response Task.60 61 Executive function will be measured using the Minnesota Executive Function Scale, a tablet-based task assessing working memory, inhibition and cognitive flexibility suitable for ages 2 through adulthood.62 63 Verbal short-term memory and working memory will be assessed using the Digit Span Forward and Backward subtests from the Wechsler Intelligence Scales (WISC-V64 or WAIS-IV).65 ToM will be assessed using the Strange Stories task, which involves interpreting short vignettes that require social inference.66

Adolescent neurophysiological assessments

Neurophysiological function will be measured using NeuroCatch2.30 NeuroCatch2 is a rapid, non-invasive and portable EEG platform used to evaluate standardised normative response amplitudes and latencies for N100 (auditory sensation), P300 (basic attention) and N400 (cognitive processing). NeuroCatch2 is a Health Canada approved (Class II Medical Device) EEG technology used clinically to monitor within-subject differences in these key neurophysiological metrics of cognitive processing. NeuroCatch2 has demonstrated feasibility in paediatric and neurodiverse populations.296769 The P300 and N400 event-related potential components are associated with brain regions and functions that underlie attention, EF and complex cognitive processing. ERPs will be compared with SC behaviours to investigate how brain electrophysiological function relates to SC. Hair cortisol concentration will be measured using a hair sample collected from the child by the researcher (see hair collection protocol in online supplemental material) and participants will be asked to complete a questionnaire about their hair care. Using hair sampling to measure cortisol, cortisone, dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), and dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S) in conjunction with subjective daily records of behaviours and emotions, allow the examination of how to look at the stress response in the context of camouflaging behaviours in autistic adolescents.70 71 Hair samples will be collected proximal to the scalp, with a recommended minimum segment length of 2 cm which measures cortisol concentrations over the past 2 months.72

Daily diary assessments

Adolescents will complete daily self-report surveys on their mobile device each evening for seven consecutive days using the Pathverse mobile app.73 Pathverse is a mobile health platform suitable for intensive longitudinal studies. This mobile survey involves daily questions about adolescents’ SC behaviours, social contexts and well-being using a Likert scale (0–5), continuous slider scale and yes/no responses. It also provides the option for an open-ended response. Each daily survey takes approximately 15 min to complete. At the end of each survey, adolescents will complete three brief EF tasks (total time 5 min), integrated into the Pathverse app, which assess visual attention/memory, processing speed and working memory.74 This will permit us to investigate the daily associations between daily SC behaviours, mental health and attention/EF. Participants will be included in the daily diary analyses if they complete a minimum of three of the seven daily surveys. Participants who complete fewer than three diary days will be excluded from the diary analyses, and additional participants will be recruited to maintain the planned sample size.

Data analysis plan

Data analyses will be conducted in R using the ‘psych,’ ‘nlme,’ and ‘brms’ packages in RStudio. The daily diary data collected will have daily assessments nested within each individual and will thus be handled with multilevel modelling. Differences in contextual predictors of daily SC behaviours over the 7-day period will be examined on both the within-person level (ie, on days when certain environments or individuals are present predictive of greater SC than days when those environments/individuals are absent) as well as the between-person level (ie, do individuals who are exposed to certain environments more frequently across the 7 days also report greater SC behaviours on average relative to others). We will also look at variability in daily diary reports within-individuals and between-individuals, and whether associations differ across groups through cross-level and between-person interactions (ie, differences in contextual predictors between formally diagnosed autistic (Group 1), self-identified autistic (Group 2) and non-autistic (Group 3) groups). To examine the effect of daily SC behaviours on fluctuations in cognition, we will use within-person coupling models to examine whether daily variation in SC (relative to one’s typical day) predicts end-of-day cognitive performance.

An average SC score will be derived from daily diary data and will be combined into a total score with both the CAT-Q, self-report and caregiver-report scales to yield an equally weighted SC composite. Correlations will be run to capture the associations between the total SC score and mental health/cognitive outcomes. Regression analysis will additionally be conducted to examine the individual variation explained between daily and self-report/caregiver-report surveys in mental health and cognitive outcomes. Multiple linear regression analysis will be used to compare baseline test scores with SC and mental health reports at 3-month follow-up.

Patient and public involvement

This study design has been co-created and is being conducted in collaboration with three autistic community partners who are meaningfully engaged on an ongoing basis in all aspects of the research process. Their involvement includes contributing to the development of research questions, materials, informing methodological decisions and providing feedback on ethical and interpretive aspects of the study. Their partnership aligns with participatory research frameworks for autistic people (eg, Academic Autism Spectrum Partnership in Research and Education [AASPIRE]) and reflects a commitment to centring lived experience in autism research.75 76

Ethics and dissemination

Our study received ethical approval through the University of Victoria Behavioural Research Ethics Board (#23–0013) and the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (#23–0641). Participants will be considered a ‘competent adolescent’ in alignment with institutional ethics guidelines. Adolescents will provide signed and verbal consent to participate. Due to school district requirements and institutional policies, caregiver consent will also be obtained. However, adolescent consent will be prioritised, and all adolescents will be provided with clear information about the study and participant rights. Caregivers will be informed of the study via the consent form. To reduce participant risk, in-lab assessments are standardised and conducted with a trained RA, with a senior RA or the principal investigator available if needed. Adolescents will be informed during consent that caregivers or appropriate authorities may be contacted if imminent safety concerns arise. Daily diary procedures involve brief items only, and research staff will monitor participant responses. Adolescents may withdraw or pause participation at any time. Research findings will be disseminated through presentations at academic conferences and co-presented with autistic partners, and publications in peer-reviewed scholarly journals, as well as via open-access platforms. Additionally, a summary report detailing key results will be provided directly to participants and stakeholder groups approximately 3 months following data analysis. Anonymised data will also be made available on request to interested participants and researchers.
