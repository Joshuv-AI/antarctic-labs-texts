Mind, Mood, and Mobility: Rethinking Functional Disability Prevention in Older Adults on the Basis of a Longitudinal Cohort From CHARLS
Authors: Lijie Ding, Ruth Stephen, Maria Ekblom, Hui Xin Wang, Emerald G Heiland, Rui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDisability among older adults in China is becoming an increasing public health challenge due to rapid population aging. We aim to investigate how cognitive deficits, depression, and physical function limitation jointly contribute to the onset and progression of disability.MethodsWe included 4226 individuals aged ≥60 years from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, who were followed for a mean of 7.8 years (2010–2020). Physical function, cognition, and depressive symptoms were assessed. Outcomes included incident activities of daily living (ADL) disability and the number of ADL and instrumental ADL disabilities. Cox regression, linear mixed‐effects models, and structural equation modeling were applied.ResultsHaving ≥2 conditions of cognitive deficits, depression, and physical function limitation concurrently at baseline was associated with a higher risk of incident ADL disability (hazard ratio [HR]2 versus 0, 1.70 [95% CI, 1.44–2.00]; HR3 versus 0, 2.10 [95% CI, 1.65–2.68]) and a faster annual increase in ADL/instrumental ADL limitations compared with those without (β2×time=0.010 [95% CI, 0.007–0.013]; β3×time=0.014 [95% CI, 0.010–0.019]). The association between physical function limitations and longitudinal ADL/instrumental ADL accumulation appeared to be mediated by the biannual rate of cognitive decline and increases in depression scores.ConclusionsThe co‐occurrence of cognitive deficits, depression, and physical function limitations may synergistically drive long‐term disability. Our findings highlight the importance of early screening and holistic management of cognitive, mental, and physical health to reduce the burden of disability in aging populations.Keywords: cognitive deficits, depressive symptoms, functional disability, mediation, physical performanceSubject Categories: Epidemiology, Lifestyle, Primary Prevention, Risk Factors, Secondary Prevention

Research Perspective

What Is New?

The mind–mood–mobility axis plays a critical role in cardiovascular health. In this study, we found that the co‐occurrence of cognitive deficits, depressive symptoms, and physical function limitations synergistically accelerates incident functional disability and its progression over time. Furthermore, the impact of physical function limitations on accelerated disability progression was largely mediated by cognitive decline and depressive symptoms.

As one of the world's most populous nations, China is undergoing a rapid transition into an aging society. The number of people aged ≥60 years is expected to rise to 402 million by 2040, accounting for about 28% of the total population 1 and presenting a significant societal challenge, particularly in addressing disability and its impact on the functioning and independence of older adults. 2

The prevalence of functional disability among Chinese adults aged ≥60 years has remained around 26% over the past decades. 3 Functional disability in older adults may be assessed through questionnaires of activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental ADL (iADL). ADL measures limitations in basic self‐care activities (eg, bathing, dressing, eating), while iADL evaluates the ability to perform more complex tasks essential for independent living (eg, shopping, managing finances). ADL disability indicates multiple deficits that often require significant assistance with basic self‐care. The prevalence of ADL disability varies in different age groups, sexes, and regions, 3 and combining ADL and iADL assessments may provide valuable insight into the preclinical stages of disability and the progression of functional decline. 4 , 5 However, longitudinal evidence on combined ADL and iADL limitations and their predictors remains scarce in China, particularly across rural–urban settings and by sex. 6

Among older adults, cognitive deficits, depression, and physical function limitation often coexist and are correlated with ADL disability, highlighting the interconnection of mind, mood, and mobility. 7 , 8 Cognitive deficits can influence essential daily activities, such as financial management and medication adherence, while also increasing the risk of depressive symptoms. 9 In turn, worsening depression may accelerate cognitive and physical deterioration. 10 , 11 Previous studies have linked cognition, depression, and mobility and ADL disability, respectively, indicating their complex interrelationships, 7 , 12 , 13 yet few studies have explored their synergistic impact on ADL and iADL disabilities, 14 , 15 and those that did primarily focused on patient populations or individuals receiving home care. Furthermore, although the natural order of physical function limitation and cognitive deficits remains unclear, evidence suggests that severe physical function limitation can occur in 70‐year‐old individuals who do not have significant cognitive deficits. 16 Cognitive decline may progress more rapidly after the occurrence of physical function limitation, 17 and changes in psychological state or mood may accelerate or mediate the relationship between physical function limitation and functional disability. 18

In aging societies, understanding the axis of mind, mood, and mobility—specifically, the combined impact of cognitive deficits, depressive symptoms, and physical function limitation on both the onset of ADL disability and changes in numbers of ADL and iADL limitations over time—is crucial for identifying the needs of older adults and developing effective strategies to support independent living. Using data from the CHARLS (China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study) of older individuals aged ≥60 years, this study aimed to (1) assess the synergistic impact of cognitive deficits, depressive symptoms, and physical function limitation on incident ADL disability and changes in ADL and iADL limitations over time and whether these associations differ by sex and rural–urban settings; and (2) assess whether cognitive decline and progression of depressive symptoms mediate the relationship between baseline physical function limitation and the progression of ADL and iADL disability.

Methods

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from CHARLS upon reasonable request and with permission from the CHARLS data access committee (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/). The analytic code used for all statistical analyses is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Participants

This study is based on CHARLS, a nationally representative, publicly available open‐cohort data set comprising multiple survey waves with detailed information on social, economic, and health conditions of community‐dwelling residents. Details on the study design have been reported previously. 19 Initially, 150 county‐level units were randomly selected from a sampling frame stratified by region, district type (rural–urban settings), and per capita gross domestic product. Administrative villages in rural areas and neighborhoods in urban areas were used as primary sampling units, from which 3 primary sampling units were randomly selected in each county‐level unit. Adults and their spouses were invited to participate in the surveys. The present study included data from wave 1 (2011–2012), wave 2 (2013–2014), wave 3 (2015–2016), wave 4 (2017–2018) and wave 5 (2019–2020) based on the probability‐proportional‐to‐size sampling technique.

Overall, 6279 participants aged ≥60 years at baseline (2011–2012) with available physical performance assessments were included. Participants were excluded for the following reasons: missing ADL/iADL information (n=17), missing physical performance tests (n=41), history of neurodegenerative or psychiatric conditions (n=416), cancer or malignant tumor (n=55), baseline ADL disability (n=1297), and lack of follow‐up information (n=237). The final analytical sample included 4226 participants, of whom 3987 participated in the first follow‐up survey (CHARLS wave 2 [2013–2014]), 3604 in the second follow‐up (wave 3), 3109 in the third follow‐up (wave 4), and 2784 in the fourth follow‐up (wave 5) (see Figure 1 for detailed information). Ethical permission for the CHARLS data collection was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052‐11015), and each participant provided a consent form.

Measurements

Assessments of Functional Disability

At baseline and follow‐up, ADL questionnaires assessed whether participants had difficulties performing any of 6 activities: bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, feeding, and continence management. The iADL questionnaire focused on tasks including shopping, cooking, managing medications and finances, doing housework, driving or using public transportation, and using the phone or looking up numbers. 20 , 21 Responses were verified by caregivers if needed. Criteria for functional disability outcomes were defined as follows: (1) incident ADL disability: occurrence of at least 1 limitation in any of the 6 ADL items during the follow‐up period; and (2) accumulated ADL and iADL disability: a combined disability score calculated by summing limitations across all ADL and iADL items.

Physical Performance Measurements and Physical Performance Limitations

Participants completed a balance test in 3 standing positions (side‐by‐side, semitandem, and full tandem), holding each for 10 seconds to earn points. Participants received 1 point for each successful completion of the side‐by‐side and semitandem positions. In the full tandem, 2 points were given for holding the position for 10 seconds and 1 point for holding it between 3 and 9.99 seconds. The total balance score ranged between 0 and 4, with a higher score indicating better balance. 8 The chair stand test was conducted using a standard 45‐cm chair with 2 arms. Participants had to go from a sit to stand position as fast as possible with 5 repetitions to assess lower‐extremity strength. Each participant received a score based on completion time: 0 points (≥16.70 seconds), 1 point (13.70–16.69 seconds), 2 points (11.20–13.69 seconds), 3 points (8.70–11.19 seconds), and 4 points (<8.70 seconds). Higher scores reflect greater lower‐body muscle strength. The 4‐meter walking speed test was conducted with a 1‐meter flying start. Participants walked twice over a 2.5‐meter level surface at a comfortable pace for the entire distance. Each participant completed 2 trials, and the faster walking speed was used for analysis. The walking score was based on the time taken to complete the walk: 0 points (unable to walk), 1 point (≤0.43 m/s), 2 points (0.44 to 0.60 m/s), 3 points (0.61 to 0.77 m/s), and 4 points (≥0.78 m/s). The Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) score included assessments of balance, chair stands, and walking speed. The total SPPB score, ranging from 0 to 12, is the sum of the 3 test scores, with higher scores indicating better lower‐extremity function. 22 Physical function was further dichotomized into limitation (SPPB 0–9) and no limitation (SPPB 10–12).

Cognitive Assessment and Cognitive Deficits

Cognitive assessments included Telephone Interview of Cognitive Status‐10 and immediate and delayed word recall. 19 Telephone Interview of Cognitive Status‐10 focuses on orientation and numeric ability (attention and working memory), which are critical domains for global cognitive function and have been validated in previous studies. The total number of correct responses on Telephone Interview of Cognitive Status‐10 was recorded on a scale from 0 to 10. 19 , 23 Episodic memory was assessed using immediate and delayed word recall, 19 with the number of correctly recalled words constituting the respective scores. The average of immediate and delayed recall scores was calculated (range, 0–10). A global composite cognitive score was then derived by summing the Telephone Interview of Cognitive Status‐10 score and the average recall score. Cognitive deficits were coded binary, defined as scores ≥1 SD below the age‐specific mean for each wave. 24

Depressive Symptoms and Depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the short form of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES‐D). 25 This scale evaluates the frequency of symptoms over the past week, including irritability, distractibility, sadness, loss of interest, and fatigue. Each item is scored from 0 (“rarely or none of the time”) to 3 (“most or all of the time”), and higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. The total CES‐D score is calculated by summing all item scores, with higher totals reflecting greater overall symptom severity. Depression was coded as a binary variable, defined by a total CES‐D score ≥10. 25

Covariates

Baseline chronological age was treated as a continuous variable. Sex was dichotomized as men and women. Residential setting was dichotomized as rural versus urban. Education was categorized as illiterate, Sishu/elementary school, or middle school and above. Smoking status was classified as current versus ever/never. Body mass index was calculated from height and weight and dichotomized as obese (≥28 kg/m2) versus normal/overweight.

Medical and health conditions, including blood test results, medication use, psychiatric questionnaires, and disease history, were recorded, including hypertension, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, heart disease, chronic lung disease, liver disease, kidney disease, arthritis or rheumatism, digestive disorders, and asthma, as well as vision, hearing, or speech impairments. 26 Information on these health conditions was used to develop a multimorbidity index.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics by incident ADL disability were compared using t tests, Mann–Whitney U test, or χ2 tests as appropriate. The associations of baseline cognitive deficits, depression, and physical function limitation with ADL disability onset were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were reported. Incident ADL disability was defined as the first occurrence of the event during the follow‐up period. Survival time was calculated from baseline to the earliest onset of ADL disability, death, or the last follow‐up date. The models were adjusted for age, sex, education, rural–urban settings, obesity, smoking status, and multimorbidity index. The analyses were weighted using baseline individual sample weights to adjust for nonresponders and ensure sample representation. 27 Linear mixed‐effects models were used to examine the associations of baseline depression, cognitive deficits, physical function limitation, and their co‐occurrence with the accumulation of ADL and iADL disability over follow‐up. Separate models were fitted for each exposure variable (depression, cognitive deficits, physical function limitation, and number of co‐occurring conditions). In each model, the exposure variable, follow‐up time, the interaction term between the exposure variable and follow‐up time, and covariates (including demographic, lifestyle, and health‐related factors) were specified as fixed effects. Participant‐specific random intercepts, capturing between‐individual differences in baseline levels of ADL and iADL disability, and random slopes for time, capturing individual variability in disability trajectories over follow‐up, were included in all linear mixed‐effects models. Subgroup analyses stratified by sex and residential setting were conducted in both the Cox and mixed‐effects models, with analyses repeated within each subgroup to examine potential sex‐ and residential setting–specific associations. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the associations of each component and the continuous measures of physical performance, depression, and cognition with disability outcomes.

For mediation roles of cognitive decline and progression of depression score changes in the relationship between baseline physical function and changes in ADL and iADL item numbers, structural equation modeling was applied in 2 steps to construct a parallel process latent growth model (Figure S1). First, the independent variable (baseline SPPB score) and the outcome, measured as the latent slope of the ADL+iADL scores, were introduced to estimate the total effect of baseline SPPB on changes in ADL and iADL disability. In the second step, 2 mediators were added simultaneously to the model: the latent slope of repeated cognitive measurements (biennial rate of cognitive decline) and the latent slope of repeated depression scores (biennial change). In this model, we estimated the direct effect of baseline SPPB scores on changes in ADL and iADL limitations, as well as the indirect effects mediated by changes in cognition and depression. To further assess whether these relationships were consistent across sex and residential areas, we used multigroup structural equation modeling. A constrained measurement model was applied, with intercepts and residual variances fixed from the full sample to ensure measurement consistency across sex and residential areas. Group differences in path coefficients and indirect effects (ie, men versus women; urban versus rural) were formally assessed using the Wald test. All analyses were performed in STATA 17.0 software (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Synergistic Impact of Cognitive Deficits, Depression, and Physical Function Limitations on Functional Disability Outcomes

Baseline depression, cognitive deficits, and physical function limitations were all significantly associated with a higher risk of incident ADL disability, and these associations were even more pronounced when these conditions were present together (Table 2, Tables S2 and S3). Specifically, compared with participants without depression, cognitive deficits, or physical function limitations, those with at least 2 of these conditions had a higher risk of developing incident ADL disability in the multivariable‐adjusted model (HR2 versus 0, 1.70 [95% CI, 1.44–2.00]; HR3 versus 0, 2.10 [95% CI, 1.65–2.68]). The synergistic association of co‐occurring cognitive deficits, depression, and physical function limitations at baseline with incident ADL disability did not differ between men and women or between rural and urban residents (Table 2).

When the total number of ADL and iADL limitations was used as the outcome in linear mixed‐effects models, similar joint effects of baseline cognitive deficits, depression, and physical function limitation on cumulative disability were observed (Table 3, Tables S4 and S5). Specifically, participants with baseline cognitive deficits experienced a significantly faster increase in ADL and iADL scores during follow‐up compared with those without deficits (βcog×time=0.004 [95% CI, 0.001–0.006]). Mediating Role of Cognitive Decline and Changes in Depressive Symptoms

Results from the first step of the structural equation modeling (total effect model) (Figure 2A) showed a significant association between poorer lower‐extremity function (lower SPPB score) and a faster increase in ADL and iADL scores over time (βtotal effect=−0.028 [95% CI, −0.032 to −0.023]). In the mediation analysis (direct‐effect model) (Figure 2B, Figure S1), the results indicated that after accounting for the mediating effects of cognitive decline and increased depressive symptoms, the direct association between baseline SPPB and changes in ADL and iADL scores was largely attenuated (βdirect effect=−0.0003 [95% CI, −0.0112 to −0.0105]). In total, 98.8% of the association between baseline SPPB and changes in ADL and iADL scores was explained by mediation through cognitive and depressive trajectories, with 13.8% attributable to cognitive decline and 85.0% to depressive progression (see details in Table S6). No statistically significant differences by sex or residential setting were detected in the multigroup comparison models.

Discussion

Key findings of this study are as follows: First, cognitive deficits, depression, and physical function limitation at baseline synergistically increased the rate of developing ADL disability over 8 to 10 years and accelerated the accumulation of both ADL and iADL disability over time. These synergistic associations were consistent between men and women, as well as between rural and urban residents. Second, association between baseline physical function limitation and the progression of ADL and iADL disability over the follow‐up period was largely mediated by cognitive decline and worsening depressive symptoms over time. Together, these findings indicate the interconnection between mind, mood, and mobility in relation to long‐term functional disability. Poorer physical performance may accelerate disability accumulation via cognitive decline and depressive symptoms.

The association between physical function limitations and ADL disability has been well documented in community‐dwelling older adults. 28 , 29 , 30 In particular, slow walking speed has consistently been identified as a predictor of ADL disability and is recommended as a screening tool for the prevention of long‐term ADL disability. 12 However, this association may not be straightforward and can be influenced by factors such as age, sex, cognitive status, multimorbidity, and mental health. 11 , 31 These factors have been individually linked to disability, 13 , 32 but their combined impact on the onset and progression of disability remains unclear. The joint association of depression and cognitive impairment with disability appears to vary across countries. 33 , 34 , 35 For instance, in a multinational cohort of adults aged ≥50 years from Finland, Poland, and Spain, a significant interaction between depression and cognition in relation to disability was observed only in participants from Finland. 35 Previous studies in the Chinese population have shown that the relationship between physical function limitation and cognition may be mediated by depression, while the association between cognition and depression may be mediated by ADL disability. 36 , 37 The present study highlights the synergistic effect of impaired cognition, mental health, and physical function on the onset and progression of disability, underscoring the importance of a multifaceted approach to disability prevention.

Our mediation analysis showed that changes in cognition and depressive symptoms accounted for >90% of the association between physical function and disability progression. Cognitive impairment may limit an individual's ability to adapt to physical function limitations, 38 , 39 while depression may accelerate disability by reducing participation in rehabilitation and promoting sedentary behavior. 40 The synergy among these 3 factors may arise from shared pathways, such as systemic inflammation that accelerates both neurodegeneration and sarcopenia, 41 or from behavioral cascades, where depression leads to reduced physical activity, further exacerbating mobility loss. 42 The pronounced impact of physical function limitation on disability highlights the concept of physical resilience or motor reserve, 43 suggesting that mobility and physical function are critical targets for intervention. Simultaneously, addressing cognition and mood may help slow disability progression, even in individuals with existing physical function impairments.

Multiple pathways contribute to functional disability, including conditions such as arthritis, cardiovascular disease, dementia, or injuries that initially cause organ‐level impairments, leading to functional limitations that hinder basic physical and cognitive tasks. Prolonged limitations can progress to disability, defined as sustained difficulty in performing activities necessary for maintaining independence and fulfilling social roles. 44 , 45 Another pathway is frailty, driven by senescence, characterized by declining physical function and increased vulnerability to illness, 44 where physical decline often precedes cognitive decline and depression. 17 , 46 Loss of structural brain integrity may further link cognitive impairment, depression, and physical function limitations to disability. 16 The nonsignificant association between baseline cognitive deficits and incident ADL disability or ADL/iADL score accumulation in women and urban residents may reflect selective attrition, a common challenge in the CHARLS cohort. 19 Studies have found that individuals with baseline deficits and lower socioeconomic status are more likely to drop out during follow‐up, which may lead to an underestimation of the true effect in these subgroups. 19 , 47

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the axis of mind, mood, and mobility and their joint association with disability in a representative sample of the Chinese older population. A key strength of our study is mediation analysis, which provides quantitative evidence of the pathways linking physical function, cognitive change, depressive symptoms, and disability progression. Nevertheless, certain limitations remain. First, we used a cognitive test battery that may not fully capture cognitive deficits, and recall or social desirability bias may occur when healthy, highly educated older adults repeat the same cognitive test after 2 years. Second, the CES‐D 10‐item questionnaire may not comprehensively assess mental health, and participants' education level and urban versus rural residence may influence self‐reported CES‐D items. Third, although this is a longitudinal cohort study, we cannot establish causality on the basis of the current evidence. The mediation model was conducted under the hypothesis that cognitive decline and depression progression follow physical function decline, though we did not ascertain that changes in physical function preceded changes in cognition and depression. Fourth, unmeasured bias may exist between participants lost to follow‐up and those retained, and future studies are needed to further clarify these factors. Although excluding participants with baseline ADL disability could introduce a healthy participant bias, this approach remains appropriate given our focus on incident ADL disability and the progression of ADL and iADL limitations. Finally, caution is required when generalizing our findings due to potential attrition bias, death, and the exclusion criteria, which may have resulted in a study sample that is generally healthier than the broader population. Nevertheless, we believe that, owing to the advanced methodology, our study maintains high internal validity.

In conclusion, this study is among the first to examine the axis of mind, mood, and mobility and their combined impact on functional disability and its progression. The association between physical function limitation and accelerated progression of functional disability may be mediated by cognitive decline and depressive symptoms. Early identification and targeted interventions are crucial for preventing disability in at‐risk populations. Developing optimal screening methods for the early identification of individuals at high risk due to combined deficits in mind, mood, and mobility is therefore critical. In addition, investigating the biological pathways linking the mind–mood–mobility axis to disability progression represents an important direction for future research to better understand these connections.
