Multifactorial relationships among the waist-to-hip ratio, insulin resistance, and obstructive sleep apnea in men: a large-scale cohort study
Authors: Lilin Li, Wenjun Xue, Qian Yang, Qianqian Zhang, Hangdong Shen, Huajun Xu, Yupu Liu, Jianyin Zou, Suru Liu, Huaming Zhu, Jian Guan, Hongbin Xiong, Xinyi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic comorbidities related to obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) are a major concern in regarding OSA prognosis and complexity. However, few studies have investigated the interrelationships among these metabolic comorbidities. We explored the associations among insulin resistance (IR), the waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), and OSA using innovative and rigorous statistical methods.MethodsWe enrolled 5,326 subjects after meticulous recording anthropometric, biochemical, and standard polysomnographic parameters. The relationships among IR, WHR, and OSA were analyzed using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) as well as moderation and mediation analyses.ResultsCFA showed that IR and obesity were strongly correlated (loading = 0.73, p < 0.001). OSA was associated with both obesity and IR (loading = 0.57, p < 0.001; loading = 0.46, p < 0.001, respectively). Obesity was strongly associated with both the body mass index (BMI) and the WHR (loading = 0.82, p < 0.001; loading = 0.67, p < 0.001, respectively). WHR served as a moderator; the interaction effect between WHR and IR on the apnoea–hypopnea index (AHI) was significant (coefficient = − 2.451; p = 0.023). Mediation analyses showed that WHR explained 14.93%, 14.02%, and 13.10% of the associations between IR and AHI, the oxygen desaturation index, and the micro-arousal index, respectively.ConclusionMultifactorial relationships are apparent among IR, WHR, and OSA. The complex interrelationships highlight the intricate clinical interactions among metabolic dysfunction, central obesity, and sleep-disordered breathing, emphasizing the multifaceted nature of OSA and its related health implications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-025-01020-0.Keywords: Obstructive sleep apnea, Insulin resistance, Waist-Hip ratio, Confirmatory factor analysis, Moderation analysis, Mediation analysis

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is a common sleep disorder characterized by repetitive complete or partial collapses of the upper airway during sleep, resulting in chronic intermittent hypoxemia (CIH) and sleep fragmentation [1, 2]. It is an unexpected cause of insulin resistance (IR) and diabetes [3]. Its severity is independently associated with IR in both obese and non-obese OSA subjects [4, 5]. It has been suggested that IR may serve as a key mediator linking OSA to OSA-related sequelae [6–8].

Obesity is the strongest contributing factor for OSA; most of the OSA patients are obese [9, 10]. Patients with visceral obesity are at particular risk for OSA given their fat accumulation patterns. The waist-to-hip circumference ratio (WHR) is a stronger predictor of OSA than measures of general obesity such as the body mass index (BMI) [11]. Two separate observational and longitudinal cohorts previously found that WHR is more strongly correlated with OSA than BMI [12]. WHR is the most relevant obesity index during each phase of insulin secretion in obese patients [13]. Over the past decades, many studies have independently assessed the relationships between IR status and WHR in patients with various diseases, reaching a variety of conclusions. However, only a few studies have explored the relationships among the WHR, IR, and OSA.

WHR is positively correlated with IR status in OSA patients [14]. In male patients with OSA, IR is not independently associated with sleep apnea severity when BMI, waist circumference (WC), and WHR are considered [15]. The visceral adiposity index increases with IR status but does not predict sleep apnea severity [16]. Obese patients with OSA exhibit greater insulin resistance than those with simple obesity independent of the extent and distribution of adiposity [17]. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) treatment of OSA could improve IR [18, 19]. Lifestyle interventions and surgical treatments aimed at reducing visceral obesity may indirectly ameliorate the metabolic consequences associated with OSA [20–22]. While the individual relationships among these factors have been studied, the complex, multifaceted interactions among them remain inadequately understood.

To understand the complexity of the relationships among OSA, IR, and abdominal obesity, we used sophisticated and rigorous statistical methods to analyze a large cohort of OSA patients diagnosed using standard polysomnography (PSG). Given previous studies showing significant differences in OSA presentation, pathophysiology, and comorbidities between males and females, with a higher estimated prevalence of OSA in males [23], we focused solely on male participants in the present study. The aim of this study was to investigate the interrelationships among OSA, IR, and abdominal obesity in males, and to explore how these factors may contribute to the complexity of OSA and its comorbidities.

Materials and methods

Participants

This large-scale study evaluated 6,026 participants who were consecutively enrolled in the Shanghai Sleep Health Study (SSHS) from 2007 to 2016, as described in our previous study [24]. The exclusion criteria were: age < 18 years; female sex; any history of OSA treatment (e.g., use of an oral appliance, CPAP treatment, or upper airway surgery); current treatment for dyslipidemia, diabetes, or cardiovascular disease; any systemic disease (a respiratory or endocrine disease), cancer, or a psychiatric condition; and/or other sleep disorders such as upper airway resistance syndrome, restless leg syndrome, or narcolepsy. Ultimately, 5,326 subjects were included. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All study procedures conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Jiao Tong University Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital (2019-KY-050(K)).

Anthropometric and biochemical measurements

Weight, height, neck circumference (NC), WC, and hip circumference (HC) were measured. NC was measured midway between the mid-cervical spine and mid-anterior neck (to the nearest 0.5 cm); measurement was performed just below the laryngeal prominence if that was palpable [25]. WC was measured using a tape midway between the lower costal margin and the iliac crest. HC was the maximum girth of the greater trochanters [26]. Blood pressure were measured three times on the right arm using an automated device (Omron Model HEM-752 Fuzzy, Omron Company, Kyoto, Japan) after a 10-min rest while seated; the average of three measurements was recorded according to the instructions of hypertension guidelines [26, 27]. The BMI was calculated as body weight (kg) divided by the square of the height (m), the WHR was calculated as the WC divided by the HC [28].

All blood samples were collected from participants in the morning after overnight PSG. Total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TGs), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), fasting blood glucose, and insulin levels were measured using routine procedures in a hospital laboratory following the guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology [29]. The homeostasis model assessment-estimated insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was the fasting serum level of insulin (µU/mL) × the fasting glucose level (mmol/L)/22.5 [30]. We defined HOMA-IR ≥ 2.5 as insulin resistance [31].

Polysomnographic evaluation

All participants underwent one-night hospital-based standardized PSG (Alice 4 or 5 platform; Respironics, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Electroencephalographic, electro-oculographic, chin electromyographic, electrocardiographic, nasal/oral airflow, thoracic/abdominal movement, finger pulse oximetry, and body position data were recorded from each subject during sleep. Objective sleep parameters, including the apnea/hypopnea index (AHI), the oxygen desaturation index (ODI), and the micro-arousal index (MAI) were automatically calculated and manually checked by two skilled technicians according to the 2012 standard criteria of AASM.

The AHI was the average of the apneas (absence of oronasal airflow for ≥ 10 s or at least a 90% decrease for ≥ 10 s) and hypopnea (≥ 30% reduction in airflow for ≥ 10 s accompanied by either a ≥ 3% decrease in the oxyhemoglobin saturation level or an associated arousal) per hour. The ODI was the mean number of oxygen reductions ≥ 4% per hour. The MAI was the mean number of arousal episodes per hour. OSA severity was categorized as normal (AHI < 5), mild (5 ≤ AHI < 15), moderate (15 ≤ AHI < 30), or severe (AHI ≥ 30) using the 2012 criteria of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (data collected before 2012 were rescored) [32].

Statistical analysis

All raw data were subjected to Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality testing before any statistical analysis. Normally distributed data are presented as means (with standard deviations, SDs), skewed data as medians (with interquartile ranges), and categorical data as numbers (with % values). Comparisons among the four groups in terms of baseline characteristics employed the Kruskal-Wallis H-test, one-way analysis of variance, or the χ2 test, depending on the data distributions. Correlations were derived using the Spearman or Pearson test depending on the characteristics of the data. SPSS software (ver. 26.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used for most statistical analyses. AMOS software (ver. 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was employed when comparing confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) models. These models were evaluated using the maximum likelihood estimation method and the chi-square test was employed to evaluate model fits. The fit indices included the chi-square value (CMIN), the degrees of freedom (df), and the chi-square value/degrees of freedom. Then two additional measures of model congruence were employed: the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) (threshold, < 0.08) and the comparative fit index (CFI) (threshold, >0.90). Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered significant. Mediation and moderation effects were evaluated using Models 4 and 1 of PROCESS marco software respectively. Patient age, BMI, blood pressure, and smoking and alcohol consumption status served as covariates in the two models. In terms of indirect effects revealed by Model 4, if the zero was not within the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the bias-corrected 5,000-repeat bootstrap test, a significant indirect effect was noted [33]. The moderation effect considered the significance at the 0.05 probability level if the 95% CI did not include the zero in Model 1.

The HOMA-IR was strongly related to the AHI and the ODI (r = 0.341, p < 0.001; r = 0.343, p < 0.001, respectively). Moderation effect analyses

After correlation analyses, we performed moderation analyses to examine the associations between the WHR, HOMA-IR, and OSA indices. The detailed results are presented in Table 3. In the regression analysis when AHI was the dependent variable and adjusted for age, the BMI, and smoking and alcohol consumption status, a significant interaction effect was observed (coefficient = − 2.451; p = 0.023). Importantly, the lower limit confidence interval (LLCI) and upper limit confidence interval (ULCI) did not include the zero (–4.558, − 0.344). This implies that the interaction effect was not only statistically significant but also negative, reflecting the complex interplay between these indices. The coefficients of both the WHR (coefficient = 85.933; p < 0.001) and HOMA-IR (coefficient = 2.578; p = 0.013) were positive, signifying that each independently increased OSA severity. Notably, as the HOMA-IR increased, the impact of WHR on AHI gradually diminished. Conversely, with an increase in the WHR, the influence of HOMA-IR on AHI also gradually decreased, suggesting a reciprocal relationship.

Discussion

We used several innovative, rigorous statistical methods to explore the interrelationships among the WHR, HOMA-IR, and OSA. We found strong interrelationships among all three. When the WHR was viewed as a modulator of IR, significant moderation of the AHI was apparent; the WHR mediated the relationship between HOMA-IR and OSA. OSA-related metabolic comorbidities pose major challenges for clinicians who must manage the complexity of OSA.

Our results indicate that, in males, both WHR and the HOMA-IR were positively associated with the severity of OSA. When incorporating a moderation model, moderator WHR altered the intensity of the influence of HOMA-IR on AHI. Specifically, the interaction term was negative, suggesting that in individuals with lower WHR, the direct effect of IR on OSA severity was more pronounced. Furthermore, WHR accounted for 14.93% of the influence of HOMA-IR on AHI and 14.02% on ODI. This supports the notion that IR exacerbates central obesity, and the accumulation of abdominal fat may impair respiratory function, thereby worsening OSA and hypoxemia.

Several studies have explored the relationship between IR and visceral obesity in the context of OSA using traditional statistical methods. For instance, one study demonstrates a strong association between metabolic syndrome, including IR, and OSA [14], consistent with our findings of a robust link among OSA, visceral obesity, and IR. Similarly, another study, despite its limited sample size, indicates that visceral fat, rather than subcutaneous or total body fat, plays a primary role in predisposing individuals to OSA. Furthermore, it suggests that sleep apnea contributes to fasting hyperinsulinemia independently of overall obesity [34]. These findings align with our results, emphasizing the pivotal role of visceral obesity in the interplay between OSA and IR while underscoring the complex metabolic mechanisms involved. CFA is powerful for evaluating the utility of a priori hypotheses on the relationships between observed variables, and latent parameters or factors [35, 36]. In this study, the metabolic index and all sleep-breath parameters were viewed as latent independent variables and observed indicators when exploring potential interrelationships; we found a strong relationship between IR, the WHR, and OSA, consistent with our previous report with CFA method [37]. Statistical alternatives used when seeking to provide insights into the interplay between the WHR, IR status, and OSA include mediation and moderation analyses. To the best of our knowledge, few studies have used moderation and mediation analyses to explore the complex interrelationships among OSA, visceral obesity and IR. Moderation and mediation aid explanations of causal phenomena [38, 39]. Mediation analysis assesses the extent by which the effect of IR status on OSA indices is explained by WHR mediators; the aim is to understand how IR status might affect an outcome of interest. A moderation effect is a special third-variable effect; the WHR moderator and the IR parameters interact when affecting OSA. To date, few studies have used CFA, moderation, and mediation analyses to investigate the WHR–IR interactions in OSA and the effects of OSA-related parameters.

A comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms involved in OSA-associated IR and obesity remains elusive. In mouse studies and in vitro models, CIH induces a pro-inflammatory phenotype in visceral adipose tissue; the extent of pro-inflammatory M1 adipose-tissue macrophage polarization correlates with the extent of IR [40, 41]. CIH attenuates glucose-induced insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells and enhances IR in peripheral tissues and cells [42]. A lack of heme oxygenase-1 in visceral adipose tissue may contribute to the development of IR [43]. In a previous study, the adiponectin/leptin ratio, TG level, and WHR explained almost half of all HOMA-IR variance in the context of obesity [44]. Hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), oxygen-sensitive transcriptional regulators, mediate both cellular and microenvironmental hypoxic remodeling, where HIF-1 and HIF-2 trigger obesity-associated inflammation and IR [45, 46]. During adipose tissue hypoxia, the leptin level is elevated, chronic inflammation is apparent, the adiponectin level falls, the endoplasmic reticulum is stressed, mitochondria become dysfunctional, angiogenesis is stimulated, and the MAPK–ERK, NF-kB, and HIF pathways are activated, affording new insights into the IR pathogenesis of OSA [46]. The hypoxic responses of different cell types vary; these responses may be phenotypically unique [45]. Combined with the above data, our new findings suggest that multidirectional interactions among obesity, hypoxia, and IR may be at play, consistent with previous reports of multifactorial, nonmonotonic, complex relationships among IR, the WHR, and OSA.

We are the first to substantively explore the correlations among the WHR, IR, and OSA. Unlike conventional statistical methods, we used innovative, rigorous statistical techniques when examining the interrelationships among the three variables of concern. We pioneered a new analysis of the association between OSA and various metabolic indicators; the OSA serves as a latent variable during CFA. Additionally, our large sample was enrolled at a single sleep center, where data definitions were standardized; data collection was uniform, ensuring that the statistical analyses were very reliable. The observed interrelationships among WHR, IR, and OSA have important clinical implications. These findings suggest that targeting metabolic factors, such as insulin resistance, could be integrated into OSA management strategies to reduce disease severity and improve outcomes. The identified nonmonotonic patterns further emphasize the need for personalized treatment approaches that account for individual metabolic and anthropometric profiles. For instance, prioritizing WHR over BMI in specific patient subgroups may enhance therapeutic precision. Additionally, these results highlight the value of incorporating metabolic screening into routine OSA assessments, enabling more tailored patient care and better long-term health outcomes.

However, despite these strengths, several limitations must be mentioned. First, although we excluded medicated diabetes patients, high blood pressure, hyperlipidemia, and cardiovascular disease may influence IR to some extent. Second, although we adjusted for some common confounders, we did not consider diet or physical activity level, both of which significantly impact insulin and glucose levels. Third, all study participants were enrolled after attending sleep centers; thus results should be generalized to community samples only with caution. However, these limitations do not compromise the significance of our work. Further research is required to define the mechanisms underpinning the associations among OSA, abdominal obesity, and IR.

Conclusion

We identified multifaceted interrelationships among WHR, IR, and OSA, involving moderation, mediation, and nonmonotonic patterns. These findings highlight the intricate interactions between metabolic and respiratory factors in OSA. Future research could further elucidate the underlying mechanisms and advance our understanding of OSA and its related comorbidities.
