Introduction

Since the domestication of cattle more than 10,000 years ago, humans practiced selective breeding, gradually shaping a wide range of specialized breeds uniquely adapted to diverse ecological conditions and feeding systems (Xia et al., 2023). Over the past century, genetic selection in dairy cattle largely focused on improving economically important traits to meet the ever-increasing demands of both dairy industries and consumers (Miglior et al., 2017). Early breeding efforts primarily relied on observable phenotypes such as milk yield to guide selection decisions. However, despite these efforts, the pace of genetic progress remained relatively slow until the 1970s, largely due to the limited use of sires in breeding programs (Brotherstone and Goddard, 2005). A major breakthrough occurred in 1975 with the introduction of the sire model of Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (BLUP) and the widespread application of artificial insemination, which revolutionized genetic evaluation and accelerated improvement in production traits (Van Doormaal and Kistemaker, 2003).

Although within-breed selection and improved management practices have significantly enhanced milk productivity, climate change has emerged as a major global threat to livestock performance and adaptability (Khurshid et al., 2023; Gemechu et al., 2025). Ethiopia, whose agricultural system heavily depends on seasonal rainfall, faces pronounced challenges due to the increasing variability and unpredictability of climate patterns (Moges and Bhat, 2021; Cartwright et al., 2023).

The Fogera cattle, an indigenous Ethiopian breed renowned for its tolerance to heat stress, adaptability to waterlogged environments, and resistance to diseases (Tesfa et al., 2022), represents an important genetic resource for climate-resilient dairy production.

Several studies have demonstrated that elevated ambient temperatures substantially reduce milk yield, compromise reproductive efficiency, and increase mortality risk in dairy cows (Cartwright et al., 2023; Jeon et al., 2023). Similar findings have been consistently reported across various studies documenting the detrimental effects of heat stress on dairy productivity (Armstrong, 1994; Ravagnolo and Misztal, 2000; Rhoads et al., 2009; Negri et al., 2020). This evidence underscores the urgent need to integrate economical and adaptive traits into breeding objectives as part of climate change mitigation strategies.

Recent work by Kasna et al. (2025) revealed a negative association between resilience and milk production traits in Holstein Friesian dairy cows in the Czech Republic, highlighting the necessity of index-based selection to ensure sustainable dairy production. To quantify the influence of climatic variables on animal performance, random regression models under reaction norm frameworks have been widely adopted (Falconer, 1990; Sartori et al., 2022; Stefani et al., 2022). More recently, Tesema et al. (2025) further emphasized the importance of evaluating genetic performance in relation to environmental interactions to identify within-breed genetic variation that enhances climate resilience. Selecting animals based on resilience capacity within breeds presents a promising and sustainable pathway to improving adaptive potential.

In the Tana Basin where the current study was conducted, temperature-humidity indices have risen while rainfall patterns have declined, mirroring national climate trends (Birara et al., 2018; NASA, 2025). Developing and testing hypothetical climate scenarios can therefore help refine adaptive breeding and management strategies. Despite the growing importance of climate resilience, genetic studies addressing resilience traits and their parameters in Ethiopian indigenous cattle remain scarce. Hence, this study was designed to derive resilience phenotypes, estimate their genetic parameters, and assess the phenotypic and genetic correlations between resilience traits and economically important performance traits under varying climate scenarios. The ultimate goal is to provide foundational evidence to guide the development of climate-smart breeding programs for sustainable dairy cattle improvement in Ethiopia.

Methodology

Data source and management

For this study, 646 Fogera cows were managed under sumi intensive conditions, with calves provided unrestricted access to colostrum during the first four days postpartum to ensure optimal immune development. Routine milking started on day five and continued throughout a lactation period of 360 days. The dataset consisted of 2,784 performance records collected between 1998 and 2011, including average daily milk yield, functional longevity, and heat tolerance index. Prior to statistical analysis, all records were subjected to quality control and cleaning using Microsoft Excel. Records with missing information, short lactations, or early culling due to non-genetic causes were excluded to maintain data reliability and consistency. Following this process, the complete set of 2,584 records was retained for final analysis.

Description of the farm

The study was conducted at the Andassa Livestock Research Center, located at an altitude of 1,730 m above sea level, at coordinates 37°29′ longitude and 11°29′ latitude. The centre is found approximately 22 km from Bahir Dar, along the road leading to the Blue Nile Falls (locally known as Tis-Abay). The area receives an average annual rainfall of approximately 1,150 mm and experiences mean annual temperatures ranging from 6.5 °C to 30 °C.

Animal management

The Andassa Livestock Research Center maintains an average herd size of more than 400 Fogera cattle, of which approximately 300 are breeding cows. Purebred Fogera cattle are bred primarily through natural mating. The herd is organized into groups of 40–50 cows, with each group assigned a single breeding bull. Bulls are selected from farm-born progeny using pedigree records to avoid close genetic relationships with the cows, thereby minimizing inbreeding. Each cow in the breeding program is evaluated for milk production and reproductive performance. Cows with high estimated breeding values (EBVs) for these traits are maintained, while male calves born to high-EBV dams are selected as potential sires. Selected bulls typically remain active in natural service for about four years. The centre practices a semi-intensive management system. Purebred Fogera cattle are housed in separate herd groups, with pens constructed from concrete floors and iron sheet roofing. During the dry season (October to May), cattle graze for approximately eight hours per day and receive supplementary feed consisting of green and natural pastures as well as harvested hay. The nutritional composition of these feeds is presented in Table 1, as described by Mekuriaw et al. (2020). Lactating cows are additionally supplemented during the mid-dry season (January to May) with 1–2 kg of concentrate and 2–4 kg of improved forages such as Napier grass per animal per day. Seasonal feeding management follows the classification outlined by Bitew et al. (2010), distinguishing between the wet season (June to September) and the dry season (October to May). Water for the herd is mainly sourced from the Andassa River, a major tributary of the Blue Nile, while young and sick animals housed indoors are provided with tap water. Health management at the centre emphasizes preventive measures. The entire herd is vaccinated every six to eight months against major infectious diseases such as blackleg, anthrax, and pasteurellosis, while vaccination against lumpy skin disease is administered every two years. Internal and external parasite control is carried out twice annually at the onset and end of the rainy season. For calf management, newborns are allowed unrestricted suckling during the first four days postpartum to ensure adequate colostrum intake for immune development. Thereafter, calves are separated from their dams both day and night, except during morning and evening milking sessions, when they are allowed to partially suckle from two teats. Weaning occurs at approximately eight months of age. The total daily milk yield was calculated as twice the measured partial milk yield recorded during milking.

Trait definitions

Daily Milk Yield: (DMY) refers to the average quantity of milk produced by a cow per day, expressed in liters. It represents the fundamental measure of dairy performance and serves as the baseline variable for assessing productivity and resilience under varying environmental conditions. Functional Longevity: (FL) is defined as the total number of days from a cow’s first calving to its culling, regardless of the number of lactations completed. It reflects the cow’s ability to remain productive and reproductively active over time, making it an important indicator of herd sustainability and lifetime performance. Productive longevity (PL) represents the total number of productive days from first calving to culling, excluding the dry periods between lactations. Unlike functional longevity, it directly quantifies the duration of active milk production, thereby capturing the efficiency and economic lifespan of the animal. Heat tolerance index (HTI) measures the ability of a cow to sustain milk production while enduring thermal stress. It was estimated by fitting a linear regression model in which daily milk yield was considered the dependent variable, while temperature-humidity index and rainfall served as independent variables.

Projected climate variables for the period 2014–2100 were obtained from the World Bank Climate Change Knowledge Portal (CCKP)

(https://climateknowledgeportal.worldbank.org/country/ethiopia/climate-data- projections). Long-term observations from 1954–2014 were used as baseline reference data for temperature and rainfall, derived from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) outputs. The raw datasets were imported into RStudio for processing. High-resolution historical observational data for the study area were incorporated as reference inputs to correct model biases.

Statistical downscaling of the CCKP projections to an approximate spatial resolution of 10 km was conducted using the Bias Correction Constructed Analogs with Quantile Mapping Reordering (BCCAQ) method implemented in the climQMBC package of the R programming language (Aedo et al., 2021). This procedure corrected systematic biases and aligned modeled climate outputs with observed local conditions at the study site with longitude of 37.29°east and latitude of 11.29°north. The downscaled temperature and rainfall datasets were subsequently integrated with predicted resilience phenotypes of individual Fogera cows to simulate potential reductions in resilience capacity under progressive increases in temperature–humidity index (THI) by 1 %, 2 %, and 3 %, and corresponding declines in rainfall by 1 %, 2 %, and 3 %. A scenario-based analysis was then performed to assess the hypothetical reduction in the future resilience potential of Fogera cows under projected climate change conditions. The modeling framework followed projected temperature rise patterns reported by NASA (2025) indicating an approximate increase of 1.5 °C by the mid-2024, alongside the documented declining rainfall trend across the study region (Yitayew and Amente, 2012; Birara et al., 2018).

Deriving resilience phenotype traits in dairy cattle and integration with animal performance and climate predictors

Resilience phenotypes in Fogera cattle were derived by integrating key production and adaptation traits with climate variables, specifically the temperature-humidity index and rainfall. The traits considered included daily milk yield, functional longevity, productive longevity, and the heat tolerance index.

Individual animal responses to environmental stressors were estimated following the approach of Tiezzi et al. (2017) through a two-step procedures. First, genotype-by-environment (G×E) interactions were captured using reaction norm models for each trait, allowing the quantification of individual resilience in response to environmental variability. In the second step, the resulting resilience phenotypes were included as covariates in a random regression model, which was also specified using reaction norm functions. Resilience changes over time were represented using second-order Legendre polynomials to model the dynamic patterns of phenotypic responses across the studied periods.

THI and rainfall were incorporated as environmental covariates in the reaction norm model, and a t-test for a single mean was conducted to determine whether the average resilience estimate differed significantly from zero.

Resilience phenotype genetic parameters were estimated via a linear mixed model in WOMBAT (Meyer, 2007). Both fixed and random effects were incorporated to derive heritability, genetic variance, and covariance estimates. Genetic trends were determined by regressing mean estimated breeding values (EBVs) on year of calving.

Results

Resilience phenotypes of Fogera cows to THI and rainfall variability

Resilience phenotypes of Fogera cattle under varying temperature–humidity index (THI) and rainfall conditions derived from daily milk yield (DMY), productive longevity (PL), functional longevity (FL), and heat tolerance index (HTI), are presented in Table 2, Table 3. The observed changes in phenotypic performance associated with THI ranged from -4.037 × 10⁻¹⁰ to -1.44 × 10⁻¹⁰, with all resilience phenotypes showing significant negative effects (P < 0.001). Although the magnitudes were small, these changes had highly meaningful economical losses over time. The negative values of resilient phenotypes indicated that increasing THI exerted a measurable adverse impact on Fogera cows’ performance. Specifically, a unit rise in THI corresponded to reduction in average daily milk yield of 4.037 × 10⁻¹⁰ liters per cow per day. Similarly, productive longevity and functional longevity declined by 3.096 × 10⁻¹¹ and -1.44 × 10⁻¹⁰ years−1, respectively, while heat tolerance capacity decreased by 1.644 × 10⁻¹² units per unit rise in THI. Among the traits evaluated, average daily milk yield exhibited the largest reduction in response to elevated THI, reflecting its high sensitivity to thermal stress. In contrast, the relatively smaller decline in heat tolerance suggested that Fogera cows possessed an inherent capacity to cope with heat stress. Moreover, the relatively small mean values coupled with higher standard deviations of these resilience phenotypes indicate considerable individual variability, reflecting differences in adaptive capacity within the population and suggesting potential for selective breeding to enhance resilience.

Similarly, the rainfall-driven resilience of Fogera cows, reflecting changes in individual performance for average daily milk yield, productive longevity, functional longevity, and heat tolerance index, showed negative effects, ranging from − 4.4 × 10⁻¹² to −1.34 × 10⁻¹⁰, with all resilience phenotypes being highly significant (P < 0.001). A decrease in rainfall was associated with a reduction of 1.34 × 10⁻¹⁰ liters of milk per cow per day. Productive and functional longevity declined by 1.10 × 10⁻¹¹ and 1.20 × 10⁻¹⁰ years, respectively, while the heat tolerance index decreased by 4.40 × 10⁻¹² units for each 1 mm reduction in rainfall. Notably, the impact of rainfall reduction on heat tolerance capacity was minimal, indicating that Fogera cows maintain relatively stable thermotolerance even under decreasing rainfall conditions. Among the evaluated resilience phenotypes, the highest variability between individual cows was observed for average daily milk yield, functional longevity, and productive longevity, highlighting substantial differences in how animals respond to rainfall fluctuations.

Variance components and heritability estimates for resilience to climate variability

The resilience phenotypes explained by heritability estimates related to milk yield, longevity-related and heat tolerance capacity traits under THI and RF variability are presented in Table 4, Table 5. Heritability (h2) for resilience phenotypes including average daily milk yield, productive longevity, functional longevity, and heat tolerance capacity varied between 0.10 and 0.31 under THI and between 0.13 and 0.28 under rainfall variability. Additive genetic variance expressed as a proportion of total phenotypic variance ranged from 10 % to 31 % under THI and from 13 % to 28 % under rainfall, reflecting the contribution of genetics to the observed variation in resilience traits. The additive genetic contribution for daily milk yield, productive longevity, functional longevity, and heat tolerance index was low to moderate under both climatic influences, with heritability values of 0.31, 0.10, 0.21, and 0.10 under THI, and 0.28, 0.14, 0.24, and 0.10 under rainfall, respectively. These results indicated that while genetic factors play a measurable role in shaping resilience traits, environmental variability due to temperature and rainfall significantly influences phenotypic expression, emphasizing the importance of integrating both genetic and environmental considerations in breeding programs aimed at improving climate adaptability.

Genetic correlation between resilience phenotypes and actual traits

Table 6 presents the estimated genetic and phenotypic correlations between resilience phenotypes and actual performance traits of average daily milk yield, productive longevity, functional longevity, and heat tolerance index—under temperature as the environmental predictor. Phenotypic correlations influenced by the temperature–humidity index (THI) ranged from weakly negative to weakly positive (-0.34 to 0.01), whereas genetic correlations spanned a much wider range, from strongly negative to strongly positive (-0.83 to 0.84). The pronounced negative genetic correlation between resilience and daily milk yield suggests that higher-producing cows tend to have shorter lifespans, whereas lower-yielding cows exhibit greater resilience. Longevity-related traits and the heat tolerance index, however, demonstrated strong positive genetic correlations with resilience in Fogera cows. This suggests that animals with higher resilience capacity also possess greater heat tolerance and longer productive and functional lifespans. Conversely, productive longevity, functional longevity, and heat tolerance index exhibited substantial negative genetic correlations with daily milk yield, implying that high-producing cows often have shorter lifespans and lower heat tolerance. Stimulatingly, the strong positive genetic correlation observed between heat tolerance index and longevity traits indicated cows with higher heat tolerance had extended productive and functional lifespans. Collectively, these findings highlight a clear genetic antagonism between milk productivity and both adaptability and longevity traits, while emphasizing the contradiction nature of resilience, heat tolerance, and lifespan in Fogera cattle.

Genetic trend for resilience phenotypes of Fogera cows

The estimated genetic trends for resilience phenotypes including average daily milk yield, functional longevity, productive longevity and heat tolerance index under the influence of THI and rainfall RF variability are illustrated in Fig. 3 and Table 8. Results indicated a gradual genetic decline in all resilience traits over generations. Specifically, average daily milk yield decreased genetically by -1.09 × 10⁻⁵ day ⁻1 in response to THI variations. Similarly, productive and functional longevity were reduced by −1.095 × 10⁻¹ days year⁻¹ and −1.43 × 10⁻² days year⁻¹, respectively, under the same thermal stress. Heat tolerance capacity also exhibited a genetic decline of -0.27 × 10⁻⁵ units year⁻1 due to elevated THI. These negative trends collectively indicate a declining genetic potential for resilience across generations as a consequence of temperature-humidity stress. Under rainfall variability, a comparable pattern was observed. Genetic reductions were noted for average daily milk yield (-1.15 × 10⁻⁵ liters day⁻1), productive longevity (–9.13 × 10⁻³ days year⁻¹), functional longevity (–3.46 × 10⁻³ days year⁻¹), and heat tolerance index (-2.49 × 10⁻⁵ units year⁻1), all of which were statistically significant (Table 9). These results confirm that both THI and rainfall exert negative effects on resilience traits related to milk production, longevity, and heat tolerance in Fogera cattle. Overall, the resilience phenotypes for average daily milk yield, productive longevity, functional longevity, and heat tolerance index exhibited cumulative genetic reductions of 0.0017 liters day⁻1, 1.095 days year⁻¹, 1.168 days year⁻¹, and 0.005 units year⁻1, respectively. These findings highlight the pressing need for climate-smart breeding strategies that integrate both productivity and adaptive traits to sustain genetic gains in Fogera cows under changing environmental conditions.

Scenario analysis of rainfall and THI variability at different levels

Exploring hypothetical shifts in climate variables, based on observed trends, offers a powerful approach to guide operational adjustments on farms. Table 9 presents the projected changes in resilience phenotypes of Fogera cows under different climate scenarios. Compared to the current baseline, a 1 % reduction in rainfall caused only a modest decline in resilience phenotypes, ranging from 7 % to 10 %. However, under scenarios with 2 % and 3 % reductions in rainfall, the average decline in resilience capacity reached 35.5 %, for average daily milk yield making the trait more sensitive.

When temperature-humidity index (THI) increased by 1 %, resilience capacities on average declined by an average of 19.25 %, surpassing the effect of the 1 % rainfall reduction scenario. Similarly, a combined scenario of 2 % rainfall reduction and a 2 % rise in THI resulted in an average 26.63 % decrease in resilience capacities. The most extreme scenario of 3 % rise and fall in THI and 3 % rainfall change produced the greatest reductions (36.88 %) across key traits, including daily milk yield, productive longevity, functional longevity, and heat tolerance.

Discussion

Resilience phenotypes of Fogera cows to THI and rainfall variability

The resilience of Fogera cows across diverse climatic conditions provides valuable insight into their adaptive potential, especially for economically important traits. However, the limited dataset used for analysis of resilience phenotypes represents the limitation of the study and may influence the interpretation of the findings. The observed significant negative impact of rising temperature-humidity index (THI) on resilience phenotypes is largely attributable to heat stress, which affect metabolism and hormonal regulation, impairing physiological functioning. Abera et al. (2021) reported that a THI of ≤ 66 maintains stable physiology in Fogera calves in Ethiopia. In our study, while all resilience phenotypes were adversely affected by elevated THI, the most pronounced reduction occurred in milk yield resilience. This reduction stems from decreased feed intake and the diversion of nutrients from milk synthesis toward thermoregulation, ultimately compromising reproductive efficiency. Previous studies have similarly reported that high THI reduced dry matter intake, alters rumen fermentation, and negatively affects milk production (Rhoads et al., 2009; Negri et al., 2020; Rodriguez-Venegas et al., 2023). Ghavi Hossein-Zadeh et al. (2013) also observed diminished milk potential in Iranian Holstein Friesian dairy cows under elevated THI, emphasizing the effects of heat stress on lactating Fogera cows.

Rainfall patterns likewise significantly influenced resilience phenotypes. Reduced rainfall limits forage availability, thereby constraining milk production and reproductive longevity. Adequate rainfall promotes forage growth with higher nutrient content, improving both productivity and welfare of animals (Henry et al., 2018; Silva et al., 2024). Notably, productive and functional longevity showed the highest variability under fluctuating rainfall, indicating reproductive lifespan is particularly sensitive to environmental quality. Nardone et al. (2010) emphasized that inadequate rainfall limits green pasture growth, failing to meet the energy needs for milk synthesis and reproduction of cows. Consequently, poorly nourished cows face reproductive inefficiency and earlier culling, ultimately reducing herd productivity.

In real-world conditions, rainfall and THI often act together, influencing both production and adaptive responses, even though this study analyzed them separately. The simultaneous occurrence of high THI and low rainfall can further reduce performance by limiting feed availability while increasing thermal stress, thereby negatively affecting milk yield, reproductive efficiency, and overall cow longevity.

Genetic variability for resilience phenotypes under climate stress

Genetic variation in resilience phenotypes justified on the relative contributions of genetic and environmental factors in trait expression (Tesema et al., 2025). However, heritability estimates for adaptive traits remain limited (Naskar et al., 2015). In this study, resilience phenotypes related to productive and functional longevity and heat tolerance under THI exhibited moderate genetic variability, suggesting genetic selection alone might result slower genetic improvement and strategies such management intervention would be effective for improving these traits. Similarly, resilience of daily milk yield under rainfall variability showed moderate genetic variability, indicating that selective breeding could effectively enhance this phenotype. Thus, a combined approach integrating genetic selection and targeted management interventions could optimally improve resilience traits. Oloo et al. (2023) reported similar findings, highlighting the predominant role of environmental factors in productive longevity and herd life. Unlike the present findings, Kasna et al. (2025) observed a higher genetic contribution to resilience related to daily milk yield across the full lactation.

Genetic correlation between resilience phenotypes and actual traits

Phenotypic associations between resilience and actual traits were generally weak positive to negative moderate, highlighting the potential challenge of selecting solely for high performance without considering resilience. Strong positive genetic correlations between most actual traits and resilience phenotypes, however, suggest that genetic selection for resilience could simultaneously improve production traits. Conversely, the strong negative correlations observed between daily milk yield and both longevity and heat tolerance suggested that high-yielding cows are subjected to metabolic stress, which compromised their lifespan and thermoregulatory capacity. Moreover, elevated milk production often induced an energy deficit, thereby limiting the resources available for adaptive physiological functions and long-term survival. Hence, implementing an index-based selection strategy that integrates both economic and adaptive traits is crucial to mitigating the genetic antagonism among key performance traits in Fogera cows. These findings align with previous studies (Oloo et al., 2023; Kasna et al., 2025). In agreement with Sungkhapreecha et al. (2022), productive longevity was positively correlated with functional longevity and heat tolerance, suggesting that selecting cows with higher thermotolerance can enhance lifetime productivity. Overall, our study emphasizes that breeding programs ignoring resilience may inadvertently reduce longevity and heat tolerance in Fogera cows, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions with high heat and rainfall variability (Habimana et al., 2023).

Estimated genetic change of resilience phenotypes

Estimation of genetic trends provides insights into the long-term changes in the average genetic merit of a population. The observed decline in resilience traits among Fogera cows reflected past selection practices focused exclusively on milk production, neglecting adaptive traits. Naskar et al. (2012) recommended balanced selection strategies that incorporate both production and resilience traits to optimize productivity, reproduction, and adaptation. Rauw et al. (1998) similarly reported that selection for high milk yield can adversely affect resilience. Elevated heat stress further exacerbated these effects by reducing feed intake, impairing milk production, and causing reproductive failures (Liu et al., 2019; Sesay, 2023). Additionally, high variability in rainfall patterns, combined with heat stress, can reduce milk production and fertility (Gaughan et al., 2010). Consequently, the negative genetic trend in resilience traits underscores the vulnerability of Fogera cows to climate stressors under past selection regimes.

Scenario analysis for climate variability

The principal limitation of this work lies in the unquantified uncertainty on the scenario-based projections of resilience phenotypes and small sample size of resilience phenotypes in Fogera cows. Scenario modeling indicated a consistent decline in the resilience phenotypes of Fogera cows with increasing THI and decreasing rainfall RF. The analysis suggested that slight reductions in rainfall, approximately 1 %, exerted minimal influence on forage availability and associated resilience traits, likely because such changes did not exceed the ecological thresholds necessary to compromise the pasture biomass or nutritional quality. In contrast, more pronounced declines in rainfall (2–3 %) combined with elevated THI values appeared to exceed the animals’ adaptive limits, leading to physiological strain and a marked reduction in the expression of resilience phenotypes. The current findings indicated that a 1 % increase in the temperature–humidity index (THI) reduced individual cow resilience, which aligned with Rauw et al. (1998), who reported that environmental stressors such as drought and heat can adversely affect livestock performance by limiting access to feed and water. Overall, our findings indicate that Fogera cows’ resilience is increasingly challenged by dynamic climate conditions and historical selection focused primarily on productivity. Naskar et al. (2012) cautioned that neglecting adaptive traits in breeding programs can reduce livestock populations’ ability to withstand environmental stressors, compromising long-term resilience.

Conclusion

Fogera cows’ resilience to climate variability, particularly THI and rainfall, is a key determinant of milk yield, longevity, and heat tolerance. THI imposed a negative effect through heat stress, while reduced rainfall limited forage availability and reduced nutrient composition. Moderate to low genetic variability under these stressors indicated limited scope for selective breeding alone, highlighting the need for integrated management approaches. Strong negative genetic correlations between milk yield and resilience traits, coupled with declining genetic trends, underscore the trade-offs inherent in productivity-focused breeding programs. Scenario analysis showed that incremental rise in THI (1–3 %) and reductions in rainfall (2–3 %) markedly reduce resilience capacities, reflecting physiological thresholds for thermoregulation and feed intake. A sustainable breeding strategy should therefore balance productivity with environmental adaptability, integrating resilience phenotypes into selection criteria alongside production and reproductive traits, supported by improved, environment-specific management interventions.
