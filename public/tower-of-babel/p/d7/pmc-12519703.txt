Human resource development in public health emergency management: a scoping review
Authors: Sileshi Demelash Sasie, Melkamu Asrat Alava, Neima Zeynu, Desalegn Dawit Assele, Sisay Temesgen Dema, Zenebech Mamo Argaw, Fantu Mamo Aragaw, Melkamu Abte Afele
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study systematically evaluated tools, frameworks, and methodologies to inform the development of a comprehensive and scalable human resource development (HRD) roadmap for sustainable public health emergency management (PHEM) in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), with a specific focus on Ethiopia. A scoping review was conducted following established methodological frameworks, drawing evidence from PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus, complemented by grey literature from Google Scholar and institutional websites such as the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention and the Ethiopian Public Health Institute. Studies published between 2000 and 2025 addressing HRD strategies for PHEM in LMICs, including case studies, reviews, frameworks, and policy documents relevant to Ethiopia, were eligible for inclusion. Data were extracted using a structured template capturing study characteristics, methods, and recommendations, and thematically synthesized into HRD domains. Forty-three studies met the inclusion criteria, yielding nine cross-cutting themes: public health competencies, emergency preparedness, workforce retention, training and capacity building, community engagement, decision-making frameworks, technology integration, governance and policy, and global best practices. Evidence highlighted the importance of competency-based frameworks, interagency coordination, innovative training, and equity-focused approaches. Ethiopian findings revealed progress in competency development and field training but persistent gaps in institutionalisation, financing, and sustainability. The review underscores the urgent need for a standardised HRD roadmap that integrates adaptable strategies, community engagement, gender equity, and governance reforms to enhance workforce preparedness and resilience in future health emergencies.Keywords: Public Health, Public Health Practice, Communicable Disease Control, Population Surveillance

Background

Globally, the lack of standardised human resource development (HRD) systems for public health emergency management (PHEM) and disaster management remains a persistent challenge. Recent crises—including COVID-19, Ebola and climate-driven disasters—have exposed major gaps in workforce preparedness, competency development and coordination. During COVID-19 in particular, many health systems faced acute shortages of trained personnel, limited emergency training and fragmented governance, resulting in delayed responses and higher mortality.1 2 These challenges are intensified in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), where inequitable workforce distribution and limited technology access compound vulnerabilities. Evidence from Africa, Asia and Latin America shows that resource-constrained systems struggle with retaining skilled workers, managing multihazard emergencies and maintaining preparedness, especially in underserved areas.3 4 Without a structured HRD roadmap, health systems often fail to respond effectively to emergencies, leaving populations increasingly at risk.

Global, regional and local initiatives have sought to strengthen PHEM systems, offering promising pathways for resilience. WHO-led frameworks such as the International Health Regulations (IHR) and the Sendai Framework provide strategic guidance for preparedness. Technological innovations—including digital platforms, virtual simulations and geospatial tools—have further improved real-time response and data-driven decision-making.5 6 Regionally, Africa Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has advanced workforce capacity through training centres and competency frameworks, while grassroots approaches in Guinea and India highlight the importance of community engagement.7 8 In Ethiopia, the Field Epidemiology Training Program (FETP) has strengthened surveillance, outbreak response and broader preparedness by equipping professionals through field-based learning.9 Yet, many such initiatives remain fragmented, under-resourced or insufficiently adapted to LMIC contexts like Ethiopia.

Despite notable efforts, major gaps continue to undermine HRD systems for PHEM, particularly in Ethiopia. These include the absence of standardised governance, limited integration of new technologies and inadequate attention to multihazard preparedness. Training programmes in Africa and the Eastern Mediterranean often fail to address rural workforce disparities or adapt to sociocultural contexts shaping service delivery. Ethiopia faces similar challenges of inequitable distribution and limited local adaptation.3 10 Existing frameworks also lack interdisciplinary collaboration, thematic consistency and robust monitoring and evaluation to assess workforce readiness and training outcomes.7 11

This scoping study addresses these gaps by systematically reviewing tools, methodologies and best practices from global and regional contexts. Its goal is to generate actionable insights for comprehensive, scalable and context-specific HRD roadmaps that strengthen workforce capacity, governance, equity and technology integration. In doing so, it seeks to enhance PHEM systems and build resilience against public health emergencies worldwide.

Methods

Study Design

This scoping review was conducted following the methodological framework established by Arksey and O’Malley12 and further refined by Levac et al13 and was guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews checklist to ensure rigour and transparency. The review systematically mapped global and regional evidence on HRD for PHEM, with emphasis on LMICs. It sought to identify priority themes, gaps and transferable practices in workforce competencies, training, governance and multisectoral engagement. No protocol was registered.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Web of Science and Scopus to identify literature on HRD within PHEM. In PubMed, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms were combined for concepts such as ‘public health emergency management,’ ‘emergency preparedness,’ ‘disaster management,’ ‘crisis response,’ ‘health workforce,’ ‘capacity building’ and ‘training.’ Equivalent terms were adapted for Web of Science using the TS (Topic Search) function and for Scopus using TITLE-ABS-KEY fields. Boolean operators (AND/OR) linked preparedness and workforce terms, and results were restricted to English-language, human-related studies published between 2000 and 2025.

Grey literature searches targeted repositories including WHO Institutional Repository for Information Sharing, Africa CDC (via structured Google site queries), the World Bank portal and the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI) website. Searches applied the same conceptual terms with refinements such as file-type filters (filetype:pdf), document-type keywords (report, framework, guideline, policy) and the 2000–2025 time frame. Google Scholar was systematically searched with the same terms; to maintain feasibility, only the first 200 results were screened.

Citation tracking complemented database searches. Backward tracking reviewed reference lists of included studies, while forward tracking used Google Scholar’s ‘Cited by’ function. Full database strategies, including filters and limits, are available in online supplemental table 1.

Selection Process

All retrieved records were imported into EndNote and screened in two stages: title and abstract review followed by full-text assessment. Two reviewers (SDS and FMA) independently screened all records against the predefined eligibility criteria. To ensure consistency, a calibration exercise was conducted on a random sample of 10 records at the title and abstract stage. Agreement was reached on nine of the 10 records (90% raw agreement), and Cohen’s kappa14 was calculated at 0.82, indicating substantial agreement. Following calibration, the full dataset was screened independently by SDS and FMA, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. When consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (DDA) acted as adjudicator. Data Extraction

A standardised extraction framework was piloted on five studies and refined for clarity. The domain ‘Focus area’ was expanded with examples (competencies, training, retention, governance, technology). The original combined domain ‘Best practices and recommendations’ was split into two, and ‘Pertinent findings’ was standardised to align with HRD/PHEM dimensions.

The final framework comprised nine domains: (i) authors/year, (ii) continent or country, (iii) study type, (iv) study design, (v) focus area, (vi) pertinent findings, (vii) study-reported limitations, (viii) best practices and (ix) recommendations. Data were extracted independently by two reviewers (SDS and FMA), with discrepancies resolved through discussion or adjudication (DDA). Extracted data were coded and synthesised inductively to identify overarching themes. The complete framework is provided in online supplemental table 2 and the populated extraction form in online supplemental table 3.

Critical Appraisal

As is typical for scoping reviews, no formal quality appraisal was conducted, as the aim was to map rather than assess methodological rigour. Quality varied: peer-reviewed studies generally described methods clearly, while many policy reports and programme evaluations lacked detail on design, data sources or analysis. Multicountry reviews often applied systematic approaches, but smaller case studies and institutional reports were largely descriptive. Overall, the evidence base provides useful insights for workforce development in PHEM, though findings from single-country reports should be interpreted cautiously.

Synthesis of Results

Inductive thematic analysis was used to synthesise the data. After charting, findings were iteratively coded and grouped into overarching themes. Nine thematic areas were identified: (1) public health competencies, (2) emergency preparedness and response, (3) workforce retention and recruitment, (4) training and capacity building, (5) community engagement, (6) decision-making frameworks, (7) technology integration, (8) governance and policy and (9) global best practices. Results are presented narratively with illustrative examples from LMICs and global practice.

Patient and Public Involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this scoping review. The study synthesised published and grey literature to map HRD approaches for PHEM. Although no direct involvement occurred, the findings aim to inform future workforce policies and practices that strengthen public health systems and serve communities.

Result

Study Selection

A total of 965 records were identified: 877 from databases (PubMed 822; Scopus 22; Web of Science 33), 55 from grey literature and citation tracking and 33 from Google Scholar. After removing 83 duplicates, 882 records were screened by title and abstract, with 797 excluded. 85 full texts were reviewed, and 42 were excluded for irrelevance, insufficient detail or duplication. In total, 43 studies met inclusion criteria and were included in the synthesis (figure 1).

Study Characteristics

The 43 included studies, published between 2000 and 2025, covered global, regional and national contexts, with strong representation from LMICs alongside comparative insights from high-income settings. Designs ranged from systematic reviews, qualitative and cross-sectional studies, and case reports to framework development papers. Focus areas included workforce recruitment and retention, competency frameworks, training models, governance and leadership, community engagement, policy development, and digital tools such as mHealth and virtual reality. Detailed study characteristics are presented in online supplemental table 3.

Identified Themes

This scoping review identified nine interrelated themes essential for strengthening HRD in PHEM: the development of context-specific public health competencies; comprehensive preparedness and response measures including mental health and coordination; workforce retention and recruitment strategies addressing systemic barriers; training and capacity building supported by leadership and innovative methods; community engagement rooted in cultural sensitivity and trust; evidence-based and adaptive decision-making frameworks; equitable integration of digital technologies; governance and policy reforms promoting inclusive and ethical systems; and global best practices that offer adaptable models for LMIC contexts.

Theme 1. Public Health Competencies

Competency frameworks are widely recognised as essential for workforce preparedness, yet their design and implementation differ across contexts. In high-income settings, Stoto et al15 described a harmonised European model emphasising continuous evaluation, while Batt et al16 proposed a global six-step framework that ensures stakeholder participation. These structured and well-resourced models illustrate how sustained institutional support can align competencies with evolving emergency demands.

In Ethiopia, Worku et al17 developed a national competency framework tailored to local health challenges, and Kebebew et al9 reported that the FETP improved outbreak investigation skills. However, as Okoroafor et al3 noted for many African countries, Ethiopia’s frameworks remain under-resourced, unevenly applied and weakly institutionalised. This mirrors a broader LMIC pattern where frameworks are introduced but struggle to transition into sustainable practice.

Curricular and practice gaps also persist globally. O’Meara et al18 and Randazza et al19 found weak alignment between competencies and workforce needs, while Rose et al20 and Burkle21 highlighted delays in updating competencies for emerging emergencies. Ethiopia reflects these challenges: although frameworks exist, inconsistent implementation and limited reinforcement constrain their effectiveness.

Evidence further shows that competencies must be continuously reinforced to remain relevant. Valiee et al22 emphasised ongoing training, and De Vries et al23 identified variability in training quality as a barrier to readiness. Ethiopian studies confirm this: Worku et al17 and Kebebew et al9 show progress in competency development but also highlight the need for regular updating, financing and integration into national HR policies.

Overall, Ethiopia’s experience is consistent with broader LMIC trends; competency frameworks exist but remain fragile. The contrast with high-income and global models underscores the importance of institutionalisation, resource allocation and stakeholder ownership. Embedding frameworks within Ethiopia’s HRD and PHEM systems, linked to continuous professional development, could make global lessons actionable and strengthen the country’s surge-ready workforce.

Theme 2. Emergency Preparedness and Response

Preparedness frameworks were consistently linked to workforce resilience. Training and support improved readiness: Valiee et al22 showed that mental health initiatives and continuous training enhanced retention, while Gupta et al8 24 emphasised flexible staffing and gender-equitable workforce policies as critical for sustaining capacity during COVID-19. Technology-based solutions also contributed—Wang et al5 and Bugli et al6 demonstrated the value of digital training, simulations and virtual reality for skill acquisition, although Wen et al25 cautioned that infrastructure limitations constrain uptake in low-resource settings.

Ethiopian evidence echoed these patterns. Kebebew et al9 reported that the FETP improved outbreak investigation and preparedness, but its reach was uneven and not yet system-wide.

System coordination and resources were recurring barriers. Brencic et al26 identified weak interagency collaboration, while Makuku & Mosadeghrad27 highlighted underfunding and inadequate training. Similar challenges appeared in Ethiopia, where systemic constraints limited the broader impact of FETP cohorts.9 At facility level, Cruz et al28 and Zhang & Lee29 noted deficiencies in incident management and operational capacity during crises.

Regionally, Sheffel et al30 mapped workforce shortages and fragile retention strategies, findings mirrored in Ethiopia where preparedness gains remain concentrated among trained individuals rather than the wider workforce.

Theme 3. Workforce Retention and Recruitment

Retention and recruitment remain persistent challenges across contexts, shaped by leadership, well-being and systemic constraints. Supportive leadership and career development were repeatedly linked to stability: De Vries et al23 found that strong leadership improved workforce retention, while O’Meara et al18 reported that high turnover undermined organisational capacity. Ethiopia showed similar risks, as Worku et al17 noted that weak integration of competencies into career development reduced long-term stability.

Well-being and flexibility also influenced outcomes. Valiee et al22 demonstrated that mental health programmes during crises improved retention, while Gupta et al8 highlighted that flexible staffing models supported both recruitment and retention. Ethiopian evidence echoed these findings. Kebebew et al9 reported that FETP graduates enhanced outbreak response but suffered from poor retention due to insufficient institutional support, reflecting fragile well-being mechanisms.

Structural barriers compounded these challenges. Khalil & Alameddine10 described cultural and economic obstacles to recruitment in the Eastern Mediterranean, while Stone & Deadrick31 and Mui et al32 highlighted weaknesses in global HR management. Mosam et al33 proposed a global roadmap for retention, and Makuku & Mosadeghrad27 emphasised the need for context-sensitive approaches in Africa. Regionally, Sheffel et al30 mapped shortages and fragile retention systems, trends also evident in Ethiopia. Gupta24 further underscored gender inequities in career progression, pointing to the need for equity-focused retention frameworks.

Theme 4. Training and Capacity Building

Training initiatives were widely linked to stronger emergency management capacity, though effectiveness varied. In Guinea, structured programmes-built confidence and skills (Martel et al7), while in the USA, curricular inconsistencies limited alignment with practice (Randazza et al19). Ethiopia shows similar tensions: its national competency framework embedded training priorities (Worku et al17), but uneven implementation reduced impact. Gender-responsive HRH policy remains critical, as highlighted by Gupta.24

Innovative approaches expanded training but revealed inequities. Virtual reality and simulations improved skills (Bugli et al6), and technology-supported training enhanced preparedness (Wang et al5), though infrastructure constraints limited uptake (Wen et al25). In Ethiopia, the FETP strengthened outbreak response,9 but limited integration into workforce systems reflects sustainability gaps observed globally. Leadership development through blended and scenario-based modules is increasingly emphasised.34

Governance and collaboration shaped effectiveness. Leadership training often fell short (Li et al35), inequitable access undermined progress (Gupta24) and weak governance reduced impact (Díaz-Castro et al36). Collaboration, however, improved outcomes (Zhong et al37). Regionally, donor dependence created uneven training landscapes (Okoroafor et al3). Ethiopia’s FETP mirrors these patterns—gains at the participant level but fragile institutionalisation. Overall, Ethiopia reflects a broader LMIC trend: training improves skills but remains fragmented. Embedding training into HR policy, addressing gender gaps and linking programmes to governance systems are needed for sustainability.

Theme 5. Community Engagement

Community engagement was consistently identified as critical for emergency preparedness. Community health worker (CHW) models proved effective in diverse contexts: Jackson et al38 showed that CHWs mobilised populations during COVID-19, while Corbin et al39 reported that tailored engagement strategies improved outcomes in African settings. Ethiopia reflects similar approaches through its Health Extension Program, where Worku et al17 described how health extension workers embed community actors within the health system.

Barriers limited impact across contexts. Boyce & Katz40 found mistrust in authorities undermined participation, Gomes Chaves et al41 reported that inadequate resources constrained community-based interventions, and Ranabhat et al2 showed that structural inequities hindered engagement in advancing universal health coverage. In Ethiopia, Kebebew et al9 noted that while FETP graduates supported surveillance and outbreak response, linkages with community health workers remained weak, reducing integration into preparedness systems.

Regional evidence echoed these dynamics. Corbin et al39 stressed that context-sensitive approaches-built trust, while Gomes Chaves et al41 highlighted persistent resource limitations across African health systems. Ethiopia’s reliance on health extension workers,17 combined with limited integration of FETP graduates,9 mirrors these patterns—demonstrating both the opportunities and constraints of community-based preparedness. Overall, Ethiopia exemplifies the challenge of translating strong CHW platforms into fully integrated emergency management assets.

Theme 6. Decision-Making Frameworks

Decision-making frameworks were consistently identified as critical for effective emergency response. Structured leadership models (Romiti et al42) and adaptive approaches (Príncipe et al1) enhanced responsiveness in dynamic crises. Ethiopia reflects similar concerns: Worku et al17 noted that the national competency framework did not explicitly include decision-making skills, leaving frontline workers underprepared to act independently.

Implementation gaps undermined effectiveness. Wang et al5 reported that frameworks were often poorly operationalised, while Al Qaf’an et al43 identified weak communication among stakeholders as a common barrier. In Ethiopia, Kebebew et al9 observed that decision-making in PHEM structures was highly centralised, delaying outbreak response consistent with broader global findings.

Innovations offered potential solutions. Khalil et al44 showed that incorporating local knowledge improved legitimacy and responsiveness, while Ye45 found that technology-enhanced systems strengthened evidence-based decision-making. Leadership programmes that explicitly integrated incident management and decision frameworks were most effective in improving response capacity (Li et al34). Regionally, Sheffel et al30 highlighted fragmented governance and HR systems that slowed decision-making across African countries. Ethiopia’s reliance on hierarchical structures9 mirrors these regional weaknesses, suggesting that decentralisation, communication strengthening and integration of technology could improve responsiveness.

Theme 7. Technology Integration

Technology functioned as both an enabler and a constraint in emergency management. Digital platforms improved coordination during COVID-19 (Ye45), and information technology applications enhanced data collection and response efficiency (Asadzadeh et al46). Technology-enabled leadership training, including online modules and blended learning, is increasingly common (Li et al34). In Ethiopia, District Health Information System 2 and Early Warning, Alert and Response System (EWARS) supported surveillance and reporting functions (Kebebew et al9), reflecting similar uses of digital tools globally.

Technology-enhanced training is expanding but uneven. Virtual reality improved readiness (Bugli et al6), while Wen et al25 and Li et al35 noted disparities in access that limit reach in resource-constrained settings. Li et al34 confirmed that technology-enabled educational methods are central to incident-management programmes. Ethiopia, however, has yet to embed digital competencies into workforce frameworks, with Worku et al17 highlighting limited emphasis on digital skills.

Infrastructure and analytic capacity remain critical. Inadequate infrastructure constrained technology integration (Khalil et al44), while data analytics supported evidence-based preparedness (Li et al11). Regionally, Sheffel et al30 observed uneven adoption of digital tools across African countries, limited by workforce shortages and resources. Ethiopia mirrors these regional trends: DHIS2 and EWARS are functional but weakly integrated into workforce training, leaving preparedness dependent on fragile systems.

Theme 8. Governance and Policy

Governance and policy frameworks were consistently identified as central to workforce readiness in public health emergencies. Adaptive structures enhanced responsiveness (Díaz-Castro et al36), and equity-focused policies supported sustainability (Haakenstad et al4). In contrast, rigid or fragmented governance slowed responses, as seen in South Asia and other LMIC contexts (Ranson et al47; Ranabhat et al2). Ethiopia reflects these tensions: while national competency frameworks exist (Worku et al17), they are inconsistently implemented, limiting their impact on governance and preparedness.

Transparency and accountability shaped institutional resilience. Accountable systems increased public trust (Al Qaf’an et al43), while historical analyses showed that incoherent governance undermined capacity (Rose et al20; Burkle21). Ethiopia shares these gaps, with Kebebew et al9 noting coordination challenges between the Ministry of Health and the Ethiopian Public Health Institute within the Field Epidemiology Training Program, constraining sustainability.

At the regional level, Sheffel et al30 documented weak institutional governance and fragmented HR management across Africa, patterns mirrored in Ethiopia’s limited policy translation and institutional coordination. Overall, Ethiopia exemplifies the broader LMIC challenge: governance frameworks exist but are inconsistently institutionalised. Embedding competency frameworks into policy, strengthening accountability and improving inter-agency coordination are critical for transforming governance into a driver of workforce resilience.

Theme 9. Global Best Practices for Strengthening HRD for PHEM

Evidence across settings highlighted several categories of best practices for strengthening emergency workforce capacity. Competency-based frameworks in the USA and Europe standardised preparedness and maintained alignment with evolving threats (Stoto et al15; O’Meara et al18; Randazza et al19). Ethiopia followed a similar path, with Worku et al17 developing a national framework to guide workforce readiness, though implementation has been uneven.

Community-anchored approaches in India and Guinea mobilised frontline response through CHWs and structured capacity-building (Corbin et al39; Martel et al7), while Canada advanced system-level preparedness tools (Gomes Chaves et al41). Ethiopia’s Health Extension Program similarly embedded community actors (Worku et al17), though not fully integrated into surge capacity.

Technology-enabled strategies included digital training in China (Wang et al5), geospatial applications in Japan (Ye45) and virtual reality pilots in Australia (Bugli et al6). Ethiopia adopted DHIS2 and EWARS for surveillance (Kebebew et al9) but limited workforce training constrained impact.

Retention and recruitment innovations, including mentorship and culturally adapted strategies in Kenya and Saudi Arabia (Khalil & Alameddine10; Sheffel et al30), contrast with Ethiopia’s persistent attrition among FETP graduates (Kebebew et al9). Leadership and governance reforms in Germany and Brazil (Li et al11; Díaz-Castro et al36) echo Ethiopia’s FETP emphasis on leadership, though institutional support remains weak. Regional reviews (Okoroafor et al3; Sheffel et al30) confirm systemic bottlenecks mirrored in Ethiopia, where progress in competency and field training coexists with fragile governance and limited sustainability.

Discussion

This scoping study highlights both advances and enduring challenges in HRD for PHEM. Competency-based training, flexible staffing and technology-enhanced learning have improved preparedness, yet systemic barriers such as fragmented governance, resource inequities and poor coordination persist, especially in LMICs. Community engagement and leadership development remain critical but underutilised. While global best practices illustrate potentially scalable approaches, their relevance and effectiveness ultimately depend on careful contextual adaptation and country-specific validation rather than direct transfer. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as broad patterns in the literature rather than definitive policy guidance.

The scoping study reveals critical findings that enrich the understanding of PHEM and HRD systems globally. First, core competency frameworks have emerged as essential tools for tailoring public health workforce training to diverse contexts. Worku et al highlighted Ethiopia’s development of these frameworks, emphasising their applicability in low-resource settings.17 Similarly, Stoto et al demonstrated competency models for emergency preparedness across Europe, showcasing their importance for enhancing readiness.15 However, challenges such as inconsistencies in implementation and variability in training outcomes, as noted by De Vries et al,23 underscore the need for standardising competency development globally. The proposal by Batt et al for a six-step model involving stakeholder engagement16 offers promising solutions for addressing these disparities, promoting context-specific and adaptable competency frameworks worldwide. Nevertheless, the evidence base remains largely descriptive, and further empirical testing across diverse LMIC settings is required before such frameworks can be generalised.

Emergency preparedness and response frameworks represent another significant focus area. Valiee et al identified mental health support and targeted continuous training as effective strategies for ensuring workforce stability during epidemics.22 Gupta et al reinforced this, showing the importance of flexible staffing in maintaining healthcare delivery during crises.8 In contrast, challenges like insufficient interagency coordination and resource allocation continue to undermine preparedness efforts, as highlighted by Brencic et al26 and Makuku & Mosadeghrad.27 The increasing role of technology in emergency preparedness offers pathways for improvement. Bugli et al demonstrated the efficacy of virtual reality for training emergency responders,6 while Wen et al underscored the importance of equitable technology integration to avoid disparities in preparedness.25 Taken together, these insights suggest important opportunities but should be regarded as indicative trends rather than prescriptive models, since local infrastructure, governance and resources strongly shape implementation feasibility.

Workforce retention and recruitment remain pivotal challenges in public health systems. Khalil and Alameddine revealed cultural and policy-related barriers in recruitment efforts across the Eastern Mediterranean region,10 while Valiee et al demonstrated the benefits of mental health initiatives in improving retention.22 Additionally, supportive leadership, as emphasised by De Vries et al,23 and adaptive staffing models noted by Gupta et al,8 serve as critical components for workforce stabilisation. Despite these advancements, challenges such as high turnover rates and limited professional development opportunities persist, as noted by O’Meara et al.18 Al Qaf’an et al’s findings on the effectiveness of culturally sensitive recruitment strategies43 indicate an opportunity to improve inclusivity and trust within workforce development efforts. Still, these findings derive from heterogeneous contexts, and their applicability across diverse LMIC systems including Ethiopia requires cautious interpretation.

The role of training and capacity building in public health systems is evident from the study’s findings. Martel et al’s evaluation of structured training programmes in Guinea highlighted their impact on improving emergency management capacity.7 Furthermore, Bugli et al’s findings demonstrated that modern training modalities like virtual reality enhance skill acquisition and preparedness.6 Despite these successes, disparities in educational interventions, as noted by Li et al,11 and challenges in governance structures hindering training implementation, highlighted by Díaz-Castro et al,36 remain significant barriers. Zhong et al emphasised the importance of leadership development in training programmes,37 suggesting that fostering leadership capacity could amplify the efficacy of training initiatives. Several practical examples illustrate how best practices have been successfully adapted in resource-limited contexts. In Ethiopia, the Health Extension Program has mobilised community health workers to strengthen preventive services and extend preparedness capacity to rural populations, demonstrating the value of community-based platforms for resilience.48 49 During the Ebola outbreak in Guinea and Sierra Leone, community-based workforce training and social mobilisation played a critical role in strengthening surveillance and response under severe resource constraints.50 This aligns with wider calls for harmonised international standards in emergency management to reduce governance fragmentation and strengthen coherence across contexts.51 These observations illustrate the promise of training innovations but also highlight a lack of consistent evaluation, making it difficult to determine long-term impact or generalise findings beyond specific contexts.

Finally, community engagement emerges as a cornerstone of effective public health strategies. Jackson et al demonstrated the vital role of grassroots community actions in improving health outcomes during the COVID-19 pandemic.38 Corbin et al further highlighted tailored community engagement strategies as critical for successful health promotion.39 However, barriers such as mistrust in health authorities and inadequate resources for community initiatives, as noted by Boyce & Katz40 and Gomes Chaves et al,41 present ongoing challenges. The integration of community feedback into programme design, as emphasised by Ranabhat et al,2 offers an avenue for enhancing inclusivity and cultural relevance in health interventions. Collectively, these findings affirm that community involvement is central to public health planning. However, much of the literature is descriptive and context-specific, and without stronger comparative studies, its wider applicability remains uncertain. Given variable methodological rigour, the results should be seen as indicative themes and directions for strengthening HRD in PHEM, rather than definitive evidence.

Strengths of the Study

This review provides a broad and systematic overview of HRD strategies for PHEM, with emphasis on LMICs. Its methodological rigour, guided by Arksey and O’Malley, ensures transparency and replicability. By integrating themes of governance, workforce, leadership, training and technology, it offers a multidimensional perspective on HRD and preparedness. Mapping current practices while identifying gaps, it generates insights to adapt global lessons to local contexts, enhancing relevance for both researchers and policymakers.

Limitations of the Study

Despite these strengths, limitations exist. Searches across PubMed, Web of Science and Scopus were systematic, but grey literature retrieval was less consistent. Google Scholar returned 33 reviewed records, though limited indexing likely excluded additional documents. Site-specific searches (eg, Africa CDC, EPHI) depended on institutional indexing, constraining completeness. This may have biased the evidence base toward peer-reviewed and internationally visible studies, under-representing local reports. Restricting to English-language sources likely excluded insights from francophone and lusophone Africa, limiting regional generalisability. Reliance on secondary sources adds potential bias, while thematic synthesis carries interpretive subjectivity. Finally, the scarcity of long-term evaluations limited assessment of intervention sustainability. Collectively, the findings should be seen as descriptive mapping rather than definitive policy guidance.

Recommendations

This review highlights key implications for advancing HRD in PHEM. Research should prioritise comparative, mixed-method and longitudinal studies to generate transferable lessons for LMICs. Practice should focus on competency-based training, leadership development, equitable recruitment, digital integration and community engagement. Policy efforts must align national HRD strategies with global initiatives such as harmonised competency frameworks and digital platforms while remaining responsive to community-level realities. Though not prescriptive, these insights identify evidence clusters, gaps and potential indicators such as retention rates, training coverage and simulation use. They provide a foundation for further investigation and contextual adaptation.

Conclusions

This scoping review synthesises global evidence on HRD strategies for PHEM, with a focus on LMICs. Progress is evident in competency-based frameworks, innovative training, leadership and digital integration, strengthening preparedness and workforce responsiveness. However, systemic barriers including fragmented governance, inequitable distribution and weak retention persist. While not definitive policy guidance, the findings offer an evidence-informed basis for further inquiry, piloting and local adaptation. By emphasising ethical governance, digital innovation and inclusive community engagement, the review identifies pathways for action by national stakeholders and global partners. Ultimately, HRD must be recognised as a strategic pillar for health security, preparedness and advancement toward the Sustainable Development Goals.
