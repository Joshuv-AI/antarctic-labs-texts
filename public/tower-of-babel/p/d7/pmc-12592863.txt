Caries Risk Assessment in Children with Early Childhood Caries and Late Childhood Caries

Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aimed to evaluate the caries risk in children with early childhood caries (ECC) and late childhood caries (LCC) using the American Dental Association's Caries Risk Assessment Form (ADA-CRAF) and compared contributing risk factors for cavitated and noncavitated lesions between the groups.Materials and methodsA cross-sectional study performed between January 2022 and December 2023 included 218 children aged 1–6 years, divided into ECC (<3 years, n = 109) and LCC (3–6 years, n = 109) groups, based on recently proposed caries classification terminology. Introduction

Dental caries is a common childhood disease that significantly affects the quality of life of preschool children and represents a considerable economic strain for families and community.1 Keels and Fontana redefined the term early childhood caries (ECC) as carious pathologies in the primary dentition that include the presence of any carious lesions (noncavitated or cavitated), missing or filled tooth surfaces in the primary dentition in children up to 3 years of age.2 Late childhood caries (LCC) is the new terminology that refers to caries disease patterns in the primary dentition involving any caries lesion occurring after age three until exfoliation of the primary dentition.2 The development of various caries risk assessment forms (CRAF) makes it possible to identify high caries risk children and to propose plans for early prevention and intervention.3 It also provides clinicians and healthcare providers with guidance on caries control strategies and treatment planning options.4,5

Henry et al. estimated the prevalence of ECC in India to be 49.6%, with the highest prevalence in Andhra Pradesh (63%), followed by Tamil Nadu (55.7%) and the lowest in Sikkim (41.9%).6 Preschool children can benefit from several multifactorial CRA tools that aid in the assessment of risk predictors, namely, the clinical presence of caries lesions and restorations in the oral cavity, pathological factors responsible for demineralization of dental hard tissues, and protective factors involved in remineralization of dental hard tissues.7–10 According to Kirthiga et al., there were 123 risk factors for ECC.11 The potential risk factors mentioned in the literature were enamel defects, increased levels of mutans streptococci, the presence of dental caries, obesity, increased consumption of soft drinks, and sweetened foods.11,12 In addition, noncavitated lesions need to be considered when assessing the caries risk of children, as they may lead to progression of caries to cavitated lesions in the future.13,14 Some studies have reported that about one-third of children have noncavitated carious lesions, suggesting that the lesion needs to be diagnosed and treated at an early stage to prevent progression.15–17

The term “LCC” has not been previously referenced in the literature; however, several studies have utilized the American Dental Association's Caries Risk Assessment Form (ADA-CRAF) to assess caries risk in children over 6 years of age.18 In contrast, no studies have examined CRA in children aged 0–6 years using the ADA forms. This evidence gap in the literature was critical in addressing unanswered questions regarding the assessment of cavitated and noncavitated lesions with the ADA forms. Therefore, the aims of this study were: (1) to assess caries risk in the ECC and LCC groups using the ADA-CRAF, and (2) to compare the caries risk factors contributing to cavitated and noncavitated lesions between these groups.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Consideration

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (CSP/21/MAY/94/362) of the university, and the participants were enrolled once the signed informed consent was obtained from their parents or guardians. The selection of participants consisted of children of both genders, with or without caries, aged 1–6 years. The parents of eligible participants who are willing to give their consent was included in the study. Children whose parents refused to give consent to participate in the study, children with medical impairments or syndromes, and children under 1 year of age and over 6 years of age were excluded.

Sample Size/Population

The study group contributed to 218 with an expected proportion of 0.44, a relative precision of 15% and a desired confidence level (1-alpha) of 95%. The groups were divided based on the recently proposed caries classification system: children <3 years (ECC group, n = 109) representing the early childhood caries period, and children aged between 3 and 6 years (LCC group, n = 109) representing the late childhood caries period until primary tooth exfoliation.2

Study Design

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, Sri Ramachandra Dental College and Hospital, Sri Ramachandra Institute of Higher Education and Research (Deemed to be University) in the period from January 2022 to December 2023. The study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology).19

Data Collection

The CRAF for ages 0–6 years, developed by the ADA, was employed as a tool to evaluate caries.20 Participant demographic details were entered into the ADA-CRAF, including name, age, gender, date of birth, and parent/caregiver name. The form consists of three sections, namely, (A) contributing conditions (fluoride exposure, diet, any eligible government nutrition support programs, parent or caregiver, and establishment of dental home), (B) general health (this section was not evaluated, as the participant did not meet our inclusion criteria), and (C) clinical condition (visually or radiographically detectable restoration or cavitated carious lesion, noncavitated/incipient carious lesions, missing teeth due to caries, presence or absence of visible plaque, presence of dental apparatus, and salivary flow). The lesions were grouped into cavitated and noncavitated lesions. Cavitated lesions were based on clinical evidence of lesions in the enamel through the dentin or pulp, while noncavitated lesions referred to white spots or incipient lesions without any cavitation.

Calibration of Examiners

Before the study commenced, two examiners (LV and AG) were trained to diagnose caries lesions using the International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS) criteria.21 The training included a 2-day CRA workshop at the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, Sri Ramachandra Dental College and Hospital, Sri Ramachandra Institute of Higher Education and Research (Deemed to be University), followed by a week of daily patient examinations in the outpatient clinic. The initial findings of the examiners were reviewed to identify discrepancies, and examinations were repeated until interexaminer agreement was achieved. Reliability was further assessed over 3 days on live subjects (six children) with carious lesions using ICDAS scoring codes. Two additional experienced researchers (KCV and GFP) cross-checked the results to ensure consistency. The kappa values for interexaminer agreement ranged from 0.429 to 0.920, while intraexaminer agreement values ranged from 0.680 to 0.972 and 0.494 to 0.811.

Data Scoring

Sections A and B of the ADA-CRAF were administered verbally, and responses were categorized as high, moderate, or low risk based on the answers provided by the participants' parents or guardians. Section C involved a clinical examination, with risk categorized as high or low based on the number of cavitated lesions, noncavitated lesions, missing teeth, and restorations present in the oral cavity, using the ICDAS criteria.21 Patients' overall caries risk score was classified as low risk if only conditions in the low-risk category were present, moderate risk if conditions in the low and/or moderate risk categories were observed, and high risk if one or more conditions in the high-risk category were identified.

Statistical Analysis

The data obtained from the completed ADA-CRAF was entered into a Microsoft Excel file. The collected data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software, version 16.0. The analyzed data were presented in a frequency distribution table, and a Chi-square test was performed to evaluate the association between caries risk factors and dental caries (cavitated and noncavitated lesions). A p-value of 0.05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

A total of 218 children of both genders participated in the study, with 109 participants in each group (ECC and LCC). Table 1 summarizes the overall caries risk of the included participants, categorized by age and gender. The results show statistically significant correlations between age-groups and overall caries risk, with 66.1% of participants in the 0–3 age-group and 89.9% in the 3–6 age-group categorized as high caries risk. None of the participants were categorized as “low caries risk.” The gender analysis showed that a higher percentage of females (84.7%) were at high risk of caries than males (73.7%), although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.055).

Cavitated and noncavitated lesions and caries risk in ECC and LCC groups

Table 3 shows that the prevalence of cavitated and noncavitated lesions was 25% higher in the LCC group than in the ECC group (78.9 and 63.3%, respectively), with a p-value of 0.001. Significant associations were observed with age, fluoride exposure, consumption of sugary foods and beverages, and visible plaque. Fluoride exposure significantly reduced the incidence of cavitated lesions (52.5 vs 75.2%) and noncavitated lesions (42.6 vs 58.1%), both with p < 0.05. Dietary habits also had a significant impact, with a higher percentage of lesions observed in children who frequently consumed sugary foods or drinks, particularly those who used bottles or sippy cups at bedtime (p = 0.001).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study explores the relationship between caries risk and the presence of cavitated and noncavitated lesions in children under 6 years of age. While previous studies have assessed caries risk across various age-groups,22,23 a direct comparison between ECC and LCC in terms of these lesion types has not been conducted. Numerous studies have identified various risk factors contributing to dental caries and extensively discussed preventive measures.24,25 Dental caries frequently occurs by an imbalance or growth of cariogenic microorganisms in the oral cavity, which can be prevented by taking measures like altering the pH of saliva, controlling carbohydrate intake, using fluoride, etc.26

The comparison between the caries risk and the clinically visible carious lesion categories (cavitated and noncavitated) in this study shows that visible plaque and the consumption of sugary foods are considered as the significant factors for a high caries risk in both groups. In agreement with the results of this study, Manchanda et al. reported that children with poor oral hygiene and visible plaque on the tooth surface were predicted to have a noncavitated lesion.14 In other studies, the prevalence of noncavitated lesions was estimated to be about half of the prevalence of cavitated lesions.14,17 The results of the current study indicate that 78% of children aged 0–6 years were at high risk for caries. Additionally, children without cavitated lesions (n = 77; 35.3%) and those without noncavitated lesions (n = 96; 44%) were found to be at high and moderate risk for caries, respectively. Therefore, children without visible caries should still receive preventive therapies, and oral hygiene measures should be implemented to prevent the development of future dental caries. Additionally, regular check-up visits combined with caries risk assessments (CRAs) are essential in mitigating the risk of caries in children. A study by Farsi et al. reported that 65% of children in the 4–5-year age-group were classified as high risk for caries.27 This result is consistent with our study, in which 66.1% of participants younger than 3 years of age and 89.9% of the participants in the age-group between 3 and 6 years had a high caries risk.

As children grow older, exposure to sugary foods increases, which is an important risk factor for caries development.28 Parents lack knowledge about oral hygiene choices and encounter barriers to implementing oral hygiene routines.27,28 Similarly, we observed that insufficient knowledge of oral hygiene measures, lack of exposure to fluoridated products, and lack of awareness of home dental care contribute to high caries risk. Most participants in this study (50% in the ECC group and 79% in the LCC group) reported having caries and this was their first dental visit to the dentist for an oral health check-up. Therefore, the concept of home dental home care should be considered and awareness on the early dental visits should be emphasized among the public. The home environment could have altered the caries risk scores if the children had visited the dentist more regularly, as reported by Farsi et al. in 2013.27 On the contrary, the information related to CRAF is deficient, and thus dental professionals also need to know about the application of CRA tools during their daily clinical work.29

This study highlights the necessity of integrating the CRA tool into routine dental check-ups. The implementation of CRA tools can significantly mitigate the risk of developing new carious lesions by encouraging dietary modifications, enhancing oral hygiene practices, and enabling the creation of individualized treatment plans with structured follow-up care. Furthermore, incorporating CRA tools into dental school curricula will prepare future practitioners with the essential skills to assess and manage caries risk effectively. Hands-on training and education in risk assessment will promote a preventive approach to dental care, aligning with modern principles of patient-centered practice. Collaboration among dental professionals, policymakers, and educators is imperative to ensure the standardization and widespread adoption of CRA tools in both clinical and academic settings. Integrating risk-based preventive strategies into national oral health policies can further advance public health efforts. By prioritizing preventive care and risk management in children, dental institutions can contribute to reducing the prevalence of dental caries and improving overall oral health outcomes.

The main limitation of the study is that the CRA tool was developed for the American population and could not be fully adapted to the Indian population. It has been emphasized that CRAT should include risk factors, such as tooth anatomy, proximal contact areas, etc., to predict caries risk in children.30 Although these forms and processes are widely used in the United States and around the world, Featherstone et al. noted that no published clinical outcome studies exist to validate them.7 Further modification of the form and validation through multicenter studies are required to improve its adaptation to the Indian population. Another limitation of the study is that the population was restricted to hospital-based participants, with many patients reporting that this was their first dental visit. The strength of this study is that, to the best of the author's knowledge, it is the first study in which patients' caries risk was assessed by comparing cavitated and noncavitated lesions in ECC and LCC groups using the ADA-CRAF.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study population had a high prevalence of dental caries (n = 147; 67.4%) in children under 6 years of age, as assessed by the ADA-CRAF. The analysis of risk factors, such as visible plaque and the consumption of sugary foods, shows that these factors contribute significantly to a high caries risk in both the ECC and LCC groups.

Clinical Significance

The outcome of this study suggests that workshops or courses should be provided for dentists on the use of CRA tools, along with community outreach programs to educate the population about management of dental caries in children. Furthermore, educating parents regarding the risk factors for caries development and offering anticipatory guidance consistent with the concept of the dental home would significantly reduce development of dental caries.
