The erection of two subgenera for Metarhadinorhynchus Yamaguti, 1959 (Acanthocephala: Isthmosacanthida) with a redescription of Metarhadinorhynchus (Metarhadinorhynchus) indicus in India
Authors: Omar M. Amin, Anshu Chaudhary, Nataliya Rubtsova, Bindu Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdult Metarhadinorhynchus indicus (Bhattacharya & Banerjee, 2003) Kita, Nitta, Kajihara, 2024 were originally described from Megalaspis cordyla (Linn.) in the Chennai coast, Tamil Nadu, India as Gorgorhynchoides indicus Bhattacharya & Banerjee, 2003. The genus Gorgorhynchoides Cable and Linderoth, 1963 was later synonymized with the genus Metarhadinorhynchus Yamaguti 1959.ResultsWe accept the synonymies involving 15 species of Gorgorhynchoides transferred to Metarhadinorhynchus. Our recognition of the synonymies is partially resolved with the erection of two subgenera: Metarhadinorhynchus (Metarhadinorhynchus) subgen. nov. with 12 species and Metarhadinorhynchus (Cylindratus) subgenus nov. with 3 species based on the presence or absence of anterior trunk swelling, respectively. We have recently collected cystacanths of M. indicus from the Asia seabass, Lates calarifer (Barramundi) off Andaman & Nicobar Islands, India. The cystacanths were comparable to the adults in the morphology of the common non-reproductive structures such as proboscis hooks and trunk spines with some variations mostly because of inaccuracies of descriptive statements in the original description. Comparisons are tabulated. Trunk size and reproductive structures varied because of their underdevelopment in the cystacanth stage. The anterior trunk swelling was sufficiently evident to confirm its presence in all stages of M. indicus. The long multinucleated filiform coiling lemnisci were similarly observed and the female genital pore is decidedly dorso-subterminal and not on the ventral side. A tabulated morphological description of the cystacanths and adults jointly constitute the redescription of M. indicus. The molecular characterization of the nuclear gene (18 S) and the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) sequence data of Metarhadinorhynchus (Metarhadinorhynchus) indicus is provided for the first time. Sequences from both genes were aligned and compared with other acanthocephalan species available in GenBank.ConclusionsThe phylogenetic analyses supported the establishment of the current species under the genus Metarhadinorhynchus Yamaguti, 1959. Additionally, in order to elucidate the phylogenetic relationships of the genus Metarhadinorhynchus, phylogenetic analyses were performed integrating different gene (18 S + cox1) sequence data using maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI). Our data supplement the molecular data for species description in the most reliable taxonomic frameworks alongside traditional morphological analysis.

Background

Cable and Linderoth, [8] erected the genus Gorgorhynchoides with Gorgorhynchoides elongatus as the type species originally based on a single female specimen obtained from a marine fish, Caranx chrysos (Mitchell) at Curaçao. Their generic diagnosis included a clavate proboscis with more than 20 longitudinal rows of at least 10 hooks per row, stouter ventrally and becoming gradually smaller posteriorly. The neck was declared as well developed and the trunk as narrow anteriorly and armed with numerous spines followed by a dorsal swelling either unarmed or armed with spines. The new genus was further characterized by long multinucleated lemnisci, open ligament sacs, and long uterus. Cable and Linderoth, [7] included two figures of the anterior end showing a swelling with what appears to be 7 scattered spines and the posterior end showing a long uterus, ovarian balls, and a dorso-subterminal gonopore. Later, Cable & Mafarchisi (1970) re-examined the type material and emended the generic diagnosis with more specific description of hook types, and indications of pseudo-segmentation in gravid females, brain at mid-receptacle, trunk swelling with or without spines dorsally or encompassing trunk, testes not contiguous, six tubular cement glands in a slender bundle, eggs without prolongation of middle shell, and bursa with sensory papillae in 3 species, Gorgorhynchoides lintoni, Gorgorhynchoides bullocki, and Gorgorhynchoides elongatus. Smales, [30] described 3 additional species from Australian fish; Gorgorhynchoides cribbi, Gorgorhynchoides queenslandensis, and Gorgorhynchoides gnathanodontos and created a key to 9 species: the above 6 species plus Gorgorhynchoides orientalis (Wang, 1966) Wang 1986 from China, Gorgorhynchoides epinephali Wang, 1986 from China, and Gorgorhynchoides indicus Bhattacharya and Banerjee 2003 from India. Five additional species mostly from the Indian sub-continent are also recognized: Gorgorhynchoides cablei (Gupta and Fatma, 1987) Bhattacharya, 2007 from juveniles in Mandapam, Tamil Nadu, Gorgorhynchoides golvani (Chandra and Hanumantha-Rao, Shyamasundari, 1984) Bhattacharya, 2007 from juveniles in Andhra coast, Gorgorhynchoides valiyathurae (Nadakal, John, Jacob, 1990) Bhattacharya, 2007 adults from Trivundrum, Kerala, Gorgorhynchoides pseudocarangis Huston and Smales, 2020 adults from Queensland, Australia, and Gorgorhynchoides lateolabri Yin and Wu 1984 from Liao He, China.

There was a total of 14 species of the genus Gorgorhynchoides Cable and Linderoth, 1963 that were recognized to date until Kita et al., [21] declared it as a junior synonym of their newly revised genus Metarhadinorhynchu Yamaguti, 1959 now also including the 15h species Metarhadinorhynchus lateolabraci Yamaguti, 1959 based on their molecular analysis of type material. We have recently collected cystacanths of Gorgorhynchoides indicus being transferred with qualifications to Metarhadinorhynchus, from the Asia seabass (Barramundi) Lates calcarifer (Bloch) off Andaman & Nicobar Islands, India for studies. Adult Metarhadinorhynchus indicus were originally described as Gorgorhynchoides indicus Bhattacharya & Banerjee, 2003 from the torpido scad, Megalaspis cordyla (Linn.) in its marine habitat at the Chennai coast, Tamil Nadu, India with minor addition of characters to the original generic diagnosis.

The present study combines with morphological and molecular data to explore the phylogeny of the studied species by using sequences of the 18 S rDNA and cox1 gene of mitochondrial DNA. In addition, we concisely discuss the erection of two subgenera for Metarhadinorhynchu Yamaguti, 1959 and its systematics. Incompleteness and lack of consistency in the treatment of the comparisons among species of Metarhadinorhynchus in Kita et al., [21] especially in their Table 2 prompted us to clarify the taxonomy of this genus by creating the two proposed subgenera.

Materials and methods

Collections

We have collected 4 cystacanths from individuals of Asia seabass (Barramundi) Lates calcarifer (Bloch) off Andaman & Nicobar Islands (11.68°N 92.72°E), India in the spring of 2025 for studies. Two specimens (one male and one female cystacanths) were used for microscopical evaluations. The other two specimens were used for molecular analysis.

The Andaman and Nicobar Islands are a union territory of India, comprising of a group of islands in the Bay of Bengal. Of the 572 islands that encompass this Union Territory, 38 are inhabited and they are grouped into two main clusters: the northern Andaman Islands and the southern Nicobar Islands, both are separated by a 150 km wide channel inside which we sampled our fish [38].

Specimens

Voucher specimen of one female cystacanth was deposited with the Harold W. Manter Laboratory of Parasitology, University of Nebraska State Museum collect. No. 218135.

Management of specimens

The intestinal tract was examined under a dissecting scope and acanthocephalans were collected. The fresh specimens were washed in tap water, fixed and preserved in 70% ethanol in the field then brought to the laboratory of department of Zoology, Chaudhary Charan Singh University, Meerut, Uttar Pradesh, India. For light microscopical studies, three and half acanthocephalans were shipped to our Scottsdale, Arizona facility for processing and further morphological studies. All data collected, together with digitized images, were stored on a USB for futures analysis and examination.

Study of specimens for microscopy

Acanthocephalan specimens were punctured with a fine needle and subsequently stained in Mayer’s acid carmine, destained in 4% hydrochloric acid in 70% ethanol, dehydrated in ascending concentrations of ethanol reaching 100% (24 h each), and cleared in 100% xylene, then in 50% Canada balsam and 50% xylene (24 h each). Whole worms were then mounted in Canada balsam. Ocular microscope measurements are in micrometers, unless otherwise noted; the range is followed by the mean values between parentheses. Width measurements represent maximum width. Trunk length does not include proboscis, neck, or bursa.

Line drawings

Line drawings were created by using a Ken-A-Vision micro-projector (Ward’s Biological Supply Co., Rochester, N.Y.), which uses cool quartz iodine 150 W illumination. Color-coded objective (10X, 20X, 43X) lenses are used. Images of stained whole mounted specimens were projected vertically on 300 series Bristol draft paper (Starthmore, Westfield, Massachusetts), then traced and inked using Pigma Micron 03-08™ (0.35–0.50 mm line width) für wasserfestes und lichtechtes Zeichnen, Sakura color products Corp., Osaka, Japan. Projected images were identical to the actual specimens being projected. Voucher specimens were deposited in the University of Nebraska’s State Museum’s Harold W. Manter Laboratory (HWML) collection in Lincoln, Nebraska, USA.

Optical microscopy

Images created for this presentation were acquired using a Zeiss Axioskop Transmitted Nomarski DIC Phase Contrast Microscope Trinocular (Munich, Germany) and a Canon T3i EOS 600D DSLR Camera (Melville, New York). Images from the microscope were transferred from the laptop to a USB and stored for subsequent processing on a computer.

Molecular methods

A part of the worm body was used for DNA extraction using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, USA) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The 18S from nuclear ribosomal DNA and the cox1 from mitochondrial DNA were amplified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The 18S gene was amplified using primer 18SU467F (5′-ATCCAAGGAAGGCAGCAGGC-3′) and 18SL1310R (5′-CTCCACCAACTAAGAACGGC-3′) [32]. The cox1 gene was amplified using the primer LCO1490 (5’-GGT CAA CAA ATC ATA AAG ATA TTG G-3’) and HC02198 (5’-TAA ACT TCA GGG TGA CCA AAA AAT CA-3’) [11]. The amplification was done with a volume of 25 µL in total comprised of 1.3 µL of each primer (10 µM), 2.5 µL of 10 × buffers, 2.5 µL of dNTPs (10mM), 13.40 µL of water, 3 µL of template DNA and 1 µL Taq DNA polymerase Taq (Thermo Scientific). The PCR cycling conditions for amplification encompassed denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, annealing at 55 °C for 18 S and 46 °C for cox1 for 1 min and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were then purified using the Purelink™ PCR Purification Kit (Invitrogen). The sequencing reactions were performed using ABI Big Dye (Applied Biosystems, Boston, MA, USA) terminator sequencing chemistry. The software Codon Code Aligner version 3.7.1 software (Codon Code Corporation, Dedham, MA, USA) was used to assembled the contigs, matched them with the available database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) using BLAST, and newly generated sequences were deposited in the GenBank database under the accession numbers; PX208925 for 18 S and PX207509 for cox1. The acanthocephalan species representing closely related are available on the GenBank was retrieved for the phylogenetic analysis. The data on acanthocephalan species comprised in the current phylogenetic analyses was provided in Table 2. The phylogenetic trees were generated based on the 18 S + cox1 sequence data using the maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) with MEGA version 11 [33] and TOPALi v2.5 [26], respectively. Floridosentis Ward 1953 Eoacanthocephala: Neoechinorhynchida: Neoechinorhynchidae) was chosen as the out-group.

For the concatenated analysis of 18 S + cox1 gene sequences, PhyloSuite v1.2.2 [36] was used to merge the individual alignments into a single dataset and to generate PHYLIP format file for ensuing phylogenetic analyses. The maximum likelihood inference was conducted in MEGA version 11 [33] and the substitution models were analyzed and assessed according to the BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion) using the ModelFinder [20]. For the 18 S + cox1 sequence data, the TVM + F + R3 model was determined to be the most appropriate nucleotide substitution model. The maximum likelihood analysis was established using 1000 bootstrap replications. The Bayesian inference (BI) analysis was run using the program TOPALi v2.5 [26] with two Markov chains Monte Carlo (MCMC) runs for 10 million generations and sampling every 1,000 generations. The first 25% trees were treated as “burn-in”. The uncorrected ‘p’ distances were estimated using the program MEGA version 11 [33].

Results

The present species was originally described in the genus Gorgorhynchoides (Arhythmacanthidae: Arhythmacanthinae) as Gorgorhynchoides indicus Bhattacharya and Banerjee, 2003 from adults collected from the torpedo scad Megalaspis cordyla (Linn.) off the Chennai coast, Tamil Nadu, India. All descriptive statements, measurements and counts are listed in Table 1 as originally given and with corrections by us. Much of the adjustments made to that description were observed in the cystacanths recently collected from the Asia seabass (Barramundi), Lates calcarifer (Bloch) off Andaman & Nicobar Islands in the same Bay of Bengal. Bhattacharya and Banerjee’s (2003) description was incomplete and a good number of additions, adjustments and corrections were made of their account in Table 1.

Redescription

Our description of cystacanths of Metarhadinorhynchus (Metarhadinorhynchus) indicus off Andaman & Nicobar Islands in the Bay of Bengal provided an opportunity to run comparative studies with the adults from another location off the Bengali coast, where it was originally described. Morphological description of our material with complete measurements and counts as well as line drawings, ocular microscopy images, and molecular analysis are provided. A comparison of measurements and morphology of all anatomical structures are compared with those of adults from the original description (Table 1). This table which also corrects the errors and mis-statements in the original description of adults, constitutes a redescription of the species. Disagreements between text and figures in the original description are pointed out and explained, which actually brought the two descriptions much closer together. We discuss in the following sections the findings of similar immature juveniles of the same species from other hosts in Yemeni waters of the Red Sea. In addition to the tabulated description of our cystacanths and the line drawings (Fig. 1), we provide additional images (Figs. 2-3) that better visualize the features presented in Table 1.

Molecular results

The 18 S + cox1 sequence data phylogenetic analysis results using ML and BI methods, respectively, were highly congruent. The concatenated 18 S+cox1 data generated here were analyzed together with other acanthocephalans including Metarhadinorhynchus (= Gorgorhynchoides) plus species used as outgroup (Table 2). The names Metarhadinorhynchus/Gorgorhynchoides of the species is retained in the tree the same as they are available on the NCBI database (Fig. 4). In the 18 S+cox1 tree, Metarhadinorhynchus clustered together with the representatives of Serrasentis species. This clade was sustained with high bootstrap support and Bayesian posterior probabilities (BP = 92%; PP = 1.00) (Fig. 4). The four species of Metarhadinorhynchus analyzed formed a clade, which was split into three subclades. The sub clade A corresponded with species analyzed as Gorgorhynchoides queenslandensis from Australia New Zealand and Metarhadinorhynchus (Metarhadinorhynchus) indicus from India with high bootstrap values (97%) and Bayesian posterior probabilities (1.00) (Fig. 4). In addition, sub clade B identified as Gorgorhynchoides gnathanodontos from Australia and M. lateolabracis from Japan with low to moderate support values (ML = 78%; PP = 0.85) (Fig. 4). The sub clade C associated with species analyzed as Gorgorhynchoides pseudocarangis from Australia, G. bullocki from USA and Serrasentis species with high bootstrap values and Bayesian posterior probabilities (BS = 92%; PP = 0.98) (Fig. 4). Individual analysis for 18 S gene in regard to the genetic distance between these clades formed by M. indicus + G. queenslandensis (OQ942220) was 2.38%, M. indicus + G. bullocki was 6.99%, M. indicus + G. gnathanodontos was 7.10%, M. indicus + G. pseudocarangis was 7.20%, M. indicus + M. lateolabracis was 7.59% and M. indicus + G. queenslandensis of the clade C show genetic divergence of 7.65%. Same as in case of the mt cox1 gene sequence, the intraspecific genetic divergence among the isolates of M. indicus with G. queenslandensis of the sub clade A ranged from 9.72% to 9.80%, with Metarhadinorhynchus species of sub clade 2 being 10.10% to 10.51% genetic divergence and sub clade C shows genetic divergence ranged 10.12%-10.44% among M. indicus and G. pseudocarangis and G. bullocki respectively (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Updating the generic perspectives

Our description and discussion of the reported material of M. indicus is a function of our tentative acceptance of the nomenclature of Gorgorhynchoides which is provisionary recognized herein as Metarhadinorhynchus. The revision of the genus Metarhadinorhynchus and the long list of synonymies based primarily on published descriptions of species of Gorgorhynchoides, among others, without the actual examination of the species in question, with one exception, as advanced by Kita et al., [21] is tentatively accepted, with qualifications. In their Table 2, Kita et al., [21] listed 15 species of Gorgorhynchoides that were synonymized with Metarhadinorhynchus based on proboscis shape, hook formula, trunk spine distribution whether in one or more fields, trunk shape at region of anterior spines, and number of cement glands. In that table, Kita et al., [21] did not distinguish between descriptions based on adult worms or cystacanths as this will affect trunk shape and may be the spine distribution and the expression of the anterior trunk bulbous swelling which they have not addressed in that table. They also did not make reference to the number and organization of trunk spines, position of testes, location of female gonopore, or length of lemnisci and their nuclear arrangement. All these variables together would determine affiliation to the new genus Metarhadinorhynchus. For instance, Gorgorhynchoides cablei (Gupta and Fatma, 1987) Bhattacharya 2007; G. golvani (Chandra et al., 1984) Bhattacharya 2007; and G. epinepheli Wang, 1986 were described from juveniles that have distinct differences from adults perhaps including their lack of “significant narrowing of trunk spine distribution area as noted in Table 2 of Kita et al., [21]. In our present paper, we compare adults and cystacanths of M. indicus (our Table 1) which clearly points to such differences. There is probably a significant taxonomic implication to whether trunk spines are in one field or two fields (separated or not separated per their Table 2). Similarly, the presence or absence and the location of the anterior trunk swelling when present is of grand taxonomic importance but this feature has not been incorporated in the important comparison Table 2 of Kita et al., [21].

On the Indian material

We have described our cystacanth specimens (Table 1) with line drawings (Fig. 1) and ocular microscopy images (Figs. 2-3) and tabulated our quantitative and qualitative results in comparison with those of adults in the original description by Bhattacharya and Banerjee (2003). Constant structures measured, quantified, and counted in our immature specimens such as proboscis and hooks were comparable to those reported in the adults. The size of other structures naturally varied as a result of growth of 5 mm long cystacanths to 60 mm adults. Other differences from the adults as occasionally inaccurately described in the original description stem from inconsistencies. For instance, Bhattacharya and Banerjee (2003) apparently did not measure the apical hooks on the proboscis even though they were clearly marked (their Fig. 1). It appears that the measurement reported for hook no. 1 may correspond to that of hook no. 2. Their hook formula was reported as 20–26 rows of 10–16 hooks each but 30 rows and 16 hooks per row in our specimens which perfectly agrees with their Fig. 1 of the proboscis. We noted one dorsal trunk swelling but Bhattacharya and Banerjee (2003) reported dorsal and or ventral swellings which may be the case in adult worms. We report more detail on the distribution of anterior trunk spines which appears to be more in columns (our Figs. 1-2) rather than in rings (their Fig. 2) but their overall distribution being more ventrally extended was identical. We note that the posterior tip of the inner wall of the proboscis receptacle is split which was overlooked in the original description. The shape and exact position of the cephalic ganglion was slightly off being slightly more posterior (their Fig. 2). Reproductive structures such as the separate testes were naturally variable on the account of immaturity of our specimens. Al-Zubaidy and Mhaisen, [1] found five encysted juveniles identified as Gorgorhynchus sp. identical to our material from India in the mesenteries of two longtail tuna, Thunnus tonggol (Bleeker) purchased in Al-Mehwat fish market, Hodeidah, Yemen, at the southern tip of the Red Sea. Longtail tuna normally feeds on a variety of fishes, crustaceans, and cephalopods [9]. Figures 1-2 in Al-Zubaidy and Mhaisen [1] of a whole male and a proboscis are identical to ours of Metarhadinorhynchus indicus and their 13.5–16 (15) long juveniles had 24–26 rows of 14–15 hooks each with anterior hooks larger (60–100 μm) than posterior hooks smallest (25–55 μm) with “a series of very small hooks present at the end of the upper third of the proboscis …. the proboscis sheath about twice as long as the proboscis” [1]. Their proboscis hook formula is closer to that reported in the original description of M. indicus. The distribution of anterior trunk spines with their more extended ventral (their Fig. 2A) was identical to that in our specimens. We hereby, assign these Gorgorhynchus sp. juveniles from Yemen as Matarhadinorhynchus indicus (Bhattacharya and Banerjee, 2003) Kita, Nitta, Kajihara, [21] with a world distribution that extends well beyond the Chennai coast of the Eastern Coastal Plains of India (Bay of Bengal) to the Red Sea.

Conflicting generic diagnoses

To be more specific, our difficulties in accepting the synonymization of Gorgorhynchoides with the genus Metarhadinorhynchus by Kita et al., [21] stem from the apparent disagreements between these two genera that can be clarified in the following quoted diagnoses. In their amended diagnosis, Kita et al., [21] state that trunk spines are arranged “irregularly” which is not the case in many species including M. indicus. These spines are often found in regular longitudinal columns or in rings. They also state that “Proboscis hooks gradually smaller towards base” which does not reflect the variation observed across the species. Actually, proboscis hooks become dwarfed in one or two circles at anterior third of proboscis in most species before becoming larger again and then decrease in size posteriorly. The generic diagnosis of neither Yamaguti [35] or Kita et al., [21] makes any reference to the common anterior trunk swelling, a significant character of taxonomic importance that is a point of contention in our following assignments. Kita et al., [21] make no reference to the cephalic ganglion being pre-equatorial as stated by Yamaguti [35] which is a generic trait and further state that “Testes tandem, at middle-level of trunk” (actually pre-equatorial and widely separate in their Fig. A) which appears to be consistent with the original diagnosis. (Yamaguti, [35]; Fig. 1) refers to “elliptical testes, tandem near anterior end of trunk, partly overlapping” (definitely contiguous). These accounts do not agree. The testes are actually often separate but can be contiguous in anterior trunk locations as in M. pseudocarangis or contiguous at the posterior tip of the trunk as in M. epinephali Wang, 1986, or in any other combination in-between. The amended diagnosis of Kita et al., [21] states that “Cement glands six, filiform, quite elongate…” as they appear in their Fig. A of a whole male but Yamaguti [35] additionally states that they form a swollen duct at attenuated posterior portion of Saefftigen’s pouch” which is missing from the diagnosis by Kita et al., [21]. We suspect that the “Vagina divided into two bulbous portions” in both accounts implies that “There are two vaginal sphincters (surrounding the vagina) …” [35]. Kita et al., [21] mentioned that eggs are “elongate, fusiform to elliptical” and they appear to have no prolongation of middle membrane (Fig. 1D) which appears to be the case in some but not all other species of the genus when reported. The anatomy of egg membranes is an important diagnostic trait that has been used as the determining character in the creation of new taxa at the subgeneric level such as the in the case of the subgenera of the genus Neoechinorhynchus Stiles and Hassall, by Amin (2002).

Therefore, based on the above, we declare that we only provisionally accept the synonymies advanced by Kita et al., [21] until the disagreements pointed to above are revised or satisfactorily resolved. None of their line drawings presented (Figs. 1A-G) are large enough to be of informative utility; considerable details are lost.

The synonymies in question

The above discussion puts into focus the fact that only 5 of the synonymies proposed by Kita et al., [21] have been tested at the molecular level. All else was based on published descriptions some of which was proven inaccurate or in error as is the case with M. indicus that we discuss above. Only the claviform shape of the proboscis and the 6 cement glands are stable characters and much else listed in their Table 2 may vary with developmental stage and varied interpretation of published results. The one other constant character that was not emphasized was the presence or absence of the anterior trunk swelling while detail about the organization of the anterior trunk spines remains wanting. Based on the above incomplete information and our own assessment of the importance the anterior trunk swelling as a key character of taxonomic significance, we propose to streamline the revised systematics of this reformed genus by erecting two new subgenera to the genus Metarhadinorhynchus. These subgenera are differentiated based on the character of the anterior trunk swelling as Smales [30] did to erect a key to the species of Gorgorhynchoides. The presence of spines on the swelling is a matter for future refinement of our proposal.

Taxonomic decisions

The evolution of the concept Gorgorhynchoides senso Cable and Linderoth [7] and Cable and Mafarachisi [8] into Metarhadinorhynchus senso Yamaguti [35] as revised by Kita et al., [21] is best visualized by tracking the corresponding generic diagnoses over time.

Generic diagnoses

Original diagnosis of Gorgorhynchoides [7]

Palaeacanthocephala. Proboscis clavate, its hooks in more than 20 longitudinal rows of at least 10 hooks per row, arranged in quincunx. Hooks somewhat stouter on ventral side, varying in size with level of proboscis but becoming gradually smaller toward its base. Neck well developed. Trunk with narrow anterior portion, armed with numerous spines, followed by a dorsal swelling either unarmed or with spines. Lemnisci much longer than proboscis receptacle, containing many nuclei. Ligament sacs open, uterus long. Hosts, marine fishes. Type and only species: Gorgorhynchoides elongatus.

Emended diagnosis of Gorgorhynchoides by Cable and Mafarachisi [8]

Palaeacanthocephala. Proboscis clavate, with hooks arranged quincuxically, in more than 20 longitudinal rows of at least 10 hooks per row. First three or four hooks in each row large, blade-like, followed by an abrupt reduction in size of hooks after which they gradually increase in length to about mid-level of the proboscis and then decrease; hooks somewhat stouter on ventral than dorsal region of the proboscis. Neck well developed. Trunk with narrow, cylindrical or slightly tapering anterior portion armed with numerous spines, followed by a swelling which may or may not encompass trunk or bear spines; swelling most conspicuous dorsally. Trunk posterior to swelling unarmed, with pseudosegmentation in gravid females. Proboscis receptacle with double muscle wall, attached at base of proboscis; brain at about mid-level of receptacle. Lemnisci with many nuclei, slender, much longer than proboscis receptacle. Ligament sacs open, uterus long, Vagina short. Eggs smooth, oval to broadly spindle-shaped, without polar prolongation of middle shell membrane. Testes not contiguous. Cement glands six, tubular, forming a slender bundle in which some glands reach posterior testis. Bursa with rays and numerous papillae. Parasites of marine fishes of the family Carangidae. Includes Goergorhynchoides elongatus (type species), G. lintoni n. sp., and G. bullocki n. sp.

The original diagnosis of Metarhadinorhynchus by Yamaguti [35]

Rhadinorhynchidae. Trunk cylindrical or subcylindrical, with numerous, small, hypodermal nuclei, covered anteriorly with spines which are imbedded in cuticular folds and arranged in quincunx, forming a single continuous group. Proboscis somewhat claviform, with numerous longitudinal rows of hooks which increase in size from the apex of the proboscis down to the broadest subapical region and then decrease toward the base; basal spines the smallest and not projecting very prominently. Neck short. Proboscis sheath fusiform to elongate elliptical, double-walled, with ganglion near its middle. Lemnisci slender, long. Testes tandem, contiguous, in anterior third of trunk. Seminal vesicle elongate. Cement glands 8, tubular, very long, forming paired elongate reservoirs posteriorly, one on each side of seminal vesicle and attenuated posterior portion of Safftigen’s pouch. Uterus tubular, very long; vagina divided. into two bulbous portions, anterior bulb representing the vaginal funnel; two (an anterior and a posterior) vaginal sphincters and terminal vaginal bulb present; vulva subterminal. History, shortfalls, and generic-subgeneric perspectives

The parent generic diagnosis of Gorgorhynchoides by Cable and Linderoth [7] was understandingly short as it was initially based on the study of only one female specimen. Originally, Linton found this worm during his extensive work at Woods Hole and recognized it as representing a new genus and a new species. He deposited the type specimens (USNPC 8044, 8045) but died before he could publish his results. Cable and Linderoth, [7] described the genus Gorgorhynchoides based on the material deposited by Linton. Cable and Mafarachisi, [8] later described 2 additional species in West Atlantic jacks from very few specimens: G. bullocki (1 male, 1 female, 4 juveniles) and G. lintoni (2 males, 3 females). This additional material allowed Cable and Mafarachisi [8] to redefine the genus and create a more expanded and detailed generic diagnosis with a few differences from that of Metarhadinorhynchus by Yamaguti [35]. This calls into question the rational for synonymizing Gorgorhynchoides with Metarhadinorhynchus. Kita et al., [21] de facto supported this contention by stating that “Before the present study, Metarhadinorhynchus and Gorgorhynchoides had been distinguished from each other essentially only by the number of cement glands, which was supposedly eight and six, respectively. After our finding, the type species M. lateolabracis has six instead of eight cement glands, the two genera cannot be separated any longer.” This justification for synonymy is not totally accurate and is only reluctantly accepted as Yamaguti’s [35] generic and specific diagnoses included characters that are in disagreement with those listed in the final diagnoses of Metarhadinorhychus senso Kita et al., [21]. For instance, Yamaguti (, [35], pp. 28–29) stated that proboscis hooks posterior to the broadest subapical region “gradually decrease in size toward the base of the proboscis” which is not always the case as hooks often become miniaturized at mid-proboscis point as is the case in our Indian material (Table 1; Fig. 2). Also, Yamaguti , [35] diagnosed testes as “elongated elliptical, tandem near anterior end of trunk, partly overlapping each other.” However, testes are not always elongated or anterior and are often not contiguous but rather separate. Yamaguti, [35] also referred to a large sphincter between the bursa and the terminal genital pore which have not been observed in other species of Gorgorhynchoides neither were the double vaginal sphincters in some. Lastly, Yamaguti , [35] described and illustrated (Fig. 1) the posterior portion of the cement glands as “forming paired elongate reservoirs posteriorly, one on each side of seminal vesicle and attenuated posterior portion of Saefftigen’s pouch.” Such structure has not been usually described in other species of Gorgorhynchides. We only restricted our notes to characters of taxonomic importance that should have been considered. There are others. Much home work needed to be done before these synonymies come clean.

Yamaguti, [35] gave a reasonably well-thought-out generic diagnosis of Metarhadinorhynchus but erred in counting 8 cement glands. Except for their correction of the cement gland number to 6, the emended generic diagnosis by Kita et al., [21] fell short from conveying critical information about characters of serious taxonomic implications especially that much of their taxonomic decisions were dependent upon such a diagnosis. They referred to bulbous swelling but made no reference to species without swelling or to the position of cerebral ganglion. They noted “proboscis hooks gradually smaller towards basal” but this character is highly variable and posterior hooks are usually filiform vs., being rooted anteriorly. They also made no reference to multi-nucleated lemnisci, and position of female gonopore. We are proposing the erection of the following two subgenera for the genus Metarhadinorhynchus senso Kita et al., [21] to rectify the incompleteness, misinterpretations, and inconsistencies in their treatment on the assumption that their proposed synonymies will stand and will not be revised out later on upon further testing.

This small group of gorgorhynchoid acanthocephalans comprising about 15 species now grouped in the genus Metarhadinorhynchus infects tropical marine fish especially Carangidae, and Serranidae from coastal India (Chennai), western Australia, China (Fujian), the Caribbean, and eastern Atlantic coast of N. America. Understanding the zoogeography of this related group of species would be of major interest that we have been trying to resolve, at least partially in this work.

Molecular perspective

In the acanthocephalan taxonomy, the solitary use of diagnostic morphological characters for the identification of species can still cause taxonomic misperception because sometimes the morphological characteristics are so small that they can be easily unnoticed [12, 29]. In a study by Kita et al., (2025), the authors mentioned Gorgorhynchoides [7]; as a junior synonym of Metarhadinorhynchus. Besides this, previous studies also mentioned the Serrasentis and Gorgorhynchoides as sister taxa; phylogenetic studies have repeatedly found a sister relationship between these taxa [4, 17–19, 21, 24].

Phylogenetic analyses generated with the combined (18 S + cox1) concatenated datasets represented relationship that are helpful in resolving the taxonomic status of acanthocephalan species. However, the genetic data obtained here supports the synonymies of Gorgorhynchoides to Metarhadinorhynchus, but as we have already discussed, only five of the synonyms advocated by Kita et al., [21] have endured molecular testing. In addition, the phylogenetic analyses inferred with the concatenated (18 S + cox1) dataset placed Metarhadinorhynchus (Metarhadinorhynchus) indicus with other congeners of Metarhadinorhynchus (= Gorgorhynchoides). The tree (18 S + cox1) present here is in agreement with the findings of previous phylogenetic studies that show a clear relationship among other genera [18, 21]. The present evidence also indicates that the species of Metarhadinorhynchus sequences appeared sister to the clade containing Serrasentis Van Cleave, 1923 sequences, which is the most closely related genera. However, previous studies based on the molecular data have also shown that Serrasentis is sister to Gorgorhynchoides and also demonstrated that Metarhadinorhynchus (Metarhadinorhynchus) indicus is phylogenetically distinct from allied species that are available on the database [18]. In this study, we also marked the relatedness of Serrasentis and Gorgorhynchoides, forming a resolved clade that comprises two sub-clades in separate monophyletic lineages. In a study, Huston and Smales [18], Li et al. [23] and Najjari et al. [2] advocated that the 18S rDNA and mitochondrial cox1 datasets effectively discriminate among species and make available robust phylogenetic resolution, as showed by the genera Gorgorhynchoides and Serrasentis.

Conclusions

At present, it is difficult to support the proposed division of the genus Metarhadinorhynchus into two subgenera at the molecular level, as species assigned to the subgenus Cylindratus viz.., Metarhadinorhynchus (C.) epinepheli, M. (C.) golvani, and M. (C.) orientalis lack available molecular data. Therefore, confirming this taxonomic subdivision molecularly must be deferred to future studies. There is only one option, to get the molecular data for the three species that fall under Cylindratus first to verify the proposed classification at the molecular level. To improve the taxonomic species descriptions, usability of the molecular data must become a customary practice in the future studies of these parasites that will be imitated to create a clear picture of the diversity of acanthocephalans.
