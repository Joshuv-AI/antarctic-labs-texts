Association between physical activity propensity and cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy in type 1 diabetes: evidence from a single-centre study
Authors: Simona Zaccaria, Laura Giurato, Isabella Nardone, Sium Wolde Sellasie, Innocenza Tripicchio, Marco Meloni, Pasquale Di Perna, Luigi Uccioli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate whether recent physical activity (PA), assessed in a real-world outpatient setting, is associated with a lower prevalence of cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy (CAN) in individuals with type 1 diabetes.MethodsSixty-six patients with type 1 diabetes were tested for CAN using cardiovascular reflex tests. PA level was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) and patients were classified into low, moderate, or high PA groups.ResultsPA was low in 13 (19.7%), moderate in 19 (28.8%) and high in 34 (51.5%) patients. Fisher’s exact test conducted to examine the relationship between PA groups and diabetic CAN categories revealed a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001) being absent in 67.7% and early in 29.4% of the patients belonging to the high PA group. In addition, engagement in at least 600 MET minutes per week was independently associated with a lower probability of having CAN (OR = 0.131, 95% CI: 0.017–0.986, p = 0.048).ConclusionsHigher levels of self-reported physical activity are associated with a reduced likelihood of CAN in patients with type 1 diabetes. These findings support the importance of promoting regular PA as part of routine diabetes care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01936-w.

Introduction

Diabetic autonomic neuropathy is a complex clinical condition that affects the autonomic nervous system of various organs and systems, including heart, vessels, bowel, bladder, pupillary, erectile, and sweat glands [1, 2].

Among its various forms, cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy (CAN) is one of the most clinically relevant, characterized by impaired autonomic regulation of the cardiovascular system in individuals with diabetes, in the absence of other identifiable causes [3]. In unselected cohorts of individuals with type 1 and type 2 diabetes, the prevalence of confirmed CAN ranges from 16.6 to 20% [1, 3–5].

In advanced stages, CAN presents with reduced heart rate variability, resting tachycardia, orthostatic hypotension, and reduced exercise tolerance, and may even contribute to silent myocardial ischemia [6]. However, in earlier stages, it is often asymptomatic or associated with only mild symptoms, leading to underdiagnosis in clinical practice [6].

Early identification is crucial, as CAN is a strong predictor of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [1, 7] and negatively impacts quality of life [8].

While poor glycemic control remains the main risk factor for CAN in type 1 diabetes, results from the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT) and the Epidemiology of Diabetes Interventions and Complications (EDIC) studies suggest that improved glucose control alone is insufficient to completely prevent or delay its onset [9].

Additional correlates have been identified, including age, diabetes duration, peripheral neuropathy, and coexisting cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, increased BMI, dyslipidemia, retinopathy, and nephropathy [10–12].

Physical activity (PA) is widely recognized for its benefits in reducing both cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, as well as lowering cardiovascular risk factors and helping to prevent the onset of peripheral neuropathy [13–16].

Large epidemiological studies support this: a 17-year US cohort (n > 480.000) showed that meeting PA guidelines significantly reduced 5-year mortality risk [14]. Similarly, a Taiwanese study in over 500.000 adults demonstrated a dose–response relationship between weekly Activity Quotient and lower risks of cardiovascular, cancer-related, and all-cause mortality [15].

In patients with type 1 diabetes, even short-term structured PA interventions (≥ 135 min/week for 3 months) have led to improvements in cardiovascular risk markers, including reductions in LDL-C, apolipoproteins, fat mass, waist-to-hip ratio, blood pressure, and resting heart rate, along with increased lean body [13].

Endurance exercise has also been shown to enhance heart rate variability in individuals with no or early-stage CAN [17], and long-term aerobic training may delay or prevent diabetic peripheral neuropathy [16].

Despite growing evidence from structured exercise interventions, the impact of patients’ self-reported physical activity in real-world outpatient settings remains underexplored.

This study aims to assess whether a propensity toward regular exercise-based lifestyle is associated with a lower prevalence of cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy in patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus.

Methods

In an unselected enrollment followed at the Diabetes and Endocrinology Section of the CTO Hospital, (Diabetes and Endocrinology Section of CTO Hospital) (Rome, Italy), 66 outpatients with type 1 diabetes mellitus were evaluated for microangiopathic complications.

The inclusion criteria were age between 18 and 65 years and a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes mellitus for at least 5 years.

The exclusion criteria were recent cardiovascular acute events, the presence of a cardiac pacemaker or atrial fibrillation, active foot ulcers, major lower limb amputations, the presence of neuropathy from causes other than diabetes, an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2, psychiatric diseases or conditions that prevented an understanding of the administered questionnaires, and a lack of consent for the processing of personal data.

Oral and written informed consent was obtained from each patient.

First, anthropometric, clinical and metabolic data were collected from all study participants.

Males (thirty-one subjects, 47% of the sample) were also asked to complete the International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF-5) questionnaire to screen for symptoms of erectile dysfunction.

Diabetic polyneuropathy (DPN) was evaluated by investigating the presence of symptoms using the Michigan Neuropathy Symptoms Instrument; assessing signs using the Diabetic Neuropathy Index; and conducting lower limb electroneurography to study the sural, peroneal motor, and tibial nerves.

Patients were classified according to the Toronto classification [18] of diabetic polyneuropathy as follows:

Absent polyneuropathy due to the absence of signs, symptoms, and a negative nerve conduction study; or, based on the presence of signs or symptoms;Certain or probable polyneuropathy based on the coexistence of symptoms and signs of DPN or an abnormal nerve conduction study and symptoms or signs of DPN.

Absent polyneuropathy due to the absence of signs, symptoms, and a negative nerve conduction study; or, based on the presence of signs or symptoms;

Absent polyneuropathy due to the absence of signs, symptoms, and a negative nerve conduction study; or, based on the presence of signs or symptoms;

Certain or probable polyneuropathy based on the coexistence of symptoms and signs of DPN or an abnormal nerve conduction study and symptoms or signs of DPN.

Certain or probable polyneuropathy based on the coexistence of symptoms and signs of DPN or an abnormal nerve conduction study and symptoms or signs of DPN.

Due to the small sample size of the patients studied, the DPN categories were grouped into two subsets (absent/possible and certain/probable).

The presence of cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy was determined according to guidelines by performing cardiovascular reflex tests that evaluate heart rate variability, such as deep breathing, lying to standing, and the Valsalva manoeuvre, in addition to the orthostatic hypotension test [3].

A single abnormal or two borderline results in heart rate tests suggest early CAN; two or more positive results in heart rate tests indicate confirmed CAN, whereas an advanced condition is indicated by a positive orthostatic hypotension test associated with abnormal heart rate variation tests [3].

Test abnormalities should be defined using technical and age-based normative data; in this study, we used the NeuroTester Air (METEDA Srl, Italy).

PA was assessed using the long version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) and expressed in minutes per day and/or days per week [19].

IPAQ consists of 37 items: 4 related to demographic information, 6 assessing the respondent’s understanding of the questionnaire, and 27 focused on physical activity behaviors. The questionnaire investigates four main domains: activity performed at work, during active transportation, in household and caregiving tasks, and during leisure time or sports.

Participants were classified into three PA categories based on standard IPAQ criteria: 1) High PA: patients achieving at least 1500 MET-minutes/week of vigorous-intensity activity on at least three days, or a total of ≥ 3000 MET-minutes/week of any combination of walking, moderate-, or vigorous-intensity activity on seven or more days.

2)Moderate PA: patients reporting at least 20 min/day of moderate- or vigorous-intensity activity on at least three days, or achieving ≥ 600 MET-minutes/week over five or more days of walking, moderate, or vigorous activity.

3) Low PA: patients who did not meet the criteria for either the moderate or high PA categories.

All included participants completed the IPAQ in full. Cases with missing or inconsistent responses were excluded during the initial enrollment phase; therefore, no missing data were present in the final dataset.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were carried out in Jamovi version 2.3.28 [20]. A post hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1) [21].

A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Quantitative data were presented as mean (SD) and median (interquartile range), based on the distribution of the data evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk W-test. Qualitative data were expressed as frequencies. Associations between categorical variables were analyzed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to compare continuous variables across physical activity groups. A binomial logistic regression analysis was conducted to evaluate whether physical activity is associated with a lower likelihood of CAN.

A separate model was used to explore the association between DPN and CAN, given that the two complications may share common risk factors.

Results

From November 2023 to October 2024, sixty-six patients with type 1 diabetes attending a diabetes outpatient clinic were consecutively enrolled and screened for diabetic autonomic neuropathy.

The median age of the study participants was 51 years (46–58), and 53% were female.

Twenty-seven (40.9%) diabetic patients in the study sample had at least one microangiopathic complication: 28.8% had retinopathy, 7.6% had kidney disease, and 31.8% were classified as having certain or probable diabetic polyneuropathy.

Physical activity was low in 13 (19.7%), moderate in 19 (28.8%) and high in 34 (51.5%) patients in the study sample.

Based on the results of cardiovascular reflex tests, cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy was present in 33 patients. Among these patients, 20 had early CAN and 13 had confirmed CAN.

No participants met the criteria for advanced CAN. The actual prevalence may have been slightly underestimated, as six patients with proliferative retinopathy could not perform the Valsalva manoeuvre, and one patient with a tracheal prosthesis was unable to perform both the Valsalva and deep breathing tests.

Among male participants, the IIEF-5 questionnaire revealed median scores of 12 (2–15) in the low PA group, 20 (15–24) in the moderate group, and 24 (20–25) in the high PA group.

No significant differences were observed between PA groups with respect to age (p = 0.763), sex (p = 0.085), BMI (p = 0.581), diabetes duration (p = 0.083), smoking status (p = 0.787), alcohol consumption (p = 0.836), creatinine (p = 0.315), estimated glomerular filtration rate (p = 0.462), or albuminuria (p = 0.236). Similarly, no associations were found with diabetic kidney disease (p = 0.079), carotid atherosclerosis (p = 0.402), or dyslipidemia (p = 0.777).

Conversely, significant differences were observed across PA groups in glycated hemoglobin (p = 0.047), presence of diabetic polyneuropathy (p < 0.001), retinopathy (p = 0.002), and hypertension (p = 0.026).

An association between PA level and CAN classification was confirmed by a Fisher’s exact test (p < 0.001). In the high PA group, 67.7% had no CAN, while in the low PA group, 30.8% had early CAN and 53.8% had confirmed CAN (Fig. 1; Table 2).

While early-stage CAN was similarly prevalent in low and high PA groups, the markedly lower rate of confirmed CAN in the high PA group suggests a potential role of physical activity in slowing disease progression.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that engaging in at least 600 MET-minutes/week of physical activity was independently associated with a lower likelihood of having CAN (OR = 0.131, 95% CI: 0.017–0.986, p = 0.048). In the same model, hypertension (OR = 6.678, 95% CI: 1.688–26.416, p = 0.007) and diabetic retinopathy (OR = 9.985, 95% CI: 1.837–54.275, p = 0.008) emerged as significant risk factors. A diabetes duration longer than 20 years showed a borderline association (OR = 0.213, 95% CI: 0.045–1.019, p = 0.053), while HbA1c was not significantly associated with CAN (p = 0.696).

Diabetic peripheral neuropathy was excluded from this model due to its possible mediating role in the relationship between PA and CAN. Indeed, the frequent co-occurrence of DPN and CAN is often considered an expression of generalized diabetic neuropathic damage, resulting from shared pathophysiological mechanisms. A separate binary logistic regression, adjusted for age, sex, diabetes duration, and HbA1c, showed that certain or probable DPN was significantly associated with CAN (OR = 4.905, 95% CI: 1.307–18.409, p = 0.018), corresponding to an approximately 4.9-fold increased risk compared to patients with absent or possible DPN. Among covariates, sex (p = 0.626), diabetes duration (p = 0.842), and HbA1c (p = 0.136) were not significantly associated with CAN. Age showed a borderline association (OR = 1.062, p = 0.064).

Lastly, IIEF-5 scores also differed significantly across PA groups (p = 0.024), supporting the link between higher physical activity and better autonomic function (Table 2).

Discussion

According to the American Diabetes Association guidelines and the Italian Society of Diabetology (SID) with the Italian Association of Clinical Diabetologists (AMD) national guidelines, screening for diabetic autonomic neuropathy is recommended in all individuals with type 1 diabetes starting five years after diagnosis, and at diagnosis for those with type 2 diabetes [22].

Early detection of CAN allows timely management of symptoms, such as tachycardia, and cardiovascular risk through personalized interventions [9].

Several factors influence the likelihood of developing CAN, including diabetes duration and poor glycemic control, consistently identified as key contributors in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes [23, 24]. Other risk factors include age, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and coexisting microvascular complications [24].

For instance, Tannus et al. [11] found resting heart rate, diabetic neuropathy, and retinopathy to be independent predictors of CAN in individuals with type 1 diabetes. Similarly, Bhati and Hussain reported an association between reduced physical activity, elevated HbA1c, and higher risk of autonomic dysfunction in people with type 2 diabetes [25].

This study explored whether a propensity for physical activity was associated with the presence of CAN in individuals with type 1 diabetes.

PA was assessed by IPAQ, which captures recent self-directed behavior, regardless of prior lifestyle counseling, aiming to reflect patients’ real-life habits at the time of evaluation.

The three PA groups (low, moderate, high) were generally comparable in demographic, anthropometric, and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, diabetes duration, creatinine level, estimated glomerular filtration rate, albuminuria, nephropathy, carotid atherosclerosis and dyslipidemia. This reduces the likelihood that these variables confounded the association between PA and CAN. However, significant differences were observed for HbA1c, diabetic polyneuropathy, retinopathy, and hypertension.

To better understand the association between self-reported physical activity and the presence of CAN, a multivariate logistic regression model was applied. Engagement in more than 600 MET-minutes of PA per week was independently associated with a significantly lower probability of having CAN (OR = 0.131, 95% CI: 0.017–0.986, p = 0.048), even after adjusting for key clinical variables. In this model, hypertension and diabetic retinopathy were also significantly associated with CAN, while glycated hemoglobin and diabetes duration were not.

These results are consistent with current guidelines recommending at least 150 min of moderate-intensity activity per week for people with diabetes [26, 27].

A separate regression analysis confirmed that certain or probable DPN was significantly associated with increased odds of CAN (OR = 4.905, 95% CI: 1.307–18.409, p = 0.018), independently of age, sex, diabetes duration, and HbA1c.

This finding supports the hypothesis of shared pathophysiological pathways underlying these two diabetic complications [28].

The co-occurrence of peripheral and autonomic neuropathy has been documented in previous studies. Lluch et al. reported a strong correlation between the presence of DPN and CAN in a cohort of patients with type 1 diabetes, regardless of age [29], while Piaggesi et al. observed that patients with autonomic dysfunction exhibited significantly higher vibratory perception thresholds, suggesting a parallel impairment of both somatic and autonomic nerve fibers [30].

Erectile dysfunction, another recognized marker of autonomic impairment, also differed significantly across PA groups (p = 0.024), based on IIEF-5 scores. This observation is consistent with existing literature linking physical inactivity and cardiovascular risk to erectile dysfunction.

PA is known to enhance endothelial nitric oxide production, improve insulin sensitivity, optimize lipid and glycemic control, and reduce systemic inflammation, all mechanisms that contribute to better cardiovascular and sexual function [31–33]. Accordingly, higher levels of PA have been associated with lower prevalence of erectile dysfunction and improved response to PDE5 inhibitors [34–36].

Strengths of this study include administration of tests by the same operator and neuropathy assessments using gold-standard criteria. The real-world clinical setting and inclusion of consecutively enrolled patients further enhance generalizability.

However, the relatively small sample size, particularly the low-activity group (n = 13), may have introduced instability in regression estimates. Nonetheless, a post hoc power analysis using a chi-square test (df = 4, N = 66, W = 0.53, α = 0.05) showed a statistical power of 0.949, confirmed adequate statistical power (0.949) for detecting group differences.

Additional limitations include reliance on self-reported PA, which may be subject to recall bias, and the lack of temporal data, as the IPAQ captures only recent activity. Residual confounding from unmeasured variables (e.g., socioeconomic status, comorbid depression, and medication use) cannot be ruled out and should be addressed in future research.

Conclusions

This study suggests that a more active lifestyle is associated with a lower prevalence of cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy in individuals with type 1 diabetes. Specifically, engaging in at least 600 MET-minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity per week was independently linked to a significantly reduced likelihood of CAN.

Future longitudinal and interventional studies involving larger and more heterogeneous populations are warranted to better understand the role of regular physical activity in the onset and progression of this complication.
