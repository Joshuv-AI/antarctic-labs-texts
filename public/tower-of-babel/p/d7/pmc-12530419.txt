Development and validation of a risk prediction model for preterm premature rupture of membranes: a cross-sectional study at North Wollo Zone governmental hospitals, Northern Ethiopia
Authors: Tadele Emagneneh, Chalie Mulugeta, Nigus Bililgn Yimer, Betelhem Ejigu, Abebaw Alamrew, Delelegn Tsegaye, Amanuel Tebabal Nega, Aynalem Yetwale
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo develop and validate a risk prediction model for preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM) to enable early identification of at-risk women and support clinical decision-making in North Wollo Zone, Ethiopia.DesignA hospital-based retrospective cross-sectional study.SettingSix public hospitals in the North Wollo Zone, Northern Ethiopia.ParticipantsA total of 1098 pregnant women were included in the study using systematic random sampling.Primary outcome measuresOccurrence of PPROM.MethodsData were collected between 20 November 2023 and 20 March 2024, using structured interviews and medical record reviews. A risk prediction model was developed using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator and logistic regression. Model performance was assessed through area under the curve (AUC), calibration plots and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Internal validation was conducted via bootstrap resampling. A simplified risk score was created to categorise women into high-risk and low-risk groups, and its clinical utility was evaluated using decision curve analysis.ResultsAmong the 1098 participants (100% response rate), the mean age was 21.54 years (IQR: 18–26), with 57.2% aged 20–34 years. The prevalence of PPROM was 10.75% (95% CI 9.01% to 12.77%). Seven significant predictors were identified: maternal age <20 years, urinary tract infection, anaemia, history of PROM, pregnancy-induced hypertension, short birth interval and polyhydramnios. The model demonstrated good discrimination (AUC=0.873; bias-corrected AUC=0.87). A simplified risk score (range 3–13) using a cut-off of ≥6.5 classified women into low-risk and high-risk groups, with PPROM incidence rates of 4.76% and 38.7%, respectively. Decision curve analysis showed good clinical utility, and a nomogram was developed for individualised risk assessment.ConclusionsPPROM remains a significant obstetric complication in the study area. The validated risk prediction model showed moderate to good performance and can be used to support early screening and risk-based management in antenatal care (ANC). Integrating the tool into routine ANC services, along with health education and management of modifiable risk factors, may help reduce PPROM-related adverse outcomes. Further external validation is recommended.

Introduction

A fluid-filled sac called the bag of water or amniotic fluid covers the developing baby.1 Amniotic fluid is usually produced continuously, but after about 16 weeks of pregnancy, fetal urine production takes over. It defends against infection, fetal trauma and compression of the umbilical cord. Compression of the umbilical cord and reduced placental blood flow may occur when amniotic fluid is low or absent.1 2

Preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), defined as the rupture of fetal membranes before 37 weeks of gestation and before the onset of labour, is a significant obstetric complication associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes.3 PPROM accounts for nearly one-third of all preterm births and is a major contributor to perinatal morbidity and mortality worldwide.4 5 The condition increases the risk of neonatal complications such as respiratory distress syndrome, neonatal sepsis, intraventricular haemorrhage and perinatal death. Additionally, maternal complications, including chorioamnionitis and postpartum endometritis, further emphasise the need for early identification and management of at-risk pregnancies.2 5 6

Despite advances in obstetric care, predicting the occurrence of PPROM remains challenging due to the multifactorial nature of its aetiology. Several maternal, fetal and environmental factors, including infections, previous preterm birth, short cervical length, low socioeconomic status and nutritional deficiencies, have been implicated in the pathogenesis of PPROM.79 However, the absence of a robust, evidence-based risk prediction model limits clinicians’ ability to implement timely interventions to prevent or mitigate adverse outcomes associated with this condition.

Risk prediction models have emerged as valuable tools in obstetrics for identifying high-risk pregnancies and guiding clinical decision-making. By integrating multiple risk factors, such models enhance early diagnosis, optimise prenatal care and facilitate targeted interventions.10 In the context of PPROM, a well-calibrated and validated risk prediction model could aid healthcare providers in identifying women at increased risk, thereby improving maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

This study aims to develop and validate a risk prediction model for PPROM in North Wollo Zone governmental hospitals in Northern Ethiopia using relevant clinical and demographic predictors. The model will be constructed using a robust statistical approach and validated with an independent dataset to ensure its accuracy and reliability. By establishing a predictive tool tailored to clinical settings, this study aims to enhance risk stratification, facilitate early diagnosis and promote the implementation of preventive strategies for PPROM, ultimately contributing to improved perinatal health outcomes in this region.

Method

Study setting

This study was conducted in six public hospitals within the North Wollo Zone of the Amhara National Regional State, Ethiopia. The administrative centre of the zone, Woldya, is situated 521 km from Addis Ababa and 372 km from Bahir Dar. The hospitals included in the study were Woldya Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, as well as Kobo, Mersa, Kidus Lalibela, Meket Shedo and Wadila Primary Hospitals. These facilities operate 24/7, providing maternal and child health services as well as emergency care.

Currently, all six hospitals have both clinical and non-clinical departments, offering diagnostic, curative and rehabilitation services in both outpatient and inpatient settings. Additionally, they engage in disease prevention and health promotion activities. In terms of human resources, each hospital has three Integrated Emergency Surgical Officers, while Woldya Comprehensive Specialized Hospital is staffed with six senior obstetricians and gynaecologists. Kidus Lalibela Primary Hospital has one obstetrician and gynaecologist. The remaining healthcare staff, including midwives and other direct caregivers, are allocated based on national standards.

Study design and study period

This study employed a retrospective hospital-based cross-sectional design, conducted in public hospitals of the North Wollo Zone from 20 November 2023 to 20 March 2024. The primary aim was to determine the incidence of PPROM and to develop a validated risk prediction model for pregnant women admitted to obstetric wards. Data were collected through a review of maternal medical records and structured interviews conducted with mothers after delivery to identify potential predictors of PPROM.

Population

The study targeted all mothers who gave birth at six government hospitals in the North Wollo Zone regardless of time of delivery. The study population specifically included mothers who delivered at these hospitals between 20 November 2023 and 20 March 2024.

Eligibility

Inclusion criteria

Pregnant women admitted to North Wollo Zone governmental hospitals between 28 and 37 completed weeks of gestation were eligible. Gestational age was determined by a documented last menstrual period or an early ultrasound before 20 weeks. Participants included those able to provide informed consent and with complete clinical records containing all relevant predictor variables for developing and validating the PPROM risk prediction model. Cases were defined by the timing of membrane rupture, regardless of gestational age at delivery, so women who experienced PPROM before 37 weeks but delivered at or after 37 weeks were included.

Exclusion criteria

Pregnancies with known major fetal anomalies and cases with incomplete clinical records were excluded from the study to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the data used for developing and validating the PPROM risk prediction model.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

Sample size determination

The required sample size for model development was calculated based on the standard guideline of having at least 10 events per candidate predictor variable. The formula used was N=[n×10]/I. Where: N represents the sample size, which is the number of candidate predictor variables, and I is the estimated event rate in the population. In this study, 16 candidate predictor variables were considered, requiring a total of 160 events (16×10). Based on a previous study conducted at Debre Tabor Hospital, the prevalence of PPROM was reported as 13.67%.11 Using this prevalence, the final sample size was calculated as follows: N=[16×10×10]/13.67=1098. Thus, the required sample size for this study was 1098 participants.

Sampling techniques

A systematic random sampling method was used to select participants based on delivery reports from the past 6 months for each of the six hospitals. Study subjects were identified at the postnatal ward. A total of 1098 participants were selected and proportionally distributed across the six hospitals. The sample size for each hospital was allocated based on the last 6 months of admission reports as follows: Woldya Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (355 participants), Kobo Primary Hospital (175), Mersa Primary Hospital (90), Kidus Lalibela Primary Hospital (190), Meket Shedo Primary Hospital (153) and Wadila Primary Hospital (135). The quota system determined the sampling process, with quotas set using the case reports from each hospital over the previous 5 months. Mothers meeting the inclusion criteria at each hospital were interviewed according to predetermined K values, which were calculated as follows: Woldya Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (K=1126/355=3.17), Kobo Primary Hospital (K=556/175=3.17), Mersa Primary Hospital (K=284/90=3.15), Kidus Lalibela Primary Hospital (K=602/190=3.16), Meket Shedo Primary Hospital (K=486/153=3.17) and Wadila Primary Hospital (K=428/135=3.17). Data collection continued until the predetermined sample quotas for each hospital were met (figure 1).

Variables

Dependent variable

The outcome variable was PPROM.

Independent predictor variables

The predictor variables consisted of sociodemographic factors such as age and pregnancy-related characteristics, including antenatal care (ANC), multigravida, iron folate supplementation, multiple pregnancies, history of preterm rupture of membranes (PPROM), gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), pregnancy-induced hypertension (PIH), antepartum haemorrhage (APH) and short birth intervals. Additionally, medical conditions such as illnesses during pregnancy, urinary tract infections (UTIs), anaemia and self-medication practices were also included as predictors.

Pregnancy type classification and predictor variables

In this study, multiple gestation was defined as a twin pregnancy, as no cases of triplets or higher-order multiples were recorded. Given that twin pregnancies are associated with an increased risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, they were treated as an independent variable in the development of the PPROM risk prediction model. All predictor variables, including twin pregnancy, were selected based on prior literature and clinical relevance, and their associations with PPROM were assessed using multivariable logistic regression analysis.

Operational definitions and diagnostic standardisation

Operational definitions

Preterm premature rupture of membrane

PPROM is defined as the spontaneous rupture of the amniotic sac after fetal viability and before 37 completed weeks of gestation, before the onset of labour.12

Diagnostic standardisation

The diagnosis of PPROM and other obstetric and maternal complications at all participating hospitals was conducted according to the Ministry of Health (MOH) national guidelines. Diagnosis relied on a combination of patient history (eg, sudden gush or continuous leakage of fluid), sterile speculum examination confirming pooling of amniotic fluid and confirmatory bedside tests such as Nitrazine or fern tests. Key pregnancy characteristics, including maternal age, parity, hypertensive disorders and other relevant clinical conditions, were defined using standard national definitions and applied uniformly across all sites. As all hospitals follow the same MOH guidelines, diagnostic consistency and reliability were inherently maintained.

Data collection tool and procedure

Data were gathered using structured, paper-based questionnaires adapted from existing literature and modified to suit the local context and study objectives.1318 To ensure understanding, the questions were clearly explained to participants, with a particular emphasis on PPROM. Participants were asked whether they had experienced PPROM (Yes/No), and their responses were cross-checked by reviewing medical records. The data collection was carried out by a team of six midwives with diplomas, while six midwives with bachelor’s degrees supervised the process at the study sites. Eligible participants were interviewed, and their clinical records were examined until the required sample size for each hospital was reached. Data from mothers with normal vaginal deliveries were collected 1–2 hours post partum, while those with caesarean sections or complicated deliveries were interviewed once fully awake.

Data quality control

To ensure high-quality data collection, both data collectors and supervisors participated in a 1-day training session. This training covered the study objectives, methodology, ethical considerations, checklist content and proper data recording procedures. A pretest was conducted with 55 mothers (5% of the total sample) at Hayk Primary Hospital to evaluate response consistency, interview duration and question clarity. Based on the pretest findings, necessary modifications were made before finalising the questionnaire in Amharic. Participants were thoroughly informed about the study’s objectives and procedures. To maintain data accuracy, supervisors and the principal investigator reviewed the collected data daily for completeness. Additionally, a subset of records underwent double data entry, and any discrepancies were resolved. Epi-Data’s validation features, along with regular supervision, were employed to ensure data accuracy and integrity.

Data processing and analysis

After coding, verifying and cleaning the data, it was imported into Epi-Data V.4.6.0.6 and then exported to R V.4.2.2 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, means, SD and percentages, were used to summarise sociodemographic and other characteristics of the study population, with results presented in tables. Multicollinearity among independent (predictor) variables was assessed, ensuring that all variables had a variance inflation factor (VIF) of less than 2. To validate the model, calibration was performed 1000 times, and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was applied. Predictor variables for the final model were selected through a two-step process: first, using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression coefficients, followed by the Stepwise Akaike Information Criterion (StepAIC) method.

Method of statistical analysis

Model development, performance, calibration, validation, risk score development and decision curve analysis

Model development

The predictive model for PPROM was developed using the LASSO regression to identify the most relevant predictor variables while minimising overfitting. A design matrix for predictor variables was created, and the response variable was extracted for analysis.

To optimise model performance, cross-validation was conducted, which determined the optimal penalty parameter (λ min=0.0033) by minimising cross-validation error. Given the binary nature of the outcome variable, the model was trained using binomial logistic regression. The fitted model was then used to generate predicted probabilities, and the regression coefficients corresponding to the optimal lambda were extracted.

Full model development

Following LASSO selection, a logistic regression model was constructed using the identified predictor variables. The model was fitted using the generalised linear model function with a binomial family link function. To evaluate the strength of associations between predictor variables and PPROM, ORs and 95% CIs were calculated. To enhance model efficiency, stepwise selection was conducted using the AIC. This iterative process eliminated non-significant variables while preserving the most predictive factors, leading to a streamlined logistic regression model. ORs and CIs were calculated for the final model, identifying key predictors associated with PPROM.

Model performance evaluation

The model performance was assessed using both discrimination and calibration metrics.

Discrimination

Discrimination refers to the model’s ability to differentiate between outcomes. Predicted probabilities were generated using the logistic regression model, and its discriminative ability was evaluated through a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated along with its CIs to quantify the model’s ability to distinguish between cases and non-cases.

Calibraation

Calibration measures how well the predicted probabilities align with actual outcomes. A calibration plot was generated to compare predicted risks with observed outcomes using the Calibration Curves package. The model’s predicted probabilities were assessed against the observed risk of PPROM, ensuring an accurate representation of event likelihood. The calibration plot visually demonstrated the alignment between predicted and actual outcomes. The predicted risks were processed using the fitted model, and the plot Calibration function was used to generate the calibration curve, illustrating the relationship between predicted and observed risks.

Model validation

To assess the model’s robustness and generalisability, internal validation was conducted using bootstrap resampling. The boot package was used to perform 1000 bootstrap iterations, generating AUC values for each resample. The average AUC from these bootstrap samples was used to estimate the model’s true performance. The model’s apparent performance was compared with the true performance derived from the bootstrap samples to calculate the overoptimism coefficient, which quantifies the extent to which the model’s performance may be overestimated due to overfitting. In this study, the apparent AUC was 0.873, while the mean AUC from bootstrap samples was 0.874, resulting in an overoptimism coefficient of 0.0052.

Risk score development

A logistic regression model was used to develop a risk score for predicting PPROM, incorporating key predictor variables. Significant risk factors were assigned weights based on their contributions in the multivariable model. To enhance clinical applicability, regression coefficients were standardised by dividing each coefficient by the smallest significant one and rounding to the nearest integer. The total risk score for each individual was calculated by summing the assigned points for all significant predictors. For ease of use, the predicted risk of PPROM was simplified into a binary classification system, categorising pregnant women as either high-risk or low-risk. To evaluate the performance of the risk score, key diagnostic measures—including sensitivity, specificity, positive (PPV) and negative predictive values (NPVs), and likelihood ratios—were calculated at a cut-off threshold of 6.5. These metrics helped determine the optimal threshold for identifying women at high risk of PPROM.

Model with the risk score

Predictive performance of the risk score for PPROM

A logistic regression model was developed to evaluate the predictive value of the risk score for PPROM. The model revealed a significant association between the risk score and the occurrence of PPROM. To assess the model’s discriminatory ability, an ROC curve was used, which yielded an AUC of 0.861 (figure 2). This indicates strong predictive performance, demonstrating that the risk score effectively distinguishes between individuals with and without PPROM.

Decision curve analysis

To evaluate the clinical and public health impact of the model, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed, comparing threshold probabilities (ranging from 0 to 1) against standardised net benefit. The model was assessed against three scenarios: ‘intervention based on score chart’, ‘intervention for all’ and ‘no intervention’. In this study, the intervention focused on enhancing critical care services, including prophylaxis and therapeutic antibiotics, for high-risk pregnant women at risk for PPROM.

Pregnancy, obstetrics and other clinical predictor characteristics of study participants

Most women in the study were multigravida (75.4%), and nearly half reported unplanned pregnancies (44.4%). ANC attendance was relatively common (65.8%), and two-thirds received iron supplementation. A history of medical illness (18.0%) and self-medication during pregnancy (19.8%) was also reported. Regarding obstetric history, short birth intervals (<2 years) were relatively uncommon (6.8%), while 11.0% had a previous history of premature rupture of membranes (PROM). Pregnancy-related complications included anaemia (17.5%), PIH (10.7%), APH (9.6%), oligohydramnios (13.2%) and UTIs (9.0%). Less frequent but notable conditions included GDM (3.7%) and polyhydramnios (5.8%).

A total of 75 participants (6.8%) had twin pregnancies. Compared with singleton pregnancies, these cases showed higher rates of maternal complications, particularly hypertensive disorders and APH. PROMs occurred more frequently among twin pregnancies, often before 34 weeks of gestation. Neonatal outcomes were also less favourable, with a greater proportion of low birthweight infants and increased admissions to the neonatal intensive care unit. Owing to these distinct risks, multiple gestation was treated as an independent predictor in the risk model (table 2).

Development of a prediction model for PPROM

Out of 1098 deliveries, the cumulative incidence of PPROM was 118 cases, representing 10.75% (95% CI 9.01% to 12.77%).

Significantly associated predictors to PPROM

Model development

16 candidate variables, including sociodemographic factors, pregnancy characteristics, obstetric complications and other medical and clinical predictors, were considered potential predictors for PPROM among pregnant women in the North Wollo Zone. To develop a predictive model for PPROM, we first used the LASSO regression to identify the most relevant predictors and then applied logistic regression to build the final predictive model.

Variable selection using LASSO regression and StepAIC

LASSO regression was used to identify the most significant predictors of PPROM while minimising overfitting. The response variable was PPROM, and the predictor variables included maternal age, ANC, iron and folate supplementation, presence of medical conditions, self-medication practice, GDM, anaemia, multigravida, polyhydramnios, APH, UTI, oligohydramnios, PIH, multiple pregnancies, history of PROM and short birth interval.

The optimal lambda (λ) value, determined through cross-validation, was 0.0033, which minimised the mean cross-validation error. The LASSO model selected the following significant predictors: maternal age, UTI, anaemia, ANC visits, self-medication, history of PROM, PIH, medical illnesses, APH, GDM, short birth interval, oligohydramnios, polyhydramnios and multiple pregnancies. After LASSO variable selection, a full logistic regression model was built using the significant predictors. The model revealed a strong association between PPROM and several maternal and clinical predictors. Multicollinearity was assessed using the VIF, and since all VIF values were below 2, no predictors were removed. To refine the model further, stepwise backward elimination was applied using AIC selection, resulting in a reduced logistic regression model that included the following seven final predictors: maternal age, UTI, anaemia, history of PPROM, PIH, short birth interval and polyhydramnios.

The initial null model had a deviance of 749.3. However, after incorporating the key predictors—maternal age, UTI, anaemia, history of PROM, PIH, short birth interval and polyhydramnios—the model fit improved significantly, with a reduced residual deviance of 477.83 and a lower AIC of 493.83, indicating better model performance. Model performance, calibration and validation

The predictive performance of the logistic regression model for PPROM was assessed using internal validation with bootstrapping (1000 resamples). The apparent AUC of the model was 0.873, indicating strong discriminative ability. Following bootstrapping, the bias-corrected AUC was estimated at 0.874 (87.4%), suggesting minimal overestimation in the model’s predictive performance (figure 3A). The overoptimism coefficient was 0.0052, which is well below the commonly accepted threshold of 0.1, indicating a low risk of model overfitting. These results demonstrate that the model maintains high predictive accuracy after correction for potential overfitting, supporting its robustness for predicting PPROM (figure 3B).

Predictive performance of the risk score for PPROM

The predicted probabilities from the logistic regression model showed an increasing trend in PPROM occurrence with higher risk scores. Specifically, the proportion of PPROM cases increased from 0.014 (risk score=12) to 1.0 (risk score ≥20), indicating a strong correlation between higher risk scores and PPROM incidence. These findings suggest that the risk score could serve as a valuable screening tool for identifying pregnant individuals at a higher risk of developing PPROM, enabling targeted interventions and timely obstetric care.

Density plot for model performance in differentiating PPROM and non-PPROM cases

The density plot illustrates the ability of the developed risk score model to distinguish between PPROM and non-PPROM cases. According to the model, 10.75% of pregnant women were classified as ‘1’ (PPROM), while 89.25% were categorised as ‘0’ (non-PPROM). Although some overlap exists between the two groups, the model effectively differentiates PPROM from non-PPROM cases. To assess model calibration, we analysed the density distribution of predicted probabilities by PPROM status (figure 4A). The model demonstrated distinct probability distributions, with non-PPROM cases clustering at lower probabilities and PPROM cases spanning a broader range. Despite some overlap, which may lead to misclassification in borderline cases, the model achieved strong discrimination (AUC=0.861). These findings suggest that incorporating additional clinical or demographic factors could enhance predictive accuracy. Overall, the risk score-based model shows promise for identifying individuals at higher risk of PPROM, supporting its use in clinical screening and risk stratification. Further optimisation of cut-off values and external validation are recommended to improve model robustness.

Simplified risk score development

To enhance its practical applicability, a simplified risk score was derived from the final reduced model based on significant β coefficients. Each significant β coefficient was standardised by dividing it by the smallest significant β coefficient and then rounded to the nearest integer to create the simplified prediction score. The resulting risk scores for predicting the likelihood of developing PPROM among pregnant women range from a minimum of 3 to a maximum of 13 (table 3). As illustrated in figure 4B, the AUC for the simplified risk score is 0.815, indicating good predictive performance.

Risk classification of PPROM among pregnant women using simplified risk score

Based on the Youden Index, the optimal cut-off point for the Simplified risk score was determined to be ≥6.5. At this threshold, the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, positive likelihood ratio and negative likelihood ratio were 0.60, 0.801, 0.415, 0.2836, 0.328 and 1.167, respectively. The PPV represents the proportion of individuals correctly predicted to develop PPROM out of all those who received a positive prediction (including both those who will and will not actually develop PPROM). In this study, the PPV for predicting future PPROM was 41.5%, indicating that 41.5% of pregnant women with a positive prediction would indeed develop PPROM (table 4). According to this model, pregnant women can have a risk score ranging from 3 to 13. The incidence of PPROM among pregnant women in the low-risk group (score <6.5) and high-risk group (score ≥6.5) was 31.6% and 68.4%, respectively (table 4).

Nomogram-based risk prediction model for PPROM

A nomogram was developed to provide an individualised risk assessment for PPROM (figure 6). The model integrates seven key maternal and obstetric factors: maternal age, history of PROM, anaemia, UTI, PIH, oligohydramnios and polyhydramnios. Each predictor is assigned a specific point value, and the total score corresponds to the estimated probability of PPROM occurrence. Among these factors, a history of PROM, PIH, oligohydramnios and UTI was identified as the strongest contributors to PPROM risk. Additionally, adolescent mothers exhibited a higher likelihood of developing PPROM compared with adult women. The nomogram demonstrated strong discriminatory ability, reinforcing its utility as a predictive tool for identifying high-risk pregnancies. Overall, this risk score-based nomogram serves as a valuable clinical tool for early risk identification, targeted preventive interventions and informed decision-making in obstetric care. To enhance its generalisability and reliability, further external validation in diverse populations is recommended.

Discussion

PPROM is a critical obstetric complication that significantly contributes to preterm birth and its associated maternal and neonatal morbidities. In this study, the cumulative incidence of PPROM was 10.75% (95% CI 9.01% to 12.77%), which is consistent with findings from similar settings.1721 This underscores the substantial burden of PPROM, particularly in resource-limited regions such as the North Wollo Zone, Northern Ethiopia. Given its considerable impact on maternal and neonatal health, early identification of women at risk is essential for improving clinical management and outcomes. To address this need, the development and validation of a risk prediction model for PPROM offers a valuable tool for healthcare providers. By facilitating early risk assessment, the model supports timely interventions and optimised care strategies, ultimately improving maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

This study aimed to develop a risk prediction score for PPROM based on sociodemographic and clinical predictors. The selected predictors were chosen for their clinical relevance and accessibility, particularly in resource-limited settings such as Ethiopia and other African countries, where advanced laboratory testing may not be financially feasible. The key maternal factors incorporated into the model included maternal age, PIH, anaemia, UTI, history of PROM and amniotic fluid abnormalities (polyhydramnios and oligohydramnios).

The developed risk score demonstrated moderate to good discrimination, with an AUC of 0.87 (95% CI 0.833 to 0.912), indicating promising internal validation results. While the model shows acceptable performance, its sensitivity (60%) and PPV (41%) highlight some limitations, and external validation in independent populations is essential before broader application. Importantly, the risk score and nomogram are intended for use by obstetricians, midwives and other ANC providers in hospitals and health centres. Applied during routine visits, the tool can help identify women at increased risk of PPROM, enabling closer monitoring, timely infection screening, preventive interventions such as progesterone therapy and referral when necessary. By facilitating early identification of high-risk pregnancies, this approach may support informed clinical decision-making and improve maternal and neonatal outcomes, particularly in resource-limited settings.

In many low-income and middle-income countries, reliable methods for predicting PPROM remain scarce. Traditionally, research has focused on understanding the incidence and determinants of PPROM. However, recent advancements have shifted towards the development of predictive models that integrate multiple maternal characteristics to improve risk assessment. This study contributes to that evolving field by offering a practical and effective tool to identify high-risk pregnancies, thereby supporting better clinical decision-making and management strategies.

This study evaluated a predictive model based entirely on maternal characteristics during pregnancy, eliminating the need for advanced laboratory or imaging tests. By applying the Youden Index to determine the optimal cut-off point, the model achieved a sensitivity of 60% and a specificity of 80% at a score threshold of 6.5. The model incorporated a combination of demographic, obstetric and pregnancy-related factors, including maternal age, anaemia, UTI, PIH, history of PROM and amniotic fluid abnormalities (oligohydramnios and polyhydramnios). With an AUC of 0.873 (95% CI 0.833 to 0.912), the model demonstrated moderate to good discrimination predictive accuracy based on standard diagnostic classification criteria.

The prediction model showed moderate to good discriminatory power (AUC=0.87) and a sensitivity of 60%, suggesting reasonable ability to identify true cases of PPROM. The PPV was 41.5%, meaning that approximately four out of ten individuals identified as high-risk actually developed PPROM. However, it is important to recognise that PPV is highly dependent on the prevalence of the outcome in the study population. In our study, the prevalence of PPROM was 10.75%, which directly influenced the PPV. In settings with lower or higher prevalence rates, the PPV may vary substantially, even if the sensitivity and specificity remain constant. Therefore, the reported PPV should be interpreted with caution and may not be generalisable across different clinical or geographic settings without adjustment.

We found that a history of PROM is a strong predictor of current PPROM. Similar evidence was found in different studies.2125 Women with a prior history of PPROM may have persistent structural weaknesses in the fetal membranes due to inadequate collagen remodelling, oxidative stress or genetic predisposition, making them more susceptible to future rupture.26 Additionally, undiagnosed infections, such as bacterial vaginosis or intra-amniotic infections, may lead to chronic inflammation and enzymatic degradation of membrane integrity, further elevating the risk. Cervical insufficiency or uterine anomalies may also play a role by reducing the cervix’s ability to sustain pregnancy to term, leading to premature membrane rupture.27 Studies consistently show that women with a history of PPROM have a significantly higher risk of recurrence, emphasising the need for vigilant antenatal monitoring and preventive measures. Key management strategies include enhanced prenatal surveillance through serial cervical length assessments and infection screening. Progesterone therapy may help extend pregnancy in high-risk cases, while cervical cerclage is an option for cervical insufficiency. Lifestyle modifications, such as avoiding strenuous activities and early infection detection, are also crucial. Given the strong association between prior PPROM and recurrence, proactive interventions are vital for improving maternal and neonatal outcomes.

In our study, anaemia during pregnancy was a strong predictor of PPROM. Similar studies demonstrated that anaemia is a subsequent predictor of PPROM.2830 Anaemia during pregnancy is a strong predictor of PPROM, primarily due to its effects on maternal and fetal health. The association can be explained by several physiological mechanisms. Anaemia impairs oxygen delivery, weakening fetal membranes through reduced collagen synthesis and increased oxidative stress. It also compromises immune function, increasing susceptibility to infections like bacterial vaginosis, which are known risk factors for PPROM.31 Additionally, anaemia triggers inflammation and oxidative stress, leading to enzymatic degradation of membrane integrity. Nutritional deficiencies often linked to anaemia, such as low folate and vitamin B12 levels, further contribute to membrane fragility.32 Evidence from various studies supports this association, emphasising the need for early anaemia detection and management through iron supplementation, improved nutrition and infection screening to mitigate the risk of PPROM.

The other predictor of PPROM in our study was age <20 years. Our finding was supported by different studies,16 19 33 which have consistently shown that adolescents are at higher risk for PPROM. One potential explanation for this association is that younger women may have physiological immaturity of the cervix and uterus, which increases their vulnerability to pregnancy complications like PPROM.34 Additionally, socioeconomic factors such as limited access to prenatal care, poor nutritional status and higher rates of infections in adolescent pregnancies may also contribute to the higher likelihood of PPROM in this age group. Furthermore, hormonal imbalances and the underdeveloped body’s response to pregnancy may increase the risk of PROM in adolescents.13

Our study identified abnormalities in amniotic fluid volume, particularly polyhydramnios, as strong predictors of PPROM. Polyhydramnios, characterised by excessive amniotic fluid, significantly increases the risk of membrane rupture, consistent with previous research.3538 This association is driven by multiple physiological mechanisms, including uterine overdistension, which exerts excessive pressure on the fetal membranes, making them more susceptible to rupture. Additionally, polyhydramnios is often associated with conditions such as fetal anomalies, gestational diabetes and maternal infections, further elevating the risk of PPROM. The increased fluid volume may also affect uterine contractility and contribute to cervical shortening, compounding the likelihood of rupture. Recognising polyhydramnios as a key risk factor is essential for the effective prediction and management of PPROM.

Our study also found that a short birth interval was a significant predictor of PPROM. The association between short birth interval and PPROM can be attributed to several physiological and maternal health factors.20 39 40 Insufficient time between pregnancies may lead to incomplete uterine recovery, impairing endometrial regeneration and cervical integrity, which are crucial for maintaining membrane stability.22 Additionally, closely spaced pregnancies can result in maternal nutritional depletion, particularly deficiencies in folate and other essential micronutrients, which are vital for fetal membrane strength. Repeated pregnancies within a short period may also contribute to chronic inflammation and increased uterine strain, further predisposing the membranes to early rupture.39 These findings highlight the importance of optimising interpregnancy intervals to reduce the risk of PPROM and improve maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Our study identifies UTI as a significant predictor of PPROM, a finding consistent with evidence from multiple studies conducted in similar settings.13 41 42 UTI is a well-documented risk factor for adverse pregnancy outcomes, including PPROM. The underlying mechanism may be attributed to the inflammatory response triggered by bacterial infections, which can lead to the release of proinflammatory cytokines, prostaglandins and matrix metalloproteinases.43 These biochemical mediators contribute to the weakening of fetal membranes, increasing their susceptibility to premature rupture. Additionally, untreated or recurrent UTIs may result in ascending infections, further compromising membrane integrity. Given the high burden of UTIs among pregnant women, particularly in resource-limited settings, routine screening and timely treatment of UTIs could be a crucial strategy in preventing PPROM and its associated complications.44

PIH is a significant predictor of PPROM in our study, a finding that is supported by multiple studies.17 38 45 The underlying pathophysiological mechanisms may include endothelial dysfunction, impaired placental perfusion and systemic inflammation, all of which contribute to weakened fetal membranes.45 Additionally, PIH is often associated with oxidative stress and increased protease activity, which can degrade collagen and compromise membrane integrity, ultimately leading to PPROM. Furthermore, the potential use of antihypertensive medications or iatrogenic interventions in PIH management may also influence the risk of PPROM.45

These findings underscore the importance of prioritising close antenatal monitoring, infection screening, nutritional support and preventive strategies such as progesterone therapy and lifestyle modifications for women at high risk of PPROM. The developed risk prediction model offers a potentially valuable tool to strengthen PPROM management, particularly in resource-limited healthcare settings where early identification of at-risk pregnancies is crucial. While the model demonstrated moderate to good discrimination with encouraging internal validation results, external validation across diverse populations remains essential to establish its robustness and ensure its applicability in routine clinical practice.

Strength and limitation of the study

A major strength of this study lies in the inclusion of a sufficient number of participants who experienced the outcome, enabling the development of a robust and internally validated risk prediction model. The model is based on readily accessible sociodemographic, obstetric and pregnancy-related characteristics, enhancing its feasibility and practical use in primary healthcare settings. To ensure data quality and reduce the risk of missing data during primary data collection, comprehensive training was provided to data collectors and supervisors, and a pilot test was conducted in nearby hospitals to assess and refine the data collection tools.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective cross-sectional design limits the ability to fully establish temporal relationships between predictor variables and the occurrence of PPROM. This design also constrains causal inference and may reduce the generalisability of the prediction model to other populations or healthcare settings. Second, differences in the level of healthcare services among the study hospitals, particularly between Woldya Comprehensive Specialized Hospital and the five primary hospitals, may have introduced variability in the accuracy and consistency of clinical diagnoses. Finally, reliance on maternal medical records as a primary data source resulted in the exclusion of some potentially important variables that were not routinely documented.

Conclusions

PPROM is a significant obstetric complication associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes. This study identified key risk factors contributing to PPROM and developed a predictive model with high accuracy (AUC=0.87) for early risk identification. The model includes seven critical predictors: maternal age, history of PROM, anaemia, UTI, PIH, oligohydramnios and polyhydramnios. These factors were integrated into a simplified risk score and a nomogram to enhance clinical applicability in resource-limited settings. The findings highlight the importance of early screening and risk stratification to facilitate timely interventions, ultimately improving maternal and neonatal health outcomes. Given the substantial burden of PPROM in the study region, implementing evidence-based risk assessment strategies is crucial for optimising obstetric care and reducing complications associated with PPROM.

Recommendation

To improve the early identification and management of high-risk pregnancies, the developed risk score and nomogram should be incorporated into routine ANC services. Special emphasis should be placed on expanding ANC accessibility, particularly in rural and underserved areas, given its protective effect against PPROM. Preventive efforts should prioritise the early detection and appropriate management of key risk factors, including a history of PROM, maternal anaemia, UTIs, short interpregnancy intervals, adolescent pregnancies, PIH and polyhydramnios. Health education interventions are also vital to raise community awareness regarding the importance of prenatal care, nutrition and personal hygiene in reducing modifiable risk factors. Although the model demonstrated moderate to good discriminatory ability (AUC=0.87) based on internal validation, the sensitivity (60%) and (PPV=41%) were modest. As such, external validation in diverse settings is essential to confirm its predictive accuracy and generalisability before wider implementation.

Finally, policy-makers should consider integrating PPROM risk assessment into national maternal health strategies, accompanied by the allocation of appropriate resources to support prevention, early diagnosis and effective management of high-risk pregnancies.
