Placental Mesenchymal Dysplasia: How to Approach the Diagnosis as Early as the First Trimester? A Case Report and a Review
Authors: Margheret Smalaj, Sara Törnblom Paulander, Aude Tessier, Frédéric Buxant, Marie Cassart, Kalina Gajewska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlacental mesenchymal dysplasia (PMD) has to be evoked as a diagnosis when an unusual cystic appearing placenta is noticed on prenatal scans. The pathway to diagnosis is challenging since it includes trophoblastic disease with a concrete neoplastic risk for the mother and PMD with a high rate of associated feto‐maternal complications.

Introduction

Placental mesenchymal dysplasia (PMD) is a rare and benign antenatal disorder affecting the placenta in 0.02%–0.2% of pregnancies [1, 2, 3, 4] and is predominantly seen in female fetuses with a male to female ratio of 1:3.6–8 [1, 5].

PMD exhibits a wide range of expression with its micro‐ and macroanatomic placental features such as placentomegaly, dilated chorionic vessels with an increased risk of thrombosis, and grape‐like vesicles within the placental tissue. It may be isolated or part of Beckwith–Wiedemann Syndrome (BWS) [2, 6].

Initially based on a sonographic evaluation, the differential diagnoses include partial hydatidiform molar pregnancy, complete mole and coexisting fetus, spontaneous miscarriage with hydropic changes, and placental mosaicism [7].

Diagnosing PMD is challenging, as its ultrasonographic features often mimic those of a hydatidiform mole, leading to frequent misdiagnosis [2, 7, 8].

Due to limited awareness and familiarity with this clinical entity among healthcare professionals, PMD is underdiagnosed and underreported [2, 4, 8].

The clinical implications and management differ greatly as PMD is not associated with a risk of neoplastic transformation [6] whereas a molar pregnancy carries such a risk, and unnecessary termination might be proposed. Additionally, only approximately 9% of reported cases of PMD result in an uncomplicated pregnancy [2]. The vast majority are linked to a spectrum of fetomaternal complications, including intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), macrosomia associated with BWS, fetal hamartomas, preterm labor, intrauterine fetal demise (IUFD), and preeclampsia [1, 3, 4, 7].

The definitive diagnosis is only confirmed by gross and microscopic pathologic examination [3, 7].

Detecting the disease to be able to correctly inform the patients on this rare disorder and to provide the best management is of utmost importance.

We report a case of PMD resulting in the birth of a healthy female baby with a review of the literature.

Case History

A 35‐year‐old woman, gravida 2 and para 1, was referred to the Maternal Foetal Medicine (MFM) Unit of our hospital at 9 weeks and 6 days of gestational age (GA) to start the follow‐up of this second spontaneous pregnancy. Her first pregnancy was terminated at 25 weeks of GA because of a fetal hypoplastic left heart syndrome diagnosis. A comparative genomic hybridization‐array (CGH) and exome sequencing in trio with the parents were normal. The patient was in good health, except for Hashimoto thyroiditis, treated with a low dose of l‐thyroxine.

The partner was also in good health. There is no family history of molar pregnancy or cardiac malformations. This was the couple's second pregnancy together, and both are first‐time parents.

No other remarkable personal and/or family past history is to be reported.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

The first ultrasound of her second pregnancy performed at 9 weeks and 6 days showed an embryo measuring 36 mm with cardiac activity and two ventricular flows in power Doppler, but the placenta exhibited cystic, grape‐like features (Figure 1). On the same day, human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) serum levels were obtained at 221,944 IU/L, above the median value of 74,655 IU/L expected for this GA [9].

(B) Ultrasound performed at 34+4 weeks of GA, additional thrombosis zone in one of the dilated vessels of the chorionic plate with a central location, proximal to umbilical cord insertion.

The first trimester ultrasound at 11 weeks and 6 days showed an anterior, thickened, and heterogeneous placenta with molar‐like features, an embryo with a normal nuchal translucency and no anatomical anomaly, a normal amniotic fluid quantity, and a crown‐rump length correlated to the gestational age. No signs of an evanescent twin were visible, and the patient's ovaries appeared normal.

The maternal serum alpha‐fetoprotein (MSAFP) at 12 weeks and 5 days was at 179.6 mcg/L, the median value at 12 weeks of GA being 18.75 mcg/L [10].

After multidisciplinary discussion, it was decided to carry out, with the couple's information and consent, a transabdominal chorion villous sampling (CVS) at 13 weeks and 3 days.

No triploidy was found. Following a multidisciplinary discussion, literature review, and a consultation with the couple, it was decided to retain PMD as the leading diagnosis in the differential. Consequently, a regimen of low‐dose aspirin was initiated because of the high risk of early preeclampsia associated with PMD [2]. Amniocentesis was carried out at 19 weeks and 4 days at parental demand due to the strong association with BWS [2].

With a sensitivity of 80%, this test involved a methylation‐sensitive restriction digestion combined with quantitative PCR. Maternal contamination was excluded. The test did not reveal any methylation abnormalities in the H19/IGF2:IG‐DMR (ICR1) and KCNQ10T1:TSS‐DMR (ICR2) domains at 11p15.5.

hCG levels were monitored throughout the pregnancy, with the lowest recorded value being 40.148 IU/L during the third trimester, above the median value of 13.852 IU/L [10].

A fetal anatomy scan at 20 weeks of GA was normal. Subsequently, the patient had a pathological oral glucose tolerance test at 26 weeks of GA and was diagnosed with gestational diabetes, which was kept under control with a diet throughout the pregnancy.

Fetal growth and well‐being were followed closely every 2 weeks before 28 weeks of GA and on a weekly basis thereafter. The growth was at about the 30th percentile (Intergrowth 21st curves) during the second trimester, and the placenta maintained a thickened, hypo‐echoic, multicystic, and heterogeneous aspect. At 27 weeks of GA (Figure 1A), an apical cystic zone evoking a thrombotic formation of 30 × 22 × 20 mm was detected on US. An additional investigation by MRI performed at 30 weeks showed a fundal placenta with dysplastic lesions measuring 5 × 9 × 3.9 cm consistent with the diagnosis of PMD (Figure 2). No fetal hepatic lesions were detected on MRI. From 32 weeks, the MFM team noted a slight decrease in fetal growth (11th percentile), and a few episodes of decreased subjective fetal movements were indicated by the mother with satisfactory ultrasound evaluation and monitoring. At 34 weeks and 4 days of GA (Figure 1B), an additional thrombosis zone was observed in one of the dilated vessels of the chorionic plate with a central location, proximal to umbilical cord insertion.

Axial (a) and sagittal (b) TSE T2 weighted MRI scans of the placenta performed at 30 weeks showing (arrows) the dysplastic cystic part corresponding to the PMD lesion. The rest of the placenta appeared normal.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The team decided to induce the labor at 35 weeks and 2 days by vaginal prostin E2 tablets. Labour was uncomplicated and a healthy girl of 2160 g and 46 cm, Apgar score 8‐9‐10, was born the day after. The placenta was delivered spontaneously.

The female infant was admitted to the neonatal unit due to induced prematurity.

The adaptation to extrauterine life went smoothly. A transitory hypoglycemia, probably due to the mother's diabetes and infant's prematurity, was noted as well as a neonatal jaundice requiring phototherapy. No anemia and no thrombocytopenia were detected.

The clinical examination of the newborn by the neonatologist who followed the case was reassuring and no signs suggestive of BWS were found.

Placental examination showed an enlarged and thickened placenta (weight 530 g, 90th percentile) [11]. Near cord insertion, vessels were dilated and tortuous with a varicose‐like aspect and abnormal branching (Figure 3, left). Fibrinoid depositions were found on one third of the maternal surface. On the cut sections, organized thrombi were seen in the dilated vessels, confirmed at histological examination. The walls of these vessels were thick. Microscopic examination found enlarged villi with cisterns, hypercellularity, and stromal fibrosis (Figure 3, right). No trophoblastic proliferation nor stromal trophoblastic inclusions were found. Additionally, only a limited portion of the placenta did not exhibit any histological abnormality, showing terminal villi with a normal appearance for the gestational age (Figure 4).

Right: Microscopical portion, 10× magnified, Hematoxylin and Eosin stain showing enlarged villi with cisterns with hypercellularity and stromal fibrosis.

Additionally, the parents declined testing on other tissues and additional testing on placenta after birth. CDKN1C sequencing was not performed either.

Discussion

Placental mesenchymal disorder is an uncommon vascular anomaly affecting the placenta and its development [2, 7, 8]. PMD is mainly detected after 13 weeks of GA when the placenta appears multicystic [1, 2, 7]. Its etiology and pathogenesis are yet to be fully characterized [8].

Due to the low incidence of this disease as well as the lack of sizable studies preventing issuing guidelines, diagnosis, management, and follow‐up might prove to be challenging for the medical team and the parents.

Here, we present a case where PMD was considered as a diagnosis since the earliest stages of the pregnancy, at only 9 weeks and 6 days, resulting in prompt management and eventually leading to the delivery of a healthy female baby.

The decision to consider PMD as the leading diagnosis was made by an integration of clinical, biological, and genetic findings, in close consultation with the couple. The possibility of a definitive diagnosis of a twin pregnancy with complete mole and a coexisting fetus was explained to the couple.

When morphological placental anomaly is identified on ultrasound during the first trimester, the differential diagnosis must include partial molar pregnancy, complete mole with a coexisting normal fetus, and PMD.

Pathology

Pathological examination of the placenta confirmed the PMD diagnosis. Furthermore, the absence of trophoblastic proliferation or hyperplasia allows us to exclude complete hydatiform mole [8].

Since the diagnosis of PMD can only be confirmed by the pathologist after birth, an early antenatal rigorous diagnostic approach should be adopted by the multidisciplinary medical team.

In our case, the couple was counseled on the risk of a definitive diagnosis of complete molar pregnancy on pathological examination, along with the associated risk of neoplasia. Nevertheless, in light of their obstetric history, the couple expressed their desire to continue the pregnancy.

Genetic Testing

A complete mole results from an androgenetic diploid conception, when an ovum is either fertilized by one duplicated sperm or, more rarely, by two sperms and has lost its maternal DNA. The karyotype is normal (46, XX or 46, XY). Homozygosity from a duplicated sperm can be detected via fluorescent multiplex PCR, but diagnosis is harder when two sperms are involved [12].

A partial mole arises from a diandric triploid conception—an ovum fertilized by two sperms or one duplicated sperm—resulting in a triploid karyotype (69, XXX; 69, XXY; or 69, XYY). Fluorescent multiplex PCR can detect triploidy but not its origin (diandric vs. digynic). Molecular genotyping can clarify this, but it requires both parental DNA and is not routinely available [12].

In a review of 66 cases by Cohen et al., most (32/36) showed no chromosomal abnormalities. Detected anomalies included triploidy, trisomy 13, and Klinefelter syndrome—the latter possibly incidental. BWS was suspected or confirmed in 23% of cases [13].

In our case, partial mole was excluded by normal karyotype. BWS is a multisystem genomic imprinting disorder characterized by mosaic genetic and epigenetic defects within the 11p15.5 region, which contains genes such as CDKN1C and Insulinlike Growth Factor 2 (IGF2), regulators of fetal growth [14]. Fetal clinical expression of BWS may be wide, with macrosomia, macroglossia, exomphalos, and organomegaly as main suggestive features, if present [14]. The genetic modifications found in PMD and BWS might be part of a spectrum, confined to the placenta in PMD, confined to the fetus in BWS, or both in cases with co‐concurrence [14]. In PMD, the underlying etiology may involve androgenetic biparental mosaicism, characterized by the presence of two cell lines. The androgenetic cell line is hypothesized to arise from endoreduplication of the paternal genome, leading to paternal uniparental disomy in a subset of cells, whereas the biparental cell line originates from the contribution of both maternal and paternal genomes. This could account for the observed female predominance in PMD cases, as an androgenetic 46, YY cell line would be non‐viable [15].

In BWS, the genetic hypothesis concerns the molecular disruption of the imprinting genes of chromosome 11p15.5 with, in some cases, a lack of expression of the maternal allele [15, 16]. Lastly, the observed placental overgrowth in BWS might be caused by an up‐regulation of the paternally expressed IGF2 [16].

While CVS is primarily performed for a karyotype analysis, it could also be used to obtain larger villi samples for a histological examination. A well‐known marker, p57, is a cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitor, derived from the maternally expressed and paternally imprinted CDKN1C gene located on chromosome 11p15.5 [17].

Due to the lack of contribution of maternal DNA in complete moles, the p57 expression is absent in villous stromal cells and cytotrophoblast, and this characteristic may be exploited in an immunohistochemistry analysis to differentiate between PMD and complete mole [12, 17]. This technique would not be applicable in some cases of PMD co‐occurring with BWS, and to our current knowledge, there are no immunostaining studies for IGF2, which could also be a useful tool to explore [16]. Although current diagnostic techniques do not differentiate between complete hydatidiform mole and PMD in the absence of BWS, the combined use of p57 immunohistochemistry and DNA genotyping may provide valuable adjunctive information to guide clinical decision‐making. This is not performed as a matter of routine but could be an extra option in some complicated cases.

Regarding the diagnosis of co‐occurring BWS, although molecular testing can be antenatally performed, a negative result should not provide false reassurance due to the disease's mosaicism. Postnatal testing may be necessary in certain cases when there's a clinical suspicion [18].

Clinical Presentation, Biological Data and Imaging

A molar pregnancy is more often associated with vaginal bleeding (89%–97%), ovarian enlargement due to hCG stimulation (20%–46%), hyperemesis (20%–26%), and hyperthyroidism (2%) [4]. None of these clinical features were present in our case.

Along with the early invasive testing, MSAFP dosage performed right after the first trimester ultrasound had a valuable role for the PMD diagnosis. MSAFP levels have been shown to be elevated in PMD. It is hypothesized that the enlarged placental volume resulting in a substantial surface area combined with thin‐walled arteries enables an increased transfer of AFP into the maternal circulation, while HCG levels can be normal to slightly elevated for GA [13].

In the systematic review by Nayeri et al., PMD was more frequently associated with elevated MSAFP levels, whereas increased hCG levels were reported in only 38% of cases [2]. In contrast, hCG levels are consistently elevated in complete molar pregnancies, while MSAFP is lower [19].

In our case, the elevated MSAFP served as an additional biological marker supporting the diagnosis of PMD. Additionally, hCG levels were monitored throughout the pregnancy and remained above the expected median range. This elevation is consistent with previous findings suggesting that hCG levels can be influenced by increased placental weight, a characteristic feature of PMD [19].

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is an additional diagnostic tool allowing a thorough and wider placental evaluation as well as obtaining a more detailed fetal view, thanks to its relative independence from the patient's body mass index (BMI) and high contrast resolution [20]. MRI findings of PMD include an enlarged placenta with dilated vessels as well as heterogeneous signals [20, 21]. Hemorrhage within the cystic part of the placenta might be associated with a molar pregnancy, although it is not a specific sign [21]. In our case, a large cystic area in the apical zone of the placenta was noted, supporting a PMD diagnosis.

Maternal and Fetal Complications

Only a small number of pregnancies (9%) affected by PMD are free from feto‐maternal complications [2]. Maternal complications include gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets), and eclampsia [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8].

Given the anatomical anomaly of the placenta in PMD and its theoretical association with pre‐eclampsia, our team initiated a daily regimen of low‐dose aspirin at 13 weeks of GA. To our knowledge, this has not been previously reported. In cases where PMD is suspected, antiplatelet prophylaxis could be considered, as it may mitigate the risk of platelet and clotting system activation triggered by the congestion and thrombosis within the placental vessels [22].

Fetal complications consist of induced premature labor, spontaneous preterm delivery, in utero fetal growth restriction (IUGR), in utero fetal demise (IUFD), and BWS [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8]. Neonatal complications include hematological disorders, such as anemia and thrombocytopenia, and liver tumors, specifically mesenchymal hamartomas [1].

Lastly, the rate of IUFD in a review of 109 cases was found to be as high as 29.4% at the median age of 31 weeks of GA [23]. It is theorized that the combination of vascular malformations of the placental vessels and thrombosis in the stem villi blood vessels, as well as their decreased functional capacity, leads to chronic hypoxia [24].

Joined to the high risk of hemorrhage secondary to the rupture of thin‐walled and dilated chorionic vessels, PMD ultimately might conduct to IUFD [1].

Hence our team's decision to induce labor as soon as a novel thrombotic zone was noted in association with a subjective decrease in fetal movements. In our case, BWS cannot be definitively excluded. Although the infant was examined by a pediatrician, subtle morphological features such as ear creases may have been overlooked, particularly in the absence of evaluation by a specialist familiar with BWS [14]. Furthermore, no postnatal ultrasound was performed to assess for visceromegaly [14]. Additional postnatal investigations were declined by the parents, as the child was in good health.

PMD is a rare condition affecting the placenta's histology, anatomy, and function, altering the exchanges' quality between mother and fetus and leading to a multitude of feto‐maternal complications.

While it is essential to include PMD in the differential diagnosis when the placenta appears enlarged, thick, and multicystic with a normal fetus to avoid an unnecessary pregnancy termination, it is also of the utmost importance to rule out gestational trophoblastic disease in light of the high maternal malignancy risk.

A multidisciplinary team involving obstetricians, pediatricians, MFM specialists, geneticists, radiologists, pathologists, midwives, and psychologists is key to the diagnosis and tailored management of the pregnancy in order to minimize fetal and/or maternal adverse outcomes.
