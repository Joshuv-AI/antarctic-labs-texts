Concordance analysis of common hand pathology referrals from primary care to plastic surgery: a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Taylor Incze, Kimberley Yuen, Avalon O’Connor, Cameron F Leveille, Hélène Retrouvey, Ammara Ghumman, Carolyn Lévis, Achilles Thoma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHand conditions are commonly referred from primary care to plastic surgery; however, no prior studies have assessed diagnostic concordance between family physicians and plastic surgeons.ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the accuracy and agreement of referring diagnoses for common hand pathologies.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study of 141 adult referrals from family physicians to plastic surgery for hand conditions at a tertiary care center over a 1-year period. Introduction

Hand conditions are common and have an estimated prevalence of 10% in the general population and an incidence of 7.8 per 1000 patients annually [1–4]. The most frequent diagnoses include osteoarthritis, tenosynovitis, and nerve entrapment [4]. Patients with these nonurgent hand pathologies often present to their family physicians first and are subsequently referred to plastic surgeons for definitive management.

Diagnostic concordance between primary care and specialists has been studied in other fields such as rheumatology, psychiatry, and internal medicine, often revealing moderate agreement and high rates of misdiagnosis or inappropriate referral [5–8]. Diagnostic uncertainty for hand conditions is common and likely reflects the limited exposure to hand pathology during undergraduate and postgraduate medical education [9]. Consequences of missed or unrecognized hand conditions may delay referral and result in inappropriate management, contributing to prolonged recovery, chronic deformity, long-term disability, or loss of income and livelihood for the patient [3, 10–12]. No studies to date have examined diagnostic concordance between family physicians and plastic surgeons for hand conditions.

This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy and concordance between primary care providers and plastic surgeons for common hand pathologies. Findings may help identify areas where diagnostic support could be beneficial and inform the development of targeted educational tools for primary care providers. This approach may offer value in strengthening diagnostic confidence and optimizing referral pathways for patients with hand conditions in family medicine. Accurate referrals by family physicians to plastic surgeons lead to a more efficient practice for both specialties and hopefully satisfied patients who receive timely care.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cross-sectional study took place at St. Joseph's Healthcare Hamilton, an academic medical center affiliated with McMaster University in Hamilton, Ontario, Canada. The study included all primary care referrals for hand conditions over a 1-year period, between 1 January and 31 December 2024. The Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board approved the study (Project #18022).

Participants

Eligible participants were adults (≥18 years) referred by a family physician for a non-life-threatening or limb-threatening hand condition who had not previously seen a plastic surgeon for the same issue. Exclusion criteria included referrals from other specialists, referrals for a second opinion, emergent referrals (e.g. hand fractures from the emergency department, or trauma), or referrals unrelated to the hand or wrist. Individuals without a fixed address were also excluded, as complete demographic information was required for reliable linkage between referral documentation and the corresponding plastic surgery consultation record. A total of 141 adult patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis.

Data collection

Patient charts were accessed based on the referral diagnosis. The plastic surgeon's diagnosis served as the reference standard, given their specialized training in hand surgery and greater familiarity with correlating clinical, radiologic, and operative findings. All three plastic surgeons involved in this study had completed a hand fellowship. Use of patients from three different surgical practices was done to reduce the risk of bias. Final diagnoses were extracted from the most recent clinic note. If the patient underwent surgery, the operative diagnosis was recorded. When pathology specimens were submitted, the pathologist's report was used as the final diagnosis.

Demographic, diagnostic, and intervention data included age, sex, occupation, hand dominance, mechanism of injury, referral type (elective or emergent), radiographic findings (if ordered), date of referral, date of consultation, plastic surgeon seen, investigations ordered by the referring physician and the surgeon, referral to hand therapy, date of surgery, number of plastic surgery visits, and number of hand therapy appointments, as applicable.

Diagnoses were recorded individually and, when multiple diagnoses were listed in the referral or final assessment, each was paired and classified separately. For example, a referral for carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) with final diagnoses of CTS and trigger finger (TF) was recorded as (1) CTS → CTS (correct), and (2) CTS → TF (missed other). This approach ensured equal numbers of referring and final diagnoses for analysis.

Sample size

A biostatistician calculated the required sample size using a normal approximation to the binomial distribution. To achieve a sensitivity of 90% with a one-sided α of 0.05 and a lower 95% confidence limit within 5% points, the analysis required at least 139 hand referrals.

Statistical analysis

Diagnostic accuracy was calculated as the proportion of referring diagnoses that matched the final diagnosis. Concordance between primary care and plastic surgery diagnoses was measured using Cohen's κ and Gwet's AC1 with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For each diagnosis (e.g. CTS), accuracy was calculated as the number of correct matches relative to the total referrals for that condition.

Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were reported as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as count (percentage).

For concordance analysis, referring and final diagnoses were grouped into the minimal number of clinically relevant categories to ensure equal numbers for the contingency table and concordance calculation. These groups included CTS, TF, ganglion, DD, 1st CMCJ OA, fractures (phalanx, volar plate, metacarpal fractures), extensor tendon injuries, other osteoarthritis affecting the hand including enchondroma, wrist, or fingers not involving the 1st CMCJ, giant cell tumor (GCT), other masses not classified as ganglion or GCT (such as wart, papillomas, “protruding nerve”), hand contractures or finger deformities (e.g. contracture or deformity in either referring or final diagnosis), DeQuervain's tenosynovitis, finger pain (e.g. swelling, pain, weakness, in either the referring or final diagnosis), and other (diagnoses that did not fit into the predefined categories, such as skin cancer, mallet finger, gout, scar, ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) injuries, radial collateral ligament (RCL) injuries, neuroma, infection, angiokeratoma, and neurothekeoma). Results

Patient characteristics and referral details

A total of 141 patients were included over the study period. Among the included patients, there were 142 reasons for referral from primary care physicians and 167 final diagnoses made by plastic surgeons.

The average age of patients was 61 years (range 19–94) and 56.0% (n = 79) were female. Most referrals were elective (n = 132, 93.6%), with the remainder marked as “urgent” or “emergent” (n = 9, 6.4%). Most patients were right-handed (n = 86, 61.0%). At the time of initial consultation, 34.8% (n = 49) of patients were employed. Of the 141 patients, 74.5% had investigations ordered by the family doctor, and 37.6% had additional investigations ordered by the plastic surgeon. Referring diagnoses and classification

Among the 142 referred diagnoses, 44 unique conditions were identified. The most common conditions were CTS (29.9%), DD (14.4%), TF (13.2%), non-ganglion or GCT masses (6.6%), ganglion (6.0%), fractures (5.4%), 1st CMCJ OA (4.8%), finger pain (6.0%), contracture (4.2%), extensor tendon injury (2.4%), DeQuervain's tenosynovitis (1.2%), and other (1.8%). The 167 final diagnoses made by the plastic surgeons (from 142 referred diagnoses), were classified into five diagnostic categories: correct diagnosis (n = 118, 70.7%), incorrect diagnosis (n = 14, 8.4%), missed main diagnosis (n = 3, 1.8%), missed other diagnoses (n = 20, 12.0%), and vague diagnosis (n = 12, 7.2%).

Most final diagnoses (93.4%) were made clinically by the plastic surgeon (Level 1). Within this, 106 diagnoses were classified as 1a (no investigations), and 50 were classified as 1b (supplemental investigations). A total of 1.8% of final diagnoses made by the plastic surgeon were made following investigations (Level 2), and 4.8% were confirmed after surgery or pathology (Level 3).

Referrals with incorrect diagnoses mostly reflected diagnostic overlap among common hand pathologies, as well as misclassification of soft tissue lesions. Incorrect diagnoses and their corresponding final diagnoses included the following: a vascular mass diagnosed as GCT, an extensor tendon injury diagnosed as scar hypersensitivity, a bony spur of the thumb PIPJ diagnosed as trigger thumb, a TF diagnosed as DD, a TF diagnosed as 1st CMCJ OA, a ganglion diagnosed as GCT and 1st CMCJ OA, an infection (finger swelling/abscess) diagnosed as a ganglion, a papilloma diagnosed as neurothekeoma, a wart diagnosed as angiokeratoma, and a nerve sheath tumor diagnosed as a GCT. Full case-level details for all referred and final diagnoses are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Half of the referrals with incorrect diagnoses required additional investigations or operative investigations to establish the final diagnosis (Level 2 or Level 3).

Diagnostic accuracy

Overall diagnostic accuracy was 70.7% (118/167 classified as correct diagnoses). For the five most common nongrouped hand pathologies, diagnostic accuracy was highest for CTS (95.7%) and DD (91.3%), followed by TF (66.7%), ganglion (62.5%), and 1st CMCJ OA (42.9%).

Diagnostic concordance

Overall diagnostic concordance between referring family physicians and plastic surgeons was substantial, indicating “good” agreement, κ = 0.67 (95% CI, 0.58–0.73), and Gwet's AC1 of 0.69 (95% CI, 0.62–0.77). Among the five most common hand pathologies, CTS and DD both had κ = 0.88, (95% CI, 0.80–0.96 and 95% CI, 0.77–0.98, respectively). TF had a κ of 0.69 (95% CI, 0.53–0.85), ganglion had a κ of 0.53 (95% CI, 0.21–0.85), and 1st CMCJ OA had a κ of 0.52 (95% CI, 0.23–0.81). Discussion

In this study, the overall diagnostic concordance of common hand pathologies between family physicians and plastic surgeons was substantial (Cohen's κ = 0.67, Gwet's AC1 = 0.69). The five most commonly referred conditions were CTS, DD, TF, ganglions, and 1st CMCJ OA, accounting for 68.3% of all diagnoses. Both CTS and DD demonstrated high concordance, suggesting strong agreement between referring physicians and specialists. This may be because these pathologies often have distinct clinical features and provocative testing that make them easier to recognize in a primary care setting. For example, CTS frequently presents with characteristic numbness and tingling in the median nerve distribution, nocturnal paresthesias, and decreased fine motor control, with positive Tinel's sign, Phalen's test, or Durkan's test at the carpal tunnel [13, 14]. DD often includes visible or palpable nodules and/or cords along the palmar fascia, creating a flexion contracture at the proximal interphalangeal joint and/or metacarpophalangeal joint [15]. The tabletop test, which assesses whether the patient can fully straighten their hand on a flat surface or not, helps to determine the likelihood of DD [15, 16].

While diagnostic concordance was high for DD, it should be noted that the distinction between early and late DD is important for referral practices. Early DD presenting as a palmar cord without contracture does not require referral or surgical intervention—monitoring is appropriate. Referral and surgical intervention are encouraged for those who develop a contracture (30° of flexion at the MCPJ, or any degree of flexion at the PIPJ) [17–20]. In our study, there were 24 referrals for DD, and 9 (37.5%) received surgery. While this suggests that some patients were referred before meeting surgical criteria, this may also be appropriate given long wait times, as earlier referral allows for reassessment and may support timely intervention.

In contrast, diagnostic concordance of TF, ganglions, and 1st CMCJ OA were lower (0.69, 0.53, and 0.52, respectively), which can be classified as “moderate agreement.” This is likely due to overlapping clinical features and anatomical complexity. 1st CMCJ OA, for instance, occurs near the wrist and may present with pain or swelling that resembles DeQuervain's tenosynovitis or scaphoid arthritis, making differentiation challenging without a detailed examination [21, 22]. Similarly, early DD, ganglions, and TF can all present with nodules near the flexor tendons, making differentiation difficult without a thorough and focused hand examination. Moreover, the characteristic “triggering” and “locking” of TF may not always be present during the time of clinical exam [23].

Our findings align with prior literature demonstrating diagnostic challenges in the assessment of hand pathology by nonspecialists [11]. Previous research has found that certain injuries, particularly flexor tendon injuries and lacerations, were frequently associated with incomplete diagnoses at the time of referral with diagnostic discordance clustering around pathologies with overlapping clinical features or complex anatomy.

Family physicians require a broad knowledge base to manage diverse clinical presentations, yet formal training in hand pathology during medical school and residency is often limited [9]. This may reduce opportunities to perform detailed hand examinations or pursue nuanced differential diagnoses. As a result, more prevalent or familiar conditions such as CTS (especially with supplementary diagnostic tests such as electromyography and nerve conduction studies) may be more readily identified, while less common or anatomically complex pathologies may be misdiagnosed or missed.

To address these diagnostic challenges, educational initiatives could focus on providing family physicians with practical tools to support recognition of commonly misdiagnosed conditions such as 1st CMCJ OA, TF, and DD. Such initiatives may include focused teaching during undergraduate medical education, additional targeted musculoskeletal or hand modules within Family Medicine residency training, or continuing medical education (CME) workshops and case-based sessions. These could be spearheaded by plastic surgeons to foster physician collaboration and cross-specialty learning.

Training in recognition of key clinical signs may help improve diagnostic accuracy and subsequently improve referral practices. Educational sessions should emphasize recognition of key examination findings and differentiators. This would improve referral pathways for when patients require surgical opinion or intervention. For example, recognizing early 1st CMCJ OA and that it can benefit from steroid injections and splinting enables patients to obtain a modality of treatment earlier and avoid early erroneous referrals. Limitations

This study was conducted at a single academic center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings with different referral patterns or practice environments. The study period was restricted to one calendar year, which may not reflect longer-term trends. The included referrals only included commonly encountered hand pathologies in plastic surgery and are representative of a routine hand practice, but exclude other commonly seen plastic surgery presentations such as macromastia, cubital tunnel syndrome, and acute hand fractures. We also focused specifically on referrals from family physicians, which excludes diagnostic patterns from other referring providers such as rheumatologists or orthopedic surgeons.

Conclusion

While overall diagnostic concordance between family physicians and plastic surgeons was substantial for hand pathologies, notable variability existed across specific hand conditions. CTS and DD showed high agreement, whereas TF, 1st CMCJ OA, and ganglions had lower concordance. These discrepancies likely stem from overlapping clinical presentations and the inherent challenges of detailed musculoskeletal assessment in primary care settings. Given that family physicians are often the first point of contact for patients with hand concerns, targeted educational strategies focusing on the distinguishing features of less accurately diagnosed conditions, such as the ones provided in this paper, may support more effective referrals. Future work should explore whether diagnostic concordance influences treatment timelines, healthcare utilization, and patient outcomes.
