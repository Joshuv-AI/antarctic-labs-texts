The association between blood eosinophil count and clinical outcomes during inhaled corticosteroid/long-acting β2-agonist treatment in stable-phase chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: A retrospective cohort analysis
Authors: Yang Liu, Shenghai Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Blood eosinophil (EOS) count has emerged as a potential biomarker associated with inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) responsiveness in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). However, the relationship between EOS counts and clinical outcomes during ICS treatment in stable-phase COPD remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the association between baseline EOS counts and changes in lung function, symptom burden, and exacerbation frequency during ICS/long-acting β2-agonist (LABA) treatment. A retrospective cohort study included 200 stable-phase COPD patients treated with ICS/LABA combinations for 12 months. Participants were stratified into low (<150 cells/μL), medium (150–300 cells/μL), and high (>300 cells/μL) EOS groups. Outcomes included changes in forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1%pred), COPD Assessment Test scores, 6-minute walk distance, and annualized exacerbation rates. Inflammatory markers (interleukin-5 [IL-5], eosinophil cationic protein) were analyzed. Baseline EOS counts were positively correlated with IL-5 and eosinophil cationic protein. After 12 months, higher baseline EOS counts were associated with greater changes in FEV1%pred and 6-minute walk distance. The reduction in exacerbation rates was 51.9% in the high EOS group versus 22.3% in the low EOS group. Receiver operating characteristic analysis identified a baseline EOS cutoff of 285 cells/μL for predicting FEV1%pred improvement ≥5%. Higher baseline EOS counts are associated with greater improvements in clinical outcomes during ICS/LABA therapy in stable COPD, although the absence of a non-ICS comparator precludes causal inference. These findings support further investigation of EOS-guided treatment strategies in this population.

1. Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality, affecting over 390 million individuals worldwide and ranking as the third most common cause of death in 2019 according to the Global Burden of Disease Study.[1,2] Characterized by persistent respiratory symptoms and progressive airflow limitation, COPD imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden, with annual healthcare costs exceeding $30 billion in the United States alone. Despite advances in therapeutic strategies, disease progression and acute exacerbations remain major drivers of hospitalization, functional decline, and reduced quality of life.[3-5]

Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), often combined with long-acting β2-agonists (LABA), are widely prescribed to reduce airway inflammation and exacerbation frequency.[6-8] However, clinical responses to ICS are heterogeneous, with up to 40% of patients deriving limited benefit. This variability underscores the need for biomarkers to guide personalized therapy.[9] Moreover, indiscriminate ICS use raises concerns about adverse effects, including pneumonia and osteoporosis,[10,11] making it important to identify patients most likely to benefit. Blood eosinophil (EOS) counts have emerged as a promising biomarker in this context.[12-14] Landmark trials, such as FLAME and IMPACT, demonstrated that COPD patients with elevated EOS counts (≥300 cells/μL) experience greater reductions in exacerbations with ICS-containing regimens,[15] and the 2023 Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) report now recommends EOS-guided ICS use, particularly in exacerbation-prone individuals.[16,17] However, most of this evidence derives from exacerbation-focused cohorts, and data supporting EOS thresholds in stable-phase COPD remain sparse.[18]

In stable-phase COPD, the role of eosinophils (EOS) is mechanistically distinct.[19] Eosinophilic inflammation contributes to airway remodeling through the release of cytotoxic granule proteins (e.g., ECP) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-5).[20-23] Persistent eosinophilic inflammation may drive subclinical airway damage, contributing to accelerated lung function decline even in the absence of acute exacerbations. While observational studies suggest that higher EOS counts correlate with greater improvements in lung function during ICS treatment, optimal thresholds and longitudinal outcomes in stable patients are poorly defined.[24,25] Furthermore, the interplay between EOS counts and downstream inflammatory mediators like IL-5 and ECP in this population remains underexplored.

This retrospective cohort study aimed to address these gaps by evaluating the association between baseline EOS counts and clinical outcomes during ICS/LABA treatment in stable COPD patients. The primary objective was to determine whether higher baseline EOS levels are associated with greater improvements in lung function, symptom burden, and exacerbation reduction over 12 months of follow-up. Secondary objectives included identifying an optimal EOS threshold for clinical decision-making and exploring correlations between EOS counts and inflammatory biomarkers. Our findings aim to inform EOS-guided treatment strategies in stable COPD, aligning therapeutic precision with patient-specific pathophysiology.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University between January 2020 and December 2022. A total of 200 patients diagnosed with stable-phase COPD (GOLD stages II–III) and treated with fixed-dose ICS/LABA combinations (fluticasone/salmeterol 250/50 μg twice daily or budesonide/formoterol 160/4.5 μg twice daily) were included. GOLD stages II–III were selected because these stages represent the majority of patients encountered in clinical practice who are considered candidates for ICS/LABA therapy, while patients with mild (stage I) or very severe (stage IV) disease were excluded to reduce clinical heterogeneity. COPD diagnosis was confirmed according to the 2023 GOLD criteria, defined as a post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1)/FVC ratio <0.70. Stable-phase COPD required no acute exacerbations (AECOPD) in the preceding 6 weeks, defined as a sustained worsening of respiratory symptoms necessitating antibiotics, systemic corticosteroids, or hospitalization. Treatment regimens were required to remain unchanged throughout the 12-month follow-up period; patients who underwent treatment modifications were excluded from the analysis.

2.2. Data collection

2.2.1. Clinical and demographic data

Electronic medical records were reviewed to extract age, sex, smoking history, comorbidities, and medication use. Smoking intensity was quantified as pack-years (1 pack-year = 20 cigarettes/d for 1 year). Prior ICS exposure before the study period was not systematically recorded in the medical records and therefore could not be assessed.

2.2.2. Lung function and symptom assessment

Spirometry (FEV1, FVC) was performed using a Jaeger MasterScreen PFT System (CareFusion, Germany) following ATS/ERS guidelines. Post-bronchodilator FEV1% predicted (FEV1%pred) and FEV1/FVC ratios were recorded. Symptom burden was assessed using the chronic obstructive pulmonary disease assessment test (CAT), with scores ranging from 0 to 40. Exercise capacity was measured via 6-minute walk distance under standardized conditions.

2.2.3. Biomarker analysis

Peripheral blood samples were collected at baseline (fasting, 8:00–10:00 am) in EDTA tubes. Baseline blood eosinophil counts were obtained from a single measurement at enrollment. Serum was separated by centrifugation (3000 rpm, 10 min, 4°C) and stored at −80°C until analysis. Serum IL-5 and ECP levels were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (R&D Systems; IL-5: Catalog #DY205, sensitivity 0.8 pg/mL; ECP: Catalog #DY9164-05, sensitivity 2.5 μg/L). Intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were <8% and <12%, respectively. Laboratory personnel performing biomarker assays were blinded to clinical data and eosinophil group assignment.

2.2.4. Exacerbation tracking

AECOPD episodes were recorded during the 12-month follow-up through scheduled clinic visits at 3, 6, and 12 months, supplemented by review of emergency department visits and hospitalization records. Exacerbations were defined as acute worsening of dyspnea, cough, or sputum purulence requiring antibiotic therapy, systemic corticosteroids (prednisone ≥ 20 mg/day for ≥3 days), or hospitalization.

2.3. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM) and GraphPad Prism 8.0. Normality was assessed via Shapiro–Wilk tests. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median (IQR) and compared using 1-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables (%) were analyzed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Spearman’s rank correlation assessed relationships between EOS counts and inflammatory markers (IL-5, ECP). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves evaluated baseline EOS thresholds for predicting FEV1%pred improvement ≥5%, a threshold selected based on previously reported minimal clinically important differences for FEV1 in COPD populations. The optimal cutoff was determined by maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). Multivariable logistic regression was performed using a forced-entry approach, with age, smoking status, and baseline FEV1%pred included as covariates based on their established clinical relevance; additional variables such as baseline CAT score, comorbidities, and prior exacerbation history were not available in sufficient detail for inclusion, and this is acknowledged as a limitation. A 2-tailed P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. Bonferroni correction addressed multiple comparisons where applicable. Only patients with complete data for all primary outcomes were included in the analysis; no imputation methods were applied. Of the initial cohort, all 200 patients had complete follow-up data for the primary endpoints.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline demographics and inflammatory biomarker profiles

The study cohort comprised 200 stable COPD patients, stratified into low (<150 cells/μL), medium (150–300 cells/μL), and high (>300 cells/μL) eosinophil (EOS) groups. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, smoking history (pack-years), FEV1%pred, CAT scores, and 6-minute walk distance, were comparable across groups (P > .05 for all; Table 1). In contrast, serum interleukin-5 (IL-5) and eosinophil cationic protein (ECP) levels increased progressively across EOS strata (P < .001 for both), confirming the association between EOS elevation and eosinophilic inflammation (Table 1).

3.2. Changes in clinical outcomes by EOS stratification

After 12 months of ICS/LABA therapy, improvements in lung function, symptom burden, and exercise capacity differed across EOS groups, with progressively greater changes observed in higher EOS strata (Table 2). The high EOS group showed the largest improvements in FEV1%pred and 6MWD, as well as the greatest reductions in CAT scores, compared with the low and medium groups (P < .01 for all comparisons). Exacerbation frequency also differed by EOS group, with the largest percentage reduction observed in the high EOS group (P < .01; Table 2). Reductions in IL-5 and ECP followed the same pattern, with greater decreases in higher EOS strata (P < .01).

3.5. Predictive value of baseline EOS for clinical improvement

ROC analysis identified a baseline EOS threshold of 285 cells/μL as optimal for identifying patients who achieved clinically significant FEV1%pred improvement (≥5%; Table 5). This cutoff yielded an area under the curve of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.75–0.89), with 78% sensitivity and 81% specificity. At higher thresholds (e.g., 300 cells/μL), specificity increased while sensitivity decreased, whereas lower thresholds (e.g., 150 cells/μL) showed poor discriminatory power (Table 5).

4. Discussion

This study demonstrates that baseline blood eosinophil (EOS) counts are associated with differential clinical outcomes during ICS/LABA treatment in stable-phase COPD patients, with higher EOS levels correlating with greater improvements in lung function, symptom burden, and exacerbation reduction. These findings are consistent with emerging paradigms in precision medicine for COPD, emphasizing the importance of biomarker-guided therapy to optimize clinical outcomes while minimizing corticosteroid-related risks.

Compared to the SUNSET trial, which questioned ICS utility in stable COPD regardless of EOS levels,[26] our stratified analysis suggests that the limited benefit of ICS in stable patients may be confined to those with EOS < 150 cells/μL, whereas higher subgroups show more favorable clinical trajectories. The observed gradient in clinical outcomes across baseline EOS strata reinforces the relevance of eosinophilic inflammation in COPD pathogenesis. Patients with EOS > 300 cells/μL demonstrated the largest changes in FEV1%pred and exacerbation reduction (Table 2), consistent with findings from the FLAME and IMPACT trials, which highlighted EOS-associated ICS benefits in exacerbation-prone cohorts.[11] Importantly, unlike these prior studies, our cohort comprised stable-phase patients, suggesting that the association between EOS levels and clinical outcomes extends beyond exacerbation-focused populations.[27] The strong correlation between EOS counts and IL-5/ECP levels (r = 0.62–0.71; P < .001) further supports EOS as a surrogate marker of IL-5-driven inflammation, a key therapeutic target for ICS.[28] This is consistent with the STATURE study’s bronchial biopsy data, although our noninvasive approach offers greater clinical applicability.[29,30]

The observed reductions in IL-5 and ECP during ICS/LABA treatment were most pronounced in the high EOS group, consistent with the known mechanisms by which ICS suppresses eosinophil recruitment through inhibition of IL-5 signaling and reduction of epithelial cytokine release.[31-34] Notably, biomarker reductions plateaued after 6 months, a pattern that has been observed in asthma ICS trials but has been rarely documented in COPD. This finding raises questions about optimal treatment duration and warrants further investigation, including evaluation of pulsed ICS regimens such as those being studied in the INTERVAL-COPD initiative.[28]

The ROC-derived threshold of 285 cells/μL (AUC = 0.82) closely aligns with the GOLD 2023 cutoff of 300 cells/μL but diverges from the 400 cells/μL threshold proposed in the TRIBUTE trial for dual bronchodilator nonresponders. This discrepancy may reflect differences in study populations: our inclusion of mild-moderate patients versus TRIBUTE’s focus on severe COPD with frequent exacerbations.[35] Of note, in our cohort, 35% of medium EOS patients (150–300 cells/μL) still achieved meaningful FEV1 improvements, suggesting potential clinical utility even below traditional thresholds. This observation is of interest in light of the WISDOM trial’s stepwise ICS withdrawal data, where similar patients exhibited variable relapse risks. However, in the absence of a non-ICS comparator group in the present study, it is not possible to determine whether these improvements are attributable to ICS treatment itself or to the natural disease course and other factors.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, and the single-center cohort limits generalizability. Second, and most importantly, all patients received ICS/LABA therapy, and there was no non-ICS comparator group. Therefore, the observed differences across EOS strata represent associations between baseline eosinophil levels and clinical evolution during treatment, rather than evidence of differential treatment efficacy. Confounding by indication cannot be excluded, as the clinical criteria used to initiate ICS/LABA therapy were not systematically recorded. Third, the exclusion of patients with asthma overlap or severe comorbidities may underestimate EOS variability in real-world populations. Fourth, eosinophil counts were based on a single baseline measurement. Given the known intraindividual variability of blood EOS, this may have led to misclassification of some patients across EOS strata. Fifth, the multivariable models adjusted only for age, smoking status, and baseline FEV1%pred. Other potentially relevant confounders, including prior exacerbation frequency, baseline CAT score, comorbidities, and baseline inflammatory status, were not included, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Sixth, we did not assess longitudinal EOS dynamics or genetic factors that may influence treatment responses. Future research should prioritize prospective, multicenter studies with appropriate comparator groups to validate the 285 cells/μL threshold and explore dynamic EOS monitoring during treatment. Additionally, integrating EOS counts with other biomarkers (e.g., fractional exhaled nitric oxide [FeNO]) or omics profiles could enhance predictive accuracy. Mechanistic studies are also needed to elucidate why biomarker suppression plateaus post-6 months and whether intermittent ICS regimens could sustain efficacy.

5. Conclusion

In stable-phase COPD, higher baseline EOS counts are associated with greater improvements in lung function, symptoms, and exacerbation frequency during 12 months of ICS/LABA treatment. A threshold of ≥285 cells/μL showed the best discriminatory performance for identifying patients with clinically significant lung function improvement. However, as this was an observational study without a non-ICS comparator, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of differential treatment efficacy. Future prospective studies with appropriate control groups are needed to determine whether EOS-guided treatment algorithms can improve COPD management outcomes.
