Ultrastructural studies reveal a new role for JIP3/MAPK8IP3 in endo-lysosomal maturation within the neuronal cell body
Authors: Mia Krout, Swetha Gowrishankar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent years, substantial heterogeneity in lysosomes, in terms of their composition, function, and positioning, has come to be recognized. Despite this, there are gaps in knowledge of our understanding of the molecular basis of lysosome heterogeneity, especially in neurons. To this end, we used electron microscopy to define endolysosomal organelles of human iPSC-derived neurons at the ultrastructural level. Through this, we identify endolysosomal maturation defects within neuronal cell bodies of iNeurons lacking JNK-Interacting Protein 3 (JIP3), a lysosome adaptor previously known to primarily regulate axonal lysosome movement. Loss of JIP3 results in an expansion of immature lysosomes within neuronal soma, along with a concomitant decrease in mature lysosomes. JIP3 loss also leads to delayed trafficking of endocytic cargo through these compartments, implicating JIP3 in the regulation of endolysosomal maturation within neuronal cell bodies. Our studies highlight the utility of electron microscopy in understanding neuronal lysosomal heterogeneity. Additionally, given recent links between JIP3 and a neurodevelopmental disorder, our findings here could provide new insight into mechanisms underlying neurodevelopmental pathology.

INTRODUCTION

The regulation of lysosome transport and its positioning in neurons has been linked to several critical aspects of neuronal development and function (Winckler et al., 2018; Paumier and Gowrishankar, 2024). This includes the regulation of structural and physiological changes at the single neuronal and network level: formation and maintenance of the presynapse (Y. C. Wang et al., 2017; Sambri et al., 2017; Vukoja et al., 2018; Rizalar et al., 2021), axonal growth and homeostasis (Farías et al., 2017; Y. C. Wang et al., 2017; De Pace et al., 2024), and alteration of dendritic spine morphology as well as synaptic plasticity (Goo et al., 2017; Padamsey et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2023). In further support of a role for lysosomes in neuronal development, dysregulation in their transport and positioning has been linked to several different neurodevelopmental disorders (Winckler et al., 2018; Paumier and Gowrishankar, 2024). Regulation of transport and positioning of lysosomes in neurons is particularly complex, given the spatial heterogeneity of the functional domains within them, such as axons, dendrites, soma/cell body, and synapses (V. V. Kulkarni and Maday, 2018; Yap et al., 2022; Paumier and Gowrishankar, 2024). Several factors, including small GTPases and adaptors, have been found to uniquely or differentially regulate the transport of lysosomes within some of these functional domains, particularly in axons and dendrites (Cheng et al., 2018; Farías et al., 2017; Gowrishankar et al., 2021; Yap et al., 2018). The soma and near-soma lysosomes in neurons are the most acidic and degradative (Gowrishankar et al., 2015; Cheng et al., 2018; Yap et al., 2018; Gowrishankar et al., 2021) and include input from axons and dendrites in addition to organelles generated within them (Maday and Holzbaur, 2016; V. V. Kulkarni et al., 2022). Studies suggest that the dynamics and maturation state of these organelles differ from those in the neuronal processes (Maday and Holzbaur, 2016; Gowrishankar et al., 2017; Cheng et al., 2018), but the mechanisms that control lysosome positioning and movement in the neuronal soma, including the roles of the different adaptors, have not been investigated extensively.

One lysosomal adaptor that has been particularly linked to the regulation of axonal lysosome transport and abundance is MAPK8IP3/JIP3 (Drerup and Nechiporuk, 2013; Edwards et al., 2013; Gowrishankar et al., 2017, 2021). Loss of JIP3 protein in Caenorhabditis elegans and zebrafish, as well as mammalian cultured neurons, resulted in a massive accumulation of endolysosomes within axonal swellings (Drerup and Nechiporuk, 2013; Edwards et al., 2013; Gowrishankar et al., 2017, 2021). These, as well as other studies (Cavalli et al., 2005; Abe et al., 2009; Cason et al., 2021; Celestino et al., 2022; Cason and Holzbaur, 2023), seem to suggest that the lysosomal defect due to JIP3 loss is primarily confined to axons. Recently, two independent studies linked multiple recurrent, de novo mutations in MAPK8IP3 to a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by intellectual disability and variable brain anomalies, including hypoplasia of the corpus callosum (Iwasawa et al., 2019; Platzer et al., 2019). Overexpression of human JIP3 carrying two of these missense mutations in zebrafish embryos caused axonal varicosities in the pLL neurons, suggestive of axonal developmental defects (Iwasawa et al., 2019). The second study that described these variants demonstrated that CRISPR-engineering several of the human JIP3 variants in C. elegans affected locomotor activity in these worms, but only a third of the tested variants caused axonal lysosomes to build up (Platzer et al., 2019). While this supports the importance of JIP3 for normal neuronal functioning, it also raises the possibility that JIP3 regulates other cellular processes critical to neuronal health, which may be impacted in these disease-linked variants. Indeed, we recently found that loss of JIP3 in human iPSC-derived neurons (i3Neurons) resulted in reduced uptake of certain endocytic cargo (Snead and Gowrishankar, 2022). This evidence for a possible role for JIP3 in regulating endocytic traffic in neuronal cell bodies, as well as the dearth of information on how these adaptors potentially regulate neuronal soma lysosomes, led us to carry out a systematic ultrastructural study of endo-lysosomal (E/L) compartments of neuronal cell bodies in i3 Neurons, utilizing transmission electron microscopy.

Here, we establish a detailed morphological profile of organelles present throughout the E/L pathway as well as the frequency of the different E/L intermediates, in the soma of Control i3Neurons. Through a comparative analysis of these E/L organelles in Control and JIP3 KO i3Neurons, we show that loss of JIP3 leads to E/L maturation defects even in neuronal cell bodies: their neuronal cell bodies exhibit an increase in early compartments, a dramatic expansion in the morphology of immature lysosomes, and fewer mature lysosomes when compared with the soma of Control neurons. Through a BSA-Au pulse experiment, we also reveal a delay in endo-lysosomal trafficking in the soma of JIP3 KO i3Neurons. In addition to these new phenotypes, we found the dramatic E/L organelle build-up in axonal swellings in the JIP3 KO i3Neurons that has been reported in other JIP3 loss-of-function models (Gowrishankar et al., 2017). Together, these studies reveal a previously unappreciated role for JIP3 in regulating soma lysosomes, which could have important consequences in the context of JIP3-linked neurodevelopmental disorder. Additionally, given the complexity in endolysosomal subtypes (Roney et al., 2022; Paumier and Gowrishankar, 2024) and the difficulty in their unambiguous identification (Winckler et al., 2018; Roney et al., 2022; Bond et al., 2025), we posit that E/L morphology as revealed by ultrastructural analysis is a critical tool in deciphering neuronal E/L biogenesis and transport mechanisms.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Ultrastructure examination of endolysosomal compartments in i3Neurons

Previous studies investigating JIP3 function have primarily focused on its role in axonal transport and JNK signaling (Cavalli et al., 2005; Abe et al., 2009; Drerup and Nechiporuk, 2013; Edwards et al., 2013; Gowrishankar et al., 2017, 2021; Cason et al., 2021; Cason and Holzbaur, 2023). Studies in multiple animal models have shown JIP3 to be a critical regulator of axonal lysosome abundance and transport. At steady state, healthy neurons have relatively few endolysosomes in axons (Figure 1, A and B), with the majority of these organelles progressively maturing as they move retrogradely, towards the neuronal soma, where most of the protease-rich, degradative lysosomes reside. In contrast, JIP3 KO neurons exhibit a massive buildup of E/L in axonal swellings (Figure 1, A and B). Indeed, while immunofluorescence examination of LAMP1-positive E/L compartments upon loss of JIP3 reveals this striking axonal E/L buildup (Figure 1, A and B), there is no appreciable disparity in LAMP1-positive organelles in the cell bodies when compared with Control (Figure 1, B and C). Since LAMP1 labels heterogeneous populations of E/L organelles (Cheng et al., 2018; Bond et al., 2025), including nondegradative late endosomes and fully mature, degradative lysosomes, we undertook a detailed ultrastructural analysis of all E/L compartments within the soma of Control and JIP3 KO i3Neurons. We first systematically classified E/L compartments in the soma of Control i3Neurons. We categorized the structures into six groups, based on morphology and maturation state, and comparisons with well-established descriptive criteria from non-neuronal cells (Novikoff et al., 1956; Klumperman and Raposo, 2014). Early endosomes (EEs) are notable for their small size, clear appearance and lack of intraluminal vesicles (ILVs; Chavrier et al., 1990; Gorvel et al., 1991; Bucci et al., 1992; Klumperman et al., 1993; Figure 1, D and E “1”); organelles with relatively few ILVs were classified as early (3 or fewer ILVs)- and intermediate- stage (four or more ILVs) multi-vesicular bodies (MVBs; Klumperman and Raposo, 2014; Figure 1, D and E “2–3″, respectively). Late-stage MVBs were classified as such based on having greater electron-density, and therefore a darker appearance, of their lumen compared with earlier compartments. They also had many ILVs that were often smaller in size than those contained in the two earlier stage compartments (Klumperman and Raposo, 2014; Figure 1, D and E “4”). Organelles that contained “whorls” of membrane, along with intact and likely partially degraded contents, including autophagosomes, were considered to be immature lysosomes (IL; Figure 1, D and E “5”). E/L compartments with dense and homogenous lumens, lacking any visible intact membrane content, were categorized as mature lysosomes (ML; Figure 1, D and E “6”).

At steady state, the somas of Control i3Neurons have a bias toward later structures, both immature and mature lysosomes (“5”, “6”), with the overwhelming majority being compact, electron-dense, mature compartments. EEs occur less frequently than other structures in Control i3Neurons; on average, EEs make up less than 10% of total quantified soma E/L (Figure 1, D and E “1”; Supplemental Figure S1A). Both E- and I-stage MVBs occur at slightly higher frequencies than EEs (on average around 13% and 17%, respectively; Figure 1, D and E “2–3″; Supplemental Figure S1A). L-MVBs are relatively fewer (10%) than the earlier MVB compartments (Figure 1, D and E “4”; Supplemental Figure S1A). Around 19% of all structures quantified in the soma of Control i3Neurons are immature lysosomes (Figure 1, D and E “5”; Supplemental Figure S1A). Finally, mature lysosomes make up over 30% of compartments present in the cell bodies of Control i3Neurons (Figure 1, D and E “6”; Supplemental Figure S1A).

Ultrastructural analysis reveals alteration in endolysosomal maturation in neuronal cell bodies of JIP3 KO i3Neurons

As we and others have previously reported, neurons lacking MAPK8IP3/JIP3 exhibit trafficking defects; however, these studies have primarily focused on axonal pathologies (Cavalli et al., 2005; Abe et al., 2009; Edwards et al., 2013; Gowrishankar et al., 2017, 2021; Iwasawa et al., 2019; Platzer et al., 2019). We investigated if loss of JIP3 results in an alteration of E/L compartments within the soma of these i3Neurons. Indeed, in contrast to the Control, there is a significant increase in early endosomal compartments in the JIP3 KO (13% in KO compared with 8% in Control), as well as E-MVBs (20% in KO compared with 11% in Control; Figure 1, D-F “1–2″; Supplemental Figure S1; Figure 2, A-C). Interestingly, there is also a striking increase in immature lysosomes (“5”), with them making up 30% of total E/L organelles in the mutant neurons, which exhibit a stunning expansion in the spectrum of the morphology of these compartments (Figure 1, D-F “5”; Supplemental Figure S1; Figure 2, D-F). Unlike the Control, where MLs (“6”) make up the largest fraction of E/L, there is a trend towards a relatively smaller fraction of MLs. These results, taken together, could be suggestive of a decrease in the rate of maturation of E/Ls even in the soma of JIP3 KO i3Neurons. In fact, when we compare individual cell body data, there is a significant decrease in the fraction of mature lysosomes within the soma of JIP3 KO i3Neurons compared with the Control (Figure 1, D-F “6”; Supplemental Figure S1, A and B). The striking increase in frequency of the immature E/L compartments, along with a decrease in mature lysosomes, suggests a significant defect in endolysosomal maturation in the cell bodies of the JIP3 KO i3Neurons. This is supported by the dramatic expansion in the morphological subtypes of immature lysosomes in the cell body of JIP3 KO i3 Neurons (Figure 2). Indeed, many exhibit increased intact membrane content in their lumen, including autophagosomes. Previous studies relied heavily on immunofluorescent evidence, making it hard to distinguish differences between sub-populations of LAMP1-positive organelles. Here, ultrastructural analysis has helped to reveal previously unidentified differences in the maturation state of E/L organelles in the somas of JIP3 KO i3Neurons. This endolysosomal maturation defect within neuronal cell bodies was indeed specifically from loss of JIP3 protein, as frequency of immature (Figure 3, A and C) and mature E/L (Figure 3, B and D) compartments was restored to that observed in Control i3Neurons in JIP3 Rescue i3Neurons [neurons differentiated from iPSCs where CRISPR-based editing restored the JIP3 KO line to WT i3Neurons (Rafiq et al., 2022)].

Endocytic trafficking is delayed in the soma of JIP3 KO i3Neurons

We next used an endocytic cargo to populate E/L compartments to examine trafficking at the ultrastructural level. We performed a pulse experiment using BSA-conjugated 6 nm gold beads (BSA-Au6), before fixation and processing for electron microscopy. In the Control, at least half of mature lysosomes contain BSA-Au6 (yellow arrows) while 23% of immature lysosomes have the endocytic cargo (Figure 4, A-D “5–6″).

Based on our findings here of an expansion of more immature E/L compartments in the somas of JIP3 KO i3Neurons, as well as our previous finding that these neurons exhibit defects in endocytic uptake of Dextran (Snead and Gowrishankar, 2022), we predicted a delay or reduced traffic of the BSA-Au6 in JIP3 KO somata as compared with the Control. Indeed, we discovered that only 19% of immature lysosomes and a significant drop to 19% of mature lysosomes have evidence of BSA-Au6 in the mutant, compared with 33% and 50% respectively in the Control i3Neurons and 30% and 54% in JIP3 Rescue i3Neurons (Figure 4, A-D “5–6″), supportive of delayed trafficking of endocytosed cargo within neuronal cell bodies, arising from JIP3 loss. Thus, both the morphological characterization of the E/L organelles as well as endocytic cargo delivery studies we have carried out suggest that JIP3 is involved in trafficking and maturation of E/L in the cell body of neurons.

Consistent with a role for JIP3 in regulating endolysosomal maturation, the protein has been shown to localize to Rab7- and LAMP1-positive late endosomes and lysosomes in primary murine neurons (Gowrishankar et al., 2017). JIP3 is known to bind both kinesin and dynein (Arimoto et al., 2011; Celestino et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2022; Cason and Holzbaur, 2023; Singh et al., 2024), and interacts with GTPases, Arf6 (Montagnac et al., 2009) and Rab36 (Matsui et al., 2012). Interestingly, JIP3, Arf6, and dynein were shown to mediate macropinocytosis in a cultured cell line, and Arf6 engagement with JIP3 was necessary for the process (Williamson and Donaldson, 2019). Studies have also linked endolysosomal maturation to motility in non-neuronal cells (Huotari and Helenius, 2011). Disruption to microtubules (Vonderheit and Helenius, 2005) or dynein (Driskell et al., 2006) results in a more peripheral distribution of late endosomes and lysosomes, as well as delayed maturation of these organelles, and persistence of the early endosomal GTPase, Rab5, on these compartments. This raises the possibility that maturation defects from JIP3 loss could be linked to motility defects. While our current study examines the effect of the complete loss of JIP3, studies expressing mutant JIP3 that cannot bind dynein light intermediate chain (DLIC) resulted in endolysosomal vesicles accumulating at the neurite tip (Celestino et al., 2022), highlighting the importance of motor binding for JIP3 to exert its organelle positioning effect. Future studies involving this mutant version of JIP3 or other pathogenic JIP3 variants could help determine whether motor binding is necessary for JIP3 to exert its effect on the maturation of soma endolysosomes.

Lysosomal positioning within the cell bodies of i3Neurons, as revealed by ultrastructural studies

The degradative function of lysosomes has been linked to their proximity to the nucleus (Korolchuk and Rubinsztein, 2011; Johnson et al., 2016; Paumier and Gowrishankar, 2024). Considering our new findings here, that soma of JIP3 KO i3Neurons likely also exhibit E/L maturation defects, we next investigated whether localization of immature and mature lysosomes would be altered in the cell bodies of JIP3 KO i3Neurons. When we examined the distance of mature and immature lysosomes from the nucleus in the cell bodies of Control i3Neurons, we found a pattern in which mature lysosomes are positioned near the nucleus with immature lysosomes distributed throughout (Figure 5, A-C; Supplemental Figure S2). In the cell bodies of JIP3 KO i3Neurons, more immature lysosomes appear to be positioned near the nucleus, with fewer mature lysosomes seeming to be in this same region (Figure 5, A, B, and D; Supplemental Figure S2). However, this appearance could at least in part be due to the dramatic expansion of immature lysosomes (Figure 1F, Figure 2). A role for JIP3 in regulating axonal lysosome positioning and distribution has been well-established (Abe et al., 2009; Edwards et al., 2013; Gowrishankar et al., 2017, 2021). A highly related adaptor, JIP4, has been shown to affect dynein-mediated retrograde lysosome transport in non-neuronal cells (Willett et al., 2017). We recently showed that loss of JIP4 in JIP3 KO i3Neurons resulted in an exacerbation of the axonal lysosomal accumulation, suggesting that the two adaptors have overlapping roles in regulating retrograde axonal lysosome transport (Gowrishankar et al., 2021). The lack of a strong/penetrant lysosome positioning defect in JIP3 KO soma, compared with the dramatic alterations in axons, could be because another adaptor, such as JIP4, has overlapping or even a more central role here in regulating the movement and positioning of these soma lysosomes. However, the endolysosomal trafficking and maturation defects in neuronal cell bodies arising from JIP3 loss that we report here could still contribute to JIP3-linked neurodevelopmental disorder (Iwasawa et al., 2019; Platzer et al., 2019; Paumier and Gowrishankar, 2024) and thus warrant further investigation.

Axons of JIP3 KO i3Neurons accumulate endolysosomal intermediate organelles

Multiple studies carried out in several model organisms have shown an accumulation of axonal lysosomal organelles upon loss of JIP3 function (Drerup and Nechiporuk, 2013; Edwards et al., 2013; Gowrishankar et al., 2017, 2021). Our EM studies here recapitulate this finding at the ultrastructural level in the JIP3 KO i3Neuronal model; we observed a highly penetrant and massive accumulation of immature endolysosomal intermediate organelles with intact cargo, some of which also appear to be multilamellar and contain autophagic material, in the axons of JIP3 KO as compared with Control and JIP3 Rescue i3Neurons (Figure 6, A-D). This data supports a conserved function of JIP3 in regulating retrograde trafficking of axonal E/L (Gowrishankar et al., 2017, 2021). It also offers a powerful model (iPSC-derived neurons in combination with ultrastructural studies) to investigate subtypes of axonal E/L organelles, a topic of immense interest in the cell biology and neurodegeneration fields (A. Kulkarni et al., 2018; Winckler et al., 2018; Roney et al., 2022; Paumier and Gowrishankar, 2024).

METHODS

i3Neuron culture

The JIP3 KO/MAPK8IP3 KO iPSC line (Gowrishankar et al., 2021) and the JIP3 Rescue iPSC line (Rafiq et al., 2022), both previously generated using CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing, were maintained in E8 media (Life Technologies) and passaged using Accutase (Corning). i3Neurons in this study were differentiated as previously described (C. Wang et al., 2017; Gowrishankar et al., 2021; Fernandopulle et al., 2018). MAPK8IP3 KO and Control cells were plated at a density of 60,000–100,000 cells per 35 mm glass-bottom dishes (MatTek Life Sciences) for electron microscopy or 35,000–45,000 per 12 mm glass coverslips (Fisher) for immunofluorescence. Glass was pre-coated with 0.1 mg/ml poly-L-ornithine (Sigma Aldrich) and 1 μg/ml mouse Laminin (Life Technologies). i3Neurons were cultured for 12–14 d/DIV in Cortical Neuronal Culture Medium (KO DMEM F12 [Life Technologies] with B27 supplement [Thermo Fisher], 10 ng/ml BDNF and NT3 [PeproTech], 1 μg/ml mouse Laminin and 2 μg/ml Doxycycline [Fisher BioReagents]).

Immunofluorescence of i3Neurons

i3Neurons differentiated for 12–14 d/DIV on 12 mm glass coverslips were processed for immunostaining as previously described (Gowrishankar et al., 2017). Antibodies used in this study: anti-hLAMP-1 (CST, #9091, Rb, 1:200) and anti-Tau (CST, #4019, Ms, 1:500). The nucleus was visualized using DAPI Fluoromount-G (SouthernBiotech).

Electron microscopy

DIV14 i3Neurons were fixed for one hour with 2%–2.5% Glutaraldehyde in their culture medium (described above). Cells were then post-fixed for 1 h with 2% OsO4, 2% K4Fe(CN)6 (Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.1M sodium cacodylate buffer, followed by dehydration with graded EtOH, and embedded in Embed 812 (all EM reagents from EMS). For BSA trafficking assays, i3Neurons were treated with BSA Gold Tracer, BSA-conjugated 6 nm gold beads (BSA-Au6; Aurion) as previously described for BSA-488 (Majumder et al., 2022; Snead and Gowrishankar, 2022). Briefly, BSA-Au6 were equilibrated in culture medium before being added to DIV13 i3Neuron dishes and left overnight. The i3Neurons were then fixed on DIV14 as described above. Fixed and embedded samples were processed on a Leica UC7 Ultramicrotome, 70 nm sections were collected on carbon-coated Formvar copper grids (EMS), and imaged on a JOEL JEM-Flash 1400 TEM operating at 80kV with an AMT-NanoSprint 1200S-B camera. Compartments were analyzed from one to three 70 nm sections of cell bodies where the nucleus was completely visible, ensuring that the largest /most complete portion of the soma was being evaluated. All statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism9; statistical tests are indicated in the respective figure legends.

Quantification of lysosome positioning

To account for differences in cell size, we measured the closest distance from the nuclear membrane to the organelle and divided that by the distance from the nuclear membrane to the plasma membrane, along the same angle/orientation as the organelle, to compute the normalized distance from the nucleus. Organelles in the narrow area surrounding the nucleus were not considered; only those within the fullest part of the perinuclear region were scored.

Quantification of organelle-filled dystrophies

Low magnification images (3000x) were taken of areas containing only neuronal processes. Each dystrophy containing accumulating endo-lysosomes within a process was scored and reported as “organelle-filled dystrophies per unit area”, with the unit area being 1808 μm2.
