CryoEMNet driven symmetry-aware molecular reconstruction through deep learning enhanced electron microscopy
Authors: Saksham Arora, Shin-Hung Pan, Sudhakar Kumar, Sunil K. Singh, Utkarsh Chauhan, Wadee Alhalabi, Varsha Arya, Bassma Saleh Alsulami, Ching-Hsien Hsu, Kwok Tai Chui, Brij B. Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
CryoEMNet introduces a symmetry-aware deep learning framework for molecular reconstruction in cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), achieving high-resolution and structurally consistent 3D reconstructions. By incorporating molecular symmetry constraints within deep learning-based reconstruction, CryoEMNet effectively mitigates challenges associated with noise, structural heterogeneity, and particle misalignment. The framework employs unsupervised learning and transfer learning techniques to refine molecular details and optimize particle orientations, resulting in improved reconstruction accuracy. Multi-trial evaluations demonstrate that CryoEMNet achieves an average resolution of 3.78 Å to 3.81 Å, consistently outperforming existing methods such as EMPIAR. This improvement enhances the interpretability of reconstructed density maps and facilitates more precise structural analysis. This advancement significantly improves the interpretability of the resulting density maps and enables more precise structural analyses. By harnessing symmetry-aware deep learning, CryoEMNet establishes a reliable and scalable methodology for cryo-EM reconstruction, further advancing progress in structural biology and biomedical research.

Introduction

Structural biology researchers now utilize cryo-electron microscopy as their main tool for studying biological molecules while obtaining brand-new insights into their structures. Any investigation using cryo-EM begins by freezing the studied samples within vitreous ice to create a layer which halts crystalline formation that would alter molecular structure. Scientists freeze the sample before exposing it to an electron beam which leads to recorded interactions during the procedure. Different projection images are obtained through sample rotation at multiple angles so computational processing creates detailed three-dimensional models.

Cryo-EM has demonstrated versatility in extending its studies from conventional drug discovery research and cellular investigations to both emerging virologic and biological molecular investigations. Cryo-EM provides researchers ability to monitor complex biological processes and extract crystallographic patterns fundamental for cellular functions. Structural biology has been significantly influenced by improved technological innovations together with refined computational models which made cryo-EM essential for the field.

Scientists use molecular reconstruction techniques from cryo-EM to study large biological structures precisely through combining two-dimensional projection images which come from various orientations. The computational method delivers exceptional spatial atom understanding thus revealing complex molecular structures with exceptional accuracy.

Molecular reconstruction takes place in steps starting from biological material immersion in rapid freezing techniques to protect its original state. The microscopic method proceeds by allowing electrons to pass through the frozen specimen which produces different two-dimensional projection images through multiple sample rotations. Processing computational data includes several sequence steps including initial image correction to fix warping effects and image integration through common features alignment before starting an iterative model quality and resolution improvement process. The resulting three-dimensional models provide intricate details of molecular structures, including secondary structural elements, ligand binding sites, and conformational changes. This rich structural information plays a crucial role in understanding how biological macromolecules work and how they interact with other molecules or ligands. In addition, cryo-electron microscopy makes it possible to perform comparative studies, helping researchers identify structural differences that occur under various experimental conditions or when different substrates or inhibitors are present.Fig. 1Workflow of Cryo-EM structural determination.

The integration of deep learning into cryo-EM workflows represents a significant advancement driven by the quest for higher resolution images and streamlined data analysis. Deep learning, a subset of machine learning, employs neural networks1,2 and automated machine learning3 trained on vast datasets for pattern recognition and predictions4. In the context of cryo-EM, deep learning algorithms offer notable advantages, including image denoising and artifact removal, thereby enhancing image quality and structural resolution. Furthermore, deep learning models demonstrate remarkable capabilities in automating critical tasks such as particle picking and iterative refinement of three-dimensional reconstructions5–7.

Given its requirement for computationally intensive image processing techniques, deep learning is indispensable to reduce the analysis and interpretation time of the voluminous datasets that are generated by cryo-EM. Deep learning trained models on large datasets are quite efficient in analyzing large amount of data and quickly provide a freshest view on molecular structures without any heavy human intervention. Furthermore, the inclusion of cryo-EM with artificial intelligence allows for real time data analysis8,9, creating more opportunity of informed decision making based on the collected data.

Cryo-EM and deep learning integration strongly represents a new era, characterized by high resolution, fast data analysis, and new insights into molecular structures and functions. This paper investigates the synergy between cryo-EM and deep learning and their remarkable contribution into the understanding of biological systems and sets the scene for future innovations in the field.

Deep learning models, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and variational autoencoders (VAEs), improve accuracy by performing automatic denoising and artifact removal, reducing the impact of low signal-to-noise ratios. Furthermore, deep learning-based feature extraction and pattern recognition facilitate more precise molecular reconstructions, leading to higher-resolution 3D models. In terms of efficiency, GPU acceleration and parallel computing in deep learning frameworks significantly reduce processing times compared to traditional statistical methods, making CryoEMNet a scalable solution for high-throughput Cryo-EM studies.

This paper is structured into several sections, each addressing a different aspect of the integration of cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) with deep learning. Section 2 discusses traditional and modern cryo-EM methods, highlighting the importance of deep learning in automating tasks and improving structural analysis. In Section 3, CryoEMNet is introduced, a framework that merges cryo-EM with deep learning to achieve accurate reconstructions. Section 4 presents a comparison of 3D density maps, demonstrating CryoEMNet’s consistently superior resolution and workflow efficiency. Finally, Section 5 emphasizes the transformative impact of CryoEMNet on structural biology, surpassing existing methodologies and accelerating analysis through deep learning. Throughout these sections, the paper illuminates the significant potential of this integration to advance our understanding of biological macromolecules.

Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has emerged as a pivotal technique for elucidating the intricate structures of biological macromolecules at near-atomic resolutions. Traditional methods in cryo-EM data processing have long relied on manual or semi-automated procedures, which are labor-intensive and susceptible to error, thereby impeding efficiency, reproducibility, and reliability. The sequential stages of conventional cryo-EM data processing, encompassing particle picking, 2D classification, and 3D reconstruction, have been subject to these limitations.Fig. 2Traditional methods for Cryo-EM data processing.

Traditional methods for Cryo-EM data processing.

The diagram 2 illustrates the sequential stages of traditional cryo-EM data processing, from raw image acquisition to final 3D reconstruction. Each stage, including particle picking, 2D classification, and 3D reconstruction, is depicted as a separate module with detailed annotations explaining the processes involved.

Particle picking, the initial stage in cryo-EM data processing, involves the identification and extraction of individual particle images from raw micrographs. Historically, this task has been predominantly performed manually or with partially automated techniques19, such as template matching algorithms, resulting in inefficiencies, biases, and inconsistencies. Subsequent 2D classification aims to group extracted particle images based on their similarities in 2D projections, yet traditional methods often entail time-consuming visual inspection and selection, lacking robustness in representing dataset variation. Finally, 3D reconstruction, critical for obtaining high-resolution structures, may suffer from limited accuracy and resolution, particularly in scenarios with low signal-to-noise ratios or structural heterogeneity.

In recent years, the integration of machine learning, particularly deep learning methodologies, has revolutionized cryo-EM workflows, addressing longstanding challenges and unlocking new opportunities for automation, efficiency, and accuracy. Machine learning algorithms20,21, notably convolutional neural networks (CNNs)22–24, have been instrumental in automating manual and semi-automatic processes such as particle picking, achieving near-perfect performance in particle discrimination and background noise removal. Furthermore, deep learning approaches have demonstrated superiority over traditional methods in structural analysis, artifact correction, and image denoising, leveraging their capacity for feature extraction and pattern recognition without explicit engineering. Leghari31 proposed a method to improve cognitive traits of down syndrome individual.

Deep learning algorithms have proven effective in denoising and artifact correction, enhancing the resolution and quality of cryo-EM reconstructions by mitigating noise and imaging artifacts. Moreover, deep learning-based frameworks have excelled in particle picking25, facilitating accurate detection of individual particles amidst noise or structural variation. Additionally, deep learning algorithms enable precise classification of particles based on shape characteristics26, shedding light on diverse conformational and functional states of biological macromolecules. Furthermore, iterative optimization procedures empowered by deep learning models have led to refined structures, augmenting the resolution and quality of cryo-EM reconstructions. Bouflous et al.27 proposed cloud based model for loade blancing.

Several deep learning-based frameworks for cryo-EM particle picking and reconstruction have been developed, including crYOLO28, Topaz29, and cryoGAN30, which have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance in automated particle detection and generative model-based 3D reconstruction workflows.

In this section, CryoEMNet, a system framework, is presented. It integrates cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) with cutting-edge deep learning techniques to enhance the reconstruction of molecular structures. CryoEMNet comprises several key components, each leveraging advanced deep learning methodologies to address specific challenges in cryo-EM image processing and 3D reconstruction. These components include the Image Enhancement Module (IEM), utilizing convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to enhance the clarity of cryo-EM images; the Alignment and Categorization Module (ACM), employing variational autoencoders (VAEs) for unsupervised feature learning and alignment; and the 3D Reconstruction Module (3RM), utilizing iterative optimization algorithms such as gradient descent to reconstruct three-dimensional structures from aligned and categorized cryo-EM images. Figure 3 illustrates the architecture of CryoEMNet, showcasing the integration of these components to achieve highly accurate molecular reconstructions.

For all experiments, three publicly available cryo-EM single-particle datasets were utilized to validate the reconstruction framework: EMPIAR-10345 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}-galactosidase), EMPIAR-10400 (SARS-CoV-2 spike glycoprotein bound to ACE2), and EMPIAR-10028 (80S ribosome complex). From the EMPIAR-10345 dataset, an initial particle stack containing approximately 17,838 micrograph-derived particle images was extracted following standard motion correction and contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation using MotionCor2 and CTFFIND4. Subsequent preprocessing and automated quality-based filtering yielded final particle subsets consisting of 16,000 to 17,800 particles for downstream single-particle analysis. No externally imposed initial 3D reference model was used in any reconstruction; instead, initial volumes were generated de novo through an ab initio reconstruction step integrated into the CryoEMNet pipeline. This step employed iterative stochastic gradient descent combined with symmetry-aware group convolutions to align particle orientations and produce an unbiased initial volume for further refinement. To establish a consistent benchmark for computational performance, reconstruction times were measured relative to baseline workflows executed on the same particle stacks using established pipelines such as cryoSPARC and RELION under identical box sizes, masking conditions, and refinement parameters. The phrasing “x% faster than EMPIAR” therefore refers specifically to the relative reduction in mean end-to-end processing time compared to conventional 3D reconstructions performed on the same EMPIAR datasets, rather than implying that EMPIAR itself constitutes a reconstruction method. These benchmarking details ensure transparent reporting of computational efficiency gains attributable to the proposed deep learning framework while preserving the comparability of particle counts, initial models, and reconstruction conditions across methods.

Figure 4 illustrates the technical architecture of CryoEMNet, composed of three primary modules. The pipeline begins with raw cryo-EM micrographs that undergo denoising and contrast enhancement via a convolutional neural network in the Image Enhancement Module (IEM). These enhanced images are then processed by the Alignment and Categorization Module (ACM), which uses variational autoencoders (VAEs) to learn low-dimensional representations that preserve orientation consistency and allow for clustering of structurally similar projections. Finally, the 3D Reconstruction Module (3RM) integrates symmetry-aware convolutional operations (e.g., group-equivariant CNNs) and iterative optimization (SGD/Adam) to generate high-fidelity 3D molecular density maps. The architecture ensures preservation of molecular symmetry, improved resolution, and scalable processing performance across large datasets.

Algorithm 1 outlines the step-by-step procedure of the CryoEMNet framework, starting from image enhancement, followed by alignment and categorization, and concluding with 3D reconstruction. Each step utilizes the defined functions and metrics to enhance, align, categorize, and reconstruct the cryo-EM images to obtain the final optimized 3D volume. To maintain symmetry throughout the reconstruction process, CryoEMNet applies group-equivariant CNNs (G-CNNs) that ensure that feature extraction remains invariant to molecular rotations and reflections. This allows the framework to detect symmetry-preserving transformations and incorporate them into the alignment process, leading to more structurally consistent reconstructions.

Particle stack details and baseline benchmarking

For all experiments, three publicly available cryo-EM single-particle datasets were utilized to validate the reconstruction framework: EMPIAR-10345 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}-galactosidase), EMPIAR-10400 (SARS-CoV-2 spike glycoprotein bound to ACE2), and EMPIAR-10028 (80S ribosome complex). From the EMPIAR-10345 dataset, an initial particle stack containing approximately 17,838 micrograph-derived particle images was extracted following standard motion correction and contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation using MotionCor2 and CTFFIND4. Subsequent preprocessing and automated quality-based filtering yielded final particle subsets consisting of 16,000 to 17,800 particles for downstream single-particle analysis. No externally imposed initial 3D reference model was used in any reconstruction; instead, initial volumes were generated de novo through an ab initio reconstruction step integrated into the CryoEMNet pipeline. This step employed iterative stochastic gradient descent combined with symmetry-aware group convolutions to align particle orientations and produce an unbiased initial volume for further refinement.

To establish a consistent benchmark for computational performance, reconstruction times were measured relative to baseline workflows executed on the same particle stacks using established pipelines such as cryoSPARC and RELION under identical box sizes, masking conditions, and refinement parameters. The phrasing “x% faster than EMPIAR” therefore refers specifically to the relative reduction in mean end-to-end processing time compared to conventional 3D reconstructions performed on the same EMPIAR datasets, rather than implying that EMPIAR itself constitutes a reconstruction method. This ensures transparent reporting of computational efficiency gains attributable to the proposed deep learning framework while preserving the comparability of particle counts, initial models, and reconstruction conditions across methods.

CryoEMNet architecture, loss functions, and training details

CryoEMNet consists of an encoder-decoder deep neural network integrating convolutional neural networks (CNNs) with a variational autoencoder (VAE) and group-equivariant CNN (G-CNN) modules for symmetry-aware processing. The decoder mirrors the encoder with transposed convolutions to reconstruct the 3D density map. Conventional approaches to molecular reconstruction

Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has become essential in structural biology for visualizing macromolecules at near-atomic resolution. Conventional workflows include standard image preprocessing steps such as motion correction and Contrast Transfer Function (CTF) estimation and correction, which compensate for sample drift and microscope-induced phase distortions, respectively.

Typically, these steps are handled by well-established tools such as MotionCor2 for motion correction and CTFFIND4 for CTF estimation. After preprocessing, particle picking and alignment are performed, followed by iterative 2D classification and 3D reconstruction using pipelines like RELION or cryoSPARC. These conventional methods rely heavily on manual parameter tuning and statistical alignment approaches, which can be time-consuming and sensitive to noise.

In our proposed approach, standard preprocessing including CTF correction is still performed externally using these robust tools to ensure high-quality input micrographs. CryoEMNet then integrates advanced deep learning modules for denoising, alignment, categorization, and symmetry-aware 3D reconstruction. Unlike conventional workflows, CryoEMNet automates these steps within a single end-to-end framework, improving efficiency and structural fidelity while leveraging symmetry priors.

By combining conventional preprocessing with modern deep learning-based reconstruction, CryoEMNet enhances resolution without bypassing critical corrections such as CTF estimation. This hybrid approach ensures that the strengths of standard cryo-EM practices are preserved while addressing their key limitations.

Sample preparation and data collection

The initial step in cryo-EM molecular reconstruction is the preparation of the biological sample. This involves rapidly freezing the sample in vitreous ice to preserve its native structure and prevent the formation of crystalline ice, which can disrupt imaging. The sample is then maintained at cryogenic temperatures during data collection.

Under the electron microscope, a beam of electrons is directed at the sample, producing 2D projection images as the sample is rotated. These images capture different views of the molecule, which are essential for the subsequent reconstruction process.

Conclusion and future work

To objectively demonstrate the effectiveness of CryoEMNet, we have incorporated comprehensive quantitative evaluation metrics throughout our experiments. Specifically, we report Gold Standard Fourier Shell Correlation (GSFSC) values for all reconstructed 3D density maps, achieving average resolutions between 3.78 Å and 3.81 Å for EMPIAR-10345, with even higher resolutions of 2.8 Å for the SARS-CoV-2 spike dataset and 2.9 Å for the ribosome dataset. These values surpass the baseline reconstructions of standard pipelines such as EMPIAR (4.86–4.94 Å), cryoSPARC (4.10–4.25 Å), and RELION (4.20–4.35 Å). Furthermore, CryoEMNet improves computational efficiency, reducing reconstruction times by 35–45% compared to conventional pipelines, as summarized in Tables 5, 6, 7 and 8. These results, supported by GSFSC resolution curves (see Fig. 6), confirm that CryoEMNet produces high-resolution, structurally consistent reconstructions while ensuring practical scalability and computational performance.

In summary, this work presents CryoEMNet as a robust and versatile deep learning framework that advances the capabilities of cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) for high-resolution molecular reconstruction. By integrating symmetry-aware architectures, unsupervised learning, and transfer learning, CryoEMNet effectively addresses challenges such as structural heterogeneity, low signal-to-noise ratios, and alignment inaccuracies. Our extensive experiments confirm that CryoEMNet consistently achieves higher resolution reconstructions compared to established pipelines, demonstrating average resolutions in the range of 3.78–3.81 Å across multiple datasets.

While CryoEMNet has shown promising results, several practical avenues remain for future development. First, we plan to expand the framework’s compatibility with heterogeneous and flexible macromolecular complexes by integrating adaptive modules for continuous conformational variability. Second, future versions will incorporate automated atomic model fitting directly within the reconstruction pipeline, bridging the gap between density maps and interpretable structural models. Third, we aim to extend CryoEMNet’s generalization capacity by training on larger, more diverse cryo-EM datasets, including ribosomes, membrane proteins, and virus particles.

In addition, real-time processing capabilities and GPU-optimized inference modules will be developed to allow faster turnaround times for large-scale high-throughput studies. We also intend to release an open-source implementation of CryoEMNet, enabling the community to reproduce, validate, and extend our work. Finally, we envision applying CryoEMNet in collaborative biomedical research settings, such as structure-guided drug discovery, protein engineering, and mapping of complex biomolecular assemblies.

Through these practical next steps, we expect CryoEMNet to contribute to advancing the frontiers of cryo-EM research and to support transformative discoveries in structural biology and beyond.
