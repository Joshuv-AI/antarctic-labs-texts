Evaluation of open-source robotic-assisted additive manufacturing equipment for additively manufactured three-dimensional curved structure
Authors: Quan Zhang, Jihong Wang, Zeyu Zhao, Xinwei Ye, Heng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Additive manufacturing (AM) technology is leading the revolution in the industrial sector. The robotic additive manufacturing equipment, with its high degree of flexibility and multi-directional manufacturing path capability, can break through the limitations of traditional equipment to produce more complex parts. In this paper, an open-source low-cost robotic-assisted AM framework is proposed. Through forward and reverse kinematic calculation, the position and attitude of the end actuator of the manipulator are precisely controlled. The six-axis robotic arm-based AM system was systematically designed and validated via targeted printing tasks. The results demonstrated that the open-source system performed better than the conventional three-axis system. The proposed robotic arm achieved a deposition speed of 128 ± 5 mm/s (p < 0.01 compared to 65 ± 8 mm/s, n = 10). Through employing a six-degree-of-freedom (6-DoF) motion path algorithm, it achieved efficient 6-DoF support-free printing and highly reduced printing time by 43.7%. It is highlighted that robotic-assisted AM is a transformative example of intelligent manufacturing, which enables high-speed, high-accuracy manufacturing processes of complex geometries.

Introduction

In recent years, the convergence of robotics and Additive Manufacturing (AM) has emerged as a transformative research frontier, offering significant advancements in manufacturing precision, flexibility, and efficiency1. This paper presents a structurally rigorous and technically in-depth investigation into a robotic-assisted AM system based on a 6-Degree-of-Freedom (DOF) manipulator. The core contribution of this work lies in the systematic integration and experimental validation of three pivotal areas: the design and sophisticated control of a robotic arm2, the innovative configuration of AM nozzles, and the fabrication of intricate three-dimensional (3D) structures with subsequent performance evaluation3. By holistically addressing these aspects, this research significantly advances hybrid manufacturing systems that synergize robotic manipulation with layer-by-layer deposition, thereby expanding the frontiers of contemporary manufacturing4. The implications are profound, pointing toward a future of on-demand, decentralized production. Such robotic AM systems hold the potential to construct everything from customized biomedical implants and disaster-relief shelters to optimized aerospace components and site-specific architectural forms, directly from digital models with minimal human intervention—a vision this work helps to realize.

The research commences with the development of a hierarchical supervisory control system using LabVIEW for the upper-computer interface. This system is crucial for monitoring operational states and acquiring data, ensuring the seamless and reliable operation of the robotic arm. It rigorously validates both manual and automatic modes by accurately tracking motor revolutions and joint angles. Furthermore, it synchronizes these critical parameters with MATLAB for iterative input control, thereby enabling precise and dynamic adjustments to the robotic arm’s movements5. The strategic integration of PLCs into the electrical system bolsters the control architecture, enabling precise motor reversal control and providing visual feedback via integrated indicator lights6. PLC programming, leveraging Siemens TIA Portal, facilitates intuitive manual control, while automatic control is achieved through sophisticated timer-based sequences derived from manual operation data, thus optimizing cyclic printing operations7. This proposed hybrid control strategy uniquely combines the low-level robustness of PLCs with the high-level computational power of MATLAB and the intuitive interface of LabVIEW. Its efficacy was demonstrated through the fabrication of a large-scale, sinusoidal wave-like structure, where a manual teaching process in TIA Portal established a baseline path for subsequent automated execution8.

Following the establishment of the control architecture, the robotic arm’s mechanical components underwent meticulous engineering and optimization. The motor selection process strategically adopted 28- and 42-type stepper motors with integrated reducers, complemented by detailed calculations for worm gear and belt drives to ensure optimal torque and speed characteristics9. Material selection for structural housings and fasteners emphasized a high strength-to-weight ratio and fatigue resistance—critical properties for withstanding the repetitive stresses inherent in AM cycles10. To rigorously validate the design, a joint simulation approach using MATLAB and ANSYS was employed11. A dynamic model of the motor-driven arm was constructed in MATLAB to accurately determine joint forces and torques under various operational conditions. These loads were then seamlessly integrated into ANSYS for stress analysis of the most heavily loaded joints, alongside comprehensive kinematic simulations to predict and analyze movement patterns12. Modal analysis provided valuable insights into the arm’s dynamic response to external forces and vibrations, identifying the rigidity and durability requirements essential for high-precision robotic AM and informing subsequent dynamic analyses of the full system under operational loads13.

The development and integration of the AM printing nozzle constituted another core research component. Its effective operation demands precise spatiotemporal coordination with the robotic manipulator’s motion to ensure high-fidelity material deposition14. System performance was validated through targeted experiments confirming robust communication and synchronized action between the robotic controller and the nozzle’s extrusion system—a prerequisite for fabricating components with intricate features15. A key benchmark test involved the non-planar, support-free printing of smooth, doubly-curved surfaces. This demonstration not only assessed the system’s ability to execute complex toolpaths but also validated its geometric versatility, showcasing a clear advantage over conventional 3D printing methods16. Ultimately, the research culminated in the formulation of a governing mathematical model derived from the extensive experimental and simulation datasets17. This proposed model uniquely integrates the coupled dynamics and kinematics of the robotic arm with the functional parameters of the deposition process, explicitly accounting for the novel PLC-based control architecture and bespoke mechanical design. The resulting framework provides a quantitative basis for analyzing system behavior and serves as a predictive tool for future optimization, laying the groundwork for next-generation, intelligent hybrid manufacturing platforms18.

While the integration of robotics and AM represents a transformative frontier, enabling unprecedented geometric freedom and multi-axis deposition, conventional platforms often rely on expensive, proprietary industrial manipulators and monolithic control architectures. This limits accessibility and flexibility for research and customized applications. This paper directly addresses these critical challenges by developing and validating a proposed robotic-assisted framework built upon a 6-DOF manipulator. The comprehensive methodology spans from foundational kinematic and dynamic modeling using Denavit-Hartenberg (D-H) parameters and the Lagrange formulation to the implementation of a novel, hierarchical supervisory control system. A key novelty of this work is the unique integration of PLCs for robust, low-level execution with MATLAB for advanced trajectory computation and iterative learning control, all harmonized through a LabVIEW interface. The framework’s practical value is empirically demonstrated through the successful fabrication of intricate, support-free curved geometries with high accuracy, enabled by a high-resolution stepper motor drive system and precise PID joint control. By synergizing mechanical design, dynamic simulation, intelligent path optimization, and experimental process validation, this research provides a foundational and timely methodology for advancing flexible, high-performance, and accessible hybrid manufacturing systems.

Design and manufacturing of the robot-assisted additive manufacturing machine

Selection of D-H parameters

The Denavit-Hartenberg (D-H) parametric method is a standardised technique employed extensively in the kinematic modelling of robotic arms and robots. The fundamental principle of the method is to establish chi-square transformations between joint coordinate systems by defining the four geometric parameters that describe the relative poses of adjacent connecting rods19. It provides a unified mathematical framework for analysing both forward kinematics and inverse kinematics. The method effectively reduces the dimensionality and computational complexity of kinematic modelling by simplifying the complex multi-joint system into a series of combinations of geometrical parameters of adjacent connecting rods, as shown in Fig. 1.

Schematic diagram of Denavit-Hartenberg (D-H) conventions.

The Denavit-Hartenberg (D-H) parametric method defines the relationship between the motions of neighbouring connecting rods (Fig. 1). This method enables the spatial position between two neighbouring connecting rods to be determined uniquely by four geometrical parameters: the joint angle (θ), the connecting rod deflection (d), the length of the connecting rods (a), and the angle of the connecting rods’ torsion (α). The method describes the relative motion of the connecting rods by establishing a chi-square transformation matrix (in the form of a 4 × 4 matrix) between neighbouring joint coordinate systems, where the matrix elements are determined by a combination of the above parameters and the geometrical relationship between the joint axis of rotation (Z-axis) and the axis of the connecting rods (X-axis). Local Kinematic Relationship Between Adjacent Joints The red-boxed schematic illustrates the Denavit-Hartenberg (DH) parameterisation governing two consecutive joints (Joint i and Joint i + 1) in a robotic arm. Key parameters include link offset (di, translation along the Z-axis of Frame {i-1}), link length (a.i., distance between Z-axes along Xi), twist angle (αi, rotation about Xi aligning Zi-1 to Zi), and joint angle (θi, rotation about Zi-1 aligning Xi-1 to Xi). This local coordinate transformation is formalised via a homogeneous matrix Tiⁱ(i-1) = Rot(Z, θi)·Trans(Z, di)·Trans(X, a.i.)·Rot(X, αi), which sequentially combines rotation and translation operations to map Frame i to Frame (i-1). Such parameterisation ensures minimal serial-chain kinematics representation, enabling systematic forward kinematics derivation through matrix concatenation.

In studying robotic arm control systems, kinematic analysis and dynamics modelling constitute two core levels of the theoretical system21. The core research object of kinematic analysis is to establish the mathematical mapping relationship between the spatial position, including position, velocity and acceleration of the end actuator and the motion parameters of each joint. Kinetic modelling expands the research dimension by focusing on analysing the kinetic effects of system loads and driving forces, as well as the coupling mechanism between them and the motion states. In complex trajectory-tracking tasks involving multiple degrees of freedom, developing a synergistic kinematics and dynamics model has become imperative for achieving high-precision motion control22.

Dynamic modelling of the robotic-assisted arm

This paper proposes a novel approach that combines the D-H parameters with the Lagrange method for the modelling of the dynamics of a robotic arm. The number of degrees of freedom of the robotic arm is equivalent to the number of dynamic equations when the end of the robotic arm is unconstrained23. The apparatus under consideration features a Cartesian design and structural decoupling characteristics that allow for the independent control of three axes. Rotary joints are a key component in creating an unstructured workspace, characterised by multiple rotational degrees of freedom. Horizontal joints are designed to satisfy the requirements of planar precision assembly through the synergistic movement of rotation and translation in the horizontal plane.

Hardware design

In the structural design of the robotic arm body, the first three joints utilise a belt and gear reduction structure. The input shaft of the motor combination is directly connected to the belt, which in turn is connected to the output shaft, transferring the corresponding torque29. The belt drive can absorb a proportion of the vibration and thereby reduce the impact on the precision of the robotic arm. Furthermore, it can bypass the complex mechanical structure and achieve long-distance power transmission. The stepping motor, input shaft gear, and the motor equipped with a planetary reducer constitute the input component of the system. The output shaft and gear are connected through a key connection drive, while the input and output shafts are connected via a belt. Finally, the output shaft and chassis are connected employing a bolt. The chassis has been designed with mechanical stability and expandability, allowing it to be securely fixed to the holes reserved in the lab bench or connected to other equipment.The configuration of joint 1 is illustrated schematically in Fig. 2 in the Supplementary.

In the design of the robotic arm drive system, joints 2 to 4 employ the planar asynchronous drive scheme, identical to that of joint 1, which is based on the synchronous belt drive system to achieve torque transfer. The difference is only reflected in the topological distribution of the spatial attitude of the mechanism plane (see Fig. 3 for details of the spatial configuration analysis). Here, this paper focuses on designing an asymmetric drive system for joint 5, as illustrated in Fig. 3 in the Supplementary. In consideration of the aforementioned factors, joint 5 employs a two-stage deceleration composite transmission architecture, comprising a worm gear reduction mechanism in the primary stage (deceleration ratio i = 15:1) and a secondary coupling synchronous belt wheel deceleration system in the secondary stage (deceleration ratio i = 3:1). Due to the spatial limitations of the structure, the final segment of the arm was selected for direct connection to the end-effector, the 3D printing motor, and the printing head.

Software design

The robotic-assisted arm communication system architecture is proposed that seamlessly integrates a LabVIEW-based host computer for user interface and high-level control logic. The Siemens S7-1200 PLC serves as the lower-level machine to ensure real-time execution, and a MATLAB environment for processing complex algorithms. The data transmission process begins with LabVIEW transmitting control commands to the PLC via the OPC UA protocol, which includes target position coordinates and velocity settings. Subsequently, the PLC forwards raw sensor data and associated parameters, acquired through the OPC interface, to MATLAB for computational analysis. It generates optimised control outputs, such as pulse sequences, direction signals, and stepping parameters, which are then fed back to the PLC. Subsequently, the PLC utilises digital output or pulse output (PTO) modules to generate precise pulse signals, which drive the stepper motor driver to achieve accurate motion control of the robotic arm’s joints. To implement closed-loop regulation, the feedback mechanism incorporates encoder signals to correct the system state dynamically. The communication system architecture is illustrated in Fig. 4. It demonstrates the collaborative integration of multi-level heterogeneous components, thereby significantly enhancing the precision, responsiveness, and overall performance of the robotic-assisted system.

To minimise non-extrusion idle strokes during printing, thereby enhancing manufacturing efficiency and print quality, this study proposes and implements an improved contour path algorithm, as shown in Fig. 11. The core process of the proposed algorithm is introduced. Firstly, the continuous grid-filling paths generated after layer slicing are segmented into a series of discrete line segment units. Subsequently, an enhanced greedy strategy is employed to reorder the sequence of line segments globally. This strategy adheres to a ‘nearest neighbour priority’ principle, consistently selecting the unconnected line segment with the shortest Euclidean distance from the current segment endpoint as the next printing target. To exploit optimisation, the algorithm incorporates a bidirectional search mechanism. When selecting the next segment, it evaluates not only its starting point but also the distance between its endpoint and the current endpoint, thereby choosing the more advantageous of the two possible printing directions for connection.

The mechanism effectively avoids redundant idle movements caused by fixed printing directions. To maintain topological integrity between different contour regions and clearly identify segment boundaries within the data structure, the algorithm inserts specific delimiters into the path sequence. This segmentation approach ensures the coherence and processability of complex infill region paths. A key analytical innovation lies in the decoupling of path metrics evaluation. The algorithm separately calculates and records both the print distance (the deposition path when the extruder is active) and the idle distance (the rapid movement path when the extruder is inactive). This separation of metrics allows the optimisation objective to focus precisely on reducing ineffective motion, thereby providing a more refined metric for evaluating algorithmic performance.

The dynamics of a robotic arm are intrinsically linked to its motor drive system. The dynamic characteristics dictate the requirements and selection criteria for the motor drive performance, while the motor drive system, in turn, enables precise and stable motion of the robotic arm by providing adequate driving torque and control accuracy. Consequently, the analysis of dynamics can be bifurcated into motor drive analysis and direct dynamic analysis. In the context of motor drive analysis, to enhance efficiency and streamline the computational process, the end-effector trajectory is often simplified into a sinusoidal surface with an amplitude of 5, a frequency of 1, and a height of 1. This simplification allows for a more focused examination of the motor’s response and performance under controlled conditions. The results of the motor drive analysis are presented in Figs. 14, 15 and 16. These figures illustrate various aspects of the motor’s behaviour, such as torque output, speed variation, and efficiency, as the robotic arm follows the prescribed sinusoidal trajectory. The robotic-assisted additive manufacturing system successfully fabricated complex curved specimens, as shown in Fig. 19, with distinct surface contours visible in the three-dimensional model in Fig. 19. The capability of the equipment was validated through the production of specimens with varying layer thicknesses, from 0.1 mm to 0.2 mm. The robotic arm achieves millimetre printing accuracy through a synergistic integration of a 28-step stepper motor and a 3.71:1 planetary gearbox. By reducing the motor’s inherent step angle (1.8°/step) to 0.485°/step through gear reduction, the system enhances motion resolution by 3.71 times, enabling micron-level end-effector displacement increments. This micro-stepping mechanism, combined with torque amplification from the gearbox and closed-loop control, mitigates step loss and inertial-induced layer misalignment—common limitations in direct-drive systems. Experimental validation using 1600 and 2400 steps/revolution configurations demonstrates improved surface finish and dimensional tolerances. The design prioritises mechanical simplicity by focusing optimisation on the final joint’s step-angle control, ensuring robust performance in high-resolution extrusion tasks.

The cornerstone of this study is the precise modulation of printing fidelity by leveraging the micro-stepping resolution of the stepper motors. Figure 12(a) illustrates the impact of different angular resolutions on the resulting surface quality. A comparison between 1,600 and 2,400 steps-per-revolution reveals distinct variations in seamline consistency, where the 1,600-step configuration demonstrates superior path continuity. Building upon this, the printing precision was further enhanced by optimizing the layer thickness. As shown in Fig. 12(b), reducing the layer height from 0.2 mm to 0.1 mm effectively minimizes the staircase effect, achieving a high-fidelity surface finish that satisfies the smoothness requirements for complex geometries.

While the robotic-assisted additive manufacturing system demonstrates promising capabilities in trajectory planning and dynamic control, it is subject to several practical limitations and potential failure modes inherent to its mechanical and control design. The system’s reliance on stepper motors with belt and worm gear transmissions introduces concerns regarding backlash and compliance, which can manifest as positional inaccuracies during directional reversals or under variable loads, degrading print quality in high-precision contours. Singularity points within the 6-DOF arm’s workspace, particularly when the wrist joints are aligned, can lead to uncontrolled joint velocities and a loss of dexterity, necessitating careful path planning to avoid. Vibrations induced by the stepper motor micro-stepping and transmission elements may cause resonance, especially at certain operational speeds, leading to surface artifacts on printed parts. Furthermore, thermal drift in both the motors and the mechanical structure over extended print durations can alter the accuracy of the kinematic model.

Meanwhile, calibration errors in the D-H parameters or end-effector offset can propagate through forward and inverse kinematics, causing systematic deviations. Without a fully integrated closed-loop feedback system at all joints, relying instead on open-loop control for some of the system is also vulnerable to step loss and accumulated positional error under unexpected loads or friction changes, which can potentially lead to layer shifting or print failures in complex, long-duration jobs. Conclusions

In this paper, a new multi-task robotic-assisted AM framework based on a 6-DOF robot arm is proposed in the field of advanced additive manufacturing (AM), and the feasibility and effectiveness of the framework are verified through a series of detailed designs, analyses and experiments. In terms of the design and kinematics analysis of the manipulator, a 6-DOF manipulator is designed in this paper. The forward and reverse kinematic models of the manipulator are constructed by using the D-H parameter method, which provides a theoretical basis for the precise control of the manipulator. Forward kinematics analysis reveals that the position and attitude of the manipulator’s end-effector can be determined from the joint space parameters. In terms of dynamic modelling and control, this paper combines D-H parameters and the Lagrange method to establish a dynamic model of the robot arm. It derives the kinetic energy, potential energy, and Lagrange function of the robot arm in detail, and then obtains the dynamic equation of the robot arm. In the control system, Robotics Toolbox and SimMechanics Link simulation tools in MATLAB software were used to simulate the kinematics and dynamics of the manipulator. Through the application of the PID control algorithm, the precise control of joint position, speed and acceleration of the manipulator is realised, and the stability and control accuracy of the system are improved.
