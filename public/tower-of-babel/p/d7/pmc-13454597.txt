Efficacy of Sr2⁺ and Cu2⁺ Ion Incorporation on the Mechanical, biological, and antibacterial properties of natural waste-derived borosilicate bioactive glasses for orthopaedic implants
Authors: Locherla Daloji, Raghavendra Gujjala, Shakuntala Ojha, G. Dheeraj Kumar, Prakash Saudagar, Manuela Reben, P. Syam Prasad, Aswani Kumar Bandaru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The inclusion of the metal based therapeutic ions into bioactive glasses received potential towards tissue regeneration applications due to their excellent biocompatible, mechanical and wear properties. In the current work, one such configuration is chosen, strontium (Sr) and Copper (Cu) ions based bioactive borosilicate glass system i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_{2} O_{3} - \left( {20 - y - z} \right)SiO_{2} - Na_{2} O - CaO - \left( y \right)CuO - \left( z \right)SrO$$\end{document} with varying composition of Sr and Cu for 0, 1 and 2 wt.%. The glasses were prepared through melt quench process and the as prepared specimens are characterized for their microstructural, mechanical, tribological, biocompatible and biological investigations. The results were found to appealing with microstructure and crystallographic peaks displaying the presence of extracted natural Silica (Si) and Calcium (Ca) powders. Further the same specimens are characterized for their mechanical strength and it was found to be 55.21MPa compressive strength and lowest specific wear rate overall for CuSr-1 wt.% composition. Being CuSr-1% displaying better mechanical properties, the same samples i.e., Cu, Sr for 1wt.%, were investigated for their biocompatible studies through Simulated Body Fluid immersion protocol. The SBF studies indicate the formation of hydroxyapatite that can be inferred from crystallographic peaks and Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy. The samples were also exposed to antibacterial studies against Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S.aureus) and are found to exhibit excellent antibacterial activity. The observed selective antibacterial activity highlights the potential of these bioactive glasses as advanced materials for load-bearing orthopaedic implants and bone tissue engineering scaffolds, where the simultaneous requirements of mechanical robustness and antimicrobial efficacy are critical for clinical success.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50704-x.

Introduction

Biomaterials are found to be predominant in the field of the tissue engineering and regeneration over decades1,2. Amongst these, bioactive glass has been one of the keen areas of research for the scientists in the pursuit towards bone tissue engineering and its regeneration. Ever since the development of the first artificial material with an ability to form chemical bond with the bone by Larry Hench in 1969 in the University of Florida which was later termed as 45S5 Bioglass, henceforth earmarked to a new research field of bioactive glasses3. With decades of researches and advent of new technologies, various fabrication techniques, new and modified matrix in lieu of silica-based matrix have led to the development of various subgroups and classification of bioactive glasses. They have been further classified as borate-based (B2O3), silica-based (SiO2) and Phosphate-based (P2O5) on the basis of major network former in the glass matrix4. Fabrication techniques such as solgel method from wet chemistry, melt quench, flame synthesis, microwave irradiation, powder sintering have also been in existence and new advancements have also been devised5. With the advent of technologies, further, the bioactive glass have provision to incorporate specific dopants such as Cu, Na, Ag, Zn, B, F, Mg, Ce, Ti, Co, Fe, Li, Sr, etc.6 as trace element in the glass matrix envelope the potential to tailor the properties such as osteoblast activity, osteoclast activity, bone metabolism, structure stabilization, angiogenesis, mechanical properties, and biocompatibility, anti-microbial activity7,8. Though, in vivo studies of bioactive glass have indicated comparatively faster bond formation rate than bio-ceramics, and invitro studies have predicted their osteogenic properties via osteoprogenitor cells stimulation at the genetic level, in terms of commercial success, they lag behind bio ceramics9. Consequently, substantial amount of research has been carried out to enhance the potential of the bioactive glass in terms of biocompatibility, bone-tissue interfacing, degradation rate, fabrication process, manufacturing and production cost reduction10.

This work differs from earlier studies on Cu–Sr co-doped phosphate or silicate bioactive glasses primarily through the use of a borosilicate network, which modifies glass structure, dissolution behaviour, and therapeutic ion release. Unlike silicate glasses, where Cu and Sr mainly act as network modifiers influencing antibacterial and osteogenic responses, the incorporation of B₂O₃ alters network connectivity and accelerates yet controllable degradation, enabling a more balanced and sustained release of Cu2⁺ and Sr2⁺ ions11,12. Compared with phosphate glasses, which often show rapid dissolution and limited long-term stability, borosilicate systems provide improved structural integrity while still promoting bioactivity13. Additionally, the presence of boron introduces distinct biological effects, including enhanced angiogenic and osteogenic signalling, which are not typically reported in Cu–Sr co-doped silicate or phosphate glasses alone14,15.

Amongst all the bioactive glasses explored, borosilicate bioactive glass offer certain unique potentials such as compositional flexibility of dopants, controlled degradation, mechanical stability, tailorable bioactivity, thermal stability, lower risk of crystallization, multifunctional application and has bioactive boron benefits16,17. The in-vivo & in-vitro studies on the borosilicate glass as a tool for bone tissue regrowth and damage repair have indicated excellent bioactivity in terms of simulating bone tissue regeneration and formation of extensive bond with bone at the molecular level with optimized rate offering healing and degradation in synchronization18. The composition of the borosilicate bioactive glass is primarily composed of silica oxide (SiO2), boron oxide(B2O3), Sodium oxide (Na2O), and Calcium Oxide (CaO)19.

Studies from Shaowen Cheng et.al.20reported that boron could enhance osteogenic effect by stimulating osteogenic differentiation-related marker gene synthesis during the proliferation and differentiation phase in human bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs) and could be a promising approach for enhancing osteogenic capacity of cell-based construction in bone tissue engineering. In addition, the in-vitro studies evident that, Si alone can promote osteogenic differentiation, cell proliferation, and provide desirable gene expression for bone regeneration21–23. Incorporating substantial amounts of transition metal oxides (TMOs) into bioactive glasses enables the tuning of their properties for targeted applications, improving mechanical performance while also providing antimicrobial activity. Among various metal oxides, the incorporation of strontium (Sr) and copper (Cu) into bioactive glasses can improve their functionality. Specifically, Sr enhances osteoblast activity and suppresses bone resorption by osteoclasts, whereas Cu provides pro-angiogenic and antibacterial effects. For instance, Yann C. Fredholm et.al24 studied the effects of the Sr2+ substituted bioactive glass on osteoblasts and osteoclasts and fabricated the series of BGs (SiO2–P2O5–Na2O–CaO) in which calcium was substituted by strontium due to their charge and ionic radius similarity. Their in-vitro studies evident that strontium-substituted BG promotes anabolic effects on osteoblasts and an anti-catabolic effect on osteoclasts, promoting apatite formation and inhibiting bone resorption. In addition, Sadegh Firoozi et.al25 reported about the antibacterial effect of Sr substituted 58S BG against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) bacteria. Their study was based on elucidating the effects on bioactivity, alkaline phosphatase, biocompatibility & antibacterial activity of the Sr substituted, sol–gel derived 58S-BG and to suggest the optimal content of SrO in BG-58S composition to achieve the highest cell proliferation and activity as well as antibacterial efficiency against MRSA.

On the other hand, studies investigating Cu-doped silica-based glasses by Marta Miola et.al26 reported improved antibacterial efficacy against common pathogens like S.aureus and E.coli highlighting the antimicrobial effect against fungi, gram negative and gram positive bacteria over a broad spectrum. Peiman B. Milan et.al27 in their studies of vascular grafts containing modified BGs indicated the better chemical stability and degradation rate of borate-based BGs for endothelialization and angiogenesis. And also highlighted that the melt-derived, Cu modified BGs enhance the vasculogenic and angiogenesis factors accelerating tissue generation patient-specific vascular prostheses. Alongside from bioactivity and physicochemical stability, tribological performance is also a critical consideration for bioactive glasses to be used in biomedical applications. Glass-based composite or coatings in implants may endure sliding, abrasion, and contact stresses at implantation and service and lead to wear and the formation of debris28. Therefore, tribological performance testing of doped and co-doped glasses assists in determining their reliability and biological safety over an extended period.

In addition, some studies have demonstrated that co-doped bioactive glasses with combining the angiogenic and antimicrobial benefits of copper with the osteogenic effects of strontium28. This co-doping strategy has been shown to improve the material’s performance in bone tissue engineering applications. Hanna Kankova et.al29 developed Cu and Sr co-doped mesoporous silicate based MBGs and highlighted the influence of Copper-Strontium co-doping on bioactivity, cytotoxicity and antibacterial activity and found that, the overall properties are enhanced. However, only a few studies have focused on multifunctional Cu–Sr co-doped bioactive glasses prepared by either melt quenching or the sol–gel route. In the previous studies, doping Cu and Sr improved bioactivity and hydroxyapatite formation. Wherein Sr act as network modifier that enhances the glass dissolution thus aids to enhanced hydroxyapatite layer formation. Whereas, Cu can further influence surface reactions and protein adsorption. Together, they improve bioactive surface layer formation, promoting better cell attachment and bonding with native bone. Cu provides strong antibacterial activity, reducing infection risk at the implantation site. Sr helps stabilize the glass network and improves mechanical integrity.

Methodology

Extraction of silica and calcium oxide from natural waste

Waste-derived silica and CaO differ from conventional high-purity chemical precursors primarily in terms of sustainability, resource efficiency, and functional bioactivity, while maintaining comparable or even enhanced biological performance. From a sustainability perspective, waste-derived sources (e.g., agricultural residues, industrial by-products) significantly reduce reliance on energy-intensive mining and chemical purification processes. Their use lowers carbon footprint, diverts waste from landfills, and supports circular-economy principles, aligning well with the growing emphasis on environmentally responsible biomaterials development. In terms of bioactivity, waste-derived silica and CaO often contain trace biologically relevant elements (e.g., Mg, Na, K, P) that are absent in ultra-pure commercial precursors. These trace ions can synergistically enhance glass dissolution behaviour, promote hydroxyapatite formation, and stimulate osteogenic cell responses. For bone tissue regeneration, such multi-ionic release profiles are advantageous, as they more closely mimic the ionic complexity of natural bone mineral environments.

The raw rice hull was collected from the rice mills and washed using distilled water. This washing process removes physical impurities like dust, soil, and organic residues to have a cleaner raw material for silica extraction. After the cleaning process, the rice husk is treated with hydrochloric acid (HCl). The mixture is filtered in order to separate the acid solution from the treated rice husk. This process eliminates the acid and dissolved impurities, leaving only the purified husk. The process of extracting calcium oxide (CaO) from eggshells comprises a series of thermal and mechanical treatment processes to transform the calcium carbonate content in the eggshells to calcium oxide. Initially, waste eggshells were collected and well cleaned with hot water to eliminate organic residues and membranes. The shells were dried in an oven after cleaning to remove moisture. Once dried those shells were grinded to get fine powder, which results in calcium carbonate (CaCO₃). The eggshell powder is then calcinated at 900 °C for 6 h in a muffle furnace. The calcium carbonate decomposes at high temperature to give calcium oxide (CaO). The importance of such materials to use in the fabrication of BGs is material uniqueness and bioactivity, similarly due to their sustainability and cost effectiveness.

Bioactive glass synthesis

The borosilicate glass samples fabrication through melt-quenching is described in the Fig. 2a in the initial step the precursor powders were precisely weighed and properly grinded with pestle mortar to get homogeneity. The powder mixture is then poured into a crucible and melted in a high-temperature muffle furnace at 1100 °C to obtain a uniform molten glass. At the same time, a metal mould is preheated at 400 °C to avoid thermal shock. After the molten glass achieves the desired viscosity and homogeneity, it is slowly poured into the preheated mould. immediately moved to another furnace that was maintained at 300 °C for 3 h and allowed to cool slowly to room temperature to form a dense, cylindrical borosilicate glass (BSG) sample. This fast-quenching process contributes towards the preservation of the amorphous structure and efficient enhancement of the material’s bioactive and mechanical properties for biomedical use. On the other hand, Porosity and trapped gas bubbles were minimized through careful control of raw material preparation and melting parameters. The precursors were thoroughly dried and finely ground to ensure compositional homogeneity and to reduce gas evolution during heating. A stepwise melting schedule with an initial decarbonation stage was employed to allow complete removal of CO₂ and other volatile species before reaching the final melting temperature. The melts were held at high temperature for sufficient time to promote bubble coalescence and escape, followed by casting onto a preheated mold and controlled annealing to prevent thermal shock and residual porosity. This combination of precursor conditioning, optimized thermal treatment, and controlled cooling effectively reduced trapped gas bubbles and bulk porosity in the final glasses.

Statistical analysis

Experiments were performed at a fixed sliding distance of 200 m and a sliding velocity of 1 m/s, but systematically applied load (5, 10, and 15 N), track radius (30, 45, and 60 mm), and material composition varied. Every test was carried out twice for reproducibility purposes and the mean of the two readings was utilized for analysis. The repeated measurement variation was kept within ± 3% to provide experimental consistency. The signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio was calculated to analyse the performance characteristics with a view to minimizing SWR), according to the "smaller-the-better" criterion. Biocompatibility studies

To assess the in-vitro bioactivity of the synthesized bioactive glasses, the simulated body fluid (SBF) was prepared according to the standard method established by Kokubo31, which closely simulates the ionic composition and concentration of human blood plasma. The glass powders were submerged in the prepared SBF solution with a concentration of 1.5 mg/mL, where there was adequate contact between the glass particles and the physiological medium32. This arrangement was intended to allow ion exchange and the subsequent growth and nucleation of hydroxyapatite on the glass surfaces so that their bioactivity could be evaluated under physiological-like conditions. In the present work, Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) was done using a Bruker Alpha II spectrometer with the KBr pellet technique to obtain spectra between 500–4000 cm−1. Powder X-ray Diffraction (XRD) was performed using a PANalytical X’Pert Powder diffractometer with CuKα radiation, scanned from 10° to 60° with a step size of 0.02° and a counting time of 50 s per step.

Melt quenching at 1100 °C produces glasses with a highly homogeneous and dense network, as the elevated temperature ensures complete melting, dissolution of precursors, and removal of residual organics or carbonates. As a result, melt-quenched glasses generally exhibit fewer intrinsic defects, such as closed porosity, residual stresses, or unreacted phases, compared to sol–gel–derived glasses, which often retain nanoporosity, hydroxyl groups, and microstructural heterogeneities due to low-temperature processing. In sol–gel systems, these features can act as defect sites that promote cracking and reduce mechanical strength, although they may enhance bioactivity. Therefore, melt quenching at 1100 °C favors structural integrity and mechanical reliability, while sol–gel processing prioritizes high surface area and reactivity at the expense of increased defect density.

Mechanical properties

Based on the hardness values shown in Fig. 4c, the Cu- and/or Sr-doped borosilicate glasses exhibit hardness values in the range of 5.39–5.71 GPa. In comparison, benchmark silicate bioactive glasses such as 45S5 Bioglass® typically report Vickers hardness values of 4.5–5.0 GPa, while 58S sol–gel-derived glasses are generally lower, around 4.0–4.6 GPa, due to their higher porosity and lower network connectivity. Quantitatively, this corresponds to an approximate 8–25% enhancement in hardness relative to 45S5 and a 15–40% improvement compared to 58S, depending on composition. This mechanical strengthening can be attributed to the borosilicate network, which provides higher packing density and improved resistance to deformation while still maintaining bioactivity.Fig. 4Variation in (a) density, (b) void fraction, (c) hardness, and (d) compressive strength of borosilicate bioactive glass (BSG) with different concentrations of Cu and Sr dopants.

The actual and theoretical density comparison of the glass samples is exhibited in the Fig. 4a. The more massive Cu and Sr ions (molar masses equal to 63.546 g/mol and 87.62 g/mol) of their atom configuration can be broken and local density fluctuations can be caused by various structural defects observed in glasses, including glass modifiers, hanging or fragmented bonds. These defects also contribute to the overall reduction in densities. The increased packing of atoms in the glass structure due to the higher coordination number of Cu and Sr ions increases the density in overall36. It is remarkable that for every sample, the actual density of the glass is constantly less than the theoretical density. It’s possible that trapped gas bubbles or gaps were created during the glass fabrication process, resulting in a porous structure that lowers the density overall. The lowest actual density is noted for the BSG-2% Cu composition at 2.56 g/cm3, while for BSG-2% Cu & Sr, a slightly increased value of 2.65 g/cm3 is observed29. This overall trend of the actual density being less than theoretical density can be explained by the existence of voids, porosity, and other melt-quenching-caused microstructural defects. Theoretical densities were estimated from constituent oxide molar volumes and molecular weights, assuming ideal packing and complete densification. XRD analysis

X-ray diffraction spectra of borosilicate glass samples measured inprior and after immersion in SBF are shown in Fig. 9. The diffraction pattern at 0 days revealed a broad halo within the 20–35° range, which is typical for an amorphous glass structure, evidencing that no crystalline phases existed straight after synthesis. The progressive evolution of clear-cut peaks indicates a high tendency of Cu and Sr dopant doped borosilicate glasses to produce a stable and crystalline apatite layer replicating the mineral content of natural bone. The combined impact of Cu and Sr dopants is seen to enhance nucleation and stabilization of hydroxyapatite at a faster rate, consistent with their role of enhanced bioactivity24. On the other hand, the antibacterial activity of the Cu–Sr–doped borosilicate glasses is primarily attributed to ion release–mediated mechanisms, with secondary contributions from membrane disruption, rather than direct evidence of reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. Copper ions (Cu2⁺) released from the glass matrix are well known to exert antibacterial effects by interacting with bacterial cell membranes, leading to increased membrane permeability, protein dysfunction, and intracellular damage. Strontium ions (Sr2⁺), while not strongly bactericidal on their own, are reported to enhance antibacterial performance by modifying surface chemistry and ionic strength, thereby facilitating Cu2⁺ interaction with bacterial cells. The significantly enhanced inhibition observed for 1% CuSr co-doped glass (Fig. 13d) suggests a synergistic effect driven by combined ion release. The fluorescence microscopy images (Fig. 14) show a marked reduction in bacterial density and disrupted cellular morphology for both E. coli and S. aureus upon exposure to Cu-, Sr-, and CuSr-doped glasses, supporting a mechanism involving cell membrane damage and loss of viability. However, no direct assays for ROS generation (e.g., DCFH-DA or oxidative stress markers) were conducted in this study; therefore, ROS-mediated antibacterial action cannot be conclusively confirmed.

In summary, based on CFU reduction trends and fluorescence observations, the antibacterial behavior of Cu–Sr–doped borosilicate glasses is predominantly governed by Cu2⁺/Sr2⁺ ion release–induced membrane disruption, with potential but unverified contributions from oxidative stress. Further studies involving ion release profiling and ROS quantification are planned to fully elucidate the antibacterial mechanism.

Conclusions

Borosilicate glass samples were successfully synthesized via the conventional melt-quenching method using partially biogenic precursors such as rice husk for SiO₂ and eggshells for CaO. Mechanical and tribological assessments, including hardness, compressive strength, and wear resistance, were conducted. Co-doping with Cu and Sr increased the actual density from 2.61 to 2.65 g/cm3. The BSG-1wt.% CuSr sample exhibited the highest hardness (5.71 GPa) and compressive strength (55.21 MPa). However, at 2%CuSr, network disruption by Cu2⁺ and Sr2⁺ ions reduced the mechanical properties. With increasing track radius, specific wear rate decreased across all samples; yet, as CuSr content increased from 1 to 2wt.%, the wear rate increased slightly from 0.013 to 0.014 mm3/N·m at 60 mm track radius, due to greater material removal. SEM of worn surfaces showed smoother, denser morphology for 1%CuSr, indicating better micro-fracture resistance. In contrast, the 2% sample showed rougher surfaces, consistent with reduced stability. The synthesized borosilicate bioactive glasses showed strong in-vitro bioactivity, as indicated by the nucleation and development of hydroxyapatite layers on the surface of the glasses, as verified by FTIR (phosphate and hydroxyl vibrational signals) and XRD (sharp hydroxyapatite crystalline peaks) after immersing in SBF for 7 and 14 days thus confirming their suitability for bone-bonding purposes. Cu/Sr-doped glasses demonstrated excellent cytocompatibility in MG-63 cells and significant antibacterial activity, especially against E. coli and S. aureus, even at low concentrations. The 1wt.% CuSr and 2wt.% Cu samples showed the highest inhibition at 50 mg, confirming dose-dependent antibacterial efficacy via Cu-mediated membrane disruption and oxidative stress. Combining enhanced mechanical strength, cytocompatibility, and broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, BSG-1wt.% CuSr emerges as a promising material for orthopaedic implants and bone tissue engineering applications.
