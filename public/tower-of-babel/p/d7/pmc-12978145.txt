Clinical Heterogeneity and Candidate Biomarkers in POLG-Related Mitochondrial Disease
Authors: Laura Bermejo-Guerrero, Juan Luis Restrepo-Vera, Paloma Martin-Jimenez, Maria Navarro-Riquelme, Rocío Garrido-Moraga, Luz Edith Ochoa, Adrián González-Quintana, Aurelio Hernandez-Lain, Jessica Castillo-Villalba, Germán Morís, Nuria Muelas, Elena García-Arumí, Victoria González, Elena Martínez-Sáez, Ana Vesperinas, Laura Llansó, Raul Juntas-Morales, Carmen Paradas, Solange Kapetanovic, Alberto Blázquez, Ramon Martí, Joaquín Arenas, Miguel A. ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesPOLG-related disorders exhibit marked phenotypic heterogeneity and frequent clinical overlap, often leading to delayed diagnosis. A precise delineation of their clinical spectrum, natural history, and the identification of reliable biomarkers is essential to improve diagnostic accuracy and guide therapeutic development.MethodsWe analyzed a cohort of 34 patients with confirmed pathogenic POLG variants, assessing clinical phenotypes, molecular findings, and biomarkers (plasma growth differentiation factor-15 [GDF15] in 16, plasma neurofilament light chain [NF-L] in 14, and mitochondrial DNA [mtDNA] copy number in muscle in 16).ResultsThirty four patients (0.6–71 years) from 33 families were included. Juvenile/adult onset (12–40 years) was the most common presentation (62%). The predominant phenotypic categories were ataxia-neuropathy spectrum ([ANS], 44%), autosomal recessive PEO-plus (arPEO-plus, 26%), and autosomal dominant PEO-plus ([adPEO-plus], 15%), with frequent phenotypic overlap. Recessive inheritance accounted for 74% of cases, with the most common variants being p.([Thr251Ile; Pro587Leu]) paired on 1 allele, p.(Ala467Thr), and p.(Trp748Ser). Dominant variants were associated with milder, primarily myopathic phenotypes. The most common dominant variant was p.(Tyr955Cys). No clear genotype-phenotype correlations were identified among recessive variants. Compared with previously reported cohorts, our patients exhibited a lower prevalence of seizures, hepatopathy, and stroke-like episodes. GDF15 was elevated in 87.5% of patients, with a mean level of 3,315 pg/mL (±1,559.79), showing no significant differences between myopathic and ANS phenotypes, supporting its role as a general biomarker of mitochondrial dysfunction. NF-L was elevated in 78.6% of tested individuals but did not correlate with phenotype or clinical severity (as per Newcastle Mitochondrial Disease Adult Scale score).On average, muscle mtDNA copy number in patients was 76% of that observed in controls, with no differences by phenotype or inheritance pattern. All but 1 patient exhibited multiple mtDNA deletions, likely representing the primary mechanism of oxidative phosphorylation dysfunction rather than mtDNA depletion.DiscussionPOLG-related disorders demonstrate extensive clinical variability with no consistent genotype-phenotype correlation. GDF15 and NF-L may serve as useful, though nonspecific, biomarkers of mitochondrial and neuroaxonal dysfunction, respectively. Prospective studies incorporating advanced molecular profiling are essential to establish reliable outcome measures and inform future therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Primary mitochondrial diseases are a heterogeneous group of genetic disorders caused by dysfunction of the oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) system, essential for cellular ATP production. The proteins involved are encoded by both mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and nuclear DNA (nDNA). Variants in nuclear genes involved in mtDNA maintenance lead to quantitative defects (mtDNA depletion, i.e., reduced copy number), qualitative defects (multiple mtDNA deletions), or both.1-3 DNA polymerase gamma, responsible for mtDNA replication, is encoded by nuclear genes POLG (catalytic subunit that holds the 5′-3′ DNA polymerase and 3′-5′ exonuclease activity) and POLG2 (accessory subunits that enhance the processivity and fidelity of mtDNA synthesis).2,4

Classical POLG-related syndromes include Alpers-Huttenlocher syndrome (AHS), myocerebrohepatopathy spectrum, myoclonic epilepsy with myopathy and sensory ataxia, mitochondrial recessive ataxia syndrome, sensory ataxic neuropathy, dysarthria and ophthalmoplegia (SANDO)—both part of the ataxia-neuropathy spectrum (ANS)—and progressive external ophthalmoplegia (PEO), with or without additional features.4,5 However, clinical overlap is common. Large cohort analyses have identified 3 clinical groups based on age at onset5: (1) early-onset (<12 years), with hepatopathy, feeding difficulties, encephalopathy with seizures, hypotonia, and weakness; (2) juvenile/adult-onset (12–40 years), with peripheral neuropathy, ataxia, seizures, and limb weakness; and (3) late-onset (>40 years), predominantly presenting with ptosis and PEO, with or without neuropathy, ataxia, or myopathy.

No definitive genotype-phenotype correlations have been previously established.4 Nonetheless, the type of mtDNA defect partially correlates with severity: Profound mtDNA depletion typically correlates with severe, early-onset phenotypes, while multiple deletions are more commonly linked to milder, later-onset forms.2,4,5

The pronounced phenotypic variability of POLG-related disorders and the limited sensitivity of first-line tests, such as lactate or creatine kinase (CK) levels, often delay diagnosis. Here, we comprehensively characterize 34 patients with POLG-related disease, integrating clinical, imaging, pathologic, and genetic data. Additionally, we explore the diagnostic and prognostic utility of circulating biomarkers—growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) and neurofilament light chain (NF-L)—alongside mtDNA copy number quantification in muscle tissue.

Methods

A total of 34 patients were included, recruited from 7 tertiary Spanish centers (Hospitals 12 de Octubre, Vall d'Hebron, Santa Creu i Sant Pau, La Fe, Central de Asturias, Virgen del Rocío and Basurto). Twenty-six were prospectively followed, while data from 8 were collected retrospectively because of loss of follow-up (5 of whom had died). Patients with sufficient clinical information available up to December 2024 were eligible. Clinical assessments were conducted as part of routine care. Disease severity was evaluated using the modified Rankin Scale ([mRS], 0 = no symptoms to 6 = death) and sections I–III of the Newcastle Mitochondrial Disease Adult Scale ([NMDAS], 0 to 145 points with higher score meaning greater severity).6 Patients were phenotypically categorized as follows: isolated autosomal recessive or dominant PEO (arPEO or adPEO); adPEO-plus and arPEO-plus (associated with prominent myopathy and/or additional features; myopathy is defined by a myopathic pattern of weakness [i.e., predominant weakness of proximal limb muscles and/or cervical or axial muscles] together with supportive histologic findings); ANS when these manifestations were the most predominant features; isolated exercise intolerance; and epilepsy, when seizures were the most prominent clinical feature. Plasma concentrations of GDF-15 and NF-L were assessed only in prospectively enrolled patients with regular follow-up, as no samples were available for retrospectively collected cases.

GDF-15 was quantified in 16 patients and 41 age-matched healthy controls at Hospital “12 de Octubre” using the same quantitative electrochemiluminescence immunoassay kit, ensuring consistency in measurement procedures (Elecsys GDF-15; Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland).7 The manufacturer provided age-specific reference values, which closely matched the results of our control group (median, 95th percentile): 30–40 (500, 852), 40–50 (614, 1,229), 50–60 (757, 1,466), 60–70 (866, 1,476), >70 (1,060, 2,199).

Plasma NFL levels from 14 patients were measured at the Neuroimmunology Service of the La Fe Research Institute using the ultrasensitive Simoa platform(Simoa NF-Light V2 Advantage kit, Ref#: 104073, for Simoa SR-X Analyzer instrument; Lexington, MA) following the manufacturer's protocol.8 Each plate included duplicate measurements of 2 quality control samples provided with the kit, ensuring reliability. Mean intra-assay coefficients of variation were below 10%. Age-specific reference values were (pg/mL) (95% CI of the mean) 20–40(5.67–7.02), 40–60 (8.98–11.21), and >60(11.7–15.57).

Muscle biopsies, obtained in 28 patients, were processed for routine histochemical and immunohistochemical analyses following standard protocols as part of the diagnostic workup. Mitochondrial-specific features included the presence of ragged-red fibers (RRF) on Gomori staining and cytochrome c oxidase (COX)–negative fibers. Mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes and citrate synthase enzyme activities in skeletal muscle homogenates were determined spectrophotometrically as previously described.9,10 The presence of mtDNA deletions in skeletal muscle was assessed by Southern blot and/or single-fragment amplicon long-range PCR.

The identification of POLG variants was performed by using a customized next-generation sequencing panel of 13 genes associated with defects in mtDNA maintenance (DGUOK, MFN2, MPV17, OPA1, POLG, POLG2, RRM2B, SLC25A4, SUCLA2, SUCLG1, TK2, TWNK, and TYMP) or by whole-exome sequencing. Alternatively, direct Sanger sequencing of POLG gene was performed in relatives of known probands. The pathogenicity of the POLG variants was evaluated based on published literature, population databases, and in silico prediction tools and was interpreted according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines11 and the ClinGen specifications for POLG.12 The mtDNA copy number (mtDNAcn) was quantified using quantitative PCR. Total DNA was extracted from muscle biopsy samples using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (51304, Qiagen). Following extraction and ultra-purification with Genomic DNA Clean & Concentrator-10 (D4011, Ecogen) to remove impurities and concentrate total DNA, the absolute copy number of mtDNA and nDNA was determined using the ABI-Quant Studio 5 Real-Time PCR platform. This method involves the specific amplification of the mitochondrial MT-CYB (cytochrome b) gene and the nuclear B2M (β-2-microglobulin) gene, their detection via the fluorescent intercalating dye iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (1725120, Bio-Rad), and absolute quantification using a plasmid construct containing both mitochondrial and nuclear genes with a known copy number per cell. The mtDNAcn per cell was calculated using the formula: (MT-CYB copy number ×2)/B2M copy number. The analysis included 16 patients and 16 controls (10 female patients), of whom 12 were pseudocontrols—individuals referred for suspected myopathy but ultimately found to have normal findings, including unremarkable muscle biopsies after comprehensive diagnostic workup—and 4 were healthy donors who provided samples during orthopedic surgery following informed consent.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized for the full cohort and by subgroups. Continuous variables were described using mean ± SD or median and IQR, as appropriate, categorical variables as absolute and relative frequencies. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test (2 groups) or Kruskal-Wallis test (multiple groups), with post hoc pairwise tests when relevant. Spearman rank correlation assessed associations between clinical, functional, respiratory, and biochemical variables, with 95% CIs for correlation coefficients. For group comparisons, Cohen effect size was calculated when appropriate. Nonparametric tests were used because of nonnormal distribution (Shapiro-Wilk test) and small subgroup sizes. A p value <0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were conducted in RStudio (v4.4.1); figures were generated with GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Local Research Ethics Committee. All participants, or their legal guardians when applicable, provided written informed consent for participation.

Data Availability

Data are available from the corresponding author, on reasonable request.

Results

Clinical Features

A total of 34 patients from 33 families were analyzed, including 20 female patients (59%). Two patients were siblings (patients 4 and 34; further details are provided in etable 1). The mean age at onset was 31.5 years (range: 0.6–71). According to established age-at-onset categories, 4 patients (11.8%) had early-onset disease (<12 years), 21 (61.8%) juvenile/adult-onset (12–40 years), and 9 (26.4%) late-onset (>40 years). Inheritance was autosomal recessive in 25 (73.5%) and dominant in 9 (26.4%). A family history suggestive of POLG-related disease was documented in 14 cases (41%) (eTable 1).

Ophthalmoplegia (88%) and ptosis (85%) were the most frequent features (Figure 1A). Muscle weakness was observed in 68%, most commonly in limbs (56%), followed by neck (35%) and facial muscles (32%). Distribution of limb weakness was predominantly proximal in 9/19 (47%), distal in 6/19 (32%), both proximal and distal in 3/19 (16%), and undetermined in 1/19 (5%). Peripheral axonal neuropathy was confirmed in 67% of those tested (18/27, sensory in 61% and sensorimotor in 39%). Sensory symptoms were present in 50% and ataxia in 47%—all classified as sensory in nature, with 25% also displaying a cerebellar component. Other common features included dysarthria (32%), dysphagia (29%), fatigue (26%), exercise intolerance (24%), tremor (24%), and hearing loss (18%). Less frequent symptoms were seizures (9%), migraine (9%), parkinsonism (9%), cognitive decline (9%), depression (9%), and developmental delay (3%). Two patients (6%) had restrictive respiratory insufficiency, both requiring nocturnal BiPAP. Additional systemic features included cataracts (9%) and tubulopathy (3%); no cases of hepatopathy were observed (eTables 1 and 2). Cardiac screening was performed in a total 16 patients when considered clinically relevant (ECG in 13, Holter in 7, echocardiogram in 11), with only 1 patient displaying a nonsignificant abnormality on imaging.

Brain Imaging

Brain MRI findings showed normal results in 7/16 patients, brain atrophy in 7/16, cerebellar atrophy in 3/16, leukoencephalopathy in 2/16, and ischemic lesions in 2/16. Leukoencephalopathy consisted of diffuse periventricular T2 hyperintensities on MRI at age 24 years in one patient and hyperintensities of cerebellar white matter (particularly surrounding both dentate nuclei) in another with predominant cerebellar symptoms at age 45 years. The ischemic lesions observed in 2 patients were likely unrelated to the underlying condition, as the MRI findings were consistent with microvascular ischemia in older individuals with long-standing cardiovascular risk factors.

Laboratory, Muscle Biopsy, and Genetic Findings

Laboratory Variables

The serum and plasma biomarkers assessed included CK, lactate, GDF15, and NF-L. The results, stratified by phenotype and age, are presented in Tables 1 and 2.

The mean CK was 286 ± 223.2 U/L, elevated in 15/23 patients (65.2%) with a mean 2.2-fold increase above the threshold. The mean lactate was 1.98 ± 0.96 mmol/L, elevated in 5/20(25%). No significant differences in CK or lactate were found across phenotypic subgroups. GDF15 was measured in 16 patients (3,315 ± 1,559.8 pg/mL) and 41 age-matched controls (908 ± 477 pg/mL) being significantly higher in patients (p < 0.001) (Figure 3A). Age-specific reference values were exceeded in 14 patients (87.5%). No significant differences were observed between patients with ANS and those with predominantly myopathic phenotypes (Figure 3B).

Similarly, NF-L concentrations did not differ significantly between patients with ANS and those with predominantly myopathic phenotypes (Figure 3C).

Muscle Biopsies and mtDNA Analysis

Histologic data were available from 26/28 biopsies. Signs of mitochondrial dysfunction (i.e., RRF and COX-negative fibers) were present in 22/26 (84.6%) in variable proportions (2.5%–28%). Assessment of respiratory chain complex activity yielded normal results in 18/21 (85.7%), complex I deficiency in 2/21, and combined III + IV complexes deficiency in 1/21.

Multiple mtDNA deletions were detected in muscle tissue in 19/20 patients (95%). The only negative case corresponded to a patient with very early disease onset who underwent biopsy at age 3 years; mtDNA copy number was not analyzed in this case.

mtDNAcn in skeletal muscle was assessed in 16 patients and 16 controls/pseudocontrols (Figure 4A). The mean mtDNAcn in POLG individuals was 3,776 ± 1,645, significantly lower than in controls (4,779 ± 162; Mann-Whitney U test, p = 0.002; Cohen d = −0.66, 95% CI −1.38 to 0.05). Moreover, 9/16 POLG samples (56%) fell below the 25th percentile of the control distribution (3,824 copies) and 13/16 (81.25%) below the median (4,838 copies), further underscoring the mtDNAcn reduction in the patient group. POLG patients showed an average 21% reduction compared with controls. Additional analyses stratified by sex and age group showed no significant effects of these variables in either controls or POLG patients. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential differences in mtDNAcn within the POLG cohort. No correlation was observed between age at symptom onset and mtDNAcn (Figure 2D). However, the only muscle sample available from an infantile-onset case showed 1734 copies per cell, corresponding to a 63.7% reduction compared with the mean value observed in controls. To further evaluate the impact of inheritance pattern, mtDNAcn was compared between dominant and recessive phenotypes. Given the predominance of recessive cases, the analysis focused on clinically comparable subgroups with distinct modes of inheritance: arPEO vs adPEO (Figure 4B). No statistically significant differences were found between these groups. Similarly, no significant differences were observed among other phenotypic subgroups.

Molecular Analysis Results

A total of 20 distinct POLG variants were identified across 59 alleles in the cohort (Table 3). The most frequent was the in-cis combination c.[752C > T; 1760C > T], resulting in p.[Thr251Ile; Pro587Leu], found in 13 alleles (22%), followed by c.1399G > A p.(Ala467Thr) in 11 (18.6%) and c.2243G > C p.(Trp748Ser) in 7 (11.9%). All 3 are associated with autosomal recessive inheritance. Only 3 variants were linked to dominant inheritance and previously reported with this pattern as pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants: c.2864A > G p.(Tyr955Cys), c.2573C > T p.(Thr858Ile), and c.2834A > T p.(His945Leu).

Five variants were novel, each occurring in trans with another variant in a biallelic pattern: c.2537C > T p.(Thr846Ile), c.3644-14_3644-4del, c.3680_3684del p.(Thr1227Argfs54), c.1832C > G p.(Pro611Arg), and c.2803A > T p.(Thr935Ser). Based on ACMG/AMP criteria and ClinGen specifications for POLG,12 c.3680_3684del, c.2803A > T, and c.1832C > G were classified as likely pathogenic, while c.2537C > T and c.3644-14_3644-4del were considered variants of uncertain significance (VUS). Classifications were supported by combinations of evidence codes, including PVS1, PM2, and PP3. Specifically, c.3680_3684del was deemed likely pathogenic due to a predicted loss-of-function effect (PVS1) and absence from population databases (PM2). Other variants met moderate criteria such as absence in controls (PM2) and deleterious in silico predictions (PP3). Additional support for pathogenicity included clinical phenotypes consistent with POLG-related disease, histologic evidence of mitochondrial dysfunction, and detection of multiple mtDNA deletions (PP4) (eTable 2).

To explore genotype-phenotype correlations, we analyzed patients carrying the mentioned 3 most frequent POLG variants. Among homozygous individuals, both cases with p.(Ala467Thr) and both with p.(Trp748Ser) showed the ANS phenotype. Of the 3 homozygous for the in-cis variant p.[Thr251Ile; Pro587Leu], 2 presented with arPEO-plus and 1 with ANS. No significant differences in mtDNAcn were found among patients homozygous for these 3 variants. Among compound heterozygotes: carriers of p.(Ala467Thr) (n = 7) predominantly presented with ANS (n = 4), followed by arPEO-plus (n = 2) and epilepsy (n = 1). In those with p.(Trp748Ser) (n = 3), ANS (n = 2) and arPEO-plus (n = 1) were observed. For the in-cis variant (p.[Thr251Ile; Pro587Leu]) (n = 7), arPEO-plus was slightly more frequent (n = 4) than ANS (n = 3).

Discussion

In this multicenter cohort of 34 patients with genetically confirmed POLG-related disease, we provide a detailed clinical characterization highlighting the predominance of juvenile/adult-onset cases (62%) and the phenotypic diversity across inheritance patterns. The most frequent presentations were ANS (44%), arPEO-plus (26%), and adPEO-plus (15%). Core clinical features included PEO, limb and axial muscle weakness, sensory ataxia, and axonal polyneuropathy. Phenotypic overlap was common, particularly between ANS and overt myopathic involvement, observed in 21% of patients—an association less commonly reported in the literature.13 When present, myopathy in ANS was characterized by predominant involvement of neck flexors and proximal limb muscles. Movement disorders, a recognized feature of the ANS phenotype, were also observed. Parkinsonism, the most frequently reported in this context,14-16 was identified in 1 case, and notably, chorea was documented in another—an observation not previously described in POLG-related disease. Autosomal dominant inheritance accounted for only 26% of cases and was typically associated with milder, predominantly myopathic phenotypes.

When compared with other series published over the past 15 years5,17-19 (eTable 3), our cohort exhibited generally similar clinical profiles. However, the proportion of early‐onset patients in our series (12%) was lower than that reported elsewhere (35%–100%), with the closest comparator being a previoulsy published cohort reporting 23%.18 Our cohort included patients diagnosed at 2 national reference laboratories for mitochondrial disease—covering both pediatric and adult cases—and adult patients followed in 7 neuromuscular units. This recruitment strategy likely contributed to the lower frequency of early-onset presentations observed, although additional factors cannot be excluded. A recent epidemiologic study in Spain identified 37 POLG patients as of 2021 (16 pediatric and 21 adult cases), but did not report the age-at-onset distribution, thereby limiting direct comparisons of early- vs juvenile-onset proportions with our cohort.20 In line with the low proportion of early-onset cases, no patients in our series presented with hepatopathy or stroke-like episodes. Seizures were reported in only 3/33 patients (9%), including 2 with early-onset disease (50% of that subgroup) and 1 with juvenile-onset (5% of that subgroup). These frequencies are consistent with previously published data.18 In contrast, other studies have described stroke‐like episodes in 15%–41% of cases,5,17,19 hepatopathy in 51–64%—rising to 87% among early‐onset patients in one series5 and seizures in 35%–71% of patients, particularly within early and juvenile‐onset subgroups. The low seizure prevalence may be partially explained by the small number of early-onset patients, although it remains low even among juvenile-onset patients, which suggest the existence of additional factors potentially influencing phenotypic expression.

The POLG variants p.(Ala467Thr) and p.(Trp748Ser) are also among the most frequently reported across cohorts, though some variability exists. For example, onse study did not identify p.(Ala467Thr)18, whereas another19 did not detect p.(Trp748Ser) but instead reported p.(Trp748Cys) as the third most common variant in a pediatric series. The in-cis variant p.[Thr251Ile; Pro587Leu] was the most prevalent in our series, accounting for 22% of alleles—substantially higher than in previous reports.5,17-19 Haplotype analysis could not be performed in our patients. All these variants are consistently associated with autosomal recessive inheritance. Despite their prevalence, no clear genotype-phenotype correlations were identified, in agreement with previous reports. This highlights the marked heterogeneity of POLG-related disorders and suggests that additional modifying factors are likely to influence disease expression. Nevertheless, the limited sample size of our series precludes drawing definitive conclusions, and prospective detailed studies are needed to settle these questions. Early diagnosis is crucial for genetic counseling and candidate identification for trials.21,22

Muscle biopsy can support the diagnostic process for suspected POLG-related disease. Its sensitivity was higher in our cohort than in previous studies (85% vs ≤ 50%–80%)5,18—possibly because of the higher proportion of juvenile and late-onset cases, which display biopsy abnormalities more frequently than early-onset hepatocerebral phenotypes. Detection of depletion or multiple mtDNA deletions in muscle can further point toward mtDNA maintenance syndromes and provide supportive evidence of pathogenicity for VUSes. It should be noted, however, that multiple mtDNA deletions are not specific and may also occur in conditions, such as myofibrillar or inclusion body myopathies, where the clinical and histologic presentations are distinct and also at low levels in elderly individuals.

Traditional noninvasive biomarkers such as lactate lack sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing or monitoring mitochondrial disease23; only 25% of our patients had elevated levels. More reliable biomarkers are therefore needed to support diagnosis and to advance the understanding of disease mechanisms. We evaluated GDF15, plasma NF-L, and muscle mtDNAcn. Although preliminary signals were observed for them, their interpretation remains exploratory given the small size of the subgroups, which substantially limits statistical power and precludes definitive conclusions.

GDF15 has emerged as a promising sensitive and specific biomarker for mitochondrial diseases, with elevated levels observed across a broad range of mitochondrial disorders regardless of phenotype.23-27 Among patients with muscle involvement, its sensitivity is similar across different genotypes and correlates with disease severity.28 Its potential as a treatment response biomarker has also been demonstrated in patients with TK2 deficiency receiving nucleoside therapy,29,30 supporting its inclusion in future studies.

In our cohort, GDF15 levels did not significantly differ between myopathic and ANS phenotypes. Although data are limited in hepatocerebral presentations, results from the previous study21 suggest reduced sensitivity in this subgroup: only 3/7 patients with early-onset AHS showed elevated GDF15 levels despite their severe phenotype. These findings highlight the need for additional biomarkers in early-onset disease.

NF-L, a structural neuronal-exclusive cytoskeletal protein, is released into blood and CSF in response to neuroaxonal damage regardless of the underlying cause, having its levels increased proportionally to the degree of axonal damage and thus being a useful and sensitive biomarker in both central and peripheral nervous system disorders. Ultrasensitive Simoa determinations have greatly improved the sensitivity in blood samples, allowing reliable detection even in healthy individuals and widening its potential applications as a noninvasive test.31

NF-L has proved diagnostic and prognostic utility across neurologic diseases, including multiple sclerosis, ALS, and Guillain-Barré syndrome.31-34 It also serves as a treatment-response marker in conditions like SOD1-ALS and multiple sclerosis.31-36 NF-L levels are the highest in rapidly progressive diseases (e.g., ALS, CJD, HIV-associated dementia), often reaching 10-fold increases compared with controls.32,37 More moderate elevations (∼2-fold) are seen in Huntington, Parkinson, and various dementias.32,37 Although no universal cutoffs exist, age-adjusted thresholds have been proposed.38 For reference, the median serum NF-L values range from ∼15 pg/mL in healthy controls to >100 pg/mL in rapidly progressive ALS.33,36,39-41 In primary myopathies, NF-L levels increase only when there is associated clinical or subclinical nervous system involvement.38 The mean NF-L concentrations are elevated 3.2-fold in mitochondrial myopathies, 2.1-fold in DM1, and 1.4-fold in DM2 compared with age-matched controls.42 By contrast, myopathies caused by muscle-specific gene defects show no NF-L elevation.42 In mitochondrial disease, NF-L correlates with neurologic involvement: levels are higher in PEO-plus than in isolated PEO, with the highest values (∼50 pg/mL) in stroke-like episodes.42,43

In our cohort, 78.6% of patients tested had NF-L levels above age-adjusted thresholds, typically 1.5–2-fold higher than normal. No significant difference was found between ANS and predominantly myopathic phenotypes, possibly reflecting mild or subclinical CNS or PNS involvement in some myopathic cases (e.g., polyneuropathy, cognitive impairment, or parkinsonism in PEO-plus). None of the tested patients had acute or severe CNS pathology. The small sample size may have limited statistical power, underscoring the need for larger studies. NF-L levels did not correlate with NMDAS scores, likely due to the scale's multidimensional nature, which includes items unrelated to neuroaxonal damage. Prospective studies are warranted to assess their utility and potential as an outcome measure in POLG-related trials.

In our cohort, we quantified mtDNAcn in muscle tissue from available samples, mostly from patients with juvenile- or adult-onset disease, and observed a modest but statistically significant reduction in mean copy number in POLG patients compared with controls. However, the critical threshold at which this reduction begins to impair OXPHOS function and precipitate mitochondrial dysfunction remains undefined. Moreover, the widely accepted cutoff of residual mtDNA below 30% of control levels—commonly used to define mtDNA depletion—was originally established on an arbitrary basis.44 In our cohort, no significant differences in muscle mtDNAcn were detected when stratifying by clinical phenotype or by inheritance pattern. Although recessive cases showed a trend toward greater mtDNA reduction, this did not reach statistical significance, likely because of the limited sample size. Similarly, no differences in mtDNAcn were observed among patients homozygous for the 3 most common variants. We also found no correlation between age at onset or disease severity and mtDNAcn across the analyzed age range. Notably, the only infantile-onset case showed a twofold greater reduction compared with juvenile/adult-onset cases, supporting the notion that more pronounced mtDNA depletion is associated with more severe phenotypes. Additional studies are needed to define the critical mtDNAcn thresholds at which OXPHOS dysfunction emerges and how these thresholds translate into earlier onset and greater clinical severity. Multiple mtDNA deletions, likely as a result of mtDNA replication stalling,2 were detected in all patients except 1 with very early-onset severe disease in whom mtDNA depletion was the most likely defect, although this could not be directly assessed. The relatively preserved mtDNA copy number observed in most patients, despite the consistent accumulation of deletions of varying sizes, suggests that impaired OXPHOS function in POLG-related disease is more likely driven by multiple deletions rather than by global mtDNA depletion in this age group.

These observations underscore the need for more refined approaches to characterize the molecular mechanisms underlying OXPHOS dysfunction in POLG-related disease. Advanced tools such as eKLIPse and MitoSAlt pipelines45,46 may enable quantitative analysis of mtDNA deletions and their links to genotypes and phenotypes, and also their prognostic value as biomarkers.

In conclusion, POLG-related disorders exhibit a broad and overlapping clinical spectrum. Genotype-phenotype correlations could not be consistently demonstrated in our cohort, and future studies with larger patient populations will be required to clarify these potential relationships. GDF15 and plasma NF-L emerge as useful, although nonspecific, biomarkers of mitochondrial and neuroaxonal dysfunction, respectively, and in this study, we have provided preliminary data on their performance specifically on POLG-related disease. However, their roles in clinical decision making and as outcome measures in trials require validation through larger prospective studies. Our findings suggest that multiple mtDNA deletions, rather than copy number reduction, are the primary drivers of pathogenicity in most patients, underscoring the importance of advanced molecular approaches for accurate characterization. The marked clinical heterogeneity of POLG-related disease, together with the current lack of natural history studies in large cohorts with homogeneous phenotypes, poses a major challenge to identifying robust clinical outcome measures for use in therapeutic trials. This highlights the urgent need to validate reliable surrogate biomarkers applicable across a wider phenotypic spectrum.
