FQGR-net: Morphology-based litchi flower quantification and gender recognition
Authors: Jun Li, Zhe Ma, Jiaquan Lin, Qian Wang, Xiaoting Lin, Juan Xia, Bolai Xin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As a crucial agricultural crop in China, litchi exhibits a biennial bearing pattern with alternating high-yield and low-yield cycles, known as on-year and off-year respectively. Research has identified unstable floral initiation as the primary cause of irregular fruiting in mid-to-late maturing cultivars. Rapid and accurate quantification of female to male flower ratios during the flowering phase enables targeted management strategies to optimize floral development and enhance fruit-setting rates. This study proposes Flower Quantification and Gender Recognition Network (FQGR-Net), a three-branch neural network architecture for simultaneous classification and counting of female and male flowers. Through module-level optimization, FQGR-Net improves both counting accuracy and computational efficiency, achieving average MAE of 8.498 and RMSE of 13.209 across categories in experiments conducted on the self-constructed dataset. Comparative experiments with other deep neural network models on public datasets show the proposed method achieves optimal performance. A regression analysis between predictions and ground truth produces R2 values of 0.930 and 0.971 for female and male flower quantification respectively. A dedicated litchi flower phenotyping analyzer was developed to address the technological gap in automated floral census systems. Field trials demonstrated over 80% accuracy in female/male flower counting.

Introduction

The litchi industry plays a vital role in the regional economy of tropical and subtropical regions in China, with a cultivation covering approximately 0.523 million hectares. Litchi production exhibits a pronounced alternate bearing pattern, characterized by a cyclic transition between “on-years” and “off- or medium-years” [1]. Since the national yield first surpassed 1.5 million tons in 1999, this cyclical pattern has been consistently observed. As of late March 2024, based on floral development status, the total yield for 2024 is estimated at 1.781 million tons - a 45.94% decrease compared to the previous year [2]. This sharp fluctuation poses significant challenges to the stability of the litchi industry. Litchi development is vulnerable to various meteorological stresses throughout its growth stages, and floral instability is the primary cause of alternate bearing, especially in mid-to late-maturing cultivars [3]. Litchi is monoecious with unisexual flowers derived from selectively aborted bisexual flowers after meiosis [4]. A high ratio of female to male flowers generally correlates with an improved fruiting rate [5,6].

Litchi flower bud differentiation and flowering are stage-specific, seasonal and rhythmic: mature shoots form before autumn cooling; flower induction occurs under low temperatures in late autumn and winter; flowering takes place in warm spring [7]. Its flowering sequence is unstable. For a single panicle, the first male flowers bloom for five to nine days, female flowers three to four days; the second male flowers bloom for eight to twelve days, with a female-to-second-male ratio of 1: 2 [8,9]. Litchi shows a pronounced dichogamous flowering pattern, where male and female flower peaks on the same panicle do not overlap. The male-to-female ratio is highly imbalanced [10,11], with the female flower proportion ranging from <10% to 60% across cultivars [7].

Litchi panicles typically exhibit a cymose panicle structure. Excessive flower production may deplete the nutrient reserves of the tree, ultimately hindering fruit development and resulting in poor fruit set [12]. Therefore, manual pruning or the application of plant growth regulators at an appropriate stage of panicle development can effectively control panicle density, reduce nutrient competition, and mitigate “flowering without fruiting” phenomenon in which plants flower successfully but fail to develop mature fruits [13]. Wei et al. reported that the application of paclobutrazol during panicle development enables significant suppress of male flower formation, which increased the female-to-male ratio while reducing the total flower count [14]. Yang et al. used the late-maturing litchi cultivar ‘Ziniangxi’ to study the effects of plant growth regulators sprayed four times at the critical flower-induction stage (apical bud germination, shoot elongation <5cm) on flowering rate and sex ratio [15]. Chen et al. applied manual, mechanical and chemical flower thinning before anthesis, where manual thinning performed best in reducing total flowers and improving female flower ratio and yield [16]. In 2023, Yan et al. sprayed compound plant growth regulators on mature litchi panicles before flowering, effectively reducing male flowers and improving the female flower ratio and fruit set rate [17]. In 2024, they used auxin inhibitors and significantly increased the female flower ratio to 30.29%. Their research also illuminates that onset time of fruit setting was significantly positively correlated with female flower ratio (r=0.70), and final fruit set was significantly correlated with both fruit set time (r=0.73) and female flower ratio (r=0.51) [18]. Consequently, rapid and accurate estimation of floral quantity, coupled with precise identification of male and female flowers, is essential for research on floral sex differentiation and effective panicle management. These strategies are critical for improving fruit setting and ultimately increasing yield in litchi production.

Traditional methods for flower quantity estimation primarily rely on manual observation, which is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and prone to subjective errors due to visual fatigue. With the rapid advancement of digital image processing, computer vision has emerged as a promising approach for automated flower detection. A substantial body of research has been conducted globally on automatic flower quantification techniques. Early studies predominantly focused on single-flower or floral-pattern recognition using threshold-based segmentation and conventional machine vision techniques. For instance, Aggelopoulou, K-R Thorp, and Hočevar et al. proposed color thresholding methods to extract floral information [[19], [20], [21]]. However, these approaches rely heavily on low-level image features such as color and texture, and handcrafted feature extraction. Consequently, their generalization ability significantly deteriorates when applied to varying environmental conditions or across different application domains.

With the rapid advancement of deep learning technique, deep Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) that enables an automatic perception and learning of discriminative features have gradually replaced traditional image processing approaches in crop inflorescence detection tasks. Deng et al. improved the Mask R-CNN architecture to simplify the complex instance segmentation process, enabling effective detection of citrus flowers under conditions of dense distribution and severe shadow interference [22]. Xiong et al. employed a deep semantic segmentation network to classify the pixels of flowers and leaves in litchi flower images, thereby achieving separation of floral and foliar structures at whole-image level and addressing the challenges of dense and overlapping blossoms [23]. Similarly, Tian et al. proposed an enhanced Mask R-CNN based instance segmentation model that enables refined segmentation of flowering regions, facilitating the extraction of detailed phenotypic information of apple flowers at different developmental stages, including bud, semi-open, and fully open phases [24]. Wang et al. introduced DeepPhenology, a framework for estimating the phenological stages of apple flowers without requiring annotated training images [25]. Their method effectively overcomes the difficulties of distinguishing overlapping floral clusters and detecting occluded blossoms in 2D imagery, enabling estimation across eight phenological stages. To tackle the challenges of densely clustered and occluded litchi flowers, Ye et al. adopted an object detection approach and modified bounding box regression using a multi-branch loss function to improve localization accuracy and reduce missed detections [26]. The team led by Lin developed a density map regression-based approach for litchi flower counting, constructing a multi-column CNN architecture to perform pixel-wise annotation and statistical estimation of floral quantity [27]; In 2024, they further introduced a multi-task learning framework that integrates semantic information and density map regression by designing a density mask generation network, which jointly optimizes semantic segmentation and flower counting tasks, significantly improving counting accuracy under complex field conditions [28]. Bhattarai et al. proposed a weakly supervised network and a point-annotation-driven deep regression model, enabling estimations of apple flower and fruit density, quantity, and spatial distribution within the canopies [29,30].

In the specific task of classifying male and female flowers, several representative studies have been conducted. Zhang et al. combined the Ant Colony Optimization algorithm with the Gradient Vector Flow (GVF) Snake model to extract edges from preprocessed images of Korean pine flowers, enabling the identification of male and female floral structures [31]. A Back Propagation (BP) artificial neural network was then employed for final classification. Pinheiro et al. employed four novel pre-trained object detection models, and performed a bounding-box annotation of kiwi floral sex based on the morphological characteristics [32]. To ensure robust performance evaluation across data subsets, they adopted k-fold cross-validation, overcoming limitations associated with traditional data partitioning approaches. Lyu et al. proposed an innovative workflow for classifying litchi male and female flowers on embedded platforms [33]. Their approach involved detaching floral samples from the tree, arranging them against a structured background, and capturing images for training. A multi-teacher pre-activation feature distillation technique was then applied to enhance the model performance under resource-constrained settings. However, when the detection task involves images with extremely dense floral distributions and complex color patterns, many traditional methods - especially those relying on bounding box annotations, color thresholding, or pixel-wise regression - become less effective, exhibiting degraded performance and increased risk of missed detections.

In summary, although significant progress has been made in flower quantity detection and floral sex classification, methods specifically targeting the classification and counting of male and female litchi flowers remain limited. Most existing approaches focus solely on categorical identification, without providing accurate quantitative estimation. The few algorithms that do address litchi flower classification typically rely on collecting detached floral samples, placing them against structured backgrounds, and capturing images for analysis. However, such methods fail to meet the practical requirements of non-destructive and continuous monitoring of floral structures in their natural growth state.

To address these limitations, this study extends the traditional detection algorithm of litchi male flowers to multi-class object detection, in order to achieve the identification of both male and female flowers. The proposed model outputs quantitative information for multiple object categories within a single inference process, enabling rapid and accurate estimation of the number of male and female litchi flowers. Compared to conventional single-class object counting methods, the proposed multi-class framework offers better flexibility in deployment and broader applicability, making it well-suited for real-world agricultural scenarios.

Materials and methods

This study proposes a method for small-target classification and counting in dense scenes, specifically designed for litchi flower analysis. First, lichi flower images were collected and annotated before a data augmentation process to improve the diversity of the dataset. Second, a customized network architecture based on a VGG16 [34] backbone is developed, where the density map in the target counting algorithm is modified to match the number of flower categories. The density map enables multi-scale feature fusion by integrating shallow texture details and deep semantic information through parallel convolutional branches, effectively addressing scale variations while combining structural and contour features for improved learning. Third, a portable device for litchi flower phenotyping is developed by deploying the optimized model via ONNX Runtime, enabling an efficient quantification of female-male ratio in orchards.

Dataset preparation

Data collection

The image dataset used in this study were collected between February and April for three consecutive years (2022-2024) at two locations: one of them is located at College of Horticulture, South China Agriculture University, Guangzhou, China, while the other one is located at Fruit Tree Research Institute, Guangzhou Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Guangzhou, China. The collected litchi cultivars include "Feizixiao", "Guiwei", "Nuomici", "Zishuniang", and "Xiaojinzhong". Male flowers exhibit seven to ten fully developed stamens with underdeveloped or absent pistils, while female flowers contain one fully developed pistil accompanied by seven to ten underdeveloped stamens [35]. Both pistils, stamens, and functional nectaries are attached to the floral disc. The stamens typically bear six to ten swollen anthers borne on slender, trichome-covered filaments that radiate outward during flowering. Female flowers possess bicarpellate ovaries, each unilocular chamber enclosing a single ovule. As they mature, the stigmas bifurcate, curl downward, and reveal a surface of papillary cells [36]. Following litchi's dichogamous flowering pattern (characterized by temporal separation of male and female flower maturation) and staged flowering phases with distinct sexual expression sequences, we captured male and female flower images during the initial, intermediate, and late flowering stages. These images were captured using a Canon EOS 80D DSLR camera with a resolution of 6,000×4,000 pixels, so as to construct a dataset with clear images and high-quality annotations.

Point-based labeling

The original images with a resolution of 6,000×4,000 were randomly cropped into multiple sub-images with resolution of 1,024×768, 1,920×1,440 and 2,048×1,536 respectively. Within each sub-image, both female and male flowers were annotated using Labelme. For object detection tasks, the annotation process can be carried out in two ways: bounding-box annotation and point-level annotation. Bounding-box annotations encode the position and the contour of each target instance by category, which performs particularly well for sparse object detection tasks. However, for dense objects with serious occlusions, bounding-box annotations often fail to capture accurate shape details, necessitating manual delineation which may introduce subjective errors. Point-level annotation estimates the density of target distribution instead of the bounding box of object, indicating a better occlusion resistance and flower identification accuracy. Nevertheless, traditional point-level annotations are limited to single-category labeling and thus cannot satisfy the requirements of multi-class object detection. To this end, this study extends the traditional point-level annotation to multi-class object labeling by implementing a multi-channel density map prediction network. The network employs parallel processing branches to generate class-specific density maps in a single inference, and integrates these maps to obtain per-class counts.

To address the natural variations in flower morphology, a standarlization operation was introduced to the annotation process: male flowers were marked at the convergence centers of stamen filament, while female flowers were annotated at the junction of stigmas. As shown in Fig. 2(c), partially visible flowers were also annotated to ensure the performance of flower counting algorithm [27].

Totally 1,150 images were obtained, covering the cultivars of "Feizixiao", "Guiwei", "Nuomici", "Xiaojinzhong" and "Ziniangxi". These images contain 118,237 flower instances, among which 57,576 were female flowers and 60,661 were male flowers. Images acquired in 2022, 2023, and 2024 were distributed in an approximate ratio of 3:4:3. The annotated dataset was divided into training sets, test sets and validation sets at a ratio of 8:1:1. Since the phenotypic analyzer's built-in camera and the Canon EOS 80D DSLR have different imaging characteristics, online data augmentations were employed to simulate the diversity arising from the imaging sensor, thereby mitigating cross-sensor domain shift. To achieve this, random cropping [37], horizontal flipping and contrast perturbation operations were sequentially applied to input images prior to the acquisition of each training batch. Images were randomly cropped to 512×512 for scale adaptation, and both horizontal flipping and contrast perturbation were performed with a probability of 0.5. Litchi inflorescence density map label generation

The annotation process begins with parsing geometric labels from the JSON files of individual images, where multiple entries are recorded with categorical tags ("male" or "female") and associated 2D coordinates. These annotated points are categorized by their labels and converted into binary masks matching the image dimensions. Pixel values are then set to one at annotation locations and zero elsewhere, generating one-hot encoded representations for both "male" and "female" categories. The density map generation formula for each category is as shown in Equation (1):(1)D(Pj)=∑i=1Cnδ(Pj,Pin)∗Gσnwhere Pj=(xj,yj) denotes the 2D position of an arbitrary pixel, Pin=(xin,yin) denotes the annotated position of the ith flower instance in the nth category; Cn represents the total size of flower counts belonging to the nth category; σn corresponds to the Gaussian kernel assigned to the nth category within the Gaussian function G; δ refers to the distance between Pj and the nearest annotated point:(2)δ(Pj,Pin)=minPin∈A(xj−xin)2+(yj−yin)2Here, A denotes the set of coordinates for all annotated points. Finally, the ground-truth density map is represented as a two-channel map:(3)D(Pj)=[Dmale(Pj),Dfemale(Pj)],j∈[1,Np]

The density map not only preserves the positional information of annotations but also introduces spatial smoothness, aiding the model in capturing local region features of targets. Notably, flower sizes vary across images because of differences in shooting distance, which leads to inconsistent target sizes after Gaussian convolution, as depicted in Figure S1 of Appendix A. The semantic information within the pixel region of individual targets inherently encodes their actual scales. By establishing a correlation between Gaussian kernel sizes and the physical dimensions of objects, the Gaussian responses on the density map can be better aligned with the semantic characteristics of individual targets, thereby reducing the convergence complexity of the model.

By setting a distance threshold of four to divide the distance map into foreground and background, a pseudo-label for spatial attention generation can be obtained as a 0-1 mask. This mask effectively marks critical regions around annotated points, providing a basis for local loss weighting or regional focus during training.

Overall network Architecture

This study presents a Litchi Flower Quantification and Gender Recognition Network (FQGR-Net). The overall architecture of the proposed FQGR-Net. Convolutional blocks one to five of VGG16 are structured as follows: Block 1 contains two 64-channel 3×3 convolutional layers; Block 2 contains two 128-channel 3×3 convolutional layers; Block 3 contains three 256-channel 3×3 convolutional layers; Block 4 contains three 512-channel 3×3 convolutional layers; and Block 5 contains three 512-channel 3×3 convolutional layers.

By taking VGG16 as the backbone, FQGR-Net removes the last two pooling layers [28] and introduces three parallel task branches: one mask prediction branch and two density regression branches. The first branch adopts a Frequency-Aware Dilated Module (FADM) to process the output of Block 5. A three-class mask (pistillate, staminate, and background) is then generated through the subsequent convolutional layers. The second branch serves as the refined density regression branch. Conventional upsampling is replaced with DySampleFusion, and standard 3×3 convolutions are substituted with LiteIncepDWConv (LIDWC) layers for parameter efficiency. The output of Block 5 are processed by DySampleFusion and LIDWC layers, then fused with features from the third branch. The fused features are further refined via LIDWC and 1×1 convolutions, followed by a 4× bilinear interpolation [30] to generate the refined dual-category density map, which is used as the final output for counting. The third branch functions as the coarse density estimation branch. It incorporates a Multi-Scale Fusion Module (MSFM) to aggregate hierarchical features from Block 3, Block 4, and Block 5 for cross-scale contextual integration. After LIDWC optimization and cascaded convolutions with upsampling, this branch produces a scale-aware coarse density map, which models the globally consistent spatial distribution of flowers.

To enhance optimization stability, a deep supervision strategy is applied to both density branches. The third branch predicts the scale-aware coarse density map, which serves as auxiliary supervision during training to enforce global structural consistency. The second branch performs refined regression by integrating high-level semantic features and coarse structural priors, focusing on dense regions and boundary areas. Density regression loss is imposed on both branches during training; however, only the refined density map from the second branch is used during inference, ensuring improved accuracy without increasing computational complexity.

The three-branch framework achieves synergistic optimization through parameter sharing and feature interaction. The FADM-generated masks are element-wise multiplied with the refined density map to suppress background interference and inter-class ambiguity. The MSFM preserves multi-scale structural consistency and provides scale-aware priors that guide the refinement process. Final counts are obtained by channel-wise integration of the refined dual-category density maps. Through multi-task joint training, the architecture balances segmentation-guided spatial constraints and lightweight density refinement.

Optimization Module

LiteIncepDWConv

To minimize network parameters without compromising accuracy for edge device deployment, this study proposes LiteIncepDWConv (LIDWC), a lightweight module combining kernel decomposition with depthwise separable convolutions. As shown in Fig. 4(a), its multi-branch heterogeneous architecture decomposes large kernels into asymmetric horizontal (1×9) and vertical (9×1) strip convolutions, preserving equivalent receptive fields while reducing computational complexity by capturing orientation-specific patterns. This design synergistically balances parameter efficiency and discriminative power through multi-scale orientation-specific feature learning, asymmetric kernel decomposition for computational optimization, and identity-based information retention.

All branches employ depthwise separable convolution structures. This decomposition separates standard convolution into channel-wise spatial convolution and pointwise feature fusion stages, effectively reducing computational complexity compared to conventional operations. To maintain feature extraction capability while controlling computational load, the module partitions input feature channels into four groups, preserving feature diversity through parallel processing. Specifically, the horizontal and vertical branches adopt asymmetric strip kernel designs, decomposing traditional N×N kernels into cascaded 1×N and N×1 operations. This decomposition maintains an equivalent N2 receptive field while reducing parameter count to 2N of the original structure. For spatial resolution preservation, a symmetric padding strategy is implemented: a vertical padding is applied to horizontal strip convolutions, while a horizontal padding is applied to vertical strip convolutions. This directional padding alignment prevents feature map shrinkage while ensuring kernel decomposition efficacy.

DysampleFusion

To achieve flexible and precise upsampling while preserving feature map details, this study adapts the DySampleUP dynamic upsampling module [38]. DySampleUP implements two sampling strategies (“Pixel shuffle and Linear, PL” and “Linear and Pixel shuffle, LP”) and adaptively adjust sampling positions according to the content of the input features, as shown in Fig. 4(b). This content-aware mechanism preserves critical information during upsampling - particularly in fine-grained image regions - while avoiding the artifacts commonly introduced by traditional interpolation methods. Building upon DySampleUP, we propose DySampleFusion, which integrates both sampling styles through feature fusion to optimize upsampled outputs. By combining complementary patterns from “LP” (emphasizing low-frequency structures) and “PL” (preserving high-frequency details), DySampleFusion enhances reconstruction fidelity across diverse texture complexities.

Multi-Scale Fusion Module (MSFM)

In practical deployments, variations in camera-to-target distance during image capture give rise to substantial scale changes of the targets. The network must learn the mapping from target features to their Gaussian responses while simultaneously accounting for scale discrepancies. This dual learning mechanism enables robust adaptation to multi-scale targets by correlating discriminative feature representations with spatially adaptive density distributions.

To address the scale variation challenges, we designed the Multi-Scale Fusion Module (MSFM) using VGG16 as the backbone. The five pooling layers are sequentially indexed from shallow to deep as Block 1, Block 2, Block 3, Block 4, and Block 5, with Block 3, Block 4, and Block 5 serving as multi-scale inputs to the module. The lightweight convolution structure decomposes standard convolutions into depthwise and pointwise operations, significantly reducing parameters and computational costs. Dilated convolutions are incorporated to expand receptive fields while enhancing deep feature representation.

Block 4 and Block 5 features undergo dynamic upsampling via DySampleFusion to align their spatial resolutions with Block 3. The three resolution-aligned features are concatenated along the channel dimension, forming fused multi-scale representations as shown in Fig. 4(c). This integration resolves target scale diversity by combining hierarchical features, thereby improving adaptability to size variations and optimizing overall counting performance.

Frequency-Aware Dilated Module (FADM)

In actual application scenarios of litchi flower counting in orchards, model detection is often affected by background noise and interference between different categories, resulting in category misclassification and quantitative statistical deviation. For the detection task of the two categories of female and male flowers, the foreground and background regions on the density maps corresponding to female and male flowers are different, and their effective response regions also vary. This puts forward higher requirements for the anti-interference performance of the model, i.e., it is necessary to ensure an accurate focus of category-specific density maps on the foreground regions while effectively suppressing the interference responses of background noise, as shown in Figure S2 of Appendix A.

To address these challenges, we propose the Frequency-Aware Dilated Module (FADM) that processes the output feature map of Block 5 through three strategic stages. First, a frequency-aware spatial attention mechanism integrates temporal-frequency hybrid features to amplify foreground signatures while suppressing background noise caused by inter-class confusion. Second, the refined features undergo multi-scale contextual extraction through four parallel branches, each implementing 1×1 convolution followed by dilated convolutions with progressively increasing dilation rates (1→4). Finally, the branch outputs are concatenated along the channel dimension to synthesize cross-scale spatial features essential for generating precise foreground-background segmentation masks, as detailed in Fig. 4(d).

Evaluation Metrics

There are no uniform evaluation metrics for flower number quantification. In density map regression methodologies, prevailing approaches adopt standard quantitative metrics from crowd counting research: Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE). These metrics remain applicable to floral density regression tasks, with their mathematical formulations defined as follows:(7)MAE=1N∑1N|zi−zˆi|(8)RMSE=1N∑1N(zi−zˆi)2

Here, N denotes the number of test set samples, zi represents the ground-truth flower count on the i-th image, and zˆi indicates the predicted flower count for the i-th image. The MAE quantifies prediction accuracy, while the RMSE evaluates algorithmic generalization and model robustness.

However, conventional MAE and RMSE metrics are suboptimal for binary-class counting tasks. To quantify performance in such classification-aware counting scenarios, we propose MAEA and RMSEA metrics. These are derived by averaging the per-class MAE and RMSE values obtained separately for female and male flowers using the proposed dual-class counting algorithm:(9)MAEA=1N∑n=1NMAEn(10)RMSEA=1N∑n=1NRMSEn

The MAEA quantifies counting errors in multi-category scenarios, while the RMSEA evaluates robustness in cross-category counting statistics. Effective multi-class object counting algorithms must achieve both low MAEA and RMSEA values, ensuring precise counting accuracy while maintaining adaptability across diverse detection scenarios. This dual-metric framework enables comprehensive assessment of litchi female-male flower detection performance in complex orchard environments, providing intuitive insights into category-specific counting precision.

Portable litchi flower phenotype analyzer

The device integrates six core components: an image acquisition module, a signal acquisition module, an image processing/computing platform, a power supply module, a touchscreen display panel, and a software interface, as shown in Fig. 5. Experimental results and analyses

Experimental implementation Details

The experimental hardware platform in this study utilized a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3070, with the operating system configured as Ubuntu 20.04 LTS. The software environment for the experiment is PyTorch 1.10.0 with Cuda 9.2 and Python 3.9. The first 13 convolutional layers of the network were initialized with the parameters of the VGG-16 model pre-trained on ImageNet, while the remaining network layers were randomly initialized using a Gaussian distribution with a mean value of 0 and a standard deviation of 0.01. For network training, the Adam optimizer was adopted, with the initial learning rate set to 1×10−5 and weight decay set to 5×10−5. The batch size was set to 1, and the maximum number of training epochs was set to 5,000. To avoid overfitting and ineffective training, this study adopted an early stopping strategy: training was terminated in advance when the loss of the validation set no longer decreased within 100 consecutive epochs.

Discuss

Panicles are essential reproductive organs in litchi. Accurate quantification of their phenotypic traits is of great significance for flower control, yield stabilization, and technological breakthroughs in litchi production. Therefore, this study proposes a model termed FQGR-Net to support the design of intelligent orchard management equipment and the breeding of elite litchi cultivars. Existing work indicates that the proportion of female flowers is considerably lower than that of male flowers, and litchi fruit set rate is positively correlated with female flower number [7,18]. Thus, during the flowering period, flower-counting devices integrated with FQGR-Net can be used to assess panicle characteristics. These estimates can then guide variable-rate flower-thinning machinery, enabling thinning intensity to be adjusted according to local flower density. Compared with uniform-intensity thinning, variable-rate thinning can reduce mechanical damage to the remaining flowers while maintaining the desired thinning rate. Meanwhile, for interdisciplinary horticultural studies on the effects of plant growth regulators on floral sex differentiation, FQGR-Net enables rapid and reliable quantification of treated panicles, which not only saves time and labor but also reduces counting errors caused by the small and densely clustered litchi flowers. In addition, studies have shown that litchi floral sex differentiation and dichasial inflorescence branching are regulated by meristem identity genes [45]. The combination of phenotypic analysis and genome sequencing allows the mapping of related QTLs, thereby helping to reduce total flower number, increase female flower ratio, and improve panicle fruit set rate through gene editing. Therefore, panicle phenotypes obtained by FQGR-Net can be used to select optimal parents for hybridization, which is conducive to breeding offspring with high fruit set rate and strong stress resistance. This section discusses the advantages and limitations of the proposed work, and outlines potential future directions.

Advantages of point-level annotation for multi-class Counting

Point-level annotation reduces labeling costs by omitting size information, particularly advantageous for dense targets like litchi flowers. The floral center enables precise point marking with minimal human error due to its lower occlusion compared to peripheral structures such as filaments. In dense scenarios with severe occlusion and indistinguishable boundaries, point annotation achieves higher counting accuracy at lower labeling costs. Current point-based counting algorithms typically generate single density maps per inference, limiting them to single-class applications. Real-world scenarios often require multi-class counting, including mixed-crop yield estimation and female-male ratio analysis. Deploying single-class models for such tasks necessitates multiple model runs, exponentially increasing computational costs. Extending these algorithms to multi-class frameworks enables simultaneous multi-category predictions, enhancing deployment flexibility and application scope.

FQGR-net's multi-scale feature Superiority

To address scale variations and background noise in detection tasks, we propose a scale fusion module and frequency-aware dilation module. These components mitigate intra-class and inter-class scale differences while suppressing background interference. By implementing lightweight convolutions, the model reduces parameters and accelerates detection. Ablation studies on our litchi flower dataset demonstrate 28% and 26% improvements in MAEA and MSEA, respectively. Comparative experiments confirm FQGR-Net's state-of-the-art performance with MAEA of 14.609 and MSEA of 23.013.

Multi-class vs. single-class Performance

FQGR-Net achieves optimal MAE in single-class wheat head counting, proving its efficacy in high-density scenarios. Generalization tests on sunflower and pear flower datasets further validate its adaptability across plant phenotypes.

Limitations and future Directions

Despite the promising counting performance and wide applicability of FQGR-Net, it has several limitations that point to meaningful directions for future improvement. The performance of the proposed work was limited by the quality of images. Figure S8 in Appendix A. illustrates several conditions that may degrade the identification performance. As a vision-based identification system, the proposed method is inherently sensitive to illumination variations, which is a common challenge in such approaches and limits its applicability under specific weather and daylight conditions. Future work could address this issue by incorporating additional training samples from overexposed and low-light environments, thereby increasing dataset diversity and enhancing the system's robustness to illumination changes. Apart from this, image resolution has a substantial impact on identification performance. Since the proposed work employs a morphology-based identification mechanism, blurred object boundaries in low-resolution images hinder the network from extracting discriminative features of the targets. However, high-resolution inputs inevitably raise computational overhead and reduce inference efficiency. In our experiments, when using images with a resolution of 3,840×2,160, the average inference time is approximately 0.58s per image, whereas for higher-resolution images of 6,000×4,000, the inference time increases significantly to 2.32s per image. This nearly fourfold increase in processing time indicates that the current framework is sensitive to input resolution, which may restrict its practical deployment in large-scale or real-time orchard monitoring scenarios. This limitation mainly arises from the dense prediction paradigm of density map regression, which requires high-resolution feature maps to preserve spatial details for accurate localization and counting. Although this design improves counting accuracy in dense and small-object scenarios, it also leads to increased memory consumption and computational overhead. Therefore, future work could explore alternative formulations, such as direct coordinate regression or set-based prediction paradigms, to reduce model complexity and improve inference speed while maintaining counting accuracy. Apart from this, different cultivars may yield various identification performance because of their morphological differences. However, close inter-plant spacing and extensive branch overlap in the orchard prevent the determination of exact flower numbers for respective cultivars. This limitation complicates assessment of cultivar-specific identification performance but provides an important foundation for future research. In addition, the current method only performs 2D counting and does not exploit the 3D spatial structure of flower panicles. Extending the framework to 3D flower panicle reconstruction using multi-view imagery or depth information may further improve counting robustness, especially under severe occlusion. Furthermore, we will incorporate a small amount of training data based on the IMX415 sensor in practical application scenarios to improve the device's accuracy in such settings. Meanwhile, expanding the categories of counting targets and investigating multi-class counting in intercropped agricultural systems, such as wheat-pea or soybean-sesame fields, could further enhance the applicability of the proposed method for yield estimation and precision agriculture. Future work will systematically investigate relationships among panicle sex differentiation, the male-to-female flower ratio, fruit-set rate, and litchi yield, and will examine how year, cultivar, and light intensity affect classification and counting. Insights from these studies will be used to develop a counting system that enables precision flower thinning, optimizes panicle sex structure, and improves fruit set, thereby providing data support for high-efficiency litchi cultivation.

Conclusions

In this study, a three-branch architecture network was designed to conduct a systematic study on density map regression algorithms using point annotation data, aiming to identify the gender classification of litchi flowers and detect the quantity of flowers in each category. Our FQGR-Net model demonstrated excellent performance in estimating the quantity of female and male flowers within a self-constructed litchi flower dataset, with its robustness validated through public datasets. Finally, a portable litchi phenotypic trait analyzer was developed based on the proposed model, achieving an accuracy over 80%. The developed network and device can realize the function of classifying and counting female and male flowers in complex orchards.

In summary, our research holds dual practical significances: on one hand, it can provide a core parameter set for phenomics research to support the construction of flower formation efficiency evaluation models; on the other hand, it can guide interdisciplinary regulation of litchi differentiation to increase the proportion of female flowers and reduce the number of male flowers. Achieving precise perception of flower quantity dynamics will significantly enhance the spatiotemporal resolution of orchard management, thereby improving fruit commercialization rates and industrial economic benefits through optimized resource allocation.
