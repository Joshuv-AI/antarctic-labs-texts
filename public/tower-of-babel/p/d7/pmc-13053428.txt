Clinical interpretation and statistical context in exploratory analyses of scalar translocation: a response
Authors: Imogen A M L van Beurden, H Chien Nguyen, Hans G X M Thomeer
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We want to thank the authors of Naveed et al. (2026) for their interest in our study on three-dimensional (3D) cochlear reconstructions for assessing electrode positioning and scalar translocation (STL) [1, 2]. To advance research in the field of cochlear implantation surgery, a constructive scientific discussion is crucial. Therefore, we welcome the discussion and appreciate the chance to further clarify certain scientific and clinical elements of our study.

In regards to sample size and statistical interpretation, we agree that results from small cohorts should be framed with caution. For this reason, our original manuscript presents the results from the analyses on 28 cadavers as exploratory and avoids predictive claims regarding the interpretation of logistic regression and Receiver Operation Characteristic (ROC) analyses. Furthermore, the conclusion of our paper states that further investigation in a large clinical cohort is necessary. However, in our opinion, the suggestion by authors of Naveed et al. (2026) that our conclusion is too confirmatory does not fully reflect the actual wording in the original article.

Second, we acknowledge the suggestion to apply Bonferroni corrections for multiple testing to reduce the probability of type I statistical errors. On the contrary, such corrections may also increase the risk of overlooking potential relevant trends in exploratory datasets. The analytical approach of our exploratory anatomical study was chosen to find potential predictive variables that require validation in larger prospective cohorts. Nevertheless, we agree that the importance of rigorous statistical methodology should be emphasized and that future prospective studies should incorporate appropriate statistical correction.

Thirdly, the letter to the editor expresses concerns about our binary approach in assessing STL. Although Iso-Mustajärvi et al. (2023) reported that micro-computed tomography reached similar accuracy in trauma assessment as histology, these methods cannot be applied to assess cochlear trauma after cochlear implantation in clinical settings [3]. Our study focused on an innovative method for evaluation of postoperative electrode scalar location, which may become feasible within clinical routine once further developed. Furthermore, given the exploratory nature of our study and the modest sample size, a non-binary classification of STL could have caused fragmentation of outcomes among additional categories, which may have reduced statistical ability. Yet, we recognize that more detailed differentiation between minor and major trauma, for instance by utilizing a radiological imaging tool and electrode scalar location grading scale, could reveal additional effects on the investigated cochlear parameters in validation studies [4].

In conclusion, we appreciate the perspective of the authors of Naveed et al. (2026) on the methodological and statistical aspects of our study. Further research using bigger sample sizes and improved clinically applicable techniques for assessment of STL is necessary to validate our findings. We hope that this clarification helps place our methodological choices into context and supports the intended cautious interpretation of our findings.
