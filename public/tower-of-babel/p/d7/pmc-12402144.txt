Diagnostic value of magnetic controlled capsule endoscopy in patients with chronic abdominal pain: a retrospective two-center study
Authors: Jing Li, Mudan Ren, Yumei Luo, Xinlan Lu, Xiaoyu Liu, Yahan Zhao, Huanhuan Sun, Dan Zhou, Shuixiang He, Guifang Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMagnetic controlled capsule endoscopy (MCE) overcomes limitations of capsule endoscopy and achieves one-time gastro-small intestinal joint examination. However, few studies have reported diagnostic value of MCE of gastrointestinal lesions in patients with abdominal pain. This study aims to investigate clinical applications of MCE in patients with abdominal pain. 465 patients suffered chronic abdominal pain and 366 asymptomatic subjects from two hospitals were included. Patient characteristics, MCE findings, incomplete examinations, examination time and adverse events were evaluated. In the abdominal pain group, disease spectrum was more diverse covering 45 types gastrointestinal lesions. Gastric lesions including atrophic gastritis, erosion, ulcer, carditis, polyp and submucosal tumor, and small intestinal lesions including lymphangiectasia, erosion, ulcer, angiotelectasis, bleeding and Crohn’s disease, had the higher detection rates (all P < 0.05). Some patients detected small intestinal lesions accompanied by diarrhea, weight loss and hematochezia. The abdominal pain group had the lower completion rate and longer transit time (all P < 0.05). In conclusion, MCE is beneficial to detect diverse disease spectrum and atrophic gastritis, ulcer, erosion, Crohn’s disease and small intestinal bleeding in patients with abdominal pain, and to find more lesions as one-time gastro-small intestine joint examination, with efficiency, safety, noninvasiveness and high completion rate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-12527-0.Keywords: Abdominal pain, Magnetically controlled capsule endoscopy, Gastric lesions, Small intestinal lesionsSubject terms: Diseases, Gastroenterology, Cancer imaging, Gastrointestinal cancer, Techniques and instrumentation

Introduction

Abdominal pain is a common gastrointestinal symptom, and the average consultation rate was 2.8%, of which about a third of the patients could not determine the rationale of abdominal pain1. Due to the special anatomical location and long length, the small bowel is a difficult organ to effective examination and direct observation. Computer tomography and magnetic resonance allow indirect and incomplete examination of the small bowel, and double-balloon enteroscopy improves the complete visual observation whereas technical difficulty and invasiveness limit the clinical application2,3. Studies have shown that capsule endoscopy (CE) is a feasible modality in the detection of small intestinal diseases, including refractory diarrhea-predominant irritable bowel syndrome, functional abdominal pain, small intestinal bleeding4,5. Based on the possibility that abdominal pain caused by gastric lesions, CE is still insufficient for the one-time complete diagnosis of abdominal pain.

Magnetic controlled capsule endoscopy (MCE) overcomes limitations of CE and improves the efficiency of endoscopic examination, which achieves one-time gastro-small intestinal joint examination6,7. Studies have shown that the overall diagnostic yield of MCE is comparable with that of gastroscopy to diagnose focal gastric lesions (90.4% sensitivity, 94.7% specificity, and 93.4% accuracy)8,9. However, few studies have reported the value of MCE in the detection of gastrointestinal diseases in patients with abdominal pain, and further correlation analysis between lesions and abdominal pain. Therefore, we investigated the clinical applications of MCE in patients with abdominal pain, and further analyzed the relationship between various positive manifestations and abdominal pain.

Results

Patients

A total of 831 patients were enrolled, including 465 patients suffered abdominal pain and 366 asymptomatic physical examinees for health management (Table 1). The overall diagnostic yields were 96.56% (449/465) and 97.81% (358/366), respectively. In the abdominal pain group, there were 289 males (62.15%) and 176 females (37.85%), of age ranging from 18 to 90 years (48.01 ± 14.17 years). In asymptomatic subjects, 221 were males and 114 were females, with the mean age 46.53 ± 15.64 years. No statistically significant difference detected on sex, age and overall diagnostic yield between the two groups (all P > 0.05, Table 1). All patients swallowed the capsule, in which 367 (78.92%) and 318 (86.89%) subjects accepted gastric and concurrent intestinal evaluation in the abdominal pain and asymptomatic groups, respectively. The remaining patients underwent only gastric examination. Among them, 65.67% (241/367) and 60.38% (192/318) detected both gastric and small intestinal lesions, respectively.

Detection rate of gastric and esophageal lesions

Twenty types of gastric lesions were detected in the abdominal pain group, and the asymptomatic group only has 13 types without difference in the overall detection rate (94.84% vs. 97.27%, P = 0.079, Table 2). In the abdominal pain group, the detection rates of atrophic gastritis (AG) (52.26% vs. 21.58%, P < 0.001), erosion (37.42% vs. 26.23%, P < 0.001), polyp (11.83% vs. 6.01%, P = 0.004), ulcer (4.52% vs. 1.91%, P = 0.039), carditis (3.44% vs. 0.55%, P = 0.004) and submucosal tumor (3.23% vs. 0.82%, P = 0.018) were higher, whereas with the lower detection rates of non-atrophic gastritis (NAG) (40.22% vs. 63.93%, P < 0.001, Table 2; Fig. 1). The detected remaining lesions were not differed between the two groups, including intestinal metaplasia, bile reflux, verrucous proliferation, angiotelectasis, mucosal bulging lesion, xanthoma, gastrorrhagia, gastric retention, acute erosive gastritis, gastric cancer, portal hypertensive gastropathy, exposed gastric fundic vein, Helicobacter pylori infection (all P > 0.05, Table 2).

Detection rate of small intestinal lesions

The overall detection rates of small intestinal lesions in the abdominal pain and asymptomatic group were 72.48% and 69.18%, covering 14 and 12 types, respectively, without significant difference (P = 0.343, Table 3). Among them, lymphangiectasia (27.79% vs. 19.50%, P = 0.011), ulcer (13.08% vs. 8.18%, P = 0.039), erosion (8.17% vs. 4.40%, P = 0.045), angiotelectasis (5.50% vs. 0.63%, P < 0.001), bleeding (1.91% vs. 0%, P = 0.036) and Crohn’s disease (1.91% vs. 0%, P = 0.036, Table 3) had the higher detection rates in the abdominal pain group (Figs. 3 and 4). The others were not discovered difference (all P > 0.05, Table 3).

Incomplete examinations, examination time and safety

Less patients completed MCE examination in the abdominal pain group (93.98% vs. 97.27%, P < 0.05, Table 1). The abdominal pain group had longer gastric transit time (GTT) (88.56 ± 38.32 min vs. 75.36 ± 25.12 min), small bowel transit time (SBTT) (300.34 ± 102.73 min vs. 256.56 ± 88.38 min) and overall transit time (OTT) (637.56 ± 150.14 min vs. 565.35 ± 122.78 min, all P < 0.001, Table 1). Capsule retention occurred in 2 patients in the abdominal pain group, with the separate diagnosis of gastritis and Crohn’s disease. Then 2 capsules spontaneously excreted within 3 weeks after confirming the position of the capsule by abdominal radiography. No other serious adverse events occurred.

Discussion

Chronic abdominal pain is a common symptom of the digestive system with complex and diverse causes including many gastrointestinal and systemic disorders, become a challenging complaint10. Digestive endoscopy is a visualized and effective modality to evaluate the digestive tract, but multiple and invasive endoscopes lead to the patient’s fear and poor compliance11. An endoscopic modality that can simultaneously observe multiple sites improves the efficiency of examination. This appeal is reinforced by the presence of both gastric and small intestinal lesions, such as gastrointestinal bleeding and nonsteroidal anti-infammatory drugs-related diseases and cirrhotic portal hypertension5,12,13. One-time gastro-small intestine joint examination and the detection of both gastric and small intestinal diseases can be achieved simultaneously by MCE6. However, few studies have reported diagnostic value of MCE in patients with abdominal pain.

CE is recommended as a common valid and intuitionistic method to assess the small intestine, providing a noninvasive diagnostic tool for patients with unexplained chronic abdominal pain14–16. In this study, the overall diagnostic yield of MCE was 96.56% in abdominal pain group, which was significantly higher than the limited diagnostic yield (20.9%) of CE16. It might be due to the blind spot in the stomach of CE, which also confirmed the high efficiency of MCE in one-time gastro-small intestine joint examination for disease detection. The accuracy and effectiveness of MCE in detection of gastric diseases confirmed in previous studies lay the foundation for this study8,9,17. And these studies and guidelines on MCE in asymptomatic individuals, older adults who cannot tolerate flexible gastroscopy, those with relative contraindications to gastroscopy and patients with acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding provide the indication basis18–20.

In this study, the disease spectrum was more diverse and complex in the abdominal pain group than asymptomatic group, regardless of esophageal, gastric or small intestinal lesions. It suggests that MCE is more beneficial for patients with abdominal pain from identifying more positive lesions. For the abdominal pain group, both gastric and small intestinal lesions were varied, and the detection rates of AG, polyp, erosion and ulcer were higher, yet the overall detection rates were undifferentiated between the two groups. This might be related to the high prevalence of NAG in the asymptomatic group, in view of the fact that NAG is the most common endoscopic finding in Chinese patients21. In addition, the small intestinal lymphoid follicular hyperplasia partly lacks of clinical symptoms. It is also reminded that endoscopists should not relax their vigilance for asymptomatic patients.

AG, as one of precancerous lesions with the annual incidence of gastric cancer of 0.1% in patients with AG during 5-year follow-up, deserves more attention and regular endoscopic surveillance22,23. It is worth noting that AG was predominant in the abdominal pain group, with some accompanied by intestinal metaplasia, while NAG was predominant in the asymptomatic group. This difference in gastritis spectrum also indicates that more attention should be paid to the patients with abdominal pain. In fact, the cascade of NAG, AG, intestinal metaplasia and dysplasia, gastric cancer indicates the necessity of long-term follow-up surveillance for gastritis24.

Among the 20 gastric lesions included in the abdominal pain group, erosion, ulcer, gastric retention, acute erosive gastritis, gastric cancer, portal hypertensive gastropathy and other diseases may be related to the symptoms of abdominal pain25. The higher detection rates of gastric erosion, ulcer, carditis, and submucosal tumor in patients with abdominal pain can be explained by the symptom of abdominal pain. Similarly, the higher detection rates and association exists in small intestinal ulcer, erosion, bleeding and Crohn’s disease25,26. It suggests endoscopists should perform endoscopic observation carefully for patients suffered abdominal pain, especially in detecting ulcer, erosion and bleeding.

Previous studies of CE showed that inflammatory lesions (erosion, Crohn’s disease and enteritis) were the most common positive findings16,27,28. It was consistent with the results of our study, given that lymphangiectasia and lymphoid follicular hyperplasia were not taken into account in the calculation of diagnostic yield of previous studies16,27,28. Laboratory tests of inflammation could highly improve the detection rate, including elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein level, and leukocytosis28–30. Intestinal lymphangiectasia may be secondary to inflammatory bowel disease, tumor, etc., which possibly accompanied by abdominal pain and diarrhea, although its association with abdominal pain has not been demonstrated31,32. Besides, 34 (9.26%) patients with small intestinal lesions in the abdominal pain group were accompanied by other symptoms including diarrhea, weight loss and hematochezia. Studies has shown that accompanying symptom of weight loss for patients suffered abdominal pain may increase the diagnostic yield by 17.6-fold of CE29,33. It suggests that clinicians should not relax their vigilance in patients with multiple gastrointestinal symptoms, especially for unexplained abdominal pain. Therefore, for patients with abdominal pain, accompanying symptoms and laboratory tests should be combined to assist in diagnosis and improve the diagnostic yield10.

In this study, the detection rate of small intestinal lesions in the abdominal pain group of MCE was 72.48%, which was higher than previous studies of CE (20.9-52.87%)16,30,34. Small intestinal tumors account for 1-3% of gastrointestinal tumors, with the higher diagnostic yield (4.63%) compared to previous studies on CE (2.1%)16,29,35. More than 60% of simultaneous gastric and small intestinal lesions in both groups illustrates the efficiency of the MCE. Besides, the differences of capsule endoscopy completion rate (CECR) may be due to the discrepancy of GTT, SBTT and OTT between the two groups, given that reducing GTT could improve the CECR36. The longer GTT, SBTT and OTT in the abdominal pain group may be related to abnormal gastrointestinal motility, structural or functional abnormalities of the digestive tract such as inflammation, spasm, stenosis, etc., caused by more disease spectrum and higher disease rates. Also, careful observation of gastric lesions under magnetic control could prolong GTT and OTT. It should be emphasized that CECRs of MCE in both groups in this study were higher than CE (70-90%), in that MCE enhances gastric emptying of the capsule via specific magnetic controlling36–38. These results indicated the effectiveness of MCE in the etiological diagnosis of abdominal pain. Barely adverse events without marked between-group differences also further confirmed the feasibility of MCE.

Overall, MCE can detect diverse disease spectrum in patients with abdominal pain, especially AG, ulcer, erosion, Crohn’s disease and small intestinal bleeding. Simultaneous gastro-intestinal examination is more suitable for disease screening. Besides, these special groups including older adults who cannot tolerate flexible gastroscopy, those with relative contraindications to gastroscopy and patients with acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding get more benefits from MCE18–20. In terms of technology and cost-effectiveness, MCE can actively and accurately control the movement and retention of the capsule through an external magnetic field compared with CE, and one-time gastro-small intestine joint examination and precise observation under magnetic control improve the detection rate6,7. In the future, it may be more technologically advanced to have biopsy and drug release function39. In addition, although MCE is more expensive, concurrent gastrointestinal examination, better patient compliance, application to special groups and higher detection rates improve its cost-effectiveness18–20,40. Also, synchronous colorectal examination improved the cost-effectiveness of MCE41.

There are limitations to this study. Firstly, owing to the retrospective study design, we could not confirm the Helicobacter pylori infection rates, family history and medication history such as antacids, aspirin, etc., and very few cases of capsule retention could not analyze the correlation between diagnosis and capsule retention. Prospective, large-size, high-quality, stratification studies need to be performed. Secondly, all diagnoses were made by experienced endoscopists at the two centers whereas diagnostic criteria might be variable among providers. Indeed, artificial intelligence during MCE can improve the accuracy of diagnosis and localization and reduce errors and bias42,43. Also, diagnostic analysis was not performed according to the duration of abdominal pain, sex and age, considering that different age-based and sex-based groups have different disease spectrums44. Finally, wireless MCE is insufficient for esophageal observation due to the short residence time in the esophagus, leading to the low detection rate. Detachable string MCE can realize accurate control and full observation of the esophagus whereas without wide application45,46.

In conclusion, MCE is helpful to detect diverse disease spectrum and AG, ulcer, erosion, Crohn’s disease and small intestinal bleeding in patients with abdominal pain, and to find more positive lesions as one-time gastro-small intestine joint examination, with efficiency, safety, noninvasiveness and high completion rate. Endoscopists should perform careful endoscopic observation for patients with abdominal pain, combined with other accompanying symptoms and laboratory tests when necessary. However, we should not relax our vigilance for asymptomatic patients. In the future, prospective, large-size, high-quality, stratification studies need to be performed to further explore the diagnostic value of MCE in patients with abdominal pain with different clinical characteristic.

Patients and methods

Patients

Patients suffered abdominal pain lasting more than three months or asymptomatic physical examinees underwent MCE in the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi ‘an Jiaotong University and Yulin Hospital the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi ‘an Jiaotong University from January 2020 to December 2023 were included in this study. There was no restriction on sex of patients aged > 8 years. Patients were suitable for MCE without the following conditions: gastrointestinal obstruction; difficulty in drinking or swallowing; delayed gastric emptying; impaired bowel movement; pacemaker, defibrillator, or other metallic implants; poor general condition; pregnancy. The retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committees of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (No: XJTU1AF2021LSK-208) and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was voluntarily obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardians.

MCE system and procedure

The components of MCE, gastrointestinal preparation and specific procedures have been elaborated in our previous study47. The MCE system and procedure was briefly explained to all subjects before examination. Patients wearing the waistcoat laid down on a bed attached to the guidance robot and ingested the capsule. When the capsule entered the stomach, the capsule was controlled by an external magnetic field for three-dimensional movement with the cooperation of changes in body position. If the small intestinal examination was required and preoperative intestinal cleansing has been completed, the capsule entered the “small bowel mode” without magnetic control after gastric examination, and patients left with the data recorder and returned it the following day. After finishing the examination, the data recorded by the portable recorder was imported into the computer workstation and read by two independent experienced gastroenterologists. For controversies, a re-check and even third-party assessment will be performed. All patients were followed up for 2 weeks. If the capsules were not excreted for more than 2 weeks, capsule retention occurred. Further magnetic scanning or abdominal radiography was performed to confirm the position of the capsule.

Data collection

The following information were collected: (1) Basic data: patient characteristics including age, sex, complaints and symptoms. (2) MCE results: esophageal lesions, gastric lesions and small intestinal lesions, such as cancer, ulcer, polyp, et al. (3) CECR, examination time including GTT, SBTT, and OTT, and adverse events, such as capsule retention. CECR refers to the proportion of all subjects for whom the capsule successfully completed the complete observation of the target digestive tract within the battery’s duration.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) and categorical data as number and percentage. Comparisons between groups for categorical variables were performed by descriptive analysis, Chisquare test and Fisher exact test. Student’s t test was used for quantitative variables. A final 2-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. SPSS 26.0 software was used for statistical analysis.
