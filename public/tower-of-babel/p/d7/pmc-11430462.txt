Group transfer radical polymerization for the preparation of carbon-chain poly(α-olefins)
Authors: Xianjin Wang, Shuo Wang, Silin Song, Yasu Chen, Hao Sun, Chen Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Radical polymerization is a powerful technique for producing a variety of polymeric materials. However, the chain transfer reaction impedes the formation of polymers from many common α-olefins such as propene and 1-butene using this method. Consequently, poly(α-olefins) are predominantly produced via coordination polymerization. To address this limitation, we have devised a strategy involving group transfer radical polymerization (GTRP) to facilitate the radical homopolymerization to access carbon-chain poly(α-olefins). This approach enables the precise construction of a diverse array of carbon-chain poly(α-olefins) with high molecular weights. Furthermore, by using nonconventional monomers, we extend the applicability of this technique to the copolymerization of α-olefins with acrylonitrile, paving the way for the synthesis of copolymers with different monomers. To investigate the properties of the polymers obtained by this method, one of the poly(α-olefins) is studied as an interphase layer material in anode-free Li metal batteries, and the results indicate the potential of the polymer in energy storage applications.

INTRODUCTION

Conventional radical polymerization is extensively used in the industrial fabrication of high molecular weight polymers, accounting for nearly half of all produced polymers (1). The dominance of radical polymerization emanates from its multiple advantages (2): (i) Its chain polymerization processes exhibit a remarkable tolerance to water and other trace impurities, ensuring compatibility with a diverse array of polymerization environments; (ii) its proficiency in polymerizing an extensive array of monomers, particularly vinyl monomers, thereby facilitating the efficient production of polyolefins (3–5). Despite its widespread adoption, conventional radical polymerization faces a series of inherent limitations and challenges. One notable example relates to the homopolymerization of α-olefins, leading to the formation of linear carbon-chain polymers. After forming secondary carbon-centered radicals from α-olefins and their derivatives through various initiators, chain transfer reactions with other monomers may ensue, giving rise to more stable allylic radicals by hydrogen atom transfer, which poses a substantial impediment to the effective assembly of the polymer chains (Fig. 1A) (6). As a result, except ethylene, other olefins—such as propene, 1-butene, and 4-methylpentene—are more commonly polymerized using coordination polymerization techniques instead of traditional radical methods due to these challenges (7–11). Furthermore, sequence control in chain-growth radical polymerization, with a specific focus on the main chain structure of the polymer, precisely modulates its physical properties (12, 13). Notwithstanding numerous efforts devoted to the synthesis of various polymer sequences, such as AB, ABB, or ABC based on diverse strategic approaches, achieving sequence-regulated polymers continues to be a long-standing objective (14–16). While monomer design presents a viable pathway to address these issues, recent research has predominantly honed in on established vinyl monomers, to a large extent neglecting the construction of polymeric monomers and the exploration of polymerization models (17–24).

Functional group transfer serves as a powerful tool in the tapestry of small molecular architecture, not only facilitating the dual functionalization of alkenes and alkynes but also enabling direct C─H functionalization (25, 26). In 1999, Sato (27, 28) reported the radical polymerization of 2-thiocyanatoethyl vinyl ether, which proceeded through intramolecular transfer of the cyano group, resulting in a polymer that contained both ether and thioether linkages within its main chain. Very recently, Li et al. (29) have expanded upon this method, applying the use of thiocyanate monomers as precursors for homopolymerization. While both examples explored the transfer of cyano group from S atom to construct S-containing hetero-chain polymers, the transfer from C atom by cleaving inert C─C bond has never been investigated to construct more valuable carbon-chain polymers (Fig. 1B). In addressing these limitations, we took cues from our previous exploration into the realm of functional group transfer (30, 31). Here, we put forth group transfer radical polymerization (GTRP) paradigm specifically designed to surmount the polymerization barriers associated with chain transfer reactions in α-olefins through the strategic use of functional group transfer (Fig. 1C) (32). The radical polymerization commences with the reactive alkyl radical species a, which instigates a 5-exo-dig addition to the cyano or other migrating groups. This reaction yields the cyclic iminyl radical b, an intermediate that undergoes C─C bond scission followed by cyano migration. The ensuing propagating radical species c is primed to rapidly incorporate another α-olefin monomer, steering the polymerization process toward the synthesis of high-weight polymers under controlled conditions. In this discourse, we apply GTRP into both homopolymerization and copolymerization that harnesses group transfer to synthesize carbon-chain poly(α-olefins), showcasing cyano group migration as a pivotal mechanism. Consequently, our proposed strategy obviates the radical polymerization obstacles of linear α-olefins associated with chain transfer, charting a course for the fabrication of multiply functionalized polymers (Fig. 1D).

RESULTS

Our investigation began with an exploration of the optimal conditions for the homopolymerization of (3-butenyl)malononitrile monomer M1, which is easily synthesized within one step from inexpensive malononitrile and 4-bromobutene. We selected azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN) as the initiator and carried out polymerization at 65°C without using solvent. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analysis revealed a number-average molecular weight (Mn) of 115 kg mol−1 and distribution (Đ) of 1.85 (Fig. 2, entry 1). Increasing the concentration of AIBN from 2 to 4 mol % enhanced the yield of the polymer to 96% but decreased the molecular weight (Fig. 2, entry 2). Various initiators were also evaluated for their effectiveness in the polymerization process. Using 2,2′-azobis(2,4-dimethyl)valeronitrile (ABVN) or peroxide-based initiators including di-tert-butyl peroxide, benzoyl peroxide (BPO), and tert-butyl peroxyacetate (TBPA), we achieved good polymer yields with Đ values fluctuating between approximately 1.55 and 1.60 (Fig. 2, entries 3 to 6). Moreover, considering industrial scalability, solvents were incorporated into the reaction matrix with TBPA as the initiator, also leading to the successful synthesis of homopolymers (Fig. 2, entries 7 to 9). The resulting polymers were light-yellow solids with a degree of elasticity. These polymers were found to be soluble in a few polar solvents such as N,N′-dimethylformamide (DMF) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) upon heating, whereas they remained insoluble in other common organic solvents such as tetrahydrofuran, dichloromethane (DCM), chloroform, and n-hexane.

The polymer chain architecture was elucidated using 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, 1H-1H correlation spectroscopy (COSY), 1H-13C heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC), and 1H-13C heteronuclear multiple bond correlation (HMBC), which provided evidence for the migration of cyano group within the polymer backbone and the structure of poly((3-butenyl)malononitrile) (P1) emerging as a structural polymer with head to tail connected (Fig. 3). The creation of well-defined structure polymers is particularly pivotal in synthetic polymer chemistry as it bestows upon materials bespoke physical properties. The characteristic aliphatic resonance observed from 2.88 to 3.13 parts per million (ppm) was ascribed to protons H(b,e) situated proximal to the cyano groups, which was coupled with Ha, Hc, and Hd (Fig. 3A). The HMBC spectrum showed that the carbon of the cyano group coupled to Ha, Hb, Hc, and Hd (Fig. 3D). In contrast to the monomer, the aliphatic resonance typically around 4.79 to 4.71 ppm assigned to He became undetectable (figs. S1 and S2). Organic elemental analysis quantified the elemental constituents of the polymers (C: 69.97%, H: 6.71%, N: 23.31%), mirroring the calculated percentages for the repeating unit. Verification of the cyano group in the polymer was corroborated by its 13C NMR signature, presenting chemical shifts at 121.6 and 121.4 ppm. The result of matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization–time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) analysis (fig. S3) was also consistent with the above results. The kinetic studies also confirmed the migration of the cyano group as hypothesized during the GTRP process. Notably, polymers with Mn more than 75.0 kg mol−1 were obtained even at a low conversion rate, demonstrating typical characteristics of free radical chain polymerization (Fig. 3F).

Notably, P1 was produced more than 90% yield on a 100-g scale, indicating the practicality of this method (Fig. 4). P1 exhibits a structure similar to that of hydrogenated nitrile butadiene rubber (HNBR). The production process of HNBR faces challenges, including high costs, metal contamination, the use of high-pressure hydrogen gas, and suboptimal hydrogenation rates due to precious metal catalysis in the hydrogenation of NBR. In contrast, this method used for P1 avoids metal-catalyzed hydrogenation and achieves a saturation degree of 100% (33). We further explored the generality of GTRP protocol by preparing and polymerizing a variety of monomers with different structures. A yellow solid polymer P2 was yielded with an exceptional 99% yield, from the monomer mounted with an extra alkyl side chain at the vinyl position. The formation of quaternary carbon center did not impede the cyano migration instead promoted the homopolymerization. The monomers bearing either phenyl or alkyl adjacent to the malononitrile moiety led to polymers P3 and P4. Substitution of a cyano group with either ester group (to create P5 to P7) or amide group (to create P8 to P10) also allowed for the formation of corresponding polymers with yields ranging between 45 and 90%. Steric hindrance around the migrating cyano group did not inhibit polymerization, because the formation of stable tertiary radicals, subsequent to the cyano migration, may facilitate chain growth. The integration of quaternary carbon atoms into the polymer’s backbone resulted in the formation of P11. To our delight, even the benzothiazolyl group successfully migrated during the substitution of the cyano group (34), culminating in polymer P12. The thermal characteristics of these polymers were characterized using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) (figs. S14 to 17 and S25 to 27). The varied substitutions on the polymer backbones caused the observed glass transition temperatures (Tg) to span a broad range from 12.9° to 77.4°C. Tg variations in polymers like P6 and P11 can be attributed to the restricted mobility imposed by asymmetric side chains that feature quaternary carbon atoms. Overall, most of the polymers displayed exceptional thermal stabilities with decomposition temperatures (Td,5%) exceeding 300°C.

The applicability of GTRP method was further illustrated in the synthesis of copolymers. The copolymerization of α-olefin MCo-1 with olefin comonomer MCo-2 readily proceeded by manipulating the polarity of radical intermediates during polymerization process (Fig. 5). This was achieved by simply substituting a cyano group of malononitrile with electron-donating group such as phenyl or alkyl groups. The resulting nucleophilic radical, ensuing from the group transfer, is unable to undergo self-addition to another molecule of MCo-1 that contains electron-rich olefin. This peculiarity paves the way for the incorporation of alternative electron-deficient α-olefin MCo-2, allowing the assembly of meticulously structured copolymers.

The refinement of conditions for the copolymerization of 2-phenyl hexenenitrile M13 and acrylonitrile was performed using ABVN as an initiator at 75°C across an array of solvents, including N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP), DMSO, DMF, and chlorobenzene, and culminated in the synthesis of copolymers exhibiting dispersity values (1.18 to 1.22) compared to homopolymerization process (Fig. 6, entries 1 to 4). It was observed that solvent had little impact on the Mn and Đ of the resulting copolymers. Conversely, the substitution of ABVN with other initiators like AIBN, BPO, and tert-butyl peroxide resulted in suboptimal monomer conversion rates (Fig. 6, entries 5 to 7). The GTRP copolymerization process yielded a light-yellow solid product, demonstrating solubility in DCM and the miscibility with polar solvents such as DMF and DMSO. An intriguing structural attribute of the resulting copolymers was successfully constructed with the ratio of 1:2 (M13: acrylonitrile) when a slight excess of 2-phenyl hexenenitrile was confirmed using 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy (figs. S4 and S5). To verify the copolymerization with acrylonitrile instead of homopolymerization, the diffusion ordered spectroscopy spectra of the resultant product exhibited that all the characteristic signals shared single one diffusion coefficient with a sharp peak (fig. S6). We tried to replace acrylonitrile with other monomers, such as acrylic ester and acrylamide, and we can also get corresponding copolymerization products.

Polyacrylonitrile (PAN)–based copolymers are widely recognized as crucial precursors for the production of high-performance carbon fibers. In pursuit of broadening the array of PAN-type copolymers, we embarked on the synthesis of monomers adorned with diverse side-chain functionalities (Fig. 7). Our initial endeavors involved the preparation of monomers bearing various phenyl substituents—specifically, methyl, bromo, and trifluoromethyl groups. These monomers were readily polymerized, yielding a series of corresponding copolymers (Co-2 to Co-4). The method was extended further to include copolymers with benzylic moieties, five- and six-membered carbocycles, as well as oxy-heterocycle (Co-5 to Co-8). The introduction of dual electron-donating substituents around cyano moiety led to the generation of copolymers featuring quaternary carbon centers (Co-9). During this process, not only was the cyano migration tactic efficacious but also the steric hindrance exhibited minimal disruption to the copolymerization. In addition, the thermal attributes of these copolymers were assessed by TGA and DSC (figs. S18 to 20 and S21 to 24). It is noteworthy that the Tg of all polymers has only one value. This consistency in Tg not only underscores the homogeneity within the polymer structure but also serves as testament to the successful implementation of our strategic approach to constructing copolymers.

To attain a deeper understanding on the properties of the polymers synthesized via this method, we explored their preliminary applications in energy storage by using polymer P1 as an interphase layer in anode-free lithium (Li) metal battery. According to previous reports, polymers containing the cyano group (─CN), such as PAN, have demonstrated notable potential for protecting the deposited Li metal and enhance the lithiophilicity to facilitate uniform Li-ion flux and deposition (35–38). However, the inherent brittleness of PAN has limited its adaptability to the volume changes during the plating and stripping processes of Li metal. Notably, P1 displayed massively enhanced toughness of 39.4 MJ/m3, compare with only 0.5 MJ/m3 for PAN (Fig. 8A and figs. S7 and S8). It may be attributed to the unique alignment of ─CN groups in P1, which likely increases molecular flexibility including rotation, vibration, and segmental motion. These alterations enhance the free volume and interaction among the macromolecular chains, resulting in enhanced interaction between P1 and Li ions, as confirmed by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Fig. 8, B and C, and fig. S9). The proportion of the coordinated ─CN in P1 was considerably higher than that in PAN, improving the regulation for Li-ion absorption and transport, as evidenced by a high exchange current density (i0) of 0.34 mA/cm2 in Tafel plots based on symmetric Li/Li batteries (fig. S10).

The improved mechanical properties and interaction with Li ions have prompted us to further investigate the regulation of P1 on the Li plating and striping processes in Li/Cu batteries (Fig. 8D). The Li/P1-Cu battery demonstrated superior cycling stability for over 350 cycles, with an average Coulombic efficiency (CE) of ~99% at 1 mA/cm2 and 1 mAh/cm2 (Fig. 8E and fig. S11). In comparison, the batteries based on bare Cu and PAN-Cu exhibited inferior cycling stability with merely 144 and 157 cycles, respectively. Notably, dense Li deposition with high uniformity was achieved on P1-Cu, in stark contrast to the whisker-like Li growth observed on bare Cu and PAN-Cu, as verified by laser confocal scanning microscopy (Fig. 8, F to H, and fig. S12). The promoted morphology and reversibility of Li deposition could be explained by the LiF- and Li3N-rich solid electrolyte interphase based on P1-Cu (fig. S13), which could effectively suppress the parasitic reactions between the Li deposition and electrolyte. We further prepared anode-free Li metal batteries based on the P1 interphase layer (Fig. 8I). At a practical areal capacity of 2.9 mAh/cm2, the P1-Cu/LiFePO4 anode-free battery showed an average CE of ~99% during 200 cycles, outperforming 54 and 93 cycles based on bare Cu and PAN-Cu with the same capacity retention, respectively (Fig. 8J). These results indicate that the polymer P1 represents a promising interphase layer material for electrochemical energy storage applications.

DISCUSSION

In conclusion, we have disclosed a GTRP method, which facilitates the formation of high molecular weight polymers endowed with precision in terms of composition—spanning both homopolymers and copolymers—as well as intricate macromolecular structures. This versatile technique enables the generation of linear poly(α-olefins) through a radical homopolymerization process. By adjusting the monomer polarity, we have achieved the synthesis of PAN-type copolymers. This method features the production of a broad diversity of polymers, the easy availability of monomers, and the industrialization potential. Moreover, we explored the preliminary use of poly((3-butenyl)malononitrile) as an interphase layer material in anode-free Li metal batteries and validated its potential for electrochemical energy storage applications. Also, our research endeavors continue to explore and use the GTRP strategy for crafting unprecedented polymer structures.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals

All reactions were maintained under a nitrogen atmosphere unless otherwise stated. The synthesis process of polymeric monomers was carried out in references (39–44). Some of the chemical reagents were used without further purification.

Characterizations

Mn and Đ of all polymers were determined by GPC using the SHIMADZU LC-20 AD XR system equipped with Shodex GPC KD-806 M columns. High-performance liquid chromatography–grade DMF with 50 mM LiBr was used as an eluent at 1.0 ml/min at 50°C. Polystyrene standards were used for relative molecular weight calibration for systems. Samples were configured at 0.5 to 5 mg/ml and filtered through a 0.22-μm nylon filter before injection. MALDI-TOF MS was performed using MALDI TOFTOF 7090, SHIMADZU. DSC measurements of polymers were performed on a TA Instruments DSC250 analyzer at a heating and cooling rate of 10°C/min. TGA of polymers was measured on TA Instruments TGA550, and the sample was heated at a rate of 10°C/min under a nitrogen flow of 100 ml/min.

Electrochemical measurements

To investigate the interaction between polymer (P1 and PAN) and lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI), 4 mg P1 and 3 mg LiTFSI were dissolved in 2 ml of DMF. The above solution was then coated on potassium bromide, followed by drying under vacuum at 100°C for 24 hours to obtain the P1/LiTFSI sample. The preparation process of bare P1, bare PAN, and PAN/LiTFSI samples was the same with that of P1/LiTFSI. All the electrochemical measurements were performed at 25°C in a thermostatic test chamber (Neware MHW-200). FTIR was conducted on Nicolet 560 over a spectral width of 400 to 4000 cm−1 at a resolution of 2 cm−1. Scanning electron microscopy was conducted for observation of Li deposition morphology on a FEI-Quanta 250 field at an acceleration voltage of 5 kV. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopic analysis was performed on a PHI 5000 Versaprobe III. All the samples were transferred to the inlet chamber through an air-isolated chamber. All the binding energies were calibrated with the C1s peak at 284.8 eV.
