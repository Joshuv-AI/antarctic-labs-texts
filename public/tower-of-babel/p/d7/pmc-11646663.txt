PTPRJ is a negative regulator of insulin signaling in neuronal cells, impacting protein biosynthesis, and neurite outgrowth
Authors: Jannis Ulke, Simran Chopra, Otsuware Linda‐Josephine Kadiri, Peter Geserick, Vanessa Stein, Sahar Cheshmeh, André Kleinridders, Kai Kappert
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCentral insulin resistance has been linked to the development of neurodegenerative diseases and mood disorders. Various proteins belonging to the enzyme family of protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs) act as inhibitors of insulin signaling. Protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type J (PTPRJ) has been identified as a negative regulator in insulin signaling in the periphery. However, the impact of PTPRJ on insulin signaling and its functional role in neuronal cells is largely unknown. Therefore, we generated a Ptprj knockout (KO) cell model in the murine neuroblast cell line Neuro2a by CRISPR‐Cas9 gene editing. Ptprj KO cells displayed enhanced insulin signaling, as shown by increased phosphorylation of the insulin receptor (INSR), IRS‐1, AKT, and ERK1/2. Further, proximity ligation assays (PLA) revealed both direct interaction of PTPRJ with the INSR and recruitment of this phosphatase to the receptor upon insulin stimulation. By RNA sequencing gene expression analysis, we identified multiple gene clusters responsible for glucose uptake and metabolism, and genes involved in the synthesis of various lipids being mainly upregulated under PTPRJ deficiency. Furthermore, multiple Ca2+ transporters were differentially expressed along with decreased protein biosynthesis. This was accompanied by an increase in endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress markers. On a functional level, PTPRJ deficiency compromised cell differentiation and neurite outgrowth, suggesting a role in nervous system development. Taken together, PTPRJ emerges as a negative regulator of central insulin signaling, impacting neuronal metabolism and neurite outgrowth.

INTRODUCTION

More than half a billion people are affected by diabetes worldwide, accounting for around 10% of the world's adult population. 1 Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is characterized by insulin resistance, a condition in which the body fails to respond to insulin appropriately. Insulin resistance, often triggered by diet‐induced obesity, 2 occurs in the main insulin‐sensitive organs, including muscle, liver, and adipose tissue, but can also manifest in the brain independent of peripheral insulin resistance. 3 Numerous studies indicate that central insulin resistance might be a common denominator of T2D, obesity, and cognitive impairments. 4 , 5 , 6 Recently, studies suggested that insulin resistance in the brain may also contribute to diseases of the central nervous system, such as Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease, and major depressive disorder. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 However, the exact mechanisms behind the association between these conditions remain unknown. 7 , 11

Protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs) constitute a large group of enzymes that remove phosphate groups from tyrosine residues of target proteins, thereby modulating numerous signaling pathways involved in metabolism. 12 , 13 Defined PTPs act as endogenous inhibitors of insulin signaling by dephosphorylating tyrosine residues of the insulin receptor (INSR). 14 Aberrant PTP activity has been linked to obesity and T2D, making them attractive pharmacological targets for the treatment of T2D. 15 , 16 The protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type J (PTPRJ), known as density‐enhanced phosphatase‐1 (DEP‐1), belongs to the type III receptor‐type PTP (RPTP) subfamily and has been identified as a negative regulator of insulin signaling in peripheral tissues and is overexpressed in obese mice. 17 , 18 PTPRJ was able to dephosphorylate a phosphopeptide resembling the insulin receptor with the highest activity of all 35 tested PTPs. 19 We have further shown that mice deficient in PTPRJ exhibited enhanced insulin sensitivity in skeletal muscle and liver tissue, leading to improved glucose tolerance, and reduced body weight. 17 , 20 Shintani et al. further substantiated these findings and identified specific tyrosine sites of the INSR that are dephosphorylated by PTPRJ. 21

Insulin supports the overall growth of neurites, facilitating the development and regeneration of the vertebrate nervous system. 22 , 23 Furthermore, type III RPTPs have been implicated in neuritogenesis. 24 For example, PTPRO, a homolog of PTPRJ, plays a crucial role in regulating axon growth and guidance. 25 , 26 Recently, PTPRJ has been demonstrated to be crucial for the correct projection of retinal axons during development. Ptprj KO mice displayed defects with pathfinding at the chiasm and the formation of the retinocollicular topographic map. 27 Similarly, various PTPs, such as DUSP23 and PTPRS, are crucial for neuronal differentiation. 28 , 29 However, the mechanistic and physiological regulatory function of PTPRJ in insulin‐dependent neuritogenesis and neuronal differentiation remains elusive. Therefore, we created a Ptprj knockout (KO) in Neuro2a (N2a) cells using CRISPR‐Cas9 technology. Subsequently, we examined PTPRJ's interaction with the INSR and its impact on insulin signaling. Moreover, the consequence of PTPRJ deficiency on protein biosynthesis was assessed. Functionally, we investigated neurite outgrowth and neuronal differentiation in PTPRJ‐deficient neurons in conjunction with insulin supplementation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell culture

Mouse Neuro 2a (N2a) cells (American Type Culture Collection) were maintained in high glucose DMEM (Sigma‐Aldrich) containing 4.5 g/L glucose, L‐glutamine, sodium pyruvate, sodium bicarbonate supplemented with 10% FBS (Merck), 1× MEM non‐essential amino acids solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 100 units/mL penicillin G and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (PAN Biotech). For all experiments cells were seeded on crystal‐grade virgin polystyrene that was standard tissue culture (TC)‐treated. In brief, cells were plated at a density of 3.0 × 104 cells/cm2 and grown to confluence if not stated otherwise. To differentiate the cells, the medium was changed to high glucose DMEM containing 0.5% FBS supplemented with 1 mM dbcAMP. For activation of insulin/IGF‐1 signaling pathways, cells were starved with DMEM media without FBS for 4 h before insulin or IGF‐1 stimulation. For immunoprecipitated INSR the pan‐PTP inhibitor sodium orthovanadate (Sigma‐Aldrich) was added for the last hour of starvation at a concentration of 1 mM. To inhibit INSR activity, the inhibitor S961 (Selleck Chemicals) was added to the cells at a concentration of 100 nM for 1 h. All experiments presented in this study were conducted using cell passages ranging from 30 to 45.

CRISPR‐Cas9 gene editing

Ribonucleoprotein‐based CRISPR editing from Synthego was used to knockout the Ptprj gene in N2A cells. Three different gRNAs targeting an N‐terminal region within exon 3 of the Ptprj coding sequence were designed using Synthego CRISPR Design Tool (design.synthego.com). This tool selects gRNAs with the highest potential editing efficiency and the lowest off‐target effects. For negative controls (wild type; WT) a non‐targeting gRNA (GCACUACCAGAGCUAACUCA) was employed. The combination of Cas9 nuclease from Streptococcus pyogenes (SpCas9) and gRNAs formed ribonucleoproteins that were used to transfect the cells. Both components were purchased from Synthego. For transfection, the “Immortalized Cell Lipofection Protocol” from Synthego was used 30 and double amounts of lipofectamin were required to improve knockout efficiency. The cell pool with the highest insertion and deletion (indel) percentage (16%) was selected for the isolation of single clones through limiting dilution. For verification of Ptprj KO clones at protein and DNA level, anti‐PTPRJ immunoblot analysis and Sanger DNA sequencing of the PCR products that encompassed the CRISPR target sites were used. Indel percentage was analyzed with the online Synthego ICE analysis tool (2019. v3.0) utilizing the ICE algorithm (Inference of CRISPR Edits), which allows for reliable analysis of CRISPR edits using Sanger sequencing data. 31 For subsequent experiments, Ptprj KO and WT clones were either pooled (25% of each clone), generating WT and Ptprj KO cell pools, or analyzed individually, as indicated.

Immunoblotting

After insulin treatment, cells were washed with ice‐cold PBS twice. Protein lysates were prepared using lysis buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris HCl, 1% Triton‐X, 0.25% sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM EDTA, PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitor, cOmplete™ EDTA‐free Protease‐Inhibitor‐Cocktail tablets (Roche) followed by quantification with BCA Protein Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Subsequently, 15 μg of total protein was separated by using a 10% gel sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) followed by transfer to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The primary and secondary antibodies used to detect proteins and phosphorylation sites of interest are listed in Table S1. HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies and Immobilon ECL Ultra Western HRP Substrate (Merck) were employed for visualization. The data presented are from one representative experiment, which was repeated four times if not stated otherwise. Densitometric analyses were performed using ImageLab software 6.0.1 (Bio‐Rad).

Immunoprecipitation

For immunoprecipitation of insulin receptor‐associated protein complexes, 1 mg of total protein lysate was diluted in 500 μL IP buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris; 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X‐100, PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitor, cOmplete™ EDTA‐free Protease‐Inhibitor‐Cocktail tablets, 1 mM Vanadate, 1 mM PMSF, pH 7.4. In brief, 3 μL of INSR antibody (#3025, 1:1000) (Cell Signaling) was added to lysates and rotated end‐over‐end for 4 h, following incubation with 10 μL of Dynabeads™ Protein G (Invitrogen) rotating for 1 h at 4°C. Subsequently, the pellet was washed three times with 300 μL of PBS‐T and 1× with PBS. Precipitation of PTPRJ from cell lysates was performed by incubating 1 mg total protein with 30 μL of WGA (wheat germ agglutinin) agarose beads (Merck) and rotated end‐over‐end for 3 h at 4°C, followed by three washing steps with EDTA‐free IP buffer. The precipitates were dissolved in reducing SDS sample buffer supplemented with 10% β‐mercaptoethanol and boiled for 3 min at 95°C.

Proximity ligation assay

N2a cells were seeded onto a Nunc™ Lab‐Tek™ II Chamber Slide™ System (154534P, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and cultured in complete growth media for a duration of 48 h followed by starvation for 4 h and stimulation with 100 nM insulin for 15 min. After rinsing the cells twice with ice‐cold PBS, they were immobilized on the slide by treating them with 4% PFA at room temperature for 10 min. The cells were then subjected to two rounds of PBS washing, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100 in PBS for a period of 10 min, and again washed twice. Non‐specific binding sites were blocked using Duolink® Blocking Solution (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature. A hydrophobic pen was used to create boundaries around each well after removing the media chamber slides to prevent reagent spillage into adjacent wells. Cells were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies (1:50 dilution, Table S1) in Duolink® Antibody Diluent (Sigma‐Aldrich) within a humid chamber at 4°C overnight. Finally, the Duolink® PLA Fluorescence Protocol provided by Merck was used, and DAPI‐stained cells were imaged with an epifluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axio Observer 7).

Neurite outgrowth

N2a cells were seeded in a density of 0.1 × 104 cells/well in Falcon® 96‐well Clear Flat Bottom TC‐treated Culture Microplates (Corning Incorporated) and cultured in complete growth media, as described above. After 24 h, the media was removed, and cells were washed with 200 μL PBS. Subsequently, differentiation media containing high glucose DMEM, 0.5% FBS, and 1 mM dbcAMP, either with or without 100 nM insulin or 10 nM IGF‐1, was added to the cells.

The Incucyte® S3 Live‐Cell Analysis System (Sartorius AG) was used to analyze neurite dynamics at 37°C and 5% CO2. Plates were scanned every 3 h over a 72 h period using a 20× objective. Four images per well were captured and analyzed for neurite length/cell body cluster and branch points/cell body cluster using an automated IncuCyte® Neurotrack software module, as detailed previously. 32 The masks/filters adjustment for Neurotrack phase contrast image analysis were as follows: Segmentation mode: Texture; Hole fill: 0; Min cell width: 10 μm; Cell‐body cluster filter, min: 10 μm2, max: 6000 μm2; Neurite filtering: Best; Neurite sensitivity: 0.4 μm; and Neurite width: 2 μm. To determine the cell growth in a kinetic manner, cells were monitored every 3 h in the Incucyte® S3 Live‐Cell Analysis System for 7 days at 37°C and 5% CO2.

Protein synthesis assay

WT and Ptprj KO cell pools were seeded in 96‐well plates, cultured for 3 days, and starved for 4 h before they were left resting or exposed to 100 nM insulin for 2 h or 16 h prior to the period of protein labeling. Protein synthesis was measured using the Protein Synthesis Assay Kit (Red) (ab235634, Abcam) according to the manufacturer's protocol. 33 To obtain a sufficiently high fluorescent signal protein label concentration had to be increased to 3×. Cycloheximide, an inhibitor of protein biosynthesis, served as an experimental control. Fluorescence was measured with the Incucyte® S3 Live‐Cell Analysis System. Segmentation mode: top‐hat; Radius: 300 μm; Threshold: 0.2 GCU/0.1 RCU; Min. area: 80 μm, Edge sensitivity: −10; Acquisition time (green): 300 ms; Acquisition time (red): 400 ms; Objective: 20×.

RNA sequencing

Individual WT and Ptprj KO cell clones were cultured as described above. Cells were grown to 80% confluence or differentiated for 72 h. Subsequently, cells were washed with PBS twice. Total RNA was isolated using RNAeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). RNA sequencing (RNAseq) was performed with a commercially available service (BGI Tech Solutions) applying the Strand‐Specific Transcriptome Library Construction Protocol (DNBSEQ). All RNA samples had a RIN value of 10 and a 28S:18S ratio from 2.0 to 2.4, as determined with a fragment analyzer. Thus, the RNA quality of all samples was considered to be very good. After the mRNA purification from the total RNA pool with oligo(dT)‐attached magnetic beads, mRNA molecules were fragmented into small pieces using a fragmentation reagent. Subsequently, mRNA fragments were reverse transcribed into cDNA, end‐repaired, and an adapter was ligated to the fragments. Double‐stranded cDNA was purified and enriched by PCR amplification. Double‐stranded PCR products were heat‐denatured and circularized. The library was amplified with phi29 to make DNA nanoball (DNB). The DNBs were loaded into the patterned nanoarray, and single‐end 50 (pair‐end 100/150) base reads were generated using combinatorial Probe‐Anchor Synthesis (cPAS). BGI conducted pathway and GO bioinformatics analyses using their proprietary data mining system, Dr. TOM. Changes in gene expression, whether upregulated or downregulated, were quantified as log2FC, which signifies the log‐transformed fold change (log2FC = log2[B] − log2[A], with A and B representing gene expression values from WT and Ptprj KO cell clones).

Glucose uptake assay

WT and Ptprj KO cell pools were seeded in 96‐well plates, cultured for 2 days. Cells were starved with starvation medium including DMEM without glucose and sodium pyruvate + L‐glutamine (Gibco) for 4 h. Subsequently, starvation medium was discarded and cells were washed two times with pre‐warmed 1× HiMedia Krebs‐Ringer Bicarbonate Buffer (KRB). Then, 2‐NBDG diluted in KRB at a final concentration of 60 μM was added to the cells incubated for 30 min at 37°C and 5% CO2. KRB with 0.1% DMSO without 2‐NBDG served as autofluorescence control. Finally, after the cells were washed with 200 μL/well of PBS, 100 μL of PBS was added to the cells. Fluorescence was measured with Tecan Infinite M Plex at Ex = 485 nm and Em = 535 nm. Amount of flashes: 25. Background fluorescence (samples without 2‐NBDG) was subtracted from respective samples. For normalization, cell densities were assessed using a crystal violet assay, as described by Feoktistova et al. 34

Method for assessing the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR)

Pooled Ptprj KO and WT cells were seeded at a density of 10,000 cells per well in an Agilent Seahorse XFe96/XF Pro cell culture microplate. The assay was conducted the following day. The basal ECAR was measured in Seahorse XF Glycolytic Rate Assay Medium, which included glucose (10 mM), glutamine (2 mM), and pyruvate (1 mM).

Glucose measurement

All four WT and Ptprj KO clones were grown to confluence and starved with DMEM media without FBS for 4 h before insulin stimulation (100 nM) for 24 h or left resting. Glucose was measured photometrically on a Roche cobas analyzer.

Statistical analysis

Results presented as bar graphs show mean and standard deviation. Statistical differences between the Ptprj KO and WT control group were determined using the non‐parametric Mann–Whitney U test. For comparison of multiple groups in the case of the protein synthesis assay, analysis of variance with post hoc Tukey correction was used. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0.0 (GraphPad Software Inc.). p < .05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Generation of Ptprj knockout cells using CRISPR‐Cas9

After isolating single cells and performing clonal expansion, we screened 63 clonal colonies. We detected four individual clones that exhibited the absence of PTPRJ protein expression validated by immunoblotting (Figure 1A,B). RNA sequencing also revealed a significant reduction of Ptprj mRNA in the KO clones (Figure 1C, p < .0001, Log2FC = −1.23). WT clones were isolated from cell pools treated with Cas9 and non‐targeting gRNA. Figure S1A,B shows a representative indel analysis of WT and Ptprj KO cell clones by DNA sequencing of genomic PCR products. Taken together, we successfully generated four complete Ptprj KO clones that were compared to four WT clones in subsequent experiments.

Ptprj knockout leads to increased phosphorylation of insulin signaling components

Next, we analyzed the influence of PTPRJ deficiency on insulin sensitivity over a period of 1 h. For this purpose, control and Ptprj KO cells were stimulated with 10 nM insulin, and the phosphorylation status of AKT (Figure 1D–F), IRS‐1 (Figure 1G–I), and ERK1/2 (Figure 1J–L) were monitored in a kinetic manner. We detected increased phosphorylation of the downstream target AKT at site S473 in Ptprj KO compared to WT cells (Figure 1D,E). Insulin‐stimulated cells (5–60 min) revealed a significant increase (p < .0001) in AKT phosphorylation over the analyzed period (Figure 1F). Furthermore, the phosphorylation of IRS‐1 at tyrosine site Y895 was significantly increased by ~2‐fold in Ptprj KO cells upon insulin stimulation (Figure 1I, p < .0001). Specifically, a marked increase of phosphorylation in Ptprj KO cells (Figure 1H, 5 min, p = .0079, 20 min, p = .0079, 30 min, p = .0159, 60 min, and p = .0079) was detected. In addition to the PI3K‐Akt signaling pathway, we examined the insulin‐dependent phosphorylation of ERK1/2, a downstream target of MAP kinase signaling. In Ptprj KO cells, the ERK1/2 phosphorylation sites T202 and Y204 were significantly increased after 5 min, showed a trend toward elevated phosphorylation after 10, 20, and 30 min (Figure 1J,K) and an increase in phosphorylation (p = .0234) when quantifying the entire period (Figure 1L).

Furthermore, we assessed the phosphorylation of insulin signaling components in each of the four WT and Ptprj KO clones separately (Figure S2). A dose‐dependent stimulation with insulin (1, 10, and 100 nM) for 5 min led to elevated phosphorylation in the majority of Ptprj KO clones. This was particularly pronounced in pINSR (Y1150/Y1151), pAKT (S473), and pERK1/2 (T202/Y204) (Figure S2A,E,G). Except for KO clone 3, Ptprj KO clones showed greater efficacy in activating the insulin signaling cascades than the WT clones. Pooled data points from all WT and KO clones also revealed a significantly increased phosphorylation of INSR and AKT at all three insulin concentrations applied (Figure S2B,F). Phosphorylation of the INSR (Y1150/Y1151) from unstimulated Ptprj KO cells (Figure S2B), ERK1/2 phosphorylation (T202/Y204) (Figure S2H), and IRS‐1 phosphorylation (Y895) upon insulin stimulation (Figure S2D) were also significantly increased in Ptprj KO cells. In summary, Ptprj KO cells showed an enhanced insulin sensitivity compared to WT cells as evidenced by increased phosphorylation of downstream nodes of the insulin signaling cascade. Our data demonstrate PTPRJ as a negative regulator of insulin signaling in neurons.

PTPRJ directly interacts with INSR

To further characterize the phosphorylation of the INSR, we performed immunoprecipitation and analyzed total tyrosine phosphorylation and specific Y1146 phosphorylation of the INSR. We detected a 2.5‐fold increase in phosphorylation (p = .003) of all tyrosine phosphorylation sites (Figure 2A,B) and a 7.3‐fold increase (p < .0001) of the specific phosphorylation site Y1146 (Figure 2A,C) in Ptprj KO cells upon insulin stimulation. Treating the cells with sodium orthovanadate, a pan‐PTP inhibitor, resulted in hyperphosphorylation of the INSR.

To investigate INSR expression and a potential interaction between PTPRJ and INSR, we utilized an in situ proximity ligation assay (PLA), as depicted in Figure 2I. As expected, we observed no PTPRJ expression in Ptprj KO cells and further no difference in INSR expression between WT and Ptprj KO cells (Figure 2D). A heightened interaction of 1.4‐fold between the INSR and PTPRJ in insulin‐stimulated WT cells was observed (p = .008), indicating recruitment of PTPRJ to the INSR upon insulin stimulation (Figure 2E,G). On the contrary, in Ptprj KO cells, this interaction could not be detected, validating this technical approach. To further assess the activation of the INSR by phosphorylation in PTPRJ‐deficient cells, we used INSR and pan‐phosphotyrosine‐specific antibodies (Figure 2F). In both unstimulated and insulin‐stimulated cells, tyrosine phosphorylation was increased to 2.2‐fold and 1.7‐fold (p = .01562, p = .032, respectively) in Ptprj KO cells (Figure 2H). The corresponding controls are depicted in Figure S3. These findings align with the immunoblotting analyses of p‐INSR, which also revealed an elevated tyrosine phosphorylation of the INSR in Ptprj KO cells (Figure 2A,B). Thus, the data suggest that PTPRJ dephosphorylates the INSR by direct interaction.

Given the close interconnection between insulin signaling and insulin‐like growth factor‐1 (IGF‐1) signaling, we conducted additional experiments to assess PTPRJ's involvement in IGF‐1 signaling. Our results revealed increased phosphorylation (p = .008) of the IGF‐1 receptor (IGF‐1R) at Y1135/36 in Ptprj KO cells following IGF‐1 stimulation (Figure S4A,B). To investigate IGF‐1 signaling independently of insulin signaling, we simultaneously inhibited the INSR with S961 during IGF‐1 stimulation (Figure S4C). This approach revealed a significant increase (p = .03) in IGF‐1R (Y1135/1136) phosphorylation (Figure S4D), as well as elevated phosphorylation (p = .01) of the downstream target AKT (S473) in Ptprj KO cells (Figure S4E). These findings suggest that, in addition to targeting the insulin receptor, PTPRJ may also act as a negative regulator of IGF‐1 signaling by dephosphorylating the IGF‐1R. Furthermore, IGF‐1 represents a neurotrophic factor that induces neurite outgrowth. 35 Therefore, we additionally performed a neurite outgrowth assay with differentiated N2a cells upon IGF‐1 stimulation (Figure S4F). Indeed, IGF‐1 stimulation enhanced neurite outgrowth in both WT and Ptprj KO cells. While unstimulated Ptprj KO cells display decreased neurite length from 60 h onwards (p = .005, asterisks below graphs), IGF‐1 stimulated Ptprj KO cells had significantly longer neurites (p < .0001 after 84 h, asterisks above graphs) compared to WT cells (Figure S4F). The number of branching points was not affected by IGF‐1 stimulation (data not shown).

Ptprj KO regulates important genes of glucose and lipid metabolism

RNA sequencing analysis identified a broad gene expression dataset in which more than 1132 genes were upregulated and 1082 were downregulated (FDR <0.1) by Ptprj KO in N2a cells (Figure S5B). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that one of the four Ptprj KO clones was clearly outside the KO cluster and had a confounding impact on the statistical analysis (Figure S5A). Therefore, this clone (Ptprj KO‐3) was considered an outlier and removed from further analysis.

In previous studies, suppression of one specific PTP has been shown to result in a compensatory increase in the expression and/or activity of other PTPs, exemplified by PTP1B and TC‐PTP. 36 However, RNA sequencing analysis revealed that among the 17 classical PTPs being expressed in N2a cells only the gene of interest, Ptprj, was differentially expressed (Log2FC = −1.23, FDR <0.0001) in Ptprj KO cells (Figure S5C), demonstrating the absence of compensatory PTP expression due to this specific KO.

While brain glucose uptake is considered to be insulin‐independent, however, recent gene expression analyses have identified new insights into mechanisms for insulin‐dependent glucose uptake, metabolism, and lipid biosynthesis in the hypothalamus. 37 A gene ontology analysis employing the Wikipathways database showed that 43 genes related to the PI3K‐Akt–mTOR signaling pathway 38 and 23 genes connected to MAPK signaling 39 were differentially expressed in PTPRJ‐deficient cells (Figure S5D). Since Ptprj KO cells exhibited improved insulin signaling (Figure 1) as evidenced by hyperphosphorylation of the INSR (Figure 2), we investigated how PTPRJ deficiency affects the expression of gene clusters known to be influenced by insulin in the brain. 37 Ptprj KO significantly enhanced expression of the facilitated hexose transporters Slc2a1 (Glut‐1), Slc45a1 (Proton‐Associated Sugar Transporter A) and Mfsd4b4 (Major Facilitator Superfamily Domain Containing 4B) (Figure 3A). Furthermore, we found a trend toward increased expression of Slc2a9 (Glut‐9, FDR = 0.2, p = .04) and Slc2a6 (Glut‐6, FDR = 0.3, p = .08, both not shown) in Ptprj KO cells. Furthermore, the expression of Hk1 (Hexokinase 1), responsible for the initial step in intracellular glucose metabolism, was increased in Ptprj KO cells. Following phosphorylation by Hk1, glucose‐6‐phosphate can flow into either the glycolysis pathway for ATP and pyruvate production or the pentose phosphate pathway for NADPH generation. Gene expression of both pathways was predominantly increased in Ptprj KO cells, as shown in Figure 3A. Therefore, we measured glucose uptake utilizing the fluorescent glucose derivative 2‐NBDG. Indeed, glucose uptake was increased by 22% (p < .0001) in Ptprj KO cells under basal conditions (Figure S6A). In addition, we assessed the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) as indicator of increased glycolysis. Ptprj KO cells displayed increased ECAR by 45% (p < .0001, Figure S6B). However, we did not detect any discernable difference in glucose concentration in high‐glucose culture media between WT and Ptprj KO cells under basal conditions (Figure S6C).

In relation to lipid biosynthesis, Ptprj KO mainly increased the expression of multiple enzymes involved in the synthesis of fatty acids, cholesterol, ceramide, sphingolipids, and phospholipids (Figure 3B). Here, Hacd4 (Very‐Long‐Chain (3R)‐3‐Hydroxyacyl‐CoA Dehydratase 4) and Hmgcs2 (3‐Hydroxy‐3‐Methylglutaryl‐CoA Synthase 2), contributing to very long‐chain fatty acid and cholesterol biosynthesis were the most upregulated genes (Log2FC = 1.15, FDR = 0.0002 and Log2FC = 0.89, FDR = 0.06, respectively). In summary, Ptprj KO increased glucose uptake and oxidation as well as transcriptionally enhanced lipid biosynthesis.

Ptprj KO regulates genes involved in neurotransmission

Apart from its impact on neurometabolism, the deletion of Ptprj substantially affected gene expression related to neurotransmission. Ptprj KO resulted in regulatory effects that both inhibited and stimulated gene expression of exocytosis (involving VAMPs, syntaxins, synaptogatmins, Stxbps), K+ channels, glutamate receptors, and neuropeptide receptors. These effects did not exhibit a clear tendency toward up‐ or downregulation. However, in Ptprj KO cells, 16 calcium ion (Ca2+) transporters were differentially expressed, of which the majority (12 genes) were upregulated (Figure 3C). Additionally, GO analysis identified regulated genes in various synaptic compartments necessary for neurotransmission such as glutamatergic synapses (100 genes), postsynaptic density (71 genes), vesicle‐mediated transport (53 genes) and presynaptic membrane (11 genes) (Table S2). Hence, our data suggest that PTPRJ may be implicated in neurotransmission by regulating Ca2+ channels, exocytosis, and synaptic proteins.

Knockout of Ptprj decreases protein biosynthesis

Understanding spatial and temporal aspects of protein biosynthesis is critical for characterizing the cellular response under various physiological and pathological conditions. 40 The RNA sequencing data revealed that crucial genes for ribosomal biogenesis such as Nop10 (Nucleolar Protein 10), Mdn1 (Midasin AAA ATPase 1) and Nvl (Nuclear VCP Like) were downregulated in Ptprj KO cells (Figure 4A). By using a fluorescent assay based on OP‐puromycin with a fluorescent azide recognizing newly synthesized proteins (Figure 4D), a significant decrease of protein biosynthesis by ~40% (p < .0001, Figure 4B,C) in PTPRJ‐deficient cells was observed. After insulin stimulation for 2 h protein synthesis was still decreased by one‐third (p = .0005), but 16 h of insulin stimulation compensated the effect caused by the absence of PTPRJ (p > .05). We detected a statistical significance between the genotypes and the duration of insulin stimulation (F(2, 44) = 5.132, p = .0099) on protein synthesis (p < .0001, p = .0078, respectively). This indicates that PTPRJ deficiency leads to decreased protein synthesis, which can be rescued through insulin stimulation.

To further elucidate the underlying mechanisms of decreased protein biosynthesis, we analyzed the expression of cellular stress markers. Indeed, several genes associated with endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and unfolded protein response (UPR), such as Map3k5 (Mitogen‐Activated Protein Kinase Kinase Kinase 5), Niban1 (Niban Apoptosis Regulator 1) and Sdf2l1 (Stromal Cell Derived Factor 2 Like 1) were upregulated in Ptprj KO cells as evidenced by RNA sequencing data (Figure 4E). Moreover, an unbiased gene enrichment analysis using the KEGG pathway database revealed a significant enrichment of differentially expressed genes involved in protein processing in the ER (48 genes, rich ratio 0.28, FDR <0.0001) (Figure S5E). In the context of insulin signaling, we examined the phosphorylation of S6 ribosomal protein, which is known to correlate with increased translation. 41 In line with our protein synthesis assay, unstimulated or shortly stimulated (0–5 min) Ptprj KO cells showed decreased (p = .02) phosphorylation of S6 (S235/236), whereas longer stimulated (10–60 min) Ptprj KO cells were characterized by an elevation (p = .06) in S6 phosphorylation (data not shown). In summary, Ptprj KO cells exhibited a significant reduction in protein biosynthesis and homeostasis by transcriptional activation of genes involved in ER stress and in regulation of protein processing.

Ptprj KO compromises differentiation and neurite outgrowth

An unbiased gene ontology analysis revealed that knockout of Ptprj profoundly impacted several biological processes in differentiated neurons. In line with the proposed roles of PTPRJ, 42 cell adhesion was identified as one of the biological processes most significantly affected by Ptprj KO. Several other processes, such as axon guidance, nervous system development, dendritic spine development, and neuroblast differentiation, were significantly regulated in differentiated Ptprj KO cells (Figure 5A). Therefore, we studied the role of PTPRJ for neurite outgrowth and neuronal differentiation by applying the Incucyte Live‐Cell Analysis System. There was no difference in the growth kinetic between WT and Ptprj KO cells under normal growth conditions over 7 days (Figure 5B). In contrast when cells were subjected to differentiation media a proportion of Ptprj KO cells failed to undergo complete differentiation, and continued to divide, as indicated by a 50% higher confluence at the end of the 7 days culturing period (Figure 5C). Since differentiation was compromised in Ptprj KO cells, we conducted all previous experiments with undifferentiated N2a cells. This was done to rule out the possibility that observed effects could be attributed to variations in cell differentiation. The neurite outgrowth assay revealed a decrease in neurite length in Ptprj KO cells (Figure 5D upper panel, E), and a reduction in neurite branching points, both by ~32% (Figure 5D upper panel, F). When cells were exposed to insulin, we found a marked enhancement of neurite outgrowth but no significant difference in neurite length (Figure 5D lower panel, G) and number of neurite branching points (Figure 5D lower panel, H) between WT and Ptprj KO cells.

Additionally, key genes associated with the development and differentiation of the nervous system, as well as axon guidance, were downregulated in Ptprj KO cells (Figure 5I,J). In detail, along with 18 downregulated genes involved in nervous system development, multiple markers of neuron differentiation such as Six3 (SIX Homeobox 3), Ophn1 (Oligophrenin 1), Gap43 (Growth Associated Protein 43) and Tafa1 (TAFA Chemokine Like Family Member 1) were suppressed in Ptprj KO cells (Figure 5I). Conversely, Rhob (Ras Homolog Family Member B) and Fmr1 (Fragile X Messenger Ribonucleoprotein 1), which are negative regulators of dendritic spine formation and neuron differentiation, respectively, 43 , 44 were both upregulated in Ptprj KO cells. Similarly, crucial genes responsible for axon guidance, such as Nfasc (Neurofascin), Ntng2 (Netrin G2), Lama2, and Lama5 (Laminin Subunit Alpha 2 and 5), were significantly downregulated. This is in line with our findings from the growth kinetic (Figure 5C) and neurite outgrowth assay (Figure 5E,F), which uncovered compromised differentiation and decreased neurite length and branching in Ptprj KO cells. Taken together, besides negatively regulating insulin signaling and impacting neuronal protein biosynthesis, PTPRJ tightly regulates neuron differentiation and neurite outgrowth from transcription to the cellular phenotype.

DISCUSSION

Previous studies demonstrated PTPRJ as an endogenous modulator of insulin signaling in peripheral tissues and may, therefore, constitute a potential target for the treatment of insulin resistance and obesity. 20 , 21 To date, there are no reports about PTPRJ's role in insulin signaling in neuronal cells. Therefore, we investigated the regulatory function of PTPRJ in insulin signaling in N2a cells, a murine neuronal cell line that constitutes a suitable model for neuronal insulin signaling and neurite outgrowth. 45 , 46 Moreover, to get a deeper understanding of PTPRJ's physiological role within neurons, we conducted an extensive gene expression analysis and examined Ptprj KO cells, including glucose and lipid metabolism, protein biosynthesis, neurotransmission, and neurite outgrowth.

PTPRJ's role in insulin signaling

Despite substantial evidence indicating PTPRJ as a suppressor of insulin signaling, this enzyme tends to be overlooked. 47 , 48 , 49 In the current study, deletion of Ptprj showed a marked enhancement of insulin sensitivity compared to WT cells, as shown by increased phosphorylation of crucial insulin signaling components such as INSR, IRS‐1, AKT, and ERK1/2. Additionally, by applying a PLA assay, we provide proof of a direct interaction between PTPRJ and the INSR in neurons, which was elevated when cells were subjected to insulin, indicating the recruitment of PTPRJ to INSR upon insulin stimulation. In line with our own data, enhanced interaction between PTPRJ and INSR in liver tissue of mice upon insulin stimulation was demonstrated. 17 Furthermore, by using synthetic phosphopeptides, PTPRJ has been shown to dephosphorylate specific phosphorylation sites of the INSR at Y960 in the juxtamembrane region and Y1146 in the activation loop among other members of type III RPTPs (15). Congruently, we showed that tyrosine side Y1146 was hyperphosphorylated in PTPRJ‐deficient N2a cells. Interestingly, in a pulldown approach using recombinant PTPRJ‐His6 protein in A549 lung carcinoma cells to identify potentially interacting proteins, the INSR was not detected by mass spectrometry. 50 This suggests that the interaction between PTPRJ and INSR may be tissue‐specific. Moreover, it has been shown that PTPRJ expression is increased in obese mice in peripheral tissues. 17 , 18 This upregulation has also been implicated in central leptin resistance, thereby increasing food intake. 18 A cross‐talk between insulin and leptin signaling in the brain is vital for glucoregulation and maintenance of a healthy energy balance. 51 , 52 Since PTPRJ impacts both of these signaling pathways it may constitute a central modulator of glucose homeostasis. Collectively, our study substantiates previous findings that PTPRJ is an endogenous antagonist of insulin signaling by dephosphorylating tyrosine residues in the activation loop of the INSR.

PTPRJ regulates gene expression of glucose and lipid metabolism

Broad gene expression analysis revealed that the absence of Ptprj resulted in altered regulation of more than 2000 genes under basal conditions. We aimed to analyze whether Ptprj KO led to a similar expression signature related to glucose and lipid metabolism than what was found in insulin‐infused brains. In fact, it has been shown previously that the quantity of insulin‐regulated genes in certain brain areas, such as the hypothalamus, surpasses the combined number of regulated genes found in two classical insulin target tissues, liver, and muscle. 53 Ptprj KO resulted in the activation of multiple genes that are responsible for glucose uptake and metabolism and the synthesis of fatty acids, cholesterol, and other lipids. Clearly, the expression patterns related to glucose metabolism and lipid biosynthesis in Ptprj KO cells align with those observed in murine primary neurons stimulated with insulin. 37 , 54 Cai and colleagues also showed that insulin mainly enhanced the expression of glycolytic genes and genes involved in long‐chain fatty acyl‐CoA and cholesterol biosynthesis in primary neurons. 37 Conversely, in primary astrocytes and the hypothalamus of insulin‐infused mice, glycolytic genes were largely downregulated. In contrast, the transcriptomic changes with regard to fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis resembled that of primary neurons. 37 This emphasizes the varied impact of insulin on gene expression across diverse cell types and model systems. In accordance with the observed transcriptomic alterations related to glucose metabolism, we found that PTPRJ‐deficient neurons exhibited increased glucose uptake and oxidation. This finding is particularly noteworthy, as recent studies have demonstrated that enhanced glucose uptake and metabolism can improve cognitive function and mitigate neurodegeneration. 55 , 56

Low insulin levels in the brain can lead to decreased cholesterol synthesis and impaired synaptic function, possibly contributing to compromised neuronal activity and neurodegenerative disorders. 54 Additionally, the concentration of insulin and the duration of insulin stimulation may profoundly impact gene expression. Indeed, Cai et al. found that a greater number of genes were regulated upon low‐dose insulin infusion compared to the high‐dose infusion in the hypothalamus of mice. 37 Consistently, differential expression between WT and Ptprj KO cells was more pronounced under basal growth conditions compared to cells that were stimulated with high concentrations of insulin. However, we cannot definitively assert that the regulation of glucose and lipid metabolism is a result of enhanced insulin sensitivity in Ptprj KO cells but is rather due to other affected signaling pathways.

The role of PTPRJ in Ca2+ transporter genes and protein biosynthesis

Ca2+ plays a versatile role as an intracellular second messenger in various biological processes within neurons, such as the regulation of metabolism, protein synthesis, and gene transcription. 57 , 58 The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stores intracellular Ca2+ and facilitates protein folding. 59 When cellular homeostasis is dysbalanced, leading to disruptions in Ca2+ signaling, ER stress response is triggered, and unfolded protein response (UPR) is activated, reducing protein synthesis and eliminating misfolded proteins. 60 , 61 Moreover, PTPRJ has been implicated in calcium homeostasis by inhibiting intracellular calcium mobilization in lymphocytes. 62 , 63 In the current study, genes involved in the ER stress response and UPR were significantly upregulated along with decreased protein biosynthesis in Ptprj KO cells. The simultaneous dysregulation of multiple Ca2+ transporters in PTPRJ‐deficient cells suggests a potential mechanism by which protein biosynthesis is attenuated. However, this finding was rather unexpected as increased insulin signaling typically enhanced protein biosynthesis by activating the mTOR pathway. Dysregulation of Ca2+ homeostasis and the activation of the ER stress response may counteract the physiological effects of insulin signaling on protein biosynthesis.

PTPRJ regulates neurite outgrowth and differentiation

To further elucidate PTPRJ's physiological role in neurons, we measured neurite dynamics in Ptprj KO cells. We demonstrated that Ptprj KO leads to impaired neuron differentiation and decreased neurite outgrowth in N2a cells. Recently, it has been suggested that PTPRJ is crucial for the correct projection of axons during development. Moreover, retinal axons of Ptprj KO mice displayed defects with pathfinding at the chiasm and the formation of the retinocollicular topographic map by increasing Eph receptor activity. 27 Both this study and our investigation highlight the critical role of PTPRJ in facilitating axon growth and guidance during development. Furthermore, several lines of evidence suggest that insulin and insulin‐like growth factors (IGFs) directly influence the growth, development, and regeneration of the vertebrate nervous system. 22 , 64 , 65 Since Ptprj KO enhanced insulin signaling, we also measured neurite dynamics upon insulin treatment. In line with previous research, we observed an increase in neurite length and branching points upon insulin stimulation in WT and KO cells. Notably, the reduction in neurite length and neurite branching points in Ptprj KO cells was largely rescued upon insulin treatment. Additionally, IGF‐1 stimulation led to longer neurites in Ptprj KO cells compared to WT cells. This indicates that enhanced insulin and IGF‐1 sensitivity compensates for the retardation of neurite growth in Ptprj KO cells.

The data from the neurite outgrowth assay provide insights into neuritogenesis and serve as an indicator of neuronal differentiation. While axon growth and guidance is mainly mediated through the phosphorylation of ephrin receptors, EphA and EphB, 66 neuronal differentiation is influenced by phosphorylation of ERK1/2, JNK, and p38. 67 , 68 , 69 Several studies have shown that various PTPs are crucial for neuronal differentiation. 28 PTPRJ has been implicated in cell differentiation 70 , 71 , 72 but has not yet been described specifically in neuronal differentiation. Nonetheless, PTPRJ has been linked to oligodendrocyte differentiation, which is crucial for the myelination of axons in the brain. 73 , 74 The proposed mechanism by which PTPRJ impacts cell differentiation is through using ERK1/2 as substrate. 73 , 75 Here, we also show an increased phosphorylation of ERK1/2 in PTPRJ‐deficient cells under insulin‐stimulated conditions, indicating that compromised cell differentiation may be due to enhanced ERK1/2 phosphorylation. According to the cells' phenotype, numerous genes involved in axon guidance and neuron differentiation were downregulated in Ptprj KO cells. For example, Gap43, expressed in the neurite growth cone and a major determinant of neurite outgrowth and neuronal differentiation, 76 was suppressed in Ptprj KO cells. Collectively, our study indicates that Ptprj KO leads to transcriptional and post‐transcriptional changes impacting both neuron differentiation and neurite outgrowth. To gain a deeper understanding of PTPRJ's role in central insulin signaling, employing an experimental mouse model with a brain‐specific knockout of Ptprj would be beneficial.

RPTPs exhibit robust expression within the nervous system well beyond the period of axon growth and guidance. Subsequently, during later stages, their primary function shifts to the synapse. Multiple genes related to pre‐ and postsynaptic structures were regulated due to Ptprj KO. Therefore, investigating PTPRJs impact on synaptic plasticity would further deepen our understanding of PTPRJ’ role in the nervous system.

Notably, common markers of dopaminergic signaling such as TH, MAO‐A, MAO‐B were not expressed in both differentiated WT and Ptprj KO cells. Our RNA sequencing data correlated with protein and mRNA expression analysis, showing that N2a cells did not express common dopaminergic markers. Whether this cell line is a suitable model for dopamine signaling 77 , 78 or rather other cell lines as suggested recently, 79 , 80 needs to be further elucidated.

CONCLUSION

Insulin plays a vital role in the brain by modulating metabolism and cognitive function. Here, we establish PTPRJ as a novel negative regulator in neuronal insulin signaling. PTPRJ interacts with the insulin receptor, and PTPRJ deficiency leads to alterations in the transcription of genes involved in glucose metabolism, lipid biosynthesis, and Ca2+ homeostasis. Furthermore, neuronal Ptprj KO cells are characterized by decreased protein biosynthesis, which is compensated upon insulin stimulation. Finally, neurite outgrowth is impaired in neuronal cells when PTPRJ is absent. Thus, PTPRJ plays a pivotal role in neurons by regulating insulin signaling and neurotigenesis. This study provides several promising avenues for follow‐up studies on the role of PTPRJ within the brain.
