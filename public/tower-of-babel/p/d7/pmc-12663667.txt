Whitlockite-loaded silk fibroin/polyvinyl alcohol hydrogel composited with porous tantalum scaffold enhances bone regeneration and immunomodulation via the TGF-β/NRROS signaling pathway
Authors: Bing Ge, Haolin Jiao, Jianfeng Xu, Di Wu, Qinwen Xie, Guoshuang Zheng, Fang Cao, Guangxiao Yin, Dewei Zhao, Junlei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bone defects, caused by diseases or trauma, significantly impact patients’ quality of life. While porous Tantalum (pTa) scaffolds have shown promise in bone defect repair due to their excellent properties, their bioinert nature cannot meet the bioactive stimulation required for bone regeneration. To address these challenges, this study designed a composite scaffold composed of a 3D-printed pTa scaffold and a silk fibroin-crosslinked polyvinyl alcohol (SP) hydrogel loaded with whitlockite (WH). WH, a secondary inorganic component of bone tissue, was incorporated into the hydrogel to enhance its osteogenic, angiogenic and anti-inflammatory properties. The optimal proportions of PVA and WH were determined through extensive experiments. The resulting scaffold, designated as SP-pTa@WH, demonstrated remarkable biocompatibility and effectively promoted bone tissue repair in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistic studies revealed that SP-pTa@WH significantly modulated macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype and upregulated the expression of key osteogenic genes. High-throughput gene sequencing analysis further identified the TGF-β signaling pathway, particularly the NRROS gene, as critical in mediating the anti-inflammatory effects of SP-pTa@WH. Overall, this study provides a novel composite scaffold with enhanced osteogenic and immunomodulatory capabilities, offering broad prospects for clinical applications in bone defect repair.

Introduction

Bone defects, characterized by the pathological or traumatic loss of osseous tissue, pose distinct challenges to healing, which vary according to the dimensions of the lesion. While the innate osteogenic capacity facilitates the spontaneous regeneration of minor injuries (such as microfractures and cracks), critical-sized defects (defined as those exceeding 2.5 cm in length or twice the diaphyseal diameter in long bones) demonstrate impaired self-repair mechanisms, leading to permanent functional deficits. This widespread clinical issue affects millions each year, imposing substantial socioeconomic burdens. Current therapeutic approaches prioritize autologous bone transplantation as the gold standard for treating bone defects; however, its effectiveness is constrained by donor site morbidity, limited graft availability, and the necessity for additional surgical interventions. These limitations have driven advancements in synthetic biomaterials, positioning artificial bone substitutes as promising alternatives for osseous reconstruction [1]. Bone implants fulfill dual roles as structural fillers and osteoconductive platforms, conforming to the three-dimensional defect geometry to restore contours while providing nucleation sites for mineralization and new bone formation. Therefore, the optimal design of implants requires three essential characteristics: exceptional biocompatibility to avoid adverse host reactions, biomechanical compatibility that replicates the structural integrity of native bone, and bioactivity that facilitates the orchestration of bone regeneration processes [[2], [3], [4]].

Commonly used biomaterials in bone implantation include metallic alloys, bioceramics, synthetic polymers, and composite materials. Among these, tantalum exhibits remarkable stability within physiological environments and demonstrates a strong affinity for bone tissue. As a material for bone implants, it elicits weaker immune responses compared to traditional biomedical metals such as 316L stainless steel, CoCrMo, and titanium alloys, thus earning the designation of a “Biophilic Metal” [[5], [6], [7]]. To address its high density, porous tantalum structures are employed to reduce the weight of implants. These structures are fabricated using techniques such as chemical vapor deposition, additive manufacturing, foam impregnation, or powder metallurgy. When optimally designed, porous tantalum can achieve biomechanical properties that closely align with those of human bone tissue [8]. Through the strategic design of its porous architecture, tantalum can emulate the biomechanical characteristics of human bone tissue [[9], [10], [11]]. These attributes render tantalum scaffolds highly promising for clinical applications. Several porous tantalum implants have already obtained Medical Device Product Registration Certificates (MDPRC), affirming their safety and reliability as solutions for orthopedic treatments.

Additive manufacturing (AM, also known as 3D printing) has emerged as a prominent technique for the fabrication of pTa scaffolds, primarily due to its capability to produce complex geometries and enable customized designs. Recent studies suggest that AM-fabricated pTa scaffolds can effectively restore bone morphology and enhance the microenvironment for bone regeneration [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. To further optimize their regenerative potential, Maria Cámara-Torre et al. [16] proposed the integration of degradable fibers or hydrogel fillers, which incorporate bioactive agents such as cells, cytokines, or metal ions, within the pores of AM-fabricated scaffolds. This composite strategy shows significant potential for the repair of large bone defects.

To achieve optimal bone tissue repair and regeneration, the role of biomaterials in regulating the immune microenvironment cannot be overlooked. Macrophages, which are critical immune cells, rapidly accumulate at injury sites following trauma. The balance between M1 and M2 macrophage phenotypes is crucial in regulating bone healing: M1 macrophages drive inflammation and recruit stem cells during the initial phases, whereas M2 macrophages predominate in later stages by secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines that enhance osteoblast activity [17,18]. Although both phenotypes are essential, a prolonged dominance of M1 macrophages can result in excessive fibrosis and delayed healing [18,19]. Consequently, therapeutic strategies that promote M2 polarization hold significant clinical potential. For instance, Toita et al. [20] developed implants that induce the transition from M1 to M2 macrophages, thereby enhancing bone regeneration and osseointegration. Similarly, Yin et al. [21] designed M2-polarizing nanocapsules that improve the repair of inflammatory bone defects.

WH recognized as the second most abundant inorganic constituent of bone, comprises approximately 20 % of the bone matrix by weight. It is distinguished from hydroxyapatite (HA; Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2) by the incorporation of magnesium ions within its Ca9MgH(PO4)7 structure [22]. These magnesium ions have been shown to reduce inflammation and facilitate bone regeneration [23,24]. In addition, the calcium ions, hydrogen phosphate ions, and phosphate ions (which convert to hydrogen phosphate and dihydrogen phosphate in the physiological environment) contained in WH can provide the necessary inorganic mineral ions for bone regeneration [25,26]. The combined osteogenic and anti-inflammatory characteristics of WH have prompted its exploration in various applications, such as scaffolds or particulates to improve osseointegration [[27], [28], [29], [30]], as particulates with anti-inflammatory and anti-tumor properties [31]. However, the limited mechanical strength of WH scaffolds restricts their use in load-bearing defects [32]. This limitation has prompted the strategic incorporation of WH as a bioactive additive into pTa, thereby merging the immunomodulatory benefits of WH with the excellent mechanical performance of pTa.

This study explores the integration of WH with pTa scaffolds using hydrogels, which are recognized as an optimal drug delivery platform due to their biocompatibility, three-dimensional network structure, and controlled release properties [[33], [34], [35]]. At present, a wide range of hydrogels, including natural polysaccharides, proteins, and synthetic polymers, are extensively utilized in drug delivery applications [36]. Among these, protein-based hydrogels, such as silk fibroin (SF), present notable advantages due to their low immunogenicity, ease of purification and sterilization, and inherent gelation capability without the necessity for additional crosslinkers [[37], [38], [39]]. However, the inherent brittleness and protein instability associated with SF pose limitations to its effectiveness as a drug carrier [40,41]. To resolve these challenges, we propose the incorporation of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), a durable synthetic polymer, to crosslink with SF. While existing research has shown that PVA improves the mechanical properties of SF, its impact on drug-loading capacity remains insufficiently explored [42,43].

Therefore, the experimental idea of this study is to achieve multiple optimizations of the materials and synergistically combine the drug-carrying capacity of SP hydrogel, the biological activity advantages of WH, and the excellent mechanical properties of pTa. Specifically, SF derived from bombyx mori cocoons was crosslinked with varying concentrations of PVA to synthesize SP hydrogels. The optimal loading of WH was subsequently determined via biocompatibility assays to reduce the risk of drug overdose toxicity. Following the evaluation of a SP hydrogel integrated with WH and combined with pTa scaffold, which demonstrated favorable biocompatibility, the therapeutic efficacy of SP-pTa@WH in treating bone defects was validated through both in vitro and in vivo experiments. Furthermore, the underlying mechanisms were elucidated using high-throughput gene sequencing technology. This study aims to develop a composite scaffold with sustained functionality that not only enhances bone regeneration but also facilitates immune regulation, thereby offering a novel biomaterial-based approach for the treatment of bone defects. Our findings have the potential to positively impact the clinical management of patients with bone injuries and contribute to advancements in the field of regenerative medicine.

Experimental section

Material preparation and characterization

Preparation and mechanical properties characterization of pTa scaffolds

Spherical Ta powder (purity >99.95 wt%) was purchased from Stardust Technology Co., Ltd. Porous Ta samples were fabricated using selective laser melting (SLM) technology (Renishaw AM400, UK). The pTa was designed with a diamond-type pore structure, featuring a porosity of 75 % and a pore size of 500 μm. The printing process was conducted on a Ti6Al4V substrate under a high-purity argon atmosphere, with the oxygen content in the working chamber maintained below 100 ppm. The SLM process parameters were as follows: a laser power of 260 W, a laser scanning point spacing of 50 μm, an exposure time of 100 μs, a layer thickness of 30 μm, and a hatch distance of 65 μm. After printing, the samples underwent heat treatment (1100 °C, 2 h, vacuum degree of 10-2 Pa). Following heat treatment, the samples were subjected to sandblasting to remove incompletely melted Ta powder, followed by ultrasonic cleaning in acetone, anhydrous ethanol, and ultrapure water for 15 min each, and then dried for further use.

For immersion and cell experiments, the samples were prepared as discs with a diameter of 10 mm and a thickness of 3 mm. For animal experiments, cylindrical samples with a diameter of 3 mm and a length of 8 mm were used. For mechanical testing, cylindrical samples with a diameter of 10 mm and a height of 15 mm were prepared. The mechanical properties of pTa were assessed through quasi-static compression tests conducted using a universal mechanical testing machine (Zwick/Z050, Germany). The compressive strain rate was maintained at 0.01 s−1, in accordance with ISO 13314 standards. Engineering strain (ε) was computed by dividing the crosshead displacement by the initial specimen length. The quasielastic modulus (E) was derived from the slope of the linear region at the onset of the compressive stress–strain curve. Yield strength (σy) was identified as the compressive stress at a 0.2 % offset. Plateau stress (σpl) was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the stresses occurring between 20 % and 30 % compressive strain. The plateau end stress (σ130) and strain (εple) were determined at the point on the stress–strain curve where the stress reached 1.3 times the plateau stress. The surface morphology of pTa was determined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi 3500, Tokyo, Japan). The elemental composition of pTa was analyzed by energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, Element, AMETEK, USA). The crystal structure of pTa was characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD, Empyrean-DNL2001, Netherlands).

Extraction and characterization of silk fibroin

SF was extracted and purified from bombyx mori cocoons. The methods of extraction and purification refer to Yonesi et al. [44] and Kundu et al. [45]. The cocoons were cleaned, cut into small pieces, and boiled in a 0.2 wt% sodium carbonate (Macklin, 99 %) solution for 60 min to remove sericin. The SF was then repeatedly rinsed with distilled water and dried overnight at 80 °C before being weighed. Above process was repeated twice. The degummed SF was dissolved in 9.4 mol/L lithium bromide (LiBr, Macklin, 99 %) and dialyzed in a dialysis bag (Solarbio, molecular weight cutoff 8000–14000) against distilled water for 5 days, with the water changed every 8 h. After dialysis, the solution was frozen at −20 °C and subsequently freeze-dried to obtain solid SF, which was stored at −20 °C. The crystal structure and functional groups of SF were characterized using XRD and attenuated total reflectance infrared spectroscopy (ATR-IR, Hyperion 3000), respectively.

Fabrication and characterization of whitlockite

Using Ca(NO3)2·4H2O (AR), K2HPO4·3H2O (AR) and MgCl2·6H2O (AR) as raw materials, concentrated ammonia water was employed to adjust the pH of the calcium nitrate and magnesium chloride solutions to >11, and the pH of the K2HPO4 solution to >10. Initially, the calcium nitrate and magnesium chloride solutions were mixed in a molar ratio of 9:1. Under continuous stirring, the K2HPO4 solution was slowly added dropwise to the mixed solution, ensuring thorough mixing, with the final molar ratio of Ca:Mg:P being 9:1:7. The mixture was then transferred to a hydrothermal reactor and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 300 °C for 8 h. The resulting product was washed twice with anhydrous ethanol and distilled water. The powdered product was dried overnight at 80 °C, subsequently crushed using a grinder, and filtered through a 100 μm sieve. The crystal structure and functional groups of WH were characterized using XRD and ATR-IR, respectively. The elemental composition of WH was determined using energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, Element, AMETEK, USA).

Fabrication and characterization of SP-pTa composite scaffold

SF and PVA (Sigma-Aldrich) were separately dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Cytiva) to prepare solutions with a mass fraction of 8 wt%. Subsequently, SF and PVA mixed solutions were obtained by blending the solutions at mass ratios of SF:PVA = 8:2, 6:4, 4:6, and 2:8. The resulting precursor solutions of the mixed hydrogels were labeled as SF, SP1, SP2, SP3, SP4, and PVA, corresponding to SF:PVA ratios of 10:0, 8:2, 6:4, 4:6, 2:8, and 0:10, respectively. These precursor solutions were then infused into pTa scaffolds. The mixed samples were placed in flat-bottomed tubes and heated in an oven at 120 °C for 30 min. After natural cooling, the samples were stored in a −20 °C freezer for 12 h, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 12 h. This freeze-thaw cycle was repeated three times to obtain pTa samples uniformly filled with hydrogels.

After freeze-drying, the surface morphology of SP2-pTa was characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The crystal structure and functional groups of the SP2 hydrogel were analyzed using XRD and ATR-IR. To evaluate the swelling properties of the SP2 hydrogel, cylindrical hydrogels of SF, SP2, and PVA were prepared and immersed in PBS at a ratio of 3 cm2/mL. The swelling capacity was determined by weighing the hydrogels. Similarly, multiple groups of samples were prepared and placed in a shaker at 37 °C and 70 rpm. At specified time points, the samples were removed, freeze-dried, and weighed to assess their degradation properties over 90 days.

In vitro experiment

Cell proliferation

The cell proliferation assay was conducted using MC3T3-E1 cells (obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Model Cell Resource Center). The cells were cultured in a 5 vol% CO2 incubator at 37 °C, using DME/F-12 medium (Cytiva) supplemented with 10 vol% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1 vol% penicillin-streptomycin (Abbkine). Cells were passaged or cryopreserved (Abbkine) every 2–3 days. Cells were detached using trypsin (Gibco), counted, and confirmed to have reached sufficient cell numbers. A 48-well plate (Corning) was used, with 5000 MC3T3-E1 cells seeded per well. Each well was filled with 500 μL of DME/F-12 medium. After overnight incubation, the medium was replaced with 0.5 mL of material extract, which was refreshed every two days. The extract was prepared by immersing the materials in DME/F-12 medium at a surface area-to-volume ratio of 3 cm2/mL, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 3 days (the extraction ratio was performed in accordance with ISO 10993–12:2012 standards). Experiments were conducted on the samples listed in Table 1 and the pTa scaffolds. On days 1, 4, and 7 after replacing the medium with the extract, cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo, CK04) and measured with a microplate reader (MR, Bio-Tek).

Cell live/death staining

The specific procedures followed those described in Section 2.2.1. On days 1, 4, and 7 after replacing the medium with the extract, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were gently rinsed twice with PBS. Staining was performed using the Calcein-AM/PI Double Staining Kit (Dojindo, C542), and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, CX43). The staining solution was prepared by adding 2 μL of Calcein-AM and 3 μL of propidium iodide (PI) to 1 mL of PBS.

Cytoskeleton staining

A 48-well plate was used, with 20,000 MC3T3-E1 cells seeded per well, and 1 mL of extract from pTa, SP2-pTa, and SP2-pTa-4 % was added. After 2 h and 24 h, the cells were gently washed twice with PBS. Then, 4 vol% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio), pre-cooled, was added to each well, and the plate was kept on ice in the dark for 30 min. The cells were washed three times with PBS, followed by permeabilization with 0.1 vol% Triton X-100 (Solarbio) diluted in PBS at room temperature for 30 min. After washing the cells three times with PBS, 100 μL of AbFluor594-Phalloidin (Abbkine, BMD0084) working solution was added to each well, and the cells were stained at room temperature for 30 min. The cells were then washed three times with PBS, stained with DAPI staining solution (Coolaber, SL7100-50 mL) for 5 min, and washed three more times with PBS. Finally, 100 μL of PBS was added to each well. After completing the above steps, images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, CX43).

In vitro tube formation assay

The influence of scaffold materials on the in vitro tube formation of C166 cells was examined utilizing a Matrigel-based cell culture system. Matrigel (Corning, CLS354234) was pre-incubated overnight at 4 °C to achieve complete liquefaction. To maintain optimal conditions, ice-cold boxes, pipettes, tips, and microplates were prepared. Subsequently, 50 mL liquefied Matrigel was dispensed into each well of a pre-cooled 96-well plate, ensuring the plate remained on ice to facilitate uniform distribution. The plate was then transferred to a cell culture incubator for 30 min to allow for Matrigel solidification. C166 cells were subjected to trypsinization, centrifugation, and resuspension in ECM complete medium (Sciencell, 1001). Experimental groups were treated with ECM extracts derived from different scaffolds, whereas the control group was administered plain ECM medium. Cell suspensions were standardized to a concentration of 1 × 105 cells/mL, with 100 μL being added to each well. After a 5-h incubation period, the medium was removed, and cells were stained with Calcein-AM for 10 min. Cellular morphology was subsequently observed and photographed using a fluorescence microscope, and branch numbers and lengths were quantified employing ImageJ software.

SP-pTa@WH modulates macrophage polarization in vitro

RAW264.7 macrophages (Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank) were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Cytiva) supplemented with 10 % FBS, 1 % penicillin-streptomycin, and 1 % sodium pyruvate (Coolaber). Cells were passaged every 2–3 days using a cell scraper (Nest). For immunofluorescence staining, 30,000 cells were seeded into 15 mm confocal dishes (Nest) and allowed to adhere overnight in DMEM. The culture medium was then replaced with 2 mL of experimental solutions including: (1) pTa, SP2-pTa, or SP2-pTa-4 % extracts (prepared as per Section 2.2.1), (2) LPS (100 ng/mL)-induced M1 polarization group, and (3) IL-4 (20 ng/mL)-induced M2 polarization group, followed by 48 h incubation. Following two gentle PBS washes, cells were fixed with ice-cold 4 % paraformaldehyde (Coolaber) for 30 min in the dark, permeabilized with 0.3 % Triton X-100 (Solarbio) in PBS for 30 min at room temperature, and blocked with 10 % goat serum (Solarbio) for 1 h. Primary antibodies Arginsae-1 Rabbit Polyclonal Antibody (Arg-1, Proteintech, 16001-1-AP, 1:200) and CCR7 Polyclonal Antibody (Proteintech, 25898-1-AP, 1:200) were applied overnight at 4 °C. After PBS washes, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam, 1:200) and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam, 1:200) were incubated for 2 h in darkness. Nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 min. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Zeiss, CLSM510).

For qPCR analysis of macrophage polarization markers, RAW264.7 cells were cultured in 10 cm dishes until 50 % confluency. The medium was replaced with extracts and changed daily. After 7 days, cells were harvested for qPCR (protocol detailed in Section 2.2.4) using GAPDH as the endogenous control. Target genes included the M2 markers Arg-1 and NRROS, and the M1 markers INOS and IL-6. Primer sequences (Accurate Biology) are provided in Table 2.

To evaluate cytokine levels in macrophages, RAW264.7 cells were grown to 50 % confluency in 10 cm dishes. The medium was replaced with extracts daily. After 48 h, supernatants were collected, and cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, and TGF-β were measured using commercial ELISA kits (Abbkine) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

For RAW264.7 cells, 10 cm culture dishes were used. When the cells reached 80 % confluency, the medium was replaced with 10 mL of extract from pTa, SP2-pTa, and SP2-pTa-4 %, and the volume was increased to 15 mL on day 4. The medium was changed daily. After 7 days of culture, the cells were collected, and Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15596018CN) was added. The integrity and total amount of RNA were precisely assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). The library is quantified using a Qubit instrument and qPCR, while fragment size distribution is assessed using a bioanalyzer. After quality control, libraries were pooled according to effective concentration and the required sequencing data volume for Illumina sequencing.

The raw reads were initially processed using the Fastp software to obtain high-quality clean data, which served as the basis for all subsequent analyses. For sequence alignment, the reference genome index was constructed using HISAT2 v2.0.5, and the paired-end clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using the same tool. Gene expression levels were quantified using FeatureCounts (v1.5.0-p3) to calculate read counts for each gene. The fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values were then computed based on gene length and read counts. Differential expression analysis between the two groups was performed using the DESeq2 R package (v1.20.0), with a significance threshold set at an adjusted P-value ≤0.05 and |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1. To investigate functional enrichment, differentially expressed genes were analyzed for statistical overrepresentation in KEGG pathways using the ClusterProfiler R package (v3.8.1). Additionally, Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)was conducted using predefined gene sets. For further exploration of the TGF-β signaling pathway, protein-protein interaction networks of the associated DEGs were constructed using STRING and visualized in Cytoscape (v3.10.3), with a minimum required interaction score of 0.4 (medium confidence).

ROS assay

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 15 mm confocal dishes at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. After overnight culture in normal medium, the cells were stimulated with normal medium containing 1 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (Beyotime, ST1470-10 mg) for 6 h, followed by replacement with the extract medium. Normal culture RAW264.7 without LPS was also used as a negative control group (Control-). Three replicates were used for each sample. After 4 h, reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the cells were stained using the ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0033S). Finally, cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime, C1027), and images were captured using a confocal microscope (Zeiss, CLSM510).

In vivo experiment

Animals and surgical procedures

All animal procedures in this study were conducted in compliance with ethical standards approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Dalian University Affiliated Zhongshan Hospital (Approval No. DW2024-015-01). Healthy male Japanese white rabbits (n = 12 total, 5 months old, 3.0 ± 0.5 kg body weight) were acclimatized and monitored for health stabilization prior to surgery. Anesthesia was induced through marginal ear vein injection of 3 % sodium pentobarbital (25 mg/kg body weight). After aseptic preparation, a surgical drill was used to create a 3 mm diameter × 8 mm deep defect in the predetermined anatomical location of the lateral femoral condyle. Based on the in vitro optimization (Section 2.2.1), sterilized cylindrical implants (3 × 8 mm in size) of the SP2-pTa-4 % composite scaffold (experimental group, n = 6) and pTa control (n = 6) were surgically implanted. Postoperative care included three-day gentamicin prophylaxis and daily health monitoring. Groups were stratified with three rabbits per group euthanized at 6-week and 12-week intervals for comparative analysis of osseointegration progression.

Micro-CT characterization

After euthanizing the rabbits at 6 and 12 weeks post-surgery, the femurs were harvested, and all samples were fixed in 75 vol% ethanol. To evaluate bone regeneration around the scaffolds at 6 and 12 weeks, all samples were scanned using micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT, Siemens Inveon CT). Scanning was performed using the Inveon Acquisition Workplace software with the following parameters: voltage of 80 kV, current of 500 μA, exposure time of 1000 ms, average frame count of 1, bin factor of 1, effective pixel size of 9.41 μm, scan rotation angle of 360°, and 180 scan steps. After data acquisition, the bone morphology at the surgical site was reconstructed using Inveon Acquisition Workplace with a reconstruction scaling factor of 2. Parameters such as the bone volume to tissue volume ratio (BV/TV) and trabecular number per unit length were analyzed.

Fluorescent labeling

Two weeks before euthanasia, alizarin red S (Coolaber, CA1481) was injected. The powder was dissolved in PBS, filtered, and sterilized, then administered via intramuscular injection at a dosage of 30 mg/kg body weight. One week before euthanasia, calcein (Coolaber, CC3041) was injected. The powder was dissolved in PBS containing 2 wt% NaHCO3 and administered via intramuscular injection at a dosage of 25 mg/kg body weight. After Micro-CT scanning, the materials were excised using a bone cutter and subjected to gradient dehydration with alcohol at 50 times the material volume. The dehydration process involved 75 vol%, 85 vol%, 95 vol%, and absolute ethanol, with each concentration maintained for 10 days and the alcohol replaced every other day. Following dehydration, hard tissue sections were prepared and polished. The fluorescence distribution in each group was then captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Histological and immunohistological assessments

The procedures for hard tissue sectioning followed those described in section 2.3.3. The sections were stained with toluidine blue solution (Servicebio, G1032) at 60 °C for 15 min, followed by rinsing with distilled water. Subsequently, the sections were stained with acid fuchsin solution (Macklin, A800389) at room temperature for 10 min. Finally, the sections were rinsed with distilled water to complete the staining process. Images were captured using an optical microscope.

A 10 wt% disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate (EDTA) solution (Coolaber, CE4971) was prepared as the decalcification solution, and the pH was adjusted to 7.3 using sodium hydroxide (Macklin, S835850). Bone tissue surrounding the implanted material was cut into 1–2 mm thick sections and decalcified in 50 times the volume of 10 wt% EDTA solution, with the EDTA solution replaced every three days. After two months, the samples were subjected to gradient dehydration using 75 vol%, 85 vol%, 95 vol%, and absolute ethanol, with each concentration maintained for one day. Following this, the samples were immersed in xylene (AR) for 7 h and then in paraffin overnight, with fresh paraffin replaced after 4 h. The next day, the samples were embedded in paraffin and sequentially frozen at 4 °C and −20 °C. Subsequently, the samples were sectioned, and the sections were dried, dewaxed, and rehydrated. The processed tissue sections were placed in a humid chamber for hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining. First, hematoxylin staining solution (Servicebio, G1004) was applied to completely cover the tissue, and the sections were incubated at room temperature for 10 s, followed by rinsing under running tap water for 2 min. Then, eosin staining solution (Servicebio, G1001) was applied for 10 s. The tissue sections were dehydrated in a gradient, cleared, and mounted. Images were captured using an optical microscope (Jiang Nan, XD202).

After dewaxing and rehydration of the paraffin sections, the procedure was carried out according to the universal two-step detection kit (mouse/rabbit enhanced polymer method detection system) (ZSGB-BIO, PV-9000). The primary antibodies used were OCN (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 33–5400, 10 μg/mL) and Col Ⅰ (Proteintech, 67288-1-Ig, 1:200). Finally, a freshly prepared DAB chromogenic solution (ZSGB-BIO, ZLI-9017) was added, and the sections were incubated at room temperature for 5–8 min, followed by rinsing with PBS. Counterstaining was performed using hematoxylin staining solution to stain the nuclei for 20 s, followed by differentiation with 1 vol% hydrochloric acid-alcohol solution for a few seconds and rinsing under running water for 2 min. The sections were then dehydrated, cleared, and mounted for observation. Images were captured using an optical microscope (Jiang Nan, XD202).

The paraffin sections were exposed to ultraviolet light for more than 4 h to quench the fluorescence of fluorescent labels. Following dewaxing and rehydration of the paraffin-embedded sections, the samples were blocked with goat serum and subsequently permeabilized with 0.1 vol% Triton X-100 for 1 h. Primary antibodies (CCR7 and Arg-1, 1:50) diluted in PBS were applied to the sections, which were then placed in a humid chamber and incubated at 4 °C overnight. After 24 h, the slides were washed in PBS. Following the removal of the primary antibody Arg-1, a secondary antibody (1:50) goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488, Abcam, AB150077) was added. Similarly, after washing off the primary antibody CCR7, a secondary antibody (1:50) goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 594, Abcam, AB150080) was added, and the sections were incubated in the dark for 2 h. After incubation, the slides were washed again in PBS, and DAPI staining solution was applied, followed by incubation in the dark for 10 min. The tissue slides were then washed once more in PBS, dehydrated, and mounted. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, TFM680).

Statistical method

All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, USA). Quantitative data are expressed as average ± SD. The difference between groups was tested by the two-way analysis of variance (2 way ANOVA) test (n ≥ 3) and the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test (n ≥ 3), Tukey's test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, ∗ represents p-value <0.05, ∗∗ represents p-value <0.01, ∗∗∗ represents p-value <0.001, ∗∗∗∗ represents p-value <0.0001, and blank represents no significant difference.

Discussion

Due to the unique properties of bone tissue compared to other soft tissues, along with its role in providing structural support within the human body, artificial implants used for the repair of bone defects must possess specific characteristics. These characteristics include exceptional biocompatibility, mechanical properties that closely match those of bone tissue, and the ability to regulate the bone repair microenvironment. With continuous advancements in orthopedics and biomaterials, researchers have consistently refined material properties to achieve better therapeutic outcomes. This study focuses on enhancing the performance of the widely used porous Ta bone implant material.

The diamond-type pore structure (75 % porosity, 500 μm pore size) was selected for pTa scaffolds based on a comprehensive rationale that integrates mechanical, osteogenic, and vascularization requirements. Our prior studies demonstrated that 75 % porosity optimally balances compressive strength (35–40 MPa) with scaffold promotion of bone regeneration, ensuring structural integrity while facilitating tissue ingrowth [46]. The 500 μm pore size was specifically chosen as it falls within the 425–600 μm range identified by previous study as critical for microvessel ingrowth, which is essential for nutrient supply and osteogenesis [47]. The diamond architecture further enhances these properties through its interconnected pore network, which promotes both cell infiltration and vascularization, and its load-bearing efficiency, which mimics the trabecular bone structure [48]. This design ensures that the scaffolds not only support bone regeneration but also create an environment conducive to vascular network formation, thereby accelerating the healing process.

Regarding the hydrogel studied in this study, SF used for hydrogel preparation was subjected to purification followed by characterization. As illustrated in Fig. 2B and D, the ATR-IR spectrum and XRD pattern of SF confirm the successful extraction of SF. During the research process, SF demonstrated excellent thermal stability, maintaining a well-preserved porous structure even after heating at high temperatures. For instance, Fig. 3E illustrates SP2-pTa and SP2-pTa-4 % hydrogels subjected to high-temperature followed by freeze-thaw reinforcement, revealing that SP2 effectively maintained the porous structure and filled the pores within pTa.

In addition to enhancing its carrier properties, another objective of incorporating SF was to improve its cytotoxicity profile for cell proliferation. Despite SF's well-documented biocompatibility, certain components, such as fragmented proteins, may still inhibit cell proliferation, as shown in previous studies [45,49]. Cell proliferation assays performed on hydrogels with varying concentrations of SP demonstrated that SP2-pTa, SP4-pTa, and PVA-pTa hydrogels exhibited the most pronounced enhancements in cell proliferation, as illustrated in Fig. 4A. The change in SF performance stems from its widely acknowledged brittleness [40,41]. When mixed with PVA and undergoing gelation, hydrogen bonds form between them, endowing the brittle SF structure with PVA-derived toughness. Consequently, the structural stability and properties of the SP2 hydrogel are enhanced, minimizing fragment breakage and release while reducing associated biotoxicity. Notably, the observed reduction in cell proliferation inhibition in the SP4-pTa group was largely attributed to the significant incorporation of PVA. Consequently, for subsequent experiments, SF-pTa, SP2-pTa, and PVA-pTa were chosen to exemplify the differences in hydrogel performance.

The cell proliferation assays for the three groups, with the addition of WH, are shown in Fig. 4C–F. It can be observed that the addition of 1 % WH to the SF-pTa group and 4 % WH to the PVA-pTa group resulted in significant inhibition of cell proliferation. This is preliminarily speculated to be due to the drug toxicity caused by the excessive release of WH. To verify this hypothesis, as shown in Fig. 4C, this study used WH concentrations of 1 %, 2 %, 4 %, 8 %, and 12 % to confirm that the proliferation inhibition of SF-pTa and PVA-pTa worsened with the addition of WH. Specifically, the SF-pTa and PVA-pTa groups exhibited significantly inhibited cell proliferation due to the excessive amount of WH. This suggests that the structures of SF-pTa and PVA-pTa are unsuitable for loading WH. Conversely, the SP2-pTa group displayed enhanced cell proliferation upon WH addition, which increased with WH concentration until reaching an inhibitory effect at concentrations exceeding 8 wt%. Although the release curve of SP2-pTa-4 % was not compared with those of SF-pTa-4 % and PVA-pTa-4 %, the cytotoxicity test results before and after the addition of WH confirm that the excessive release was due to its structurally unsuitable configuration. These findings indicate that the structural modifications in SP2-pTa, arising from the combination of the two molecules, decelerated the release of WH. Key contributing factors include an increased number of loading sites and an extended diffusion pathway. Notably, the SP2-pTa-4 % group achieved a 125 % increase in cell proliferation, significantly surpassing other drug delivery systems employed for bone repair in previous studies [50,51]. This suggests that the proportional incorporation of the two components in SP2 induced favorable modifications in the hydrogel, thereby enhancing its loading and release capabilities.

To elucidate the structural changes in SP2, the ATR-IR and XRD spectra of SF and SP2 were analyzed, as illustrated in Fig. 2A–B. The XRD spectra revealed no significant changes, indicating that the characteristic β-sheet structure of SF remained intact. Similarly, the ATR-IR spectra showed that, although the peak positions remained constant, there was a notable change in the intensity of the absorption peaks. Specifically, the absorption peaks at 1637 cm−1 (C=O), 3073 cm−1, and 1514 cm−1 (N-H) exhibited significant interference, suggesting the formation of hydrogen bonds between SF and PVA molecules [52,53]. This observation suggests that hydrogen bonding is a key factor contributing to the structural modification of SF [52,54]. Subsequently, the performance differences arising from these structural changes in the SP2 hydrogel were examined. The characterization of the SP2 hydrogel demonstrated a marked enhancement in its swelling capacity.

As shown in Fig. 3A–B, the swelling ratio of the SP2 group reached 186 wt%, whereas those of SF and PVA were only around 65 wt%, indicating a substantial reduction in the crosslinking density of SP2. This property is highly favorable for the loading of drug particles into the hydrogel interior during drug encapsulation. Moreover, since drug release is partially dependent on its diffusion within the hydrogel's spatial structure [55], the subsequent release of WH from the hydrogel interior is expected to improve. This was further validated in the cell proliferation assay (Fig. 4C), where the extract from SP2-pTa-WH significantly enhanced cell viability and proliferation. In contrast, SF-pTa-WH and PVA-pTa-WH groups exhibited significant suppression of cell proliferation due to unsuitable drug loading and excessive release properties. Subsequent degradation assays on the SP2 hydrogel revealed that its degradation rate was intermediate between those of SF and PVA, with 42 wt% remaining after a period of 90 days. The SP2 hydrogel demonstrated a relatively slow degradation rate in this study. As the spatial structure that confines WH undergoes degradation, the ions of WH is expected to be released in a steady and continuous manner. This stable release mechanism can provide sustained bioactive stimulation, thereby fostering a conducive microenvironment for the gradual process of bone repair [56,57].

The WH utilized in this study was synthesized through a hydrothermal method, which, due to its mild reaction conditions, imparted an appropriate crystalline structure to the WH. As illustrated in Fig. 2D, the ATR-IR spectra reveal the well-preserved functional group structure of WH. Although the XRD pattern indicates the presence of some impurity peaks associated with HA, these do not substantially impact its overall functionality. Furthermore, the EDS spectrum depicted in Fig. 2E verifies that the atomic ratio of WH remains consistent with (Ca:Mg:P) = (9:1:7). The characterization of SP2-4 %, which is synthesized by incorporating WH into SP2 followed by heating and cyclic freeze-thawing, is shown in Fig. 2A–B. In comparison to the spectra of SP2, the XRD pattern of SP2-4 % exhibits diffraction peaks corresponding to WH, while the overall attenuation of ATR-IR absorption peaks and the emergence of a PO43− absorption peak at 1042 cm−1 suggest the successful integration of WH into SP2. The distribution of WH is visually represented in Fig. 3F.

Fig. 3C illustrates the release profile of Mg2+, Ca2+ and PO43−. PO43− is converted into HPO42− or H2PO4− within the buffer system prior to cellular uptake. Ca2+, Mg2+, HPO42−, and H2PO4− can be directly absorbed by cells through specific channels. Although a relatively high release was observed on the first day, the results of cell experiments presented in Fig. 4C indicate that this does not negatively impact cell proliferation. Subsequently, the release proceeded at a stable rate over a period of 40 days. When compared to the ion release levels reported in previous studies [58,59], the gradual and controlled release observed in this study minimizes the risk of high cytotoxicity associated with burst release, which is advantageous for promoting bone repair.

Following the determination of the final experimental groups, their efficacy in promoting cell spreading and bone regeneration was validated. Additionally, the cell spreading results (Fig. 5A–B) indicated that SP2-pTa-4 % improved cell spreading, which is conducive to cell adhesion and growth following implant implantation. The outcomes of ALP staining, ALP quantification, and alizarin red staining demonstrated that the ions released from SP2-pTa-4 % significantly enhanced both early and late stages of osteogenesis.

A comprehensive analysis of qPCR results (Fig. 5D) at two time points revealed that SP2-pTa-4 % significantly enhanced the expression of four osteogenic genes. Although the pTa group demonstrated some regulatory influence on osteogenesis at day 7, by day 14, its effects were not significantly different from those of the Control group. Previous studies have also reported the limited osteogenic efficacy of pTa [[60], [61], [62], [63]]. In the case of the SP2-pTa group, the cumulative findings across both time points suggested a relatively modest osteogenic effect. The qPCR analysis in this study employed a fixed amount of RNA, which reduced the slight inhibitory impact of SP2-pTa on cell proliferation. Consequently, although SF possesses some osteogenic-promoting properties [[64], [65], [66]], under identical conditions, its inhibitory effect on cell proliferation led to a reduced overall osteogenic outcome compared to the Control group, as illustrated in Fig. 5C and. 5E–H.

To validate the osteogenic capability of SP2-pTa-4 % in vivo, a series of animal experiments were conducted, employing various techniques such as Micro-CT, fluorescence labeling, VG staining, and immunohistochemistry for observation and analysis. In Fig. 11, the experimental results demonstrated a significant enhancement in osteogenic capacity in the SP2-pTa-4 % group. Specifically, the osteogenic effects of SP2-pTa-4 % continued to improve over time, indicating its strong potential in promoting bone tissue regeneration and repair. Based on the comparative results among the experimental groups, it was found that the osteogenic-promoting ability of SP2-pTa-4 % largely stems from WH. Previous studies have shown that WH plays a crucial role in the osteogenic cascade reaction, as it can induce the gene expression level of platelet-derived growth factor receptor β (PDGFRB) [22]. PDGFRB directly promotes the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of stem cells through the well-established extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) pathway. ERK activation is a critical factor in determining osteogenesis during the initial stages of bone formation [67,68]. WH releases PO43−, Ca2+, and Mg2+, promoting osteogenic differentiation via ERK pathways and suppressing osteoclast formation [22]. Activation of this pathway by WH leads to enhanced expression of genes related to osteogenesis, thereby facilitating the formation and mineralization of the bone matrix.

Vascularization is a pivotal factor in successful bone regeneration. To assess the pro-angiogenic properties of the scaffold, we conducted a tube formation assay (Fig. 6). Our findings reveal that the biofunctionalized SP-pTa@WH composite scaffold significantly enhances the formation of endothelial cell networks. Existing literature underscores the role of magnesium ions (Mg2+) as essential in early-stage angiogenesis during bone regeneration, primarily by enhancing oxygen and nutrient supply through the activation of the HIF-1α/VEGF pathway [69,70]. In comparison to growth factors such as VEGF and BMP-2, or RNA therapies, Mg2+ offers inherent stability, low immunogenicity, and direct activation of endogenous signaling pathways without the need for complex delivery systems [71].

To assess the immunomodulatory potential of SP2-pTa-4 %, immunofluorescence staining was conducted (Fig. 7), revealing that SP2-pTa-4 % possesses significant anti-inflammatory properties. In contrast, the pTa group exhibited comparatively weaker anti-inflammatory effects, a finding that has been well-documented in previous studies [72,73]. The SP2-pTa group, while promoting the expression of both CCR7 and Arg-1, displayed an overall mild pro-inflammatory tendency. Furthermore, its ability to promote anti-inflammatory phenotypic differentiation is hypothesized to be due to the presence of SF, a hypothesis supported by previous research [66]. However, the role of PVA in promoting macrophage phenotypes has not yet been discovered. In comparing the differential effects of the experimental groups on macrophage polarization, it is apparent that the inclusion of WH significantly enhances the promotion of anti-inflammatory phenotypes.

Additionally, in vivo experiments validating the anti-inflammatory effects of the composite scaffold demonstrated that the SP2-pTa-4 % group exhibited notable anti-inflammatory outcomes, as evidenced by tissue immunofluorescence and HE staining (Fig. 12). The anti-inflammatory efficacy of the composite scaffold increased over time across both observed periods. Previous studies have also underscored the favorable anti-inflammatory properties of WH, although its excat mechanisms remain to be elucidated [31].

In the transcriptomic analysis of anti-inflammatory mechanisms across different groups, the TGF-β signaling pathway demonstrated significant differences, as depicted in Fig. 8B. A detailed examination of the pathway diagram in Fig. 8C revealed distinct variations in the GARP protein. GARP is pivotal in immune regulation by modulating Smad signaling alterations induced by TGF-β [74,75]. Further analysis of genes associated with this pathway, as illustrated in Figs. S1–2, identified the NRROS and Lrrc32 genes, which are associated with GARP in the SP2-pTa-4 % group. NRROS and Lrrc32 are instrumental in facilitating the presentation of TGF-β on the cell membrane, thereby enhancing its subsequent expression [76,77]. Based on the expression profiles of NRROS and Lrrc32 across different groups as illustrated in Fig. 10A, Lrrc32 exhibited comparable expression levels in the pTa and SP2-pTa groups. In the SP2-pTa-4 % group, after accounting for the influences of pTa and SP2, WH appeared to play a pivotal role in the upregulation of Lrrc32. Moreover, NRROS demonstrated variable regulatory directions and magnitudes among the three groups, indicating its heightened sensitivity to alterations in material composition. This suggests that NRROS is likely a critical factor underlying the differential inflammatory responses of macrophages across the groups.

The protein-protein interaction network depicted in Fig. 9 further highlights the significance of NRROS, as genes located closer to the center with more connections are considered key regulators due to their extensive downstream effects. Subsequent analysis of NRROS expression across groups, as shown in Fig. 7, Fig. 10A, revealed that its upregulation or downregulation and expression levels were consistent with qPCR results and corresponded well with the inflammatory phenotypes observed in immunofluorescence assays. For instance, the SP2-pTa-4 % group exhibited pronounced anti-inflammatory properties, with a significant upregulation of NRROS genes. Moreover, the pTa group displayed only mild anti-inflammatory activity and moderate NRROS upregulation. The SP2-pTa group showed pro-inflammatory tendencies alongside a slight downregulation of NRROS. Although the precise roles of NRROS and Lrrc32 within the TGF-β/Smad pathway necessitate further investigation, the experimental findings from this study underscore the critical importance of NRROS relative to other genes.

Concerning the role of NRROS in mediating the anti-inflammatory effects of the composite scaffolds, the pathway diagram (Fig. 8C) pertaining to immunosuppression illustrates notable differences in the expression of genes encoding downstream proteins of the TGF-β pathway. Specifically, in the pTa and SP2-pTa-4 % groups, genes associated with NRROS, Smad2/3, and SARA (Zfyve16) were upregulated. In contrast, the SP2-pTa group demonstrated a downregulation of Smad2/3 and NRROS. These differential gene expressions elucidate the variations in macrophage inflammatory phenotypes observed among the groups. The TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway is well-documented for its role in promoting the M2 polarization of macrophages, characterized by an anti-inflammatory phenotype [[78], [79], [80], [81]]. Furthermore, the upregulation of NRROS has been associated with a reduction in ROS production in macrophages, thereby attenuating ROS-induced bone resorption and decreasing M1 macrophage polarization [[82], [83], [84], [85], [86], [87]]. The upregulation of NRROS is particularly advantageous for the osteogenic effects of the materials, providing a mechanistic explanation for the immunofluorescence results observed. To further explore the impact of these materials on cellular behaviors modulated by NRROS, we evaluated ROS levels across the groups. The SP2-pTa-4 % group exhibited significant ROS scavenging effects, with the effects of the other groups also substantiated, as illustrated in Fig. 10B–C.

Excluding the contributions of SP2 and pTa in SP2-pTa-4 %, it can be inferred that the immunomodulatory effects mediated by the TGF-β signaling pathway, activated by the release of WH's ions following SP2-pTa-4 % implantation, are responsible for the material's anti-inflammatory properties and foster a microenvironment favorable for osteogenesis. This study revealed the intrinsic pathway through which WH regulates NRROS gene. By elucidating the specific mechanisms underlying WH's anti-inflammatory effects, we enhance our understanding of its pivotal role in the regulation of the immune microenvironment. This insight not only establishes a theoretical foundation for future research but also facilitates the development of innovative clinical applications.

Based on the comprehensive evaluation of SP-pTa@WH, the composite scaffold developed in this study ensures the sustained release of bioactive ions in vivo, a feature that is critical for its application in bone tissue repair. WH not only significantly promotes osteogenesis but also effectively reduces inflammatory responses and reduces ROS levels during bone regeneration. The combination of the slow-release properties of SP-pTa@WH and the multifaceted efficacy of WH creates a more favorable microenvironment for bone healing, significantly accelerating the repair and regeneration of damaged bone tissue.

The SP-pTa@WH scaffold demonstrates distinct advantages over existing composite systems for bone regeneration. While magnesium-ion coatings on porous Ta scaffolds achieve sub-1 ppm release rates [88,89], our hydrogel-encapsulated system maintains therapeutic Mg2+ concentrations (1–12 ppm) within the optimal osteogenic range. Compared to growth factor-loaded systems, which face high costs, instability, and rapid degradation, the SP-pTa@WH composite scaffold offers multifunctional synergy (osteogenesis/angiogenesis/immunomodulation) at significantly lower production expenses. Unlike single-functional growth factors requiring precise dosing to avoid adverse effects (e.g., BMP-2-induced ectopic bone formation), the natural composition of WH ensures safer therapeutic outcomes [90,91] The combination of 3D-printed pTa's mechanical properties with WH's bioactive microenvironment creates a biomimetic platform that more closely replicates native bone tissue architecture and function, establishing a balanced efficacy-cost-safety profile superior to conventional approaches.

The clinical translation of SP-pTa@WH scaffolds presents several practical considerations. Regarding scalability, 3D printing of pTa scaffolds demonstrates comparable efficiency to porous titanium. Although the cost of Ta powders (approximately $1000 per kilogram) is higher than that of Ti6Al4V powders, implants used for bone defect repair are typically fabricated as porous structures with porosities ranging from 65 % to 85 %. Consequently, the weight of a porous Ta scaffold is generally around 100 g, making such scaffolds economically feasible for clinical applications. Storage stability is addressed through lyophilization and vacuum drying, preserving the WH component until implantation, where hydrogel re-gelation occurs upon site hydration. Importantly, controlled Mg2+ release (<10 ppm/day) ensures safety, as this rate is well below the 300 mg/day adult intake threshold, minimizing systemic risks even in renal-impaired patients. These attributes collectively support the scaffold's clinical viability. The current study did not include histopathological analysis of major organs, which represents an important limitation in our biosafety evaluation. Future studies will systematically investigate systemic toxicity through comprehensive organ histology and blood biochemical analyses at multiple timepoints.

Conclusion

This study developed SP-pTa@WH, an innovative composite scaffold combining porous Tantalum (pTa) with a silk fibroin-polyvinyl alcohol (SP) hydrogel loaded with whitlockite (WH). The scaffold was optimized for enhanced osteogenic and immunomodulatory functions in bone defect repair. The experimental findings corroborated the exceptional biocompatibility and osteogenic potential of the scaffold, as evidenced by the significant upregulation of osteogenic markers such as RUNX2, OCN, OPN, and Col I by WH. Furthermore, SP-pTa@WH demonstrated a pronounced ability to enhance angiogenesis, as indicated by its increased tube formation capacity. WH notably elevated the expression of osteogenic markers. Additionally, SP-pTa@WH influenced macrophage polarization towards the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype through the TGF-β/NRROS pathway, a phenomenon confirmed by high-throughput sequencing analysis. In vivo rabbit bone defect models demonstrated SP-pTa@WH composite scaffold exhibited superior bone regeneration performance over pTa, showing increased bone volume fraction, trabecular number, and accelerated healing. Histological analysis confirmed reduced inflammation at the implant site. The scaffold's dual functionality, which involves promoting osteogenesis and regulating immune responses, establishes it as a promising candidate for clinical applications in bone repair and immune-related bone disorders.
