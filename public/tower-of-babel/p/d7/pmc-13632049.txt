Service continuity of community-based health and social care organisations in times of disaster and health emergencies: a mapping review
Authors: Farhana Haque Nila, Michelle Villeneuve, Kuo-Yi Jade Chang, Parvathi Subramaniam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe physical and economic impacts of disaster and health emergencies have become a global concern that continues to generate discussion regarding disaster preparedness, response, and mitigation strategies. Maintaining continuity of services during these challenging and dynamic circumstances requires adequate organisational preparedness. This study aimed to map the existing research on the emergency management capabilities of community-based health and social care organisations and identify the factors that impact service continuity as a critical component of business continuity planning.MethodThis mapping review was carried out in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines. Eight databases were searched: Medline, PsycINFO, Embase, CINAHL, Scopus, ProQuest, Web of Science, EBSCOhost. Studies written in English and published between January 2000 and August 2022 were included. Eligible studies were primarily focused on business or service continuity of community-based health and social care organisations. Thematic analysis of data was conducted.ResultsOut of 3,195 articles, 176 met the inclusion criteria, mostly conducted in developed countries. These studies investigated various natural hazard emergencies, disease outbreaks, and pandemics, using different study designs, offering valuable insights into emergency management capabilities of community-based health and social care organisations across the disaster cycle. The findings suggest that several factors can serve as both barriers and enablers to the continuity of care, depending on the context and the specific stage of the disaster cycle at which they are addressed. Limited research was found on organisational preparedness for at-risk clients, including the elderly and individuals with disabilities. Additionally, there is a scarcity of research on the prevention and recovery stages of the emergency management cycle.ConclusionThis mapping review offers a comprehensive overview of the factors influencing service continuity, highlights existing research gaps and targeted recommendation for service providers and governments to inform effective planning that ensures continuity of service and supports for people whose health, safety and well-being can be compromised in emergency situations. Future research could focus on strengthening organisational resilience during the prevention phase, examine tools and approaches for optimising emergency management efforts, and investigate strategies to improve cross-sector collaboration across all stages of disaster management cycle.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15001-3.

In 2023, there were 399 natural hazard emergencies worldwide [1]. Similarly, recent decades have witnessed a surge in health-related emergencies, which strained the health-care system and have caused economic and social disruption [2]. For example, the COVID-19 pandemic alone caused a cumulative death toll of seven million people worldwide [2, 3]. The physical and economic impacts of these catastrophic emergencies have become a global concern that continues to generate discussion regarding disaster preparedness, response, and mitigation strategies. The impact of disasters and health emergencies are more extensive and devastating for those who are already at-risk, including the elderly, people with long-term health conditions, people with disabilities, and those who are homeless [4]. Community-based health and social care organisations play a vital role in providing emergency support services at all stages of a disaster, as they have a comprehensive understanding of their clients’ individual risks and functional needs [5]. Disasters can disrupt these services that people rely on and create an overwhelming demand for critical care services [6]. Inadequate preparation can hinder the capability of organisations to maintain essential services during and after emergencies.

Effective preparedness requires a clear understanding of organisations’ roles, responsibilities, and capabilities throughout the emergency or disaster management cycle [7]. This cycle includes four phases: Prevention, Preparedness, Response, and Recovery [7]. The Prevention phase, also known as mitigation, involves proactive measures to prevent or reduce the impact of disasters, such as establishing alternative communication systems and power sources [7]. Preparedness includes developing emergency plans, building networks, conducting drills, and training staff to minimise loss of life and property, and ensure early recovery [7]. The Response phase, lasting hours to weeks, involves activities like providing aid, coordinating with emergency services and community organisations to provide critical care [7]. Finally, the Recovery phase, spanning months to years, focuses on supporting communities and individuals in restoring their emotional, social, economic, physical, and psychological well-being [8].

Maintaining continuity of services during these challenging, and dynamic circumstances requires adequate organisational preparedness through effective business continuity planning [6]. Business continuity planning (BCP) refers to a comprehensive set of policies, procedures, and information that is meticulously developed and maintained for use in the event of a service disruption [9]. The aim of a BCP is to ensure the provision of essential services until regular operations can be restored [9]. A comprehensive BCP encompasses several essential elements, including a shared understanding of disasters and emergencies, and their impacts on the services; identification of essential services; risk assessment (i.e., considering how service delivery may be affected); strategic planning and documentation; and testing and reviewing the plan [6, 10, 11]. It is important for community-based health and social care organisations to have a BCP that outlines the actions that will be taken to address specific obstacles to the continuity of essential services during and in the aftermath of a disaster [6, 12].

This study aimed to map the existing research on the emergency management capabilities of community-based health and social care organisations and identify the factors that impact service continuity as a critical component of BCP. This mapping review is an integral part of a larger research project that seeks to examine the capability of community-based health and social care organisations in contributing to disaster risk reduction (DRR) with the people they support. Findings of this mapping review informed the scope of a separate systematic review, focused on the service continuity support initiatives of community-based healthcare and social care organisations. The systematic review is reported elsewhere [13]. The challenges faced by community-based organisations during emergencies are not universally understood, and addressing these gaps can provide actionable insights to guide future research and practice. Additionally, these organisations are often frontline responders, yet they face various barriers to service continuity during crises. Studying the factors that hinder or enable their operations can lead to significant improvements in organisational emergency preparedness, response, and recovery strategies. The studies included in this review report various disasters and health-related emergency experiences of the community organisations across the world and integrate insights from a wide range of healthcare workers, including general practitioners, nurse practitioners, social workers, mental health practitioners, disaster relief workers, pharmacists, and home health aides. In this study, we explore the key obstacles and facilitators of service continuity across a spectrum of emergencies, encompassing both natural hazard disasters and public health crises such as pandemics. This approach provides a comprehensive understanding of the factors needed to maintain critical services during diverse emergency situations. This knowledge enables community health and social care organisations to identify potential risks, allocate resources efficiently, develop organisational preparedness and business continuity planning. Furthermore, identifying the barriers and enablers to service continuity provides valuable insights for policymakers, government officials, and key stakeholders in this field to prioritise targeted actions that address these barriers to service continuity. By mapping the primary research focus of the included studies, this review highlights areas that necessitate further exploration, thereby setting the stage for future systematic research.

Methods

A mapping review systematically describes, categorises and identifies evidence and evidence gaps on a specific topic, and offers a broad overview of the existing knowledge [14]. This design is well-suited to the purpose of this paper, as it provides a comprehensive overview of existing research examining the emergency management roles, experiences, and capabilities of community-based health and social care organisations and identifies the factors influencing the service continuity during emergencies. This mapping review was carried out in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [15].

Search strategy

Eight electronic databases were searched, namely Medline, PsycINFO, Embase, CINAHL, Scopus, ProQuest, Web of Science, and EBSCOhost. These eight electronic databases were selected to capture a wide range of literature across multiple disciplines, including health, social care, and disaster management to ensure a comprehensive review of the service continuity of community-based health and social care organisations during natural hazards and health emergencies. These databases were chosen based on their relevance to the research topic, the breadth of their indexed journals, and their accessibility for retrieving peer-reviewed articles [16–21]. A search string was developed based on the guidance of an experienced research librarian, who provided critical expertise in developing search strings tailored to the study objectives. The databases were searched using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords relevant to the topic. MeSH terms such as “disaster,” “natural disaster,” “pandemic,” “health personnel,” “community health services,” and “primary health care” were incorporated alongside keywords designed to capture broader concepts and important aspects of the topic. For example, keywords included “emergency preparedness”, “service continuity”, “business continuity”, “community health worker”, and “social worker”. The final search string adopted for the database search and the full electronic search strategy for MEDLINE are included in supplementary materials (see supplementary file 1). Only peer-reviewed articles accessible online were included in the search, which was limited to English articles published between 1 January 2000 to 26 August 2022. The rationale behind selected time period was the increased frequency and severity of the natural hazard emergencies and the infectious disease outbreaks over the past two decades [22]. By concentrating on natural hazard emergencies and health-related emergencies, this study aims to address the most urgent and widespread challenges faced by community-based health and social care organisations. While man-made hazards, such as industrial accidents, are economically significant and increasingly prevalent in industrialised nations [23], they require distinct strategies that differ from those needed for natural and health-related emergencies. Effective disaster response plans must distinguish between these types of hazards to account for their unique challenges and management approaches. No restrictions were imposed on the geographical inclusion.

Eligibility criteria

The eligible studies were randomized controlled studies (parallel-group or crossover), cohort studies, longitudinal studies, case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, observational studies, and case studies. The included studies had either one or more of the following exposures: natural hazard emergencies, disease outbreaks, or pandemics. Eligible studies were those that focused on business or service continuity of community-based health and social care organisations. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in Supplementary materials (see supplementary file 2).

Screening and study selection

Following deduplication, the initial database searches were imported into the Covidence software for the screening phase. Dual screening of the titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review of included studies was conducted independently by two reviewers (FHN and PS). At both stages of screening, any disagreements were resolved by consulting with a third reviewer (KJC). All authors agreed to the final inclusion of the articles.

Data extraction

The first author (FHN) extracted data from eligible publications using a charting framework developed by the research team. The charting framework which guided the data extraction was developed based on the research aims: 1) general characteristics of the studies, 2) focus areas of research activities, and 3) factors influencing service continuity and care delivery in community-based organisations. The charting framework included: title, authors, publication year, publication country, type of disaster exposure, study aims/objectives, study design, study participants/population, barriers and facilitators of service continuity, key findings, and recommendations. Data were checked for accuracy by two reviewers (PS and KJC). Any disagreements were resolved through discussions among all authors. A summary table outlining relevant data is provided in Supplementary materials (see supplementary file 3).

Data analysis

The initial step of data analysis involved familiarisation of the charted data through vigorous reading. Subsequently, an inductive analysis was conducted to address the specific research questions. Codes and themes were generated using a well-established six-stage thematic approach, which involves familiarization with data, generation of initial codes, searching for categories, reviewing themes, defining themes, and final analysis [24]. The first author (FHN) conducted the initial inductive coding. Two authors (MV and KJC) verified the accuracy of the coding and discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus. After initial coding, the data were revisited and discussed collectively to group the codes into categories. This process helped us to identify and group the main focus of the included research studies (presented in Tables 1 – 3 in the Results section). All authors reviewed the extracted data describing factors influencing service continuity and care delivery. Working collectively, we differentiated barriers and enablers and grouped them accordingly (presented in Tables 4 and 5 and further discussed in the results section). This mapping review was conducted to identify and categorise the barriers and facilitators faced by community-based health and social care organisations during disasters and health emergencies. In line with the purpose of mapping reviews [14], the primary focus was on providing a comprehensive overview of the evidence base rather than evaluating the methodological quality of the included studies. As such, no formal quality assessment of the studies was undertaken.

Results

Out of 3,195 total records retrieved from the database search, 970 duplicate records were removed. At the title and abstract screening stage, 2225 records were screened for eligibility and 2020 records were excluded for the following reasons: irrelevant (n = 2002), duplicate (n = 3), and unavailable full text (n = 15). The irrelevant (n = 2002) studies primarily focused on emergency departments within healthcare settings, disease-specific critical care emergencies, and secondary or tertiary healthcare services, such as hospitals or specialised care centres. As these topics fell outside the scope of this study (see supplementary file 2), they were excluded during the title and abstract screening phase. General characteristics of selected studies

Geographical distribution of selected studies

Of the 176 studies, 174 were carried out in 60 different countries, with the United States accounting for the highest number of studies at 29% (51 studies). Additionally, 13 studies each were conducted in Canada and Australia. One study employed a global online survey distribution [25], and one study was conducted across European countries [26]. Some studies were conducted in more than one country [27–37]. Types of exposure

The selected studies covered a range of disaster exposures. Out of the included studies, 104 (59%) were related to the Covid-19 pandemic, 55 (31%) focused on natural hazard emergencies and 14 (8%) explored infectious disease outbreaks, epidemics, and other pandemics. Three studies [38–40] addressed emergencies caused by both natural hazards and disease outbreaks.

Methodologies

Nearly half (46%; 81) of the included studies reported the use of qualitative methodologies. The majority of these qualitative studies (83%, 67) relied on primary data collected via interviews, focus groups, town halls, or participatory research. Additionally, 11% (9) studies adopted a case study approach. And 6% (5) studies conducted qualitative content analysis. Of the 176 studies, 32% (57) reported the use of quantitative methodologies, with 13% (23) being quantitative descriptive studies. A small portion, comprising 8% (14), adopted a mixed-method approach and only one study used individually randomized, parallel-group, controlled trials.

Stage of disaster management cycle

Of the 176 studies, 51% (89) investigated factors or interventions related to the response stage of the disaster management cycle, while 25% (44) were related to both preparedness and response phases. Twelve percent (21) of the studies were related to both response and recovery phases. Only one study [41] was related to all stages of the disaster management cycle, covering prevention, preparedness, response, and recovery.

Factors influencing service continuity and care delivery

This section shares the factors influencing service continuity and care delivery in community-based health and social care organisations. First, we describe the barriers identified in the literature (Table 4). This is followed by a description of the enablers (Table 5). The rationale behind presenting similar barriers and enablers lies in illustrating their dynamic and interconnected nature within the context of disaster preparedness and response. This approach emphasises that many barriers are not fixed obstacles but can be transformed into enablers through targeted interventions, particularly in the pre-disaster phase. For example, insufficient funding can act as a barrier by preventing community-based organisations from acquiring necessary disaster response resources. However, through preparedness interventions, such as securing grants or forming strategic partnerships, this challenge can be turned into an enabler, empowering organisations to enhance their readiness. Similarly, a lack of disaster-specific training leaves staff unprepared, but implementing accessible training programs can transform this weakness into a strength, equipping staff with the necessary skills to respond effectively during emergencies. Barriers to service continuity

Factors negatively influencing service continuity coalesced into ten barriers. Each is summarised in Table 1 and discussed here.

Limited workforce capacity: Shortage of personnel [140, 171] was one of the major challenges during and after emergencies. Several reasons for this shortfall were reported, including staff being redeployed [93, 101], being sick or quarantined [151], staff turnover [174, 198], staff inability to report at work due to roadblocks, and disaster-induced threats to personal properties [65, 70]. Moreover, during emergencies, service providers were often required to take on new and extra tasks [64, 95, 123, 140] that added extra burden [121, 138, 139] to their routine care delivery. For example, one study reported that increased responsibilities such as patient and visitor screening, and mandatory use of personal protective equipment due to implementation of new infection control protocols and procedures were the major obstacles during the SARS (severe acute respiratory syndrome) outbreak [97].

Inadequate emergency management capability: This barrier highlights systemic issues like insufficient disaster-specific training and education programs that hinder organisations’ ability to prepare for emergencies. Inadequate training and education [64] posed unique challenges to service continuity. Reportedly, during the COVID-19 pandemic, staff often had to take on new tasks without any orientation or prior training [32]. Similar barriers were also cited by other studies, such as insufficient training on different software [58], poorly organized training programs that were designed in a bureaucratic fashion [106, 107], lack of training on mental health [33, 77, 165, 197], needs assessment [81] and clinical management [154]. Moreover, due to lack of experience and knowledge in disaster management [54], service providers often perceived themselves as being unprepared and less competent [37, 56, 61, 148, 150, 159, 161].

Impeded social and emotional well-being: Service providers’ social and emotional well-being was one of the most cited factors that affected service continuity. During emergencies, healthcare workers often experience burnout [32, 36], social isolation [118, 196], fear [101, 123], anxiety [56, 121], and other psychological distress [122] owing to increased workload and safety concerns. For example, many studies highlighted the need for formal and informal support networks to mitigate psychosocial concerns. Examples of these support networks include supervisor and managerial support [140], family support [75, 108, 124, 140], peer support [124, 167], organisational support [100, 146], government support [49, 65], and gestures of support and appreciation from the workplace and community [32, 117].

Unclear communication: Disinformation was one of the most frequently cited barriers that hindered the emergency management of community organisations [55, 164]. Reportedly, inadequate coordination between various stakeholders also impeded timely and accurate information sharing and collaboration [64, 97]. Examples of other communication barriers include lack of up-to-date information [65], improper sources and content of information [55], unclear communication between multiple authorities [96, 186], lack of collaboration with at-risk communities [45], and myths and misconceptions circulating in the community [29, 82].

Inaccessible technology: Limited access to tools and technology also constrained the care delivery process [151, 186]. For instance, many studies cited technological barriers in terms of poor technology literacy [74], need for high-speed Internet [151], scarce funding for tools and technology [188], and insufficient time to learn about new software and technical issues [58]. Healthcare workers also mentioned patient privacy and liability concerns related to virtual care sessions [43, 186]. Several studies highlighted the need for in-person visits and cited virtual care as infeasible and inadequate to meet the needs of individuals with cognitive impairments, and those requiring interpretation assistance [36, 179, 190]. Moreover, sudden shift to virtual care during the COVID-19 pandemic also made the services inaccessible for many elderly patients who lacked devices or technology-related knowledge [179].

Insufficient resource and supply: Supply chain disruptions negatively impacted the capability of some community organisations to provide services [25, 151]. Many participants shared their concern about inconsistent guidelines [96, 114] and uneven distribution of protective gear across facilities [32]. For example, one cross-country study conducted in Denmark and Germany, found that personal protective equipment and surveillance were stronger in the hospital sector than in long-term care sector [33]. Furthermore, suboptimal infrastructure [66, 95], inadequate resources including equipment, disposables, and medicines [66], unsafe working environments [141, 168], and limited financial support [145, 174] were among the other major facility-level challenges that service providers confronted during emergencies.

Inefficient administration and professional support: This barrier pertains to operational inefficiencies during response phase, including unclear roles, improper chains of command, and the lack of comprehensive operational guidelines that hinder effective emergency response. Many participants stated their struggle with limited workplace leadership and guidance [31, 32, 73]. Participants reported perceived challenges due to lack of supervision [37, 106, 110], improper chain of command and emergency guidelines [44, 114], unclear roles and responsibilities [98, 180] during emergencies, and the dilemma of the planned and practical enforcement of health laws and policies [34]. For example, several countries implemented a ‘no-touch’ policy for the community healthcare workers (CHWs) during the Ebola outbreak to minimise the risk of exposure [34]. However, the lack of clear guidelines on when to treat children with fever or diarrhoea in the community versus when to refer them to a healthcare facility led to inconsistent referral practices among CHWs [34]. Some CHWs referred all community cases, while others referred only if patients did not recover within 24–48 hours of initial treatment or if additional Ebola symptoms appeared [34].

Accessing clients at their home: Transportation barriers such as damaged and blocked roads [62, 65, 141], limited availability of accessible public transport [61, 105, 166], and long-distance travel to work [90] affected service providers’ ability to report to work during and after natural hazard emergencies. Research indicates that the displacement resulting from natural hazard emergencies (e.g. earthquake) also made it harder for the service providers to track down their clients [62]. Furthermore, common challenges in accessing clients at their home during infectious disease emergencies include hostile responses and stigmatisation [29, 34]. Reportedly, service providers received hostile responses when attending home visits during COVID-19 pandemic, which were often elicited due to the fear of infection transmission [29]. Similarly, during the Ebola outbreak, community health workers were stigmatised as potential “Ebola carriers” and faced mistrust from the community members [34].

Lack of preparedness among organisations and staff: This barrier reflects shortcomings at the organisational and individual preparedness efforts, such as scarcity of organisational policies and guidelines, inadequate implementation of business continuity plans and limited participation in preparedness drills. This barrier highlights issues related to how organisations and staff engage in emergency preparedness. Inadequate disaster preparedness planning [103, 104, 148, 200], absence of clear guidelines on supply chain management [72], lack of comprehensive staff management plan for situations where a significant number of staff are unavailable [32] and scarcity of policies and advocacy [199] to enforce preparedness measures impacted service continuity. In some cases, participants also stated that poor implementation and dissemination of the available BCP led to confusion and disorganisation [126, 200]. Often service providers were uncertain about their roles as they were not included in the decision-making process [126]. Other factors that triggered organisational inefficiency in care provision included inadequate funding for disaster contingency planning [32], failure to incorporate the nearest primary health care centres to the emergency protocol [126], lack of continuous improvement of the available contingency plan [126], and poor participation in disaster drills and practice emergency exercises [46, 54, 78]. One study also highlighted the need to assess organisations’ readiness to utilise modern e-health solutions in the context of a rapidly changing situation such as Covid-19 pandemic [153].

Inaccessible facilities: Some studies reported that during natural hazard emergencies, facilities became damaged and inaccessible, therefore impeded organisations’ ability to continue service provision [50, 166]. For instance, hospitals and medical centres were often forced to close or reduce their operating hours due to flooding damage, such as blocked roads, blackouts, and backup generator failures [50, 141, 166]. In some cases, these barriers were compounded by inadequate access to supplies and medicines [158], difficulty reaching clients in evacuation centres [65] and/or long-term care facilities [80], and reduced capacity to support referral systems [197].

Enablers to service continuity

Factors facilitating or supporting service continuity grouped into seven enablers. Each is summarised in Table 5 and discussed here.

Policy guidance and administrative support: Studies highlighted the significance of government support initiatives in terms of effective communications, policies, and procedures [55, 140]. For example, many European countries implemented policies that reduced the minimum requirements for extended professional licenses for the retired and allowed recruiting of international medical graduates to meet the health workforce demand [26]. Furthermore, during the COVID-19 pandemic, some countries relaxed the medication dispensing policies and provided guidelines to direct supply chain prioritisation and working arrangements [36]. The dissemination of clear guidelines and recommendations by government and other health authorities was also identified as a key enabler to achieving optimal performance [87, 155].

Adequate social and emotional support: To promote staff health and wellbeing during emergencies, numerous organisations adopted compassionate leadership approaches and fostered supportive cultures [62, 140]. Some examples of support initiatives provided by organisations included developing strong team cohesion through online support groups [157], ongoing leadership and daily debriefing sessions [117, 169], informal support from team members [142, 167], onsite and remote mental health support service [191], emotional support from professional network [125], and support from family and friends [139]. Many organisations implemented strategies to leverage existing community support networks, such as community leaders and influential individuals, to rebuild trust [34, 96, 108]. Additionally, the existence of prior relationships and collaborations with other health facilities was deemed crucial during disaster response [67, 68, 175]. Moreover, participants stated that the appreciation received from their workplace and the community fostered dedication and willingness among service providers to perform during the crisis [49, 136].

Professional development opportunities: Several studies recommended that organisations implement ongoing professional development programs that offer tailored training materials and educational workshops [94, 102]. Examples of capacity-building interventions implemented by community organisations included training sessions on medication administration guidelines [99], infection prevention and control workshop [72], education programme to improve psychological recovery in post-disaster settings [178], training on mindfulness and self-care [67], and effective techniques for managing hostile or agitated patients [133]. Studies also found that the provision of digital resources, including computer-based video modules and accessibility of education materials on mobile devices was associated with increased participation and subsequent utilisation of training and education programs [36, 72, 198].

Being prepared: Studies identified several key enablers to ensure organisational disaster preparedness, which included conducting regular disaster exercises and drills [44, 150, 161], having proactive contingency plans [58, 148, 152], and performing regular risk assessments [44]. Organisations with an existing BCP that outlined the job responsibilities for staff, alternative service delivery methods, and protocols for communication and coordination with external partners were found to be effective in responding to emergencies and providing adequate support to individuals [39, 57, 91].

Effective communication and collaboration: The significance of effective communication between healthcare professionals and clients was emphasized in multiple studies [93, 97, 166]. Several organisations and healthcare workers employed various strategies to promote clarity and rapport during virtual care. Examples of such strategies included contacting patients prior to their appointments to assess their telehealth capabilities, collaborating with the caregivers to facilitate virtual visits with the elderly patients who lacked technology-related knowledge and using chat features as an alternative to audio communication in cases where it was challenging [179]. Participants also reported that access to up-to-date information and accurate media coverage is critical to decision-making and emergency response [69, 103]. For instance, having reliable information on health-related and drug-related legislation [82], sharing updates via organisation websites and social media platforms [169]. Moreover, studies suggest that service providers can further enhance their emergency management capacity by collaborating with other organisations to establish a network of agencies and outreach services [51, 62, 95]. For example, participants reported that their established connections with other healthcare providers enabled them to arrange emergency assistive devices such as wheelchairs and hoists for individuals with disabilities at evacuation centres during emergencies [68].

Alternative modes of care delivery: Community organisations often employ various strategies to maintain uninterrupted services. These include, implementing alternative modes of service delivery, such as transitioning to telehealth and virtual care [25, 28, 87], interacting with clients in innovative ways [95], obtaining remote access to clinical records [36], reorganizing infrastructure to provide tailored care for individual needs [99], and special delivery services to clients’ who have relocated [139, 184, 193]. The use of tools and technologies can further enhance proactive disaster management. For example, Geographic information system (GIS) can be used to identify community resources; assess the risk and needs of clients to prioritize emergency management, including shelters; and to visualise threats for particular emergencies, such as flooding or storms [58].

Having resources and support: The availability of logistical resources such as protective gear, medicine, and equipment, significantly contributed to an organisation’s ability to implement infection prevention policies and safety measures during emergencies [97, 165]. Many organisations also arranged alternative accommodation and transportation services for their staff such as additional buses [112] and shuttle services [36]; separate accommodation for staff who prefer to be apart from their families due to fear of spreading the illness [136], and those who have been relocated post-disaster [92]. Research indicates that prior experience and training in disaster preparedness, response, and relief activities allow service providers to quickly adapt to new roles and responsibilities [96, 140]. Other frequently stated enablers are having sufficient financial resources to invest in emergency preparedness and response efforts [26], having proactive employees [33, 49, 69, 70], and mindful attitudes and willingness from clients and the community to engage with emergency measures [103, 163].

Discussion

This mapping review offers a comprehensive overview of the research archive by exploring extensive literature on the emergency management capabilities of community-based health and social care organisations in the context of various emergency exposures including natural hazard emergencies, pandemics, and infectious disease outbreaks. This knowledge enables community, health and social welfare organisations to identify potential vulnerabilities in their own systems and adopt context-specific measures that ensures service continuity during and after various emergencies. Additionally, the use of bubble maps in this review enhances understanding by visually representing the focus areas and distribution of research efforts across different stages of the disaster cycle. This visualisation reveals where scholarly attention has been focused and where critical gaps exist.

This mapping study included 176 articles, most of which were conducted in developed countries, indicating a paucity of research in developing countries. Tables 1–3 show the current evidence-base and gaps grounded in the extant research. The three evidence-gap (“bubble”) maps provide a visual overview of the extent and nature of research in three focal areas: (a) impact/needs assessment; (b) well-being of service providers; and (c) service continuity strategies, their implementation and/or evaluation. Studies focused on impact and need assessment emphasised the roles of service providers and the shifts in service demand and delivery caused by emergencies (see Table 1). Most of these studies were informed by qualitative and quantitative descriptive research designs. These studies primarily focused on the response phase by providing critical insights into immediate actions required to address community needs during emergencies. However, they offer limited guidance on proactively identifying roles and experience of service providers during the prevention phase or facilitating long-term recovery efforts.

The health and well-being of healthcare and other service providers have been another key focus of the reviewed literature, with studies exploring the psychological, emotional, and physical toll of emergencies (see Table 2). These studies predominantly focused on the disaster response stage, with additional attention given to the recovery and preparedness phases. They play a critical role in supporting service providers by examining stressors, coping mechanisms, and strategies aimed at promoting health and well-being during and after emergencies. However, there remains a noticeable gap in exploring resilience factors and proactive measures that could help mitigate the impact on service providers before disasters occur. The majority of studies used quantitative and qualitative methodological approaches to examine the emergency management experience and intricacies within the context of infectious disease outbreaks and natural hazard emergencies.

Studies exploring and evaluating service continuity strategies were informed by a wide range of study types and methods, providing valuable insights into the effectiveness of support initiatives, strategies, and tools across different phases of the disaster cycle (see Table 3). Findings from these studies are critical in ensuring that essential services remain operational during crises. Research on service continuity strategies, including their implementation and evaluation, predominantly centred on the preparedness and response phases of the emergency management cycle. Hence, the applicability of such strategies in the recovery stage, where rebuilding systems and addressing long-term needs necessitate more adaptive and flexible approaches remains unexplored. Consistent with previous study findings [201], this mapping review highlights the scarcity of research on the prevention and recovery stages of the emergency management cycle. Investigating the roles, experience, and capabilities of community organisations during the prevention phase can inform the organisations about proactive measures that effectively minimize the impact of emergencies. Further research is needed to explore new approaches and technologies that facilitates speedy recovery. Organisations can use this information to tailor their recovery efforts and ensure prompt resumption of essential care and services during the recovery period.

This study also identified and mapped the key factors that impact service continuity (See Tables 1 and 2). Identifying barriers (Table 1) to service continuity is a critical starting point for developing effective organisational-level emergency preparedness. By also recognising enabling factors (Table 2), service-based organisations are provided with a host of strategies they can use to overcome obstacles to maintaining essential services and supports that people rely on in emergencies. This review underscores the fact that several factors can serve as both barriers and enablers to the continuity of care, depending on the context and the specific stage of the disaster cycle at which they are addressed. For example, a proactive contingency plan can greatly reduce the risks associated with a disaster by identifying potential hazards, developing effective response protocols that are well-communicated through the organisation, establishing communication networks to prioritize individual needs, mobilising resources, and coordinating responses in a timely manner [58, 152]. To enhance emergency preparedness, service providers and clients should be included in service continuity planning [93, 140]. Clear and transparent discussions are needed to clarify key roles and responsibilities of staff [98]. Conducting disaster drills [44] can raise awareness of emergency preparedness across organisations. In addition to having a functional BCP and conducting regular review of the emergency preparedness protocols, community-based organisations can support operational stability during emergencies by fostering collaborative relationships with other healthcare and social welfare entities [50, 162, 195].

The provision of up-to-date information and a streamlined chain of commands is essential to ensure the effectiveness of emergency communication [39, 44]. Studies suggest that to address disinformation, it is important to implement measures, such as maintaining an up-to-date database of patient information organised by postal code [44], adhering to reliable emergency guidelines [36, 87], disseminating emergency information in newsletters to all staff and clients [169], and conducting daily debriefing sessions to increase situational awareness about risks and responsibilities [98]. Organisations and staff should pay special attention to tailoring emergency information for vulnerable populations, such as considering appropriate modes of communication, assessing the need for translations and/or sign language materials [26, 179].

To ensure uninterrupted service provision, government can also play a critical role in developing surge capacity and providing logistical support to community organisations [26, 111, 139]. By mapping available resources and capacities of community organisations, government can also optimise the utilisation of existing resources. Additionally, mapping existing local resources, including the skills and abilities of community members and volunteers, can considerably strengthen an organisation’s capacity and emergency response capabilities. During emergencies, the utilisation of digital tools such as Geographic Information Systems (GIS) [58] and information systems that support operational continuity and decision making [40] in conjunction with other technologies can contribute to the effective management of resources and personnel. In regard to enhancing capacities and capabilities, employees must be trained on a variety of tools and technologies, clinical knowledge, as well as people-centred emergency preparedness activities [43, 72, 98, 144]. Community-based organisations play a vital role in ensuring that such training and educational programs are delivered in a flexible manner that addresses various barriers that may prevent employees from accessing such programs, including transportation, time, and financial constraints [36, 144, 174].

Community-based organisations should make effort to ensure the psychological well-being of health-care workers and develop plans to address the mental health challenges and impacts of burnout, stress, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [42]. Regular assessment of staff health and wellness should be arranged to ensure emotional wellbeing and promote effective behavioural coping mechanisms [122]. This may entail engaging in activities that enhance well-being, such as spending time with family, prioritising self-care practices, and offering support to both colleagues and clients [140].

Limitation

This study was restricted to peer-reviewed publications in English, which may have overlooked some relevant information published in other languages and in the grey literature, such as organisational reports, government reports and policy documents, which may have heightened the potential for publication bias. Future research or reviews could incorporate a wider range of grey literature to capture a more comprehensive assessment of service continuity challenges and enablers experienced by the community organisations in times of emergency. Although the search strategies employed for each electronic database were thorough and comprehensive, some relevant literature may have been missed. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings and conclusions of this study. This review also recognises that the enablers and barriers identified may differ depending on the context. Therefore, the findings of this review may not be universally applicable across different regions. Therefore, when applying findings to diverse settings, potential modifications or adaptations may be required based on regional variation, local expertise and knowledge. Finally, it was beyond the scope of mapping review to evaluate the quality of the included studies; therefore, this mapping review does not consider the specific strengths and weaknesses of included publications.

Recommendations

The findings of this mapping review highlight that community-based health and social care organisations must prioritise a proactive and comprehensive approach to emergency preparedness. This involves conducting detailed risk assessments to identify potential threats and develop robust emergency plans tailored to the unique demands of various emergencies, including pandemics and natural hazard disasters. These plans should be updated frequently to reflect the needs of both staff and the people they support, fostering resilience during emergencies. Training and regular disaster drills should be conducted to ensure staff are well-prepared and aware of their roles, enhancing the immediate response capabilities. Furthermore, establishing strong partnerships with other service providers and community organisations is crucial for resource sharing and enhancing response capabilities, addressing the identified gap in proactive measures for resilience building.

Governments play a pivotal role in supporting these organisations through legislation that mandates and facilitates comprehensive emergency preparedness. This includes providing necessary funding and resources. Governmental support should extend to creating centralised platforms for guidance and training, tailored to the diverse needs of service providers. By fostering an environment that encourages research into preventive and recovery phases, governments can help close the critical gaps identified in the study. Additionally, governments should enhance service continuity by developing surge capacities and logistical support, ensuring that community organisations can maintain operations during and after emergencies. Training programs funded or supported by the government should address barriers to access and include diverse learning modalities to ensure comprehensive staff preparedness across the sector.

Conclusion

This mapping review provides a concise summary of the extant research on organisational emergency preparedness and reveals the barriers and enablers of service continuity for community, health, and social welfare organisations. This study highlights the paucity of research on organisational preparedness that engage clients who are at risk, such as the elderly and individuals with disabilities. Further research is warranted to address the gaps in knowledge and enhance understanding of effective emergency management strategies that ensures continuity of services and supports for people whose health, safety, and well-being can be compromised in emergency situations. Future research could focus on enhancing the resilience of community organisations during the prevention phase through evaluations of best practices and their applicability across various contexts. Studies could also investigate the roles of community organisations during the prevention phase, identifying their resource needs, challenges, and innovative solutions to improve readiness. Additionally, inquiries into tools and technologies for optimising recovery efforts and supporting operational resilience and service continuity are essential. Furthermore, there is a pressing need to explore strategies for improving communication and collaboration between government and emergency sectors, community organisations, and private agencies at all stages of the disaster management cycle to ensure cohesive and effective emergency management.
