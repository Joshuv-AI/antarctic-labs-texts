Comparative Analysis of Laboratory-Scale Immunoglobulin G Purification Methods from Human Serum
Authors: Louisa Bourel, Fabrice Bray, Solange Vivier, Stéphanie Flament, Lucile Guilbert, Aurélien Chepy, Christian Rolando, David Launay, Sylvain Dubucquoi, Vincent Sobanski
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Immunoglobulin G (IgG) purification is a critical process for evaluating its role in autoimmune diseases, which are defined by the occurrence of autoantibodies. Affinity chromatography with protein G is widely considered to be the optimal technique for laboratory-scale purification. However, this technique has some limitations, including the exposure of IgG to low pH, which can compromise the quality of the purified IgG. Here, we show that alternative methods for IgG purification are possible while maintaining the quality of IgG. Different techniques for IgG purification from serum were evaluated and compared with protein G-based approaches: Melon Gel, caprylic acid-ammonium sulfate (CAAS) precipitation, anion-exchange chromatography with diethylamino ethyl (DEAE) following ammonium sulfate (AS) precipitation, and AS precipitation alone. The results demonstrated that the purification yield of these techniques surpassed that of protein G. However, differences in the purity of IgG were observed using GeLC-MS/MS. The avidity of purified IgG against selected targets (SARS-CoV-2 and topoisomerase-I) was similar between purified IgG obtained using all techniques and unpurified sera. Our work provides valuable insights for future studies of IgG function by recommending alternative purification methods that offer advantages in terms of yield, time efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and milder pH conditions than protein G.

Introduction

Immunoglobulins (Ig) represent 15–20% of the total serum proteins. Among Ig, immunoglobulin G (IgG) is the most abundant, accounting for 75% of Ig, and is further divided into four subclasses.1 IgG is a 150 kDa glycoprotein composed of two heavy and two light chains, forming variable and constant regions. IgG is a major effector of the humoral immune response by antigen binding via its variable chains and possesses effector functions mediated by its Fc region: activation of complement and binding to FcγR.2 IgG is known to react with external antigens. However, in cases of autoimmunity, autoreactive IgG may be present in the sera of the patients and recognize self-antigens.3 In many autoimmune diseases, autoantibodies play a significant role in the pathogenesis.4 However, they may only be a secondary consequence of tissue damage or simply bystanders of the etiological agent.5 For instance, in systemic sclerosis (SSc), one of the most severe autoimmune diseases, autoantibodies targeting nuclear proteins, such as topoisomerase-I, are important biomarkers. Although these autoantibodies may possess pathogenic potential, further research is needed to confirm their role in disease progression.6 It is essential to assess the function of autoreactive IgG in autoimmune diseases to enhance the comprehension of their pathophysiology and to optimize therapeutic interventions for patients. This requires the isolation of IgG from other serum proteins (such as complement, cytokines, medications, or pathogens) to eliminate any potential ambiguity regarding their effects while preserving their intrinsic properties. Additionally, it is essential to use a purification method with a high yield, because research settings often have a restricted supply of patient serum.

Large-scale industrial IgG purification methods are used for the preparation of intravenous IgG (IVIg). IVIg is an immunomodulatory agent containing polyclonal IgG with diverse specificities. IVIg is derived from the plasma of thousands to tens of thousands of donors, depending on the manufacturer.7 As a result, IVIg preparations contain a diverse range of IgG with various specificities, which reflect the immune repertoire of all donors.

IVIg is prepared from large volumes of plasma (>1000 L), which are initially frozen at −20 °C and then thawed at 2 °C for cryoprecipitation. Subsequently, a first ethanol precipitation step is employed, and this fractionation is combined with multiple purification steps, including other ethanol precipitation steps, ion-exchange (IEX) chromatography, solvent-detergent precipitation, and caprylic acid (CA) precipitation.8 The elaborate purification process for IVIg preparations typically involves a combination of 5–10 distinct steps, in addition to virus inactivation processes and galenic formulation. Since the introduction of Cohn’s fractionation method in the 1940s, multiple purification steps have been implemented to ensure optimal purity.9 In clinical settings, highly pure IgG preparations are necessary to guarantee the safety of the final drug product as the presence of other components may cause adverse effects. Moreover, consistent batch-to-batch composition is crucial to drug quality. These combinations of methods are well-adapted and validated for large-volume samples in the plasma industry. However, they are hardly applicable to a laboratory-scale setting, which often involves purifying less than a milliliter of sample. Moreover, there is inevitable loss of IgG at each purification step in IVIg production: yield (i.e., final quantity of IgG/initial quantity of IgG) remains relatively low, around 30–40%.8

Laboratory-scale IgG purification aims to maximize IgG preservation and to ensure the highest level of purity while starting from small volumes of serum. Certain techniques used in IVIg production can be employed independently to produce a sufficiently pure sample for research purposes, without the need for multiple purification steps, as the strict consistency or safety requirements are not identical with those in clinical settings. Additionally, other separation techniques could be employed.

Affinity chromatography using protein G is a popular method for laboratory-scale purification of IgG.10−12 Affinity chromatography is a separation technique based on the reversible interaction between a selected protein and its ligand covalently attached to a matrix.13 Bacterial proteins can be used for IgG affinity purification. Indeed, protein G is a surface protein initially isolated from Streptococcus sp., and it features binding sites for the Fc part of IgG.14 Natural protein G also possesses a binding site for albumin, but this site has been suppressed in recombinant protein G produced in Escherichia coli, which is currently employed for the purification of IgG. IgG can be effectively eluted from the protein G matrix at a low pH, which disrupts the interaction between the two. It is considered the gold standard for purifying antibodies because of its high selectivity.15 However, it is not used in the production of IVIg because protein G has limited binding capacities. A large amount of resin would be required to purify a single batch, and the high cost of the resin makes it an unreasonable option for large-scale purification.16 Despite this, protein G affinity chromatography is frequently employed in laboratory settings, and many user-friendly commercial kits are available without the need for specialized equipment, such as fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC). Despite its widespread use, affinity chromatography using protein G has some drawbacks. One of the primary concerns is that IgG is exposed to low pH during the process, which can result in partial denaturation of the protein and conformational changes leading to aggregation, loss of reactivity, and cross reactivity.17,18 This aggregation could alter its antigen-binding behavior and ultimately compromise the quality of purified IgG.

Alternative methods for IgG purification are possible, such as the use of commercial Melon Gel (Melon) Purification Kits. This patented technique does not involve harsh acidic elution conditions although the exact mechanism of action of Melon resin is not known. This is a pseudoaffinity chromatography technique. The Melon gel resin allegedly captures all serum proteins aside from IgG, which is isolated via negative selection in a single step.19 In this process, IgG is gathered in the flowthrough after the application of the sample to the resin. Thus, this technique does not involve an elution step and avoids the passage of IgG at an acidic pH.

Other techniques often involve the precipitation of IgG, other serum proteins, or both. IgG can be precipitated according to their solubility; an ammonium sulfate (AS) concentration of 50% can lead IgG to precipitate out of solution due to the “salting-out” effect of proteins.20 In solution, proteins interact with water molecules via polar residues to form hydrogen bonds. Protein solubility increases upon the addition of a low salt concentration (salting-in effect). However, when the salt concentration increases dramatically, these charged molecules compete with proteins to interact with water. The amount of accessible solvent used to solubilize proteins decreases. Hydrophobic residues of the proteins will react together and lead to protein precipitation without modification of the protein conformation.21 The main advantage of the AS precipitation is that it does not denature IgG, on the contrary, it stabilizes the protein conformation.22 The precipitated proteins can be isolated by centrifugation, and the residual salt in the reconstituted pellet can be easily removed by filtration.

This technique is widely used in combination with other techniques, such as CA precipitation, which is one of the commonly used methods during IVIg preparation. Under mildly acidic conditions, CA, a short-chain fatty acid, precipitates most serum proteins, especially albumin, with the exception of the Ig fraction and other macroglobulins.23,24 At low concentrations, CA stabilizes albumin and protects it from denaturation. However, an increase in the CA concentration leads to protein precipitation. CA binds to specific protein sites accessible to the solvent, inducing a conformational change and partial protein denaturation. This process unveils additional binding sites, and more CA molecules are incorporated into the protein in micelle form. As a result, the protein surface becomes highly hydrophobic, leading to protein–protein interactions, and formation of protein complexes, which precipitate out of solution.25 The precipitation of serum proteins by CA, followed by the precipitation of IgG by AS, is a technique employed in the industry for IgG purification for IVIg preparation. This technique can be employed at the laboratory-scale, as demonstrated in the literature. However, the starting volume of the serum is not mentioned. It has been reported that high-affinity IgG can bind CA and precipitate with the other serum proteins.26 This technique should not be employed for samples with low concentrations of IgG. Despite this, no specific limit on the IgG concentration has been established, and it could be used at the laboratory-scale.

Another method is IEX chromatography, which relies on the isoelectric point (pI) of IgG being higher than that of most other serum proteins.27 These properties allow for negative isolation of IgG in the neutral pH range. This technique is widely used in combination for the preparation of IVIg.28 IEX is a separation technique based on the amphoteric properties of proteins. Separation is based on the reversible interaction between a molecule and a resin charged oppositely. Diethylamino ethyl (DEAE) is an anion-exchange resin, charged positively, which will bind all molecules charged negatively. The DEAE resin can be used to purify IgG from serum based on the isoelectric point (pI) of IgG. The pI of a protein is the pH at which the net surface charge of the protein is equal to zero. If the pH is below the protein pI, then the net charge of the protein is positive. Inversely, if the pH is above the pI of a protein, it will carry a negative net charge.29 IgG has a pI of approximately 8, which is higher than that of the vast majority of serum proteins such as albumin, for instance (pI = 5). By maintaining a pH level below the pI of IgG, but still within a neutral range (e.g., pH 6.3), it is possible to prevent IgG from binding to the anion-exchange resin while allowing other serum proteins to do so.30 This approach enables the purification process to be performed under neutral pH conditions. To prevent column saturation, it is customary to perform an AS precipitation of IgG upstream.

Although these techniques offer benefits compared to protein G kits, such as milder pH conditions, faster processing times, and lower costs (except for Melon), the preservation of IgG quality after purification remains to be evaluated. Few studies have assessed the efficacy of various IgG purification techniques that are practical at a laboratory-scale.31−33 Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, no investigation has explored the preservation of autoantibody reactivity following polyclonal IgG purification.

The aim of this study was to compare different purification methods, including protein G as a reference (Figure 1). Several methods were selected: (i) protein G affinity chromatography using a commercial spin column kit (Spin-protG) and an FPLC (FPLC-protG), (ii) Melon gel (Melon), (iii) caprylic acid and ammonium sulfate (CAAS) precipitation, (iv) IEX using a DEAE resin following AS precipitation, and (v) AS precipitation alone. IgG from an SSc patient with anti-topoisomerase-I autoantibody (ATA) and from a healthy donor was purified using these different techniques. The following procedures were used to evaluate and compare the methods: (i) the concentrations of purified IgG were quantified to determine the yield; (ii) the purity of the samples was assessed through the use of SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and combination of protein separation by SDS-PAGE followed by in-gel digestion and liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis of digests from all bands, referred to as GeLC-MS/MS, was performed; (iii) the quality of IgG was examined through different characteristics, including a comparison of their avidity before and after purification on two specific antigenic targets (SARS-CoV-2 and topoisomerase-I), the preservation of their specific reactivity, and assessment of potential polyreactivity on other nuclear proteins.

Study design.

Experimental Procedures

Materials

ATA positive serum from an SSc patient was obtained at the Lille Hospital biobank, from the cohort FHU PRECISE (CPP 2019-A01083-54, CNIL Data Protection Officer 2018_82/DEC19-553, NCT04334031). Healthy serum was purchased from Etablissement Français du Sang from a unique donor.

All chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) unless otherwise indicated.

Antinuclear Antibodies (ANA) Detection

SSc patient serum and its purified samples were tested with an indirect immunofluorescence assay (IIF) on Hep-2 cells and identified using the BioPlex 2200 system (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA) at the immunology department of Lille University Hospital according to routine care practices.

Protein G Affinity Chromatography and Melon Gel

Detailed protocols for IgG purification by FPLC-protG, Spin-protG, and Melon are described in Supporting Material S1 as the protocols were carried out following the manufacturers’ instructions with no modifications. Purified samples from FPLC-protG and Spin-protG were buffer exchanged with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) using centrifugal filter units (Amicon Ultra 4 mL 100 kDa; Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany).

AS Precipitation

Serum (800 μL) was transferred to 2 mL tubes; an equal volume of a saturated AS solution (4.32 M AS in water) was added dropwise under rigorous mixing and was incubated at room temperature for 30 min with rigorous mixing. The samples were then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min. The supernatants were discarded, and the Ig-containing pellet was reconstituted in 300 μL of PBS. Buffer exchange with PBS was performed using 2 mL Zeba Spin Desalting Columns (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Caprylic Acid and Ammonium Sulfate Precipitation (CAAS1 and CAAS2)

IgG purification via CAAS was conducted following two different protocols (CAAS1 and CAAS2) that were adapted from Perosa et al.24 For each protocol, one volume of serum was diluted with four volumes of acetate buffer (60 mM, pH 4.0), and the pH was adjusted to 4.5 with 1 M Tris-HCl. For CAAS1, 800 μL of serum was utilized, while for the CAAS2 protocol, 7 mL of serum was applied. Subsequently, pure CA (25 μL per mL of diluted sample) was added dropwise under rigorous mixing. The samples were incubated at room temperature for 30 min under mixing, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 20 min. The supernatant was retrieved and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter (Millex-HA, Merck Milli-pore, Burlington, MA, USA), and the pH was adjusted to 7.4 with 1 M Tris-HCl. An Ig precipitation step using AS was then performed. For the CAAS1 protocol, AS precipitation of the supernatants was realized using the previously mentioned procedure. The purified samples were desalted utilizing Zeba Spin Desalting Columns (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the CAAS2 protocol, the supernatant was cooled to 4 °C, and solid AS was slowly added to a proportion of 0.277 g per mL of sample under rigorous stirring which was continued for 30 min at 4 °C. The sample was then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the Ig-containing pellet was resuspended in 3 mL of PBS before desalting and concentrating using centrifugal filter units (Amicon Ultra 4 mL 100 kDa; Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany).

AS Precipitation and DEAE Anion-Exchange Chromatography

Prior to anion-exchange chromatography, an IgG precipitation by AS was performed to reduce sample complexity and avoid saturation of the column. It was performed as described above except that the pellet was resuspended in 300 μL of start buffer (0.07 M sodium phosphate buffer, pH 6.3) instead of PBS. HiTrap Capto DEAE 1 mL columns (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) were utilized manually with a syringe. The column was washed with 1 mL of ultrapure water, subsequently equilibrated with 5 mL of start buffer, followed by 5 mL of elution buffer (0.07 M sodium phosphate buffer containing 1 M NaCl), and last with 15 mL of start buffer as per the manufacturer’s guidelines. The sample was then applied to the column, and the flowthrough was collected. Subsequently, the column was washed with 1 mL of start buffer, and the flow through containing IgG was gathered. The remaining serum proteins were retained by the column and eluted using 10 mL of elution buffer to regenerate the column. Buffer exchange with PBS and protein concentration was performed using centrifugal filter units (Amicon 0.5 mL 100 kDa; Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany).

Evaluation of IgG and IgG Subclasses Concentrations

IgG and IgG subclass concentrations were assessed by turbidimetry on the Optilite analyzer (Binding Site, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at the immunology department of Lille University Hospital. IgG purification yields were determined by comparing the quantity of IgG in each purified sample to the quantity of IgG in the same volume of unpurified serum. The mean yield of the various purification techniques was then compared to the mean yield of Spin-protG purified IgG, the reference laboratory-scale technique, using one-way ANOVA with GraphPad Prism (10.1.2).

SDS-PAGE

The volume equivalent to a quantity of 10 μg of purified IgG was transferred in 0.5 mL tubes for each sample. Laemmli 2X (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA) was added in proportions of 1:1 v:v, and the samples were vortexed. Then, dithiothreitol (DTT) 550 mM was added in proportions of 1:10 v:v. Proteins were then heated at 100 °C for 10 min. Denatured samples were loaded onto a 4–20% gradient polyacrylamide Tris-glycine gel (Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and size-based separation was performed by electrophoresis (135 V, 90 min), followed by Coomassie staining. Gel photography and densitometry (iBright; ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were used to determine the theoretical purity of the purified samples.

Band Excision and In-Gel Tryptic Digestion of Proteins

Band excision from the SDS-PAGE gel was performed on a clean glass plate with a scalpel. Each excised band was cut into 1–2 mm cubes and transferred to 96-well plates V bottom (Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany). Gel pieces were destained with 50% acetonitrile (ACN) and 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer (ABC) for 20 min three times, prior to dehydration in 100% ACN for 5 min. Proteins were reduced by incubating the gel bands in 15 mM DTT, 50 mM ABC at 46 °C for 45 min. Alkylation was performed with 55 mM iodoacetamide and 50 mM ABC for 45 min at 37 °C. The gel pieces were dehydrated in 50% ACN, 25 mM ABC for 10 min at room temperature three times, and then with 100% ACN for 5 min before dehydration at 37 °C for 10 min. Trypsin/Lys-C Mix, Mass Spec grade (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) aliquots were reconstituted in 50 mM ABC (20 ng/μL), and added to each well. The plates were incubated at 4 °C for 15 min before the supernatant was discarded, and 50 mM ABC was used to cover the gel pieces before overnight incubation at 37 °C. The resulting peptides were extracted with 100% ACN for 10 min, twice. The peptide extracts were dried in a vacuum concentrator, and the dry pellets were reconstituted in 10 μL of water and 0.1% formic acid before transfer in nanoHPLC (high-performance liquid chromatography) vials. The samples were stored at −20 °C until analysis by nanoLC-MS/MS.

nanoLC-MS/MS Analysis

A nanoflow HPLC instrument (U3000 RSLC, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was coupled online to a Q Exactive Plus (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using a nanoelectrospray ion source. Gel-band peptide extracts (1 μL) were loaded onto a preconcentration trap (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, Acclaim Pep-Map100 C18, 5 μm, 300 μm i.d. × 5 mm) using partial loop injection for 5 min at a flow rate of 10 μL.min–1 with buffer A (5% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid) and separated on a reversed-phase column (Acclaim PepMap100 C18, 3 μm, 75 mm i.d. × 500 mm) with a linear gradient of 5–50% buffer B (75% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid) at a flow rate of 250 nL.min–1 and temperature of 45 °C. The gradient length was 100 min. The column was washed with 99% buffer B for 10 min and then reconditioned with buffer A. The total time for the LC-MS/MS run was approximately 120 min.

LC-MS/MS Data Analysis

MaxQuant 2.4.3.0 was used to identify proteins in each gel fraction from the mass spectrometry raw data with a 1% FDR.34 iBAQ intensities from the MaxQuant analysis were uploaded in Perseus 1.5.6, proteins matching to the reverse database, proteins only identified by sites were filtered out, and keratins were manually removed.35 The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the data set identifier PXD050732.36

Avidity Assessment

Anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG and ATA avidity were assessed using commercial ELISA kits: anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG ELISA kit (Human SARS-CoV-2-Spike (trimer) IgG ELISA Kit; Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and ATA ELISA kit (ORG 512 Anti-Scl-70, Orgentec Diagnostika GmbH, Mainz, Germany). Both assays were carried out in accordance with the manufacturers’ guidelines but included an additional step of incubation with a chaotropic agent.37 For the anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG tests, the SSc patient’s serum was diluted 1:60,000 v:v, and the healthy subject's serum was diluted 1:5000 v:v to fit in the calibration range. For the ATA test, the SSc patient serum was diluted 1:1000 v:v with a diluent buffer. Each IgG-purified sample was diluted to the same IgG concentration as the corresponding serum for each test. Diluted samples were added in four replicates to the antigen-coated wells. After the incubation step, two of the four sample replicates were washed with either wash solution to serve as a reference or wash solution with urea, the chaotropic agent. Urea concentration was selected based on previous tests and was chosen to remove approximately 50% of the anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG or ATA in the different sera. For the anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG tests, 8 M urea for the healthy samples or 10 M urea for the SSc patient samples were added to the wash solution. For the anti-ATA test, 8 M urea was used. After a 10 min incubation, three wash steps were performed, followed by the rest of the procedure according to the manufacturer’s protocol (see Supporting Information S1 for detailed protocols). Averages of duplicates were used, and a calibration curve with a 4-parameter logistic regression was fit on the calibrator results. The concentration of anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG or ATA was determined for each condition (serum or purification methods and with/without urea) in U/mL. Results and Discussion

The standard method for laboratory-scale IgG purification involves the use of protein G columns, which is based on the ability of these proteins to bind the Fc portion and retain IgG, whereas the other methods rely on the physicochemical properties of IgG.38 The objective of this study was to compare these alternative methods and determine whether they could match or surpass the performance of protein G. To assess this hypothesis, the same serum from a healthy donor was purified five times per technique, and the serum from an SSc patient with ATA was purified once per technique, given the limited sample quantity.

Purification Yield Assessment

Purification yields (Figure 2) were evaluated using the healthy serum purified samples (n = 5, except for CAAS2, n = 1 because of limited sample volume). It was calculated according to the quantity of IgG measured by turbidimetry in each purified sample compared with the quantity in the same volume of unpurified serum.

Mean IgG purification yields values (±SD) obtained with the different purification methods tested, n = 5 purification replicates from the healthy serum for each technique except CAAS2, n = 1. The mean purified IgG yields were compared to the mean yield of Spin-protG purified IgG using a one-way ANOVA with *p < 0.05; ****p < 0.0001.

It is essential to achieve a high purification yield when working with small-volume samples (<1 mL) at the laboratory-scale. Using the protein G reference technique, which involves FPLC-protG (protein G FPLC columns) and Spin-protG (a commercial spin protein G column kit), IgG purification yields were approximately 50% of the initial amount of IgG. Significantly higher yields were obtained using other techniques, except for CAAS1. However, with the implementation of the CAAS2 protocol, improved results were observed when the starting volume of serum was increased from 800 μL to 7 mL, achieving a yield of 65% of the initial amount of IgG. The disparity may be attributed to the two pH adjustments required for the protocol, which may be less accurate for smaller samples than for larger ones in our experience. Indeed, it is important to ensure that specific parameters are carefully monitored during the purification process. These parameters include, but are not limited to, the temperature and pH of the samples and buffers, as well as the ionic strength of the buffers.

The CAAS2 protocol requires a significant amount of serum, making it difficult to perform replicates due to limitations in biological material. Despite its limitations in handling small-volume samples, the CAAS protocol emerges as a compelling alternative for larger samples (≥3 mL).

IgG subclasses present different biological functions (such as interaction with antigen, C1q complement fixation, or binding to mononuclear cells) and differences in primary structure that can modify the physicochemical properties of Ig.39 Because the majority of the purification methods tested in this study relied on the physicochemical properties of the molecules, it was necessary to determine whether all of the subclasses were purified. The assessment of IgG subclass quantities by turbidimetry was included in each sample except for CAAS1 purified samples due to limitations in detectability. The proportion of each subclass was maintained for each method, as shown in Figure S1.

Purification Efficiency Assessment

SDS-PAGE (Figure 3) was performed to assess the purity of each sample before each lane was cut into bands for parallel processing, and an LC-MS/MS analysis of each band composition was performed. SDS-PAGE allows for an intermediate level of purity control of the samples as well as quantification of purity based on the density of the purified Ig bands with a densitometry analysis (Figure S2).

SDS-PAGE analysis of the purified samples from healthy serum. Molecular mass of protein ladders is displayed in kDa (left lane) and the position of the bands selected for LC-MS/MS analysis (right). The same quantity of purified sample by the different techniques was deposited in each well.

As shown in Figure 3, two major bands were observed in each lane. The first band, around 52 kDa, corresponds to the heavy chains of IgG, whereas the second band, around 24 kDa, corresponds to the light chains of Ig.40 The protein gel electrophoresis results revealed a limited number of protein bands in the protein G and CAAS-purified IgG samples. The Melon-purified sample displayed a similar profile, except for the presence of a high-density (∼18%, see Figure S2) band at approximately 76 kDa. The DEAE- and AS-purified samples appeared to have more bands and impurities than those from the other techniques. To assess the complete composition of the samples after the purification procedures, GeLC-MS/MS analysis was performed. Gel lanes were cut into 16 similar slices prior to in-gel digestion, and the extracted peptides were subjected to nanoLC-MS/MS using a Q Exactive Plus Orbitrap.

The number of proteins in each purified sample was determined by combining all identified proteins in each band for each lane (Figure 4A). For protein G (FPLC-protG and Spin-protG), as well as CAAS2 techniques, approximately 150 proteins were identified in each lane. A larger number of proteins were identified using Melon (201), DEAE (237), and CAAS1 (280), and the number of proteins identified in AS was greater (440) than those identified through the other techniques. AS precipitation is a method that relies on the solubility of IgG. The addition of a high concentration of salt causes the precipitation of IgG. However, it should be noted that other serum proteins may become trapped or coprecipitated alongside IgG during this process.21 AS precipitation is recognized as an initial step in the purification process for IgG, which enables a significant reduction in sample volume and matrix complexity, but is typically combined with other methods to achieve optimal results. The Ig-related proteins (identified proteins attributable to Ig: heavy constant chains, heavy variable domains, light constant chains, and light variable domains; see Table S1 for a comprehensive list) represented roughly half of all identified proteins for the protein G methods and CAAS2 (Figure 4B). They represented around 30% for Melon, DEAE, and CAAS1. AS had the lowest percentage of Ig-related proteins (16%).

(A) Number of identified proteins by GeLC-MS/MS in purified samples for each technique; (B) Percentage of Ig-related proteins among all identified proteins in purified samples (list of Ig-related proteins, comprising Ig heavy constant chains, heavy variable domains, light constant chains, and light variable domains is presented in Table S1).

Regarding relative quantity, Ig was the most abundant protein in each sample, as evidenced by its high iBAQ (intensity-based absolute quantitation) values, as shown in Figure 5A. The iBAQ algorithm can estimate the relative abundance of the proteins within each sample. The sum of peak intensities of all peptides matching to a specific protein is divided by the corresponding number of theoretically observable peptides.41 IgG heavy chains (from IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, and IgG4) were the most abundant proteins, with iBAQ values in the 109 range, along with Ig light chains (Figure S3A,B). Other isotypes of Ig were detected in each purified sample, including IgD and IgE, which were present in small amounts compared to IgG (104–106 iBAQ values). However, the presence of IgA heavy chains was more notable in techniques that did not involve protein G. For protein G, the maximum iBAQ values for IgA1 were 107, while for Melon and CAAS2, the values were around 108, and for CAAS1 and AS, the values were around 109. The abundance of IgM heavy chains was more important in the DEAE-, CAAS2-, and AS-purified samples with iBAQ values falling within the 108 range. IgM was detected with 107 iBAQ values in FPLC-protG, 106 for Spin-protG and CAAS1 and 105 with Melon (Figure S3C).

Profile plots of protein iBAQ values in each band for every purified sample analyzed by LC-MS/MS with the techniques used (lanes) and the numbers corresponding to the band analyzed with (A) all Ig-related proteins highlighted (green); (B) other high abundance serum proteins present in the samples: albumin (pink), serotransferrin (blue), hemopexin (black), and α2 macroglobulin (red).

The iBAQ profiles of all of the proteins identified in each sample were comparable. While a larger number of proteins were identified in purified samples through methods other than protein G, the iBAQ values of the other proteins detected were generally lower than those of IgG, with a few exceptions.

Although albumin was present in each purified sample in the 106 range, it was more prevalent in the Melon-, DEAE-, and AS-purified samples (109 range), as shown in Figure 5B. The same trend was observed for α2 macroglobulin, which was more prevalent in the DEAE- and AS-purified samples with 108 iBAQ values, whereas for the other techniques, it was approximately 105 (106 for CAAS2). Hemopexin was found to be more prevalent in the Melon sample, with a 108 iBAQ value, while in the DEAE- and AS-purified samples, it was less abundant (107). This protein was present in FPLC-protG and CAAS1 samples at low iBAQ values (105) but was not detected in Spin-protG and CAAS2 samples.

The band with high density in the Melon sample at approximately 76 kDa (band no. 7) corresponds to serotransferrin. This protein was detected in every sample, in small amounts for CAAS2 (105 iBAQ value), protein G methods and CAAS1 (106), and was more abundant in the DEAE- and AS-purified samples which had iBAQ values in the 108 range. However, serotransferrin was the second most abundant protein in the Melon-purified sample (109 iBAQ value). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, transferrin can only be detected in purified IgG samples from rat and mouse. Based on these results, we can conclude that this protein can also be detected in purified IgG from human samples. For troubleshooting, the manufacturer suggests conducting AS precipitation prior to the purification procedure. Although this process was not tested in this study, serotransferrin was less prevalent in the AS-purified sample than in the Melon-purified sample. Therefore, performing AS precipitation prior to the Melon procedure may be sufficient to obtain pure IgG. However, it should be noted that an additional step would decrease the yield of the purified IgG.

The band around 24 kDa (band no. 13) is believed to be composed solely of Ig light chains, which is the case for all techniques, except AS. With the other techniques, approximately 70 proteins were identified in this band, the vast majority of which were Ig light chains (constant and variable domains). However, in the AS-purified sample, 312 proteins were identified in the band no. 13, and only 16% were Ig-related proteins. With the AS procedure, several other proteins, including apolipoproteins, 14-3-3 proteins, complement proteins, serpins, or S100 proteins (to cite only a few), have been identified in this band. The full protein composition of each band is available in Tables S3 and S4. However, even if a large number of proteins were identified, 256 proteins were present at low iBAQ values (in the 104–106 range) compared with the Ig light chains identified (108–109 iBAQ).

As mentioned above, apart from Ig, the only proteins that were abundant in the purified samples from all techniques were serotransferrin, albumin, α2 macroglobulin, and hemopexin. Complement C3, α1 antitrypsin, and apolipoprotein A1 were more abundant in AS- and DEAE-purified samples but were not clearly displayed in the iBAQ profile plots. The other proteins were present in relatively small amounts. The assessment of the purity of a purified IgG sample typically involves densitometric analysis using SDS-PAGE. This is performed by determining the fraction of the total area and the intensity of the IgG light and heavy chain bands at 25 and 50 kDa. The LC-MS/MS analysis of the mentioned bands revealed that these bands not only comprised Ig proteins but also contained a certain number of other serum proteins (see Tables S3 and S4 for the list of all proteins identified in each band), particularly for the Ig light chain band for the AS-purified sample. Consequently, the visual assessment of the purity by SDS-PAGE may not be accurate for all samples.

The objective of the GeLC-MS/MS analysis was to comprehensively assess the composition of the purified IgG samples and to consider proteins at very low concentrations, over a conventional bottom-up LC-MS/MS analysis. The latter was also performed and only a few proteins of low molecular weight were identified, which changed the sample composition. The number of identified proteins was significantly reduced, especially in the AS-purified IgG samples (from 440 with GeLC-MS/MS to approximately 160 with LC-MS/MS). However, the number of Ig-related proteins remained roughly the same (Figure S4). Therefore, the compositions of all samples were similar between all techniques when analyzed by LC-MS/MS (see Table S5).

Although more bands were visually present for techniques other than protein G, IgG was the most abundant protein in each sample, as confirmed by the LC-MS/MS analysis of all samples.

It should be noted that to maintain Ig conformation stability, it is preferable to concentrate them and they can be stored in PBS with carrier proteins such as bovine serum albumin.42 The presence of other serum proteins in IgG-purified samples is not an issue in most applications.43 However, if IgG is involved in reactions with amine groups, such as the covalent attachment of fluorochromes to IgG in immunofluorescence assays, the presence of albumin and other high-intensity proteins may be problematic.44 In such cases, it is important to consider the potential interference of these proteins. Achieving a pure sample containing solely IgG is hardly achievable, and in the laboratory setting, it is crucial to maintain the quality of IgG in order to study its functions.

Preservation of Specific Antigen–Antibody Interaction After Purification

One of the fundamental functions of IgG is to interact with antigens to induce an immune response. The strength of this interaction, characterized by the affinity and avidity of IgG for its target, can play an important role in the development of some diseases.45 Highly purified human IgG is commonly used in preclinical research for several applications such as in vivo animal immunization.46 However, only a few studies have investigated the effect of purification on the affinity of IgG for its target.31 Several IgG purification techniques, including protein G, involve adjusting the sample to a low pH during the purification process. This may result in the degradation of Ig or affect antigen recognition. In this experiment, we sought to evaluate the avidity of anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG, and ATA in the serum and following purification using modified commercial ELISA kits (Figure 6).

IgG avidity assessment for two specific antibodies, expressed as a percentage of relative avidity (quantity of specific IgG with/without chaotropic agent, determined by ELISA in each purified sample and sera): (A) Anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG avidity from the healthy subject; (B) Anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG from the SSc patient; and (C) ATA avidity from the SSc patient.

For the healthy subject serum, anti-SARS-CoV-2 reactivity was tested with IgG-purified samples from five independent purification experiments for each method (except CAAS2, n = 1). For the SSc patient, anti-SARS-CoV-2 reactivity and ATA reactivity were assessed with one sample per purification technique. Equal amounts of IgG, as compared to the unpurified sera, were deposited in each well, and wash buffer or wash buffer containing urea (a chaotropic agent) was added to the corresponding wells to determine the avidity percentage. Independently of the purification method, IgG anti-SARS-CoV-2 were detected in every sample, and no drastic changes in IgG avidity were observed compared with the unpurified corresponding serum (Figure 6A,B). A Kruskal–Wallis test, conducted on the SARS-CoV-2 avidity results of the purified samples from the healthy serum, showed significant differences between methods (p = 0.02). Subsequent application of Dunn’s multiple comparison tests did not reveal any appreciable distinction between the levels of IgG avidity of the purified samples obtained via each technique and those found in the unpurified serum. The only discernible difference emerged upon comparing the CAAS1 group with the Spin-protG (p = 0.005) and DEAE (p = 0.02) groups. These findings are not considered to be reflective of a substantial avidity change.

To confirm the preservation of specific IgG avidity, it was tested on ATA specificity (Figure 6C). When purified samples were diluted to the same IgG concentration as the serum sample, ATA were detected. Similar to the case for anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG, no substantial changes in avidity were observed with any purification technique used.

Furthermore, it has been previously shown that exposure of IgG to low pH, even for a brief period, can enhance their binding polyreactivity.47,48 To ensure that the purified autoantibodies still recognized only their target, purified IgG samples from the SSc patient were tested using a widely used routine diagnostic test for antinuclear autoantibodies (ANA) detection by indirect immunofluorescence (Figure S5). No polyreactivity was observed for ANA and the fluorescence pattern remained the same (TOPO-I-like pattern, AC-29).49 This indicates that the purification process was not associated with reactivity toward other targets.

Conclusion

The purification methods for IgG studied here demonstrated several key advantages, making it possible to limit the use of costly protein G spin columns for laboratory-scale purification of IgG. Indeed, better yields were obtained while preserving the quality of IgG with techniques other than protein G: antigen specificities were preserved in the purified samples, no changes in avidity were observed, and no polyreactivity of purified IgG was induced. The significant difference between the techniques utilized lies in the purity of the IgG sample, following purification. The protein G methods yielded the purest samples along with CAAS2. The purity of the samples obtained using the Melon kit was not as optimal as anticipated, and considering its price, alternative methods could be used instead (see Table S2 for a summary of the characteristics of the various techniques). When selecting a purification method, it is important to take into account the intended application of the purified IgG, as it is not possible to obtain a sample that solely consists of IgG. As expected, the impurities detected in each sample were major serum proteins, but they were present at lower intensities than IgG. These proteins were more abundant in samples purified by techniques other than protein G. The presence of certain proteins, such as complement proteins, which could have a biological effect in in vitro experiments, should be considered when designing the experiment. However, if the aim is solely to standardize IgG conditions, due to factors such as serum viscosity or hemolytic serum, only partially purified IgG is required, then a straightforward AS precipitation may suffice. In addition, among the methods presented here, AS is the most cost-effective and time-efficient and is not constrained by a specific volume. For larger sample sizes, we would recommend opting for the CAAS2 technique over protein G spin kits. Melon presents several advantages, including a short manipulation time, high yield, and mild pH conditions. Furthermore, there is no need to adjust the buffer to PBS. However, the costs of Melon kits and their limited capacity to handle large volumes of serum in a single batch constitute two major drawbacks. DEAE-purified IgG exhibits a better level of purity than AS-purified IgG, and this technique is more cost-effective than Melon gel and is suitable for small-volume samples, unlike CAAS. However, the yield and purity of DEAE-purified IgG were not optimal. The use of FPLC could significantly enhance these parameters. In this study, manual operation of the DEAE columns using a syringe was employed, as this scenario is commonly encountered in laboratories where specialized equipment is not readily available. Despite its advantages, the high cost of an FPLC makes it an impractical option for many. Protein G spin columns remain an accessible and user-friendly method commonly used in the literature. Methods relying on the physicochemical properties of IgG offer a choice of alternatives, which could also be applied to purify IgG from other species.
