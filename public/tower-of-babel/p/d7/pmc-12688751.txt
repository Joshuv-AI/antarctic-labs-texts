The interplay of the microbiome and breast cancer: beyond the gut: a narrative review
Authors: Pranjal Kumar Singh, Diya Rathi, Mohammedsadeq A. Shweliya, Ayesha Farooq, Zainab Anfaal, Noor ul Ain Saleem, Muhammad Hamza, Maria Qadri, Shree Rath, Mohamed Fawzi Hemida, Hina Rani, Abdulhadi M.A. Mahgoub, Hakim Ullah Wazir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Breast cancer remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among women worldwide, with emerging evidence underscoring the microbiota’s pivotal role in its etiology, progression, and therapeutic response. This narrative review synthesizes the intricate interplay between the breast tissue, skin, and lung microbiomes in breast cancer pathogenesis, with particular emphasis on inflammatory breast cancer (IBC) and metastatic dissemination. The healthy breast microbiome, dominated by Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidetes, maintains tissue homeostasis through pH regulation, metabolite production, and immune modulation. Dysbiosis disrupts this equilibrium, fostering carcinogenesis via chronic inflammation, estrogen deconjugation, and DNA damage-induced genomic instability, with subtype-specific microbial signatures influencing tumor growth and therapy resistance. In IBC, skin microbiome alterations characterized by overgrowth of pathogens like Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus exacerbate inflammation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and lymphatic invasion, while promoting a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment enriched in regulatory T cells and M2 macrophages. Concurrently, lung microbiota dysbiosis impairs immune surveillance, remodels the extracellular matrix, and facilitates metastatic seeding through neutrophil extracellular traps and cytokine storms. Cross-talk among these microbiomes amplifies systemic effects, highlighting their synergistic contributions to disease aggressiveness. Advanced analytical techniques, including 16S rRNA sequencing, metagenomics, and metabolomics, offer promising microbial biomarkers for early detection and risk stratification. By elucidating these host-microbe dynamics, this review advocates for microbiome-centric interventionssuch as probiotics, fecal microbiota transplantation, and targeted antimicrobials to enhance precision diagnostics and therapies, ultimately improving outcomes in breast cancer management.

Introduction

Inflammatory breast cancer (IBC) is a rare form of locally advanced breast cancer, clinically presenting as diffuse induration of the skin with an erysipeloid appearance, typically without a visible underlying mass. The most recent American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) guidelines classify this condition as a distinct clinicopathologic entity with erythema and Edema involving at least one-third of the breast, can extend to the whole breast and the other breast involving the mediastinum, upper extremities and neck area[1]. About 20–30% of patients with IBC, a rare and extremely aggressive subtype of breast cancer, present with de novo distant metastases, and their 5-year overall survival rate is just 40–60%. It accounts for 2–4% of breast cancer in the United States. Although not frequent, IBC accounts for 7% of breast cancer-related deaths[1,2]. A collection of microbiotas, including bacteria, fungi, and viruses, colonize the skin and help to defend the skin against invading pathogens. Human skin microbiome is varied and influenced by factors including age, body site, and gender. Different skin sites prefer to select different bacteria to accommodate the respective microenvironments like temperature, secretion of sebum, and amount of sweat, at the occupied niche on the body site[3] .These commensals are involved in the regulation of host health maintenance by limiting invasion by pathogenic microbes, initiating host immune processes, and maintaining adequate defense against possible disruption of the skin barrier by pathogenic microorganisms[3] Commensals can modulate immunological responses by stimulating host pattern recognition receptors like nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptors and Toll-like receptors (TLRs). Immune tolerance to commensals and activation against pathogenic intruders is governed by such mechanisms. For example, Staphylococcus epidermidis can stimulate the production of Antimicrobial Peptides (AMP) by inducing TLR2 stimulation on keratinocytes and enhancing regulatory T-cell (Treg) function, thus reducing excessive inflammation[4]. Another role of skin microbiome in maintaining the health of an individual includes wound healing. It can heal wounds by regulating regeneration and innate immune response. Repeated exposure of symbionts under a constant condition induces microbiota-specific T-cell responses without provoking inflammation. These responses are crucial for antimicrobial defense and regeneration of damaged skin tissue[5].

This review aims to investigate the potential role of the skin microbiome in the pathogenesis or progression of IBC and to propose the skin microbiome as a potential biomarker or therapeutic target for diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment modulation in patients with IBC. While IBC was addressed in terms of cancer biology, and the skin microbiome was investigated in dermatological contexts, this review tries to connect oncology and microbiome science – an underexplored intersection.

This review assesses if there a distinct skin microbiome signature in patients with IBC compared to healthy individuals or those with non-IBC and if modulation of the skin microbiota (e.g., using probiotics or microbial therapy) reduce inflammation or improve outcomes in patients with IBC.

Methodology

The purpose of this narrative review was to investigate the complex association between the microbiome and breast cancer, with a focus on processes that go beyond the conventional gut-focused paradigm. Five major electronic databases – PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science and Google Scholar – were searched extensively for relevant material. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keyword-based searches, such as “breast cancer,” “microbiota,” “microbiome,” “dysbiosis,” “carcinogenesis,” and “pathogenic bacteria,” were used in conjunction with the technique. To improve search results, Boolean operators and filters were used. These were used to incorporate research on immunological regulation, hormone metabolism, and the function of microbial communities outside of the gastrointestinal tract.

Study selection criteria

To guarantee thematic relevance and recentness, peer-reviewed studies published in the last decade were given priority; however, foundational papers from previous decades were also included. The inclusion of studies that addressed the relationship between the onset, progression, or responsiveness to treatment of breast cancer and microbial composition, immune response, dysbiosis, or microbial–host interactions was given consideration. To connect the gap between mechanistic understanding and possible translational uses, articles addressing both preclinical and clinical evidence were selected. Research on new treatment approaches, such as microbiome-modulating techniques or biomarkers informed by microbiota, was also given priority to offer prospective viewpoints.

After selection, a framework for narrative synthesis was used to qualitatively combine findings from various study types. The extracted results were arranged chronologically and thematically (e.g., hormone–microbiota interaction, inflammation-mediated carcinogenesis, and gut–breast microbial axis) to show changing research trends. The main areas of focus were microbial diversity mapping, dysbiotic profile identification, potential roles of these microbial ecosystems in oncogenesis, tumor microenvironment regulation, and treatment resistance in breast cancer.

The breast tissue microbiome

Numerous bacterial species make up the fragile ecosystem known as the healthy breast microbiome, which supports the general well-being of the mammary gland. Other noteworthy phyla that are present are Actinobacteria and Bacteroidetes in addition to Firmicutes and Proteobacteria. Each of these phyla has distinct characteristics and roles in breast tissue[6].

Firmicutes: Mostly belonging to the Lactobacillaceae family, these bacteria are well-known for their probiotic qualities. Lactobacillus species are essential for preserving the pH’s acidity and preventing the growth of harmful bacteria by producing lactic acid.

Proteobacteria: The complex phylum Proteobacteria includes families like Acetobacteraceae that are engaged in a few metabolic processes. For instance, certain species of Acetobacter can contribute to the local metabolic environment by converting carbohydrates into acetic acid through metabolism.

Actinobacteria: These bacteria, which are represented by genera like Propionibacterium, break down complex lipids and produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which have anti-inflammatory qualities.

Bacteroidetes: Prevotella, one of the genera in the phylum Bacteroidetes that is involved in the breakdown of complex polysaccharides and the generation of advantageous metabolites. Hence, Bacteroidetes also contribute to the breast tissue microbiome[6,7].

Dysbiosis in the breast cancer

The imbalance in the microbial community is known as dysbiosis, and it can contribute to the development of breast cancer. According to studies, the microbial composition of breast cancer tissues is different from that of healthy tissues. The number of potentially dangerous bacteria is rising while that of beneficial bacteria is decreasing. One genus in the Proteobacteria phylum, Ralstonia, has been discovered to be more prevalent in breast cancer tissues. Due to this imbalance, the local immune milieu may alter, potentially promoting inflammation and facilitating tumor growth[8].

Mechanism of development of breast cancer and role of breast tissue microbiome

The breast microbiome can have a few effects on the development of breast cancer. Inflammation is one of the primary causes. Certain bacteria produce inflammatory cytokines and other mediators, which promote a pro-inflammatory environment and contribute to the development of cancer. Another method is hormone regulation[7,9]. The microbiota can affect how hormones, particularly estrogen, are metabolized. Bacterial enzymes such as beta-glucuronidase may deconjugate estrogens, increasing their bioavailability and perhaps playing a role in estrogen-driven breast cancer. Additionally, certain bacteria produce genotoxins or reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can damage the DNA of host cells. This DNA damage can lead to two critical phases in the genesis of cancer first one being mutations and other one is genomic instability[10].

Role of the breast microbiome in different breast cancer subtypes

The breast microbiome has a major impact on the onset and course of several breast cancer subtypes. Research has connected distinct microbial profiles to a number of breast cancer subtypes, such as Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2-enriched, and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Certain bacteria, for instance, have the ability to change hormone levels and promote inflammation, which may have an effect on tumor growth and the effectiveness of treatment. The immune system and tumor microenvironment are also impacted by the microbiome, which may have an impact on how cancer progresses and how effective a treatment is[9,11].

Influence of the breast microbiome on response to local therapies

The breast microbiome has a significant impact on how the body reacts to local therapies like radiation and surgery. There are several ways in which this interaction influences the tumor microenvironment and treatment outcomes. Furthermore, the microbiome plays a crucial role in wound healing and the risk of postsurgical infections. The microbial makeup of breast tissue affects the healing process following surgery. While an imbalance or presence of pathogenic bacteria can result in difficulties and a delayed recovery, beneficial bacteria can encourage appropriate wound healing and lower the risk of infection. Improving surgical results and customizing postoperative care require an understanding of these dynamics[12].

Furthermore, research indicates that the breast microbiome may have an impact on the development of radioresistance in breast cancer cells. Radioresistance is a disease that occurs when cancer cells become less sensitive to radiation, which lowers the efficacy of treatment. Some bacterial species have the ability to create metabolites and enzymes that shield cancer cells from the harmful effects of radiation, which reduces tumor shrinkage and leads to worse outcomes[13].

The skin microbiome and IBC

Unique features of the skin microbiome in IBC

IBC is a rare and severe type of invasive breast cancer that affects 1–5% of newly diagnosed breast cancer cases in the United States. IBC starts with an inflammatory response in the breast tissue, which can be triggered by factors such as infections. Breast erythema, edema, and nipple alterations are characteristic symptoms of IBC. IBC is diagnosed through clinical evaluation and tissue biopsy, followed by chemotherapy, surgery, and radiotherapy for treatment[14]. According to recent research, the pathophysiology of IBC may include the skin microbiota.

The skin microbiome, comprised of various bacteria such as S. epidermidis, Cutibacterium acnes, and Corynebacterium, preserves skin health by supplying nourishment, warding off infections, boosting immunity, and regulating epidermal differentiation[15]. A healthy skin microbial balance termed as eubiosis, is responsible for maintaining the integrity of the skin barrier, whereas dysbiosis, marked by excessive growth of certain bacterial species and reduced community diversity, can lead to compromised wound healing, increased inflammation, and increased microbial infection risk[16].

A study reported a correlation between the occurrence of IBC and the presence of harmful bacteria in the skin, such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, and certain Gram-negative anaerobes[17]. The probable leading phenomena behind this are oncogenic and pro-inflammatory processes initiated by these microbes. S. aureus, for instance, produces exotoxins and superantigens that can result in aberrant immune activation, a chronic inflammatory state, and the formation of tumors. Additionally, the over-representation of P. aeruginosa has been linked to biofilm development, which may contribute to immune resistance and opposition to conventional therapies[18]. The continuous inflammation caused by these dangerous pathogens promotes the malignant transformation of cells. Inflammatory processes also seem to activate oncogenes and simultaneously block tumor suppressor genes, which lead to the production of ROS and reactive nitrogen species. This damages DNA and raises the risk of genetic alterations that lead to the development of cancer[19].

Inflammation caused by these microbiomes triggers the cancer cells to take on mesenchymal characteristics through an epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). This transition increases their propensity for invasion and motility. This process is critical for tumor metastasis and contributes to the aggressive nature of IBC. Chronic inflammation also alters the tumor microenvironment by promoting angiogenesis and creating a niche that facilitates the growth of tumors. IBC’s aggressive character is reinforced by the enhancement of cancer stem cells (CSCs) triggered by inflammation. CSCs are closely associated with the development and reappearance of tumors. Collectively, these mechanisms show how the skin microbiome-induced inflammation plays a major role in IBC development and progression[19,20].

Pathogenic microbes accelerate tumor growth by activating TLRs, triggering NF-κB signaling and increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β. Gram-negative bacteria further contribute to the persistence of this tumor-promoting inflammatory milieu by releasing lipopolysaccharides (LPS)[21].

The immunological environment in IBC differs from that of other subtypes of breast cancer because tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and Tregs are more prevalent in IBC than in other breast cancer subtypes[22]. Superantigens from S. aureus, for instance, have been demonstrated to increase Tregs and reduce antitumor immune responses. Similarly, macrophage polarization can be influenced by microbially generated SCFAs, which favor an M2 phenotype that is pro-tumorigenic[23].

The distinct characteristics of the skin microbiota in IBC underscore its possible involvement in the etiology and progression of the condition. Evidence indicates that microbial dysbiosis has a role in the inflammatory and immune-modulatory landscape of IBC, even if direct causal linkages have not yet been thoroughly established. Future research should focus on longitudinal microbiome profiling in patients with IBC, mechanistic studies exploring host–microbe interactions, and the development of microbiome-targeted interventions to improve patient outcomes.

The role of skin microbiome in chronic inflammation

The skin microbiome is a complex community of microorganisms that is essential for maintaining skin homeostasis and modulating immune responses. In healthy individuals, commensal bacteria such as S. epidermidis and C. acnes dominate the skin microbiota, contributing to barrier integrity and immune regulation. These microbes produce antimicrobial peptides and interact with host cells to suppress excessive inflammation, highlighting their protective role[3,24,25]. However, debates persist regarding the precise mechanisms by which specific microbial strains influence immune signaling, particularly in the context of systemic diseases.

Disruptions in microbial equilibrium which is termed dysbiosis can lead to immune dysregulation and chronic inflammation, exacerbating conditions such as atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, and rosacea. A key gap in current research lies in distinguishing whether dysbiosis is a cause or consequence of these diseases. For instance, while reduced microbial diversity is consistently observed in inflammatory skin disorders, it remains unclear whether this shift initiates disease or results from underlying immune dysfunction[25–27]. Additionally, dysbiosis-associated impairments in lipid metabolism and barrier function may create a vicious cycle of inflammation, further compromising skin health[25,26].

Emerging evidence suggests that chronic skin inflammation, driven by dysbiosis, may extend beyond localized effects, potentially influencing systemic conditions such as breast cancer. One hypothesis posits that persistent inflammatory signaling from the skin could promote a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment. Supporting this, studies have found associations between oral microbiome dysbiosis (e.g., periodontal pathogens) and increased breast cancer risk, raising questions about whether similar mechanisms apply to the skin microbiome[24,27–29]. However, direct causal evidence remains limited, highlighting a critical research gap.

Furthermore, the skin microbiome may modulate treatment responses in patients with breast cancer. For example, reduced levels of commensal bacteria prior to radiotherapy correlate with higher severity of radiodermatitis, suggesting a protective role for a balanced microbiome against treatment-induced inflammation[26,28]. This observation opens avenues for hypothesis-driven research – could probiotic interventions mitigate cancer therapy side effects by restoring microbial balance?

Potential biomarkers in the skin microbiome for early detection and risk stratification

Identification and validation of microbial biomarkers

Techniques for microbiome analysis

Various techniques are currently available for microbiome analysis, and the selection largely depends on the specific research question. Several of these methods are gaining popularity in contemporary studies. For example, while 16S rRNA sequencing is helpful for specific species identification and the presence of bacteria, it provides no clue about its functionality. On the other hand, metagenomics and meta-transcriptomics are used to predict microbial functions or comprehend activity in specific metabolic pathways, respectively. Metagenomics (aka whole-genome sequencing) can sequence the whole genome from DNA, avoiding targeting a specific gene, leading to wide application in exploring functional potentials. Meta-transcriptomics identify active genes by sequencing RNA and converting it to its complimentary DNA[30,31].

Culture microbiome and studying their characteristic is a well-known method. Although traditional, it is widely used in probiotic research. Proteomics metabolomics uses mass spectrometry to study host–microbe interactions[32,33]. Metabolomics helps link disease states with microbial function, e.g. using metabolomics to classify responses to immunotherapy into multiple subtypes, with each having a different prognostic value. One limitation of this technique is its dependence on microbial metabolites, requiring fast sample processing before metabolite degradation[31].

Bioinformatics techniques are an application of machine learning in microbiome analysis. In addition to processing sequencing, it identifies species and microbial functions and predicts interaction and network building between microbial species. The application of machine learning and cloud computing by many models leads to a promising technique soon[34]. Promising microbial biomarkers for IBC

Microbial biomarkers are emerging as a promising environmental factor that could affect the development of breast tissue. More than 20 clinical trials studied the association between microbiome and skin cancer. Still, both healthy control and diseased groups showed comparable or contracting growth results regarding several species, including Fusobacterium and Lachnospira[35].

Urbaniak et al reported an abundance of Staphylococcus in breast cancer tissue. Further investigation is needed to report which characteristic is involved in Staphylococcus pathogenesis. Furthermore, a strong association was found between S. aureus and squamous cell carcinoma[36,37]. So, we need to investigate such associations regarding IBC. However, there is still no established evidence that confirms the association between skin biomarkers and IBC in an unbiased way as there are confounders and discrepancies. Gao et al introduced “xMarkerFinder,” a computational framework that can help in biomarkers finding and validation by combining machine learning and meta-analyses across cohort datasets[38]. Although this model was primarily developed for the skin microbiome, it can be adjusted for use in IBC. NetMoss is another algorithm presented by Xiao et al which is also used for the same purpose[35].

Clinical utility of skin microbiome biomarkers

Early detection of IBC

The skin microbiome is a promising biomarker for early detection of IBC. Complementing such biomarkers with the already used methods for diagnosis could enhance early detection by providing a noninvasive and cost-effective measure of screening. Hieken et al confirmed the presence of differences in the microbiome between benign and malignant breast tissue, which opened the door for further investigation into its role in carcinogenesis and its prevention[39].

Risk stratification and personalized medicine

Beyond enabling earlier identification of at-risk individuals, abnormal microbiota may also serve as an independent risk factor for IBC, urging more focused preventive strategies and frequent screening among individuals with a higher abundance of dysbiotic. Furthermore, Gopalakrishnan et al showed a different response to immunotherapy and related it to the gut microbiome[40]. A similar mechanism could be applied to the skin microbiome.

The lung microbiome and breast cancer metastasis

Lung as a common site for breast cancer metastasis

Breast cancer is known for its high malignancy, poor prognosis, and strong tendency to metastasize distantly, particularly to the lungs. Lung metastases occur in approximately 21–32% of breast cancer cases, with a poor prognosis and median survival of about 25 months[41]. Basal-like breast cancer is especially prone to spreading to the lungs, where the median survival drops to only 22 months[42]. Furthermore, autopsy findings reveal that 60–70% of patients with metastatic breast cancer had lung metastases at the time of death.

The most associated subtypes of invasive ductal breast cancer with lung metastases include basal-like, luminal B, and triple-negative p53-negative. These patterns are influenced by the biological evolution of these subtypes, as well as their treatment responses, morbidity, and mortality rates[43]. Lung metastasis is particularly frequent in severe TNBC. Supporting this, TNBC cells have shown higher migration towards lung-conditioned media in vitro and a preference for lung metastasis in vivo[44].

Metastases remain the primary cause of cancer-related mortality. The lung, being one of the primary targets for solid tumor metastasis, undergoes complex changes that facilitate this process. Bone marrow-derived neutrophils are integral to tumor cell colonization in the lung immune microenvironment[45]. There is a dynamic interplay among lung stromal cells, immune cells, bone marrow-derived cells, and tumor microenvironmental components. These interactions collectively reshape the lung into a niche favorable for metastatic colonization[46].

TNBC, being more aggressive, is strongly influenced by its microenvironment which includes both cellular and acellular components[47]. Additionally, the lung’s resident microbiome can modulate immune responses that either inhibit or facilitate tumor progression[48]. Primary breast tumors release exosomes rich in proteins and microRNAs that prime lung endothelial cells for metastatic seeding[49].

Among the molecular players, ZNF8 – a Smad3-interacting protein – has been identified as a key factor in driving breast cancer lung metastasis through epigenetic regulation mechanisms[50].

A thorough understanding of the molecular and microenvironmental dynamics underlying lung metastasis in breast cancer is crucial. Subtypes of breast cancer and their lung metastatic patterns

Mechanism of lung microbiome involvement in metastasis

The lung: a common site of breast cancer metastasis

Breast cancer can spread to distant sites: One of the most frequent locations for breast cancer to spread is the lungs[51]. In a study, 60–70% of metastatic patients with breast cancer who died were found to have lung metastases[52]. Tumor size, histologic grade, nodal stage, and receptor status are typical risk factors that impact the metastasis processes of breast cancer[53,54]. The molecular type of breast cancer determines the likelihood of distant metastases. According to Yhim et al’s analysis of lung metastasis survival rates, TNBC and HER2+ tumors had the poorest prognosis, whereas hormone receptor-positive breast cancers had the greatest clinical outcome[55]. Furthermore, a greater incidence of lung metastases was observed in the HER2+ subtype[56]. Because of their distinct morphological and physiological features, the lungs are the preferred site for metastasis.

The influence of the lung microbiome on the establishment and growth of breast cancer metastases

Once considered sterile, the lung is now known to host a dynamic microbiome of bacteria, viruses, and fungi that interacts with the immune system and modulates diseases such as cancer metastases[57]. This microbiome-malignancy link is a growing area of study, with prior work showing that distinct microbial patterns in blood and tissues can help differentiate cancer types and stages[58]. In breast cancer, the lung microbiome may influence metastasis. In murine models, chronic pulmonary infections, especially from P. aeruginosa and S. aureus, have been shown to promote breast cancer lung metastasis by altering the immune environment and recruiting MHCIIhi neutrophils[59]. Intertumoral bacteria are also associated with higher metastasis risk, and their depletion significantly reduces lung metastases without affecting primary tumor growth[60]. These findings suggest that local microbial communities can shape immune responses and create conditions favorable for tumor cell colonization and proliferation in the lung.

Mechanisms by which the lung microbiome influences the metastatic process

According to new research, the lung microbiota influences cancer metastasis in several ways.

Inflammation and immune modulation

Chronic inflammation caused by changes in the lung microbiome can foster a microenvironment that supports tumor growth and metastasis, suppresses anti-tumor immune responses, and promotes the survival and expansion of metastatic cells. For instance, some bacteria, such as Streptococcus and Veillonella, can promote metastasis by upregulating the respiratory epithelial cells’ ERK and PI3K pathways[61].

Metabolic interactions

Chemicals produced by microbial communities in the lungs might interact with host cellular pathways to affect tumor behavior and perhaps increase the proliferation and potential for metastasis of cancer cells[62].

Epithelial barrier disruption

The lung microbiome’s dysbiosis can weaken the epithelial barrier, which facilitates the invasion and growth of secondary tumors by cancer cells. This disturbance enables the movement of microorganisms and their products, further accelerating metastatic processes[63].

Angiogenesis promotion

Through inflammatory mediators and cytokines, the lung microbiome can promote angiogenesis or the creation of new blood vessels. Neovascularization promotes tumor growth and creates channels for metastatic spread[61].

Therapies targeting the lung microbiome to prevent/treat breast cancer metastasis

Emerging evidence highlights the lung microbiome’s role in breast cancer metastasis, particularly to the lungs. Modulating this microbiota presents a promising therapeutic strategy.

Studies show that altering the lung or tumor microbiome can impact treatment responses. Certain commensal bacteria enhance radiation efficacy, while microbiome composition influences chemotherapy outcomes. Immune checkpoint inhibitors, effective in TNBC, rely partly on microbiome-mediated immune regulation[64].

Segal et al demonstrated probiotics’ ability to modulate lung microbiota and reduce inflammation in chronic lung diseases, offering insights for cancer therapy[65]. Microbial metabolites and their functions

Potential for targeting the microbiome in breast cancer

Recent findings have demonstrated the potential of the lung microbiome as a major player in lung health and disease. Microbial imbalances are found to lead to cancer progression through mechanisms such as inflammation, immune dysregulation, and genotoxin production, promoting tumor development. They not only show local effects, but systematic effects in their influence on cancers in multiple body organs, most notably, breast cancer[67].

In lung cancer, dysbiosis influences immune homeostasis via the signaling of PI3K and ERK, leading to the activation of metastatic pathways. Several types of bacteria like Streptococcus, Veillonella, Prevotella, and Fusobacterium are a driving force behind tumorigenesis by inducing inflammation and DNA damage. Since the gut microbiome’s role in cancer is well-known, growing evidence links lung microbiota to systemic inflammation and breast cancer progression by affecting epithelial permeability[68,69].

SCFAs which are metabolites of lung microbiota, influence immune responses and the tumor microenvironment. SCFAs control gene expression and tumor metabolism, while polyamines promote cell proliferation[69]. Microbial modulation of immune pathways, like IL-17 activation, further promotes tumor growth and metastasis. The lung microbiome has shown an effect on metastasis by stimulating cytokines and chemokines that draw circulating tumor cells and prepare distant organs for colonization. Targeting these microbial mechanisms could offer innovative ways to prevent metastasis[70,71].

The lung microbiome offers promising new paths for breast cancer treatment. Antibiotics, probiotics, inhaled microbial therapies, and immunotherapy could reduce inflammation and enhance immune responses[72]. Future research should identify microbial biomarkers for early diagnosis and assess microbiome-targeted therapies in clinical trials. Microbial biomarkers in IBC

Crosstalk between lung microbiome and breast cancer

One significant mechanism is the alteration of the lung’s immune environment brought about by changes in the microbial composition. The microbiome can alter immune responses by influencing the activity of immune cells such as Tregs, dendritic cells, and macrophages. For instance, certain bacterial species have been found to promote an anti-inflammatory environment, which may stop tumor growth and metastasis[74]. However, more microbial alterations might lead to a pro-inflammatory state, which would boost cancer cell growth and survival[75].

Furthermore, the lung microbiome can affect the integrity of the epithelial barrier, which is the body’s first line of defense against infections and carcinogens. Chronic inflammation can result from the disruption of this barrier caused by dysbiosis, which can let harmful substances and pathogens in Ma et al[59]. Chronic inflammation is a well-known risk factor for the development of cancer because it creates a microenvironment that promotes tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis. According to the study by Ma et al, lung inflammation caused by dysbiosis may contribute to the development of lung metastases in patients with breast cancer. It has been proposed that chronic infections caused by S. aureus may hasten lung metastases in patients with breast cancer [76].

The production of microbial metabolites is another significant aspect of crosstalk. Butyrate, acetate, and propionate are among the metabolites known as SCFAs that are produced when microorganisms ferment dietary fibers. These SCFAs have been shown to possess anti-inflammatory and anticancer properties[77]. For example, butyrate can inhibit histone deacetylases, which can stop tumor growth and induce apoptosis in cancer cells. The levels of these beneficial metabolites may be affected by changes in the lung microbiome, which may influence how cancer develops[78]. However, recent research has shown a positive correlation between the expression of Roseburia, a butyrate-producing microbe, and the metastasis of lung cancer[79], suggesting that butyrate may not always function as an antitumor mediator. Therefore, more research is necessary to fully understand the connection between butyrate and cancer.

Furthermore, through systemic circulation, the lung microbiota can communicate with distant organs. LPS could set off systemic immune reactions. Increased cancer risk and a poor prognosis for patients with breast cancer have been linked to elevated LPS levels. This systemic inflammation can help circulating tumor cells colonize the lungs by establishing a pre-metastatic niche[80], mainly through mediating the NF-KB/Snail/HK3 pathway. Studies are, however, divided in the consensus, as certain studies have noted LPS-induced monocytes and inflammatory responses to halt the progression of breast cancer[81,82]. This suggests there might be a “cutoff” for the levels of LPS, with an excess of it promoting tumor growth.

Current research and future directions

Summary of recent studies on the skin microbiome and IBC

Key findings and advancements

Recent studies on the microbiome have offered novel insights into the pathogenesis of IBC, suggesting a possible microbial contribution to its rapid progression and aggressiveness. Unlike earlier assumptions that IBC is purely noninfectious, emerging evidence shows that bacteria such as C. acnes and S. epidermidis are significantly more prevalent in IBC tumors compared to non-IBCs[83,84]. These microbes often reside in biofilm communities, enabling them to evade immune detection, sustain chronic inflammation, and cause DNA damage[36,83]. Furthermore, advanced sequencing techniques have uncovered microbial signatures unique to IBC lesions. A particularly significant finding is the presence of viral coinfections, such as human cytomegalovirus (HCMV), in some IBC tumors, which raises the possibility of viral-microbial synergism in tumor progression[84]. These observations challenge conventional notions and open new hypotheses about microbial influences on tumor biology, particularly through immune modulation and pro-inflammatory mechanisms[36]. The presence of biofilm-forming bacteria in IBC may enable persistent inflammation, thereby promoting angiogenesis, immune evasion, and tumor invasion.

Limitations and research gaps

While these findings are promising, several critical limitations persist. First, the rarity of IBC limits sample availability, resulting in small cohort sizes that weaken statistical power. Additionally, the risk of contamination, particularly from reagents during sequencing remains a concern. For example, Methylobacterium, previously reported in IBC samples, was later traced to contaminants in sequencing kits, not the tumors themselves[27]. Another notable gap is the underrepresentation of skin microbiome studies, as most research has focused on breast tissue while neglecting the inflamed and erythematous skin regions typical of IBC. This is particularly relevant since skin dysbiosis has been associated with persistent inflammation in other skin-related cancers[27,85], but it remains unclear whether the skin microbiome in IBC is functionally and compositionally distinct. High-resolution studies targeting the affected skin areas (e.g., erythema and peau d’orange) could help define dysbiosis patterns specific to IBC.

Future research opportunities and challenges

Emerging technologies and methodologies to advance understanding

Advancements in microbial sequencing and imaging are set to overcome previous limitations. While traditional 16S rRNA sequencing offers only genus-level resolution, shotgun metagenomics enables strain-level identification and functional gene annotation, particularly of virulence factors[85]. Techniques such as meta-transcriptomics can profile actively expressed microbial genes, and metabolomics can reveal microbial by-products that influence the tumor microenvironment. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) provides visual confirmation of microbial presence within tumor tissues, while longitudinal microbiome sampling which is currently used in breast cancer radiotherapy trials, can uncover dynamic microbial shifts throughout treatment courses[26]. Three-dimensional coculture systems of skin and breast tissue with isolated microbial strains serve as powerful tools to explore microbe–tumor crosstalk and validate causality.

Interdisciplinary collaboration

Microbiome research in IBC requires interdisciplinary collaboration. Oncologists and surgeons provide crucial clinical context and annotated samples; microbiologists characterize strain-level differences; immunologists interpret host–microbiome immune interactions; and bioinformaticians manage the extensive multi-omics datasets. Due to the rarity of IBC, international collaboration is vital. Pooling data from multiple centers can enhance sample size and capture regional microbial variation[86], which may influence disease presentation and treatment outcomes. Regional microbiome profiles may affect IBC incidence and therapeutic response, highlighting the need for location-specific microbial studies.

Ethical and regulatory considerations in microbiome research

As skin microbiome research transitions into clinical domains, ethical concerns are increasing. Microbiome data may disclose environmental or lifestyle exposures, which raises privacy issues[87]. Protocols must ensure informed consent and de-identification. Therapeutic applications such as topical probiotics, antibiotics, or microbiota transplantation will necessitate robust regulatory oversight[86,87]. Furthermore, the lack of standardized protocols for sampling, storage, and analysis introduces variability in results, highlighting the need for global microbiome research guidelines in oncology.

Unique features of the skin microbiome in IBC

Emerging evidence suggests that the IBC skin microbiome is enriched with opportunistic commensals, particularly S. epidermidis and C. acnes, which thrive in inflamed environments[83,84]. Unlike mastitis, IBC lesions rarely harbor S. aureus, indicating a non-pyogenic microbial profile consistent with the non-infectious nature of IBC[88]. Biofilm formation by these microbes may sustain local immune activation and tissue disruption[36,83,84]. The role of chronic inflammation in IBC progression

Chronic inflammation plays a central role in the progression of IBC. Tumor tissues in IBC are rich in inflammatory cytokines[36,83,84], and the tumor microenvironment fosters an environment conducive to rapid growth and metastasis[89,90]. The skin microbiome may contribute to this process by triggering immune responses that amplify inflammation. For instance, bacteria like S. epidermidis can release peptides that activate immune cells, leading to prolonged inflammation[83]. Likewise, a Bartonella infection in the dermis could trigger an acute neutrophilic response that transitions into chronic inflammation, as was hypothesized in one IBC case series[84]. Additionally, systemic inflammation, often associated with obesity, may further exacerbate the inflammatory state of the breast tissue, increasing the risk of IBC development[91]. Furthermore, it has been seen that the gut microbiome of patients with IBC contributes to a pro-inflammatory systemic milieu (via metabolites and endotoxins) that primes the breast for malignant transformation[91,92]. A combined gut–skin microbial axis may sustain a chronic inflammatory loop, accelerating IBC onset and metastasis.

Potential of skin microbiome biomarkers for early detection and risk stratification

One of the most exciting possibilities in microbiome research is the development of biomarkers for early detection and risk assessment in IBC. Since IBC is often diagnosed at a late stage, microbiome profiling could offer a novel way to identify the disease at an earlier point. A unique combination of commensal bacteria or microbial metabolites could potentially differentiate IBC from benign conditions such as infection or mastitis[85]. Furthermore, the baseline skin microbiome could serve as a tool for predicting the risk of IBC recurrence, aiding in more personalized treatment strategies[27]. A recent study in radiation oncology demonstrated that patients whose skin microbiome was enriched with certain bacteria had worse radiation dermatitis, pointing to the microbiome as a biomarker of inflammatory response propensity[26]. Strategies for targeting the skin microbiome to improve treatment outcomes

The possibility of targeting the skin microbiome to improve IBC treatment outcomes is an exciting frontier in cancer therapy. Several strategies could be explored, such as antibiotic treatments aimed at disrupting biofilms or using probiotics to restore a healthy microbial balance[83] and an anti-biofilm approach.

Evidence from other cancers that altering the gut microbiome can improve responses to therapy[86,91] similarly, a “probiotic lotion” for the breast skin might be conceived as an adjunct to reduce local side effects of therapy or infection risk. Moreover, anti-inflammatory agents could help manage the chronic inflammation that is a hallmark of IBC[90]. Each of these approaches would need to be carefully tested for safety and efficacy in clinical trials, as they could interact with traditional cancer therapies[86,91]. Controlled modulation of skin microbiota may reduce radiation side effects, mitigate inflammation, and decrease IBC recurrence.

Implications for clinical practice and patient care

Integrating skin microbiome research into clinical practice

Although still emerging, skin microbiome research is poised to redefine the clinical management of IBC. As microbial profiling becomes more accessible, there is potential to integrate microbiome signatures into diagnostic workflows for earlier detection, particularly in patients with ambiguous or nonresponsive symptoms. For instance, the co-occurrence of specific commensals, such as S. epidermidis and C. acnes, along with their biofilm-forming capabilities, may eventually serve as risk markers or complementary diagnostic tools for distinguishing IBC from mastitis or cellulitis[36,83,84]. Moreover, microbiome data could be utilized to monitor treatment response, predict adverse effects, or even guide personalized adjuvant therapies. In the future, a skin swab or noninvasive patch test may become part of the routine assessment for IBC, especially in dermatologically ambiguous cases. A predictive skin microbiome panel, once validated, could reduce diagnostic delays and enable personalized therapeutic planning for IBC.

Recommendations for healthcare providers

Healthcare providers should stay informed about the evolving role of the skin microbiome in IBC. While microbiome-targeted therapies are not yet standard practice, maintaining awareness of emerging research will help guide decisions. It is essential to use antibiotics judiciously and avoid unnecessary treatments that may disrupt the patient’s natural microbiome. Furthermore, optimizing general skin health and maintaining a high index of suspicion is important as IBC can masquerade as infection[9]. IBC should be suspected in any patient with persistent breast skin changes, even when classical signs of infection are not present. The skin microbiome may offer important clues when imaging and pathology are inconclusive.

Patient education and awareness

Educating patients is crucial for managing expectations around microbiome research. Patients often confuse IBC with infection due to its presentation. Clinicians should reassure patients that IBC is not caused by a contagious infection, and it cannot be passed to others through casual contact. Furthermore, clinicians should be clear about the current state of science, explaining that while microbiome manipulation holds promise, it is not yet a proven therapy for IBC. Patients should also be encouraged to maintain a healthy lifestyle, as this can support overall immune function and potentially benefit their microbiome. Framing IBC as a complex interaction of immune, microbial, and genetic factors helps patients avoid stigma and emphasizes that modern treatments require multitargeted approaches.

In conclusion, while the field of microbiome research in IBC is still developing, it holds great potential to improve both diagnosis and treatment outcomes. By fostering collaboration and addressing ethical concerns, the future of microbiome-based approaches in cancer care looks promising.

Conclusion

IBC is a rare, yet highly aggressive breast cancer variant that is marked by swift progression, poor outcomes, and unique clinical indicators like redness, swelling, and a peau d’orange appearance. Despite progress in breast cancer studies, the underlying mechanisms of IBC are still not fully understood. Recent findings indicate that the microbiome – specifically the skin, breast, and lung microbiomes – plays a significant role in influencing inflammation, immune responses, and the advancement of tumors in IBC. This narrative review underscores the interaction between microbial communities and the development of IBC, highlighting the potential for microbiome-based biomarkers and therapeutic strategies.

Breast tissue microbiome and dysbiosis in IBC

The breast microbiome comprises a variety of bacterial phyla (Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidetes) that are essential for maintaining tissue health. Dysbiosis, defined by an excess of harmful bacteria (like Ralstonia and Staphylococcus), is linked to ongoing inflammation, hormonal imbalances, and DNA damage, which could facilitate the emergence of IBC. Microbial byproducts (such as SCFAs and beta-glucuronidase) affect estrogen processing, immune responses, and the remodeling of the tumor microenvironment.

Skin microbiome and chronic inflammation in IBC

The skin microbiome, which includes commensal organisms like S. epidermidis and C. acnes, is vital for preserving skin barrier function and immune regulation. A dysbiotic skin microbiome (e.g., excessive growth of S. aureus and P. aeruginosa) may lead to persistent inflammation, EMT, and tumor advancement in IBC. Microbial biofilms and pathogen-associated molecular patterns trigger TLRs and NF-κB signaling pathways, creating a pro-tumor environment.

Lung microbiome and metastasis in IBC

The lungs are a frequent location for breast cancer metastasis, especially in aggressive types like TNBC. The lung microbiome affects the potential for metastatic colonization by modulating the immune system (e.g., attracting TAMs and Tregs) and producing inflammatory cytokines. Chronic lung infections (for instance, Streptococcus and Veillonella) may elevate the likelihood of metastasis by compromising epithelial barriers and encouraging angiogenesis.

Microbiome as a biomarker and therapeutic target

Microbiome signatures (like Fusobacterium and Lachnospira) could function as diagnostic or prognostic indicators for IBC. Methods such as probiotics, antibiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation might be used to adjust the microbiome to enhance treatment efficacy. Combining microbiome investigations with multi-omics techniques (including metagenomics and metabolomics) could improve precision medicine approaches for IBC.

Limitations

Although evidence increasingly connects the microbiome with IBC, various limitations impede the application of these findings in clinical settings. A significant obstacle is the infrequency of IBC, leading to small sample sizes and limited statistical power in studies focused on the microbiome. Moreover, differences in sampling methods such as variations among tissue biopsies, skin swabs, and blood samples – can result in inconsistencies in microbial profiling. Contamination that occurs during the collection or sequencing of samples adds further challenges to data interpretation, as some identified microbes may not be relevant to IBC biologically. An additional crucial limitation is the absence of mechanistic studies that establish causality; most existing research has been correlational, leaving unresolved whether microbial dysbiosis contributes to IBC progression or if it is simply a result of the disease. Additionally, the intricate relationships among the gut, skin, and breast microbiomes are still not well understood, complicating the determination of whether systemic microbial alterations or localized disruptions have a more substantial impact on IBC development. Lastly, the lack of standardized procedures for microbiome analysis makes it difficult to compare results across studies and validate findings.

Future implications

Future studies should focus on extensive, long-term research to gain a better understanding of microbiome changes in patients with IBC from the time of diagnosis through their treatment journey. Utilizing multi-omics strategies – such as metagenomics, metabolomics, and transcriptomics – could shed light on the functional roles of microbes in tumor development. Investigating mechanisms through animal models, such as germ-free mice that are introduced to microbes associated with IBC, would aid in establishing causal links between dysbiosis and the disease. Furthermore, examining the interactions between the microbiome and host immune responses, particularly the influence of microbial metabolites on inflammatory processes, may reveal new therapeutic targets. From a clinical perspective, creating noninvasive microbial biomarkers (such as skin or blood signatures) for the early detection of IBC and risk assessment is highly promising. Therapeutic approaches, including the use of probiotics, specific antibiotics, and microbiota transplantation, should be evaluated in controlled trials to determine their effectiveness in modifying the tumor microenvironment and enhancing treatment outcomes. Lastly, developing standardized protocols for microbiome research in IBC is critical to ensuring reproducibility and speeding up translational efforts. By tackling these issues, upcoming research can advance microbiome-based precision medicine in the management of IBC.
