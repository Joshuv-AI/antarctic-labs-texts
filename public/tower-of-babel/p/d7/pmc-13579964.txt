A case of peritoneal dialysis-associated peritonitis caused by Rhodococcus pyridinivorans
Authors: Wenjuan Lei, Chuishun Yang, Zhen Yang, Xingli Cai, Jun Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRhodococcus infections are exceedingly rare and largely reported in immunocompromised hosts, with no consensus on optimal therapy due to limited evidence.Case presentationThis article reports for the first time a case of peritoneal dialysis–associated peritonitis caused by Rhodococcus pyridinivorans in a patient with diabetic nephropathy. The dialysate leukocyte count declined substantially with combination antimicrobial therapy, but complete normalization occurred only after replacing icodextrin with a glucose-based dialysate.ConclusionsThese findings support the use of combination regimens and emphasize the need to consider icodextrin-associated sterile peritonitis when dialysate abnormalities persist despite appropriate antimicrobial treatment.

Introduction

Rhodococcus is a genus of Gram-positive, obligate aerobic bacteria and a rare opportunistic human pathogen with generally low virulence [1]. Most documented infections involve R. equi in immunocompromised individuals, whereas Rhodococcus spp infections are exceedingly uncommon [2]. Here, we report a case of Rhodococcus pyridinivorans peritonitis in a peritoneal dialysis (PD) patient with diabetic nephropathy. By integrating a detailed case description with the existing literature, we analyze the clinical characteristics, management approaches, and predisposing factors associated with this infection, offering practical insights for clinical care.

Case presentation

A 36-year-old man was admitted on October 19, 2025, with a three-day history of cloudy peritoneal dialysate. The effluent became turbid without an apparent trigger and lacked flocculent material. One day prior to admission, he developed abdominal pain, distension, nausea, and vomiting, prompting hospitalization. He had a 5-year history of diabetes mellitus and has not been using antidiabetic medications regularly. He gradually developed blurred vision. On February 17, 2024, he presented with fatigue and poor appetite. His blood pressure was 184/96 mmHg. Laboratory studies showed BUN 55.26 mmol/L, serum creatinine 1940 µmol/L, hemoglobin 59 g/L; urinalysis demonstrated protein 3+, erythrocytes 1+; urinary microalbumin was 598 mg/g, and serological tests were negative. Bilateral renal ultrasonography demonstrated increased renal parenchymal echogenicity, loss of corticomedullary differentiation, and reduced intrarenal blood flow. Renal biopsy was not feasible. Fundoscopic examination revealed retinal hemorrhages and exudates. The patient was diagnosed with diabetic nephropathy, stage 5 chronic kidney disease, and has been undergoing peritoneal dialysis since then.In 2024, he had a past episode of Neisseria sicca peritonitis that resolved after a 3-week course of ceftriaxone and ciprofloxacin.His history is notable for pulmonary tuberculosis diagnosed in September 2020, successfully treated with a one-year course of standard quadruple therapy. He also has chronic hepatitis B and has been on long-term entecavir, with no evidence of active viral replication. On admission, vital signs were: temperature 36.5 °C, pulse 92 bpm, respiratory rate 22 breaths/min, and blood pressure 204/123 mmHg. Pulmonary auscultation revealed clear breath sounds bilaterally without rales.the peritoneal catheter was present in the right lower abdomen; the exit site showed no erythema, swelling, or discharge. The abdomen was soft with diffuse tenderness, rebound tenderness, and shifting dullness. No lower-extremity edema was noted.

Initial laboratory testing showed WBC 11.89 × 10^9/L, hemoglobin 89 g/L, platelets 346 × 10^9/L, and neutrophils 81%. Peritoneal dialysate analysis revealed a WBC count of 11,074 × 10^6/L with 86% polymorphonuclear cells. Serum biochemistry demonstrated K + 3.63 mmol/L, Na + 134.29 mmol/L, Ca2 + 2.24 mmol/L, P 0.97 mmol/L, BUN 16.84 mmol/L, creatinine 1390.85 µmol/L, albumin 37.01 g/L, ALT 15.66 U/L, AST 15.28 U/L, prealbumin 244.77 mg/L, hs-CRP 125.53 mg/L, and iPTH 75.3 pg/mL. Peritoneal dialysis–associated peritonitis (PDAP) was diagnosed. Empiric therapy was initiated with intraperitoneal cefazolin 0.1 g once daily (QD) and amikacin 0.2 g QD (days 0–3), plus intravenous piperacillin–tazobactam 4.5 g every 12 h (days 1–3). Due to poor ultrafiltration and difficult control of blood glucose, the peritoneal dialysis solution was changed from 2.5% dextrose to icodextrin (days 3–18). On day 2, Rhodococcus rhodochrous was detected by mass spectrometric identification using a matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI) system, the MALDI Biotyper® (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) (Fig. 1). Further, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed to verify the results of mass spectrometry identification. The obtained sequences were subjected to BLAST analysis in the NCBI database, which showed the highest similarity with Rhodococcus pyridinivorans at 98.23%, whereas Rhodococcus rhodochrous showed 98.16% similarity. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was conducted using the Kirby-Bauer (K-B) method, including Amoxicillin/Clavulanate Potassium, Levofloxacin, Ciprofloxacin, Vancomycin, Imipenem, Compound Sulfamethoxazole, Gentamicin, Linezolid, and Piperacillin/Tazobactam, as summarized in Table 1. Based on antimicrobial susceptibility results and in consultation with the pharmacy department, the patient’s antibiotic regimen was adjusted as follows: intraperitoneal vancomycin 1 g every 3–6 days (days 3–18), with dose modifications according to trough levels (October 26: 9.29 µg/mL; November 6: 21.94 µg/mL). This was combined with intravenous moxifloxacin 400 mg once daily (days 3–10) and oral rifampin 600 mg once daily (days 3–23). During treatment, PD fluid leukocyte counts decreased significantly but remained abnormal (130–413 × 10^6/L), consistent with refractory peritonitis. Multiple recommendations were made to remove the PD catheter; however, the patient reported symptomatic improvement and refused catheter removal, insisting on continuing PD. Following further pharmacy consultation on day 10, intravenous antibiotics were adjusted: moxifloxacin was discontinued and replaced with doxycycline 100 mg IV once daily (days 10–16). Due to the patient’s diabetes and prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, oral fluconazole 200 mg once daily (days 10–23) was added for fungal prophylaxis. Minimal improvement in PD fluid cell counts and gastrointestinal adverse effects prompted a switch to intravenous levofloxacin 250 mg once daily (days 16–19). Routine PD fluid analysis remained abnormal. Subsequently, vancomycin and levofloxacin were discontinued, and intraperitoneal ciprofloxacin 100 mg once daily (days 19–23) was initiated, alongside continued oral rifampin. Additionally, oral linezolid 600 mg every 12 h (days 19–22) was administered but stopped after three days due to gastrointestinal intolerance. Throughout anti-infective therapy, the patient’s abdominal symptoms improved, and PD fluid leukocyte counts significantly decreased. Repeated PD fluid cultures on day 15 were negative, indicating effective infection control. However, routine PD fluid analysis remained abnormal, with a shift from polymorphonuclear to mononuclear predominance. On day 18, nucleated cell differential showed neutrophils 19.6%, lymphocytes 63.8%, monocytes 16.6%. Considering potential icodextrin-induced sterile peritonitis, the icodextrin exchange was replaced with a glucose-based solution (days 18–22), and the modality was changed to continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD) with 2.5% dextrose, 2 L × 4 exchanges. Repeat dialysate analysis on day 19 normalized (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Rhodococcus, a genus within the family Nocardiaceae, comprises 43 species, several of which are clinically significant [3]. These include R. equi, R. rhodnii, R. erythropolis, R. rhodochrous, and R. bronchialis [4]. Human infections specifically attributable to R. rhodochrous are exceptionally rare [5]. This case was identified as Rhodococcus pyridinivorans, a member of the genus Rhodococcus with a relatively short history of formal scientific description. The species was first isolated and named by Yoon et al. in 2000. It shares 99% 16S rDNA sequence similarity with Rhodococcus rhodochrous and was confirmed as a distinct species through DNA-DNA hybridization and phenotypic analysis [6]. Since its discovery, research on R. pyridinivorans has focused primarily on its potent biodegradation capabilities. To date, no cases of human infection caused by Rhodococcus pyridinivorans have been reported.

Based on the patient’s gastrointestinal symptoms, a peritoneal dialysate leukocyte count > 100 × 10^6/L with neutrophil predominance, and growth of R. rhodochrous in dialysate culture, a diagnosis of peritoneal dialysis–associated peritonitis (PDAP) was established. 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed to verify the results of mass spectrometry identification, and the highest similarity was found with Rhodococcus pyridinivorans, with a similarity of 98.23%. To the best of our knowledge, this appears to be the first reported case of PDAP caused by P. pyridinivorans globally. Previous literature has reported that peritonitis caused by Rhodococcus is predominantly due to Rhodococcus equi, with recent reports also indicating Rhodococcus hoagii as a causative agent of PDAP [7], suggesting that non-R. equi species can also act as opportunistic pathogens in peritonitis.Rhodococcus species can be acquired via the respiratory mucosa, cutaneous wounds, soil exposure, and the gastrointestinal tract. This patient’s peritonitis occurred in the context of a recent typhoon. Our leading hypothesis is an environmentally-mediated exposure. The storm likely facilitated the introduction of pathogens into the home, with possible sources including contaminated soil or animals, leading to infection in the absence of standard ultraviolet disinfection. Clinical data on Rhodococcus infections remain limited, and no standardized treatment regimen has been established. Given its intracellular propensity, effective therapy should include agents with good cellular penetration, administered at full doses for an adequate duration, often in combination—an approach associated with improved outcomes [3]. Extrapolating from management strategies for R. equi [8–10], active agents may include vancomycin, levofloxacin, cefazolin, cefoperazone, gentamicin, and piperacillin. In 1989, Brown et al. reported a case of PDAP due to R. erythropolis initially treated with cefazolin for 10 days, with symptom resolution followed by relapse one week after discontinuation; the infection was subsequently cured with a 2-week course of vancomycin [11]. In the present case, a triple regimen of moxifloxacin, rifampin, and vancomycin was associated with a rapid decline in dialysate leukocyte counts, indicating responsiveness to combination therapy. Nonetheless, after eight days, the counts had not fully normalized.

Remarkably, the patient returned to normal within just one day after switching from Icodextrin to glucose-based dialysate. This dual therapeutic response (partial efficacy with combined antimicrobial treatment and complete recovery after replacing Icodextrin) suggests that, in addition to bacterial peritonitis, this case may have been complicated or secondary to Icodextrin-associated sterile peritonitis. The use of Icodextrin has been associated with sterile peritonitis syndrome [12]. Previous studies have indicated that peptidoglycan contamination in certain batches of Icodextrin is a key factor in triggering sterile peritonitis, as it induces peritoneal cytokine bursts and mild peritoneal damage leading to inflammation [13]. Furthermore, research by Touré et al. demonstrated that, unlike bacterial peritonitis, Icodextrin-induced sterile peritonitis has characteristic features in the peritoneal dialysate cytology: a relatively low white blood cell count, with a low neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio [14]. In this case, during ongoing antimicrobial treatment, the leukocyte differential in the peritoneal dialysate shifted from predominantly polymorphonuclear cells to predominantly monocytes/lymphocytes (with the neutrophil percentage dropping to 19.6% on October 27). This cytological shift closely matches the features of Icodextrin-associated sterile peritonitis described in the literature, further supporting the Icodextrin hypothesis. Therefore, when dialysis fluid abnormalities persist after appropriate antimicrobial treatment and a cytological shift to a predominance of monocytes occurs, Icodextrin-associated sterile peritonitis should be considered. Timely identification of this potential sterile inflammation induced by Icodextrin is crucial, as it can prevent unnecessary antibiotic exposure and avoid serious complications such as catheter removal or dialysis interruption [15].

There have been no reports of human infections caused by Rhodococcus pyridinivorans so far.Based on this case and the existing literature, several points merit emphasis. First, most Rhodococcus infections occur in immunocompromised hosts, including individuals with diabetes, hematologic disease, malignancy, or exposure to immunosuppressants. Notably, cases of R. erythropolis meningoencephalitis following rituximab therapy have had poor outcomes, suggesting that pharmacologic suppression of B-cell proliferation substantially impairs host defense and increases both susceptibility to and severity of infection [16]. As an opportunistic pathogen of relatively low virulence, Rhodococcus pyridinivorans likely caused infection in our patient—who was not receiving immunosuppressants—primarily in the context of diabetes and PD. Second, Rhodococcus species exhibit broad tissue tropism, with reported infections involving the bloodstream, lungs, and surgical sites. Our case indicates that PD patients may be at risk for Rhodococcus pyridinivorans peritonitis, underscoring the need for heightened vigilance and preventive measures in immunocompromised PD populations. Third, diagnosis in prior cases has relied on culture, as in the present report. However, molecular diagnostics, particularly metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), offer substantial value for detecting Rhodococcus and are crucial for confirming peritonitis when cultures are negative. Tanaka [17] et al. reported the identification of Rhodococcus corynebacterioides by 16S rRNA gene sequencing as the cause of recurrent PDAP in a 57-year-old Japanese man.Regarding antimicrobial therapy, Rhodococcus species often demonstrate resistance to many β-lactams but may be susceptible to quinolones, aminoglycosides, vancomycin, tetracyclines, and rifampin. Monotherapy is frequently suboptimal; combination regimens administered for at least 10 days are recommended. Although the lack of established guidelines for treatment duration, a 3-week regimen was adopted by analogy with that used for Corynebacterium (a Gram-positive rod) peritonitis.The favorable outcome in this case supports the effectiveness of combination therapy.

In conclusion, Rhodococcus is a rare pathogen that predominantly affects immunocompromised hosts and presents with nonspecific clinical features.To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of peritoneal dialysis–associated peritonitis caused by Rhodococcus pyridinivorans. Microbiological confirmation is key to the diagnosis, and molecular detection techniques, such as 16S rRNA analysis, can provide important support. Treatment should favor combination regimens—such as a quinolone with a tetracycline or rifampin—which generally yield favorable outcomes. In addition, the dual treatment response observed in this case—partial improvement with combined antimicrobial therapy followed by complete recovery after switching from icodextrin—along with the shift of peritoneal effluent leukocytes from predominantly polymorphonuclear to mononuclear cells, suggests that icodextrin-related sterile peritonitis may have been superimposed or secondary. Therefore, when abnormal peritoneal effluent persists despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy, icodextrin-related sterile peritonitis should be considered. Timely recognition of this possibility is crucial, as it can prevent unnecessary antibiotic exposure and avoid serious complications such as catheter removal or interruption of dialysis.
