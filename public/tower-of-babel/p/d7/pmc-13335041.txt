Pediatric head injury in the emergency department: balancing radiation risk, missed diagnoses, and decision-rule evolution

Introduction

Pediatric head injury remains one of the most frequently encountered presentations in emergency departments (ED). Among children aged 0–6 years, US national estimates suggest roughly 1.3–1.4 million ED visits for mild traumatic brain injury between 2016 and 2019, the vast majority (> 98%) of whom are discharged home with estimated ED charges of 540–681 million USD per year [1]. Historically, in the 1990’s and early 2000’s, physicians liberally requested computed tomography (CT) to exclude clinically important traumatic brain injury (ciTBI) and alleviate parental anxiety, despite most pediatric head CTs being ultimately negative. In the large PECARN cohort, only 0.9% of children with minor head trauma sustained a ciTBI [2]. Additionally, CT is costly, sometimes requires sedation and exposes children to a potential long-term risk of malignancy [3].

Concerns about radiation exposure - particularly in children whose developing tissues are more radiosensitive - prompted the development of evidence-based clinical decision rules in the late 2000’s. Decision rules such as PECARN (USA), CATCH (Canada), and CHALICE (UK) were designed to identify children at very low risk of ciTBI, thereby safely avoiding CT use. The Pediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network (PECARN) categorizes children into very-low, intermediate, and high-risk groups, using separate algorithms for infants (< 2 years) and older children (≥ 2 years). By assessing objective clinical findings - such as altered mental status, palpable skull fracture, mechanism of injury, loss of consciousness, vomiting, and age-specific red flags - PECARN identifies children at very low risk of ciTBI for whom CT can be safely avoided with high sensitivity of ~ 100% (< 2 yrs) and ~ 99% (≥ 2 yrs) for ciTBI [4]. CATCH and CHALICE - validated and implemented in Canada and the UK, respectively - show comparable sensitivity to PECARN [4].

Despite a measurable decline in unnecessary CT imaging since the emergence of decision rules, practice variation persists, and the extent to which these rules reduce CT utilization remains unclear. Recent data reveal ongoing overuse in certain subgroups - particularly infants under two years and children presenting to non-teaching or non-metropolitan hospitals - underscoring the partial penetration of decision-rules in these settings [5]. Moreover, limited access to pediatric specialists, rising emphasis on patient-centered care, and parental anxiety all influence decision-making and create practice inconsistencies.

A decade after the widespread implementation of decision rules, new technologies and strategies have emerged to further refine the evaluation of pediatric head injury. Multiple EDs have encouraged the replacement of CT with rapid Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI). Although rapid MRI takes few minutes to complete and eliminates radiation exposure, it may fail to detect skull fractures and subarachnoid haemorrhages [6]. Recently, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning tools such as optimal classification tree (OCT) and deep artificial neural networks (DANN) have demonstrated significantly higher specificity and comparable sensitivity to PECARN in preliminary studies; however, these tools remain experimental and require prospective external validation before clinical implementation [7]. In addition, parental decision aids have facilitated shared decision-making and reduced decision conflict [8]. Collectively, rMRI, emerging AI tools, and decision aids represent a promising but still-developing frontier in pediatric head injury evaluation.

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed, MEDLINE, and Cochrane Library databases for articles published between 2000 and 2025. Search terms included ‘pediatric head trauma’, ‘computed tomography utilization’, ‘clinical decision rules’, ‘PECARN’, ‘artificial intelligence triage’, and ‘rapid MRI’. Only peer-reviewed, English-language studies were included. Exclusion criteria comprised non-human studies, conference abstracts without full manuscripts. The objective of this narrative review is to foster a clinical approach that safely minimizes radiation exposure while accurately identifying ciTBI.

Methodology

This article constitutes a narrative review of the literature. Epidemiology & Burden of CT use and ciTBI

CT utilization patterns

Computed tomography (CT) has long been a key tool in diagnosing pediatric head trauma due to its rapid detection of intracranial pathology. However, concerns about radiation exposure, cancer risk, and unnecessary imaging in low-risk children have prompted more selective use. The introduction of clinical decision rules has significantly reshaped imaging practices in pediatric emergency care. This section reviews CT utilization trends, the impact of clinically important traumatic brain injury (ciTBI), and evolving practice patterns. The use of head CT scans in children with blunt head trauma increased significantly from late 1990s to early 2000s. This is due to increased CT availability, faster scan times, and legal concerns in clinical practice. Pediatric head CT usage peaked in many parts of the United States and Canada in the later 2000s. During this period, physicians did not have much access to high quality clinical decision making tools to identify children who truly needed neuroimaging versus those at low risk for ciTBI. Early decision aids like CHALICE (2006) [9] and CATCH (2010) [10] helped address this issue, but widespread change in clinical practice did not occur until the PECARN prediction rules were published. The growing research interest in neuroimaging for TBI is well-documented; a recent bibliometric analysis confirmed a sustained global rise in neuroimaging-related investigations, reflecting the increasing scientific and clinical prioritization of imaging-based approaches in TBI management [11]. In 2009 a study involving 42,000 patients was conducted for predicting pediatric ciTBI; the PECARN study [2]. This involves the usage of highly sensitive age-specific clinical decision-making rules to predict children at low risk of TBI and do not require CT imaging. This was significant because it led to widespread changes for CT use in practice. Since implementation to present, the use of CT scans in pediatric head injury declined by 25%, up to 30–40% for institutions that used the PECARN criteria, with data supported by multiple medical institutions internationally. ciTBI incidence in pediatric population

Pediatric head injury is one of the most common presenting chief complaint in the emergency department worldwide. In USA alone, every year millions of children present to the emergency department for concussions or minor blunt head trauma, however only a small amount of these patients truly have a ciTBI requiring a CT scan for diagnosis. Thus, there’s a major discrepancy between perceived risk and the actual burden of disease [1].

Of the large prospective cohort studies that were conducted, such as PECARN [2], CATCH [10], CHALICE [9] the number of children with true ciTBI is in fact relatively low (about 0.8–1.2%) and of these cases only 0.1–0.2% truly required neurosurgical intervention [2, 9, 10]. Additionally, around 90% of CT scans performed on patients with minor head injuries are unremarkable. This highlights the need for applying the clinical decision rules to make targeted decisions to avoid unnecessary imaging.

Children under 2 years consistently undergo higher CT scan rates due to diagnostic challenges, including limited communication, nonspecific symptoms, and higher risk of non-accidental trauma; falls are the most common mechanism, often causing scalp hematomas. This underpins the separate PECARN algorithm for this age group. Adolescents also show increased CT use, mainly from sports injuries and motor vehicle collisions. Despite this, the incidence of clinically important traumatic brain injury (ciTBI) remains low across all age groups. Although CT is the gold standard for detecting intracranial injury, it exposes children to ionizing radiation.

The causal relationship between pediatric CT exposure and solid tumors remains contested in the current literature; however, evidence for a modestly increased risk of hematologic malignancies - particularly leukemia - is more robustly supported by large cohort data [3, 14]. This evidence reinforces the importance of minimizing unnecessary imaging and emphasizing justification for the necessity of a head CT in each patient with a TBI.

Observational approach in patients with TBI

A key advancement over the past decade, supported by Lumba-Brown et al. (2018) guidance [13], is the observation approach, where selected children - particularly those with negative or borderline PECARN criteria but reassuring clinical features - are managed with ED observation rather than immediate CT. Structured observation pathways have been shown to safely reduce CT utilization without increasing missed diagnoses or return visits [15], with studies reporting up to a 50% reduction in imaging among intermediate-risk patients. Additionally, shared decision-making with parents reduces anxiety and preference for imaging [16], promoting safer, lower-radiation care.

More recently, advances in machine learning have begun to complement clinical assessment, with AI-supported triage tools demonstrating promising accuracy in identifying children at risk of clinically important traumatic brain injury (ciTBI) [17, 18]. These tools remain investigational and have not yet been prospectively validated for routine clinical use. Currently, PECARN is the most reliable and extensively validated tool; AI tools may eventually serve as adjuncts, but cannot currently replace established decision rules.

Multi-center studies conducted across North America, Europe, Australia, and Asia consistently reveal a similar pattern: high usage of CT scans before adopting decision rules, followed by significant reduction after implementing PECARN/CHALICE/CATCH. They also consistently report low rates of ciTBI needing intervention in patients who fall in the low-risk categories and are highly sensitive risk-stratification pathways. Clinical decision rules & guideline comparisons

The clinical conundrum

Head injuries in the pediatric population, both accidental and sports-related, are the most common cause of emergency visits [21]. The varying mechanisms of injury and wide age range poses a significant challenge for clinicians in the pediatric ER. While the majority of the patients who present to the ED are at a low risk, the repercussions of missing clinically important traumatic brain injuries (ciTBI) can be gravely dangerous [2]. The problem lies with balancing the need to recognize ciTBI against the risks of unnecessary exposure to radiation [22].

Specific clinical predictors - including mechanism of impact, injury site, and scalp findings - have been identified as significant indicators of underlying skull fracture and intracerebral pathology, reinforcing the importance of a careful physical examination to guide imaging decisions [23]. Among injury mechanisms, recreational vehicle-related TBI represents a preventable subset; helmet use has been shown to significantly reduce injury severity and TBI incidence in this group, underscoring the role of prevention alongside clinical decision-making [24].

Thus, the development of clinical decision rules has evolved the decision making process for this common condition, this framework has aided in the goal to standardize care and reduce unnecessary radiation exposure. Analysis of methodology

Analysis of clinical decision rules requires an understanding of their methodological foundations. These rules are typically derived from large perspective cohort studies involving over 1,000 pediatric patients across multiple emergency departments, aiming to identify injuries requiring intervention. Statistical methods, particularly recursive partitioning, are used to select variables with high sensitivity and specificity for clinically important outcomes [2]. This methodological consistency enables meaningful comparison across rules, though differences in specific outcomes and other variables contribute to performance variation and implementation across clinical environments.

PECARN rules

Pediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network rule (PECARN), derived from a prospective study of over forty thousand children across twenty-five emergency departments [2]. It represents the largest and most validated decision making instrument for pediatric head trauma to date [4]. The algorithm uses a distinct age-based framework dividing patients into ages under and over two years [2]. The framework also incorporates a diverse range of clinical presentations and injury patterns across developmental stages. This enables the system to approach cases through an optimized prediction criterion thus identifying children who are at very low risk for ciTBI for whom imaging is not needed. The criterion of meeting a ciTBI for PECARN rule is defined as a composite outcome including: death from TBI, neurosurgery, intubation for more than 24 h and/or hospital admission for two or more nights in association with traumatic brain injury (TBI) on imaging [2].

Clinical application

The PECARN rule has demonstrated high diagnostic performance in validation studies, with sensitivity approaching 100% and negative predictive values (NPV) exceeding 99.9% [2]. This high sensitivity means that when the rule classifies patients as very low risk, clinicians can discharge with confidence. Recent studies of approximately 3,000 patients found that application of PECARN reduced CT use by 29% while maintaining 100% sensitivity for identifying ciTBI [26]. The system divides patients into three tiers: high risk (CT recommended), intermediate risk (clinical judgment and/or observation), and very low risk (imaging not recommended). This risk stratification allows clinicians to navigate clinical complexity using the decision-making flowchart. It is important to note that PECARN’s primary clinical strength lies in its rule-out function - its high sensitivity and NPV allow safe identification of children at very low risk; its specificity is moderate, and it is not designed as a rule-in tool.

CATCH rule - The Canadian approach to risk stratification

The CATCH (Canadian Assessment of Tomography for Child Head Injury) rule was established after a cohort study of approximately four thousand children who had suffered from minor head traumas across nine emergency departments [26]. Focusing on children with Glasgow Coma Scale score of 13–15 who suffered loss of consciousness, amnesia, disorientation, persistent vomiting and/or irritability [10]. Divided into two groups; high risk and medium risk factors, the system is designed to be able to identify and dictate both, the need for intervention as well as the type of brain injury [10]. The high-risk criteria - predictive of neurosurgical intervention - including GCS < 15 at 2 h post-injury, suspected open or depressed skull fracture, signs of basal skull fracture, and neurologic deterioration (e.g., worsening headache, irritability, or altered mental status). While the Medium-risk criteria - predictive of brain injury on CT - include amnesia ≥ 30 min before impact and dangerous mechanism of injury (motor vehicle collision, fall ≥ 3 feet or ≥ 5 stairs, or unhelmeted bicycle fall) [10].

CATCH rule performance and implementation

Recent derivation studies following the CATCH rule confirmed a sensitivity of 100% for predicting the need for neurological intervention. The medium-risk factors showed a 98.1% sensitivity to predicting brain injury on CT [10]. The need for further prospective validation studies need to be conducted before further implementation. The CATCH rule is able to provide clinicians in the emergency setting an alternative to PECARN rule, this can be valuable for very specific patient presentations [4].

The children’s head injury algorithm for the prediction of important clinical events (CHALICE) rule - The British prediction tool

The CHALICE Rule was derived after a study of 22,000 children in the United Kingdom [26]. It was designed as a methodological approach of a single detailed flow chart with various step-offs consisting of clinical indicators, mechanism of injury and features on physical exam [4]. The algorithm was designed to be highly sensitive to identify traumatic intracranial injuries, signalling that CT is warranted if any single feature is present [4]. This comprehensive approach reflects the universal healthcare system of the UK’s National Health Service (NHS), which has its unique set of practice patterns.

Professional organizations recommendation of guidelines

Major professional organizations, including the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) and the American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP), endorse the use of validated CDRs like PECARN rule to standardize assessment and reduce unnecessary CT imaging [13]. General consensus also agrees that when patients are stratified as low risk, it is the serial observation and physical examination which has shown to excel over CT alone [27]. Guidelines across major organizations emphasize shared decision-making at all levels of risk stratification, with clear communication as a central component. For low-risk patients, caregivers should receive guidance on observation and monitoring for red flag symptoms such as vomiting, headache, or loss of consciousness. In intermediate-risk cases, shared decision-making is particularly important, as clinicians must clearly communicate the risks and benefits of radiation exposure. Recent updates have further refined prediction rules, including considerations such as the frequency and duration of vomiting and the mechanism of injury [4].

Algorithm for clinical application

Guidelines from both the American College of Pediatrics and emergency medicine professional bodies support an integrated decision-making approach using PECARN as the primary tool, given its extensive validation and high sensitivity [2]. Patients stratified as very low risk require no further imaging; however, serial observation and physical examination remain important clinical practices [13]. For intermediate-risk patients, shared decision-making should guide the clinical pathway, with the choice between observation and CT imaging individualized based on clinical judgment and caregiver preferences [27]. High-risk patients should receive immediate CT imaging, with the understanding that even among high-risk children, not all develop ciTBI. Diagnostic accuracy, radiation risks, and AI alternatives

PECARN diagnostic accuracy

The PECARN decision rule is distinguished from other rules by its age-specific classification. Besides this classification, PECARN rule has demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy. A 2017 meta-analysis of 10 studies assessing the PECARN rule for identifying clinically important traumatic brain injury (ciTBI) demonstrated its strong diagnostic performance. The summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) analysis reported an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.97 (95% CI: 0.95–0.98) for the entire pediatric age ranged 0–18 years as well as specifically for the subgroup aged 2–18 years. For the subgroup of children younger than 2 years, the AUC was reported as 0.85 (95% CI: 0.82–0.88). The PECARN model demonstrates high sensitivity, reported at 0.98 for children < 2 years and 0.97 for those aged 2–18 years with moderate specificity (0.56 and 0.60 respectively). These findings reinforce PECARN as a reliable tool for identifying children at very low risk of ciTBI for whom CT can be safely avoided, while helping reduce unnecessary CT use [32, 33].

Radiation risk

Despite the availability of multiple clinical decision rules, pediatric head CT use remains high, with over five million scans in the United States and one million in the European Union in the past decade [34]. A single head CT delivers approximately 2 mSv in children aged 0–10 years, with lower doses in older patients, though exposure varies by age, sex, and body habitus [35].

Children are more sensitive to ionizing radiation due to rapidly dividing tissues and longer life expectancy, increasing the window for carcinogenic effects. The estimated risk of radiation-induced brain cancer is approximately 1 in 4,000–10,000 per scan with a small but measurable increase in lifetime cancer risk, particularly brain tumors and leukemia [14, 36, 37]. Cohort data further suggest a significant proportion of brain tumors may be attributable to prior CT exposure, with higher cumulative exposure associated with increased risk [38]. This not only emphasizes the importance of avoiding unnecessary CT examinations but also the importance of adhering to appropriate radiation procedures and established guidelines. High radiation doses can be minimized through dose optimization, applying the principle of keeping doses As Low As Reasonably Achievable (ALARA). Furthermore, the diagnosis of pediatric head trauma can be improved by adhering to validated clinical decision rules. Additional, optimization can be achieved by incorporating advanced approaches such as AI-based triage and non-ionizing imaging modalities.

Risk communication: Shared decision-making

In pediatric emergency department (PED) settings, the PECARN rule stratifies children with suspected clinically important traumatic brain injury (ciTBI) into high-, intermediate-, and very low-risk categories. High-risk children are recommended to undergo cranial CT imaging, whereas very low-risk children may be safely discharged. For intermediate-risk patients, clinical judgment guides whether CT imaging is performed or observation is chosen. Shared decision-making (SDM) with parents is particularly important in this group. SDM involves active participation of clinicians, patients, and parents in medical decisions and can be facilitated by decision aids (DAs). These structured tools help parents understand their child’s risk and the benefits and risks of available options, including CT exposure versus clinical observation. Use of DAs improves parental knowledge, enhances trust in physicians, and increases acceptance of observation, especially among low-literacy or lower socioeconomic populations. Ultimately, this approach reduces unnecessary CT scans, lowers exposure to ionizing radiation, and minimizes decisional conflict [33].

AI and rapid MRI: Emerging strategies and machine-learning triage tools

AI-assisted triage tools: Who should undergo imaging?

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have emerged as promising tools to enhance clinical practice, though their routine clinical implementation in pediatric emergency settings remains limited by the need for prospective validation. AI and ML triage tools have demonstrated AUCs ranging from 0.88 to 0.93 for predicting head CT need, ICU admission, or hospitalization [40]. Compared with PECARN rules, machine learning–based Optimal Classification Tree (OCT) rules demonstrate higher predictive specificity in preliminary studies, potentially reducing unnecessary CT scans and radiation exposure [7]. However, these tools require prospective external validation, clinician acceptance, EHR integration, and resolution of ethical and medicolegal concerns before widespread deployment. Current evidence is insufficient to recommend AI tools as replacements for PECARN in routine clinical practice; they should be regarded as investigational adjuncts [41].

Rapid MRI protocols: How should imaging be performed?

Given the long-term malignancy concerns associated with ionizing radiation, non-ionizing imaging modalities - particularly rapid-sequence MRI (rMRI) - offer a promising radiation-free alternative. rMRI can typically be completed in 1–16 min (most < 6 min) [42] and often does not require sedation, making it a feasible option in many emergency settings. Prospective cohort studies show that rMRI reduces radiation exposure and maintains adequate sensitivity for ciTBI, though its sensitivity for isolated skull fractures and small subarachnoid haemorrhages is lower than CT [6, 36]. In a single-center retrospective study, implementing four rMRI protocols increased rMRI utilization from 10.8% to 38.5%, while head CT use decreased from 70% to 48.5%, reflecting a 20% relative reduction in CT utilization [43]. These data support making rMRI more widely available in pediatric emergency departments as a complementary imaging strategy. At present, rMRI is best considered an adjunct or alternative in specific clinical contexts rather than a universal replacement for CT [42, 44].

Economic impact: AI triage and CT utilization

The transition to AI-driven triage can address key challenges in emergency departments, including overcrowding, prolonged waiting times, inconsistent patient prioritization, and inefficient resource allocation. AI-driven systems reduce treatment delays, decrease time-to-treatment by up to 20% and enabling faster interventions in critical cases [45]. Unlike conventional triage - which may be influenced by clinician experience and workload - AI relies on objective criteria and standardized protocols, potentially providing more consistent patient assessments. However, these advantages have been demonstrated primarily in single-center or modeling studies and require prospective multi-center validation before generalization [7, 45]. In diagnostic imaging, AI modalities such as OCT have demonstrated the potential to reduce unnecessary CT scans in preliminary studies; cost modeling suggests potential savings, though real-world implementation costs remain uncertain [7, 45].

Implementation in ED workflow (shared decision, observation, education)

Observation units

Observation of pediatric patients for 4–6 h with minor head trauma, prior to CT decision-making could decrease the number of unnecessary CT scans performed [15, 28, 29]. Nigrovic et al. reported that out of 42,412 children included in the study, clinicians noted that the CT rate was lower among the observed group (30%) compared with non-observed children (35%) [15]. Similarly, a Japanese study evaluated the impact of introducing an observation unit (OU) on CT use among children. The introduction of OU led to a 30% decrease in CT use [28]. In another cohort by Singh et al., conducted on 18,471 children < 18 years, it was noted that planned observation reduced cranial CT use, even in settings with low scan rates. The unadjusted CT rate was 4.4% in the observation group versus 10.1% in those without planned observation. The results demonstrated that planned ED observation reduced the adjusted odds of CT use by 80% [29].

Parent communication: Decision aids

Parents’ decisions regarding head CT for their child are influenced by prior experience, knowledge, emotional state, and trust in the clinician, all of which depend on effective communication [8]. Hess et al. developed the decision aid Head CT Choice to support shared decision-making in children at intermediate risk for ciTBI. In their study of 971 children (493 using the decision aid, 478 receiving usual care), parents using the aid demonstrated better knowledge, reduced decisional conflict, and more active involvement in CT decisions. Initial ED CT rates were similar (22% vs. 24%), but fewer imaging tests were performed within 7 days in the decision aid group, with no missed ciTBI cases [8]. Skains et al. observed that parents presenting with head trauma often experience elevated anxiety, which can be mitigated through shared decision-making, especially among socioeconomically disadvantaged families [29].

Beyond the acute ED encounter, community-level awareness remains a critical gap; concussion knowledge among middle school-aged children and their caregivers has been shown to be suboptimal, highlighting the need for targeted educational interventions to improve early symptom recognition and appropriate care-seeking behavior [46]. Another prospective study highlighted that adequate information, satisfaction with care, and cranial imaging assessment significantly reduce parental anxiety and perceived risk of brain injury [30]. Systems integration: EHR decision-support

Clinical prediction rules provide structured, evidence-based tools to estimate TBI risk and support clinician decision-making [15]. Successful implementation requires informatics support to ensure compatibility and usability within standard workflows [47]. Atabaki et al. demonstrated that integrating the PECARN rule into an electronic health record (EHR) reduced CT use from 27% to 19% without increasing missed TBIs, ICU admissions, or 7-day hospital visits [48]. Similarly, Ballard et al. implemented a clinical decision support system in the EHR to deliver ciTBI risk assessments and emphasize observation over CT for minor trauma with a single intermediate PECARN predictor. Among 3,859 children, CT rates decreased from 24.2% to 21.6% [49]. A pilot study evaluating a PECARN-based clinical decision support tool combined with structured parent discussions showed reduced CT use without missing clinically important TBI, while improving parental understanding of risks and alternatives. These findings highlight the effectiveness of integrating evidence-based prediction rules with EHR-supported decision tools to optimize imaging decisions and patient safety in pediatric head trauma [50].

Education & audit: Provider feedback loops sustain long-term CT reduction

Implementation of quality improvement (QI) programs enhances information accuracy and accessibility, reducing errors in head CT indications and improving patient care. Elmoheen et al. conducted a one-month audit of 320 pediatric head trauma patients, 63 of whom underwent CT. Review revealed that approximately 45% of CTs lacked clear guideline-based indications, and over a third of cases had incomplete clinical documentation, highlighting overuse and gaps in record-keeping [51]. To reduce CT use in PECARN intermediate-risk patients, Arora et al. implemented a comprehensive QI approach combining provider education, decision support tools, and individualized performance feedback. Among 1,535 eligible children, CT use decreased from 18.5% to 13.9% [47]. Similarly, across three community hospitals, a QI program led by pediatric clinicians reduced CT rates from 18% to 13% in 3,215 minor head trauma visits, representing a 28% relative reduction [52]. These findings emphasize the importance of optimized workflows, guideline-based assessment, and consistent adherence to internationally recognized standards to avoid unnecessary CT imaging in pediatric head trauma [51].

Future directions & research agenda

Future research should address critical gaps in imaging, decision-support, and equitable access to safe evaluation pathways for pediatric head injuries in the emergency setting. AI-driven triage systems remain investigational; current limitations include variable accuracy across hospitals and patient populations, limited prospective multi-center validation, and inconsistent clinician acceptance. Additional concerns include algorithmic bias, lack of transparency, potential over-reliance on automated decisions, and unresolved medicolegal accountability issues [41]. Prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to evaluate AI-driven triage systems against clinician-only assessments across diverse age groups before these tools can be responsibly recommended for widespread clinical use.

Rapid MRI (rMRI) is another promising tool for pediatric head trauma assessment. While CT remains the diagnostic standard, it exposes children to ionizing radiation, creating long-term safety concerns. Fast MRI offers a radiation-free alternative that can be completed rapidly, even in emergency settings. Prospective cohort studies show that rMRI reduces radiation exposure, typically does not require sedation, and can be completed in approximately 1–16 min [6, 44]. Although sensitivity does not reach 0.95, most missed injuries are isolated skull fractures that rarely require intervention. rMRI may also detect extra-axial injuries missed by CT. In a single-center retrospective study, implementing four rMRI protocols increased rMRI utilization from 10.8% to 38.5%, while head CT use decreased from 70% to 48.5%, reflecting a 20% reduction in CT utilization [43]. These data support making rMRI widely available in pediatric emergency departments to provide fast, accurate, and radiation-free imaging.

The PECARN clinical prediction rules identify children at very low risk for clinically important traumatic brain injury, guiding decisions about when CT is unnecessary. Age-specific and risk factor–based, these rules have been prospectively validated in large cohorts, showing high sensitivity and confirming that clinically important injuries are rarely missed [53]. Standardized global implementation of PECARN rules would reduce unnecessary imaging, improve patient safety, and create a harmonized framework for pediatric trauma evaluation.

Equitable access remains a critical concern. U.S. studies show that low-volume hospitals (< 10,000 visits/year) are less likely to have on-site MRI, rely on mobile units, and often use lower-resolution CT scanners, with a 46% higher likelihood of having only 1–4-slice machines [54]. Ensuring equity requires targeted financial support and incentives to enable low-volume or rural hospitals to adopt advanced imaging and AI-assisted tools, thereby providing high-quality, timely care regardless of location.

Optimizing pediatric head trauma evaluation requires several key steps prioritized by current evidence. First and most immediately actionable: established, validated clinical risk prediction tools (e.g., PECARN rules) should be universally applied to determine which children need imaging, minimizing unnecessary radiation exposure and supporting structured observation pathways for eligible patients. Second, where available, rapid MRI can be used as a safe, radiation-free alternative to CT for selected patients, reducing cumulative radiation exposure while maintaining diagnostic accuracy for most clinically important injuries. Third, educating parents is important - providing clear information about why imaging is needed, its risks, and alternatives empowers shared decision-making. Fourth, large-scale prospective cohort studies on AI-driven triage should be prioritized; once rigorously validated, such tools may support real-time decision-making and further reduce unnecessary imaging. Conclusion

Past trends

From 2002 to 2006, pediatric traumatic brain injury (TBI) imposed a substantial burden on emergency departments, resulting in 473,947 visits, 35,136 hospitalizations, and over 2,000 deaths. During this period, computed tomography (CT) was the primary diagnostic tool for head trauma in children. Growing awareness of the long-term radiation risks of CT raised concerns about overuse. Although the PECARN clinical decision rule was introduced to reduce unnecessary imaging, national data from 2007 to 2015 indicate that CT utilization did not meaningfully decline, highlighting gaps in quality improvement efforts, including caregiver communication, technology integration, and adherence to radiation-conscious imaging strategies.

Current practices

By 2020, pediatric TBI evaluation had evolved to incorporate refined PECARN-based risk stratification, classifying patients as high-, intermediate-, or very low-risk. High-risk children require CT, intermediate-risk children benefit from structured observation and shared decision-making with caregivers, and very low-risk children can often be safely discharged without imaging. Although the PECARN rule reflects current best practice, it still yields CT utilization rates of ~ 35% in pediatric head injury. This raises the question of further optimization.

Practice shift

Management of pediatric head trauma is increasingly grounded in informed shared decision-making, risk stratification with proven clinical decision rules, and consideration of alternative imaging methods. The combined application of PECARN, CATCH, and CHALICE supports a stronger assessment framework. Shared decision-making encourages caregiver involvement, particularly in observation-based management. Newer modalities such as rMRI demonstrate adequate diagnostic accuracy for many relevant injuries while minimizing or eliminating radiation exposure. Emerging AI-based systems have shown preliminary potential to reduce CT utilization; however, their role remains investigational. The integration of validated clinical decision rules, observation pathways, rMRI, emerging AI tools, and transparent communication collectively reflects a modern, patient-centered evolution in emergency care.

Future directions

Next-generation diagnostic strategies, including machine learning–based triage systems and ultra-fast MRI protocols, represent promising future directions for pediatric TBI care. However, their clinical implementation requires rigorous prospective validation in diverse multi-center settings before they can be broadly recommended. Refinements of clinical decision rules incorporating injury location and anatomical data may enhance observation strategies and safely reduce radiation exposure. Continued development of caregiver decision aids, clinician education, and expanded access to on-site rMRI and validated AI platforms are essential for equitable, safe, and efficient care. Adherence to PECARN-guided evaluation remains the cornerstone of safer, evidence-based, and patient-centered management.
