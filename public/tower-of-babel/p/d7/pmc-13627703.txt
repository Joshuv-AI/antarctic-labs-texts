Skin acetone as a clinical diagnostic biomarker of ketosis
Authors: Anh Ngan Nguyen, Oscar Osorio-Perez, David Jackemeyer, Eliana Terrera, Shaun Victor, Ayushi Pradhan, Fang Chen, Rodrigo Ezequiel Dominguez, Yuyan Hua, Charlotte Chou, Shan Cheng, Erica Forzani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/ObjectiveWe present the first receiver operating characteristic (ROC) diagnostic analysis of skin-excreted acetone as a biomarker of ketosis.Participants/MethodsIn an 8-week controlled crossover pilot study (n = 16), we evaluated the ability of skin-emitted acetone to differentiate metabolic ketosis from non-ketosis states. Non-ketosis was established via a normal diet/energy balance, while ketosis was induced through dietary and energy manipulations, including ketogenic diets and negative energy balances. Alongside skin acetone concentration and excretion rate, we quantified a comprehensive set of ketone body parameters for comparative diagnostic purposes, including breath acetone concentration and excretion rate, blood beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration, urine beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration and excretion rate, and urine acetoacetate concentration and excretion rate. Blood BHB (mM) served as the clinical benchmark for ketosis.ResultsROC curves were generated to evaluate the diagnostic performance of each biomarker in distinguishing between ketosis and non-ketosis. Both breath and skin acetone excretion rates achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.83, outperforming several other ketone biomarkers. Similarly, breath acetone and skin acetone concentrations also exhibited AUCs of 0.82 and 0.83, respectively. In comparison, blood beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration and urine acetoacetate excretion rate both showed an AUC of 0.81, while urine beta-hydroxybutyrate excretion rate and concentration achieved AUCs of 0.66 and 0.68, respectively. Furthermore, breath and skin acetone excretion rates were systematically compared within and across participants. The correlation between the two reinforces the significance of skin acetone as a body ketone biomarker that diffuses through the skin, representing the body’s acetone concentrations.ConclusionsOverall, the results highlight the potential of skin acetone excretion rate as a reliable, non-invasive biomarker of ketosis, with diagnostic accuracy comparable to that of blood-based standards, offering significant promise for continuous, wearable metabolic monitoring in clinical and health settings.

Introduction

Ketosis is a metabolic state characterized by elevated levels of circulating ketone bodies, typically defined as blood beta-hydroxybutyrate concentrations exceeding 0.5 mM [1]. This metabolic state arises as a result of accelerated fat oxidation, which stimulates the production of ketone bodies—acetoacetate, beta-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone—through a process known as ketogenesis. Acetoacetate is the primary enzymatic product, which is subsequently reduced to beta-hydroxybutyrate or undergoes spontaneous decarboxylation to form acetone. Over the years, various strategies to induce ketosis have garnered significant attention for their potential roles in weight management [1, 2], enhancing healthy aging [3, 4], optimizing human performance [5], and treating conditions such as epilepsy [6] and diabetic kidney disease [3, 7]. Given this therapeutic breadth, reliable and real-time monitoring of ketosis is essential to ensure adherence, assess efficacy, and enable personalized metabolic feedback. However, transitioning from laboratory research to practical application depends on validating the diagnostic accuracy of non-invasive biomarkers against established clinical standards. This validation is a prerequisite for developing advanced wearable technologies capable of capturing individual metabolic responses to dietary and physical activity interventions [8, 9].

Ketosis biomarkers are traditionally measured through the detection of ketone bodies in blood (beta-hydroxybutyrate)[10], urine (acetoacetate), or breath (acetone)[11]. While blood and urine ketones have been widely used for ketosis detection via finger-prick blood analyzers and urine test strips [12], these methods are discrete, invasive, or inconvenient for continuous monitoring. Breath acetone has emerged over the past decade as a preferred non-invasive biomarker for early ketosis detection [7, 11, 13–16]. Notably, studies have shown that breath acetone can detect increases in ketone levels earlier than blood or urine analyses, underscoring its potential as an ideal biomarker for identifying the early activation of lipid metabolism [11, 14]. Additionally, breath acetone has proven valuable for public health applications, such as screening undiagnosed cases of type 1 diabetes and ketosis during community health events [17]. Compared to urine acetoacetate [16] and blood beta-hydroxybutyrate [14], breath acetone has demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy, further solidifying its value in assessing ketosis stages. Despite these advantages, breath acetone measurement has a fundamental practical limitation: it requires active, cooperative exhalation into a collection device, making continuous or unobtrusive monitoring difficult to implement in wearable devices, during sleep, or in populations with compromised respiratory capacity. By contrast, acetone emitted from human skin (skin acetone), once present in the cutaneous vasculature, diffuses passively through the dermis and epidermis, driven by the concentration gradient established between blood vessels in the skin and the external environment (Fig. 1B). According to Fick’s first law of diffusion [18] and our observations, the skin acetone concentration (Cs) and its excretion rate (i.e., the amount of acetone accumulated over a defined period on a specific skin area) are directly proportional to the blood acetone concentration (CB) [19, 20]. This passive process requires no subject participation and occurs naturally at a peripheral surface, making it ideal for wearable sensor integration. Moreover, because transdermal acetone flux is primarily governed by blood acetone concentration and skin diffusion properties — rather than ventilation rate, tidal volume, or respiratory patterns — it offers a more stable and continuously accessible window into systemic ketone metabolism. Detailed analyses of systemic acetone production and the blood-to-skin diffusion modeling used to determine physiological time lags are provided in the Supporting Information. This includes the mechanistic basis of the skin acetone time lag (Section S1) and a diffusion model analysis (Section S2). To capture skin-emitted acetone for quantitative analysis under controlled, leak-free conditions, we developed a custom passive headspace sampler placed directly on the inner wrist (Fig. 1C). The device defines a fixed skin-exposure area and headspace volume, enabling reproducible measurements that approximate the form factor of a future wearable.

The biological relationship between exhaled breath and skin acetone has been progressively established across multiple studies. Naitoh et al. (2002) were among the first to demonstrate this link, reporting a correlation factor of 0.81 [21]. Yamai et al. (2009)[22] further confirmed this during cycle exercise, finding that skin-gas acetone concentration was significantly correlated with expired air (r = 0.752, p < 0.01). Building on this foundation, Yamada et al. (2015)[23] advanced the field toward practical wearability by demonstrating that transdermal acetone could be detected at the wrist using a zeolite-based pre-concentrator paired with a semiconductor sensor, establishing skin acetone as a viable indicator of fat metabolism linked to diet and exercise. However, this approach remained limited to intermittent measurements and was challenged by mechanical deformation of the skin during movement. Most recently, Zhang et al. (2025)[24] showed continuous transdermal acetone monitoring at the external auditory canal — an anatomically stable, low-movement site — reporting a particularly strong correlation with exhaled breath (R = 0.95). Together, these findings trace a clear progression from foundational breath-skin correlations to skin-based wearable prototypes, and ultimately to more stable, continuous sensing platforms. Yet a fundamental clinical question remains: Can skin acetone accurately classify a person’s state of ketosis? Transitioning skin acetone from a known physiological signal to a reliable diagnostic biomarker requires rigorous validation through clinical metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, and formal Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis.

In this study, we address this translational gap by conducting the first comprehensive ROC-based diagnostic analysis of skin-excreted acetone as a biomarker for ketosis, benchmarked in a head-to-head comparison with established biomarkers: breath acetone concentration and excretion rate, blood beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration, urine beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration and excretion rate, and urine acetoacetate concentration and excretion rate (Fig. 1A). Utilizing a rigorously controlled 8-week crossover study across four distinct metabolic conditions, we evaluated the diagnostic performance and correlation of these markers at both the cohort and individual levels. By systematically characterizing sensitivity, specificity, and actionable diagnostic thresholds, this work transforms skin acetone from a physiologically interesting signal into a clinically characterized biomarker and establishes a translational foundation for its integration into wearable health monitoring devices and regulatory-grade metabolic screening protocols.

Methods and Procedures

Human subject study

We performed a systematic statistical analysis of ketone body profiles from 16 healthy participants (9 males and 7 females) [25]. The study spanned an 8-week duration and involved 4 combinations of diet type and caloric intake: 1- normal diet/energy balance (NDEB), 2- ketogenic diet/energy balance (KDEB), 3- normal diet/negative balance (NDNB), and 4- ketogenic diet/negative balance (KDNB). Sixteen participants were chosen based on a power calculation (0.80 power, 0.05 alpha) to detect an increase in ketones from two mean values differing by 20% in average [26].

Each dietary and energy condition lasted for 2 weeks. The first week was dedicated to the subject’s conditioning, and the second week was focused on assessment, with 3 appointments scheduled every other day for interventions of exercise, fasting, and meals. Study design: subjects’ conditions during the 8-week study.

At each intervention, the subject was assessed both before (pre-intervention) and after (post-intervention). Pre-intervention assessment conditions included 12 hours of overnight fasting. The exercise intervention included 1 hour of running on a treadmill at ~60-80% of the maximum heart rate, and the assessment was taken after 1 hour of finishing the exercise. The fasting intervention included an additional 8-hour fasting period on top of the 12-hour overnight fasting. The ketogenic meal intervention consisted of a single ketogenic meal, with caloric intake representing 25% of the subject’s measured REE. The assessment was conducted 2 hours after the meal completion. The same ketogenic meal was provided for breakfast on the last day of each condition period, specifically in weeks 2, 4, 6, and 8.

The normal diet comprised a macronutrient distribution of approximately 50-60% carbohydrates, 20-30% fat, and 10-20% protein. In contrast, the ketogenic diet (KD) consisted of 3-10% carbohydrates, 65-80% fat, and 20-30% protein. The energy balance condition entailed a caloric intake equal to each subject’s personal total energy expenditure [27]. The negative energy balance had a caloric intake of 500 kcal/day lower than the personal energy expenditure [28]. The personal total energy expenditures were assessed through indirect calorimetry (Medical Graphics Metabolic Cart, MN, or Breezing MedTM Wearable Metabolic Rate Analyzer, Phoenix, AZ, both FDA-cleared instruments) at the beginning and periodically throughout the study to ensure personalized dietary caloric intake (see details below in Description of the diet meals) [25].

We assessed the ketone bodies from different sources in the 16 healthy participants for pre- and post-exercise, pre- and post-fasting, and pre- and post-ketogenic single meal interventions performed at 1- normal diet/energy balance (NDEB), 2- ketogenic diet/energy balance (KDEB), 3- normal diet/negative balance (NDNB), and 4- ketogenic diet/negative balance (KDNB) conditions.

Participants’ physical characteristics

All participants were informed about the study, its design, and purpose. An informed consent form approved by the Institutional Review Board at Arizona State University (IRB protocol number: STUDY00016374) was obtained from each subject before the study. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

The sixteen healthy participants participating in the study included 9 males and 7 females. The participants’ mass and body composition were assessed with a 4-electrode bioimpedance scale (Omron, model HBF-514C, Kyoto, Japan). The subject’s height was assessed with a stadiometer (Seca, model 213, Hamburg, Germany). The subject’s body surface area was estimated based on principles of weight and height published elsewhere [27]. Physical activity levels (PAL) of the participants were classified based on reported frequency, duration, and type of physical activities, and were determined to be sedentary (low physical activity) for all the participants. The 16 participants of the study had an average BMI of 25 ± 4 kg/m2. More specifically, the males had a body mass index (BMI) average of 26.4 kg/m2 (SD: 4.3), and the females had a BMI average of 22.5 kg/m2 (SD: 3.9). The participants were between 20 and 60 years old. For males, body fat percentage was 23.9% (SD: 7.2%), muscle mass percentage was 36.9% (SD: 4.6%), and body surface area was 2.0 m2 (SD: 0.2 m2). For females, body fat percentage was 31.2% (SD: 9.9%), muscle mass percentage was 28.6% (SD: 4.3%), and body surface area was 1.7 m2 (SD: 0.1 m2).

None of the participants was on regular medication, nor had any history of respiratory disease or diabetes, which enabled comparison of ketone buildup capabilities under conditions of healthy acid-base balance and alveolar gas exchange [27].

Description of the diet meals

The calorie intake for each subject was determined based on Total Energy Expenditure (TEE), which was estimated from the measured Resting Energy Expenditure (REE) (Ultima CCMTM, Medical Graphics, Saint Paul, MN, USA; or Breezing Med Wearable Metabolic Rate Analyzer, Phoenix, AZ, USA) and the Sedentary Physical Activity Level coefficient (PAL) as TEE = REE × PAL [27].

The diet caloric intakes were determined at the beginning of week 1 (Normal Diet – Energy Balance condition), week 3 (Keto Diet – Energy Balance Condition), week 5 (Normal Diet, Negative Energy Balance), week 7 (Keto Diet, Negative Energy Balance), as well as multiple points in time during the study to ensure accuracy of the intake caloric condition [25].

Food from Healthy Choices (Conagra Food Services, Chicago, IL) and Factor75 (Factor75 LLC, Batavia, IL) was provided to the participants throughout the study to sustain a normal diet and KD, as well as to meet caloric intake goals. The allowed caloric intake was distributed across breakfast, lunch, dinner, and snacks. The participants were instructed to eat only the food provided, and they were followed by the study coordinator every few days to ensure they had everything they needed for dietary needs and to answer questions they might have. This procedure ensured adherence to caloric intake under the free-living conditions of the study [25].

Ketone bodies measurements

Ketone bodies were assessed according to methods previously described in the literature [25].

Urine acetoacetate concentration and excretion rate

The urine acetoacetate concentration in the collected volume of urine at the appointment was measured with urine strips (Fisherbrand, 10SG Urine Reagent Strips, Fisher HealthCare, Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Urine acetoacetate excretion rate was assessed by measuring the urine volumes excreted during the assessment appointment. The participants were instructed to empty their bladders at the beginning of the appointment, and the exact period of the excretion was timed when they urinated at the end of the appointment.

Skin acetone concentration and excretion rate

It was measured from the skin acetone concentration determined by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) (see below details of the method), taking into account the time required for the skin acetone to accumulate in the skin headspace for 45 minutes (refer to Section S3 for additional details on this rationale).

Breath acetone concentration and excretion rate

It was measured by determining breath acetone concentration using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) (see below for details of the method: Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry Method) and assessing the breath exhalation rate while collecting the breath sample in a Mylar bag, using an adapter connected to an indirect calorimetry system (Ultima CCMTM, Medical Graphics, Saint Paul, MN, USA or Breezing Med Wearable Metabolic Rate Analyzer, Phoenix, AZ, USA).

Blood beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration

Blood beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration (in mM) has been used as the historical clinical biomarker of ketosis [5, 27]. It was determined with a Precision Xtra blood ketometer (Abbott, USA). It should be noted that, in this study, the change in blood beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration between pre- and post-intervention was insufficient to evaluate changes in excretion rates, and, therefore, blood beta-hydroxybutyrate excretion rates are not used for comparison.

Urine beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration and excretion rate

It was assessed from the urine samples obtained during the assessment appointment using the same samples and collection method described for urine acetoacetate excretion rate. The urine beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration was assessed using a Beckman Coulter Instrument with the beta-hydroxybutyrate 21 FS assay (Vidan Diagnostics, LLC, 24123 Boerne Stage Rd, Ste 205, San Antonio, TX 78255 USA).

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry method

Overall method

Measurements from skin acetone and breath acetone were assessed using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) (Agilent, 6890 N Network GC System, 5973 Network Mass Selective Detector, USA) with a conventional gas chromatography method using a solid phase microextraction (SPME) fiber modified with hydroxylamine derivative [29].

Modification of the SPME fiber

We used SPME fiber assemblies with 65 um PDMS/DVB, fused silica 24Ga, and Manual Holder (Supelco, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The SPME fibers were modified with O-2,3,4,5,6-(Pentafluorobenzyl) hydroxylamine hydrochloride (PFBHA) (Sigma-Aldrich) in HPLC-grade deionized water (Sigma-Aldrich), which was initially adsorbed on the SPME fiber of polydimethylsiloxane–divinylbenzene (PDMS–DVB) for 10 minutes at room temperature, using a septum vial with 1 mL of the solution under stirring conditions.

Skin sample collection method

Skin acetone samples were collected from the inner wrist area, where skin thickness typically ranges from 1.1 to 2.1 mm [30] (Fig. 1B), using a custom-designed passive headspace sampler schematically illustrated in Fig. 1C. The sampler consists of a customized clean 5-mL borosilicate glass vial (Fisherbrand autosampler vials, Fisher Scientific, USA), with its base removed, sealed against the clean skin by a 3D-printed holder and elastic fabric band that together define a fixed skin exposure area of 4.42 cm² and a fixed headspace volume. A PTFE/silicone septum at the top of the vial allows insertion of a PFBHA-modified SPME fiber for sample collection. All sampler components (glass-holder-elastic band system) were degassed overnight at 100 °C (glass vial) and 50 °C (holder and elastic band) before use.

Immediately before each collection, the inner-wrist skin was cleaned with a deionized water-impregnated wipe (Kimwipe, Kimberly-Clark®) and dried with a fresh Kimwipe. The sealed sampler was then secured to the skin to prevent leakage of excreted acetone and its headspace purged with ultra-high-purity nitrogen (Matheson, NJ, USA) for 1 minute. Skin-excreted acetone was accumulated in the headspace for a total period of 45 minutes. The PFBHA-modified SPME fiber was inserted through the septum at the top of a 5-ml glass vial during the final 15 minutes of the 45-minute sample-collection period.

Breath sample collection

The collection of breath acetone required the subject to breathe into a 40 L aluminum Mylar bag for ~10 minutes while the exhalation rate was measured by the indirect calorimeter. The modified SPME fiber was then exposed in the bag through the septum for 5 minutes for acetone collection, and it was quantitatively analyzed by GC-MS as described for skin acetone.

Results and Discussion

Skin acetone diagnostic accuracy of ketosis

To define clinical stages of true ketosis and no ketosis, we apply the criteria described as follows. We defined clinical ketosis as the metabolic state in which ketone levels are elevated due to accelerated fat oxidation [1, 31]. Therefore, a ketosis state was defined for the participants under Negative Energy Balance (NB) conditions, e.g., participants experiencing a caloric deficit of approximately 500 kcal/day relative to their personalized total energy expenditure [32]. According to the First Principle of Thermodynamics [28], under NB, the body utilizes its tissues, including adipose tissue, as an energy source, leading to increased fat oxidation and, consequently, ketosis [7, 8]. The NB was created for either a Normal Diet (ND) or a Ketogenic Diet (KD).

Additionally, the ketosis state was also defined when participants followed the Ketogenic Diet under Energy Balance (EB) conditions. This is because the high-fat content of the KD provided a significant amount of fat as an energy source, promoting accelerated fat oxidation and ketone production [33, 34].

In contrast, the no-ketosis state was defined as the condition where participants were in Energy Balance (EB) while following a Normal Diet (ND) [28]. Under these conditions, caloric intake matched total energy expenditure, and no significant fat oxidation and ketone production was expected [27].

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of various ketone biomarkers in differentiating between ketosis and non-ketosis states, we generated Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves [35]. Each ketone measurement was treated as a continuous test variable, while the reference classification of ketosis (true positive) or no-ketosis (true negative) was based on the dietary and energy balance conditions described above. ROC curves were generated by plotting sensitivity (true positive rate) against 1-specificity (false positive rate) across multiple thresholds for each biomarker. Sensitivity was defined as true positives/(true positives + false negatives), and specificity as true negatives/(true negatives + false positives). A data point was considered a true positive if it correctly classified a participant as being in ketosis (e.g., NDNB, KDEB, or KDNB) or a false positive if it incorrectly labeled a no-ketosis condition (NDEB) as ketosis. To determine the optimal threshold for each biomarker, we selected the point on the ROC curve closest to the ideal classifier (0, 1; e.g., 100% specificity and 100% sensitivity) [35]. This method identifies the threshold that simultaneously maximizes sensitivity and specificity by minimizing the Euclidean distance to (0,1) on the ROC plot [35]. This threshold was then used to define the concentration limit from which diagnostic classification of ketosis will be applied.

The ROC analysis was performed for each measured ketone (breath acetone, skin acetone, urine beta-hydroxybutyrate, urine acetoacetate, and blood beta-hydroxybutyrate) using both excretion rates and concentration units. Figure 3A and B illustrate the corresponding ROC curves. On these plots, the y-axis represents the true positive rate (sensitivity), and the x-axis represents the false positive rate (1-specificity) [35]. The Area Under the Curve (AUC) of each ROC curve was used to quantify the overall diagnostic ability of the biomarker, reflecting how well it discriminates between ketosis and no ketosis, independent of any particular threshold [35]. A higher AUC value (closer to 1) indicates better discrimination between ketosis and non-ketosis states. From Fig. 3A and Table 1A, it can be observed that breath acetone excretion rate and skin acetone excretion rate achieved the highest AUC of 0.83 among all the ketone biomarkers, including urine beta-hydroxybutyrate and urine acetoacetate excretion rate, and blood beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration, which is the commonly used ketosis biomarker [36, 37]. Notably, breath acetone excretion rate and skin acetone excretion rate performed equivalently to the blood beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration, which exhibited an AUC of 0.81 in this study. The urine acetoacetate excretion rate demonstrated an AUC of 0.81, comparable to the blood beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration, while the urine beta-hydroxybutyrate excretion rate exhibited lower diagnostic performance than other ketone biomarkers, with an AUC of 0.66.

Similar trends were observed in the comparison of ketone biomarker concentrations. From Fig. 3B and Table 1B, it can be seen that breath acetone concentration and skin acetone concentration also performed well, with AUCs of 0.82 and 0.83, respectively. In comparison, blood beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration and urine acetoacetate concentration showed AUCs of 0.81, while urine beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration achieved an AUC of 0.68.

Overall, the results demonstrate that breath acetone and skin acetone, whether measured as excretion rates or concentrations, consistently achieved the highest AUC values among the ketone biomarkers analyzed. Breath acetone has already been FDA-approved as a biomarker for fat oxidation [15, 38] and is widely used to assess an individual’s capacity to oxidize fat [1, 2]. However, skin acetone has not been previously characterized as a biomarker for ketosis. This study demonstrated the potential of skin acetone measurements as reliable, non-invasive indicators for identifying ketosis.

Building on these findings, we further examined the correlation between breath acetone and skin acetone to evaluate the feasibility of using skin acetone as a non-invasive diagnostic tool for ketosis.

A key methodological difference between this study and those by Naitoh et al. [21] and Yamai et al. [22] lies in how skin acetone is measured. In our study, skin acetone concentration was quantified using GC-MS with a SPME fiber, which allows quantification of acetone-derived oximes with lower vapor pressures than acetone, thereby enabling control of acetone quantification. In addition, we captured acetone from a well-defined volume on the skin surface using an inert material—a customized, clean 5-mL glass vial with a septum (Fisherbrand autosampler vials, Fisher Scientific, USA). Finally, we secured the vial with a custom-made 3D-printed support, which enabled us to firmly attach it to the cleaned skin surface (Fig. 1C) using an elastic band (illustrated in green). This setup enabled the collection of a well-defined headspace volume and a consistent skin exposure area, enhancing both the precision and reproducibility of the measurements. On the other hand, Naitoh et al. [21] used a homemade polyacrylic plate attached to the skin with a continuous nitrogen stream for collection in a cold trap. Meanwhile, Yamai et al. [22] employed a flexible gas-collection bag pressed against the skin. Despite differences in methodology and measurement conditions, all three studies support the conclusion that skin acetone levels are associated with systemic ketone metabolism, demonstrating the relationship between skin and breath acetone. Our study, to our knowledge, is the first to evaluate and analyze skin acetone for its clinical diagnostic value in ketosis and to compare it with other ketone biomarkers.

Limitations and future scope

Beyond systemic concentration, the physical properties of the skin barrier also significantly influence the acetone excretion rate. Parameters such as skin barrier function—quantified by water vapor transmission rate (WVTR)—and the lipid content of the Stratum Corneum dictate the permeability of the skin to small polar molecules. In this study, inter-subject differences in skin permeability may account for some of the variance in the correlation between skin and breath acetone. Incorporating individual skin characteristic assessments into future wearable platforms could provide a ‘personalized calibration’ that further enhances the diagnostic sensitivity of skin-excreted biomarkers. Therefore, future work should prioritize: (1) development of leak-resistant, wearable-compatible skin acetone sampling devices to address the collection variability observed in 6 of 16 participants; (2) investigation of the physiological drivers of inter-individual slope variability in the skin–breath acetone relationship; and (3) validation of the derived diagnostic thresholds in larger, more demographically diverse cohorts, including populations with obesity, type 2 diabetes, and other metabolic conditions for which ketosis monitoring is clinically relevant.

Conclusions

This study establishes, for the first time, that skin acetone measured by passive diffusion achieves diagnostic accuracy equivalent to the established clinical biomarker blood beta-hydroxybutyrate (AUC 0.83 vs. 0.81) and superior to urine ketone biomarkers, while requiring no blood draw, active exhalation, or subject cooperation. By conducting the first formal ROC diagnostic analysis of skin acetone and benchmarking it simultaneously against all established ketone biomarkers in a rigorously controlled 8-week crossover study, this work advances skin acetone to a clinically characterized diagnostic tool. The above-mentioned findings mark an important step toward expanding the use of skin acetone for monitoring fat oxidation metabolism via passive sampling methods and provide potential opportunities for continuous ketone monitoring.
