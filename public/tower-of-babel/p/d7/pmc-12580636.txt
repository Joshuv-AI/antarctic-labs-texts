Ursodeoxycholic Acid Attenuates B Cell Susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 Spike Protein by Interfering Its Binding to ACE2
Authors: So-Jeong Park, Eun-Yi Moon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractB cells are essential for the defense against various infectious agents including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-Cov-2) causing coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). COVID-19 is caused by interaction of the spike protein (SP) with the receptor-binding domain (RBD) and its receptor, angiotensin converting enzyme 2 (ACE2). Bisphenol A (BPA), a plasticizer and endocrine-disrupting chemical, can enter the human body through several exposure routes. Previously, we reported human B cell death by BPA treatment via autophagy induction. Here, we investigated whether the exposure to BPA affects B cell susceptibility to SP of COVID-19 and how to interfere the interaction of SP and ACE2. We observed an increase in ACE2 gene expression in human B cells by BPA treatment and more SP binding in BPA-treated B cells. Our data also showed more B cell death accompanying increased autophagic puncta count and lysosomal intracellular activity by co-treatment with BPA and SP compared to those in BPA treatment alone. Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) reduced SP binding in B cells in BPA-exposed B cells. UDCA treatment also inhibited B cell death and lysosomal enzyme activity which were enhanced by co-treatment of BPA and SP. Taken together, results demonstrate that BPA-exposed B cells are more susceptible to COVID-19. It also suggests that UDCA could be protective to SP-responding B cells exposed to BPA.Keywords: Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), SARS-CoV-2 spike protein (SP), ACE2, B cell

INTRODUCTION

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-Cov-2), a novel beta coronavirus, emerged in late 2019 and causes coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID 19), which rapidly escalated into a global pandemic with substantial morbidity and mortality (Masters-Waage et al., 2020; Rodal Canales et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2020). The interaction of SARS-CoV-2 spike protein (SP) and angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) has been found to be a major determinant of virus replication and disease severity (Hoffmann et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2020). Electron microscopy shows that the knobs at the wider end of SP trimers are decorated by ACE2 receptor (Li et al., 2006). SP, particularly its receptor-binding domain (RBD), facilitates host cell entry by binding to ACE2 cell surface receptor (Hoffmann et al., 2020; Li et al., 2006), and the serine protease TMPRSS2 for SP priming (Hoffmann et al., 2020; Xiao and Dimitrov, 2004).

B cells are pivotal for humoral immunity to produce antibodies through terminal differentiation into plasma cells and release various cytokines (DiLillo et al., 2011; Geahlen, 2014; Kalampokis et al., 2013). Then, B cells play a critical role in defense against SARS-Cov-2 (Mustajab et al., 2025). B cells also play key roles in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus (Youinou et al., 2009). SP affects the release of proinflammatory cytokines and a subsequent downregulation of immune capability (Halajian et al., 2022). However, it has not been clarified about whether B cells may also act as targets for viral proteins and undergo functional impairment.

Bisphenol A (BPA), a widely used endocrine-disrupting chemical, is present ubiquitously in plastics and food-contact materials, and human exposure is nearly universal (Zahra et al., 2020). BPA interferes with estrogen receptors, modulates oxidative stress, and has been implicated in metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, and immune dysregulation (Jang et al., 2020; Zahra et al., 2020). BPA also enhances autoantibody production by B cells both in vitro and in vivo (Yurino et al., 2004) by augmenting immune responses, especially Th1 responses (Yoshino et al., 2003). Our previous studies demonstrated that BPA exposure induces autophagy and disrupts autophagic flux in human B cells via Nrf2-mediated upregulation of Atg7 and Beclin1, subsequently triggering B cell death (Park et al., 2023). Autophagy involves autophagosome formation followed by fusion with lysosomes to form autophagolysosomes in which autophagic cargo is degraded (Marino et al., 2014; Yang and Klionsky, 2010). Lysosomes play an indispensable role in different types of autophagy and various cell death pathways (Mahapatra et al., 2021). However, the physiological effects of SP on BPA-exposed B cells remain poorly understood.

Ursodeoxycholic acid (3α, 7β-dihydroxy-5β-cholanic acid, UDCA), a secondary bile acid traditionally used for cholestatic liver diseases, is known for its immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic properties (Hagey et al., 1993). UDCA is valuable for the remediation of hepato-cholestatic disorders (Paumgartner and Beuers, 2002) and it is also pathophysiologically effective in non-hepatic models including airway inflammation (Miyaguchi and Mori, 2005; Willart et al., 2012), cystic fibrosis lung disease (Mroz and Harvey, 2019), colorectal carcinoma (Kim et al., 2017), and Parkinsonism (Abdelkader et al., 2016). In observational clinical analyses, UDCA use was associated with reduced risk of COVID-19-related hospitalization and mortality, suggesting potential prophylactic or therapeutic benefits (Costello et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2024). UDCA may inhibit the entry of SARS-CoV-2 into the host cell (Rodal Canales et al., 2021) through reduced RBD–ACE2 binding (Carino et al., 2020). Recent studies have shown that UDCA can interfere with the SP–ACE2 interaction in bronchial epithelial cells (Thuy et al., 2022). Despite accumulating evidence on effects of UDCA in epithelial and hepatic cells, its role in B cells remain undefined yet.

In these studies, we investigate whether UDCA can play a role in BPA-exposed B cell death following damage caused by SARS-CoV-2 SP by using WiL2-NS human B lymphoblast cells. Our results may reveal novel mechanisms underpinning B cell susceptibility to SARS-Cov-2. It suggests actionable strategies for immune preservation under environmental stress by identifying the interplay between B cell death by BPA exposure, interaction of host ACE2 and viral ligand SP, and therapeutic modulation of UDCA.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Reagents

A biotinylated SARS-Cov-2 (2019-nCoV) spike protein, RBD (40592-V08B-B) were purchased from Sino Biological Inc. (Wayne, PA, USA). Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA, 15121) was purchased from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA). CellTiter-Glo® Luminescent cell viability assay kit (G9241) was purchased from Promega Co. (Madison, WI, USA). Antibody to ACE2 (4355S) were obtained from Cell signaling Technology (Beverly, MA USA). Antibody which is reactive with LC3 was obtained from Novus Biologicals (Centennial, CO, USA). Antibodies to β-tubulin were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCF-DA, D399) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc (Waltham, MA USA). Assay kit for lysosomal intracellular enzyme activity (ab234622) was purchased from Abcam Inc (Waltham, MA, USA). Except where indicated, all other materials are obtained from the Sigma Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA).

pEGFP-C3 plasmid was kindly provided by Prof. Mi-Ock Lee, College of Pharmacy, Seoul National University (Seoul, Korea). pEGFP-LC3 plasmid was kindly provided by Prof. Dong-Hyung Cho, Kyungpook National University (Daegu, Korea).

Cell culture

WiL2-NS (ATCC, CRL-8155), human B lymphoblast cells was acquired from the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology (KRIBB) cell bank (Taejeon, Korea). Cells were incubated with the method reported previously (Jang et al., 2020). Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium with supplement of 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (GIBCO, Grand Island, NY, USA), 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 units/mL penicillin and streptomycin (GIBCO). Cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 maintenance.

Cytotoxicity assay

Cell viability was assessed by trypan blue exclusion assay, which was described in previous report (Park et al., 2023).

Measurement of LC3 puncta

Autophagy was determined by the observation of LC3 puncta-positive structures, which are the essential dynamic process in autophagosome formation (Wu et al., 2015; Yao et al., 2017). LC3 puncta-positive structures were measured by the method as previously reported (Park et al., 2023).

Measurement of lysosomal intracellular activity

Lysosomal intracellular activity was measured by modified instructor’s manual (ab234622) from Abcam Inc, in which the fluorescence signal, generated by substrate degradation, is proportional to the intracellular lysosomal activity. Briefly, 200 μL of 3.2×104 WIL2-NS cell suspension was plated into 96-well black culture plate and incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 incubator overnight. Then, self-quenched substrate was added and incubated for additional 1 h. Then, the fluorescence was measured with emission at 500-550 nm following excitation at 475 nm by using GlomaxⓇ luminometer (Promega Co.).

Measurement of SARS-CoV-2 spike protein−ACE2 interactions

Inhibitory effect of UDCA on SARS-CoV-2 spike protein (SP)−ACE2 interactions was measured by a previous method modified (Thuy et al., 2022). Briefly, 5×105 WiL2-NS cells were suspended in 2% FCS containing Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) and incubated with UDCA at 37°C CO2 incubator for 1 h. Then, 25 ng/mL of a biotinylated SARS-CoV-2 (2019-nCoV) SP were added and incubated for an additional 1 h on ice. After washing cells with HBSS containing 2% FBS twice, cells were incubated with PE-conjugated streptavidin for 30 min on ice. Cells were washed twice and suspended with HBSS containing 2% FBS. Then, 10,000 cells were evaluated by CELLQuestTM software in FACScaliburTM (Becton Dickinson, San Jose, CA). SP binding rates on ACE2 in Beas-2B cells were analyzed by using FlowJoTM software (ver. 10.8.0) (BD Biosciences, Ashland, OR, USA).

Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted by using NucleoZol reagent (MACHEREY-NAGEL GmbH & Co., Duren, Germany). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of isolated total RNA, oligo-dT18, and superscript reverse transcriptase (Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea) in a final volume of 20 μL. For standard PCR, 1 μL of template cDNA was amplified with Taq DNA polymerase. PCR amplification was performed with 25-35 thermocycles for 30 s at 95°C, 30 s at 55°C, and 60 s at 72°C using human (h) oligonucleotide primers specific for human ACE2 (sense: 5’-cat tgg agc aag tgt tgg atc tt-3’; anti-sense: 5’-gag cta atg cat gcc att ctc a-3’) (Tao et al., 2016), and β-actin (sense: 5’-gtc acc aac tgg gac gac at-3; anti-sense: 5’-gca cag cct gga tag caa cg-3’). Amplified PCR products were separated by 1.0-1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis and detected on Ugenius 3® gel documentation system (Syngene, Cambridge, UK) (Lee et al., 2019).

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer (Triton X-100,) containing protease inhibitor (2 μg/mL aprotinin, 1 μM pepstatin, 1 μg/mL leupeptin, 1 mM phenylmethylsufonyl fluoride (PMSF), 5 mM sodium fluoride (NaF) and 1 mM sodium orthovanadate (Na3VO4)). The protein concentration of the sample was measured using SMARTTM BCA protein assay kit (Pierce 23228) from iNtRON Biotech. Inc. (Seoul, Korea). Same amount of heat-denatured protein in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer was separated in sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and then transferred to nitrocellulose membrane by using electro blotter. Equal amount of loaded sample on membrane was verified by ponceau S staining. The membrane was incubated with blocking solution (5% non-fat skim milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST)), and then followed by incubation with the specific primary antibodies. Horse radish peroxidase (HRP)- or infrared (IR) fluorescence dye-conjugated secondary antibody were used for target-specific primary antibody. Immuno-reactive target bands were visualized by the reaction with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL-PS250) (Dongin LS, Seoul, Korea) on X-ray film (Agfa HealthCare, Seoul, Korea) or by the detection of IRdye with Odyssey CLx Infrared Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, Germany), respectively (Lee et al., 2019).

Statistical analysis

Experimental differences were verified for statistical significance using ANOVA and student’s t-test. P value of <0.05 and <0.01 was considered to be significant.

RESULTS

SARS-CoV-2 SP bound to WiL2-NS cells

SARS-CoV-2 SP, particularly its receptor-binding domain (RBD), employs ACE2 cell surface receptor for cellular entry (Hoffmann et al., 2020; Li et al., 2006), Then, we examined ACE2 expression in WiL2-NS human B lymphoblast cells. The results showed that WiL2-NS cells expressed ACE2 transcripts and proteins as observed by RT-PCR (Fig. 1A) and western blot analysis (Fig. 1B), respectively. We also examined the interaction of SARS-CoV-2 SP and ACE2 on WiL2-NS cells. SP–ACE2 interaction was assessed by using flow cytometry analysis. SP binding significance on ACE2 in WiL2-NS cells was observed by shifting rightward of mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) from 2.7 in SP-untreated group to 4.3 compared to 1.6 in unstained control cells. In addition, SP binding rates on ACE2 in WiL2-NS cells were ≈ 5.7% in SP-non-incubated group and ≈ 28.8% in SP-incubated group, compared to ≈ 2.2% in unstained control cells, respectively (Fig. 1C). In contrast, when cells were treated with SP for 6, 12 and 24 h, no changes in cell viability was observed (Fig. 1D). These findings suggest that B cells bind appropriately SP on cell surface without showing cell death caused by SP.

BPA-mediated cell death was enhanced by the addition of SARS-CoV-2 SP

We examined the effect of SP on B cells by the measurement of cell death in BPA-expoured B cells in the presence of SP using trypan blue exclusion assay. WiL2-NS cell viability was decreased by treatment with various concentration of BPA and time-dependent manner (Fig. 2A, 2B). Cell viability was ≈ 81, 73 and 54% in response to 50, 75 and 100 μM BPA, respectively (Fig. 2A). Cell viability was ≈ 83, 76, 72, and 55% by 100 μM BPA treatment for 3, 4, 5, and 6 h, respectively (Fig. 2B). BPA treatment increased ACE2 expression as detected by RT-PCR (Fig. 2C). In addition, SP binding significance on ACE2 in WiL2-NS cells was observed by shifting rightward of mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) from 2.2 in BPA-untreated group to 3.3 in BPA-treated group, compared to 1.2 in unstained control cells. In addition, SP binding rates on ACE2 in WiL2-NS cells were ≈ 6.4% in BPA-untreated group and ≈ 27.5% in BPA-treated group, compared to ≈ 1.2% in unstained control cells (Fig. 2D). Following BPA exposure in the presence of SP, B cell viability was significantly reduced compared to BPA alone (Fig. 2E). These results suggest that BPA-exposed B cells can be more injured through SP–ACE2 interaction.

SARS-CoV-2 SP increased autophagic cells

Given that B cell death has been triggered by BPA-induced autophagy (Park et al., 2023), we examined whether SP induces autophagy in B cells. When WiL2-NS cells were treated with SP, LC3 protein levels were increased up to treatment tine of 12 h. LC3 protein level was respectively ≈ 1.9 and 1.5 times at 6 and 12 h, compared to control (Fig. 3A). Then, we investigated intracellular LC3 punctate structure formation by the transfection with pEGFP-LC3 plasmid DNA, in which cells with over 5 LC3 punctate structures were estimated as a positive autophagic cells (Fig. 3B). Then, when cells were treated with 50 ng/mL of SP, results showed an increase in the percentage of autophagic cells ≈ 25% (fig. 3C). We also examined LC3 punctate structure formation by 100 μM BPA treatment in the presence or absence of SP. Data showed higher LC3 punctate structure formation in group co-treated with BPA and SP ≈ 35% than in BPA only-treated group (Fig. 3D). It suggests that SP can aggravate BPA-exposed B cell death via autophagy induction rather than direct cytotoxic effect.

Lysosomal intracellular activity is related to B cell death by BPA and SP co-treatment

Since lysosomes play a role in autophagy and cell death (Mahapatra et al., 2021), we examined lysosomal intracellular activity. When WiL2-NS cells were treated with SP, no changes in lysosomal intracellular activity were observed in response to various concentration of SP (Fig. 4A). When cells were treated with 100 μM BPA, results showed an increase in lysosomal intracellular activity ≈ twice (Fig. 4B). We also examined lysosomal intracellular activity by 100 μM BPA treatment in the presence or absence of SP. Data showed higher lysosomal intracellular activity in group co-treated with BPA and SP ≈ 20% than in BPA only-treated group (Fig. 4C). It suggests that SP can aggravate B cell death via the enhancement of lysosomal intracellular activity in BPA-exposed B cells, even though SP alone has no effect on lysosomal intracellular activity.

Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) inhibited the effect of SP on BPA-exposed B cells

Since UDCA may reduce SP–ACE2 binding to inhibit SARS-CoV-2 entry into host cell (Carino et al., 2020; Rodal Canales et al., 2021; Thuy et al., 2022), we investigated whether UDCA can prevent SP binding on ACE2 of B cells to inhibit cell death. We detected no changes in the viability of WiL2-NS B cells (data not shown). According to flow cytometry analysis, UDCA inhibited the SP–ACE2 interaction on B cell (Fig. 5A). SP binding significance on ACE2 in WiL2-NS cells was observed by shifting rightward of mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) from 3.6 in UDCA-untreated group and then leftward to 2.2 in UDCS-treated group, compared to 1.7 in unstained control cells. In addition, SP binding rates on ACE2 in WiL2-NS cells were ≈ 25.6% in UDCA-untreated group and ≈ 16.3% in UDCA-treated group, compared to ≈ 6.1% in unstained control cells (Fig. 5A), suggesting that UDCA may inhibit SP binding on ACE2 of B cells.

Furthermore, we investigated whether UDCA could recover an increase of lysosomal intracellular activity and cell death of BPA-exposed B cells treated with BPA and SP. When cells were co-treated with BPA and SP in the presence or absence of 20 μM UDCA, lysosomal intracellular activity increased by SP co-treatment with BPA ≈ 30% was reduced to the level with BPA alone treatment (Fig. 5B). Cell viability decreased by SP co-treatment ≈ 40% was restored to the viability observed in the BPA-only treatment group (Fig. 5C). Together, these data suggest that UDCA may be a novel molecule for the prevention of B cell death via the interference of SARS-CoV-2 SP binding on ACE2 (Fig. 6).

DISCUSSION

B cells play a pivotal role in antibody production to defend against infectious diseases including SARS-CoV-2 (DiLillo et al., 2011; Geahlen, 2014; Kalampokis et al., 2013; Mustajab et al., 2025). However, it has not been clarified about whether B cells may act as targets for viral proteins and undergo functional impairment. Given the potential impact of BPA on immune cell function (Jang et al., 2020; Yurino et al., 2004; Zahra et al., 2020), we hypothesized that BPA exposure could enhance B cell susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 SP via modulation of ACE2 expression. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the impact of BPA on ACE2 expression and SARS-CoV-2 SP binding in human B cells and to evaluate the protective effects of UDCA on SP binding and B cell viability.

RBD of the SARS-CoV-2 SP recognizes ACE2 on the membrane, which is a major determinant of virus replication and disease severity (Hoffmann et al., 2020; Xiao and Dimitrov, 2004; Zhou et al., 2020). Our data showed that ACE2 was expressed in WiL2-NS human B lymphoblast cells and SP binds to control WiL2-NS B cells without changes in B cell viability (Fig. 1). Moreover, increased ACE2 expression was observed in immunologically relevant cells upon chemical exposure, raising concerns about enhanced viral binding and entry. Then, we found that SP-bound B cell population were increased by BPA treatment, leading to increase in cell death of BPA-exposed B cells by co-treatment with SP (Fig. 2). It suggests that SP enhanced BPA-exposed B cell death.

Various agents including BPA or SP induced autophagy, which led to autophagosome formation with changes in autophagy-related genes (Atg) and microtubule-associated protein 1A/1B-light chain 3 (LC3). The autophagosome is eventually degraded by fusion with lysosome with intracellular activity (Itakura and Mizushima, 2010; Yu et al., 2018). SP treatment increases LC3 protein level in B cells and number of cells with over 5 LC3 punctate structures, which was more increased by co-treatment of BPA and SP (Fig. 3). While no changes in lysosomal intracellular activity were observed in response to SP, it was enhanced by BPA treatment in the presence of SP (Fig. 4). It suggests that SP can aggravate B cell death via the increase in lysosomal intracellular activity in BPA-exposed B cells. Data also provide the possibility which the observed lysosomal activation was a downstream consequence of SP-induced autophagy or if it reflected an independent process.

Although many studies have reported a correlation between cell damage and the recovery of various cell function, the mechanism by which UDCA influences B cell viability has remained yet to be defined. In this study, data showed that B cell viability was increased by the role of UDCA-mediated cell recovery or prevention following damage by the effect of BPA with/without SP. It demonstrates that UDCA could mitigate BPA-induced vulnerability in B cells by reducing SP binding and cell death (Fig. 5). Further study of the mechanism underlying the effects of UDCA on B cell viability is required.

Previous studies have demonstrated that UDCA is known for its immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic properties (Hagey et al., 1993; Niu et al., 2019, 2020; Willart et al., 2012). UDCA may inhibit the entry of SARS-CoV-2 into the host cell (Rodal Canales et al., 2021) through reduced RBD–ACE2 binding (Carino et al., 2020; Thuy et al., 2022). So, these reports could indirectly support our results that UDCA prevent damage caused by SP–ACE2 interaction in BPA-exposed B cells and may enhance cell function against SARS-CoV-2 infection.

It has been reported that UDCA treatment restored liver plasma membrane fluidity and reduces oxidative stress in ethinylestradiol and progesterone-induced cholestasis in pregnant rats (Shi et al., 2003). UDCA possesses direct antioxidant properties by activating the cellular antioxidant defense system via the Nrf2 pathway (Lapenna et al., 2002; Lukivskaya et al., 2006). UDCA alleviates experimental liver fibrosis and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease by inhibiting apoptosis and improving autophagy (Wu et al., 2020; Ye et al., 2020). UDCA may offer therapeutic benefits against paclitaxel-induced hepatotoxicity by reversing elevated LC3B and cleaved caspase-3 levels (Eki̇ci̇ et al., 2025). So, it is possible for signaling pathways or molecular targets affected by UDCA to be in not only inhibition of lysosomal hyperactivation, modulation of autophagy-related proteins, or direct interaction with SP–ACE2 binding but also UDCA’s effect on membrane fluidity or its known antioxidant/autophagy-regulating effects.

Although our results do not provide a straightforward explanation of the mechanism of UDCA action in SP-mediated B cell death, we demonstrated that UDCA can recover B cell viability against SARS-CoV-2 infection following exposure to BPA. However, the signaling molecules that regulate B cell viability and their mechanism remain unclear, and many questions remain about the mechanisms of SP- and UDCA-mediated cell viability.

In addition, it may not be ruled out whether BPA-induced ACE2 upregulation is a phenomenon specific to transformed cells or may also be observed in primary human B cells. It has been reported little to no expression of ACE2 on most human peripheral blood immune cells (Song et al., 2023). So, the reliance on WiL2-NS cells, an EBV-transformed human B lymphoblastoid line, introduces limitations regarding physiological relevance, particularly given that ACE2 expression in primary B cells is generally low or undetectable (Song et al., 2023).

Our results are depicted in Fig. 6, which shows the amelioration of SP-mediated B cell death due to interference on the SP–ACE2 interaction or the inhibition of signaling molecules. It suggests that UDCA could represent a protective molecule for restoring B cell damage caused by SARS-CoV-2, and that UDCA may be useful in treating certain deficiency in individual humoral B cell immunity exposed to various types of injury.
