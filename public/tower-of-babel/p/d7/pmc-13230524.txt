Prognostic value of point-of-care testing for cardiac myosin-binding protein C in early risk assessment of acute myocardial infarction: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Zengguang Chen, Jing Huang, Jing Wang, Yiyang Zhan, Qianwei Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The prognostic value of myocardial myosin-binding protein C (cMyC) in acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains insufficiently studied. To evaluate the association between admission cMyC levels and the risk of 30-day major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients with AMI. In this prospective, single-center study, patients with AMI admitted between March 2022 and July 2024 were included. cMyC and hs-cTnI were measured by point-of-care testing (POCT) at admission. The primary endpoint was 30-day MACE. Predictive performance was evaluated using ROC analysis and multivariable Cox regression. A total of 285 patients were included, with a 30-day MACE incidence of 27.4%. Admission cMyC was independently associated with 30-day MACE after adjustment for clinical covariates. Exploratory analyses suggested a graded association between cMyC levels and the risk of adverse outcomes. Admission cMyC measured by POCT was independently associated with 30-day MACE in patients with AMI. cMyC may serve as a rapid adjunctive biomarker for early risk stratification, although its incremental predictive value requires further validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47454-1.

Introduction

Myocardial biomarkers are essential for diagnosing and assessing the prognosis of AMI. Currently, cardiac troponin (cTn) remains the gold standard for AMI diagnosis and prognosis evaluation1–3. However, cTn typically begins to appear in the bloodstream approximately 3 h after myocardial injury. Even with high-sensitivity troponin I (hs-cTnI), levels typically start to rise within 3–6 h post-injury and peak between 12–24 h4–6, limiting its utility for early diagnosis and immediate risk assessment.

In recent years, cMyC has emerged as a promising biomarker for myocardial injury7. Research indicates that cMyC can be detected in plasma within 30 min of myocardial ischemia, with a significant increase within 3 h, while cTnI and cTnT levels remain largely unchanged during this period8–10. This suggests that cMyC has the potential for earlier diagnosis and risk assessment. While its diagnostic value has been preliminarily validated, evidence for its prognostic role remains limited. For instance, in a study by Tong et al., 158 patients undergoing exercise stress echocardiography were followed for one year11. The study found that baseline serum cMyC not only reflected the severity of cardiovascular disease but also predicted future cardiovascular events, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 8.1 for major events at a 96% sensitivity threshold (P = 0.041). Additionally, a prospective multicenter study of 794 patients with acute heart failure (AHF) revealed that those with cMyC levels above the median (72 ng/L) on admission had a significantly higher risk of death (HR = 2.19, 95% CI 1.66–2.89, P < 0.001)12. Its prognostic efficacy is comparable to NT-proBNP and hs-cTnT. In another prospective study of 776 patients with suspected acute myocardial infarction and chest pain, early pre-hospital testing (median symptom time of 70 min) revealed significantly higher cMyC concentrations in confirmed myocardial infarction patients compared to those without myocardial infarction13. Moreover, cMyC can predict the risk of death within 2 years and the composite endpoint of non-fatal myocardial infarction/death. In conclusion, cMyC demonstrates high sensitivity for the early diagnosis of AMI and holds considerable predictive value for prognosis assessment.

Historically, cMyC testing primarily relied on laboratory-based platforms, which were time-consuming and lacked POCT capabilities13,14. More critically, prospective cohort studies have not yet established a clear threshold or independent prognostic value for early risk stratification in AMI patients, which limits its clinical utility for early diagnosis and treatment. This study aims to conduct a prospective observational cohort study to systematically evaluate the independent predictive value of POCT for cMyC, immediately upon admission, in predicting MACE in AMI patients over the short term (30 days). The goal is to establish and verify the critical value of cMyC for early risk stratification, providing evidence-based support for early clinical risk assessment.

Method

Research design and subjects

This prospective, single-center observational clinical study aims to assess the prognostic value of plasma cMyC concentration measured immediately upon admission in predicting the risk of MACE in patients with AMI over 30 and 365 days. The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. [2022]YLJSA079), ensuring compliance with international ethical standards, including the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. The study is prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No. ChiCTR2300069171).

The study enrolled patients aged 18 and older with AMI who presented within 12 h of symptom onset. These patients were treated at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University between March 2022 and July 2024. Two cardiologists independently confirmed the AMI diagnosis according to the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction15. The exclusion criteria were: (1) a history of viral myocarditis, dilated cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, or aortic dissection; (2) a history of uremia or malignant tumors; and (3) inability to understand or refusal to sign the informed consent form.

POCT detection of cMyC and hs-cTnI

Upon admission, blood samples were collected from all patients at admission, for POCT hs-cTnI assay (Getein Biotech, Inc, Nanjing, China) by chemiluminescence immunoassay and POCT cMyC assay (Nanjing Bottests Biotechnology Co, Ltd, Nanjing, China) by gold nanocage-based colloidal gold. cMyC levels were measured using a POCT kit based on a patented gold nanocage complex. This technology utilizes hollow cubic nanocrystals conjugated with monoclonal antibodies against human cMyC, replacing conventional spherical gold nanoparticles to enhance signal resolution16. The method offers a detection sensitivity of 0.1 pg/mL and provides results within 15 min. Compared to standard colloidal gold POCT products, the signal amplification factor is increased by 5,000 to 10,000 times. Photos of the testing equipment and typical results are provided (see Supplemental Fig. S1A and B).

Baseline variable collection

The study collected baseline demographic data, clinical history, medication details, and laboratory test results for all patients. Medical history was obtained from the patient’s self-reported diagnoses, in-hospital electronic medical records, and use of hypoglycemic, antihypertensive, or statin medications. Demographic and vital signs included age, gender, height, and weight. Clinical history encompassed conditions such as coronary heart disease (CHD), myocardial infarction (MI), coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), stroke, peripheral vascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and smoking history. Myocardial infarction characteristics and visit status included ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), direct percutaneous coronary intervention (pPCI) treatment, anterior wall myocardial infarction, and the time from chest pain onset to admission. Laboratory tests included total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), creatine kinase isoenzymes (CK-MB), N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), D-dimer, red blood cell count (RBC), white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil count (N), hemoglobin (HB), platelet count (PLT), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), lipoprotein (a) [Lp(a)], blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Cardiac structure and function indicators included left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). Relevant biomarkers for the study included cMyC and hs-cTnI.

Endpoint event

The primary endpoint was defined as MACE occurring within 30 days after admission. These events included all-cause death, recurrent myocardial infarction (MI), unplanned target vessel revascularization, cardiogenic rehospitalization, new-onset or worsening heart failure, and cardiogenic shock. Secondary endpoints included the aforementioned MACE and all-cause mortality within 365 days. Detailed definitions of specific endpoints are provided in Supplemental Table S1.

All endpoint events were independently adjudicated by two experienced cardiologists according to predefined criteria. The adjudicators were blinded to cMyC results. Recurrent myocardial infarction was defined in accordance with the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction, incorporating cardiac troponin dynamics and the 99th percentile threshold. Follow-up assessments were conducted at 30 ± 3 days, 90 ± 7 days, 180 ± 7 days, and 365 ± 14 days after admission. Follow-up methods included outpatient visits, electronic medical record review, or telephone interviews, during which any adverse cardiovascular events were recorded.

Statistical analysis

Sample size estimation was based on the event-per-variable (EPV) principle for prognostic modeling17,18. The primary endpoint of this study was 30-day MACE. Based on previous studies in patients with AMI, the incidence of 30-day MACE is typically reported to range from 20 to 30%. In this study, we conservatively assumed an event rate of approximately 25%19,20. Considering five prespecified predictors (cMyC, age, sex, LVEF, and hs-cTnI), selected based on clinical relevance, and adopting an EPV of 14, a minimum of 70 events was required, corresponding to an estimated sample size of 280 patients. To account for potential loss to follow-up, the target sample size was increased to at least 295 patients. Given the exploratory nature of this study, this sample size was considered reasonable and acceptable.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3) and SPSS (version 25.0). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using independent-sample t -tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Non-normally distributed variables are expressed as median (interquartile range) [M (IQR)], with comparisons made using the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables are presented as frequency (percentage) [n (%)] and were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

The predictive performance of admission biomarkers for 30-day MACE was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the area under the curve (AUC) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. Differences between AUCs were compared using DeLong’s test. To account for time-to-event data, model discrimination was further assessed using the concordance index (C-index).

Continuous biomarkers with skewed distributions were transformed prior to analysis, including natural logarithm transformation for cMyC and log10 transformation for hs-cTnI, while NT-proBNP was scaled by dividing by 100.To identify predictors of 30-day MACE, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator LASSO-Cox regression with tenfold cross-validation was applied to 39 candidate variables. Variables with non-zero coefficients at the optimal penalty parameter were included in subsequent multivariable Cox proportional hazards models to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs.Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. Discrimination was assessed using both ROC curves and the C-index. Calibration was evaluated using calibration curves, and clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA). Incremental predictive value was further evaluated using the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and net reclassification improvement (NRI).

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis within the Cox proportional hazards framework was performed to explore potential nonlinear associations between cMyC levels and 30-day MACE. Based on the RCS curves and percentile distributions, exploratory cutoffs were selected at the 75th and 90th percentiles to categorize cMyC into three groups. These groupings were intended for exploratory risk stratification and not for defining clinically established thresholds.Survival analyses were conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, with loss to follow-up treated as censored data. Differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test, and pairwise comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction when appropriate.Subgroup analyses were performed to assess the consistency of associations between cMyC groups and 30-day MACE across clinically relevant strata, including age, sex, renal function, myocardial infarction type, receipt of pPCI, and infarct location. Interaction effects were tested by including multiplicative interaction terms in the Cox models.

To address potential overfitting and improve model stability, penalized regression and internal validation via cross-validation were applied. Although the effective EPV in the final model was lower than conventional thresholds, the use of LASSO and consistent findings across multiple analyses support the robustness of the model. These findings should be interpreted in the context of the exploratory design.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 300 patients with AMI were initially screened, of whom 15 were excluded due to malignant tumors, aortic dissection, or refusal of consent. Consequently, 285 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1) and were categorized into the MACE group (n = 78, 27.4%) and the non-MACE group (n = 207, 72.6%) according to the occurrence of MACE within 30 days after admission.The distribution of early MACE events was as follows: heart failure or cardiogenic shock (n = 62, 21.8%), all-cause death (n = 14, 4.9%), and cardiogenic rehospitalization (n = 2, 0.7%). No recurrent myocardial infarction or unplanned target vessel revascularization events were observed during the early follow-up period. Predictive performance of admission biomarkers for 30-Day MACE

The predictive performance of admission biomarkers for 30-day MACE was evaluated in 285 patients with AMI. ROC analysis (Fig. 2A) showed that cMyC demonstrated moderate discrimination (AUC = 0.715), which was slightly higher than that of NT-proBNP (0.702), D-dimer (0.698), and CK-MB (0.671).Time-dependent predictive performance over the 30-day follow-up period is presented in Fig. 2B. Discussion

In this prospective single-center study, we evaluated the prognostic value of admission POCT measured cMyC for predicting short-term outcomes in patients with AMI. The main findings can be summarized as follows. First, cMyC levels at admission were independently associated with the risk of 30-day MACE after adjustment for established clinical risk factors. Second, cMyC demonstrated moderate discrimination for 30-day MACE in single-marker analysis. Third, exploratory categorization based on restricted cubic spline–derived inflection points and percentile thresholds revealed a graded association between cMyC levels and MACE risk, which was consistently observed across multiple time points and clinical subgroups.

Despite these findings, the incremental improvement in risk prediction provided by cMyC appeared to be limited when evaluated in multivariable models. Reclassification analyses (e.g., NRI and IDI) also did not demonstrate statistically significant improvement, suggesting that the additional prognostic information offered by cMyC beyond established biomarkers and clinical variables may be modest. Therefore, cMyC may be better considered a complementary biomarker for early risk stratification rather than a replacement for hs-cTnI.

The observed prognostic relevance of cMyC may be partly explained by its distinct release kinetics and molecular characteristics compared with cardiac troponins. cMyC is a structural protein of the myocardial sarcomere that undergoes rapid proteolytic cleavage following ischemic injury, leading to the early release of detectable fragments into the circulation21–24. Experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated that cMyC rises earlier and peaks sooner than cardiac troponins in the setting of acute myocardial injury9,25. This rapid release profile suggests that cMyC measured at admission may better reflect the early phase of myocardial necrosis, which is closely related to infarct size and subsequent clinical outcomes26,27. In contrast, hs-cTnI may be influenced by delayed release kinetics, prior myocardial injury, and comorbid conditions such as renal dysfunction, which could reduce its specificity in the very early stage of AMI. In addition, emerging evidence suggests that cMyC fragments may be involved in immune-inflammatory responses following myocardial injury, potentially linking acute myocardial damage with adverse remodeling and prognosis28. However, these mechanistic hypotheses require further validation in dedicated translational studies.

Our findings are broadly consistent with previous studies evaluating cMyC in acute coronary syndrome. Cardiac troponins have been well established as robust predictors of adverse outcomes in patients with acute coronary syndromes29–31. In this context, cMyC performs comparably to high-sensitivity troponins in early diagnostic and prognostic settings13,32. In the present study, the use of a rapid POCT platform further demonstrates the feasibility of obtaining clinically actionable information shortly after admission (approximately 15 min), which may have practical implications in emergency settings where early risk stratification is critical for clinical decision-making33. However, given the limited incremental predictive value observed in our analysis, cMyC may be better considered a potential adjunctive biomarker for early risk stratification.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the composite endpoint included recurrent myocardial infarction, which is partly defined by cardiac troponin dynamics and the 99th percentile threshold. This may introduce potential incorporation bias when comparing cMyC with hs-cTnI for predicting MACE. Although endpoint adjudicators were blinded to cMyC results, the involvement of troponin in endpoint definition should be considered when interpreting comparative findings. Second, the composite MACE endpoint comprised heterogeneous clinical events, and the observed associations may have been influenced by differences in the relative contribution of individual components. Third, the categorization of cMyC based on restricted cubic spline–derived thresholds and percentile cutoffs was exploratory and data-driven, which may lead to overestimation of effect sizes; these thresholds require external validation before clinical application. Fourth, both biomarkers were measured at admission using a single POCT-based assessment. In contemporary clinical practice, risk stratification using high-sensitivity cardiac troponin typically relies on serial measurements and delta changes. The lack of serial hs-cTnI data in the present study limits direct comparison with real-world diagnostic and prognostic strategies, and our findings should therefore be interpreted within the context of single time-point measurements at admission. Fifth, this was a single-center study with a relatively modest sample size, which may limit generalizability. Although LASSO regression was applied to reduce overfitting, the effective event-per-variable ratio in the final model remained limited, and residual overfitting cannot be entirely excluded. Sixth, only baseline cMyC levels were analyzed, and the potential prognostic value of serial measurements was not assessed. Seventh, calibration performance was not comprehensively evaluated in the primary analysis, and further validation of model performance is warranted. Eighth, the absence of cardiac magnetic resonance data precluded direct assessment of infarct size, limiting mechanistic interpretation. Finally, as this study focused on patients with confirmed AMI, the applicability of these findings to broader populations, such as those with undifferentiated chest pain or non-ischemic cardiac conditions, remains to be determined.

Conclusion

In this prospective cohort study, cMyC measured by POCT at admission was independently associated with 30-day MACE in patients with acute myocardial infarction. These findings suggest that admission cMyC may serve as a rapid adjunctive biomarker for early risk stratification in AMI. Further validation in larger studies is warranted.
