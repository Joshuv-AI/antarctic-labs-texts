Nonlinear dynamics of reverse osmosis high pressure high speed centrifugal pumps
Authors: Hussein Sayed, Tamer A El-Sayed, Michael I Friswell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-pressure pump rotors are critical components in a wide range of industrial and mechanical systems, including turbomachinery, hydraulic systems, and aviation engines. This study presents a dynamic model for a single-stage high-speed high-pressure centrifugal pump supported by hydrodynamic journal and thrust bearings. The model is based on a rigid rotor framework and incorporates the coupling effects between the journal and thrust bearings. The influence of key bearing parameters is systematically investigated, with particular attention given to the impact of introducing a central groove in the left bearing. Furthermore, the analysis explores the effects of integrating a third central journal into the system which acts also as rear wear ring. The study also examines the role of bearing boundary conditions, which can be regulated through auxiliary lines, and evaluates the influence of unbalance forces on system performance. The results demonstrate that boundary conditions significantly affect the load-carrying capacity and stability of the bearings, potentially leading to bearing failure if not properly managed. Additionally, while a coupling effect between the journal and thrust bearings is observed, its influence is found to be relatively minor in the context of the rigid rotor model employed in this analysis. The findings of this study provide valuable insights for the design and optimization of high-speed centrifugal pumps, offering a deeper understanding of the dynamic interactions and operational constraints inherent in such systems.Keywords: High pressure pump, Journal bearing, Thrust bearing, CFD, Reverse Osmosis, Bifurcation diagramsSubject terms: Engineering, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

High-pressure pumps are essential components in numerous industrial applications. Among the critical parameters in pump design is the specific speed, which determines the operational range and classification of pumps. Specific speed depends on the pump’s flow rate, head, and rotational speed. For centrifugal pumps operating at low specific speeds, achieving the desired performance often necessitates the use of multistage configurations to reduce the head per stage or the adoption of high-speed pumps. High-speed pumps offer advantages such as higher efficiency and compactness relative to power output. However, their design, particularly the bearings, poses significant challenges.

In desalination systems, particularly in reverse osmosis processes, the pump’s working fluid is often seawater. This creates a unique set of requirements, including the use of water-lubricated bearings to handle both thrust and lateral loads. The risk of oil leakage from bearings contaminating the reverse osmosis membranes is a major concern, driving the preference for water as a lubricant. High-speed operation enhances the load-carrying capacity of such bearings but necessitates careful monitoring of system stability. In this study, the rotor dynamics of a non-traditional single-stage high-pressure pump is investigated. The rotors of such pumps are supported by internal journal and thrust bearings and with no external bearing box which enables compact and simple pump design. An example of such pumps is the SSD Model that manufactured by FEDCO. The present study focuses on the influence of bearing design parameters on system performance and stability.

Jafarzadeh et al.1 employed computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to analyze low-specific-speed, high-speed centrifugal pumps. Their findings demonstrated that CFD can accurately predict pump performance and reduce reliance on physical prototypes. They further explored the effect of increasing the impeller blade count on performance. Verhoeven2 studied the rotodynamic behavior of multistage high-speed pumps, emphasizing that hydraulic excitation forces dominate mechanical forces such as rotor imbalance. Rapid rotor rubbing due to high operating speeds was also highlighted as a significant issue.

Doshi et al.3 presented a comprehensive review article on vibrations in turbomachinery, with a particular focus on pumps. The study discussed common faults in pumps, including damaged impellers, bent shafts, inadequate foundations, and friction caused by rubbing, among other issues. Yoshida et al.4 investigated the hydraulic forces arising from geometrical manufacturing deviations in high-speed pumps. Their study, based on experimental analysis, revealed the emergence of significant hydraulic forces whose magnitudes vary with changes in flow rate. This observation confirmed that these forces are distinct from conventional unbalance forces. Furthermore, the authors concluded that hydraulic unbalance must be accounted for when balancing high-speed pumps to ensure optimal performance and reliability. Birajdar and Keste5 analyzed hydraulic unbalance forces using a one-way fluid-structure interaction (FSI) methodology. Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) was employed to determine the pressure distribution within the pump, which was subsequently applied in structural analysis to evaluate the deformation at various pump locations. The computed deformations were compared with the measured vibrational displacements of the pump during operation, revealing a strong agreement between the two sets of results.

Yuan et al.6 conducted a theoretical and experimental investigation into the fluid-structure interaction of centrifugal pump rotor systems. The analysis focused on single-stage centrifugal pumps and examined three types of impellers: open, closed, and split impellers. Their findings indicated that, for the same flow rate, the closed impeller experienced the highest stress levels compared to the split impeller. Additionally, the study revealed that hydraulic forces increase significantly during part-load operation compared to operation at the best efficiency point (BEP). In another study, Li et al.7 investigated the fluid-structure interaction in vertical centrifugal pumps. They evaluated the natural frequencies of the pump both in air and when submerged in water. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in the natural frequencies when the pump was immersed in water, highlighting the influence of the surrounding fluid on the dynamic behavior of the system.

Li et al.8 studied the fluid-structure interaction in centrifugal pumps with a focus on vibrational behavior during startup. The analysis employed transient Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) to simulate the unsteady flow conditions occurring during startup. The computed pressures were applied to the pump impeller to evaluate the transient forces acting on the system. The study concluded that radial forces are the primary contributors to pump vibrations. Furthermore, the authors recommended operating the pump near its design point to minimize vibrational effects and improve operational stability.

Li et al.9 proposed an enhanced analytical method for performing modal analysis of a four-stage centrifugal pump. The accuracy of the proposed method was validated by comparing its results with three-dimensional finite element simulations conducted using the ANSYS software, demonstrating strong agreement between the analytical and numerical approaches. Diewald and Nordmann10 analyzed the dynamic behavior of centrifugal pumps using a Jeffcott rotor model. The dynamic equations were solved using the finite element method, incorporating the effects of fluid forces arising from journal bearings, balance pistons, and impeller interactions. Their findings demonstrated that these fluid forces significantly influence the lateral vibrations of the pump, underscoring their importance in the dynamic analysis of centrifugal pump systems.

Gong et al.11 investigated the dynamic stability of large hydraulic turbines. They utilized Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) to evaluate the hydraulic forces acting on the impeller at various journal offset positions. Subsequently, the rotor dynamic equations were applied to assess the stability of the rotor system. The study revealed that the threshold speed decreases with an increase in the length of the labyrinth seal clearance. In contrast, the threshold speed increases as the labyrinth seal width and pressure drop increases.

Journal bearings are employed in various applications to support rotating loads, and their proper design is crucial for ensuring continuous operation and prolonged equipment life. Accurate rotordynamic analysis requires precise evaluation of the bearing forces. Journal bearings are classified into short, finite, and long bearings. The governing equation for journal bearings is the Reynolds equation. For short bearings12–14 and infinitely long bearings15, the Reynolds equation can be simplified to a form that allows for analytical solutions. In contrast, for bearings of finite length, the Reynolds equation must be solved numerically, typically using methods such as finite difference or finite element techniques.

In rotordynamic analysis, bearing forces must be evaluated at each time step, which can be computationally intensive. This challenge has led to the adoption of equivalent bearing coefficients, such as first-order, second-order, or higher-order coefficients16–20, to simplify the analysis. Additionally, several studies have explored the use of multivariable polynomial functions to represent bearing forces21,22.

In rotordynamics analysis, the rotor may be modeled as either flexible or rigid, depending on its degree of flexibility. Holmes23 investigated the response of a rigid rotor supported by two short bearings, while Akers et al.24 were among the pioneers in modeling the response of a rigid rotor supported by finite-length journal bearings. Meanwhile, numerous researchers have focused on flexible rotor models, as seen in studies such as19,21,25–28.

In most rotating machinery, axial forces are generated, necessitating the use of thrust bearings to manage these forces. Much of the research on thrust bearings focuses on evaluating and enhancing their load-carrying capacity, as well as examining the influence of various bearing parameters on this capacity29–33. Additionally, several studies have addressed the approximation of bearing forces using first- and second-order bearing coefficients34,35.

Frêne36 conducted both theoretical and experimental investigations on tapered-land thrust bearings under the assumption of isothermal conditions. The study examined the influence of groove inclination on friction torque and film thickness. The authors concluded that centrifugal inertia effects in the laminar regime have negligible influence on the bearing characteristics. However, the onset of non-laminar flow was found to increase both the friction torque and the load-carrying capacity of the bearing. In a subsequent work, Frêne and Constantinescu37 revisited the classical theory of hydrodynamic lubrication, with particular emphasis on laminar and non-laminar flow regimes in bearings. Their study underscored the importance of inertia forces in these regimes and provided a systematic overview of research advances that have facilitated applications in aerospace and nuclear engineering. Mittwollen et al.38 developed a computational approach to predict the static and dynamic characteristics of both tilting-pad and fixed-pad thrust bearings operating under high surface velocities and significant thermal loads. Their work also addressed the influence of thrust bearings on rotor vibrations, supported by experimental validation. The study concluded that, within specific operating conditions, thrust bearings can exert a substantial effect on rotor lateral vibrations.

Deng et al.39 examined the transition from oil-lubricated to water-lubricated bearings in high-speed machinery, highlighting key challenges such as turbulence effects and numerical convergence issues. They proposed an optimization methodology for determining the appropriate number of cross-film elements in water lubrication models, thereby improving predictive accuracy and supporting the design of future bearing systems. Feng et al.40 introduced a misaligned thermo-hydrodynamic (THD) model for water-lubricated hydrodynamic journal bearings. Their analysis investigated the combined effects of turbulent flow, rotational speed, and eccentricity ratio on bearing performance. The results demonstrated that turbulence enhances load-carrying capacity, while THD effects significantly reduce frictional power losses, underscoring the necessity of incorporating these factors into the design of high-speed water-lubricated bearings.

The existing literature indicates that numerous studies have focused on fluid-structure interaction and rotor-bearing systems, but few have investigated the combined effects of both journal and thrust bearings. Furthermore, the pump under investigation is planned to be fabricated as a prototype as part of a funded research project, which serves as the motivation for the present study. The pump consists of a thrust bearing and two journal bearings, and due to the high rigidity of the rotor (with a low ratio), a rigid rotor model is adopted. Following this introduction, “Analytical models” section presents the pump model and the underlying theoretical framework. “Results and discussions” section discusses the results and provides a detailed analysis. Finally, “Conclusions” section presents the key findings and conclusions.

Analytical models

This section describes the principal components of the pump model. The rotor is supported by two journal bearings and two thrust bearings. A rigid rotor formulation is employed for the dynamic analysis of the rotor system, with the pump impeller represented as a rotating rigid disk. A comprehensive description of this dynamic model is provided in the “Dynamic model” section. The journal and thrust bearings are modeled based on the Reynolds equation, with the specific approximations detailed in the subsection entitled “Journal and thrust bearings model approximations” section. The bearing fluid medium is water, modeled as a laminar flow; the exclusion of turbulent effects constitutes a recognized limitation of the present model. The hydraulic forces acting on the pump impeller are derived from a transient computational fluid dynamics (CFD) analysis performed using ANSYS CFX. These forces are subsequently represented in the form of a Fourier series, as elaborated upon in the subsection “Fluid forces using computational fluid dynamics” section.

Fluid forces using computational fluid dynamics

The pump under investigation is designed to meet specific operational requirements, including a flow rate of and an output pressure of . The objective is to achieve these performance requirements using a single-stage centrifugal pump design. Empirical equations for the pump design were employed to determine the optimal impeller diameter and rotational speed, resulting in an impeller diameter of and an operational speed of . The number of impeller blades is 6. These parameters were selected to maintain high efficiency while utilizing a single-stage impeller configuration.

The impeller and volute profiles were designed using established empirical formulas, as referenced in prior literature (e.g., Tuzson2000,Lobanoff2013). A three-dimensional model of the corresponding fluid domains was subsequently created and discretized into approximately 977,163 elements using ANSYS Meshing module. This mesh density was determined to be sufficient for stabilizing the pressure solution and ensuring computational accuracy. ANSYS CFX-Pre is then used for CFD preprocessing. The fluid domain was partitioned into four distinct regions: the inlet pipe, the impeller, the volute, and the outlet pipe, see Fig. 5c. All domains were modeled as stationary, with the exception of the impeller, which was defined as a rotating region. The inclusion of inlet and outlet pipe extensions served to minimize the influence of boundary conditions on the results within the pump itself. The simulation utilized seawater as the working fluid, with physical properties as listed in Table 1. Boundary conditions were defined by a constant static inlet pressure of and a fixed outlet flow rate of , see Fig. 5c. To account for turbulent flow effects, the standard k– turbulence model was employed for the numerical simulations.

To evaluate the hydraulic forces acting on the impeller, the blade, hub, and shroud surfaces were selected. The resulting net forces represent the instantaneous hydraulic loads acting on the impeller.

This force evaluation procedure was applied at each time step of the transient simulation to obtain the time histories of the hydraulic forces. This approach facilitates further analysis and provides a robust foundation for evaluating the pump’s dynamic behavior.

Effect of central bearing width

Figure 14 displays the effect of central bearing width () on the dynamic response of a pump rotor system. The figure consists of four subplots that illustrate the influence of the central bearing geometry on the minimum film thickness and journal motion characteristics. Conclusions

This study investigates the dynamic modeling of a high-speed, high-pressure centrifugal pump, incorporating two journal bearings and two thrust bearings. Additionally, the feasibility of introducing a third, central journal bearing is explored. The rotor is modeled as a rigid rotor, while the journal bearings are represented as finite-length journal bearings.

The study examines the influence of an internal groove in the left bearing within a two-journal-bearing configuration. Furthermore, the impact of employing a three-journal-bearing arrangement is analyzed under varying left bearing boundary conditions. Various bearing parameters are considered, including external pressure, the widths of the left and central bearings, and the diameters of both the left and thrust bearings. Additionally, external unbalance magnitude and phase angle are also investigated.

The results indicate that, although coupling between the thrust bearing and the journal bearings exists, its influence on the overall system response is relatively weak. In contrast, the bearing boundary conditions play a dominant role in governing system stability, as they directly affect the threshold speed of instability. The study emphasizes the necessity of thoroughly investigating the dynamic behavior of the complete rotor-bearing assembly. In particular, parameters that may intuitively be expected to enhance dynamic performance can, when increased beyond certain limits, trigger bifurcations that alter the system response from periodic to chaotic motion, as observed for variations in the left journal bearing diameter and width. Overall, this work underscores the importance of comprehensive dynamic modeling of rotating machinery prior to manufacturing, as it facilitates the early identification of potential instability mechanisms and helps to mitigate the risk of premature failure.
