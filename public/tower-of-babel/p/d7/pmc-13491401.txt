Capacity Analysis of Urban Water Systems under Centralization: A Case Study in Denmark
Authors: F. Sousa Braga, C. L. Gargalo, Ll. Corominas, K. Poulsen, S. Erbs Poulsen, J. Comas, K. V. Gernaey, X. Flores-Alsina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Water utilities across Europe face increasing challenges like aging infrastructure, which often lacks the capacity and flexibility to meet future demands. Centralization of wastewater treatment, redirecting flows from smaller, outdated plants to larger, more advanced facilities, offers opportunities to improve performance and optimize system-wide capacity. This study introduces a comprehensive, plant-wide simulation framework designed to operate under dynamic conditions, evaluating the technical feasibility and capacity impacts of centralization strategies, using a real case involving five wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) (Døjsøvej, Skovby, Ry, Hørning, and Gl.Rye) in Skanderborg, Denmark. Mechanistic models were developed for existing and centralized configurations, integrating dynamic influent generators, data reconciliation, plant-wide process modeling, and key performance indicators to assess system behavior under realistic operational and environmental conditions. Monte Carlo simulations were employed to quantify uncertainty propagation and evaluate long-term infrastructure resilience. The calibrated models were evaluated using mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) and reproduced influent and effluent pollutant dynamics satisfactorily (average deviations <5% and <15%, respectively); they closely matched operational metrics, including aeration/pumping energy demand, chemical usage, and biosolids production (average deviation <5%). While centralization was found to be technically feasible under current operational conditions in the short term, it reduced the operational buffer of receiving facilities. Long-term scenario-based predictions revealed performance degradation and increased risk of noncompliance, particularly for Døjsøvej, which may require upgrades within approximately five years under the assumed growth scenario. The framework enables scenario-based proactive, data-driven planning by revealing system constraints and upgrade needs, offering a scalable solution to enhance efficiency, compliance, and infrastructure longevity.

Introduction

Wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) across Europe face increasing operational and capacity challenges due to aging infrastructure. According to ref , 775 active WWTPs were constructed during the 1980s and 1990s and are now in need of renovation. A typical design lifespan for a WWTP is approximately 30 years, and lower for certain components like pumps, blowers; consequently, many of these facilities are nearing or have exceeded their operational lifetimes. Simultaneously, about 6336 WWTPs are currently operating at or near their maximum design capacity, which raises concerns about resilience, compliance, and future scalability. Overall, around 27% of Europe’s operational WWTPs are either outdated or overloaded, highlighting an urgent need for strategic interventions.

One potential strategy to address these challenges is centralizationthe consolidation of smaller, aging treatment plants into larger, modernized facilities. This approach can offer several benefits, including a potential for improved process efficiency, economies of scale in operation and maintenance, and enhanced environmental performance, although the overall economic outcome depends on site-specific capital and operational expenditures. , Centralized systems are often more flexible and better equipped to integrate advanced technologies and process monitoring. However, centralization also presents potential risks. The redirecting of flows increases the hydraulic and organic loading of the receiving facilities, overloading critical units, and unexpected compliance failures without proper infrastructure upgrades. Evaluating the capacity of receiving facilities under such conditions requires robust tools capable of capturing both design and operational complexity.

In recent decades, dynamic process modeling has become a foundation of wastewater systems engineering. These models enable virtual testing of new operational strategies and infrastructure changes, minimizing the risks associated with full-scale implementation Industry standard models include influent generators, process models for different units, − key performance indicators (KPI) and uncertainty/risk analysis.

Influent generators have successfully reproduced wastewater dynamics for both conventional , and emerging pollutants while enabling the simulation of disturbance scenarios using realistic catchment characteristics. Process models offer detailed insights into the transformation and fate of key constituents such as Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P). These tools have been used for plant design, process optimization, real-time control, and retrofit evaluation. When complemented with robust evaluation criteria and uncertainty quantification such models support comprehensive performance assessment, covering effluent quality, energy demand, chemical usage, sludge production, and resource recovery.

This study proposes a modeling framework under dynamic conditions to evaluate the technical feasibility and capacity implications of centralization strategies for municipal WWTPs. While prior studies have addressed centralization using static optimization, life cycle assessment (LCA), or multicriteria decision-making methods, only some have employed fully dynamic, plant-wide simulations under realistic loading conditions. , This work addresses the following research questions:What are the immediate impacts of centralization on the hydraulic capacity, treatment performance, operational demands, and greenhouse gas emissions of the receiving WWTPs?How do centralized and decentralized configurations compare over the long term, particularly under projected increases in flow and pollutant loads?How can uncertainty quantification in KPIs be used to assess the overall system, detect early signs of capacity stress, and guide timely infrastructure upgrades and investment decisions?

The proposed framework is applied to five WWTPs in Skanderborg, Denmark, using site-specific data, calibrated influent profiles, and mechanistic models. The study emphasizes the importance of integrating process simulation, data reconciliation, and uncertainty analysis to inform infrastructure planning. By simulating long-term, high-stress scenarios, the study reveals capacity limits, operational trade-offs, and upgrade triggers. Even if this methodology is applied to a specific case-study, the approach is generic and supports utilities in making data-driven decisions that balance performance, cost, and environmental compliance.

Methods

Case Study Description

This study focuses on the wastewater infrastructure managed by Skanderborg Utility (SU) in Denmark, which operates five conventional WWTPs (Figure ): Døjsøvej, Skovby, Ry, Hørning, and Gl.Rye. These facilities vary in size and capacity. Based on operational records provided by Skanderborg Utility, Døjsøvej WWTP is the largest facility in the service area, treating approximately 5,400 m3/d and serving around 40,000 PE, while the smallest, Gl.Rye WWTP processes about 194 m3/d for 1,900 PE.

All five WWTPs employ conventional treatment technologies and lack advanced recovery processes. Ry, Gl. Rye and Hørning were built in the 1970s–1980s, and although they were upgraded 15–20 years ago, they are now approaching the end of their functional lifespans and will soon require renovations or replacements.

The WWTPs in this study are equipped with monitoring systems, providing reliable long-term operational data sets. While the number of high-frequency sensors is limited, the available composite samples, SCADA flow records, and historical data sets are consistent with standard practice in full-scale modeling studies.

Influent Generation and Characterization

In this study, Skovby and Døjsøvej WWTP influent dynamics were reproduced using a modified version of the Benchmark Simulation Model No. 2 (BSM2) influent generator. BSM2 influent generator simulates dynamic influent flow and composition over extended periods (e.g., one year), producing realistic time series for key pollutants such as COD, total nitrogen (TN), and total phosphorus (TP). It incorporates empirical models for household water usage, stormwater contributions, and infiltration/inflow effects, based on diurnal, weekly, and seasonal patterns. The generated profiles account for variability due to (1) Daily household routines, (2) Rain events and runoff effects, (3) Groundwater infiltration, (4) Temperature-driven seasonal variations.

Historical data from 2022, including influent flow and pollutant load measurements, were used to calibrate the BSM2 influent generator. The study also includes a 15-year growth scenario assuming a uniform annual increase of approximately 1.5% in hydraulic and pollutant loading. This value was selected in consultation with the utility and reflects expected population and wastewater load growth within the study area. The scenario is intended for exploratory stress-testing rather than predictive forecasting

Mass Balancing and Data Reconciliation

Mass balances were developed for key pollutants (COD, TN, and TP), as well as volumetric flows, across all major treatment units, including influent channels, primary clarifiers, biological reactors, secondary clarifiers, and sludge handling systems. To ensure the consistency and reliability of the input data, a data reconciliation procedure was conducted before model calibration. This addressed common issues in historical data sets, such as missing values, measurement errors, and sampling gaps. The procedure involved generating the identity matrix to define the system equations, performing systematic data cleansing and curation, estimating missing values using historical references and interpolation, calculating optimal flow and load distributions using Lagrange multipliers, and finally validating the reconciled data against known process constraints and observed trends. Although detailed measurements were not available, the combination of data reconciliation and influent generation ensured that the calibrated data sets were consistent and representative of the actual operating conditions.

WWTP Process Modeling

WWTP models were developed using the structure of the BSM2 as reference framework. ,, Each model incorporates key process units, including primary clarification, an activated sludge system with aerobic, anoxic, and anaerobic zones, secondary clarification, sludge thickening and dewatering, chemical dosing (e.g., FeCl3 for P removal), and energy and chemical consumption estimators.

Biological transformationssuch as carbon degradation, nitrification, denitrification, and enhanced biological P removalare described using a modified version of Activated Sludge Model No. 2 (ASM2d), incorporating extensions proposed by refs − . The model enables prediction of simultaneous carbon, N, and P removal, along with key chemical and biological processes involving sulfur, iron, anammox activity, and nitrous oxide (N2O) production and emissions.

Secondary settling is represented using the double-exponential velocity function proposed by ref implemented through a ten-layer settler model. The sludge thickening and dewatering units are treated as ideal solid–liquid separation processes, following the methodology described in ref and does not capture the full complexity of full-scale sludge behavior.

Key Performance Metrics

To assess the implications of centralization on overall system performance and to identify potential bottlenecks, a comprehensive set of quantitative KPIs was defined. These metrics were used to evaluate hydraulic and treatment capacities under dynamic loading conditions, based on simulation outputs from the calibrated plant-wide models described in Section . Only operational KPIs such as energy use, chemical consumption, and biosolids production were directly validated against plant records. TIV or GHG emissions cannot be measured continuously in full scale systems and are therefore evaluated exclusively through model outputs.

Hydraulic Capacity Indicators

Hydraulic performance was evaluated by comparing simulated influent flow rates against the design capacities of key process units, such as biological reactors and secondary clarifiers. As part of this study, and in consultation with the water utility, specific engineering thresholds were selected to guide the analysis. The following indicators were applied: Hydraulic Loading Rate (HLR) [m 3 /m 2 /d]: Defined as the time during which surface overflow rates exceed the critical threshold of 20 m3/m2/d, indicating a risk of washout or impaired clarification during peak flow events. Hydraulic Retention Time (HRT) [hours]: Expressed as time during which retention times fall below the design minimum of 7 h, potentially leading to reactor overloading and reduced treatment efficiency.

Treatment Capacity Indicators

Treatment capacity was assessed based on the system’s ability to maintain effluent quality within regulatory limits for key pollutants, including COD, TN, and TP. The following metrics were computed: Time in violation (TIV) [%]: The percentage of simulation time during which effluent concentrations exceed discharge limits complying with the Danish standards for the utility applied in the case-study (COD: 100 g/m3, TN: 8 g/m3, TP: 0.5 g/m3), serving as a proxy for regulatory compliance and process robustness under dynamic loading conditions.

Operational Expenditures (OPEX) Indicators

To complement the analysis of capacity and treatment performance, operational demands for energy and chemical consumption were monitored across scenarios. These indicators provide a direct measure of the system’s resource intensity under dynamic loading conditions and highlight potential stress points associated with centralized configurations. Aeration Energy Consumption (AED) [kWh/d]: Total daily energy consumption by aeration systems across all biological reactors. This indicator reflects the increased oxygen demand associated with higher organic and N loads and serves as a key benchmark for process energy efficiency. Pumping Energy Consumption (PED) [kWh/d]: Aggregate energy used by influent pumps, internal recirculation loops, return activated sludge (RAS) pumps, and effluent pumping stations. Elevated pumping demand may indicate hydraulic overloads or suboptimal flow management under centralized loading conditions. Total Chemical Consumption (CHEM) [kg/d]: Combined daily usage of key chemicals such as ferric chloride (FeCl3) for P removal and polymer for sludge conditioning. Tracking absolute chemical use allows for identifying spikes in dosing demand and potential process bottlenecks in chemical handling and storage. Biosolids Production Rate (BPR) [t/d]: Quantifies the total sludge generated daily, supporting the evaluation of solids handling system capacity and identifying potential bottlenecks in sludge thickening or/and dewatering processes. Sludge-related indicators should be interpreted as high-level proxies rather than direct predictors of sludge line limitations, given the simplified representation adopted.

Uncertainty Analysis

Dynamic simulation of wastewater treatment processes inherently involves multiple sources of uncertainty, including variability in influent characteristics and model parameter estimation. To enhance the robustness of the capacity assessment and to identify critical factors influencing system performance under centralization, a comprehensive uncertainty analysis was conducted. The uncertainty analysis focuses on the sources with the strongest impact on WWTP performance. This analysis focuses on uncertainty propagation rather than parameter sensitivity ranking. Other sources of variability such as upstream sewer failures, leakage, and pipeline faults remain outside the scope of this study.

Process Parameters Uncertainty

Key parameters related to biological reaction kinetics, influent composition, and operational settings were perturbed within a range of ±5–10% from their calibrated values. No correlation between the parameters was assumed. These parameters included yield coefficients and maximum specific growth rates for heterotrophic and autotrophic organisms, half-saturation constants for substrates such as carbon and N, precipitation constants for P removal processes, and characteristics of the secondary clarifiers, including settling velocity and layer structure. This analysis aimed to evaluate how sensitive the model outputs were to typical uncertainties in process configurations and biological behavior.

Influent and Load Uncertainty

In parallel, influent, and loading (organic matter, TP and TN) uncertainty were assessed to capture the effects of variations in incoming wastewater conditions. Multiple synthetic influent profiles were generated by introducing stochastic perturbations, up to 25%, to the baseline data produced by the BSM2 influent generator. These perturbations accounted for variability arising from extreme rainfall events and the resulting peak hydraulic loads, infiltration dynamics from stormwater and groundwater intrusion, seasonal temperature fluctuations that influence biological reaction rates, and long-term increases in the number of PE associated with projected urban development. These profiles enabled the simulation of a wide range of operational scenarios, reflecting both short-term disturbances and long-term planning challenges.

Monte Carlo-Based Methodology

To systematically assess the combined influence of these uncertainties, a Monte Carlo-based methodology was applied, as previously described in refs and . The analysis began with the definition of probability distribution functions for both process parameters and influent variables. Latin hypercube sampling was then used to generate multiple input scenarios, each of which was evaluated using the calibrated dynamic model. For each simulation run, key performance metricssuch as effluent quality, peak flow handling, sludge production, and energy and chemical consumptionwere computed and recorded. The results of the Monte Carlo simulations (MCS) were then analyzed using descriptive statistical techniques, including the use of box plots, error bar charts, and three-dimensional representations of interquartile ranges. This probabilistic framework enabled a detailed characterization of model sensitivity, performance robustness, and the likelihood of constraint violations under variable conditions.

Simulation Setup

Although a 15 min time resolution was adopted in this study, the BSM framework allows users to define alternative temporal resolutions depending on data availability, computational requirements, and study objectives. This temporal granularity ensures that both short-term variations (e.g., storm events, diurnal peaks) and long-term trends (e.g., seasonal changes, temperature effects) are accurately captured. The influent profiles were generated using calibrated BSM2-based influent generators (Section ), and the WWTP models were calibrated and validated for each site (Section ).

Operational set points (SRT, internal recirculation, chemical dosing) were maintained constant between scenarios unless directly affected by changes in loading conditions. Each scenario was simulated under identical boundary conditions, such as temperature, rainfall patterns, and population behavior, to ensure a fair comparison.

Simulation outputs included time series for influent and effluent concentrations, internal state variables, energy and chemical usage, and sludge production. These results were used to compute key performance metrics outlined in Section . By comparing these indicators across both scenarios, the analysis aimed to identify performance trade-offs, infrastructure constraints, and necessary operational or structural adjustments to support future centralization.

Baseline Decentralized Results

WWTP Influent Dynamics

The calibration followed a four-step procedure to ensure that the simulated profiles accurately reflected local conditions. First, base flow levels were adjusted to represent observed dry-weather conditions. Second, stormwater inputs and infiltration dynamics were incorporated. In both cases (Skovby and Døjsøvej WWTP), a representative distribution of flow contributions was assumed: 60% from households (HH), 10% from industry/agriculture, 20% from infiltration, and 10% from wet weather events (RAIN). This is consistent with previous influent characterization studies. ,, Next, COD, TN, and TP load factors were estimated using measured composite samples. In this context, the relative contribution of HH versus industrial/agricultural sources was modified to reflect the characteristics of each catchment. Specifically, Døjsøvej required a higher HH/Ind-Agri ratio (70%/30%) compared to Skovby WWTP (90%/10%) to align with observed data. This adjustment reflects the fact that the average daily load to Døjsøvej WWTP is approximately 1.75 times higher than that to Skovby WWTP. First flush parameters were also fine-tuned to capture transient dynamics during rainfall events. These values were extracted from previous studies. ,, Inorganic loads were adjusted to mimic weak acid base chemistry (buffer capacity) and pH. Finally, local temperature profiles were integrated to account for both daily and seasonal variability, affecting process kinetics and influent concentrations (parameter list in ref ).

Scenario Analysis

Centralization Strategy at the Current Time

To assess the short-term impacts of centralization, the influent generator was adapted to simulate the redirection of flows from Hørning to Skovby WWTP and from Ry and Gl.Rye to Døjsøvej WWTP. At Skovby, this reconfiguration resulted in a 36% increase in average influent flow and a nearly 50% increase in pollutant loads (COD, TN, and TP). At Døjsøvej, flow increased by 38%, while the corresponding load increase remained below 30%, as the plant was already operating under relatively high baseline loading conditions. The main simulation results are summarized in Figures – (columns 2 and 4).

Hydraulic performance indicators show that Skovby, the frequency of HLR exceedance rose from 5.5% to 11.5%, and HRT violations increased from 6.5% to 14.8%, suggesting moderate hydraulic stress (Figure , columns 1 and 2). In contrast, Døjsøvej maintained 0% exceedance for both HLR and HRT, indicating sufficient hydraulic capacity to accommodate the additional inflows (Figure , columns 3 and 4). Treatment performance deteriorated slightly in both plants, with an increase in time in violation (TIV) for COD, TN, and TP. However, effluent quality remained within discharge limits for over 90% of the simulation period, confirming that biological treatment processes were not critically impaired by the increased loading. Operational demands increased proportionally to the additional load. At Skovby, AED rose by 59% and PED by 20% (Figure , columns 1 and 2). At Døjsøvej, the respective increases were 23% and 13% (Figure , columns 3 and 4). CHEM rose by approximately 30% at both plants, primarily due to increased P loads. Although polymer consumption remained stable during the simulation, higher sludge production suggests it will increase under continued centralized loading. In terms of environmental performance, direct GHG emissions increased slightly due to intensified aeration and chemical use. No significant changes were observed in ERE or UDE (Figure ). This relatively limited variation is consistent with the modeling assumptions and emission factor formulation.

The results suggest that both WWTPs can accommodate redirected flows under current conditions. Despite moderate increases in hydraulic stress and operational demandsespecially at Skovby WWTPeffluent quality and compliance were maintained, confirming the short-term technical feasibility of the centralization strategy.

To assess the long-term implications of both decentralized and centralized configurations, simulations were extended over an assumed 15-year horizon to reflect the planning period relevant for short-medium-term decision-making and potential systems upgrades. The simulations incorporate a moderate annual growth rate of approximately 1.5% in hydraulic and pollutant loading according to the prospects for the area. This approach enabled comparison of infrastructure stability, performance decline, and upgrade needs under increasing stress. Uncertainty Analysis

The MC framework described in Section is applied using 250 simulations with uniform probability distributions within predefined bounds. Parameter ranges were defined based on literature values and are consistent with previous BSM-based studies. , All parameters were assumed independent, which is a simplifying assumption. Figures , , and illustrate how input uncertainty is propagated through the model structure using MCS. This uncertainty is reflected in a range of KPIs, including hydraulic capacity (HLR, HRT), treatment capacity (TIV for COD, TN, TP), energy and cost-related indicators (AED, PED, CHEM, BPR) and GHG emissions. Each set of box plots reveals the impact of these uncertainties under different loading and configuration scenarios.

The results reinforce the deterministic conclusions: while centralization is technically feasible in the short term, it significantly reduces the operational buffer of the receiving WWTPs, especially under increasing load scenarios. The probabilistic analysis adds a critical dimension to this conclusion by quantifying the likelihood and severity of performance deviations. Specifically, the centralized configurations display broader interquartile ranges and more frequent exceedance of regulatory and design thresholds, indicating a higher probability of stress events and system noncompliance.

A key insight from this analysis is the compounding effect of uncertainty over time. As influent variability increases due to urban growth, stormwater dynamics, or temperature-driven kinetic shifts, the probability of KPI deviations also grows. This is particularly evident in effluent violations (TIV), where outliers suggest that even moderate increases in load can push the system into frequent and prolonged noncompliance. These patterns underscore the importance of robustness, the ability of infrastructure to maintain stable performance despite uncertainty and show that centralized configurations are less robust than decentralized ones within the same probabilistic envelope. The simulation outputs also highlight potential failure modes: for instance, combinations of high influent COD, low temperature, and elevated internal recycle loads frequently lead to simultaneous stress in aeration systems and reduced effluent quality. These compound effects cannot be captured using point-estimate models and demonstrate the unique value of a Monte Carlo-based framework for infrastructure risk analysis.

For utility planning, the probabilistic results support informed risk-based decisions. Instead of designing reinforcements based on average or worst-case scenarios, planners can prioritise interventions that mitigate high-impact risks. For example, if 75% of simulation runs under centralized conditions predict overuse of aeration capacity within 10 years, this justifies the pre-emptive investment in blower upgrades or energy-efficient process controls.

Discussion

Critical Analysis of the Main Results

The scenario analysis highlights clear differences in system response under decentralized and centralized trajectories.

Under decentralized growth, both Skovby and Døjsøvej WWTPs exhibited manageable increases in hydraulic stress and operational demand. Effluent violations remained below 15%, and all OPEX indicators stayed within the 40% threshold. These findings suggest that decentralized development can be sustained over the 15-year horizon through operational optimization alone, without requiring structural upgrades.

In contrast, centralized growth at Skovby WWTP led to progressive overload. HLR and HRT violations increased more than 4-fold, and TIVs for N and P exceeded 20%, indicating chronic treatment instability. Although OPEX increases remained close to threshold levels (Section ), the severity of compliance breaches underscores the need for targeted upgrades within the next decade.

The most critical situation emerged at Døjsøvej WWTP under centralized growth. Despite stable hydraulic conditions, biological system exhibited early failure. TIVs for TN and TP exceeded 50%, and COD violations rose 8-fold. Combined with a 45–47% increase in energy and chemical demands, these trends suggest that the plant requires immediate intervention to restore performance.

Although the data set lacked high frequency measurements, the combination of mass balancing, data reconciliation, and influent generator calibration ensured that the modeling basis followed established international guidelines and standard practice, remaining scientifically robust. These limitations should be considered when interpreting model performance and the robustness of long-term projections. Indeed, these timelines should be seen as scenario-dependent indicators rather than precise forecasts, as the analysis does not account for alternative growth trajectories, operational adaptation, asset aging, or long-term system nonstationarity. The results are therefore best understood as comparative stress-test outcomes rather than deterministic predictions of infrastructure upgrade timing

Evaluating broader benefits such as cost savings or hybrid network optimization lies beyond the scope of the present capacity-focused analysis. The same applies for sensitivity analysis. Additional uncertainty sources such as sewer infiltration dynamics or asset failures could be explored in future work but fall beyond the intended scope of this WWTP-focused study.

Overall, centralized strategies increase treatment and operational risks over time.

The reader should be reminded that the modeling framework is transferable; however, the conclusions regarding centralization and system resilience are case-specific and should not be generalized beyond the studied system.

Contextualizing within Existing Literature

These findings complement and extend earlier studies on wastewater infrastructure centralization, which often relied on static optimization methods, life cycle cost (LCC), or qualitative assessments. ,, Unlike previous approaches, this study employs a fully dynamic simulation framework capable of reproducing diurnal, seasonal, and long-term variability in influent conditions. This has been scarcely handled in the literature. , The integration of influent generators, mechanistic plant models, and dynamic KPI tracking allows for a more realistic and fine-grained assessment of operational bottlenecks and capacity constraints. Another distinctive contribution of this work is the application of MCS, which provides a probabilistic understanding of system behavior under input variability and process parameter uncertainty. This has never been applied before when assessing water infrastructure centralization.

Alignment with the EU Directive

These findings impact the evolving European regulatory frameworks, particularly the revised Urban Wastewater Treatment Directive. This directive introduces more rigorous standards for effluent quality, energy efficiency, and climate impact mitigation. In contrast to earlier requirements, the new directive mandates advanced treatment levels and sector-wide goals for energy. The framework offers a robust way to assess wastewater capacity under current and future loads, guiding utilities on upgrades and operational improvements.

By identifying critical thresholds for effluent quality violations, hydraulic overload, and operational stress, the model directly supports strategic compliance planning in alignment with Article 5 of the Directive (EU) 2024/3019, which requires early identification of infrastructure constraints and prioritization of targeted investment.

Practical Implications, Limitations and Future Work

The model’s outputs indicate a reduced lifespan relative to standard expectations of 10 years for mechanical components and 5 years for core treatment infrastructure under centralized conditions. These results provide critical insights for utility managers, supporting the prioritization of capital investments and proactive upgrade planning. Rather than relying on reactive interventions triggered by performance decline or regulatory breach, utilities can use such simulation tools to design phased, cost-effective reinforcement strategies.

The results should be interpreted as scenario-dependent indicators rather than deterministic forecasts, as long-term projections are based on a single growth scenario and do not account for alternative development pathways, asset aging dynamics, or system nonstationarity.

The assumption of fixed operational set points is a simplification. While this approach isolates structural effects, real systems would adapt through operational optimization. Therefore, the reported upgrade needs should be interpreted under current operational strategies, and adaptive control could partially mitigate some of the observed effects. Similarly, the uncertainty analysis assumes parameter independence and uniform distributions within predefined bounds, which may not fully capture the complexity of biological and operational interactions.

Other points of improvement are the sludge line where its simplified representation limits detailed mechanistic interpretation of conditioning and dewatering performance. Accordingly, sludge-related indicators should be interpreted as high-level proxies rather than direct predictors of sludge handling capacity.

In addition, model calibration and evaluation were performed on aggregated data sets, and comparisons between measured (composite) data and dynamic simulations are therefore indicative of overall trends rather than transient predictive accuracy. GHG-related indicators were not directly validated and should be interpreted with caution, particularly in comparative rather than absolute terms

Moreover, the framework supports data-driven decision-making by enabling utilities to virtually test centralization pathways before implementation. This is particularly relevant for small/medium-sized municipalities facing limited budgets and regulatory pressures.

Although the study is based on a specific case in Denmark, the proposed methodology is transferable to other regions and utilities, provided that appropriate influent data and detailed information about plant configurations are available. However, the conclusions regarding centralization impacts on resilience and upgrade timing remain case-specific and should not be generalized without further analysis.

Future work could extend this framework to incorporate real-time data feeds, economic and environmental analysis, or even embed it within a digital twin environment for continuous infrastructure planning and control. Future studies will potentially integrate extreme climate-driven events or multiyear data sets to evaluate system resilience under rare but highly impactful conditions. Other potential extensions could include more sophisticated sampling methods for the Monte Carlo analysis, for example, where parametric correlation is accounted for.
