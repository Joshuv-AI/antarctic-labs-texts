SEL1L confers reduced EMT and drug resistance to breast cancer cells by promoting Shh degradation
Authors: Hao Zhou, Jie Wang, Xu Wang, Zhaoji Guo, Yan Chen, Erli Gao, Jie Sun, Siying Zhou, Xinyi Guan, Yuxin Wu, Zhe Zhao, Yuting Kuang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe expression of SEL1L (suppressor/enhancer of Lin-12-like) is found to be downregulated in clinical breast cancer (BRC) and lymph node metastasis tissues, as well as in carboplatin-resistant BRC cell lines. In this study, reduced SEL1L expression was linked to poor prognosis in BRC, while silencing SEL1L in BRC cells led to increased epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and metastasis. Furthermore, SEL1L sensitized cancer cells to the anti-cancer drug carboplatin, leading to enhanced apoptosis in both in vivo and in vitro settings. Mechanistically, SEL1L suppressed the expression of oncogenic Shh protein by promoting its ubiquitination-proteasomal degradation, thereby inhibiting EMT and improving cellular sensitivity to carboplatin in BRC cells via hedgehog signaling pathway. Our findings emphasize the tumor-suppressive function of endogenous SEL1L in BRC and demonstrate the crucial role of the SEL1L-Shh-Gli1 axis in controlling cancer cell progression, offering a promising foundation for the development of innovative therapeutics against BRC.Subject areas: Biological sciences, Cancer

Introduction

Breast cancer (BRC) has surpassed lung cancer as the most frequently diagnosed cancer in women globally.1 Among its subtypes, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) represents a highly aggressive form characterized by low patient survival rates, primarily due to the frequent emergence of therapy resistance.2 The standard treatment for BRC often involves DNA-damaging agents, including platinum-based drugs such as cisplatin and carboplatin.3 Traditionally, carboplatin achieves its anti-tumor effects by cross-linking neighboring nucleotides, resulting in cell-cycle arrest and alterations in angiogenesis.4,5,6 However, despite initial treatment success, resistance to chemotherapy often develops, largely driven by genetic mutations.7,8 This resistance is a significant predictor of poor prognosis and is associated with the presence of cancer stem cells (CSCs), which may further contribute to tumor metastasis.9,10,11,12 Thus, there is a pressing need for the development of strategies to improve therapeutic outcomes for patients with BRC.13

SEL1L (suppressor/enhancer of Lin-12-like) is the most conserved component of the endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation (ERAD) pathway across eukaryotes, with numerous substrates essential for maintaining cellular functions in vivo and in various cell types, including tumors.14,15,16 SEL1L primarily regulates the stability of downstream proteins through ubiquitination.17,18 It has been widely reported to inhibit proliferation and demonstrate tumor suppressor activity in multiple cancer types, including liver17 and esophageal cancers.19 Additionally, SEL1L plays a key role in regulating several critical signaling pathways, such as Notch and SMAD. Despite its established roles in other cancers, the function of SEL1L in BRC progression and its influence on drug resistance remain largely unexamined.

Sonic hedgehog (Shh) signaling has been implicated in the regulation of key events during mammalian developmental processes.20 Although significant research has focused on hedgehog signaling in embryonic development, its relevance extends to stem cell biology and cancer, making it a critical area of study. Notably, Shh has been shown to activate genes associated with CSC induction and proliferation, such as MYC, cyclin D1, and BMI1, during cancer progression.20,21 In Shh ligand-dependent autocrine hedgehog signaling, the overexpression of the Shh ligand occurs, which is subsequently taken up by neighboring or adjacent tumor cells upon release. This uptake activates downstream genes, including Gli1, further driving tumor progression. Additionally, hedgehog signaling is implicated in regulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in tumors. Multiple studies have established the involvement of hedgehog signaling in tumorigenesis and its association with poor prognosis in BRC.22,23

In this study, SEL1L expression was found to be significantly reduced in clinical BRC, lymph node metastases tissues and carboplatin-resistant (CR) BRC cells, suggesting that SEL1L may function as a tumor suppressor in BRC development. Altering SEL1L expression influenced the metastatic ability and EMT in BRC cell lines. Mechanistic investigations revealed that SEL1L exerts its anticancer effects by regulating the critical protein Shh within the hedgehog signaling pathway. SEL1L modulates Shh expression by promoting its ubiquitination and degradation, thereby affecting the hedgehog signaling pathway.

Result

SEL1L is reduced in stable carboplatin-resistant BRC cell lines

To establish a suitable model for studying carboplatin resistance (CR) in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells, MDA-MB-231 (231) and BT-549 (549) TNBC cells were exposed to gradually increasing concentrations of carboplatin, as described in the “STAR Methods” section. This process yielded derivative carboplatin-resistant TNBC cells (231 CR and 549 CR). The carboplatin resistance of these cells was assessed by exposing them to escalating doses of carboplatin for 96 h, followed by sensitivity evaluation using the CTG assay. The sensitivity of CR cells was compared with that of their parental BRC cells. The CTG assay revealed a significant (p < 0.001) increase in carboplatin resistance in CR cells (Figures S1A and S1B). The IC50 values of the CR cells were markedly elevated compared to their control counterparts. Specifically, the IC50 of control 231 cells was 26.7 μM, whereas that of 231 CR cells was 70.2 μM. Similarly, the IC50 of control 549 cells was 38.42 μM, compared to 68.7 μM in 549 CR cells. Additionally, when parental and CR cells were treated with the same concentration of carboplatin, CR cells exhibited significantly greater cell viability compared to the parental cells (Figures S1C–S1F). Furthermore, reduced levels of γ-H2AX observed in the IF assay for CR cells treated with carboplatin, compared to parental cells, indicated resistance to carboplatin in the CR cells (Figures 1A and 1B).

To investigate the role of the tumor suppressor gene in carboplatin resistance in TNBC, we examined the expression levels of various genes in CR cells compared to their parental counterparts. As shown in Figure 1C, there was a significant change in the expression of the SEL1L protein. However, SEL1L mRNA levels showed no discernible difference between parental and CR cell lines (Figure S1G). Western blot analysis of EMT markers revealed that the epithelial markers ZO1 were significantly downregulated in CR cells, while the mesenchymal markers N-cadherin, vimentin, β-catenin, and ZEB1 were upregulated (Figures 1C and 1D). These findings prompted further investigation of cell migration and invasion abilities using transwell assays, which demonstrated enhanced metastatic potential in both 231 CR and 549 CR cell lines (Figures 1E–1H). This observation was corroborated by the wound healing assay results, which showed similar enhancements in cell migration in 231 CR cells (Figure 1I). To explore the clinical relevance of SEL1L in BRC, we analyzed data from TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas). The results indicated that higher SEL1L expression was positively associated with survival in clinical BRC patients, suggesting that SEL1L may function as a tumor suppressor during BRC progression (Figure 1J). Furthermore, in lymph node-positive and mesenchymal TNBC patients, elevated SEL1L expression was significantly linked to prolonged overall survival, indicating a potential role for SEL1L in mitigating tumor metastasis and drug resistance (Figure S1H).

Silenced SEL1L promoted BRC cell EMT and carboplatin resistance

To investigate the role of SEL1L in BRC cells, we knocked down SEL1L expression in MCF-7 cells using SEL1L siRNA in a concentration-dependent manner. Western blot analysis showed a clear induction of EMT in MCF-7 cells (Figure 2A). Similarly, SEL1L expression was downregulated in M-231 and BT-549 cells using SEL1L shRNA, with both western blot and qPCR data confirming enhanced EMT progression in these cells (Figures 2B–2D). Further functional assays revealed that cells transfected with SEL1L shRNA exhibited significantly greater migration and invasion capabilities compared to control groups, as shown by transwell assays (Figures 2E, 2F, 2H, and 2I) and wound healing assays (Figures 2G and 2J). These findings collectively indicate that silencing SEL1L promotes EMT progression in TNBC cell lines.

Given the earlier observation that SEL1L expression was reduced in carboplatin-resistant TNBC cell lines, we tested whether SEL1L influences carboplatin resistance in TNBC cells. IC50 assays demonstrated that knockdown of SEL1L in M-231 and BT-549 cells enhanced their resistance to carboplatin (Figures 2K and 2L). Additionally, IF assays revealed reduced levels of cell apoptosis and γ-H2AX in SEL1L knockdown cells treated with carboplatin compared to control cells, further indicating increased resistance to the drug (Figures 2M and S2A–S2C). Interestingly, the cell proliferation, colony formation ability, and apoptosis levels showed no significant changes in SEL1L-silenced cells (Figures S2D–S2G).

To explore the impact of SEL1L overexpression on CR TNBC cell lines, we generated stable SEL1L-overexpressing cell lines by transfecting 231 CR and 549 CR cells with SEL1L or Ctrl lentivirus. Successful overexpression of SEL1L was confirmed by western blot analysis. Consistent with previous findings, western blot and qPCR analyses demonstrated that SEL1L influenced the EMT process by reducing the expression of N-cadherin, vimentin, ZEB1, Slug, and β-catenin, while increasing ZO1 expression (Figures 3A–3C). These results indicate that SEL1L effectively counteracts the elevated EMT induced by carboplatin selection we then assessed the metastatic potential of CR cells through transwell and wound healing assays. The results showed that the promoted migration and invasion abilities observed in CR cells were significantly reduced by SEL1L overexpression (Figures 3D–3G).

In addition, SEL1L overexpression effectively reversed carboplatin resistance in CR cells. The IC50 values were significantly reduced to 9.758 μM and 11.62 μM in 231 CR and 549 CR cells, respectively (Figure 2H). Moreover, in SEL1L-overexpressing 231 CR cells, the apoptosis ratio increased significantly following treatment with 10 μM carboplatin compared to Ctrl 231 CR cells (Figures 3I and 3J).

In summary, these findings demonstrate that SEL1L regulates cell metastasis and carboplatin sensitivity in BRC cells while having no discernible impact on tumor proliferation or baseline apoptosis. These results suggest that SEL1L functions as a tumor suppressor gene involved in clinical metastasis and drug resistance.

SEL1L modulates TNBC progression through regulating hedgehog signaling pathway

The mechanism by which SEL1L influences EMT and carboplatin resistance remains unclear. To investigate this further, we analyzed the expression of core proteins from nine critical signaling pathways (PI3K-AKT, NF-κB, Wnt, Hedgehog, Jak/Stat, Notch, MAPK, TGF-β/Smad, and Hippo) in SEL1L-silenced, SEL1L-overexpressing, and control cell lines using western blot analysis. SEL1L overexpression significantly reduced the expression of Shh and Gli1, which are key proteins in the hedgehog signaling pathway (Figure 4A). To confirm this observation, we examined the expression of Shh and Gli1 in SEL1L-downregulated cells. Consistent with previous results, SEL1L knockdown significantly increased the expression of Shh and Gli1 (Figure 4B). No significant changes were observed in the other signaling pathways (Figure S2H), further highlighting the specific regulatory role of SEL1L in hedgehog signaling. Next, we employed qPCR to assess the impact of SEL1L on Shh and Gli1. Results from all experimental groups demonstrated that SEL1L regulates Gli1 mRNA expression while exerting no significant effect on Shh mRNA levels in both SEL1L-overexpressing and SEL1L-downregulated cells (Figures 4C and 4D). These findings suggest that SEL1L may influence Shh expression via post-transcriptional regulation.

As is well known, the hedgehog signaling pathway exhibits abnormal activation in various cancers. Upon Shh binding to PTCH1/2, the inhibitory effect of PTCH1/2 on SMO is removed, allowing SMO activation. This, in turn, facilitates the nuclear translocation of full-length Gli1 protein, leading to the activation of downstream oncogene transcription and promoting critical tumor progression. Within the hedgehog signaling pathway, Shh is positioned upstream of Gli1; therefore, this study seeks to explore the regulatory effects of SEL1L on Shh. Numerous studies have demonstrated the key role of Shh-hedgehog signaling in processes essential for tumor development, including EMT. However, the mechanism by which SEL1L exerts its influence on these processes via the regulation of Shh-hedgehog signaling remains unclear. To address this, we investigated whether the attenuation of EMT induced by SEL1L could be reversed by Shh overexpression. First, SEL1L overexpression in 231 CR cells significantly reduced the expression of Shh protein. However, transfection of Shh plasmid into SEL1L-overexpressing cells restored Shh expression and reversed the EMT attenuation caused by SEL1L overexpression. This was evidenced by increased expression of N-cadherin and vimentin, alongside decreased levels of E-cadherin and ZO1 (Figure 4E). The same observation was confirmed in 549 CR cells (Figure 4F). Furthermore, SEL1L overexpression significantly reduced cell metastasis and Carboplatin resistance in 231 CR cells. Importantly, reintroduction of Shh in SEL1L-overexpressing 231 CR cells restored both cell metastasis and Carboplatin resistance (Figures 4G and 4H).

Given the key role of Shh in the hedgehog signaling pathway, we further investigated the mechanism underlying the interaction between SEL1L and Shh. Since SEL1L does not affect the mRNA expression levels of Shh, it was hypothesized that SEL1L regulates Shh protein expression through post-transcriptional mechanisms. To test this, we treated BRC cells with the ubiquitination-proteasome inhibitor MG132. The results demonstrated that MG132 increased Shh protein levels in 231 CR cells. Furthermore, the downregulation of Shh induced by SEL1L overexpression was prevented by MG132, indicating that SEL1L promotes Shh protein degradation via the ubiquitination-proteasome pathway (Figure 5A). Similarly, MG132 treatment also impacted the upregulation of Shh observed in SEL1L-downregulated 231 cells (Figure 5B). Additionally, SEL1L overexpression in 231 CR and 549 CR cells accelerated the degradation of Shh protein, as evidenced by reduced Shh protein decay (Figures 5C and 5D). Protein dynamics analysis further showed that SEL1L overexpression shortened the half-life of Shh compared with control cells. Co-immunoprecipitation experiments revealed that the levels of ubiquitinated Shh protein were markedly increased in SEL1L-overexpressing BRC cells compared to those in vector control cells (Figures 5E and S3A). Conversely, SEL1L downregulation significantly reduced the levels of ubiquitinated Shh protein (Figures 5F and S3B). Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that SEL1L regulates the stability of Shh protein by regulating its ubiquitination and degradation, thereby influencing its cellular content.

The SEL1L-HRD1 complex of ER-associated degradation (ERAD) suppresses tumorigenesis in mice.17 SEL1L and HRD1, both independently and collectively, have been previously associated with various cancer pathogeneses in humans.24 To determine whether SEL1L regulates the ubiquitination and degradation of Shh protein through HRD1, we carried out the following experiments. The results indicated that SEL1L significantly affects HRD1 protein expression (Figure 5G). Consistent findings have been reported in numerous studies.25,26 Moreover, we generated M231 cell lines with HRD1 knockdown (three shRNAs exhibited varying degrees of knockdown), yet no significant change in SEL1L protein expression was detected (Figure 5H). HRD1 shRNA 1 showed the strongest knockdown effect, we used shRNA 1 to knockdown HRD1 in the subsequent experiments. Subsequently, we assessed the ubiquitination level of Shh protein in HRD1-knockdown M231 cells. The results demonstrated that HRD1 depletion led to a significant reduction in Shh ubiquitination (Figures 5I and S3C). Overexpression of SEL1L, on the other hand, was found to enhance Shh ubiquitination (Figures 5E and S3A). Notably, when HRD1 was knocked down in SEL1L-overexpressing cells, the increased ubiquitination of Shh protein was markedly attenuated (Figures 5J and S3D). Furthermore, we constructed of the HRD1-C2A mutant plasmid (C291A/C294A).27,28 The data demonstrated that the increase in Shh ubiquitination was significantly attenuated upon comparison of cells transfected with the HRD1 mutant to those expressing wild-type HRD1 (Figures 5K and S3E).These findings suggest that SEL1L-mediated regulation of Shh ubiquitination is highly dependent on wild-type HRD1, thereby further supporting previous research conclusions.29

Furthermore, to assess whether Shh protein accumulates in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), we isolated ER fractions and compared them with whole cell lysate (WCL). Subsequently, we evaluated Shh accumulation in the ER following SEL1L knockdown. Our findings revealed a significant increase in Shh protein accumulation within the ER following SEL1L knockdown (Figure 5L). Besides, as an endogenous substrate of SEL1L-HRD1 ERAD, IRE1α protein level was elevated approximately 8-fold in SEL1L silenced M-231 and BT549 cells, which means that the activation of ER stress may be induced by the IRE1α signaling pathway (Figure 5M).

Expression and tumor-suppressive function of SEL1L in clinical BRC tissues and mouse

To investigate the role of SEL1L in clinical BRC, we analyzed its expression at both mRNA and protein levels in six BRC patient tissue samples (The clinical information of the patients is partially presented in Table S1). Consistent with previous findings from the TCGA database and BRC cell lines, SEL1L mRNA and protein levels were significantly reduced in BRC tissues (Figures 6A and 6B). Additionally, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of SEL1L was performed on clinical samples, including adjacent tissues, primary BRC tissues, and lymph node metastases from the same patient. The results showed that SEL1L levels were highest in adjacent tissues and lowest in metastatic lymph nodes, suggesting a critical role for SEL1L in BRC metastasis (Figure 6C).

We also assessed the impact of SEL1L on carboplatin resistance in a mouse model. SEL1L-silenced and negative control M231 cells were subcutaneously implanted into nude mice (N = 5). When tumor volumes reached approximately 400 mm3, both groups received intraperitoneal injections of carboplatin (40 mg/kg) for 20 days. After 71 days, the tumor volume and weight data showed that xenografts in the SEL1L negative control group were significantly reduced following carboplatin treatment compared to the SEL1L-silenced group (Figures 6D–6F). Meanwhile, there were no notable differences in body weight or organ coefficients between the two groups (Figures S3F and S3G). To further understand the molecular changes, we measured mRNA and protein levels of SEL1L, Shh, and Gli1 in the xenografts. Consistent with cellular data, SEL1L silencing did not affect Shh mRNA levels but significantly increased its protein content (Figures 6G–6J). Furthermore, Gli1 mRNA levels were significantly upregulated in SEL1L-silenced xenografts (Figure 6G).

We further established an orthotopic mouse model of BRC development through in situ injection to assess the differences in metastatic potential between Ctrl and SEL1L-overexpressing cells (N = 7). Concurrently, low-dose carboplatin was administered via intraperitoneal injection at 20 mg/kg, twice a week. The results demonstrated that, compared to the Ctrl group, the SEL1L-overexpressing group exhibited significantly reduced tumor volumes and well-demarcated tumor boundaries within the mammary gland without evident metastasis. In contrast, the Ctrl group displayed poorly defined tumor margins and visible rib adhesion (indicated by white arrows), along with distinct metastatic lesions in the lung (Figures 7A–7E). These findings suggest that the combination of SEL1L overexpression and low-dose carboplatin effectively suppresses tumor progression and metastasis in vivo, indicating that SEL1L exerts a potentially significant anti-tumor effect both in vitro and vivo. To further elucidate the molecular alterations, we assessed the protein levels of SEL1L and Shh in the xenograft tissues. Consistent with the in vitro findings, SEL1L overexpression markedly suppressed Shh expression (Figure 7F). Moreover, IHC results further confirmed that Gli1 expression was also reduced by SEL1L (Figures 7G and 7H).

To broaden the impact of the SEL1L in clinic, we treated M-231 cells with two other commonly used chemotherapeutic agents, docetaxel30 and palifosfamide (an active metabolite of cyclophosphamide), in a concentration-gradient manner. Our results demonstrated that both drugs induced a reduction in SEL1L protein expression (Figures S4A and S4B). Furthermore, using the same methodology described in the manuscript, we established Doc-resistant (231-DR) and Pali-resistant (231-PR) cell lines. Notably, SEL1L expression was significantly downregulated in both resistant variants, consistent with our observations in the carboplatin-resistant model (Figures S4C and S4D). On the other hands, we established a SEL1L knockdown MCF-7 cell line and compared its resistance to carboplatin. The results demonstrated that SEL1L shRNA reduced cellular sensitivity to carboplatin, increasing the IC50 value from 18.7 to 32.5 μM (Figure S4E). These findings support the broader significance of our study.

In conclusion, SEL1L is significantly downregulated in BRC tissues, with further reductions observed in carboplatin-resistant TNBC cell lines and clinical lymph node metastases. SEL1L regulates Shh expression by influencing its ubiquitination and degradation via HRD1, thereby affecting the hedgehog signaling pathway. This regulation influences EMT, metastasis, and carboplatin resistance in tumor cells both in vitro and in vivo (Graphical abstract).

Discussion

The activation of various oncogenes, coupled with deficiencies in endogenous tumor suppressors, plays a crucial role in uncontrolled tumor metastasis and drug resistance in cancer. Addressing the dual challenge of suppressing oncogenes while activating tumor suppressors remains a significant focus in cancer research. SEL1L is notably deficient in multiple cancer types, including pancreatic adenocarcinoma, liver cancer, and uterine carcinosarcoma.17,31,32 Regarding BRC, previous studies have reported that SEL1L reduces tumor cell aggressiveness both in vivo and in vitro.33 However, the downstream signaling pathways and specific mechanisms involving SEL1L in BRC have remained unclear. In this study, we identified SEL1L as a tumor suppressor in BRC, functioning through the promotion of Shh protein ubiquitination and degradation. These findings provide novel insights into tumor suppressor mechanisms and establish a foundation for the development of innovative therapeutics targeting BRC.

SEL1L, along with HRD1, has previously been linked to cancer pathogenesis. Within this complex, HRD1 serves as an E3-ubiquitin ligase that tags proteins for degradation by the proteasome.34,35 For example, in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, upregulation of the SEL1L-HRD1 complex destabilizes ALKBH1, a mitochondrial protein.36 Similarly, in liver cancer, the SEL1L-HRD1 ERAD pathway plays a quality control role by degrading misfolded nascent tumor suppressor WNT5A in the ER.17 Moreover, the SEL1L-HRD1 complex has been shown to regulate cell adhesion by regulating tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) and enhancing the transcriptional activity of the tumor suppressor gene PTEN. These regulatory changes have been linked to the accumulation of pancreatic cancer cells in the G1 phase of the cell cycle, potentially inhibiting their metastatic spread.37,38 Additionally, studies have established that SEL1L functions as a critical adaptor molecule, stabilizing HRD1 and facilitating the recruitment of substrates for degradation.16,39 These findings strongly suggest that SEL1L mediates the ubiquitination and degradation of Shh protein via HRD1. Notably, we observed a similar phenomenon in BRC, where SEL1L-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of Shh also relies on HRD1. This highlights the functional conservation of the SEL1L-HRD1 complex across different cell types and provides further evidence supporting the role of this complex in maintaining ER homeostasis.

EMT serves as a critical initial step in the development of an invasive phenotype, and the Sonic hedgehog (Shh) signaling pathway is closely associated with poor prognosis and EMT in cancers.40 In the presence of the Shh ligand, it binds to PTCH1, inhibiting its ability to deactivate SMO. Once activated, SMO interacts with SUFU, which is bound to KIF7 and GLIFL. SMO inhibits SUFU, preventing the phosphorylation of GLIFL, allowing KIF7 to dissociate and migrate to the cilia. GLIFL is subsequently cleaved into GLI1/2, which translocates to the nucleus and initiates the transcription and translation of PTCH1, NANOG, SOX2, c-MYC, and other Shh-related genes.41 It is clear that Shh and Gli1 are pivotal components of the hedgehog signaling pathway. Our current study confirms that SEL1L regulates Shh protein levels by influencing its ubiquitination and degradation. However, the precise mechanism by which SEL1L influences Gli1 expression remains unclear, particularly its regulation of Gli1 mRNA and protein levels. This remains a key question for future research, which we intend to address in subsequent studies.42,43

Carboplatin is one of the most commonly used chemotherapeutic agents for BRC in clinical practice; however, the development of cellular resistance often compromises its efficacy and threatens patient survival. In this study, we identified SEL1L as a key regulator of carboplatin resistance in BRC. Notably, during the establishment of drug-resistant cell lines for other clinically relevant chemotherapeutic agents, we observed similar phenomena, suggesting that SEL1L-mediated regulation of drug resistance may have broader clinical implications. To date, no small molecule inhibitors targeting SEL1L have been identified to effectively modulate its expression in tumor therapy. Building upon the findings of this study, we propose to explore the discovery of small molecule compounds targeting SEL1L using emerging technologies such as AlphaFold, with the ultimate goal of developing strategies to mitigate chemotherapy resistance in clinical settings.

In conclusion, SEL1L suppresses TNBC cell metastasis and EMT, while enhancing sensitivity to carboplatin and others chemotherapeutic agents by increasing the ubiquitination and degradation of the oncogenic protein Shh. These findings reveal a novel tumor-suppressive SEL1L-Shh-Gli1 signaling axis in cancer cells. Therefore, strategies to upregulate SEL1L expression represent a promising therapeutic approach for the treatment of TNBC.

Limitations of the study

To investigate the clinical correlation between SEL1L expression and drug resistance, comparing SEL1L levels in the same patient before and after neoadjuvant therapy is of significant value, as it helps control for multiple confounding factors. However, obtaining samples that meet these criteria has proven to be challenging. We attempted to collect appropriate clinical samples, but during our collection period, several patients who underwent neoadjuvant therapy were assessed as pathological complete response (pCR) in post-treatment evaluations, leaving no residual tumor tissue available for analysis. Nevertheless, we plan to continue collecting such samples in the future to further investigate the potential association between SEL1L expression and clinical responses to drug treatment.

Moreover, to date, we have not further analyzed the structural features of SEL1L. Elucidating the structure of SEL1L in future research and identifying its agonists or inhibitors could provide novel insights into developing targeted therapies for BRC.

Experimental model and study participant details

Patient recruitment and ethical approval

Six paired BRC samples, lymph node metastasis tissues, and adjacent normal tissues were collected from the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (ethical official number: (2023) No. 249). The studies were approved by the hospital’s ethics committee, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Given that this study specifically focuses on BRC, a disease predominantly affecting women, all collected samples were derived from female individuals. As such, the potential confounding effect of gender was inherently minimized, ensuring that gender did not act as a confounding variable in the analysis. All resected cancer samples were verified by clinical diagnostic pathology. None of the patients had received local or systemic neoadjuvant radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or targeted therapy prior to sample collection. The detailed information of the patients is presented in Table S1.

Tumor xenograft in mice

Tumor xenograft experiments were conducted following protocols approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee, Suzhou Institute of Nano-Tech and Nano-Bionics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (SINANO/EC/2025-099), adhering to all relevant regulatory standards.44,45 Briefly, 107 human BRC M231-NC or M231-SEL1L shRNA cells were subcutaneously injected into 8-week-old female nude mice. Once tumor volumes reached approximately 400 mm3, the mice were treated with intraperitoneal injections of Carboplatin at a dose of 40 mg/kg for 20 days. Mice were subsequently euthanized, and tumors were harvested for further analysis. Tumor growth inhibition (TGI) was calculated using the formula: TGI = [1 – (Ti - T0)/(Ci - C0)] × 100%, where Ti represents the mean tumor volume (TV) of the treatment group on the initial measurement day, T0 is the mean TV of the treatment group on the first day of dosing, Ci is the mean TV of the control group on the initial measurement day, and C0 is the TV of the control group on the first day of dosing. Tumor volume was measured and calculated using the formula: Tumor volume = 0.55 × length × width2.

In this study, all experimental mice were female, consistent with the sex-specific nature of BRC research.

Cell culture

Cancer cell lines, including MDA-MB-231, BT-549, M231 CR, 549 CR, MCF-7, and 293T, were re-authenticated by Short Tandem Repeat (STR) profiling and matched the expected profiles. Cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. Mycoplasma testing of all cell lines is performed every 6 months.

Method details

Cell proliferation assay

Breast cancer cells, including both normal and Carboplatin-resistant cells, were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1×103 cells per well (100 μL/well) and incubated overnight. The cells were treated with 100 μL of CTG solute (#G7571, Promega) and incubated for 10 min at 37°C at specific time points: 0, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h. Cell viability readings were subsequently measured using a Synergy plate reader (BioTek).

Western blot analysis

Cells or tissues were harvested and lysed in cold RIPA lysis buffer (#20101ES60, YEASEN) supplemented with PMSF and protease inhibitors. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (#P0010, Beyotime). A total of 40 μg of protein was loaded onto a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred to a 0.22 μm PVDF membrane (#03010040001, Merck). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk and incubated overnight at 4°C with specific primary antibodies (1:1000). After three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Blots were visualized using chemiluminescence and imaged with the Fujifilm LAS 4000 luminescent image analyzer (Fujifilm Life Science). Grayscale values were quantified using Quantity One software (Bio-Rad).

Quantitative real-time PCR (QRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (#R401-01, Vazyme) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA with the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol (#R211-01, Vazyme). mRNA levels of SEL1L, Shh, Gli1, and EMT markers were measured using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (#Q411-02, Vazyme) on the LightCycler480 II (Roche). GAPDH was used as the normalization control, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primers used in the PCR reaction are list in Table S2.

Stable transfection of cell lines

SEL1L cDNA, SEL1L shRNA, or control (Ctrl) shRNA were cloned into the lentiviral vector pMD 2.G. 293T cells were transfected with these constructs to generate infectious viral particles. The BRC cell lines MDA-MB-231, BT-549, M231 CR, and 549 CR were infected with the viruses, and transfected cells were selected using puromycin. SEL1L expression in the stably transfected cells was assessed using Western blot and qPCR.

The full-length Shh cDNA plasmid was constructed using the PCDNA3.0+ vector and obtained from Vigene Biosciences. To generate stable Shh-overexpressing cells, the cells were transfected with the Shh cDNA plasmid and treated with G418 (50 ng/mL) at 22 μL/mL for 10 days. The overexpression of Shh was confirmed via Western blot analysis.

Cell migration, invasion and wound healing assays

Cell migration and invasion assays were conducted using 24-well plates. For the migration and invasion experiments, 2000 cells were seeded in the upper chamber with 200 μL of serum-free DMEM medium, while 10% FBS-conditioned medium (500 μL) was added to the lower chamber of the plates. For the invasion assay, 50 μL of Matrigel was coated in the upper chamber before cell seeding. Following a 12-h incubation period, the membranes of the chambers were stained with Giemsa, imaged microscopically, and analyzed.

For the wound healing assay, cells were cultured in 6-well plates until they reached approximately 100% confluency. Scratches were created on the cell monolayer using a 20 μL pipette tip, and the cells were subsequently washed with PBS and incubated in DMEM containing 2% FBS. Images of the scratch area were taken at 0 h and 12 h to assess wound closure.

Reconstruction of drug-resistant breast cancer cell lines

In this study, we employed the drug concentration increment method, which was primarily based on the classical protocol described in Journal Anticancer Research.46 MDA-MB-231 or BT-549 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were initially treated with 1 μM Carboplatin for 24 h. After treatment, the culture medium was discarded, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. Following a 24-h recovery period, a second treatment with 1 μM Carboplatin was applied for another 24 h, after which the cells were digested and passaged. This process of drug exposure and recovery was repeated until the cells regained their original morphology. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to 5 μM and 10 μM Carboplatin in 6–8 treatment cycles, continuing until the cells demonstrated stable growth in the presence of 35 μM Carboplatin. After a 6-month screening period, Carboplatin-resistant BRC cell lines were successfully established.

Following the same methodology used to generate carboplatin-resistant cell lines, we employed a stepwise concentration-increasing selection strategy to establish docetaxel- and Palifosfamide-resistant 231 cell lines (231-DR and 231-PR), and subsequently carried out downstream experiments.

Cell morphology

Following the reconstruction of stable transfected or Carboplatin-resistant cell lines, the cells were cultured in 24 mm dishes for 2 days in the appropriate medium. Cells were then stained with DIO dye and visualized using confocal microscopy to examine their morphology.

Cell apoptosis analysis

Cells were seeded in six-well plates and treated with or without Carboplatin for 48 h. After treatment, cells were centrifuged and resuspended in 500 μL of binding buffer containing PI and FITC-Annexin V, as per the instructions of the Apoptosis Detection Kit (#A211-01-AC, Vazyme). Apoptosis was subsequently analyzed using a flow cytometer, following established protocols.

Colony formation assay

The bottoms of 35 mm2 dishes were coated with 1 mL of 0.5% agarose supplemented with complete medium to form a base layer. After this bottom layer solidified, 5000 cells in 1 mL of complete medium were mixed with 0.15 mL of 4% low melting agarose, then spread onto the dishes and allowed to solidify. The dishes were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 14 days. Colonies were counted and imaged under a Zoom-Stereo microscope SZX16 (OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan).

Co-immunoprecipitation

Co-immunoprecipitation was performed as previously described.44,45 Briefly, 400 μL of M231 cell lysates were incubated with a primary monoclonal antibody (1:500 dilution) or mouse normal IgG (control) at 4°C for 4 h. This was followed by incubation with 20 μL of 1% fetal bovine serum (FBS)-blocked protein A/G agarose beads at 4°C overnight with rotation. The immune complexes were released from the beads in SDS loading buffer and analyzed by Western blotting as described earlier.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

IHC staining was performed on paraffin-embedded clinical BRC or mouse xenograft tissue sections. The sections were dewaxed and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Heat-mediated antigen retrieval was conducted using Citrate Unmasking Solution (#P0083, Beyotime). After blocking at room temperature for 1 h, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with gentle rocking using primary antibodies against SEL1L, Gli1 and Shh (Proteintech), diluted at a ratio of 1:200 in 3% BSA solution. Diaminobenzidine (DAB) (#DA1010, Solarbio) was used to achieve optimal staining intensity. Stained tissues were visualized using the NanoZoomer S210 microscope (Visiopharm, Denmark).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Significant differences were designated as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and data are reported as mean ± S.D unless otherwise noted. Specific statistical tests, multiple comparison analyses, and numbers of replicates that were used for each comparison are provided in the figure legends.
