Beyond cargo clearance: p62 coordinates cAMP signalling and autophagy to drive cell fate decisions in Dictyostelium discoideum
Authors: Saksham Gautam, Shweta Saran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTp62 is a multi-domain selective autophagy receptor and signaling scaffold conserved across eukaryotes. However, its evolutionary roots and functional significance in early-diverging eukaryotes like Dictyostelium have remained unexplored. In our recent study, we characterized p62 in the social amoeba Dictyostelium discoideum. Loss of p62 significantly delayed the starvation induced development and led to the formation of smaller multicellular structures. Altered p62 levels also disrupted autophagy flux and cell fate commitment in Dictyostelium cells. Treatment of p62 knockout (p62−) cells with pulses of exogenous cyclic AMP (cAMP) partially rescued developmental defects, suggesting a potential role for p62 in sustaining intracellular cAMP levels necessary for development. Taken together, p62 emerges as an evolutionarily conserved coordinator of nutrient sensing, second messenger signaling, and autophagy that promotes multicellular development in Dictyostelium.KEYWORDS: Dictyostelium, p62 cell differentiation, p62 (SQSTM1), autophagy

Under nutrient stress conditions, a cell must coordinate three fundamental responses: adapt, survive, or die. In most conditions, these responses function through metabolic reprogramming, autophagy and cell fate commitment, which are largely studied as parallel independent pathways. In metazoans, the multi-domain scaffolding adaptor p62 serves as a pivotal signaling hub during different types of stress responses, making it a compelling candidate through which these responses may be integrated. While the significance of p62 is well recognized in metazoans – with its dysregulation being linked to neurodegeneration and cancer, its evolutionary origins and functional significance in unicellular eukaryotes remain largely unexplored. To address this knowledge gap, we selected the eukaryotic amoeba, Dictyostelium discoideum (hereafter referred to as Dictyostelium) (Figure 1). In Dictyostelium, multicellular development is part of the survival strategy upon nutrient starvation. Upon depletion of food, solitary amoebae start to aggregate in response to cAMP pulses, ultimately giving rise to a mature fruiting body consisting of a sorus (with several viable spores) atop a stalk, composed of dead vacuolated cells. This transition is regulated by rhythmic pulses of cAMP that guide the chemotactic cells and also act as an essential secondary messenger for the expression of key signaling proteins, including genes involved in cAMP signaling. During development, metabolically stressed amoeba, heavily rely on macroautophagy to recycle cellular components and drive autophagic cell death for stalk-cell differentiation, making it an inseparable part of development.

p62 coordinates cAMP signaling and autophagy for efficient multicellular development and cell-fate decisions in Dictyostelium. In wild-type cells, p62 helps maintain intracellular cAMP at threshold levels, potentially by functioning as a scaffold for RegA, as well as specific aspects of degradation during autophagy. Loss of p62 knockout (p62−) results in altered cAMP levels and reduced autophagic flux, leading to impaired development, which is characterized by defects in aggregation, gene expression, and differentiation. Exogenous cAMP partially rescues the developmental defects in starved p62− cells by directly restoring intracellular cAMP and autophagy flux levels, possibly through modulation of the AMPK-mTOR axis.

p62 is among the well characterized selective autophagy receptors. Notably, only a subset of p62 domains, primarily those involved in autophagy, are structurally conserved from Dictyostelium to humans, underscoring its ancient and likely indispensable role in selective autophagy. To elucidate the functional significance of this simpler form of p62, we characterized p62 mutant strains of Dictyostelium in our recent study [1]. The p62 knockout (p62−) cells showed defects in nutrient uptake, resulting in reduced intracellular glucose and impaired proliferation. These metabolic defects set the stage for more vital developmental abnormalities, as starved p62− cells failed to elevate intracellular cAMP levels to wild-type ones, required for initiating normal development. p62− cells also exhibited reduced expression of genes involved in cAMP signaling, including adenylyl cyclases and the cAMP receptor, except intracellular phosphodiesterase (regA), possibly contributing to aberrant intracellular cAMP levels. Development was delayed and Dictyostelium formed smaller structures with less viable spores.

In addition to these developmental defects, p62− cells also exhibited reduced basal and starvation-induced autophagy flux, accompanied by an accumulation of ubiquitinated proteins and reduced level of phosphorylated AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPKα) at Thr172. Treatment of p62− and wild-type cells with external cAMP pulses partially restored aggregate size, fruiting body length, and autophagic flux levels in p62− cells, while leaving wild-type cells unaffected. This reflects that intracellular cAMP and autophagic flux operate at optimal threshold levels in wild-type cells.

The most interesting downstream consequence of this dual failure in cAMP signaling and autophagy manifested clearly in fate determination in p62− cells. Using cell-lineage tracing experiments with fluorescently labeled cells, we determined the cell fate commitment of p62 mutants. In chimeric structures, the majority of p62− cells were consistently excluded from the prestalk/stalk regions and diverted toward prespore/spore formation, while p62-overexpressing (p62OE) cells showed the opposite phenotype. This shows that p62 levels not only modulate the developmental phenotype but also influence cell fate decisions.

These findings establish p62 as a signaling gatekeeper that helps sustain intracellular cAMP at threshold levels, possibly by restricting phosphodiesterase (RegA) activity, a mechanism analogous to the mammalian cells. Furthermore, our results also show that, p62 contributes to the autophagy not only as a receptor for ubiquitinated proteins but also influences autophagy through cAMP signaling. These observations represent a novel mechanism involving p62 and cAMP in the fine-tuned regulation of autophagy in an early-diverging eukaryote such as Dictyostelium. In the absence of p62, neither the cAMP nor autophagy reaches the required threshold, leading cells to preferentially adopt abnormal prespore identity, possibly due to a failure of the mechanism permitting prestalk differentiation. When p62 is present in excess amounts, the balance moves in opposite direction, favoring prestalk fate. Thus, cell fate decisions in Dictyostelium appear to be indirectly influenced by p62-mediated signaling pathways. Remarkably, the inability of cAMP treatment to completely rescue p62− phenotypes highlights a broader role of p62 beyond cAMP signaling. Our unpublished data addresses this question, suggesting a critical role of p62 in maintaining mitochondrial integrity and function.

Collectively, our study positions p62 as an evolutionarily ancient mediator of cell fate commitment and provides evidence for its versatile roles in coordinating nutrient sensing, second messenger signaling, and autophagy in a simple eukaryote, Dictyostelium discoideum. These findings also raise the possibility that pathways regulated by its mammalian counterpart, such as the NF-κB (Nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells), mTORC1 (Mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1), and NRF2 (Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2) axes, may also function in a coordinated manner under stress conditions. However, the precise mechanisms by which p62 regulates intracellular cAMP levels, autophagy, and sustains AMPK phosphorylation in Dictyostelium remain open questions that will define the next phase of our studies.
