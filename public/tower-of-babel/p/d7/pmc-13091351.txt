Engineered Salmonella-mediated c-di-AMP delivery activates STING to remodel the tumor microenvironment
Authors: Yuanjia Huang, Linghua Piao, Haiwen Zhang, Xiande Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway is a pivotal mediator of innate immunity that senses microbial cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs) to initiate potent type I interferon (IFN-I) responses and proinflammatory cytokine production. In this study, we developed a novel cancer immunotherapy platform by engineering an attenuated Salmonella Typhimurium ΔppGpp strain (designated SLdisA) to constitutively produce the STING agonist cyclic di-AMP (c-di-AMP). Through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo analyses, we demonstrated that SLdisA preferentially colonizes tumors and induces robust dendritic cell (DC) maturation through STING-dependent IFN-I signaling. This activation cascade promoted significant infiltration and activation of T cells, leading to potent antitumor immunity in CT26 colorectal carcinoma models. Furthermore, SLdisA exhibited synergistic tumor regression when combined with PD-1 blockade. Our findings establish engineered STING-activating bacteria as a transformative approach for cancer immunotherapy, offering: 1) targeted delivery of STING agonists to tumors, 2) reversal of immunosuppressive microenvironments, and 3) synergy with existing checkpoint blockade therapies. This platform addresses critical challenges in the field and presents new opportunities for treating immunotherapy-resistant cancers.

Introduction

Tumor immunotherapy is a transformative therapeutic strategy that augments the immune system’s ability to recognize and respond to tumor-associated antigens, thereby eliciting a robust, tumor-specific immune response.1 Central to this process is the tumor microenvironment (TME), a complex ecosystem that not only drives tumor progression but also critically modulates the efficacy of immunotherapeutic interventions. A key objective is to enhance immune cell infiltration while overcoming immunosuppressive barriers to create favorable conditions for tumor eradication.2 Within this intricate microenvironment, dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages serve as pivotal antigen-presenting cells (APCs), orchestrating the adaptive immune response against malignancies. These specialized cells mediate antigen capture, processing, and subsequent presentation through major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and II molecules to CD8+ and CD4+ T lymphocytes, respectively.3 Following uptake of tumor cell debris, DCs activate antitumor CD8+ T cells via cross-presentation, while macrophages primarily activate CD4+ T cells through the MHC class II pathway.4 The expression of co-stimulatory molecules and immunomodulatory cytokines facilitates the activation and expansion of effector T cell populations, including cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T helper cells, culminating in a potent antitumor immune response.5 However, effective APC activation within the TME faces substantial challenges. A primary limitation stems from the inherently poor immunogenicity of tumor cells, which frequently exhibit diminished or absent expression of MHC molecules, thereby compromising antigen presentation.6,7 Compounding these issues, even when tumor antigens are recognized, they may undergo suboptimal processing by APCs, significantly impeding the generation of effective immune responses.8,9 To overcome these barriers, significant research efforts have been directed toward the development of innate immune agonists. Notably, Toll-like receptors (TLR) agonists and stimulator of interferon genes (STING) agonists have emerged as promising candidates capable of enhancing intratumoral APC activation and antigen presentation, thereby potentially counteracting TME immunosuppression.10,11,12,13

The STING signaling pathway has gained prominence as a key target in antitumor immunity. However, emerging clinical evidence indicates that monotherapy with STING agonists shows limited efficacy in tumor regression and may paradoxically facilitate immune evasion mechanisms. A pivotal study led by Jenny P.Y. Ting’s research group at the University of North Carolina revealed that STING activation triggers substantial accumulation of regulatory B cells within the TME.14 This cellular response, mediated through the STING-IL-35 axis in B lymphocytes, subsequently suppresses natural killer (NK) cell proliferation and impairs NK-mediated antitumor immunity.14 These findings highlight the limitations of STING agonist monotherapy. Additionally, off-target STING activation in non-APCs, such as effector T cells, can induce apoptosis and impair immune memory.15 These insights into the dual-edged nature of STING pathway activation have spurred the exploration of targeted delivery strategies, including bacterial-mediated systems.

Salmonella species, as facultative anaerobic intracellular pathogens, demonstrate remarkable tumor tropism, colonizing neoplastic tissues at levels 100- to 1000-fold higher than in normal organs.16 The attenuated Salmonella typhimurium ΔppGpp strain employed in this study harbors dual mutations in the relA and spoT genes, which disrupt the synthesis of guanosine-3′,5′-bis(diphosphate) (ppGpp). This genetic modification results in a 105-to 106-fold reduction in virulence compared to the wild-type strain while maintaining robust tumor-targeting capabilities. Notably, the ΔppGpp strain exhibits lower pathogenicity than both the VNP20009 and SalpIL2 strains.17 Upon tumor colonization, S. typhimurium ΔppGpp elicits a robust immune response characterized by enhanced infiltration of macrophages and neutrophils. These immune cells, through the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, contribute to the suppression of tumor growth.18,19,20 To augment its therapeutic potential, the ΔppGpp strain has been genetically engineered to express various anticancer agents, including Noxa,21 FlaB,22 and ClyA,18 within the TME.

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks among the most prevalent malignancies, with an estimated 1,926,136 cases reported in 2022 and a high associated mortality.23 Over the past decade, neoadjuvant therapies utilizing immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have demonstrated promising efficacy in treatment.24 The TME functions as a complex ecosystem that encompasses immune cells, stromal cells, and extracellular components that collectively influence tumor progression and treatment response.25 It has been demonstrated that cytotoxic T cells within the TME are irreversibly depleted, while peripheral T cells are drawn to tumor locations as a result of immunotherapies. Consequently, an antitumor immune response is crucial for the effectiveness of such immunotherapeutic approaches.26 In this study, we engineered an attenuated S. typhimurium strain (SLdisA) to produce cyclic di-AMP (c-di-AMP), targeting STING activation in APCs within the TME. This approach leverages the bacterium’s intrinsic tumor tropism for targeted c-di-AMP delivery and harnesses complementary innate immune pathways activated by the bacterial vector, aiming for synergistic antitumor efficacy through a multimodal immunotherapeutic mechanism.

Results

Construction of a strain capable of producing c-di-AMP

The STING pathway serves as a critical link between innate and adaptive immunity by activating APCs, inducing type I interferon (IFN-I) production, and promoting neoantigen recognition.27 To exploit this mechanism for tumor immunotherapy, we engineered an attenuated S. typhimurium ΔppGpp strain (SL) to express cyclic di-AMP (c-di-AMP, CDA) synthetase (SLdisA; Figure 1A). Immunoblot analysis confirmed L-arabinose-inducible expression of the recombinant protein (Figure 1B), and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) verified successful production of c-di-AMP by SLdisA, as evidenced by its co-elution with a synthetic standard (Figure 1C). While SLdisA exhibited a modest growth delay during the early log phase compared to the parental SL strain, both cultures reached comparable optical densities by the stationary phase (Figure 1D), suggesting stable maintenance of the genetic modification without severe fitness costs. To assess potential toxicity, we conducted comprehensive histopathological evaluation of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) and serum biochemical analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed no obvious differences in pathological attributes among the different treatment groups (Figure 4C). Serum markers of hepatic function (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST]) remained within normal ranges across all groups (Figure 4D), confirming the absence of treatment-related hepatotoxicity. Together, these results demonstrate that SLdisA retains favorable tumor-targeting capability while exhibiting an excellent safety profile.

Comparative analysis revealed substantially greater gene upregulation in SLdisA-treated group relative to both phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)- and SL-treated groups (Figure 5A). Functional annotation identified significant enrichment of immune activation pathways in SLdisA-treated tumors (Figure 5B), which was consistent with elevated levels of key cytokines (IFN-β, granzyme B, IL-2, TNF-α, IL-6, and TGF-β) (Figure S3). Discussion

In this study, we developed SLdisA, an attenuated S. typhimurium strain engineered to deliver the STING agonist c-di-AMP, which potently activates DCs and enhances tumor-specific T cell responses. Our preclinical studies demonstrate that both SL and SLdisA exhibit sustained tumor tropism, maintaining viable colonies for 12 days—a property consistent with previous reports of Salmonella’s preferential colonization of diverse tumor types.29 One mechanisms for targeting tumors involves the chaotic network of blood vessels within them, which leads to the formation of oxygen-deficient and necrotic regions, thereby establishing ideal conditions for vital anaerobic microorganisms.30 Additionally, nutrients liberated from necrotic tumor cells, in conjunction with bacterial motility and chemokines, foster the growth and expansion of bacteria. Salmonella is compelled to infiltrate the tumor for its own safeguard following the host’s inflammatory response, as the TME exhibits a pronounced immunosuppressive nature, thereby thwarting the host immune system’s ability to eliminate the bacteria.31

This tumor-targeted delivery system achieves a dual therapeutic effect: (1) bacterial-mediated STING activation increases tumor immunogenicity and (2) intrinsic bacterial pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) provide additional immune stimulation, collectively reprogramming immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” microenvironments.32 Notably, the tumor-specific localization of SLdisA ensures systemic safety, as confirmed by comprehensive histopathological analysis, which revealed no treatment-related organ damage. Our findings establish microbial vector-mediated STING agonist delivery as a promising therapeutic paradigm that combines precise tumor targeting with potent immune activation. This approach addresses key limitations of conventional immunotherapy by simultaneously overcoming tumor immune evasion while minimizing off-target toxicity.

DisA functions as a canonical c-di-AMP synthetase that potently activates the STING signaling pathway through c-di-AMP.33 STING activation in APCs orchestrates all three critical signals required for T lymphocyte activation: (1) antigen presentation via MHC-peptide complexes, (2) co-stimulation through CD28-CD80/CD86, CD40/CD40L, 4-1BBL/4-1BB, and SLAM-SLAM associated protein interactions, and (3) cytokine-mediated paracrine signaling that ultimately determines T cell differentiation and function.27 Our experimental data demonstrate that SLdisA enhances BMDC maturation, upregulating expression of MHC II involved in antigen presentation and co-stimulation. This DC activation translated functionally to increased splenocyte proliferation, providing mechanistic insight into the antitumor effects of SLdisA. Salmonella infection is initially detected by TLRs in macrophages and DCs.34 These receptors recognize various PAMPs and stimulate the host’s immune response, thereby promoting antitumor effects.34 The c-di-AMP produced by disA increases the antigen-presenting ability of APCs, activating T cells to eliminate tumors. This coordinated activation of DCs within the TME robustly stimulates lymphocyte responses, which underlies the enhanced therapeutic efficacy of SLdisA.

Through comprehensive immunological profiling, we demonstrate that SLdisA (1) activates multiple immune-related pathways, (2) promotes DC maturation within the TME, and (3) enhances infiltration of M1 macrophages, CD4+, and CD8+ T cells. Notably, SLdisA treatment upregulated expression of T cell activation markers (CD8a, granzyme B) and stimulated robust IFN-I responses, indicating the establishment of a pro-inflammatory tumor milieu conducive to effective antitumor immunity. These findings establish that SLdisA-mediated immune reprogramming initiates potent T cell responses through DC-dependent mechanisms. Our results align with previous work showing that SLdacA, another c-di-AMP-producing S. typhimurium strain, similarly enhances T cell recruitment and activation,29 collectively demonstrating that bacterial-mediated c-di-AMP delivery represents a robust and translatable strategy for overcoming tumor immune evasion.

Notably, we observed significant PD-L1 upregulation following SLdisA treatment. The upregulation of PD-L1 expression in the tumors of the SLdisA group is attributed to the activation of STING signaling35 and the ensuing inflammatory TME.36 Recognizing this as an adaptive immune resistance mechanism, we hypothesized that combining SLdisA with PD-1 blockade would enhance therapeutic efficacy. This combination indeed demonstrated superior antitumor activity compared to either monotherapy. The PD-1/PD-L1 axis plays a pivotal role in regulating T cell activation, proliferation, and effector functions, with its inhibition representing a cornerstone of modern cancer immunotherapy.37,38,39 While PD-1/PD-L1 blockade has demonstrated remarkable clinical success across multiple malignancies,40 its efficacy remains limited in non-inflamed (“cold”) tumors.41 Bacterial therapies like SLdisA address this limitation by converting immunologically quiescent tumors into inflamed microenvironments. This paradigm is supported by recent studies showing enhanced therapeutic effects when combining bacterial therapies with checkpoint inhibition. For instance, E. coli Nissle1917 synergized with PD-L1 blockade by expanding tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes42 and FlaB-expressing Salmonella potentiated IL-15/PD-L1 combination therapy in mouse 4T1 and B16F10 models.43 Our findings reveal that SLdisA further augments PD-1 blockade efficacy through STING pathway activation, creating a pro-inflammatory tumor milieu with enhanced T cell infiltration. This work establishes bacterial-mediated STING activation as a powerful strategy for overcoming resistance to checkpoint immunotherapy.

A variety of bacterial-based therapies have previously been explored for cancer treatment.44 In clinical trials, these oncolytic strains have either failed to demonstrate robust efficacy or have been impeded by dose-limiting toxicities.44 Although further preclinical studies are required to demonstrate the long-term safety of SLdisA, positive indications of efficacy would facilitate the development of additional engineered strains with functionalities tailored to the specific requirements of different cancers and patients. While bacterial-mediated tumor immunotherapy is still in the early stages of application in human therapeutics, our research highlights its potential and provides guidance for developing future clinical evaluation strategies.

It is important to note that the present study was conducted in a single murine CRC model. Future work should validate the therapeutic efficacy of SLdisA in a broader panel of human CRC cell lines and patient-derived organoids to fully establish its translational potential.

In summary, in this study, we engineered an attenuated S. typhimurium ΔppGpp strain to express the c-di-AMP synthase DisA, creating a potent microbial-based immunotherapeutic platform. SLdisA exerts its antitumor effects through a multi-pronged mechanism: (1) STING pathway activation enhances DC antigen-presentation capacity, (2) subsequent T cell activation and recruitment are amplified, and (3) synergistic enhancement of PD-1 checkpoint blockade efficacy is achieved. These findings advance the clinical translation potential of bacterial-mediated cancer immunotherapy by demonstrating how engineered microbes can remodel the TME to overcome key limitations of current immunotherapies.

Materials and methods

Cell line, plasmid, and bacterial strain

CT26 cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; VA, USA) and cultured under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2) in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Gibco, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Newzerum, Christchurch, New Zealand) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The S. typhimurium ΔppGpp strain (relA:cat, spoT:kan, SL),45 genetically engineered to express the luciferase gene (lux) and a kanamycin resistance marker, was generously provided by J. J. Min (Institute for Molecular Imaging and Theranostics, Chonnam National University Hwasun Hospital, Jeonnam, Republic of Korea). The disA gene, encoding c-di-AMP synthase, was derived from Bacillus thuringiensis BMB171 (GenBank accession: CP001903.1).46 A C-terminal FLAG epitope tag was incorporated at the C-terminus of disA, and the modified gene sequence was synthesized by General Biol (Anhui, China). The disA-FLAG construct was subsequently cloned into the pBAD plasmid vector, which carries an ampicillin resistance gene, using NcoI and PmeI re-striction sites. The recombinant plasmid (pBAD-disA) was then introduced into S.typhimurium ΔppGpp via electroporation (1.8 kV; Bio-Rad). Transformed strains were maintained in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with kanamycin (50 μg/mL) and ampicillin (100 μg/mL) and preserved at −80°C in 25% glycerol for long-term storage.

CDA test by HPLC

To assess cyclic di-AMP (CDA) production in vitro, bacterial cultures were collected by centrifugation, washed twice with Dulbecco’s PBS (DPBS), and resuspended to a concentration of 1010 cells/mL before storage at −80°C. After extraction from bacterial pellets, CDA was quantified using HPLC (Waters e2695). Chromatographic separation was performed using a gradient elution system comprising two mobile phases: mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid) and mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). The column temperature was maintained at 30°C, and the flow rate was set at 0.8 mL/min.

Culturing dendritic cells

BMDCs were generated by aseptically harvesting bone marrow cells from murine tibiae and femora. Following extraction, erythrocytes were lysed using a sterile erythrocyte lysis buffer (Wuhan Servicebio Technology, Co., Ltd.). The isolated bone marrow cells were subsequently resuspended in RPMI 1640-GlutaMax medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol, penicillin-streptomycin (100 IU/mL and 100 μg/mL), and 20 ng/mL granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF; Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Starting on day 3, fresh culture medium was added every other day. After 8 days, BMDCs were ready for subsequent experiments.

Mice experiments

Specific pathogen free male BALB/c mice (5–6 weeks old) were purchased from the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center (Guangzhou, China). The Hainan University Animal Research Committee approved the animal protocols used in this study. To establish tumor-bearing models, CT26 cells (1 × 106 cells) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse. Tumor growth was monitored every other day, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: (length × width2)/2. Once tumors reached a volume of 90–200 mm3, mice were randomized into treatment groups and administered either PBS, S. typhimurium ΔppGpp (SL), or SLdisA at a dose of 3.75 × 107 colony-forming units (CFUs) in 125 μL via intravenous injection. To induce gene expression, L-arabinose (V900920, Sigma, USA) was administered intraperitoneally at 0.15 g per mouse daily, starting 24 h post-bacterial injection and continuing for 15 days.

For combination therapy experiments involving PD-1 blockade, mice were randomly assigned to the following groups: (1) control, (2) anti-PD-1, (3) SL, (4) SL + anti-PD-1, (5) SLdisA, and (6) SLdisA + anti-PD-1. The PD-1 antibody (Sintilimab Injection) was administered intravenously at 10 mg/kg on days 3 and 10 post-tumor inoculation. Humane endpoints were strictly observed, and mice with tumors exceeding 1500 mm3 were euthanized.

Western blotting analysis

To validate in vitro expression of the disA protein, bacterial cultures at an optical density (OD600) of 0.5–0.7 were induced with 4% L-arabinose. Following a 4.5-h induction period, bacterial cells were pelleted by centrifugation. Protein expression was subsequently confirmed via immunoblotting using an anti-FLAG antibody (Biosynthesis Biotechnology, Beijing, China).

qPCR analysis

To evaluate cytokine expression within the TME, tumor tissues were harvested 24 h post-L-arabinose administration. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Wuhan Servicebio Technology, Co., Ltd.) and subsequently reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a SuperScript II cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara Biomedical Technology, Beijing, China). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis was conducted using ChamQ SYBR Color qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) to quantify target gene expression levels. In vitro immune activation assay

To characterize STING-dependent DC activation, BMDCs were co-cultured with bacterial strains for 90 min before replacing the media with antibiotic-containing RPMI 1640. To investigate the STING dependence of SLdisA-induced immune activation, co-treatment experiments were performed with the STING inhibitor H-151 (2 μM final concentration; MedChemExpress, USA). Supernatants were collected 4 h post-treatment for subsequent analysis. The concentration of IFN-β in BMDC supernatants was quantified using commercially available ELISA kits (Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd), with absorbance measurements normalized to a recombinant IFN-β standard curve.

Splenocyte proliferation assay

To assess T cell proliferation capacity, splenocytes were aseptically isolated from murine spleens and labeled with carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Following stimulation of BMDCs as previously described, CFSE-labeled splenocytes were co-cultured with BMDCs at a 10:1 ratio. After 72 h of co-culture, cells were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry to evaluate splenocyte proliferation.

Biochemical index test

Serum levels of ALT and AST were quantified using a BS-240vet biochemical analyzer. All assay kits were procured from Shenzhen Mindray Animal Medical Technology Co., Ltd., and analyses were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Biodistribution in vivo

To assess the in vivo biodistribution of SLdisA, tumor-bearing mice received intravenous injections of either SL or SLdisA via the tail vein. L-arabinose was administered intraperitoneally 24 h post-bacterial inoculation. Bioluminescence imaging was performed on days 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 using an IVIS 100 imaging system (Caliper Life Sciences) to monitor Lux-derived signals from S. typhimurium ΔppGpp, enabling longitudinal tracking of bacterial localization and persistence.

Bacteria counting

To quantify bacterial colonization in various organs, the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and tumor were aseptically harvested from tumor-bearing mice at 3 and 5 days post-inoculation. Tissues were homogenized and serially diluted in sterile PBS. Aliquots of tissue suspensions were plated on LB agar supplemented with kanamycin (50 μg/mL) and incubated overnight at 37°C. Bacterial loads were determined by colony counting and normalized to tissue weight using the following calculation: Y × 10Z × (1 + X) × 10/X (CFU/g; where X = tissue weight, Y = colonies on the Petri dish, and Z = dilution factor).

H&E staining

For histological analysis, mice were euthanized 3 days post-treatment, and major organs (heart, lungs, liver, spleen, and kidneys) were immediately harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. The tissues were subsequently processed using standard paraffin embedding protocols and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. For paraffin section dewaxing, the following solutions were applied sequentially: environmentally friendly dewaxing solution I for 20 min, environmentally friendly dewaxing solution II for 20 min, anhydrous ethanol I for 5 min, anhydrous ethanol II for 5 min, and 75% alcohol for 5 min, followed by a rinse with clean water. For H&E staining, the paraffin sections were soaked in hematoxylin staining solution for 3–5 min, then washed with water. Sections were subsequently treated with differentiation solution, washed again with water, soaked in blue solution, and rinsed with running water. Paraffin sections were then sequentially immersed in 85% and 95% alcohol solutions for 5 min each, followed by staining in eosin solution for 5 min. To dehydrate and clear the sections , they were sequentially immerse in 95% ethanol I for 5 min, 95% ethanol II for 5 min, anhydrous ethanol I for 5 min, anhydrous ethanol II for 5 min, xylene I for 5 min, and xylene II for 5 min. After drying, sections were sealed with neutral gum. Digital images of the stained sections were acquired using a high-resolution slide scanner for comprehensive pathological evaluation.

Flow cytometry analysis

In vitro immunophenotyping: BMDCs were stimulated with bacterial strains, and splenocytes were co-cultured with activated BMDCs. Cells were subsequently stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies targeting surface markers, including FITC anti-mouse CD11c (clone N418, Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and PE-Cy7 anti-mouse MHC II (clone M5/114, Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), and analyzed using a CytoFLEX cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA).

In vivo immune profiling: Tumors were excised 48 h post-bacterial administration and processed into single-cell suspensions via enzymatic digestion with 1 mg/mL collagenase type I (Wuhan Servicebio Technology) for 45 min at 37°C. After mechanical dissociation and filtration through a 70 μm strainer, cells were stained with fluorescent antibodies for 30 min. The following antibodies were used: FITC anti-mouse F4/80 (clone CI: A3-1), APC anti-mouse CD86 (clone GL-1), FITC anti-mouse CD3 (clone 17A2), APC anti-mouse CD4 (clone GK1.5), and APC anti-mouse CD8 (clone YTS-169), all purchased from Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Immune cell populations were quantified using a CytoFLEX cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), acquiring a minimum of 10,000 events per sample.

RNA sequencing and transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from tumor samples (n = 3 per PBS/SLdisA group; n = 2 per SL group) using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA integrity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Prior to sequencing, the raw data underwent filtration to produce high-quality clean data. All subsequent analyses utilized these clean data. cDNA libraries were prepared with the Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit v2 (Illumina) as previously described.50 Sequencing reads were aligned and quantified using Cufflinks (v2.2.1),51 with gene expression levels reported as transcripts per million (TPM). Differential expression genes (DEGs) analysis was performed using the criteria |log2(FoldChange)| > 1 & padj< 0.05.

Three comparative DEG sets were generated: PBS vs. SL, SL vs. SLdisA, and PBS vs. SLdisA. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) software (version 4.0.3) was used to perform enrichment analysis for DEGs between the SL and SLdisA group in GO and KEGG pathways. For immunocytometric analysis, transcriptomic profiles were analyzed using CIBERSORT (v1.03) with a murine immunocyte reference matrix to infer TME composition.52

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software). Two-tailed Student’s t test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to analyzed statistical significance. Survival curves were analyzed using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise noted.
