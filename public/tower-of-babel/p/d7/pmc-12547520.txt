DRP-PSM: Multi-Level Feature Integration Reveals Hierarchical Contributions to Pathogenic Synonymous Mutation Prediction
Authors: Jinsong Cai, Fangfang Jin, Na Cheng, Junfeng Xia, Chen Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Synonymous mutations, a unique class of genetic variants, do not change the amino acid sequence of the encoded protein. Despite this, they can impact protein stability and function through diverse molecular mechanisms. Such subtle alterations can contribute to disease initiation and progression, making the prediction of pathogenic synonymous mutations crucial for understanding disease mechanisms as well as improving clinical diagnosis and treatment. In this study, we introduce DRP-PSM, a novel method for predicting pathogenic synonymous mutations that integrates DNA, RNA, and protein-level biological features. Building upon our earlier method, DRP-PSM greatly expands the feature set by incorporating protein-level information alongside enriched DNA and RNA signatures. Our goal is to elucidate how features from distinct biological levels contribute to the pathogenicity of synonymous mutations and to construct a comprehensive, multilevel prediction framework. DRP-PSM systematically integrates DNA-, RNA-, and protein-level sequence and structural features. Experimental results indicate that DNA-level features contribute the most to prediction accuracy, followed by RNA-level features, whereas protein-level features add only marginal utility. Notably, incorporating additional sequence- and structure-based descriptors yielded little performance gain, while biological features such as DNA conservation and splicing effect consistently dominated. These findings highlight that synonymous mutations primarily exert pathogenic effects through perturbations in splicing or transcriptional efficiency, rather than through translational or post-translational processes. This insight enhances our mechanistic understanding of their biological impact and underscores regulatory mechanisms as key targets for future therapeutic intervention.

Introduction

Synonymous mutations are increasingly recognized as contributors to disease through diverse molecular mechanisms affecting DNA, RNA, and protein levels. These include altering regulatory element activity, causing aberrant RNA splicing, reducing mRNA stability, and impairing cotranslational protein folding. − Consequently, a comprehensive analysis of their functional impacts across these levels is essential for understanding pathogenic mechanisms. Current methods for predicting pathogenic synonymous mutations fall into two main categories: general methods (assessing various mutation types, including synonymous mutations) and specific methods (focused exclusively on synonymous mutations). General methods for predicting pathogenic mutations include ML-GVP, CADD, PhD-SNPg, PredictSNP2, FATHHMM-MKL, and DANN. These methods are designed to predict the pathogenicity of various types of mutations, including but not limited to synonymous mutations. ML-GVP collects features from DNA, RNA, and protein levels to predict six types of pathogenic mutations. At the RNA level, it focuses on RNA-binding protein sites and splicing-related features. At the protein level, it gathers sequence and structural features, but for structure, it only collects amino acid residue disorder probability features. Experimental results indicate that splicing and conservation features are the main ones effective for predicting pathogenic synonymous mutations. CADDv1.7, based on a logistic regression algorithm, integrates advanced protein language model scores, regulatory variant effect predictions, and sequence conservation scores. This integration enhances the accuracy of coding and regulatory mutation assessments. PhD-SNPg was trained and tested on nearly 104,000 pathogenic and benign SNVs from ClinVar. However, relying on a single source for data collection may be limiting. PredictSNP2 constructed three data sets covering various disease-related mutations. It evaluated the performance of DANN, CADD, FunSeq2, FitCons, FATHMM-MKL, and GWAVA using these data sets. It then integrated the results of the five top-performing tools and developed a consensus scoring tool. DANN was trained on thesame training data and features as CADD (2014) to develop its deep neural network structure.

Specific methods for predicting pathogenic synonymous mutations include FDPSM, EPEL, epSMic, PredDSMC, frDSM, usDSM, EnDSM, PrDSM, TraP, DDIG-SN, and SilVA. These methods employ unique strategies to analyze and predict the pathogenicity of synonymous mutations. For example, SilVA employs random forests with 26 features across six categories, while FDPSM integrates genomic context, conservation, splicing effects, functional effects, and epigenomic features. It extracts useful information directly from their interactions and distribution, without relying on scores from other variant effect prediction tools. EPEL, epSMic, and PredDSMC are designed to predict driver synonymous mutations in human cancers. Each employs unique feature combinations and machine learning models. frDSM integrates multidimensional features using 76 XGBoost classifiers to generate a feature vector, followed by a two-stage feature selection before logistic regression training. usDSM addresses imbalance via under-sampling with 14 features and random forest. EnDSM combines four feature categories, trains 32 models, selects optimal ones via cross-validation, and ensembles them. PrDSM integrates TraP (using sequence, conservation, and splicing features with random forests), SilVA, and FATHMM-MKL via soft voting. DDIG-SN excels through integrative DNA, RNA, and protein-level analysis but suffers from a limited 54-dimensional feature set and fails to comprehensively evaluate cross-level biological impacts.

To address these limitations, we develop DRP-PSM, a method that expands on FDPSM by incorporating additional sequence and structural features at the DNA, RNA, and protein levels. This model aims to assess how biological features from different levels influence the prediction of synonymous mutations. Despite utilizing FDPSM’s feature generation and selection methods, DRP-PSM distinguishes itself by significantly enlarging the feature set. It delves into DNA, RNA, and protein levels to combine their sequence and structural features, thereby building a comprehensive model. This model evaluates the roles of features from various levels in predicting synonymous mutation pathogenicity, with the goal of highlighting their importance and advancing the development of relevant prediction tools.

Materials and Methods

DNA-Level Features

We systematically constructed a high-throughput bioinformatics framework to generate a comprehensive DNA-level feature space using five resources and tools: FDPSM, iFeatureOmgea, DNAshapeR, frtCOOL, and MathFeature. These tools generated features grouped into three categories reflecting genomic functional attributes: (1) genomic context, (2) conservation scores, and (3) sequence-derived features (see Additional file 1: Table S1). These features were extracted from both reference and mutated sequences centered on each mutation site, and differences between reference and mutated sequences were also calculated.

Genomic context features evaluate the mutational landscape surrounding a variant, including mutation frequency (counts of frequent and rare mutations within sliding windows near the variant), predictions of regulatory elements, and functional impact. Conservation scores describe the evolutionary conservation constraints of the DNA sequence. The genomic context and conservation scores categories comprised a total of 35 features, which were adopted from FDPSM. In contrast, the sequence-derived features introduced in DRP-PSM were newly developed and subdivided into five subcategories to capture structural and compositional properties: nucleotide composition, pseudonucleotide composition, physicochemical properties, autocorrelation and cross-covariance, and chaos game representation (CGR). Nucleotide composition features, from tools like iFeatureOmega and ftrCOOL, use k-mer spectrum analysis to describe base distribution, including cumulative frequencies and positional weights. Pseudonucleotide composition, an advanced form provided by iFeatureOmega, considers nucleotide frequencies and positional factors to capture nucleotide interactions. Physicochemical property features, from iFeatureOmega and DNAshapeR, profile the physical and chemical characteristics of DNA sequences. Autocorrelation and cross-covariance features, from iFeatureOmega, measure nucleotide spatial correlations. Chaos game representation, from MathFeature, characterizes DNA sequences using chaos theory to reflect base distribution and patterns. The dimensionality of sequence-derived features varies with sequence length, ranging from 7467 features at 21 base pairs (bp) to 21,507 features at 201 bp. Subsequently, the genomic context, conservation, and sequence-derived features were concatenated, followed by feature selection to reduce dimensionality and discard uninformative features (Figure A).

We evaluated the performance of features across different sequence lengths and selected the window size that achieved the highest mean AUC and AUPR under 5-fold cross-validation. Ultimately, using a 183 bp window centered on each variant, the pipeline produced a 20,138-dimensional DNA-level feature vector per mutation. After feature selection, 69 features remained (Figure B).

RNA-Level Features

In this study, we established an RNA-level multimodal feature engineering framework. We leveraged computational biology tools, including FDPSM, iFeatureOmega, ftrCOOL, MathFeature, RNAfold, and RNAPKplex to extract features spanning sequence composition to structure (see Additional file 1: Table S1). These features were categorized into three primary types: splicing impact, sequence, and structural features. Similarly, at the RNA level, we extracted the mRNA sequence features of both the reference and mutated sequences centered on the mutation site, as well as the differences between these features.

Splicing effects, including SpliceAI and MMSpliceAI scores, were obtained from FDPSM, comprising 21 dimensions. Sequence and structural information represented the newly incorporated features in DRP-PSM. Sequence-derived features were further divided into four categories: (1) nucleotide composition, (2) pseudonucleotide composition, (3) autocorrelation and cross-covariance features, and (4) chaos game representation. Sequence-derived features varied with sequence length, ranging from 2972 dimensions at 21 bp to 4592 dimensions at 201 bp (Figure A). Structural features included minimum free energy (MFE) and mRNA tertiary structure, totaling 28 dimensions.

We further evaluated the performance of features across different sequence lengths and found that 129 bp yielded the best results. At this length, the initial feature set contained 3993 dimensions, which was reduced to 44 dimensions after feature selection (Figure B).

Protein-Level Features

DRP-PSM further provided a comprehensive protein-level feature framework (see Additional file 1: Table S2). Sequence features were categorized into amino acid composition, pseudoamino acid composition (extending amino acid composition with sequence-context parameters), physicochemical properties (covering characteristics like hydrophobicity and electron–ion interaction), and autocorrelation (inter-residue positional correlations). Structural features encompass protein secondary structure (α-helices, β-sheets, and random coils), solvent accessibility, and residue depth. Centered on the amino acid (aa) encoded by the codon containing the mutation, we extracted the surrounding protein sequence. Since synonymous mutations do not alter the translated amino acid, only features derived from the reference sequence were considered (Figure A).

Both sequence- and structure-based protein features varied with sequence length. To ensure consistency with DNA and RNA inputs, the protein sequence length was set to one-third of their length, as three nucleotides encode one amino acid. For example, a 21 bp DNA window (corresponding to 7 aa) produced 1637 features, while a 201 bp window (67 aa) produced 7449 features. Based on the performance results across different lengths, we selected a 39 aa window, which initially yielded 5377 features and was reduced to 28 after feature selection (Figure B).

Feature Selection Based on the DART Model

The feature selection method used in this study was based on the DART model, which is a dimensionality reduction technique that quantifies feature contributions to model predictions and identifies the optimal feature subset. By assessing feature importance via the DART model and statistically eliminating redundancy and noise, the model exhibits improved generalization performance. A key feature of DART is its random dropout mechanism, which addresses overfitting in gradient boosting trees, a limitation of traditional MART (Multiple Additive Regression Trees). During each iteration, DART randomly drops a fraction of previously generated decision trees with probability p. When constructing a new tree, the model selectively integrates it with the surviving trees from the prior steps. This technique, similar to dropout in neural networks, improves regularization by reducing reliance on individual trees and mitigating overfitting risks. In addition, DART automatically assesses feature importance throughout the iterative process, offering a robust foundation for identifying key features in high-dimensional data sets. For the aforementioned DNA, RNA, and protein initial features, the DART model retained 69, 44, and 28 features, respectively. After concatenating the three modalities, a 141-dimensional feature vector was obtained and fed into the final feature interaction, selection, and prediction modules.

DRP-PSM Framework

The DRP-PSM method employed a multilevel feature optimization strategy to systematically integrate biological feature information from DNA, RNA, and protein levels. The workflow proceeded in four steps (Figure ). First, high-dimensional features are collected from the DNA, RNA, and protein data sources. Second, a DART-based feature selection method was applied independently to each biological level. This iteratively assessed feature importance and eliminated redundant features, obtaining optimal feature subsets for each level. Third, the biological level-specific feature subsets were concatenated into a comprehensive feature set, from which OpenFE generated a combined feature set. DART-based feature selection was then reapplied to this integrated set to get an optimal comprehensive feature subset. Finally, DRP-PSM leveraged LightGBM to classify samples using the optimized feature subset.

Framework of the DRP-PSM model. (A) Synonymous mutations were first annotated at the DNA, RNA, and protein levels, with relevant biological information, including genomic context, conservation, sequence properties, and structure properties, extracted from each molecular layer. Then, the DART-based feature importance model identified representative feature subsets from the annotation results at the individual molecular level. (B) The optimal feature subsets from all three molecular levels were merged to form a comprehensive feature set. The OpenFE was used to generate new features by expanding interactions between the optimal features. Finally, the DART model was applied to the generated feature set to select the most discriminative features. These features were combined with LightGBM to generate the final prediction model. DART: dropouts meet multiple additive regression trees; FI: feature importance; OpenFE: automated feature generation for tabular data; LightGBM: light gradient boosting machine.

Data Set

In this study, we utilized the training set (4251 positive and 4251 negative samples) and test set (408 positive and 408 negative samples) from the FDPSM method. Pathogenic synonymous mutations were collected from HGMD and ClinVar. In HGMD, mutations tagged as “DM” or “DM?” were selected as positive samples. In ClinVar, mutations clinically labeled as “Pathogenic” or “Likely Pathogenic” were included as positive samples. Benign synonymous mutations were obtained from ClinVar and VariSNP, and further matched with gnomAD. Specifically, ClinVar mutations labeled as “Benign” or “Likely Benign” were selected as negative samples. To address potential bias from imbalanced data during model training, a negative sample was selected for each positive sample based on the closest chromosomal location, namely the close-by strategy.

Measurements for Performance Evaluation

To evaluate model performance, we primarily adopted two widely used metrics: The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) and the Area Under the Precision–Recall Curve (AUPR). The ROC curve (Receiver Operating Characteristic curve) plots the True Positive Rate (TPR) against the False Positive Rate (FPR) at different classification thresholds. AUC quantifies the overall ability of the model to discriminate positive from negative samples, with a value of 1 indicating perfect discrimination and 0.5 representing random guessing. The Precision–Recall (PR) curve plots Precision against Recall at varying thresholds. AUPR measures the area under this curve, providing a summary of model performance in terms of how well positive samples are identified while avoiding false positives. PR curves are particularly informative in data sets with class imbalance. In addition, we also used Accuracy, Specificity, Precision, Recall, and F1 as evaluation metrics. The definitions of the evaluation metrics and curve components are as follows 1 accuracy=TP+TNTP+FP+TN+FN 2 specificity=TNTN+FP 3 precision=TPTP+FP 4 recall=TPTP+FN 5 F1=2·precision·recallprecision+recall where TP, FP, TN, and FN denote the numbers of true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives, respectively. In these definitions, TPR and FPR determine the ROC curve, while Precision and Recall determine the PR curve. Higher values of AUC and AUPR indicate better predictive performance, reflecting stronger discrimination between pathogenic and benign variants. Conversely, lower values suggest poorer performance.

Implementation Details of Training and Inference

Feature extraction and experiments were conducted on a Linux workstation equipped with two Intel Xeon E5–2630 v4 CPUs (20 cores, 40 threads, 2.20 GHz), 125 GB RAM, and two NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs (24 GB each). On this platform, hyperparameter optimization and sequence length experiments (using a 6 bp resolution, ranging from 21 bp to 201 bp, totaling 31 experiments) required approximately 1 week for feature extraction and model training. Using the resulting optimal hyperparameters and sequence length, subsequent experimentsincluding OpenFE processing, DART feature selection, and model training took around 12 min, while prediction on the test set required approximately 2 min and 30 s. All code was implemented in Python 3.9.23. The LightGBM version used was 4.6.0, and the OpenFE version was 0.0.12.

Human genome-wide inference is available on figshare: https://figshare.com/articles/software/DRP-PSM_CLI/29948723?file=57305909. Feature quantification, processing, and inference for 1000 variants take roughly 8 min. Running the local command-line interface (CLI) requires GPU support and at least 7 GB of storage.

Results

The Impact of Different Sequence Lengths

To evaluate the impact of sequence lengths on pathogenic synonymous mutation prediction, we conducted a systematic analysis across DNA, RNA, and protein levels. By analyzing DNA and RNA sequences from 21 to 201 bp in 6 bp increments, and protein sequences from 7 to 67 aa in 2 aa increments, we performed hierarchical feature selection using the DART model and subsequently trained a LightGBM model with 5-fold cross-validation. Model performance was assessed using the average AUC and AUPR values under optimal hyperparameters. The results showed that DNA-level performance was relatively stable across different sequence lengths (Figure A). At the RNA level, performance was also stable (Figure B), with the best results obtained at an RNA sequence length of 129 bp, suggesting this length is optimal for capturing RNA’s structural and functional features for predicting pathogenic synonymous mutations. Protein level performance showed minimal variation with different sequence lengths (Figure C), indicating a smaller impact on prediction performance.

The pentagrams indicate the sequence lengths achieving the highest average AUC and AUPR. (A) Impact of varying DNA sequence lengths. (B) Impact of varying RNA sequence lengths. (C) Impact of varying protein sequence lengths. bp: base pair; aa: amino acids.

In summary, different biological levels show different sensitivities to sequence length in predicting pathogenic synonymous mutations. Our findings highlighted the importance of selecting appropriate sequence lengths to enhance model performance. At the DNA level, the model maintained stable predictive performance across different sequence lengths and achieved the best performance at the length of 183 bp. At the RNA level, the optimal prediction performance was achieved at a sequence length of 129 bp. At the protein level, sequence length had a relatively minor impact on prediction performance and performed best at a length of 39 aa. These insights provide guidance for future research.

The Impact of DNA-Level Features

Synonymous mutations, which alter DNA sequences through codon substitutions while preserving encoded amino acids, can impact local DNA physicochemical properties despite not changing the amino acid sequence. For instance, shifts in GC content influence DNA double-helix stability and nucleosome binding affinity. , Codon preference may also affect RNA polymerase movement during transcription, altering splicing efficiency and gene expression. Beyond coding regions, synonymous mutations in exonic splicing enhancers (ESE) or silencers (ESS) regions can disrupt splicing regulation. Overall, synonymous mutations exert broad biological effects through mechanisms such as regulatory element interference, epigenetic modifications, and evolutionary adaptability. ,

In this study, we systematically evaluated DNA-level features for predicting pathogenic synonymous mutations. An initial 21,507-dimensional DNA-level candidate feature set was selected using a DART model-based ensemble feature selection strategy, yielding a 69-dimensional feature subset for the 183 bp DNA sequence. At the optimal sequence length and feature subset, we performed a grid search to determine the best parameters, followed by 5-fold cross-validation. For each fold, we applied the feature_importance function of LightGBM to obtain the importance scores of the features, which were then used to generate the feature importance ranking (Figure A). Notably, ZooVerPhyloP, ZooPriPhyloP, priPhyloP, GerpS, and GerpN are conservation features to assess genomic site conservation. These highly conserved sites, due to their important biological functions, can affect gene function and increase pathogenicity risk if mutated, even without amino acid sequence changes. As a result, conservation features are critical indicators of pathogenicity, aligning with prior studies. Structural feature ProT92_alt and MGW89_ref revealed the potential of synonymous mutations to alter local DNA conformation. Sng100bp, Rare100bp, Freq100bp, and Rare10000bp quantify the number of BRAVO. Sng100bp qualifies single-occurrence SNVs within 100 bp windows surrounding the synonymous mutation site. Rare10000bp measures the number of rare BRAVO SNVs (MAF < 0.05) within a 10,000 bp window. These features reflect localized mutational constraints surrounding the synonymous mutation site.

The LightGBM model trained on this feature subset achieved an AUC of 0.86 and AUPR of 0.89 on the test set (Figure ), highlighting the pivotal role of DNA-level features in accurately predicting pathogenic synonymous mutations. However, we found that using only the sequence- and structure-derived features newly introduced in DRP-PSM achieved an AUC of 0.60 and an AUPR of 0.63, indicating that the majority of the predictive contribution comes from the conservation scores and genomic context features included in FDPSM. These results demonstrate that integrating conservation, structural, and regional constraint features may provide a robust framework for identifying pathogenic synonymous variants.

The Impact of RNA-Level Features

Synonymous mutations preserve the amino acid sequence but alter the RNA sequence, potentially disrupting RNA function through multiple mechanisms. These alterations may affect the secondary structure of mRNA, impairing its stability and ribosome binding efficiency. , Furthermore, mutations in ESE or ESS sequences can interfere with splice protein binding, leading to abnormal splicing. − To systematically evaluate RNA-level contributions to pathogenic synonymous mutation prediction, we compiled 3993-dimensional RNA features. Using the DART ensemble feature selection framework, we obtained a compact 44-dimensional optimal feature subset. The optimal subset was then used to train and predict with the LightGBM model. On the test set, RNA-level features showed an AUC of 0.63 and an AUPR of 0.67 (Figure ), confirming RNA-level features contribute meaningfully, though not dominantly.

Feature importance analysis (Figure B) revealed splicing-related factors as the top 7 indicators, aligning with previous studies on their critical role. ,,, The remaining features are all RNA-level sequence composition features, with Adaptive Skip Dipeptide Composition (ASDC) and Series Correlation Pseudo Dinucleotide Composition (SCPseDNC) predominating. To assess the contribution of RNA sequence-derived and structural features independent of splicing scores, LightGBM models were trained and evaluated using either combined sequence-derived and structural features or structural features alone. Using only structural features, the model achieved an AUC and AUPR of 0.55. When sequence-derived features were incorporated alongside structural features, both AUC and AUPR increased to 0.59. Thus, including sequence-derived features enhanced the performance of the structure-only model by 4 percentage points, whereas incorporating splicing features increased AUPR by 8 percentage points. These results indicated that RNA sequence-derived and structural features have limited predictive contribution, with splicing features exerting a dominant effect.

The Impact of Protein-Level Features

Although synonymous mutations do not change the amino acid sequences, they may influence the protein function. For instance, synonymous mutations can affect mRNA stability, thereby influencing protein translation efficiency. Additionally, they may disrupt protein folding, leading to structural changes that impact protein function. To systematically evaluate these effects, we compiled 5377-dimensional protein-level features potentially predictive of pathogenic synonymous mutations. Using a DART-based iterative feature selection strategy with cross-validation, we progressively eliminated low-contribution features, ultimately identifying a 28-dimensional optimal feature subset for 39 aa sequences. The LightGBM model trained on this feature subset achieved limited predictive power (Figure ), indicating minimal contribution from general protein-level features. Feature importance analysis (Figure C) revealed Zscale_p20.z3, ZScale_p21.z1, and ZScale_p21.z2 as importance sequence features. Z-scale is a method that converts the physicochemical properties of amino acids into numerical vectors, , and each amino acid corresponds to five Z-scale values (representing five different physicochemical attributes). This feature potentially detected critical biophysical properties at mutation sites. On the other hand, pq8_G_5, pq8_S_2, and rsa_18 are important structural features, where the trailing numbers indicate the amino acid positions in the sequence. Here, pq8_G and pq8_S correspond to the 3–10 helix and Bend regions, respectively, and rsa denotes Relative Surface Accessibility.

Given prior evidence that synonymous mutations may perturb protein structure, , we specifically analyzed the protein structural feature set. After DART-based selection, LightGBM modeling showed an AUC and an AUPR of 0.54 and 0.52 (Figure ), implying that while protein structural features hold theoretical relevance, their practical utility for predicting pathogenic synonymous mutations remains marginal.

Integration of Features Across Three Molecular Levels

In this study, we investigated the impact of different sequence lengths at the DNA, RNA, and protein levels on predicting pathogenic synonymous mutations. Systematic analysis revealed optimal prediction performance at 183 bp for DNA, 129 bp for RNA, and 39 aa for proteins. To enhance prediction accuracy, the optimal feature subsets from each biological level were combined into a 141-dimensional comprehensive feature set. Subsequently, OpenFE was employed to generate cross-level interaction features, expanding dimensionality to 8840 features. To address redundancy, a DART-based feature selection strategy systematically eliminated ineffective features, yielding an optimized 63-dimensional feature subset.

On the test set, the model achieved an AUC of 0.88 and an AUPR of 0.91 (Figure C,D). To further examine the contribution of each modality, we removed the optimal feature subsets from DNA, RNA, or Protein before the final feature interaction calculation in OpenFE. We found that excluding DNA-level features, i.e., DRP-PSM (without DNA), led to the largest performance drop, with the AUC and AUPR decreasing sharply by 0.25 and 0.22, respectively. When RNA-level features were removed, i.e., DRP-PSM (without RNA), the performance also declined, with AUC and AUPR reduced by 3 and 2 percentage points, respectively. By contrast, removing Protein-level features, i.e., DRP-PSM (without Protein), caused almost no change, with only a one percentage point decrease in both AUC and AUPR. Therefore, we conclude that DNA-level features play a dominant role in prediction, while RNA-level features provide additional support. Although protein-level features contribute only marginally to overall performance, their inclusion still yields a positive gain, suggesting that Protein features provide complementary information for the model’s decision-making.

Performance Comparison with Other Prediction Methods

Moreover, we systematically compared DRP-PSM with other existing classical prediction methods. Since some tools lacked predictions for certain variants in the independent test set, we divided the comparison into two groups: tools with “missing prediction scores” (Figure A,B) and tools without missing scores (Figure C,D). For tools with missing values, we conducted the comparison on the subset of variants for which all tools provided predictions. On this subset, DRP-PSM achieved an AUC of 0.87 and an AUPR of 0.91. For tools without missing values, the comparison was performed on the complete independent test set. Overall, the results indicate that DRP-PSM outperforms most current tools, while its performance remains comparable to FDPSM, achieving an AUC of 0.88 and AUPR of 0.91.

We further compared the performance of different tools using Accuracy, Precision, Recall, Specificity, and F1. Among tools with missing values, DRP-PSM outperformed most tools across the majority of metrics (Figure F). For tools without missing values, DRP-PSM achieved higher Accuracy, Recall, and F1 than FDPSM, with only slight shortcomings in Precision and Specificity (Figure E). However, in mutation effect prediction, missing pathogenic variants can have severe clinical consequences; therefore, when overall performance is comparable, we prioritize improving Recall to reduce the risk of overlooking true pathogenic mutations.

The results indicate that while features from the three biological levels collectively improve prediction accuracy, their incremental contribution beyond FDPSM is moderate. Critically, our findings establish that features from all three biological levels contribute to predicting pathogenic synonymous mutations, albeit with varying impact magnitudes: DNA-level features showed the strongest predictive power, RNA-level features provided moderate contributions, and protein-level features offered minimal but measurable utility.

Correlation Analysis of DNA and RNA Sequence Features

To explore potential correlations between DNA and RNA sequence features, we systematically analyzed cross-level relationships using matched 183 bp sequences. This length standardization ensured comparability while minimizing DNA length-dependent artifacts. Six feature categories were extracted for both molecular levels: nucleic acid composition (NAC), dinucleotide-based autocovariance (DAC), trinucleotide-based autocovariance (TAC), Z-curve, Moran autocorrelation, and Geary autocorrelation features.

Comparative correlation analysis revealed distinct patterns across feature types (Figure ). NAC features exhibited strong positive correlations, indicating evolutionary conservation in base usage. However, Z-curve features showed minimal correlation, suggesting divergent spatial configurations between DNA and RNA (Figure ). Dinucleotide composition (ASDC) displayed moderate correlations (Figure B), whereas Moran and Geary autocorrelation features had consistently low associations (Figure C), implying limited conservation of long-range interaction patterns. Similarly, DAC, DCC, and DACC features universally demonstrated weak correlations (Figure D), highlighting fundamental differences in local sequence dynamics.

Overall, DNA and RNA showed significant positive correlations in some features and low correlations in others. This suggests that while certain sequence features are conserved, distinct differences also exist. Therefore, when predicting pathogenic synonymous mutations, it is essential to collect features from both the DNA and RNA levels. This approach enhances the comprehensive understanding of synonymous mutations and improves the accuracy of the prediction model.

Molecular Mechanisms and Feature Performance Relationships

To further investigate the underlying causes of the differing contributions of DNA-, RNA-, and protein-level features, we analyzed the training data set (Figure ). In our data set, differences between pathogenic and benign variants are most pronounced in splicing-related features. Based on records in TarBase-v9.0, 1200 variants (731 pathogenic, 469 benign) are located within experimentally validated miRNA-mRNA (CDS region) binding sites. A total of 356 variants (354 pathogenic, 2 benign) have SpliceAI scores higher than 0.8, potentially causing donor/acceptor gain or loss. Additionally, 654 variants (649 pathogenic, 5 benign) have dbscSNV Random Forest and AdaBoost scores both higher than 0.6, indicating potential splicing-altering effects. Moreover, 870 variants (798 pathogenic, 72 benign) fall within consensus splice regions, among which 35 variants (25 pathogenic, 10 benign) disrupt more than five exonic splicing regulatory motifs. Using RNAfold to predict minimum free energy (MFE) changes before and after mutation, 301 variants (165 pathogenic, 136 benign) caused an absolute MFE change higher than 4, resulting in more stable or unstable mRNA structures.

Splice: variants predicted to affect splicing, located at splice consensus sites, or disrupting exonic splicing regulatory motifs. mRNA: variants are predicted by RNAfold to alter the mRNA secondary structure. m6A: variants are located in m6A-modified regions. ChromHMM: variants fall within chromatin-state signature regions. Enhancer: variants are located in enhancer regions, TFBS: variants overlap transcription factor binding sites. ENCODE: variants overlap Encyclopedia of DNA elements. Unknown: variants whose associated mechanisms are currently undetermined.

Regarding regulatory elements, 2764 variants are located in DNA enhancer regions, 1312 in transcription factor binding sites, and 3646 in m6A-modified regions. Among these, 3140 and 182 variants correspond to chromatin-state signature regions and ENCODE peaks exceeding half of the genome-wide maximum for synonymous variants. For these regulatory elements, the ratio of pathogenic to benign variants is approximately 1.5:1. This is likely due to the close-by matching strategy, where regulatory elements simultaneously cover both positive and negative sample pairs, limiting the model’s ability to discriminate between them. Notably, pathogenic variants dominate the splicing effect category, which explains why splicing-related scores play a dominant role in the model’s predictions.

Discussion

To enhance our understanding of multilevel features in predicting pathogenic synonymous mutations, we proposed the DRP-PSM framework using the LightGBM model. Systematic evaluation revealed that DNA-level features exhibited the strongest predictive power, RNA-level features provided moderate contributions, and protein-level features showed minimal impact. To leverage these complementary signals, we integrated optimal feature subsets from all three levels into a unified feature space. OpenFE was then applied to generate cross-level interaction features. Due to the resultant high dimensionality, a DART-based feature selection strategy was implemented, refining the set to 63 discriminative features. The final LightGBM model trained on this refined subset demonstrated significantly enhanced performance, confirming the value of multilevel feature integration. This model innovatively achieves the following: (1) collaborative utilization of multilevel biological features; (2) dual-stage feature optimization based on the DART model; (3) effective capture of interactions among features.

However, we acknowledge the following limitations of DRP-PSM. Compared with FDPSM, DRP-PSM achieves comparable performance in terms of AUC and AUPR, without additional improvement. It outperforms FDPSM in Accuracy, Recall, and F1. In the context of mutation effect prediction, false negatives for positive samples carry a high cost, as failure to detect pathogenic mutations may lead to severe clinical consequences. Since other performance metrics are similar between FDPSM and DRP-PSM, DRP-PSM is a more suitable choice in this scenario. Notably, in the feature importance of the final model (Figure D), although DRP-PSM introduces additional features such as Chaos Game Representation (CGR) and Accumulated Nucleotide Frequency (ANF), the top-ranking features are dominated by splicing effects and conservation scores, which were incorporated from FDPSM.

After evaluating sequence and structural features across different lengths, we found that DNA- and RNA-level features primarily drive predictive performance, while adding additional features yielded results comparable to FDPSM without further improvement. Despite incorporating extensive protein-related sequence and structural features, DRP-PSM exhibit limited predictive utility either independently or integrated into the final model. Interestingly, protein features, while informative in missense variant effect prediction (e.g., PSMutPred, MMPatho), provide little improvement for synonymous mutation prediction. A possible explanation is that synonymous mutations do not alter the amino acid sequence, and our data set construction uses a close-by matching strategy, resulting in similar genomic positions for positive and negative samples. Consequently, it is difficult for the model to capture discriminative representations at the protein level. While protein-level mechanismssuch as cotranslational folding or conformational effectsmay be affected, these are challenging to infer directly from sequence and structural features. Nevertheless, ablation experiments show that including protein-level features provides a modest positive contribution to AUC and AUPR, suggesting that future work could further explore protein-related features for synonymous mutation prediction.

In addition to exploring feature contributions from DNA, RNA, and protein levels, we also analyzed the correlation between DNA and RNA sequence features. Results showed strong positive correlations in base composition features, indicating evolutionary conservation. On the other hand, there are weak correlations in spatial descriptors (e.g., Z-curve) and long-range interaction features (e.g., Moran and Geary autocorrelation), highlighting structural divergence between DNA and RNA. This suggests that in future research, it is crucial to focus on key DNA features while also taking into account the unique contributions of RNA features.

Conclusions

In this study, we introduced DRP-PSM, a novel computational framework for predicting pathogenic synonymous mutations by integrating multilevel biological features. The significance of this study stems from its systematic evaluation of which biological layers provide the most informative signals for predicting the pathogenicity of synonymous mutations. The main contributions are as follows: (1) Systematic evaluation across three biological levels. We are the first to comprehensively investigate features from DNA, RNA, and protein levels to determine which molecular layers are most likely to mediate the pathogenic effects of synonymous mutations. By exploring a vast set of 12,160 to 33,632 sequence- and structure-based descriptors from DNA, RNA, and protein levels, we move beyond building just another predictor to generating a fundamental biological insight. Compared with the FDPSM method, DRP-PSM evaluates an expanded feature space that includes DNA sequence composition features (e.g., nucleic acid composition, physical and chemical properties), DNA shape, RNA sequence (e.g., codon usage, codon fraction) and structure (e.g., RNA structural stability, pseudoknots), and all protein-level descriptorsnone of which were considered by FDPSM. (2) Biological insights. Our analysis provides a clear hierarchy of feature importance: DNA-level features exert the greatest influence, followed by RNA-level features, and then protein-level features. The fact that protein structural and sequence features contributed only modestly is a decisive result, not a limitation. It strongly suggests that the pathogenic effects of synonymous mutations are mediated primarily through pretranslational mechanisms (e.g., disrupting splicing or mRNA stability) rather than through protein structure or function. The complementary role of protein features (as shown in Figure C of the revised manuscript) still adds predictive value and highlights an avenue for future work to explore novel descriptors or modeling approaches. In future studies, discovering novel biological features and optimizing feature selection are necessary to further improve the model.
