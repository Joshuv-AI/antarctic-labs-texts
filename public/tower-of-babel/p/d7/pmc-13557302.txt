Treating Nail Psoriasis with Topical Ruxolitinib in a Patient with Vitiligo
Authors: Francesca Ambrogio, Caterina Foti, Benedetta Tirone, Alexandre Raphael Meduri
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Vitiligo and psoriasis are distinct chronic inflammatory skin diseases that may coexist in some patients. Their association is partly explained by overlapping immune pathways and by the Koebner phenomenon, where lesions arise in mechanically stressed areas such as the nail unit.

Nail involvement in psoriasis poses a therapeutic challenge. Topical agents such as corticosteroids, vitamin D analogues, and calcineurin inhibitors often result in limited and inconsistent improvement. Moreover, prolonged corticosteroid use may lead to atrophy and nail matrix damage.

Ruxolitinib, a selective JAK1 and JAK2 inhibitor approved for nonsegmental vitiligo, blocks the phosphorylation of STAT proteins involved in cytokine signaling, and may also affect other inflammatory pathways[1], [2].

This letter presents a case of nail psoriasis successfully treated with ruxolitinib cream in a patient with periungual vitiligo, suggesting its potential as a targeted and well-tolerated option for refractory nail disease.

We report the case of a middle-aged man with a 10-year history of vitiligo, initially affecting the fingernails and face, followed by the onset of psoriatic nail involvement a few years prior to presentation to our department.

Baseline examination (Figure 1A) showed prominent distal onycholysis on both thumbs (red circles) and the third digit of the right hand (black circle), with subtle nail pitting and macroscopic vitiligo on fingers II–IV bilaterally. The severity of nail psoriasis was assessed using the Nail Psoriasis Severity Index (NAPSI), a validated scoring system that evaluates nail matrix and nail bed changes[3]. The patient was prescribed topical ruxolitinib twice daily for his scalp and periungual vitiligo. At follow-up (Figure 1B), after 9 months of treatment, the patient reported applying ruxolitinib only once daily but distal onycholysis had significantly improved despite the therapeutic non-adherence and slight improvement in vitiligo.

Close-up images (Figure 2, A–B) and dermoscopy (Figure 2, C–D) confirmed marked regression of distal onycholysis. NAPSI scores also reflected this clinical improvement.

Janus kinase (JAK) 1 and 2 inhibitors, such as ruxolitinib, block phosphorylation of several STAT proteins, notably STAT1 and STAT3. These transcription factors mediate signaling for cytokines like interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which act upstream in psoriasis pathogenesis, preceding the activation of key cytokines IL-17, IL-22, and IL-23[4], [5], [6].

Despite suboptimal adherence, once-daily topical application led to notable improvement of the patient’s nail psoriasis and, to a lesser extent, his vitiligo. At follow-up, distal onycholysis had markedly improved, although residual splinter hemorrhages persisted. These residual features could be due to reflect incomplete therapeutic adherence or to the limited ability of topical ruxolitinib to reach deeper nail bed structures.

Although the current price of topical ruxolitinib is high, the small amount required for nail application allows a single tube to potentially last several months, thereby reducing the monthly cost of treatment.

This case suggests that topical ruxolitinib might represent a novel therapeutic option for nail psoriasis resistant to common therapies. Standardized clinical trials are warranted to confirm its efficacy and define its place in treatment algorithms.
