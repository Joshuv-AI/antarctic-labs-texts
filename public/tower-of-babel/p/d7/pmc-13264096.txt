Early-apoptotic membrane engineering of M2 macrophage-derived nanovesicles enables osteoimmunomodulatory bone repair
Authors: Xiaodong Hou, Yi Yang, Yilin Jiao, Wentao Deng, Jingjiang Duan, Ziran Zhou, Shaobin Ye, Chenyuan Guo, Biao Li, Dingyun You, Hongda Gong, Jia Yang, Bing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Critical-sized bone defects remain difficult to repair because persistent inflammation, insufficient osteogenesis, and excessive osteoclast activity collectively compromise regenerative progression. Herein, an apoptotic reparative nanovesicle platform is developed by combining M2 macrophage polarization with early apoptotic membrane remodeling. Membrane-derived nanovesicles prepared from these donor cells (M2-Apo-NVs) retain the pro-repair imprint of M2 macrophages while displaying enhanced surface phosphatidylserine exposure. Relative to M2-derived nanovesicles, M2-Apo-NVs exhibit stronger cellular internalization, more effectively reprogram inflammatory macrophages toward a pro-resolution phenotype, promote osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, and suppress RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis through inhibition of the NF-κB/NFAT axis. For local delivery, M2-Apo-NVs are incorporated into a photocrosslinkable methacrylated hyaluronic acid hydrogel, enabling sustained presentation within a murine critical-sized calvarial defect. In vivo, the composite hydrogel markedly improves bone regeneration, accompanied by reduced inflammatory signaling, enhanced osteogenic activity, and restrained osteoclast-associated remodeling. These findings establish donor-membrane state engineering as an effective strategy for upgrading cell-derived nanovesicles and identify M2-Apo-NVs as a promising osteoimmunomodulatory therapeutic for bone defect repair.

Introduction

Critical-sized bone defects, especially those arising in craniofacial and maxillofacial settings, remain a formidable clinical challenge because successful reconstruction requires not only structural filling but also the re-establishment of a regenerative microenvironment capable of orchestrating osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and immune resolution [1,2]. Increasing evidence indicates that bone repair is fundamentally an osteoimmune process rather than a purely osteogenic one. In this context, the inflammatory phase is not merely permissive for subsequent regeneration; rather, its magnitude, duration, and cellular composition critically determine whether the defect progresses toward constructive healing or becomes trapped in fibrotic nonunion and chronic tissue dysfunction [3,4]. Recent multimodal analyses of bone repair have further identified macrophages as central regulators of the injury response, with temporally distinct macrophage states governing the transition from inflammatory activation to pro-repair remodeling [1]. These findings have reshaped the therapeutic landscape of skeletal regeneration, motivating the development of biomaterial and bioinspired systems that can actively reprogram the local immune niche instead of serving solely as osteoconductive scaffolds.

Among the various immune populations involved in bone regeneration, macrophages are particularly attractive therapeutic targets because they directly interface with mesenchymal progenitors, endothelial cells, and osteoclast-lineage cells, thereby coupling inflammation to tissue reconstruction [2]. Dysregulated macrophage activation impairs skeletal stem/progenitor cell behavior, exacerbates inflammatory cytokine signaling, and promotes a resorptive milieu incompatible with durable bone regeneration [4]. Accordingly, strategies that bias macrophages toward reparative states have shown broad benefit in fracture healing, osseous defect repair, and inflammatory bone loss. At the same time, extracellular vesicles (EVs) and cell membrane-derived vesicles have emerged as particularly compelling cell-free therapeutic platforms because they preserve biologically rich intercellular communication while circumventing several limitations associated with living-cell transplantation, including poor engraftment control, manufacturing complexity, and safety concerns [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. However, it is now equally clear that vesicle bioactivity is highly dependent on donor-cell identity and cellular state. Vesicles derived from distinct tissues or pathological conditions can exhibit profoundly different effects on skeletal progenitors and bone homeostasis, underscoring that vesicle source is not a trivial production variable but a determinant of therapeutic function [[9], [10], [11], [12]].

Within this broader framework, macrophage-derived EVs have attracted growing attention as osteoimmunomodulatory agents. In particular, vesicles from M2-like macrophages have been shown to enhance osteogenic differentiation, reshape inflammatory microenvironments, and suppress excessive osteoclastogenesis, thereby improving skeletal repair under diverse pathological conditions [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. Recent studies have further demonstrated that the therapeutic potential of M2 macrophage-derived vesicles can be amplified by donor-cell preconditioning, surface engineering, cargo rewiring, or biomaterial-assisted localization [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. For example, hydrogen sulfide-primed M2 exosomes displayed enhanced endocytic competence and osteogenic potency [13], glutamate-containing M2-EVs alleviated osteoporosis by reshaping osteoclast precursor fate [14], and engineered M2-EV systems have recently been exploited to reprogram neutrophils, rescue inflammatory BMSC dysfunction, or promote diabetic bone regeneration [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. These advances collectively establish macrophage-derived vesicles as powerful regulators of bone healing. Nevertheless, most current strategies focus on modulating donor polarization state, engineering vesicle cargo, or improving tissue targeting, whereas the role of donor membrane state itself remains comparatively underexplored. This is a notable gap, because the membrane is not a passive shell; rather, it constitutes the first layer of biological information encountered by recipient cells and may critically influence uptake, recognition, and downstream signaling.

A particularly intriguing but insufficiently exploited biological program in this regard is apoptotic recognition. Phosphatidylserine (PS) externalization is a canonical hallmark of early apoptosis and serves as a potent “eat-me” signal during efferocytosis. Importantly, efferocytosis is now understood not simply as debris clearance, but as an instructive resolution program capable of rewiring macrophage metabolism, cytokine output, and tissue-remodeling behavior [[19], [20], [21]]. Recent studies have shown that PS-exposing extracellular vesicles possess distinct biological functions [19], that efferocytosis activates defined pro-resolution metabolic circuits in macrophages [20], and that early-life regenerative responses can be linked to macrophage programs specialized for apoptotic-cell recognition and uptake [21]. In parallel, apoptotic vesicles (apoVs) have emerged as a functionally diverse class of regenerative mediators rather than passive by-products of cell death [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. In the skeletal field, tailored apoVs have been shown to enhance bone regeneration [23], distinct apoV subpopulations display nonredundant regenerative activities [22], and recent work has highlighted their capacity to restore age-disrupted bone–fat balance or overcome metabolic bottlenecks during bone repair [24,25]. Together, these findings suggest that apoptosis-associated membrane coding may provide a powerful way to endow vesicle therapeutics with stronger pro-resolution and pro-regenerative behavior. Yet this concept has not been systematically integrated into macrophage-derived osteoimmunomodulatory nanovesicles for bone repair.

Another persistent obstacle in vesicle-based skeletal therapy is local delivery. Free vesicles often suffer from rapid dispersion, insufficient retention at the defect site, and limited spatiotemporal control over presentation, all of which can attenuate their therapeutic impact in vivo [3]. Consequently, increasing effort has been directed toward biomaterial-enabled vesicle delivery systems, including injectable hydrogels, vesicle-capturing scaffolds, and engineered matrix interfaces that prolong local persistence while preserving vesicle bioactivity [[26], [27], [28], [29]]. Such approaches are especially attractive for calvarial and craniofacial defects, where local implantation is feasible and site-specific immunomodulation is likely to be decisive for successful regeneration. These developments support the view that optimal vesicle therapy for bone repair should combine three elements: biologically instructive vesicle composition, robust regulation of the osteoimmune microenvironment, and localized delivery that sustains activity within the defect niche. Recent regenerative biomaterial studies have increasingly emphasized hierarchical design and microenvironment-specific regulation as key determinants of tissue repair efficacy across complex injury settings [[30], [31], [32]]. In this broader context, the present work advances a complementary strategy by engineering the biological state of the donor membrane itself and coupling this vesicle-level design with localized hydrogel delivery to reshape the osteoimmune niche.

Herein, we report an apoptotic reparative nanovesicle platform for bone regeneration by integrating M2 macrophage programming with early apoptotic membrane remodeling. Specifically, RAW264.7 macrophages were first polarized toward an M2-like phenotype and then transiently driven into early apoptosis to generate membrane-derived nanovesicles enriched in surface-exposed PS, termed M2-Apo-NVs. We hypothesized that this design would combine the pro-repair molecular imprint of M2 macrophages with an apoptosis-associated membrane interface capable of enhancing cellular recognition and inflammation resolution. We further posited that such vesicles would outperform conventional macrophage-derived nanovesicles in three interconnected dimensions that are central to bone repair: reprogramming inflammatory macrophages toward a pro-resolution state, promoting osteogenic commitment of BMSCs, and suppressing osteoclastogenesis. To enable local administration and sustained presentation in vivo, the vesicles were incorporated into a photocrosslinkable methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HAMA) hydrogel and evaluated in a murine critical-sized calvarial defect model. Collectively, this work establishes a membrane-state engineering strategy for macrophage-derived nanovesicles and identifies apoptotic M2 nanovesicles as a promising osteoimmunomodulatory therapeutic for defect-scale bone regeneration.

Experimental section

Cell culture and generation of reparative macrophages

RAW264.7 murine macrophages were maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. For induction of a reparative macrophage phenotype, cells were allowed to attach overnight and were subsequently exposed to recombinant mouse interleukin-4 (IL-4; R&D Systems, USA) at 20 ng mL−1. Cells collected after 24 h were used for transcriptional analysis of M2-associated genes, whereas cells or conditioned media harvested at 48 h were used for immunophenotyping and cytokine quantification. Macrophages cultured in complete medium without IL-4 treatment were designated as control.

Early-apoptotic conditioning of M2 macrophages

To endow donor-cell membranes with an apoptosis-biased interfacial composition while minimizing terminal membrane disintegration, IL-4-conditioned macrophages were treated with staurosporine (MedChemExpress, USA) at 0.5 μM for 12 h. The apoptotic status of donor cells was examined using a FITC Annexin V/propidium iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit (BioLegend, USA). Fluorescence images were acquired with a confocal laser scanning microscope (FV3000, Olympus, Japan), and quantitative analysis was performed by flow cytometry (NovoCyte platform, Agilent, USA). Cells enriched in the Annexin V+/PI− fraction were considered suitable donors for the preparation of apoptosis-mimetic vesicles. Following staurosporine exposure, donor cells were washed thoroughly with ice-cold PBS before membrane isolation, and the membrane-rich fraction was further purified by washing during ultracentrifugation prior to extrusion and final resuspension in PBS to minimize potential residual staurosporine carryover.

Fabrication of NVs, M2-NVs, and M2-Apo-NVs

Membrane-derived nanovesicles were generated from three donor-cell populations, namely untreated RAW264.7 macrophages, IL-4-conditioned RAW264.7 macrophages, and IL-4-conditioned macrophages further driven into early apoptosis. All procedures were carried out on ice.

Cells were collected by centrifugation (200 × g, 10 min), washed twice with pre-cooled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in hypotonic lysis buffer consisting of 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4) and 10 mM MgCl2 freshly supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Switzerland). After a brief swelling step, cells were mechanically disrupted using a chilled Dounce homogenizer. Nuclei, intact cells, and large debris were removed by repeated low-speed centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 min until the supernatant became visibly free of particulate contaminants. Membrane-rich fractions were then collected by ultracentrifugation at 100000 × g for 45 min using an Optima XE ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter, USA), washed once with hypotonic buffer, and finally resuspended in sterile PBS. The total membrane-associated protein content was determined using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay (Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Vesicle dosing in cell-based experiments was normalized and reported as membrane-protein equivalents.

To convert isolated membrane fragments into nanoscale vesicles, suspensions were serially extruded through polycarbonate membranes using a Mini-Extruder system (Avanti Polar Lipids, USA). Membranes with nominal pore sizes of 1.0, 0.4, and 0.2 μm were used sequentially, and each sample was passed through each membrane 15 times to improve size homogeneity. Vesicles prepared from untreated macrophages were referred to as NVs; vesicles derived from IL-4-conditioned macrophages were termed M2-NVs; and vesicles assembled from early-apoptotic, IL-4-conditioned macrophages were designated M2-Apo-NVs. Samples were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C, and repeated freeze–thaw cycles were avoided.

Physicochemical characterization of vesicles

Surface phosphatidylserine exposure on vesicles was assessed by FITC-Annexin V staining using the manufacturer-recommended binding buffer, followed by fluorescence-triggered flow-cytometric acquisition. Imaging-based confirmation of PS presentation was further obtained by confocal microscopy. Vesicle morphology and membrane architecture were examined by conventional negative-stain transmission electron microscopy on a JEM-2100Plus instrument (JEOL, Japan). Hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and ζ-potential were determined by dynamic light scattering and electrophoretic light scattering using a Zetasizer Ultra system (Malvern Panalytical, UK). For experiments in which particle-number normalization was required, vesicle concentration was additionally measured by nanoparticle tracking analysis on a NanoSight platform (Malvern Panalytical, UK).

Fluorescent labeling of vesicles and cellular uptake

For uptake studies, vesicles were labeled with the lipophilic tracer DiO (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in the dark. Unincorporated dye was removed by ultracentrifugation at 100000 × g for 45 min, and the pelleted vesicles were resuspended in PBS. RAW264.7 cells or bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were incubated with DiO-labeled NVs, M2-NVs, or M2-Apo-NVs at an equivalent vesicle dose of 20 μg mL−1 for 4 h. After extensive PBS washing, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, stained for filamentous actin with rhodamine-phalloidin (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Quantitative uptake analysis was performed by flow cytometry.

Cytocompatibility and vesicle-associated cell death

The biocompatibility of vesicles toward RAW264.7 cells and BMSCs was assessed by a WST-8-based viability assay using Cell Counting Kit-8 (Abcam, UK). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 8 × 103 cells per well, exposed to vesicles at 20 μg mL−1, and incubated for the indicated intervals before absorbance was recorded at 450 nm with a multimode microplate reader (Synergy H1, Agilent BioTek, USA). Live/dead staining was performed using a Calcein-AM/PI viability kit (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For apoptosis analysis, cells exposed to vesicles for 72 h were stained with Annexin V/PI and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Inflammatory macrophage reprogramming in vitro

To establish an inflammatory macrophage model, RAW264.7 cells were primed with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) at 100 ng mL−1 for 24 h. Cells were then incubated with NVs, M2-NVs, or M2-Apo-NVs (20 μg mL−1, protein equivalent) for an additional 48 h. Reprogramming of macrophage phenotype was evaluated at both transcriptional and protein levels. Specifically, mRNA expression of inflammatory markers (e.g., Tnf-α and Il-1β) and reparative markers (e.g., Tgf-β and Il-10) was quantified by qRT-PCR, whereas secreted cytokines in culture supernatants were measured by ELISA. Representative marker expression was also examined by immunofluorescence staining.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA from cultured cells and calvarial defect tissues was isolated using silica membrane-based RNA purification kits (QIAGEN, Germany). RNA concentration and purity were measured spectrophotometrically, and 500 ng RNA was reverse-transcribed using a PrimeScript RT reagent system (Takara Bio, Japan). Quantitative PCR was carried out using TB Green chemistry (Takara Bio, Japan) on a QuantStudio 5 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA). Relative transcript abundance was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method with Gapdh serving as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Immunofluorescence staining and cytokine ELISA

For immunofluorescence analysis, cells grown on coverslips or paraffin-embedded tissue sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked in bovine serum albumin-containing buffer. Samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against RUNX2 or p65 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA), or against OCN, TNF-α, and IL-10 (Abcam, UK), followed by Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired by confocal microscopy and quantified using ImageJ.

For cytokine quantification, culture supernatants were cleared by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 20 min and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Mouse TNF-α, IL-10, and TGF-β1 levels were measured using commercial ELISA kits (R&D Systems, USA) in accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols.

BMSC culture, immunophenotyping, and osteogenic differentiation

Primary C57BL/6 mouse BMSCs were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (China) and expanded in α-MEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Medium was renewed every other day, and cells from passages 3–5 were used for all experiments. To confirm lineage identity, BMSCs were characterized by flow cytometry using a mesenchymal marker panel, showing positive staining for CD73 and CD29 together with negligible expression of hematopoietic/myeloid markers such as CD34 and CD11b.

For osteogenic induction, BMSCs were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured in osteogenic differentiation medium containing α-MEM supplemented with ascorbate, β-glycerophosphate, and dexamethasone, with or without vesicles (20 μg mL−1). Early osteogenic commitment was evaluated on day 7 by alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and ALP activity measurement using commercial kits (Solarbio, China). Matrix mineralization was analyzed on day 14 by Alizarin Red S staining; bound dye was subsequently extracted with cetylpyridinium chloride and quantified spectrophotometrically. Osteogenesis-related genes, including Runx2, Opn, Bmp2, and Ocn, were measured by qRT-PCR, and RUNX2/OCN protein expression was visualized by immunofluorescence.

Osteoclastogenesis and TRAP-based readouts

To induce osteoclast-like differentiation, RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded in 24-well plates and stimulated with recombinant mouse RANKL (R&D Systems, USA) at 50 ng mL−1 in the presence or absence of NVs, M2-NVs, or M2-Apo-NVs (20 μg mL−1). Culture medium was replaced every 48 h until multinucleated osteoclast-like cells became evident. RANKL-treated cells without vesicles were used as a positive differentiation control.

Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining was conducted using a commercial TRAP staining kit (Solarbio, China). The formation of TRAP-positive multinucleated cells was recorded microscopically. TRAP enzymatic activity in parallel cultures was measured colorimetrically and normalized to total cellular protein content. In addition, the expression of osteoclast-associated genes, including Acp5, Dcstamp, Fos, and Nfatc1, was quantified by qRT-PCR.

NF-κB/NFAT reporter assays, p65 nuclear redistribution, and F-actin rings

To investigate signaling events involved in osteoclast suppression, RAW264.7 cells were transiently co-transfected with either an NF-κB-responsive luciferase reporter construct (GeneCopoeia, USA) or an NFAT response-element luciferase reporter plasmid (pGL4.30; Promega, USA), together with a Renilla normalization vector, using a lipid-based transfection reagent. After transfection, cells were pre-incubated with NVs, M2-NVs, or M2-Apo-NVs (20 μg mL−1) or with pathway inhibitors including BAY 11-7082 and cyclosporin A (both from MedChemExpress, USA), followed by stimulation with RANKL. Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were sequentially measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay system (Promega, USA) on a Synergy H1 reader.

For assessment of p65 nuclear translocation, RAW264.7 cells cultured on glass-bottom dishes were pretreated with vesicles for 2 h and then challenged with RANKL (50 ng mL−1) for 30 min. Cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained with anti-p65 antibody, followed by fluorescent secondary antibody and DAPI counterstaining. Confocal microscopy was used to assess subcellular redistribution of p65.

To visualize cytoskeletal maturation in osteoclasts, differentiated cultures were fixed and stained with rhodamine-phalloidin to reveal F-actin ring structures, with nuclei counterstained by DAPI.

Preparation of HAMA-based vesicle depots and in vitro characterization

Because a commercially available methacrylated hyaluronic acid precursor was used in this study, no in-house synthesis step was involved. Lyophilized HAMA precursor (PhotoHA® Stiff; CELLINK, Sweden) was dissolved under sterile conditions to generate the hydrogel precursor solution, after which lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added to a final concentration of 0.25% (w/v). NVs, M2-NVs, or M2-Apo-NVs were gently dispersed into the precursor immediately before gelation to minimize vesicle aggregation and interfacial loss. For in vivo implantation, the vesicle content was standardized to 5 × 1011 particles per construct on the basis of nanoparticle tracking analysis.

The precursor mixture was transferred into molds of defined geometry and photocrosslinked under sterile conditions using a 365 nm LED curing system (OmniCure LX500, Excelitas, Canada). Freeze-dried hydrogel samples were sputter-coated and imaged by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (ZEISS Sigma, Germany) to examine porous microarchitecture. To evaluate release behavior, vesicle-loaded hydrogels were incubated in PBS at 37 °C, and supernatants collected at predetermined time points were analyzed for released particle content. Hydrogel cytocompatibility was assessed by exposing RAW264.7 cells and BMSCs to hydrogel-conditioned medium, followed by CCK-8 analysis.

Murine critical-sized calvarial defect model

Male BALB/c mice (8 weeks old) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (China). All animal procedures complied with institutional and national regulations were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University (Approval No.: 2026DF015). After induction of general anesthesia and sterile preparation, a single full-thickness calvarial defect with a diameter of 5 mm was created in each mouse using a trephine bur while preserving the underlying dura mater. Animals were randomly allocated to receive HAMA alone, HAMA–NVs, HAMA–M2-NVs, or HAMA–M2-Apo-NVs. The material was photocrosslinked in situ, and the overlying soft tissues were closed in layers. At 12 weeks after surgery, calvarial specimens were harvested for micro-computed tomography, histology, immunofluorescence, and RNA analysis.

Micro-CT, histology, TRAP staining, and tissue-level immunofluorescence

Bone regeneration within the defect region was assessed by micro-computed tomography using a SkyScan 1276 system (Bruker, Belgium). Three-dimensional reconstructions were generated, and quantitative morphometric parameters including bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and bone mineral density (BMD) were determined within a standardized region of interest corresponding to the original defect.

Following micro-CT scanning, samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified in EDTA solution (pH 7.4) at 4 °C, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed to evaluate tissue bridging and defect closure, while Masson's trichrome staining was used to examine collagen-rich matrix deposition and new bone organization. TRAP staining of adjacent sections was carried out using the Solarbio kit to visualize osteoclast distribution at the bone interface. Tissue immunofluorescence for TNF-α, IL-10, and OCN was performed as described above.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). At least three independent experiments or biological replicates were analyzed for each condition. Statistical significance among multiple groups was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc multiple-comparisons test. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Characterization of NVs, M2-NVs and M2-Apo-NVs

To construct a vesicle platform that integrates pro-reparative macrophage programming with an apoptosis-associated membrane cue, RAW264.7 cells were first polarized with IL-4 and subsequently subjected to a controlled apoptotic-conditioning step prior to overt membrane disintegration. IL-4 stimulation induced a robust M2-like state, as evidenced by elevated Arg1 and Mrc1 transcription together with increased secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β1 (Fig. 1A and B). Phenotypic analysis further supported successful polarization of the donor macrophages (Fig. 1C). Following staurosporine treatment, Annexin V/PI analysis showed that the donor-cell population became enriched predominantly in the Annexin V+/PI− fraction (Fig. 1D and E), indicating that phosphatidylserine had been externalized while plasma membrane integrity remained largely preserved. After membrane isolation and serial extrusion, all vesicle formulations exhibited a nanoscale size range and membrane-bounded spherical morphology by TEM (Fig. 1H and I). However, M2-Apo-NVs were compositionally distinguishable from both NVs and M2-NVs. Most notably, M2-Apo-NVs displayed substantially stronger Annexin V binding (Fig. 1F and G), confirming enhanced PS presentation at the outer membrane leaflet, and exhibited a more negative ζ-potential (Fig. 1J), which is consistent with increased exposure of anionic phospholipids. These observations indicate that M2-Apo-NVs are not simply macrophage vesicles; rather, they represent membrane-reprogrammed vesicles in which the pro-repair donor-cell is further augmented by a defined apoptosis-associated surface chemistry.

PS-exposing extracellular vesicles constitute a biologically active membrane population with distinct interfacial behavior, underscoring the functional importance of PS-rich vesicle surfaces rather than considering membrane lipids as passive structural elements alone [33]. Under IL-4-rich conditions, the identity of apoptotic cargo can instruct macrophages toward distinct remodeling programs rather than a generic anti-inflammatory endpoint [34]. Moreover, efferocytosis activates an IDO1-dependent tryptophan–kynurenine pathway in macrophages that promotes IL-10-associated tissue resolution [20]. Taken together, these studies indicated that M2-Apo-NVs likely function as an apoptotic reparative nanointerface, in which donor-cell polarization and apoptotic membrane coding are integrated to improve downstream injury-resolution signaling.

M2-Apo-NVs exhibit preferential cellular internalization and more potently reprogram inflammatory macrophages

A first functional consequence of this membrane engineering became evident at the level of vesicle internalization. Confocal imaging and flow cytometry consistently showed that DiO-labeled M2-Apo-NVs generated the highest intracellular fluorescence in RAW264.7 macrophages among all formulations (Fig. 2A and B), indicating more efficient cell uptake. Importantly, this enhanced intracellular signal was not accompanied by measurable cytotoxicity, as metabolic activity remained stable and live/dead staining showed negligible membrane-compromised cells after exposure to vesicles (Fig. 2C and D). These data exclude the possibility that the stronger signal in the M2-Apo-NVs group arose from nonspecific membrane damage or a toxicity-driven uptake artifact. Under inflammatory challenge, this uptake advantage translated into a more pronounced macrophage phenotype shift. In LPS-primed macrophages, both M2-NVs and M2-Apo-NVs suppressed the inflammatory program, reflected by decreased Tnf-α and Il-1β expression, while simultaneously increasing Tgf-β and Il-10 (Fig. 2E). Immunofluorescence imaging corroborated this pattern at the protein level, with attenuated TNF-α staining and enhanced IL-10 signal after treatment (Fig. 2F and G). ELISA of culture supernatants further confirmed a coordinated cytokine rebalancing characterized by diminished TNF-α secretion and elevated IL-10 release (Fig. 2H). Notably, M2-Apo-NVs consistently elicited the strongest response across transcriptional, immunofluorescent, and secreted-protein readouts.

Mechanistically, this pattern is highly consistent with an efferocytosis-mimetic mode of action. PS-rich membranes are recognized by cellular clearance machinery as hallmarks of apoptotic material, and such recognition typically initiates a coordinated switch from inflammatory amplification toward resolution, cytokine restraint, and tissue repair [20,33,34]. The present data indicate that M2-Apo-NVs exploit an apoptosis-inspired recognition logic that operates in parallel with the reparative donor-cell signature inherited from M2 macrophages. This may explain why they outperform M2-NVs despite being generated from the same immunological lineage. Zhou et al. showed that engineered M2 exosomes with enhanced endocytic competence displayed stronger uptake and superior osteoregenerative activity than conventional M2 exosomes, indicating that vesicle internalization efficiency is itself a major determinant of downstream therapeutic potency [13]. Our results extend that notion by showing that membrane apoptosis can similarly amplify uptake and immunoregulatory efficacy.

This finding resonates with recent literature showing that the function of macrophage-derived vesicles can be tuned beyond basal M2 polarization. Zhou et al. reported that hydrogen sulfide-preconditioned M2 exosomes exhibited enhanced uptake by MSCs and promoted osteogenic differentiation through moesin-associated endocytosis and β-catenin activation, ultimately improving calvarial repair [13]. Wen et al. developed an engineered M2 exosome-loaded biomimetic periosteum and demonstrated improved BMSC recruitment, immunoregulation, angiogenesis, and bone regeneration in large defects [35]. Together, these studies support that macrophage-derived vesicles gain osteogenic potency when their interface or cargo is further optimized. Our data extend this principle in a distinct way: rather than engineering the vesicle with an added ligand or pharmacologic pretreatment, we modified the membrane state of the donor cell itself. The resulting apoptotic vesicles showed a clear functional increment over M2-NVs, suggesting that donor-cell state engineering may be an underappreciated axis for upgrading osteoinductive nanovesicles. EV-mediated regulation of bone homeostasis depends on tightly coordinated communication across osteogenic and resorptive compartments, reinforcing the relevance of vesicles that act on more than one skeletal cell type [36].

M2-Apo-NVs restrain osteoclastogenesis by suppressing the RANKL–NF-κB–NFATc1 axis

RANKL induced a typical osteoclast-like phenotype in RAW264.7 cells, characterized by abundant TRAP-positive multinucleated cells. Both M2-NVs and M2-Apo-NVs markedly reduced osteoclast formation, but the latter consistently exerted the stronger inhibitory effect, as reflected by fewer and smaller TRAP+ multinucleated cells and lower TRAP enzymatic activity (Fig. 5A and B). Mechanistic assays indicated that this inhibitory action occurred early in the signaling cascade. M2-Apo-NVs most effectively suppressed NF-κB reporter activity after RANKL stimulation and attenuated nuclear translocation of p65 (Fig. 5D–F). Because activation of NF-κB is a prerequisite for full induction and amplification of NFATc1, it is therefore that NFAT reporter activity was also reduced (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, mature osteoclast cultures displayed impaired F-actin ring formation after vesicle treatment (Fig. 5G), indicating that vesicle-mediated inhibition extended beyond transcriptional initiation to the cytoskeletal machinery required for late-stage osteoclast function. Taken together, these results support that M2-Apo-NVs suppress osteoclastogenesis at multiple levels, from early pathway activation to terminal structural maturation.

The relevance of this finding is reinforced by recent work from Huang et al., showing that M2 macrophage-derived extracellular vesicles can alleviate bone loss by reshaping osteoclast precursor fate and restoring the balance between osteoclastogenic and reparative myeloid states [14]. Our data are aligned with that study, but further suggest that appending an early apoptotic membrane signature to the M2 vesicle surface can strengthen the anti-osteoclastogenic effect. In the context of osteoimmunology, this is particularly important because bone repair rarely fails for a single reason; instead, impaired regeneration often reflects simultaneous persistence of inflammatory signaling and excessive resorptive activity. EV-based skeletal therapeutics are especially attractive when they can rebalance both bone formation and bone resorption rather than acting unidirectionally [36]. From this perspective, the superior performance of M2-Apo-NVs lies in their dual capability: they enhance osteogenic competence while simultaneously suppressing osteoclastogenic signaling.

HAMA-based local delivery converts vesicle-level osteoimmunomodulation into defect-scale regeneration

To translate the in vitro activity of the vesicles into a local regenerative strategy, NVs, M2-NVs, and M2-Apo-NVs were incorporated into a photocrosslinkable HAMA matrix. Freeze-dried constructs displayed a porous interconnected microstructure irrespective of vesicle loading (Fig. 6A), indicating that vesicle incorporation did not grossly alter scaffold architecture. Moreover, all vesicle-loaded formulations exhibited sustained release behavior with broadly comparable kinetics (Fig. 6B), suggesting that the superior performance of M2-Apo-NVs in vivo is unlikely to be explained merely by faster release from the matrix. These results fit well with the current view that successful EV-mediated bone repair depends on local microenvironmental rewiring. Yao et al. recently reported that M2-derived EVs improve inflamed bone regeneration by reprogramming immature neutrophils into an Anxa1hi reparative subset, highlighting that M2-vesicle therapeutics can remodel the broader immune ecology of bone defects rather than acting solely on macrophages [37]. Similarly, Wen et al. showed that engineered M2 exosomes immobilized within a biomimetic periosteal construct simultaneously promoted immunoregulation, endogenous BMSC recruitment, osteogenic differentiation, and angiogenesis [35]. In this work, when M2-derived vesicles are further endowed with an apoptosis-associated interface, their capacity to reshape the defect milieu becomes even stronger. This likely explains why M2-Apo-NVs outperform M2-NVs not only in vitro, but also at the defect scale.

The delivery format itself is also mechanistically meaningful. Although EVs possess considerable therapeutic promise, their clinical translation remains constrained by limited retention, stability, and targeting, and that smart material-enabled delivery systems are increasingly required to overcome these barriers [38]. In parallel, Wang et al. demonstrated that cell membrane vesicles can preserve parent-cell membrane functionality and enhance bone regeneration, supporting the broader translational value of membrane-derived vesicle systems beyond canonical exosomes [12]. Our HAMA-based strategy is consistent with these emerging principles: the hydrogel primarily serves as a localization and persistence platform, whereas the vesicle subtype determines biological potency. Within this framework, M2-Apo-NVs appear to be the most effective vesicle configuration for reconstructing a pro-regenerative osteoimmune microenvironment.

Taken together, the present study supports a multicomponent engineering framework for cell-derived nanovesicles. Compared with NVs, M2-NVs already inherit a donor-cell program biased toward inflammation resolution and repair. Introducing an early apoptotic membrane phenotype on top of this reparative background generates M2-Apo-NVs, which display stronger PS exposure, greater cellular internalization, deeper suppression of inflammatory macrophage activation, more robust enhancement of BMSC osteogenesis, more effective inhibition of osteoclastogenesis, and ultimately superior calvarial defect repair.

The biology of apoptotic recognition is increasingly understood as an instructive rather than purely clearance-related process, capable of directing immune-cell state transitions and tissue remodeling outcomes [20,34]. EVs and membrane vesicles are now recognized as powerful regulators of bone homeostasis and skeletal repair, especially when they can simultaneously target immune and skeletal compartments [[12], [13], [14],[35], [36], [37]]. Current translational thinking places increasing emphasis on delivery formats that improve local persistence and biological precision of vesicle therapeutics [38]. Our findings unify these trends into a single framework: engineering the state of the donor membrane may be as important as choosing the donor cell itself. M2-Apo-NVs should not be viewed merely as a stronger version of M2-NVs; rather, they represent an apoptotic osteoimmunomodulatory vesicle system with enhanced ability to engage the injury-resolution machinery and re-establish regenerative balance in bone defects.

Despite the encouraging results, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the present work was mainly conducted in murine cell systems and validated in a mouse critical-sized calvarial defect model; therefore, caution is needed when extrapolating these findings to human bone regeneration, especially in load-bearing defects. Second, although our data indicate that early-apoptotic membrane engineering enhances cellular uptake and downstream osteoimmunomodulatory effects, the precise receptor-mediated recognition mechanisms and the relative contributions of specific membrane-associated lipids, proteins, and cargo molecules were not fully resolved. Third, long-term biosafety, in vivo biodistribution, pharmacokinetic behavior, and large-scale manufacturing feasibility of M2-Apo-NVs remain to be systematically investigated in future studies before clinical translation can be considered.

Conclusion

In summary, an apoptotic macrophage-derived nanovesicle system was established by integrating M2 polarization with early apoptotic membrane remodeling. The resulting M2-Apo-NVs displayed a distinct PS-enriched interfacial phenotype and consistently outperformed conventional M2-NVs across multiple regenerative dimensions, including inflammatory macrophage reprogramming, BMSC osteogenic commitment, and suppression of osteoclastogenesis. When delivered locally within a HAMA hydrogel, M2-Apo-NVs further translated these in vitro osteoimmunomodulatory advantages into significantly improved calvarial defect repair in vivo. Collectively, these results demonstrate that engineering the donor-cell membrane state, in addition to donor-cell phenotype, provides a powerful route for enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of cell-derived nanovesicles. This study therefore offers a multicomponent engineering framework for osteoimmunomodulatory vesicle design and highlights M2-Apo-NVs as a promising candidate for bone regeneration.
