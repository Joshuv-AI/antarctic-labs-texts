RNA sequencing reveals novel lncRNA modulators in gemini nanocurcumin–treated colorectal cancer cells
Authors: Zohre Zibaei, Hewa Jalal Azeez, Esmaeil Babaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Due to a high rate of recurrence coupled with resistance towards modern therapies, colorectal cancer (CRC) is considered as the third cause of cancer-related death worldwide. Gemini curcumin (Gemini-Cur) is one of the last nanoformulation of curcumin with significant toxicity on colorectal cancer. Herein, we aimed to unravel the modulated lncRNAs, related mRNAs and downstream cellular pathways in Gemini-Cur treated HT-29 colorectal cancer cells. 9805 lncRNAs were found to be differentially expressed in nanocurcumin-treated cancer cells versus non treated group. The top 20 lncRNAs were selected for further studies and, 14,472 co-expression relationships between these RNAs and 70,711 mRNAs were identified. Among top 20 lncRNAs, tumor-suppressive C8orf31 and ARHGAP5-AS1, as well as oncogenic XIST, FTX, and NEAT1 were the most notable due to their involvements in cancer-related cellular pathways. Functional enrichment analyses demonstrated that the modulated lncRNAs and their targets are involved in cell cycle, p53 signaling, translation, and helicase activity pathways. In conclusion, our study elucidated new molecular mechanisms of nano-curcumin in the regulation of lncRNA expression and the discovery of potential targets in therapeutic interventions for CRC. More studies are needed to confirm the therapeutic implications of these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24202-5.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a significant public health concern, with its incidence steadily rising over the years1. Despite advancements in medical knowledge and technology, the 5 year survival rate for CRC remains stagnant at around 50%, primarily due to its high recurrence rate2. Therefore it is imperative to explore new, safe and effective treatment options for CRC patients.

One of the central sources of drug discovery is natural products, because these molecules have huge potential for anti-cancer properties; nowadays, their potentiality can reach up to 60% of all antitumor agents applied in clinic practice3.

Curcumin, a polyphenol bioactive compound, is extracted from Curcuma longa4. The anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-cancer activities have been of great interest in the recent reviews5. By modulation of multiple signaling pathways of cellular mechanisms fundamental to growth, apoptosis, and metastasis including NF-kB, MAPK, P53, PI3K/Akt, Wnt/β-catenin, and JAK/STAT, this compound has positioned itself at the very front line of possible cancer therapy6. Its clinical efficacy is however impaired by poor solubility and low bioavailability7. In parallel, our research group has developed a nanotechnology-based formulation, Gemini surfactant nano curcumin (Gemini-Cur), to improve the therapeutic efficiency of curcumin. Gemini surfactants are two analogous surfactant molecules connected by flexible or rigid spacers and act as lipid delivery systems.

Conventional nanocurcumin formulations, such as PLGA nanoparticles, liposomes, and micelles, have been shown to improve curcumin’s solubility, stability, and bioavailability in colorectal cancer7. However, Gemini surfactant–based nanocarriers offer additional advantages, including stable micelle formation at very low concentrations, potentially reducing carrier-related toxicity and enhancing cellular delivery efficiency.

The physicochemical characterization of Gemini-Cur has been reported in detail by Karimpour et al. in 20218.

The recent advancement in RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) facilitates the exploration of the gene expression profile that can help find potential targets of therapeutic and preventive interventions9. Transcriptome analysis helps to make out changes in the expression of differentially expressed genes and gets valuable insight into the molecular pathways involved in the disease process10. Additionally, this area of research has made a huge contribution to basic knowledge about gene expression and the mechanism underlying it in most intrinsic diseases, including CRC, with especial emphasis on long non-coding RNAs (LncRNAs)11.

LncRNAs, are distinguished by their length, which, as a rule, constitutes more than 200 nucleotides12. Their transcription is mainly done by RNA polymerase II and III13, thereby playing a key role in the interaction of proteins, RNAs, and various cellular compartments to form ribonucleoprotein complexes14. These lncRNAs possess a range of mechanisms, namely epigenetic, transcriptional, or post-transcriptional regulations, which can modulate the expression of the targeted gene, suggesting that they might have therapeutic applications15.

A growing body of evidence indicates the lncRNAs associated with CRC are crucial to mechanisms including among others invasion, metastasis, chemo resistance, and radio resistance16. Those lncRNAs whose function is associated with CRC have been described to act on the pathways regulating them, e.g., by using the ones such as the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), Wnt, and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling pathways17. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that lncRNAs induced by CRC have the capacity to engage with miRNAs, thereby introducing an additional layer of regulatory mechanisms pertinent to gene expression associated with this category of RNAs18.

The HT-29 colorectal cancer cell line is a popular model for cancer pathogenesis and treatment evaluation in vitro among the most widely studied19. Nevertheless, the presently induced transcriptomic changes through curcumin in HT-29 cells still largely unexplored.

Thus, this study was designed to investigate the molecular involvement of Gemini-Cur affecting the expression of lncRNAs in HT-29 colorectal cancer cells. Here, we utilized previously available RNA-seq data to comprehensively identify new lncRNAs that are differentially expressed in Gemini-cur-treated cells. The Gemini-Cur, at its effective concentration determined by the IC50 value from our previous study20, was applied to treat the HT-29 cells. Then the RNA-seq analysis was used to exlpore lncRNAs and their respective mRNAs in both the treated and non-treated cell populations. In this regard, the identification of differentially expressed lncRNAs, together with their corresponding mRNAs, supported by functional enrichment analysis, add valuable insight into biological processes, molecular functions, and pathways affected by the action of Gemini-Cur.

Advanced bioinformatics resources and databases were used to look for the prioritization of data sets and validation of the potential lncRNA targets identified for downstream analysis through transcriptome profiling in our study. All these methods were aimed at establishing the new target genes, molecular interactions and the signaling pathways responsible for the anti-cancer activity of Gemini-Cur.

Materials and methods

Library construction and RNA sequencing

cDNA libraries for RNA sequencing were generated using the TruSeq RNA Sample Prep Kit v2 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), with approximately 1 µg of total RNA from each sample. Library preparation followed the manufacturer’s guidelines and involved poly(A) RNA isolation with poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads, enzymatic fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, adapter ligation with barcodes, and PCR amplification. External RNA Controls Consortium (ERCC) Spike-In Control Mix 1 (Ambion) was included to ensure reliability. The libraries were sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform with paired-end 150 bp reads, and raw data were converted into FASTQ format using the bcl2fastq software package (Illumina).

Reference genome mapping and transcriptome annotation

Trimmomatic software (https://github.com/usadellab/Trimmomatic/releases/tag/v0.39) was used to remove the reads that contained adaptor contamination, low-quality bases and undetermined bases. The sequence quality was then verified using FastQC (https://github.com/s-andrews/FastQC/releases/tag/v0.11.8). Subsequently, the trimmed reads were mapped to the reference human genome (GRCh38.p14, Ensembl) using the HiSat2 (https://github.com/DaehwanKimLab/hisat2/releases/tag/v2.2.1) aligner, which is capable of accounting for splice junctions during the alignment process. Following read mapping, the HTSeq (https://github.com/simon-anders/htseq/releases/tag/release_0.11.3) was employed to quantify gene expression levels based on the lncRNA and mRNA annotation files separately in the Ensembl database, generating gene expression tables in the form of raw count values.

Screening of differentially expressed RNAs

In this respect, the DESeq2 v1.36.0 workflow from the Bioconductor package (R v4.2.1) was applied for the normalization of gene count data and differential expression analysis. Differentially expressed lncRNAs and mRNAs were chosen by log2 fold change > 2 or < −2, while a p-value less than 0.05 served as a criterion of statistical significance. These cutoffs were set to focus on lncRNAs and mRNAs with extremely big expression changes that were also statistically solid. In addition, Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-values (false discovery rate, FDR) were calculated for all tested RNAs, and these values are provided in Supplementary Table S1 for transparency.

The results were visualized on volcano plots using the “ggplot2” package in R. This way, there is a clear and very condensed view of the statistical significance, together with the magnitude of differential expression for every lncRNA and mRNA. Heatmaps were also created with the help of the “pheatmap” R package and allowed expression patterns for the differentially expressed lncRNAs and mRNAs across samples to be visualized.

Prediction of lncRNA target genes

To identify potential target genes of lncRNAs, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between the twenty most significantly differentially expressed lncRNAs (selected based on highest log2 fold change and lowest p-value) and differentially expressed mRNAs (log2 fold change ≥ 2 or ≤ −2 and p-value < 0.05). A threshold of Pearson correlation > 0.99 and FDR < 0.01 was applied, yielding 14,472 significant lncRNA–mRNA co-expression relationships involving 20 lncRNAs and 70,711 mRNAs for downstream functional analysis. For validation, 10 pairs with the highest correlation and lowest FDR were selected.

Function and pathway enrichment analyses

Functional enrichment at Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway levels was carried out to identify the functions of these lncRNAs. Since functional enrichment analysis was done with consideration of targets of the lncRNAs with respect to the DAVID Bioinformatics Tool (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/), this is already a very rich resource of functional annotation, facilitating the extraction of important biological insights from a given gene set. GO terms and KEGG pathways exhibiting statistical significance (p-value < 0.05) and substantial enrichment (enrichment score > 1.0) were deemed indicative of the potential functional roles of prognostic lncRNAs. The KEGG database (Kanehisa Laboratories, Kyoto University; https://www.kegg.org/) was used as the reference for pathway annotation, and the GO and pathway graphs were generated using the SRplot web tool (https://bioinformatics.com.cn/en). Pathway information was cited in accordance with Kanehisa et al.21–23.

In silico validation

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the selected genes affected by Gemini-Cur compound, the top ten selected targeted mRNAs, as top-genes, were evaluated in silico using the TCGA database. However, four of these genes could not be assessed due to their absence in the database.

mRNA expression profiling of top genes

UALCAN (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis-prot.html), a multifunctional bioinformatics database-based platform, provides an efficient way to analyze the expression of genes with high-density array data from the TCGA project. It provides a handy platform for the researcher and clinical person trying to measure gene expression in different cancer types. The tool should enable the researcher to search for genes of interest and visualize the expression profiles in a panel of different cancers, cancer subtypes, and clinical features. So, UALCAN could be one of the most influential and frequently accessed public resources for cancer research and personalized medicine in many cases. In this work, UALCAN was applied to compare the mRNA expression profiling of top-genes, affected by Gemini-Cur in CRC samples versus controls and across other cancer types.

Promoter methylation analysis of top genes

Among the most common epigenetic changes related to lots of diseases, including cancer, one can enlist promoter methylation. This process is often associated with the silencing of genes involved in cell proliferation, differentiation, and repair, which might contribute to tumor growth. The expression of top-genes concerning promoter methylation has been estimated using the UALCAN platform. The statistically significant p-value was 0.05 or less.

Survival analysis

In this study, we employed GEPIA (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), a robust online tool for survival analysis, to evaluate the relationship between top-genes expression levels and patient outcomes. GEPIA facilitated the investigation of how these genes influence overall survival and disease-free survival across specific cancer types.

Expression levels of top-genes in CRC pathological stages

To further characterize the expression patterns of the identified top-genes, their mRNA expression levels were analyzed across different pathological stages of CRC using the GEPIA platform. For the current analysis, the expression levels of the top-genes were examined across the major pathological stages of CRC, including stage I, stage II, stage III, and stage IV.

Mutational analysis

In the present study, we used the cBioPortal database mutational analysis feature (https://www.cbioportal.org/), which is a valuable resource for assessing genomic alterations across diverse cancers. cBioPortal provides detailed genomic and clinical data from a large number of cancer studies that researchers can use to mine the mutation landscape of specific genes of interest. We performed deep mutational analysis for the top-genes in CRC samples using the default settings of cBioPortal. In that respect, we evaluated the mutational status of the identified top-genes in an attempt to find genetic alterations that would probably affect their function and therefore be involved in the development and progression of CRC.

Results

Analysis of the quality of the RNA-Seq data

The Illumina HiSeq 4000 sequencing platform generated an average of approximately 5 million reads from transcriptome libraries of the HT-29 colorectal cancer cell line, both treated with Gemini-Cur and corresponding controls. Following quality control (QC), more than 90% of these reads were deemed clean. Subsequent alignment of the clean reads to the GRCh38.p14 reference genome revealed that approximately 98.5% of the total reads successfully mapped to the genome.

In this study, we employed the GEPIA tool to perform survival analysis on the top-genes in CRC patients. In contrast, the expression levels of FOSB, ATP13A4, AKR1C1, CYP4F11, and AKR1C2 showed no significant correlations with OS (p > 0.05; Fig. 6). Discussion

Due to high incidence and fast progression of CRC, this cancer takes away the possibility of cure or surgical resection for too many patients, with some even not manifesting obvious symptoms. Diagnosis at late stages thus has been common24. Surgical resection improves patient survival, together with other adjuvant therapies that could improve prognosis. However, usually CRC patients have poorer prognosis and more side effects25,26. Accordingly, the search for drugs with fine anticancer effects and fewer side effects has become a new research goal and hot spot.

Based on the therapeutic characteristics of Chinese medicine, "multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway," herbal compounds have been comprehensively employed in the research and treatment of cancers27. However, the molecular mechanisms and metabolic pathways in CRC remain not fully elucidated. Our research team has previously conducted various studies on the impact of curcumin on colorectal cancer. Notably, we have explored the significant effects of the Gemini-curcumin formulation on apoptosis pathway, metastasis, and the mRNA expression profile in this cancer20,28,29. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that lncRNAs play critical role during cancer initiation and development30. In the current study, given the lack of comprehensive understanding regarding the effects of curcumin on lncRNA molecules in cancer, we aim to investigate the influence of this compound on the expression profiles of lncRNAs in colorectal cancer. The outcomes of this research may lead to the identification of new target genes and molecular pathways, thereby enhancing our understanding of the precise mechanisms of action of curcumin in colorectal cancer.

In the present study, RNAs from colorectal cancer cells with and without Gemini-Cur treatment were sequenced, quantified and normalized. Consequently, 347 DElncRNAs (padj < 0.01, log2 FC ≥ 2) were identified that for doing the downstream analysis, Top-20-lncRNAs, from DelncRNAs, were selected. Among the 20 lncRNAs, only half of them were well studied in cancers that this is because that lncRNAs are almost new discovered types of RNAs and to know more about them, more research is still needed.

In one of the researches related to CRC, C8orf48 was used as a biomarker for the early detection of CRC by repressing the MAPK signaling pathway, regulating cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in one of the researches related to CRC31. HAS2-AS1 expression was found overexpressed in glioma tissues, the things which correlated positively with the prognosis of patients with glioma. It’s down-regulation significantly repressed the migration and invasion both in vitro and in vivo32. Liyuan Zhu et al. demonstrated the importance of ARHGAP5 expression in CRC metastasis, and impaired autophagic degradation of ARHGAP5-AS1 in chemoresistant gastric cancer cells promotes chemoresistance, targeting the ARHGAP5-AS1/ARHGAP5 axis maybe an attractive strategy for the treatment of chemoresistance in colorectal and gastric cancers33,34. LncRNA XIST boosts CRC growth and spread35. In Li’s research, Downregulation of XIST leads to a growth-inhibitory effect on colorectal cancer cells and also promotes apoptosis through differential expression of miR-338-3p/PAX5 axis, therefore targeting them could be one of the potential strategies for preventing and treating the disease36. Another down-regulated gene in our study, LncRNA FTX, functioned as an oncogene to contribute to CRC progression. Elevated FTX expression is indicative of a worse prognosis in individuals with colorectal, hepatocellular, and osteosarcoma malignancies, as well as glioma37,38. Guofang Chen et al. demonstrate that Targeted FTX depletion can block CRC cells’ movement and invasion, thus presenting a new therapeutic approach for CRC39. Silencing CHRM3-AS2 expression, One of the genes significantly altered in this study, inhibits glioma progression by targeting microRNA-370-5p/KLF4, offering potential therapeutic targets for glioma treatment40. Another down-regulated gene in our study, LINC01564, which mechanistically acts as a competing endogenous RNA for miR-107/103a-3p and attenuates the inhibitory action of miR-107/103a-3p on PHGDH, is an important player in the regulation of metabolic adaptation of cancer cells and also implicated as a potential therapeutic target for cancer41. Various reports have shown the potential of lncRNA NEAT1 to act in CRC. NEAT1 is an oncogene in CRC, enhancing cell proliferation and metastasis by competitively binding with miR-34a and promoting the Wnt/-catenin signaling pathway, as illustrated by Meng Zhang et al.42. Another study, that of Yihao Zhu et al., shows that NEAT1 promotes 5-Fu resistance of CRC by remodeling chromatin, potentially offering a new strategy for treating 5-Fu resistance in CRC43. SLC7A11-AS1 and PRNCR1 are oncogenic lncRNAs that according to studies, upregulation of them in CRC, promotes cancer formation and progression, offering potential therapeutic targets in CRC44,45.

Considering these studies that C8orf31 and ARHGAP5-AS1 were as important tumor-suppressive lncRNAs and XIST, FTX, CHRM3-AS2, LINC01564, NEAT1, SLC7A11-AS1 and PRNCR1 play effective oncogenic role in colorectal and other cancers, as the Gemini-Cur treatment of colorectal cancer HT-29 cell line significantly cause the up-regulation of C8orf31 and ARHGAP5-AS1 and the down-regulation of XIST, FTX, CHRM3-AS2, LINC01564, NEAT1, SLC7A11-AS1 and PRNCR1, it can be said that Gemini-Cur can targets the key lncRNAs in CRC and so may be an efficient therapeutic option for CRC. The observed upregulation of HAS2-AS1 in CRC, in contrast to its reported oncogenic role in other cancers, may be attributed to tissue specificity or cell-type differences. In our study, the upregulation was seen in the HT-29 colorectal cancer cell line, which may not fully represent the behavior of HAS2-AS1 in other CRC models or primary tumor tissues. The role of HAS2-AS1 could vary across different cancer types or even within different CRC subtypes, highlighting the need for more tissue-specific and cell-type specific analyses. Further studies involving a broader range of CRC cell lines, primary tumor samples, and tissue-specific models are necessary to fully elucidate the molecular role of HAS2-AS1 in CRC progression and its potential as a therapeutic target.

To determine the potential functions of Top-20-lncRNAs differentially expressed in treated colorectal cancer cells, the functions of their target genes were analyzed. GO and KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the target genes are involved in certain pathways associated with cancer, such as cell cycle, p53 signaling pathways, translation and helicase activity. Especially, the dysregulation of pathways related to the cell cycle proposes that these lncRNAs may affect proliferation in cancer cells and tumor growth. This might be mediated through the action of the lncRNAs on key regulators of the cell cycle, such as cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases, influencing cell cycle progression and contributing to the therapeutic efficacy of Gemini-Cur. Mosieniak et al. indicated that in the human colon cancer cell line HCT-116, curcumin strongly reduced cell proliferation by inducing an arrest at the G2/M phase of the cell cycle and partially also at the G1 phase46. Lim et al. further showed that curcumin downregulated cyclin D1 expression and induced cell cycle arrest at the G1 phase in the same colon cancer cell line47. Another study done on the cytotoxicity of curcumin against the HCT-116 and HT29 cell lines revealed a time- and dose-dependent sequential inhibition of proliferation following induction of the tumor suppressor p5348. Tumor suppressor protein p53 is a crucial modulator of the cell cycle and cellular response to stress. As a transcription factor, it activates or modulates cell cycle arrest, DNA repair, or programmed cell death through its influence on the translation of proteins that are critical for these processes. When activated, p53 can lead to cell cycle arrest to allow repair or induce cell death if the damage is irreparable. Dysregulation of the pathway that includes p53 removes control over cell division, allowing it to proceed unchecked and promoting cancer49,50. The involvement of p53 is not limited to cell cycle regulation but extends to the DNA repair mechanisms themselves. DNA helicases play a major role in these mechanisms and often show direct or indirect regulation by p5351. Activated p53 by Gemini-Cur can further promote DNA damage repair and genomic stability by modulating helicase activity. Thus, Gemini-Cur’s influence on the p53 pathway will alter how helicases regulate DNA repair and replication, thereby impacting the survival of cancerous cells and their response to treatment.

The current work has certain limitations. First, the predicted lncRNA–mRNA interactions were identified only through correlation analysis (Pearson r > 0.99, FDR < 0.01) without experimental validation and should therefore be regarded as exploratory. Second, validation relied on TCGA-CRC datasets, which differ from the HT-29 cell line model in both context (tissue vs. cell line) and design (tumor vs. normal vs. treated vs. untreated). These results should thus be interpreted as supportive rather than confirmatory. Future studies should include qRT-PCR or Western blot validation in HT-29 and additional CRC cell lines (e.g., HCT116, LoVo). Third, immunogenicity and potential immune responses of Gemini-Cur must be assessed in preclinical models. Lastly, GLP-toxicology studies are necessary to evaluate the safety and toxicity profile in vivo before advancing to clinical trials. These issues should be addressed in future studies to ensure the therapeutic viability of Gemini-Cur in CRC treatment.

Conclusion

This study underscores the potential of Gemini surfactant nano-curcumin as a promising therapeutic strategy for CRC by identifying novel target genes and pathways. Our results suggest that Gemini-Cur may exert its effects in part through the modulation of specific lncRNAs involved in CRC, thereby offering a potential approach to enhance therapeutic outcomes. The observed alterations in lncRNA expression and associated pathways highlight the exploratory nature of these findings. Further research, particularly functional validation and rescue experiments, will be necessary to confirm the mechanistic role of lncRNAs and to evaluate the clinical applicability of Gemini-Cur in CRC therapy. This study provides a foundation for future investigations into how Gemini-Cur influences lncRNA profiles and contributes to cancer treatment.
