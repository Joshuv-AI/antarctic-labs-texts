Regulation of glucose metabolism: Effects on oocyte, preimplantation embryo, assisted reproductive technology and embryonic stem cell
Authors: Yu-Ying Xiong, Hai-Ying Zhu, Ruo-Jin Shi, Yun-Feng Wu, Yong Fan, Long Jin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlucose is a major energy substrate for cellular life activities, and its metabolic pathways include glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway, and the polyol pathway. Here, we review the glucose uptake pathways, metabolic characteristics, glucose transport, glucose metabolism-related enzymes, and biological importance in mammalian oocyte maturation, early embryo development, and embryonic stem cell proliferation and differentiation. Moreover, the interrelationships among glucose metabolism, female reproduction-related diseases and assisted reproductive technologies are focused. In addition, we review a number of analytical methodologies with the intention to integrate a multi-tiered strategy that encompasses cutting-edge metabolomics, artificial intelligence, epigenetics, and morphological assessments, setting the stage for a pivotal approach to cultivating high-caliber embryos in the future.Keywords: Glycolysis, Pentose phosphate pathway, Hexosamine biosynthesis pathway, Polyol pathway, Oocyte maturation, Embryo development

Introduction

According to the prediction of the World Health Organization, infertility will affect 8–12 % of couples globally, making it the third most common human disease after cancer and cardiovascular diseases [[1], [2], [3]]. Environmental pollution, delayed childbearing age, increased social pressure and other factors have reduced the developmental potential of gametes and embryos, leading to declining fertility [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. In vitro maturation (IVM) of oocytes and in vitro culture (IVC) of preimplantation embryos can not only improve the quality of oocytes and embryos but also help preserve female fertility; thus, these techniques are widely used in fields such as assisted reproductive technology (ART) [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Unfortunately, the efficiency of clinical IVM and IVC is still relatively low [12]. Strategies to improve the in vitro developmental ability of gametes and embryos are a hot research topic in reproductive medicine. Recent studies have found that metabolic balance is a key factor determining oocyte and embryo quality. During oocyte maturation and early embryonic development, rapid and dynamic metabolic patterns occur, including changes in glucose metabolism, lipid metabolism, and amino acid metabolism [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. The normal metabolism of glucose is a fundamental aspect of these processes. Interestingly, common causes of infertility, such as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), and obesity, are disrupt glucose metabolism, leading to reduced energy availability for oocytes and embryos, which ultimately negatively affects pregnancy outcomes [19,20]. In addition, a study that examined the metabolic activities of 209 human blastocysts through Gardner grading, the KID Score algorithm, and artificial intelligence (AI) scoring showed that blastocysts with greater developmental and implantation potential consumed over 40 % more glucose than those of lesser potential [21]. Gardner's report also indicated that embryos resulting in clinical pregnancies had significantly higher glucose consumption on days 4 and 5 compared to those that failed to develop after transfer [22]. Recently, rapid advancements in metabolomics technology have allowed scientists to more accurately assess the effectiveness of IVC conditions and the developmental potential of germ cells by analyzing glucose metabolite levels in the culture medium. For instance, by employing ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS), researchers observed a negative correlation between the levels of glucose, pyruvate, and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+/NADH) in the follicular fluid of PCOS patients and the number of high-quality oocytes, which suggesting reduced glucose uptake by reproductive cells [[23], [24], [25], [26]].

Consequently, this paper provides a comprehensive review of the four pathways of glucose metabolism: glycolysis, pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), hexosamine biosynthesis pathway (HBP) and polyol pathway (PP) [[27], [28], [29]]. It summarizes the glucose uptake and metabolic characteristics in oocytes and preimplantation embryos, analyzes the regulatory function of glucose in the proliferation and differentiation process of embryonic stem cells (ESCs), and elucidates the role of glucose metabolism in assisted reproduction technology, which improves the viability of gamete and embryo quality, and provides a new way of thinking about the treatment of infertility.

Pathways of glucose uptake in oocytes and early embryos

Glucose metabolism begins with cellular glucose uptake. However, glucose cannot freely penetrate the phospholipid bilayer cell membrane and relies on glucose transporters (GLUTs) on the membrane to enter the cell (Fig. 1A). GLUTs are a family of transmembrane proteins that regulate extracellular glucose entry into cells, mainly including 14 members (GLUT1-14). These transporters are present in tissues and organs throughout the body, but their expression types and densities also differ due to tissue specificity [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34]]. In the female reproductive system, cumulus cells, oocytes, and the gap junctions connecting them work together to form a functional unit known as the cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs) [29]. GLUT4, distinguished by its high affinity for glucose and proficient facilitation of glucose entry into the cell, is uniquely expressed in mouse cumulus oocytes. Conversely, GLUT1, 3, and 8, which demonstrate a more modest affinity for glucose, are exclusively found in mouse oocytes, hence limiting their direct uptake and utilization of glucose [29]. While human granulosa cells (GCs) did not exhibit expression of GLUT2 and 7, the remaining 12 GLUTs family members were prominently expressed [35]. Notably, GLUT1 stands out as the sole GLUT member expressed in human oocytes [36]. Recently, scientists have employed the non-metabolizable fluorescent glucose derivative 6-NBDG to clarify the specific pathway of glucose transport into mouse oocytes. They discovered that glucose is specifically taken up by GLUT4 on cumulus cells with high affinity, and the resulting metabolites, pyruvate and lactate, are subsequently transferred to the oocytes through gap junctions [27,[37], [38], [39]]. Previous studies have shown that oocytes require a substantial and continuously increasing amount of Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) during maturation, fertilization, and embryo development, driving a shift from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) as their primary energy source to maintain normal physiological functions [40,41]. Thus, hen sufficient oxygen is available, pyruvate and lactate are primarily transported to the oocyte's mitochondria by Monocarboxylate Transporters (MCTs), where they are oxidized into Acetyl-CoA (Ac-CoA). This oxidation triggers the Tricarboxylic Acid (TCA) Cycle, producing NADH and Flavin adenine dinucleotide, ultimately generating a substantial amount of ATP through OXPHOS [29,[42], [43], [44], [45], [46], [47]]. To date, 14 types of MCTs have been identified, with MCTs 1–4 playing a role in lactate and pyruvate transport in mouse unfertilized oocytes, whereas only MCTs 1–2 are present in human unfertilized oocytes [45,48]. This intricate timing of GLUT expression suggests a carefully orchestrated regulation of glucose uptake, critical for the energy requirements and developmental progression of the embryo. Although cumulus cells have a higher affinity for glucose than oocytes, oocytes also play an important role in glucose utilization. Oocytes activate transcription of glycolytic genes in cumulus cells by secreting growth differentiation factor 9, bone morphogenetic protein 15, and fibroblast growth factors, thereby increasing glucose uptake and pyruvate delivery [13,[49], [50], [51], [52], [53]]. Hence, the collective actions of oocytes and cumulus cells synergistically impact glucose uptake and utilization processes.

Early on, the ubiquitous expression of GLUT1, 3, and 8 seems to establish the foundational glucose supply, ensuring the embryo's initial growth. As development advances past the 8-cell threshold, the emergence of GLUT2, 9, and 14 adds layers to the glucose transport system, possibly reflecting the increased metabolic demands of the rapidly dividing cells. By the blastocyst stage, the specific detection of GLUT4 points to a refined mechanism of glucose uptake, fine-tuning the final steps before implantation and further development ensues. This dynamic pattern underscores the complexity and precision of metabolic regulation within the embryonic microenvironment [30,36,54,55]. This suggests that there is a noticeable surge in glucose uptake at late-stage embryonic development. Recent studies have uncovered that employing inhibitors or RNA interference technology to suppress the expression of GLUT1, 3, and 8 can diminish cellular glucose absorption. This reduction in glucose uptake subsequently impacts the early embryo's development and its ability to implant successfully [36,[56], [57], [58]]. Interestingly, evidence closely associates GLUT3 with the initial differentiation event in the development of mammalian embryos. During embryonic differentiation, GLUT3 specifically localizes to the apical membranes of the polarized cells within the compacted morula, and subsequently, it is targeted to the apical membranes of trophectoderm (TE) cells, facilitating access to maternal glucose supplies [59]. Therefore, GLUT3 functions as a critical glucose transporter protein on trophoblast cells, playing a vital role in the formation of the blastocyst. Moreover, research by Schmidt demonstrates that mice genetically modified to lack GLUT3 exhibit embryonic lethality, further emphasizing the fundamental importance of this transporter in early developmental stages [60]. The preceding evidence underscores the pivotal role of GLUTs in the initial stages of embryonic development, highlighting their indispensable contribution to early life formation.

It is widely known that pyruvate is an essential nutrient to support embryo development from the zygote to 2-cell stage [[61], [62], [63], [64], [65], [66]]. Previous studies showed glucose supplementation during the initial 48 h of IVC decreased the 2-cell rate [[67], [68], [69], [70]], indicating the critical role of pyruvate in an early stage of development. As embryo development progresses to the 2-cell to 4-cell stage, both pyruvate and lactate become major consumption product [71,72]. Fascinatingly, when 2-cell stage embryos were cultivated in environments with diminished pyruvate concentrations, there was a notable disruption in the mRNA expression levels of genes associated with DNA methylation, histone methylation, N6-methyladenosine modifications, and the clearance of maternal mRNAs [63,64]. At the 8-cell stage, there is a significant increase in energy demands used for various biological precursors required for synthesizing cell membranes and nuclei. This surge in energy requirement is attributed to zygotic genome activation (ZGA) and mitosis [[73], [74], [75], [76]]. During this critical developmental stage, there is a marked increase in TCA and glycolysis. The embryos begin to utilize glucose to produce large amounts of ATP to support cell division and development (Fig. 1B) [77,78]. Following the blastocyst stage, glucose emerges as the primary energy substrate in embryos [21,[79], [80], [81]]. This emphasizing the precise spatiotemporal nature of glycolysis during preimplantation embryo development.

A recent study reveals the metabolic landscape of mouse embryonic development and identifies notable differences in metabolic profile between 2-cell and blastocyst stage embryos [82]. Metabolic products such as citrate, α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), and succinate are found to be enriched in blastocysts, while the competitive α-KG metabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG) exhibited higher levels in 2-cell embryos (Fig. 2). Addition of L-2-HG to IVC delays embryo development and decreases blastocyst quality by increasing an abnormal high level of trimethyl-histone H3 lysine 4 in early 2-cell and 4-cell embryos. Furthermore, the oxidized NAD+ is a key metabolite at the mouse ZGA stage, whereas TCA, glycolysis and glutaminolysis are obviously enhanced in the blastocyst stage. Treatment with FK866, a NAD+ synthesis inhibitor, significantly decreases NAD+ levels, increases Histone 3 Lysine 27 acetylation, and eventually inhibits blastocyst formation [83]. These studies suggest that the glucose metabolism-epigenetic modification axis has important regulatory effects on early embryo development.

Glycolysis is the major energy supply pathway in oocyte maturation and early embryonic development. Relevant animal experiments show that the use of enzyme activity inhibitors and RNAi-mediated gene silencing to disrupt the glycolytic pathway markedly diminishes ATP levels. This interference also leads to a decreased rate of first polar body extrusion (PB1E) and negatively impacts blastocyst formation [[84], [85], [86], [87]]. In addition, pyruvate is a key intermediate in glycolysis in germ cells. The activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH), which is responsible for converting pyruvate to Ac-CoA, is controlled by pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK) and pyruvate dehydrogenase phosphatase [40,[88], [89], [90], [91]]. PDK1 and PDK3 affects mouse oocyte maturation by regulating phosphorylation of Ser232-PDHE1α and Ser293-PDHE1α [40]. High expression of PDK in cumulus cells inhibits PDH activity and accumulates pyruvate, whereas high level of PDH in oocytes stimulates the conversion of pyruvate to Ac-CoA and maintains ATP production via TCA cycle and OXPHOS [89]. Furthermore, insulin activation can enhance the activity of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) α/γ, thereby accelerating the translocation of GLUT4 from the cytoplasm to the cell membrane and facilitating its fusion. This process promotes glucose transport [92]. In patients with PCOS, the increased production of PPARγ under hyperandrogenemia and reduced insulin sensitivity hinder the uptake and utilization of glucose by GCs, thereby becoming the primary factors influencing follicle maturation [93,94]. Moreover, the mRNA expression levels of HIF-1α and glycolysis-related genes were significantly lower in GCs from PCOS patients compared to those from non-PCOS patients, suggesting that PCOS leads to impaired glucose metabolism [[95], [96], [97]]. This mostly anecdotal evidence shows that glucose metabolizing-related enzymes, the PDH-PDK axis and GLUTs in governing the glycolytic process within reproductive cells, which significantly affects oocyte maturation and embryo development.

PPP complements glycolysis and affects the nuclear maturation of oocyte and embryonic development

PPP is a series of reactions that glucose-6-phosphate is metabolized by glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PDH) to generate nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) and phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate (PRPP) [29,[98], [99], [100]]. NADPH is essential for maintaining cytoplasmic stability and redox state homeostasis by reducing glutathione (GSH). It plays a crucial role in providing the reducing power necessary for various biosynthetic reactions, detoxification processes, and cellular antioxidant defenses [101,102]. PRPP serves as a substrate for both de novo and salvage synthesis of purine nucleotides. Its contribution to nucleotide formation is vital for cellular function, particularly in supporting the nuclear maturation of cells [103,104]. These products highlight the pivotal function of the PPP in nurturing the delicate equilibrium of cellular processes essential for the development and maturation of oocytes. Research suggests that the crucial enzymes within the PPP enhance fundamental metabolic activities, while also augmenting oocyte quality and increasing the potential for embryonic development. This highlights the essential nature of the PPP in fostering the right conditions for oocyte maturation, emphasizing the need for a delicate metabolic equilibrium for successful reproduction and the potential consequences of metabolic disturbances on fertility [[105], [106], [107], [108]]. Furthermore, glucose metabolism within cumulus cells plays a pivotal role in thwarting oocyte aging by generating pyruvate and NADPH via glycolysis and PPP. Remarkably, while the PPP continued to function following the suppression of glycolysis, the inhibition of the PPP led to the outright cessation of glycolytic activity. The introduction of fructose-6-phosphate (F-6-P), a key intermediary product of the PPP, markedly mitigated the effects of oocyte aging when the PPP was entirely inhibited. This underscores the critical influence of specific metabolic pathways in maintaining oocyte vitality and the potential for targeted interventions to promote oocyte health and longevity [109]. Compared to the PPP inhibitor group, the oocyte maturation rate, blastocyst rate, GSH, and NADPH level were higher in the group treated with glycolysis inhibitor [110,111]. It is noteworthy that PPARδ can specifically activate G6PDH, resulting in increased glucose uptake and utilization through PPP and improved hyperglycemia in clinical POI conditions [112]. Moreover, UHPLC-MS analysis reveals that the estrogen receptors- G6PDH - PPP axis can decrease both glycolysis metabolites and PPP intermediates, resulting in infertility caused by a metabolic imbalance in the endometrial tissue [113]. Collectively, PPP supports nuclear and cytoplasmic maturation of oocytes via reducing NAPDH, inhibiting oxidative stress (OS), and promoting nucleotide formation.

During early embryonic development, PPP is utilized from the late 2-cell stage to the early 8-cell stage for the synthesis of ribose and NADPH [114]. A recent study shows there is an increase of G6PDH before the 8-cell stage [78], suggesting its potential role in promoting embryo development by regulating redox balance. The final enzyme in the oxidative PPP pathway is 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase (6-PGD). After addition of 6-aminonicotinamide, embryos arrest at the morula stage by blocking 6-PGD expression, whereas some embryos could continue to develop to the blastocyst stage following supplementation with uridine [73].

HBP enhances the developmental potential of oocytes and early embryos

HBP can catalyze the conversion of glucose into the final product UDP-N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc), most of which is utilized for extracellular matrix (ECM) synthesis to regulate the expansion of the cumulus cells. A small portion of UDP-GlcNAc is involved in protein O-GlcNAcylation, sensing the extracellular glucose level [29,[115], [116], [117]]. Previous studies indicate that O-GlcNAcylation regulates signal transduction pathways in oocytes and early embryos by modulating protein activity to terminate or sustain signals [[118], [119], [120], [121], [122], [123]]. In addition, inhibiting O-GlcNAcylation induces oocyte maturation defects and decreases embryo development by blocking GLUTs expression and limiting glucose uptake [124]. O-linked N-acetylglucosamine transferase (OGT) and O-linked N-acetylglucosamine hydrolase (OGA) mediated O-GlcNAcylation is present in MII oocytes and all preimplantation embryonic stages. There are previous pieces of evidence showing that inhibition of OGA or OGT influences the expression of Pol II, disrupts ZGA, and inhibits blastocyst formation [119,[125], [126], [127]]. Prior research has demonstrated that the expression of Cdx2, a crucial specifier for the TE lineage, is governed by various transcription factors. Among these are Tfap2c, the Tead4/YAP complex, and the signaling pathways associated with Notch pathway [128,129]. Recent evidence points to the fact that, HBP metabolism facilitating the nuclear localization of the transcriptional regulator YAP1, which is involved in TE cell formation [73]. The combined effects of nucleotides synthesized by the PPP and sphingolipid lead to the activation of mTOR. Tfap2c, a crucial regulator of TE cells formation, is translated. Subsequently, YAP1 and Tfap2c interact to form a complex, activate Cdx2, and ultimately promote differentiation of TE. Thus, HBP and PPP are jointly involved in oocyte maturation, lineage choice and differentiation in embryos.

PP is a backup pathway for COCs under high glucose

PP is a glycolytic branch that mainly converts glucose to sorbitol and NAD+ to NADH under the catalytic action of aldose reductase (AR) and sorbitol dehydrogenase [29,[130], [131], [132]]. Under normal physiological conditions, the affinity of AR for glucose is low. Thus, PP is used as a compensatory pathway. However, hyperglycemia leads to the accumulation of hexokinase, activates AR, and enhances glucose metabolism via PP. Compared with the control, Sorbitol inhibited FSH-induced meiosis and reduced the incidence of germinal vesicle breakdown in a dose-dependent manner during IVM, thereby preventing oocyte maturation. Furthermore, the addition of sorbitol during IVC also significantly reduced blastocyst formation and increased embryo apoptosis [133,134]. A recent study shows that compared to the aged group, treatment with sorbinil, a specific inhibitor of AR, improves PB1E rate of aged oocytes by decreasing sorbitol and OS [135,136]. Similarly, clinical studies have suggested that hyperandrogenism of PCOS can enhance AR expression and cause ovarian dysfunction [137], while treatment with the new AR inhibitor fidarestat has been shown to improve sorbitol levels in patients' cells [136]. This indicates that high glucose levels activate the PP pathway, leading to increased sorbitol concentrations, which ultimately impacts oocyte and embryo quality.

Glucose metabolism regulates the fate of embryonic stem cells

ESCs are derived from early embryos or primordial gonads, and are self-renewing and developmentally multipotent, capable of differentiating to form multiple cell types [[138], [139], [140]]. Glucose metabolism plays a pivotal role in controlling stem cell destiny, including reprogramming and differentiation, through its influence on epigenetic alterations and gene expression [141,142]. Similar to cancer cells, ESCs utilize glucose as the primary energy source in the undifferentiated state. During rapid proliferation, ESCs exhibit high glycolytic flux to support high energy and biosynthetic demands [143,144]. Interestingly, these metabolic features are also recaptured during the remodeling of somatic cell nuclei into induced pluripotent stem cells prior to the induction of pluripotent gene expression [143,145]. Inhibiting the bFGF factor, which drives the differentiation of ESCs, leads to 44 distinct changes in metabolites, with the most prominent being a significant decrease in glycolytic efficiency [146]. More importantly, the regulation of glycolysis controls the accumulation of Ac-CoA, ensuring SMAD3 acetylation and its nuclear localization, which in turn facilitates the differentiation of human and mouse ESCs into endoderm [147]. In addition, the external supplementation of pyruvate in human ESCs alters their metabolic equilibrium, tipping the balance in favor of OXPHOS. This metabolic shift not only generates an abundance of ATP, essential for cell growth and development, but also raises the AMP/ATP ratio, which in turn activates the AMPK and mTOR signaling pathways, guiding human ESCs towards differentiation into mesodermal lineages [148]. Importantly, supplementing with exogenous lactate or boosting intracellular lactate levels enhances the differentiation of extraembryonic endoderm cells [149]. Additionally, the functioning of enolase 1 (ENO1) within the glycolytic pathway, which is under the direct influence of RNA-binding proteins, can steer metabolic reprogramming in mouse ESCs. Mouse ESCs with normally regulated ENO1 expression undergo standard differentiation into various germ layers. Mouse ESCs overexpressing ENO1 face significant hurdles in differentiating into endodermal and neural ectodermal lineages, whereas those with decreased ENO1 expression demonstrate atypical advancements in differentiation towards the endoderm. These findings underscore the critical role of RNA regulation by ENO1 in the differentiation processes of ESCs [150]. The aforementioned data indicate that exploring the interplay between glucose metabolism and ESC fate may be crucial for revealing the mechanisms underlying human embryo development.

O-GlcNAcylation is closely associated with the maintenance of pluripotency in ESCs. Several studies have indicated that overall down-regulation of O-GlcNAcylation levels promotes early differentiation of ESCs, whereas increasing the level of O-GlcNAcylation contributes to the maintenance of cell pluripotency [[151], [152], [153], [154]]. Considering the pivotal role that protein factors play in the orchestration of cellular processes, researchers have initiated studies into the O-GlcNAcylation-modified proteins in ESCs. They discovered that O-GlcNAcylation modulates the activity of key transcription factors pivotal for maintaining pluripotency in ESCs, such as Oct4, Sox2, and Nanog. These findings suggest that O-GlcNAcylation are likely to have substantial impacts on the intricate network of transcriptional regulation associated with ESCs pluripotency [152,155,156]. Consequently, delving into the interplay between glucose metabolism and the destiny of ESCs may unveil how abnormal nutritional conditions induce dysregulation in stem cell differentiation.

Metabolic disorders influence ART outcomes

Insulin resistance, marked by diminished sensitivity to insulin and impaired cellular glucose uptake and utilization, disrupts glucose metabolism and negatively impacts oocyte maturation and embryo development. Similarly, insulin deficiency reduces glucose availability to reproductive cells, which hinders energy production and decreases fertility. It is well documented that insulin-related metabolic disorders are associated with both PCOS and POI [157]. PCOS is characterized by disordered follicle development including the accumulation of small preantral follicles, and patients often show a disorder of glycolysis and pyruvate metabolism in follicular fluids and a heightened risk for diabetes [158]. Comparing the clinical outcomes of in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer (IVF-ET) between PCOS and non-PCOS patients reveals that the reduced fertilization capacity of PCOS oocytes is due to lower HIF-1a expression, which impairs GC glycolysis [95]. Moreover, the absence of SIRT3 in PCOS GCs leads to increased mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, and reduced G6PDH activity, ultimately disrupting the insulin signaling pathway [19]. POI is a disease in women characterized by the loss or dysfunction of ovarian follicles, which results in amenorrhea before the age of 40 and a subsequent decline in fertility [159]. Compared to age-matched healthy controls, women with POI exhibit a metabolic profile with higher blood glucose levels and insufficient insulin production [96,97]. A recent study by Ruan et al. [160] demonstrated that the HIF1α/CX43 pathway can improve the proliferation of GCs and promote glycolysis, which supports follicle development and improves ovarian function.

It is important to recognize that abnormal blood sugar levels are frequently linked to irregular blood lipid profiles, such as those seen in fatty liver disease and obesity. The accumulation of visceral fat can impair liver function, hindering the efficient conversion of blood glucose into liver glycogen, which exacerbates insulin resistance and disrupts glucose metabolism [161]. Furthermore, excessive adipose tissue can lead to hormonal imbalances, disrupting the hypothalamus-pituitary-gonadal axis and causing irregular gonadotropin secretion, which may result in anovulation and menstrual irregularities [162]. Obesity is a prevalent chronic condition that can diminish the expression of glucose metabolism markers, including insulin-degrading enzyme, retinol-binding protein 4, and GLUT4, mediated by PPARγ, which ultimately impairs ovarian quality [163,164]. Moreover, clinical data reveal that obese women have a lower live birth rate following ART compared to women of normal weight [165].

Nowadays, ART is becoming a more accessible means for individuals and couples to achieve pregnancy, even in cases where natural conception might be challenging, such as with infertility. However, conventional ART necessitates ovarian stimulation to augment oocyte count, potentially causing ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome and exacerbating the health and economic strains on patients [166]. Furthermore, the success rate of ART is lower for patients with PCOS, POI and obesity due to challenges in producing mature oocytes when undergoing direct IVF [[167], [168], [169], [170], [171]]. Consequently, this method is not recommended for infertility patients with follicular development delay. IVM, by facilitating the maturation of a limited number of retrieved immature oocytes, thereby enabling fertilization, embryo transfer, and clinical pregnancy, presents a viable strategy for preserving female fertility [172]. Unfortunately, IVM does not always ensure the oocyte maturation process including both nuclear and cytoplasmic maturation. This shortfall results in IVM oocytes of inferior quality and developmental potential compared to those matured in vivo [173]. There is evidence indicating a decrease in β-oxidation, an increase in glycolysis, and a significantly lower ATP/ADP ratio in IVM oocytes during IVF compared to oocytes that mature in vivo [[174], [175], [176]]. The most commonly employed methods for screening reproductive cells in clinical practice are morphological assessment and biopsy [177]. Compared to traditional morphological observation, the time-lapse system (TLS) integrated with AI technology provides a more comprehensive analysis, including morphological grading, blastocyst formation assessment, aneuploidy prediction, and live birth outcome prediction for oocytes and early embryos. This approach minimizes the influence of human error and improves the objectivity of oocyte and embryo selection [178]. Illingworth et al. [179] have developed a deep learning scoring system based on the kinetic parameters of embryo development, which exhibits high efficiency in embryo assessment but has yet to demonstrate significant improvements in clinical pregnancy rates. Moreover, a prospective, double-blind, randomized controlled trial demonstrated no statistically significant difference in pregnancy rates between embryos selected using the KID Score D5 algorithm and those selected by embryologists [180]. Although integrating AI technology with TLS has lessened the subjectivity in evaluating germ cell quality, the process remains dependent on morphological observations and predefined algorithms, posing challenges in obtaining precise information about germ cell quality. The biopsy techniques, including invasive preimplantation genetic testing (PGT), utilize genetic DNA analysis of germ cells to accurately detect severe genetic disorders or chromosomal abnormalities, and to assess the quality of germ cells [181]. In PGT, the biopsy targets typically include the polar bodies released by oocytes and the TE of the blastocyst [182,183]. Several lines of investigation have revealed that polar body biopsy has limited value because it does not provide genetic information from the father and may negatively affect cell fate specification and post-implantation development [182,184]. Moreover, TE biopsy, which focuses exclusively on TE cells that will form the placenta rather than on ICM cells, is further challenged by the high rate of chromosomal mosaicism in blastocysts—ranging from 30 % to 40%—potentially resulting in inaccurate positive or negative outcomes in PGT [185]. What's more, biopsy procedures can be traumatic, potentially increasing the risk of fragmentation and delaying the development of the retrieved oocytes and embryos [186]. Recently, a novel RNA-based genetic testing method has been developed for pre-implantation embryo screening, enabling accurate diagnosis of pathogenic mutations and assessment of embryo implantation potential, though it still presents challenges related to cellular damage and injury [187]. Despite the widespread use of biopsies in reproductive centers, their potential risks underscore the importance of developing more advanced non-invasive monitoring techniques. Consequently, various promising technologies are being explored to tackle these issues, including the analysis of glucose, lactate, pyruvate, and amino acid metabolism, proteomic profiling, oxygen consumption rate assessments, and recent advancements in metabolomic studies [[188], [189], [190]]. Based on prior research and data, we recommend the following approaches to support the maturation of oocyte nuclear and cytoplasmic. First, we suggest enhancing IVC conditions by adding an optimal concentration of glucose to provide sufficient energy for oocyte maturation. Additionally, the addition of PPP inhibitors may be beneficial in decreasing PRPP levels and delaying nuclear maturation. This approach will aid in synchronizing the maturation of the nucleus and cytoplasm, thereby improving the quality and developmental potential of oocytes and embryos. Therefore, fine-tuning glucose metabolism within the IVC conditions to elevate clinical IVF pregnancy outcomes. Secondly, the use of integrated metabolomics technology allows for the evaluation of oocytes and embryos with high developmental potential in IVF-ET procedures, which is essential for reducing transplantation failure rates and improving pregnancy outcomes. Previous research showed that nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy was used to assess glucose levels in the follicular fluid of PCOS patients, revealing a positive correlation between low glucose uptake by germ cells and the rate of embryonic development [191]. Recently, Qiao and colleagues successfully identified elevated levels of pyruvate, succinate, oxaloacetate, malate, and cis-aconitic acid in the follicular fluid of PCOS patients, along with decreased levels of lactate and cis-aconitate. The increased succinate levels were associated with reduced mitochondrial biogenesis and heightened inflammation in the germ cells, ultimately impairing the growth and development of the oocytes [192]. Thus, precise monitoring of glucose metabolites combined with AI-driven big data algorithms may facilitate the screening of high-quality gametes and embryos, thereby enhancing ART success rates.

Conclusion

This article demonstrates how glucose, through its uptake and metabolism, influences the nuclear and cytoplasmatic maturation of oocytes, the development and cell lineage differentiation of preimplantation embryos, and the proliferation and differentiation of ESCs. Glycolysis supplies essential energy for the maturation of the oocyte nucleus and cytoplasm and for blastocyst formation. PPP is crucial for glucose metabolism in oocytes and early embryos, generating intermediates like NADPH, F-6-P, and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate that enter glycolysis to produce ATP and pyruvate, thereby enhancing mitochondrial activity, reducing ROS, and promoting cytoplasmic maturation, while PRPP supports nucleotide synthesis and regulates nuclear maturation stages. HBP regulates corona expansion, ECM formation, and protein O-linked glycosylation. Various glucose metabolism pathways are interconnected: HBP controls YAP1 nuclear localization, PPP and S1P activate the mTOR pathway and Tfap2c translation, leading to the formation of a complex with YAP1, TEAD4, and TFAP2C that directs trophoblast cell lineage differentiation in the blastocyst. However, excessive glucose can trigger side pathways that increase sorbitol levels and ROS, impairing oocyte and early embryo development.

The molecular metabolic regulation of glucose on oocyte maturation, embryonic development and proliferation and differentiation of ESCs is a hot research topic, but the limitations of sample quantity and assay technology have seriously hindered the research in the field of developmental biology. With the development of micro-ultrasensitive metabolomics technology, this limitation will be effectively resolved. This will not only optimize the culture conditions of oocytes and embryos and promote the developmental potential of oocytes and early embryos, but also help to improve our understanding of the fate of stem cells for further analysis of diseases of reproductive origins caused by abnormal nutritional environments. Despite ongoing advancements in ART, the treatment of metabolism-related fertility disorders related to impaired oocyte maturation continues to face low success rates. Furthermore, the assessment of oocyte maturity and preimplantation embryo quality in IVF predominantly relies on morphological indicators. Although the incorporation of technologies such as AI has lowered the level of subjectivity in embryologist assessments [[193], [194], [195], [196]], these evaluations remain morphologically based and do not provide a precise reflection of quality. Future directions in reproductive medicine are poised to integrate multi-omics approaches, including metabolomics, genomics, transcriptomics, and epigenomics, to perform comprehensive analyses of metabolites, gene integrity, transcription levels, and DNA methylation patterns. This collaboration will progressively uncover the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying metabolic reprogramming. As detection technologies evolve, the identification of novel critical metabolites, such as glucose, lipids, and amino acids, will be enhanced. These advancements will contribute to better assessment and enhancement of gamete and embryo quality, particularly benefiting patients with conditions like PCOS, POI and obesity, ultimately increasing the pregnancy success rates of ART. This progress will not only help families affected by infertility realize their dreams of parenthood but will also support the high-quality and healthy growth of the global population.
