Experiences of extreme weather and mental health in climate-vulnerable communities: results from a large-scale survey of women living in informal settlements in Nairobi, Kenya
Authors: Haley E. Brown, Anna K. Balakrishnan, Kianna M. Stamps, Lena M Obara, Susan S. Witte, Samantha C. Winter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Climate change is a major global health threat with significant impacts on mental health, especially among vulnerable populations. Residents of informal settlements–characterized by inadequate infrastructure and housing–are particularly at risk due to their exposure to extreme weather and limited access to healthcare. This study investigates the association between experiences of extreme weather and mental health among women residing in two large informal settlements in Nairobi, Kenya. Using cross-sectional survey data from 800 women, we found significant associations between anxiety (GAD-7) and depression (PHQ-9) and women’s experiences of colder-than-usual temperatures (Anxiety β = 0.75, p = 0.03; Depression β = 0.82, p = 0.03) and droughts (Anxiety β = 3.22, p = 0.001; Depression β = 2.90, p = 0.01). This study demonstrates how self-reported experiences of extreme weather are associated with mental health challenges among marginalized populations, and highlights the value of experiential data in identifying climate-related vulnerabilities.

Background

Climate change is the greatest global health threat of our time, with profound direct and indirect effects on mental health [1]. Extreme weather events, defined by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) as “events that are rare at a particular place and time of year” [2], have been linked to anxiety, depression, and trauma [3, 4, 5]. Climate change has been associated with higher rates of anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), substance use, and interpersonal violence [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Rising temperatures exacerbate mental health conditions, increasing hospitalizations and suicide risk [9, 10]. In one systematic review, for every 1° Celsius increase in temperature, mortality due to mental health disorders rose by 2.2%, while morbidity increased by 0.9% [9]. Traumatic experiences due to extreme weather further elevate the risk of affective and anxiety disorders, particularly PTSD [11].

Globally, approximately one billion people live with mental health disorders, 80% of whom reside in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) such as Kenya [12, 13, 14]. In LMICs, fewer than 20% of individuals seeking mental health care receive adequate services [13]. Without strengthened health systems, the compounded effects of climate change will intensify mental health crises, particularly among those most impacted and least equipped to adapt [14].

Vulnerable groups—especially those in LMICs—face disproportionate risks due to pre-existing structural and environmental inequalities [12, 15, 16]. Among vulnerable groups, women and girls are particularly at risk. Their reliance on natural resources and prominent roles in agricultural labor make them especially susceptible to environmental degradation and extreme weather [17, 18]. Exposure to climate stressors also heightens the risk of intimate partner violence (IPV) against women and girls, further compounding mental health challenges [18].

These dynamics are especially pronounced in informal settlements, where gender inequalities and socio-cultural marginalization intensify the burden of anxiety and depression among women compared to the general population of Kenya [19]. Women in these communities also experience greater health-related impacts of climate change while facing significant barriers to accessing health services [18].

Residents of informal settlements–unauthorized housing areas often lacking basic infrastructure–are among the most climate-vulnerable populations. These settlements are frequently located in ecologically sensitive areas and face elevated morbidity and mortality due to extreme weather and limited access to essential services [20, 21]. Informal settlements are often characterized as having inadequate water and sanitation, overcrowded and precarious housing, and insecure tenure—all of which heighten exposure to climate-related risks [22, 23].

In Kenya, the government describes settlements as areas “plagued by increasing unemployment rates, residential overcrowding, and deterioration of already overstretched infrastructure and services, environmental degradation, and acute housing shortages.” [24] The term “informal settlements” is used intentionally in place of terms such as “slums” or “squatter settlements” to avoid the pejorative connotations and recognize the historical and structural roots that shaped these communities. Many of Kenya’s informal settlements originated under British colonial rule, when colonial urban planning policies deliberately excluded native Kenyans from formal housing and land ownership, displacing them to peripheral zones to consolidate colonial control over land, labor, and resources [21, 25, 26, 27]. Following independence in 1963, rapid rural-to-urban migration and a persistent lack of affordable housing further accelerated the growth of informal settlements [28, 29, 30].

Nairobi exemplifies the intersection of these vulnerabilities. Approximately 70% of disaster events in Kenya are attributed to extreme weather [31]. Informal settlements in Nairobi, such as Kibera and Mathare, are concentrated in floodplains, valleys, and other climate-vulnerable areas, with limited access to water, sanitation, and electricity. These settlements house over half of Nairobi’s population and are marked by extreme poverty, exploitative living arrangements, and frequent evictions [21, 22, 25, 26, 28]. Women in these communities face heightened risks due to gender-based inequalities, social marginalization, and limited access to health services [18].

While research on climate change and mental health is growing, little is known about its specific impacts on women living in informal settlements—a population uniquely exposed to overlapping environmental, social, and structural stressors. Research also suggests that weather events that may not meet meteorological or population-based thresholds for classification as “extreme” can still be experienced as extreme by members of climate-vulnerable communities [32]. It is therefore important to examine how these subjective experiences relate to health and well-being. This study addresses that gap using cross-sectional data to assess the relationship between experiences of extreme weather and mental health. Unlike most extreme weather studies that rely on meteorological indicators, this study draws on women’s self-reported experiences, collected through a community-based participatory approach, to examine associations between experiences of colder-than-usual temperatures, hotter-than-usual temperatures, and drought, and symptoms of depression and anxiety among 800 women living in two informal settlements in Nairobi.

Methods

This study employed a cross-sectional design to assess experiences of extreme weather and self-reported mental health outcomes among residents of two informal settlements in Nairobi, Kenya. Data were collected between September and October 2022, during the baseline period of a longitudinal, quantitative study examining the direct and mediating effects of climate, mental health, and violence in informal settlements in Kenya. Monthly household-level surveys were administered to women residing in informal settlements in Nairobi. The results presented in this paper are based on the baseline survey.

Study site

This study was conducted in Mathare and Kibera, two of the largest and most densely populated informal settlements in Nairobi, Kenya. Population estimates range from 200,000 to 400,000 in Kibera and 80,000 to 250,000 in Mathare, which together comprise 27 villages across both settlements (Kibera has 14 villages [33] and Mathare has 13 [34]). These settlements are characterized by extremely high density, with housing constructed in close proximity using materials such as galvanized iron sheets, mud, wood, and concrete. Narrow, unpaved paths with little vegetation run between structures, contributing to high ambient temperatures [35]. Both settlements are located in low-lying areas along major rivers and are highly susceptible to seasonal flooding, mudslides, and other climate-related hazards during periods of heavy rainfall [36, 37].

Livelihoods in Mathare and Kibera are predominantly informal, with many residents earning income through street vending, casual labor, or small-scale services such as laundry or domestic work [23, 34]. Access to essential services, including water, sanitation, and electricity, is inconsistent; most residents rely on shared water points and communal toilets located outside the home [38, 39]. While Mathare and Kibera face common structural challenges, their microclimates differ due to variations in elevation, vegetation cover, and the built environment. As a result, extreme weather may affect one settlement while leaving the other relatively unaffected [35].

Study sample

The research team used a probability sampling method previously applied by the authors in prior work [18]. In ArcGISPro version 3.0, a fishnet grid was overlaid within the previously defined boundaries of the Kibera and Mathare informal settlements (shapefiles available on OpenStreetMaps). Each grid cell of the fishnet measured 9 square meters (3 m × 3 m)—about the size of a typical mud or tin house or a room in a high-rise. A random selection function was used to identify 400 grid cells, representing 400 houses, within each informal settlement boundary. The GPS coordinates of 50 randomly selected grid cells were loaded onto each of the 16 community data collectors’ (CDCs) tablets. Using Google Earth and Google Maps, CDCs navigated to the nearest household to each GPS point.

In each household, the quasi-random “last birthday” method [40] was used to identify one woman to interview. To be eligible, participants had to be at least 18, fluent in English or Swahili, and a resident of the settlement for at least six months (i.e., not a visitor or temporary resident). Swahili was the most commonly spoken language across both communities. Using this approach, the team approached 863 households, with 63 women declining to participate (43 were from Kibera and 20 from Mathare), resulting in a final sample of 800 women, 400 from Kibera and 400 from Mathare.

Data collection & sampling method

Using a Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR) approach, local women in Kibera and Mathare were hired to lead data collection. The research was led by 16 CDCs, all women residents of Mathare and Kibera. These CDCs, trained in qualitative and quantitative research methods, collected the data and brought essential local expertise to each study phase. Over 18 months, the CDCs worked full-time conducting household-level surveys, with eight CDCs working in each settlement. All data were self-reported via a structured survey administered using Qualtrics software on tablets carried by the CDCs. CDCs received training in the use of Qualtrics and were supported throughout data collection to ensure consistency.

CDCs were also trained in ethical research principles and study protocols, including the World Health Organization’s (WHO) ethical and safety standards for research on sensitive issues like violence against women [41]. To ensure participant safety, protocols were established to address any reports of violence or mental health concerns, including referral to an on-call counselor provided by the study and/or to other services available in the community. All participants provided written informed consent after CDCs thoroughly explained the study, addressing any questions.

Measures

Experiences of extreme weather

All surveys asked participants whether they or any household members had experienced any extreme weather events in the past month, defined as events that were uncommon/more extreme than usual for that time of year in the settlement. If they responded “yes,” participants were then asked to indicate, from a checklist, each type of extreme weather event they had experienced — including extreme heat, cold, floods, heavy rainfall, drought (defined as a prolonged period of significantly reduced precipitation causing water shortages), or other (specify) — along with the date(s) on which each event occurred. Participants also had the option to report additional events not listed by selecting “other” and specifying the type and dates of event(s). This self-report measure, previously used by the research team [32], was developed to address the limitations of meteorological thresholds in capturing localized, context-specific impacts of extreme weather, particularly in climate-vulnerable communities.

Mental health (PHQ-9 & GAD-7)

Mental health measures included the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7). The PHQ-9 is a self-report questionnaire used to screen for symptoms and severity of depression. It consists of nine items rated on a four-point Likert scale, where participants indicate how often–ranging from 0 = “not at all” to 3 = “nearly every day”–they have been bothered by each symptom of depression over the past two weeks. Total scores range from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating greater severity of depressive symptoms [42]. Similar to the PHQ-9, the GAD-7 is a self-report scale used to screen for and assess symptoms and severity of anxiety [43]. Scores on this seven-item scale range from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater severity of anxiety symptoms. Both scales have been validated in this context [44, 45, 46, 47, 48].

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on socio-environmental factors previously shown to be significant predictors associated with depression among women in informal settlements in Kenya [18]. Education, marital status, and settlement location were coded as binary variables indicating whether participants had completed primary school, were currently married, and lived in Mathare or Kibera, respectively. Alcohol use was measured as a binary variable based on whether participants had consumed alcohol in the past month.

Self-reported health was assessed by asking participants to rate their health on a five-point scale with options including excellent [1], very good [2], good [3], fair [4], and poor [5]. Due to a small number of respondents selecting “excellent” (n = 29) this category was combined with the category of “very good” to create a composite category (“excellent/very good”) for analysis.

Work frequency was derived from a multi-category variable indicating how often participants engaged in income-generating activities. It was collapsed into three categories: 0 = never worked, 1 = worked occasionally (once in a while to a few days per week), and 2 = worked frequently (nearly every day or every day). Partner employment was a binary variable based on whether the participant’s husband or partner worked in the past month.

Intimate partner violence (IPV) was measured using a modified version of the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) domestic violence module. This included sub-scales for psychological (3 items), physical (8 items), and sexual IPV (3 items). A binary indicator was created to capture experiences of any type of violence in the last year.

Analysis strategy

The primary aim of this study was to examine cross-sectional associations between women’s experiences of extreme weather and their self-reported symptoms of depression and anxiety. The analysis began with descriptive analyses, including frequency distributions, followed by bivariate analyses to assess the relationship between each covariate and the two mental health outcomes. Multivariate linear regression models were then used to assess associations between women’s experiences of extreme weather and their depression and anxiety symptoms, while adjusting for relevant covariates.

Covariates were selected based on theoretical relevance and prior research identifying key predictors of depression and anxiety among women living in informal settlements in Kenya [18]. Bivariate analyses were conducted to assess associations between each potential covariate and outcome. Variables significantly associated at p < 0.05 and theoretically relevant variables were retained in the multivariate models.

Six separate adjusted multivariate linear regression models were run using R version 2023.06.1 + 524. Each model assessed the association between one of three types of extreme weather (cold, heat, and drought) and each mental health outcome: depression (measured by PHQ-9) and anxiety (measured by GAD-7). Missing data were < 5% for all variables. Single hot-deck imputation was performed using the user-written R package simputation [44] to impute missing values.

Results

Demographics

Descriptive statistics for the sample are presented in Table 1. Participants had an average anxiety score of 5.87 (SD = 5.12) and an average depression score of 7.10 (SD = 5.75). Reported experiences of extreme weather in the past month included colder-than-usual temperatures (67.4%), hotter-than-usual temperatures (8.6%), and drought events (2.6%).

The mean age was 35.4 years (SD = 11.69). Most participants (75%) had completed at least primary school, and 55% were married. Alcohol use in the past month was reported by 2.75% of the sample. Self-reported health ratings were as follows: 19.4% very good or excellent, 34.9% good, 36.3% fair, and 9.5% poor. Female-headed households made up 50.5% of the sample. Participants had an average of 2.72 children (SD = 1.72). Work frequency varied, with 46.5% working nearly every day, 30.9% working sometimes, and 22.6% not working. Just over 53% of participants’ partners worked in the past month. Intimate partner violence (IPV) in the past year was reported by 48.3% of participants. The sample was evenly divided between Kibera and Mathare, with 50% of participants residing in each settlement.

Bivariate tests

Results from bivariate tests are presented in Table 2. Age was positively associated with symptoms of anxiety and depression [β = 0.09, p < 0.001]. Self-reported health was strongly associated with symptoms of anxiety [F(3, 796) = 57.29, p < 0.001] and depression [F(3, 796) = 52.70, p < 0.001], with statistically significant differences across health categories. Having completed at least primary school was significantly associated with symptoms of anxiety [t(293) = 5.20, p < 0.001] and depression [t(293) = 3.42, p < 0.001]. Marital status was also significantly associated with symptoms of both anxiety [t(658) = 4.01, p < 0.001] and depression [t(684) = 3.09, p < 0.01 ]. Alcohol use was not significantly associated with either outcome.

The number of children was positively associated with symptoms of anxiety [β = 0.63, p < 0.001] and depression [β = 0.64, p < 0.001]. Being in a female-headed household was significantly associated with both outcomes [anxiety: t(758) = -4.04, p < 0.001 and depression: t(774) = -3.27, p < 0.01]. Intimate partner violence was significantly associated with symptoms of anxiety [t(748) = -2.42, p < 0.05] and depression [t(759) = -2.17, p < 0.05]. Having a partner who was employed was significantly associated with both outcomes [anxiety: t(678) = 5.32, p < 0.001) and depression: t(730) = 3.55, p < 0.001]. Participant work frequency was associated with symptoms of anxiety [F(2, 797) = 3.22, p < 0.05], but not significantly associated with depression.

Multivariate linear regressions

Results from multivariate linear regressions, adjusting for self-reported health, age, gender of the head of household, number of children, intimate partner violence, education, partner employment, marital status, and settlement location, are presented in Tables 3 and 4. Experiencing colder-than-usual temperatures was associated with significantly higher levels of anxiety and depression. Specifically, colder temperatures were associated with a 0.78-point increase in anxiety (p < 0.05) and a 0.84-point increase in depression (p < 0.05), controlling for covariates. Drought events showed the strongest associations with mental health: exposure to drought was associated with a 3.17-point increase in anxiety (p < 0.01) and a 2.81-point increase in depression (p < 0.05).

Although hotter-than-usual temperatures were reported less frequently during the study period (September is the cool and dry season in Kenya), results suggested a potential association with mental health outcomes. Exposure to heat was associated with a 1.08-point increase in anxiety and a 1.18-point increase in depression; however, these findings were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Results also indicate that worse self-reported health, having more children, and past-year IPV were significantly associated with higher anxiety and depression scores. Completing at least primary school and having a partner who works were significantly associated with lower anxiety scores; however, these associations were not observed for depression. Other covariates, including age, marital status, and settlement location, were not significantly associated with mental health outcomes in adjusted models.

Discussion

This study examined the associations between women’s experiences of extreme weather and their levels of anxiety and depression symptoms in a probability-based sample of women in two large informal settlements in Nairobi. The findings demonstrate that women experiencing extreme weather, specifically colder-than-usual temperatures and drought events, reported significantly higher levels of anxiety and depression. Results suggest that deviations from temperature patterns can have a measurable impact on mental health, underscoring the heightened vulnerability of this population to seasonal weather variability driven by climate change. The psychological impacts associated with experiences of drought were the most severe, emphasizing the potential toll of prolonged water scarcity, which exacerbates existing stressors such as food insecurity, economic strain, and social conflict [14, 49]. While heat events did not yield statistically significant associations with mental health outcomes in this study, the near-significant association suggests a trend worth exploring in future research, particularly during hotter seasons when such events may be more frequent or intense. These results contribute to a growing body of evidence documenting the potential adverse mental health outcomes associated with climate change in vulnerable populations [5, 7, 8, 50, 51].

Globally, extreme weather and changing temperatures exacerbate existing stressors, compounding their harmful impacts on mental health, particularly for disadvantaged groups [4, 52]. In Nairobi’s informal settlements, the prevalence of depressive disorders, suicide attempts, and psychological distress exceeds national averages, with women disproportionately affected due to intersecting vulnerabilities, including social marginalization and gender inequities [17, 18]. These mental health disparities are compounded by multifaceted barriers to accessing care, ranging from limited service availability to stigma and financial constraints, which leave many women without essential resources to cope with the cumulative effects of climate change [18, 51, 53, 54]. Our findings show that there are important associations between experiencing extreme weather, such as extreme cold and drought, and the severity of women’s anxiety and depression in informal settlements. Previous research also suggests that disruptions from extreme weather extend across socio-ecological systems in informal settlements [55]. These findings affirm that marginalized populations, particularly women in informal settlements, likely bear a disproportionate burden of climate-related stressors [11, 17, 18].

Building on this, the present study underscores the critical need to define extreme weather based on its real-world impacts, particularly for climate-vulnerable communities where current meteorological thresholds fail to capture the full extent of harm. This study intentionally prioritized participants’ experiences of extreme weather rather than relying on meteorological definitions. This approach, previously evaluated by the research team [32], was developed to help address the limitations of meteorological thresholds in effectively capturing the localized, context-specific impacts of extreme weather, particularly in climate-vulnerable communities.

Standard definitions of extreme weather, which rely on coarse meteorological thresholds, often fail to encompass the full extent of impacts experienced by populations residing in informal settlements [32]. This is especially pertinent in settings with fine-scale spatial variation, such as Nairobi’s informal settlements, where structural and environmental conditions (e.g., limited vegetation, iron sheet housing without insulation, outdated building codes, and topographic variation) can significantly intensify exposure to extreme weather. Furthermore, while temperature extremes are more challenging to pinpoint through self-report—for example, identifying the exact date(s) of a heatwave versus a discrete event like a landslide—experiential data provide critical insight into how different populations perceive, internalize, and respond to extreme weather. Although self-reported data are subject to potential recall bias, particularly for diffuse exposures like heat and cold, the associations found between women’s experiences of extreme weather and their mental health in this study illustrate the value of such methods in contexts where conventional monitoring fails to reflect lived experience. These methods can surface key vulnerabilities that meteorological thresholds may overlook, offering a more nuanced understanding of climate-related hazards. Further research should explore how integrating both meteorological and experiential data could enhance early warning systems and intervention strategies, particularly in contexts characterized by high socio-environmental vulnerability.

While the association between extreme weather and mental health was evident, additional social determinants such as IPV, household composition, and gender dynamics were also significantly associated with mental health. For example, both women in female-headed households and women with children experienced higher levels of anxiety and depression, reflecting possible stressors associated with caregiving responsibilities, economic strain, or limited social support [3, 10, 56]. Further, older age was associated with higher levels of depression and anxiety at the bivariate level, indicating that older women may face unique and compounding vulnerabilities such as declining physical health and reduced economic opportunities, which can exacerbate mental health challenges [3, 51]. These findings suggest that targeted, intersectional interventions are important to address the diverse needs of women in these communities.

Physical health and mental health are inextricably linked [57]. Poor self-reported health was associated with elevated anxiety and depression, mirroring findings from other populations in Kenya [58]. However, even when adjusting for this variable in the multivariate models, the relationship between extreme weather and mental health outcomes remained significant, indicating the independent impact of experiencing extreme weather. This highlights the interconnectedness of physical and mental health and the critical role of improving overall well-being in mitigating mental health challenges. Future research should examine interaction effects between predictors like self-reported health and extreme weather to investigate potential compounding effects.

Beyond physical health, IPV emerged as a critical risk factor for adverse mental health outcomes, particularly in the context of climate change. Droughts, for instance, exacerbate women’s labor burdens, such as water collection, and have been linked to heightened risks of IPV, child marriage, and other forms of gender-based violence during periods of environmental and economic crisis [59]. The strong association between IPV and increased anxiety and depression reinforces the urgent need for integrated mental health and social support services that address the intersection of environmental and gendered stressors.

Social marginalization, precarious living conditions, and limited health services in informal settlements may have a compounding effect on residents’ ability to cope with climate impacts. In low-and middle-income countries, such as Kenya, economic disparities widen gaps in healthcare access, making climate-related mental health challenges particularly acute for impoverished populations [60]. Even in high-income countries, low-income communities disproportionately bear the mental health burdens of extreme heat, cold, and other climate hazards [10]. The vulnerabilities in informal settlements represent an intensified version of global disparities, underscoring the urgent need for targeted intervention.

The link between experiences of extreme weather and mental health highlights the need for integrated policies prioritizing impact-based initiatives rooted in community experience. Policies grounded in local knowledge and lived experiences are more likely to effectively address the unique challenges faced by women in informal settlements. Our findings point to an urgent need to embed mental health considerations within broader climate adaptation strategies. Kenya’s National Adaptation Plan currently lacks any mention of mental health [61], and should be expanded to include initiatives, strategies, and goals to address the mental health challenges created or exacerbated by climate change. Strengthening community support systems, increasing financial investment, and integrating mental health into climate action plans can enhance resilience and equip communities with the tools to better manage the psychological impacts of oncoming environmental shifts.

Limitations

The study used cross-sectional data, which limits the ability to infer causality. While we found associations between experiences of extreme weather and mental health outcomes, longitudinal data would provide stronger evidence of cause-and-effect relationships. Given the observational nature of this study and its implementation in an uncontrolled environment, it is likely that not all potential confounders or covariates have been accounted for; however, the analysis provides a meaningful starting point for understanding these associations by adjusting for theoretically and empirically relevant variables. Data collection also occurred during a specific time frame (September 2022), which did not capture the full range of extreme weather experienced in Nairobi’s informal settlements. For example, events like heavy downpours and flooding are more common in other months, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings across all seasons.

While informal settlements in Nairobi share similar resource constraints, localized climate conditions vary between these settlements. For example, extreme weather events such as flash floods, landslides, and heatwaves can occur in one informal settlement and not another. Elevation and vegetation variations between and within informal settlements significantly affect localized climate (e.g., temperature) and related outcomes [35]. Additionally, reliance on self-reported exposure introduces potential recall bias, particularly for diffuse events such as heat or cold. Thus, the sample is specific to women living in two informal settlements in Nairobi, Kenya. While these findings provide valuable insights, they may not be generalizable to populations in different socio-cultural or geographic contexts.

Conclusion

This research demonstrates that experiences of extreme weather are associated with the mental health of women living in informal settlements, emphasizing a pressing opportunity for targeted intervention. Defining extreme weather based on the experiences and impacts of climate-vulnerable populations is essential for developing effective adaptation strategies, as it reveals the localized and context-specific nature of climate hazards often overlooked by conventional meteorological measures. Current frameworks for assessing the impacts of extreme weather typically rely on meteorological thresholds; however, by the time such thresholds are met, the most vulnerable populations have often already experienced severe consequences [32]. Shifting from broad population-level measures to capturing lived experiences can provide a more accurate and timely understanding of climate-related mental health impacts. This approach also enables more responsive and targeted interventions, ensuring that those facing the greatest risks are not overlooked. Integrating localized, experiential data into climate impact assessments can more effectively identify and address the nuanced ways in which extreme weather exacerbates existing vulnerabilities, promoting more equitable and effective adaptation strategies.

Further, mental health intervention is too often reactive rather than preventive [4, 52]. A proactive approach is essential to mitigate the compounded effects of climate stressors and to strengthen resilience in vulnerable communities. Climate adaptation and disaster management strategies must embed mental health considerations as a core component to bridge gaps between research and practice [62]. This includes equipping key stakeholders—policymakers, health leaders, educators, mental health professionals, and emergency responders—with the tools to embed mental health support within climate resilience efforts. Interventions should center the experiences of those most vulnerable to climate change, ensuring these communities receive the support they need to cope with climate impacts.
