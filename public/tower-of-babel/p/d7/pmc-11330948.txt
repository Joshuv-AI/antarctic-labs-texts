Effects of artemisinin and cisplatin on the malignant progression of oral leukoplakia. In vitro and in vivo study
Authors: Mateus José Dutra, Isabella Souza Malta, Maria Leticia de Almeida Lança, Luana Marotta Reis de Vasconcellos, Daniela Adorno-Farias, José Antonio Jara, Estela Kaminagakura
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesChemoprevention can be a treatment for potentially malignant lesions (PMLs). We aimed to evaluate whether artemisinin (ART) and cisplatin (CSP) are associated with apoptosis and immunogenic cell death (ICD) in vitro, using oral leukoplakia (OL) and oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) cell lines, and whether these compounds prevent OL progression in vivo.MethodsNormal keratinocytes (HaCat), Dysplastic oral cells (DOK), and oral squamous cell carcinoma (SCC-180) cell lines were treated with ART, CSP, and ART + CSP to analyze cytotoxicity, genotoxicity, cell migration, and increased expression of proteins related to apoptosis and ICD. Additionally, 41 mice were induced with OL using 4NQO, treated with ART and CSP, and their tongues were histologically analyzed.ResultsIn vitro, CSP and CSP + ART showed dose-dependent cytotoxicity and reduced SCC-180 migration. No treatment was genotoxic, and none induced expression of proteins related to apoptosis and ICD; CSP considerably reduced High-mobility group box-1 (HMGB-1) protein expression in SCC-180. In vivo, there was a delay in OL progression with ART and CSP treatment; however, by the 16th week, only CSP prevented progression to OSCC.ConclusionExpression of proteins related to ICD and apoptosis did not increase with treatments, and CSP was shown to reduce immunogenic pathways in SCC-180, while reducing cell migration. ART did not prevent the malignant progression of OL in vivo; CSP did despite significant adverse effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-024-05924-x.Keywords: Immunogenic cell death, Antigen-presenting cells, Oral leukoplakia, Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, Immunomodulation

Introduction

Leukoplakia (OL) is the most common potentially malignant lesion (PML) in the mouth (Carrard and Van der Waal 2018; Mello et al. 2018), characterized by a predominantly white plaque of questionable risk that excludes other known diseases or disorders without an increased cancer risk (Warnakulasuriya et al. 2007; WHO 2022). Its main risk factors are tobacco and chronic alcohol consumption, and currently, the criteria for predicting its malignant transformation are based on the degree of epithelial dysplasia observed in histopathological examinations (Warnakulasuriya and Ariyawardana 2016; Villa et al. 2019).

Although there are currently no effective treatments for OL and proliferative verrucous leukoplakia (PVL), which is the multifocal form (Dutra et al. 2023), chemoprevention has been explored in the management of these disorders (Liao et al. 2023). Chemoprevention may work by blocking DNA damage or suppressing the proliferation of pre-malignant cells, thus preventing their progression to oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), a malignant neoplasm (MN) most commonly found in the mouth (Mohan Shankar et al. 2022; WHO 2022), and is more frequently observed in patients who use tobacco and drink alcohol (Herrera Costa et al. 2019).

Artemisinin (ART) has been described to have antineoplastic effect in in vitro studies with OSCC cells (Efferth et al. 2003; Yamachika et al. 2004) and in in vivo studies, it reduced the growth of xenograft-derived OSCC (Yu et al. 2024). ART is capable of producing reactive oxygen species (ROS), which induce oxidative stress and cell death (Tin et al. 2012; Letis et al. 2017; Wong et al. 2017; Lang et al. 2019), demonstrating potential for use as a chemotherapeutic agent, similar to cisplatin (CSP), a widely used metal-based drug against a broad range of MNs (Shen et al. 2012; Ho et al. 2016). CSP binds to genomic or mitochondrial DNA, activating transduction pathways that lead to cell necrosis or apoptosis (Achkar et al. 2018). However, it has significant limitations such as drug resistance in some neoplasms and toxic side effects (Shen et al. 2012; Oun et al. 2018).

ART and CSP derivatives have been previously associated with events linked to immunogenic cell death (ICD) (Hato et al. 2014; Slezakova and Ruda-Kucerova 2017), which is a form of cell death that activates the adaptive immune system (Vanmeerbeek et al. 2020), generating a specific immune response against tumor cells that are recognized and eliminated, especially in the early stages of carcinogenesis. Therefore, drugs that induce ICD are promising in the chemoprevention of PMLs in MN therapy (Galluzzi et al. 2016; Diederich 2019; Vanmeerbeek et al. 2020; Troitskaya et al. 2022).

The present study aimed to evaluate, in vitro, whether ART and CSP, alone or in combination, can induce the expression of proteins related to ICD and apoptosis in OL and OSCC cell lines and to assess, in vivo, whether these compounds can prevent OL progression to OSCC in a murine model, acting through chemoprevention as a form of therapy for OL, which currently lacks efficient treatments.

Materials and methods

In vitro study

Cell line selection and experimental group division

The cell lines of dysplastic oral cells DOK 94122104 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), oral squamous cell carcinoma SCC-180 (American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA), and normal keratinocytes HaCat (American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA) were tested in vitro and treated with ART (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), CSP (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and ART + CSP. The HaCat and SCC-180 cell lines were cultured in DMEM medium with high glucose concentration (Nova Biotechnology, Cotia, SP, BR), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Nova Biotechnology, Cotia, SP, BR) and 1% antibiotic (Penicillin–Streptomycin-Amphotericin, Nova Biotechnology, Cotia, SP, BR). The DOK cell line was cultured in DMEM medium with high glucose concentration, supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum and 1% antibiotic (Penicillin–Streptomycin-Amphotericin).

Dilution of compounds

100 mg of ART and CSP were diluted in phosphate-buffered saline solution (Nova Biotecnologia, Cotia, SP, BR) as stock solutions and then was diluted in appropriate culture medium for each cell line to concentrations of 0.1, 1, 10, 100, and 200 μM to perform each experiment.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was measured using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA). A total of 4 × 104 cells were seeded per well in 24-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to ART, CSP, and ART + CSP. After 48 h of treatment, the compounds were removed from the wells, and MTT salt diluted in phosphate-buffered saline was added at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. Following incubation with MTT, the solution was removed, and 1 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Perfyl Tech Química Ltda, São Bernardo do Campo, SP, BR) was added to each well. After homogenization, 100 μL aliquots were transferred to wells of a 96-well plate, and absorbance was measured using a spectrophotometer (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA) set at 570 nm.

Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to the negative control. The half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) were defined as the drug concentrations required to reduce cell proliferation by 50%. These doses were used for subsequent ELISA and migration experiments.

Genotoxicity test

The cell lines were seeded at a density of 4 × 104 cells per well in 24-well plates. After seeding, a 24 h incubation period was allowed for cell adherence before treating the cells with ART, CSP, and ART + CSP at concentrations of 0.1, 1, 10, 100, and 200 μM diluted in appropriate culture medium for each cell line. After 48 h of treatment, the medium was removed, and cytochalasin B (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) was added at a final concentration of 6.0 µg/ml of culture medium. After 28 h of cytochalasin B exposure, the medium was removed, and the samples were washed with phosphate-buffered saline and fixed with methanol for 15 min. Subsequently, the samples were washed again with phosphate-buffered saline, and the nuclear stain DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) was added to the wells. The positive control group received treatment with EMS (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA).

The wells were examined under epifluorescence using a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss Microimaging GmbH – Axiovert 40C, DE). Ten photos were taken per well using Axio Vision 7.0 software. The images were analyzed to determine the number of micronuclei in 100 binucleated cells per treatment.

Cell migration assay

The tested cell lines were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 7 × 104 cells per well and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 until reaching 100% confluence. Subsequently, scratch wounds were created in the cell monolayers using a sterile 10 µl pipette tip (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA), followed by the addition of the treatments to the wells. The cell lines were exposed to doses determined based on the cytotoxicity assay, aiming to cause a reduction in viability of 10% and 50%. For this purpose, the cells were treated with the IC10 of ART, IC10, and IC50 of CSP, and IC10 and IC50 of ART + CSP, with a negative control group included.

The wells were analyzed using conventional light microscopy, and photos were taken at 5 distinct and random points using Axio Vision 7.0 software at the time of scratch creation, as well as at 24 and 48 h post-treatment. Distances were measured by counting the space using a Barr scale.

The migration rate was calculated using the following formula:

Migration distance = Initial distance between the edges of the cell-free scratch—Distance at 24 or 48 h between the edges of the cell-free scratch.

ELISA assay

The ELISA assay was performed using cell culture supernatant to measure High-mobility group box-1 protein (HMGB-1), which activates inflammatory signaling in cells and tissues (Zhang et al. 2020), and using cell lysates to assess increased levels of calreticulin (CALR) and caspases 3 (CAS3), 8 (CAS8), and 9 (CAS9), which are found in sites such as the endoplasmic reticulum, cell membrane, cytoplasm, and nucleus (Zamanian et al. 2013; Boice and Bouchier-Hayes 2020).

For supernatant collection, the tested cell lines were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 8 × 105 cells per well and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 until reaching approximately 80% confluence. The cells were then treated with IC10 doses of ART, IC10 and IC50 doses of CSP, IC10 and IC50 doses of ART + CSP, with a negative control group included. 48 h after treatment initiation, the supernatant was collected, centrifuged for 20 min, and stored in aliquots at -80 °C with proper labeling for subsequent ELISA analysis.

For cell lysate preparation, the same procedure was followed. However, 48 h after treatment initiation, the supernatant was discarded, and the wells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline. Then, trypsin (Nova biotechnology, Cotia, SP, BR) was added to each well, followed by the inactivation of trypsin, and the entire suspension was centrifuged for 5 min. Subsequently, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was dissolved in cell lysis buffer (T-PER, Rockford, IL, USA). The lysate was then centrifuged again for 20 min at 2 °C, and the supernatant was aliquoted, identified, and stored at -80 °C for subsequent ELISA analysis.

The ELISA assay was performed using commercial kits for CAS3 (Cloud-Clone Corp. TX, USA), CAS8 (Cloud-Clone Corp. TX, USA), CAS9 (Cloud-Clone Corp. TX, USA), CALR (Novus Biologicals, CO, USA), and HMGB-1 (Novus Biologicals, CO, USA) proteins according to the manufacturer's recommendations for each kit. Plate readings were conducted using a spectrophotometer (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA) set at 450 nm wavelength.

Method of analyzing results

An initial descriptive analysis of the data was carried out containing the assessment of possible Outliers. Exploratory data analysis was performed by calculating summary measurements (mean, standard deviation, minimum, median, and maximum). To evaluate cytotoxicity, log-logistic dose–response models were used. To compare the drugs in relation to cell migration and protein expression, an ANOVA model was used followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. The level and significance adopted was 5%.

In vivo study

Ethical issues

The project adhered to the Ethical Principles for Animal Experimentation established by the National Council for Control of Animal Experimentation (CONCEA, 2016) and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Institute of Science and Technology at the São José dos Campos Campus/UNESP under protocol number 02/2021. The animal model guidelines followed the recommendations of the Animal Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) (Percie du Sert et al. 2020) for the design, implementation, and reporting of animal studies. The study design included randomization, prospective planning, and appropriate control measures as outlined by ARRIVE guidelines for conducting and submitting animal research.

Sample, experimental group allocation, and induction of OL and OSCC

The sample size calculation for the study followed a 2 × 4 factorial design (General Full Factorial Design) with α = 0.05, an expected standard deviation of 15%, a maximum difference of 22%, and a test power of 95% using the Minitab 16 software (Minitab 16, Minitab LLC, State College, USA).

The sample consisted of 41 female C57BL/6 J mice aged between 4 and 6 weeks, weighing approximately 16–18 g. The animals were housed in the ICT/UNESP animal facility in cages with controlled temperature and humidity, with access to water and food ad libitum. The experiment commenced after a 30-day acclimatization period for the mice in the facility.

The animals were divided into 5 groups: Pure Control (CP), Control (only received 4NQO), DMSO (received 4NQO and DMSO), CSP (received 4NQO and CSP), and ART (received 4NQO and ART), with 5 animals per group/per period, except for the control and DMSO groups, which consisted of 4 animals per group (Fig. 1).

Frequency of euthanasia procedures for the CP, Control, DMSO, ART, and CSP groups

In the in vivo experiments 4NQO (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved in propylene glycol (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) to create a stock solution (4 mg/mL), which was then stored at 4 °C. The stock solution was diluted in autoclaved water to a final concentration of 100 μg/mL (Tan et al. 2020) and provided to the animals in their drinking water, which was changed twice a week until euthanasia. The CP group received only autoclaved water.

Treatments and euthanasia time points

The ART was diluted in DMSO to a final dose of 85 mg/kg, adapted from Kepp et al. (2014). CSP was diluted in saline solution to a concentration of 5 mg/kg, adapted from Tran et al. (2017). The DMSO group received only DMSO solution, which served as the vehicle for ART dilution. They were administered weekly via intraperitoneal injection.

The euthanasia time points are detailed in Fig. 1.

Tongue collection and sample preparation

The tongues of the mice were collected immediately after euthanasia and preserved in 4% formaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA). After fixation, the tongues were longitudinally sectioned into two halves of equal volume. The samples were processed and embedded in Paraplast™ (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) paraffin blocks, then cut and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) for histological analysis.

Histological grading

The obtained slides were evaluated by two independent observers (MJD and ISM) who were previously trained, and a third researcher (EK) was consulted in cases of discrepancy. Tissue alterations were graded according to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification into mild dysplasia, severe dysplasia, and carcinoma (WHO 2022).

Statistical analysis

The observers underwent three rounds of slide evaluations, interspersed with recalibration of diagnostic criteria. Ultimately, the agreement of grading between MJD and ISM was analyzed, as well as the intra-observer consistency using Cohen's Weighted Kappa test.

Results

In vitro study

Cell viability

For the HaCat, DOK, and SCC-180 cell lines, when ART was evaluated independently, only the IC10 could be determined, which was calculated as a percentage relative to the control groups. Therefore, the IC10 of CSP and ART + CSP was also determined for HaCat, DOK, and SCC-180 (Fig. 2).

For the HaCat, DOK, and SCC-180 cell lines, the IC50 of ART + CSP and CSP was determined. The distribution is shown in Fig. 2.

Genotoxicity test

In all groups, the nuclei appeared similar to normal, with some binucleated cells observed, but no micronuclei formation was observed. Higher doses with CSP treatment and CSP + ART caused a significant reduction in the number of cells, but micronuclei were not observed (Fig. 3).

Genotoxicity test, DOK cell line, after treatments with 10 μM of compounds for 48 h. A Positive control. B Treatment with ART. C Treatment with CSP. D Treatment with ART + CSP

Cell migration test

For the HaCat lineage, the drugs ART + CSP IC50 and CSP IC50 showed a greater average distance at time 0 h than at times 24 h and 48 h, presenting statistically significant differences (p = < 0.001), that is, they were able to reduce cell migration (Fig. 4).

E SCC-180 cell line, CSP IC50 treatment, hour 48. F SCC-180 cell line, ART + CSP IC50 treatment, hour 0. G SCC-180 cell line, ART + CSP IC50 treatment, hour 24. H SCC-180 cell line, ART + CSP IC50 treatment, hour 48

The negative control showed no variation over 48 h (distance equal to zero in all measurements).

For the DOK lineage, at 24 h and 48 h the distance between the edges was 0 for practically all measurements, that is, no treatment inhibited the migration of these cells.

For the SCC-180 lineage, ART + CSP IC50 presented a greater average distance at time 0 h than at times 24 h and 48 h and also presented a greater average distance at time 24 h than at time 48 h, showing that this association can delay cell migration malignant (p = < 0.001). The CSP IC50 presented a greater average distance at time 0 h than at times 24 h and 48 h (p = < 0.001), and no statistically significant differences were found between times 24 h and 48 h. Thus, ART + CSP IC50 and CSP IC50 had significant results in reducing the migration of SCC-180 cells (Fig. 4).

Protein expression

For the HaCat lineage, in the CALR protein, the negative control showed a higher mean expression, presenting a statistical difference when compared to the other treatment groups (p = 0.041). HMGB-1 expression was null in all samples (Supplemental File 1).

For the DOK lineage, in the CAS8 and 9 proteins, ART IC10 showed the highest mean expression and, together with the negative control, statistically differed from the other treatments (p = 0.003 and p = 0.001, respectively), (Supplemental File 1).

For the SCC-180 strain, in the CALR protein, all CSP IC50 concentrations were equal to zero. In the CAS3 protein, all concentrations of CSP IC10 and CSP IC50 were equal to zero.

In this cell line, in the HMGB-1 protein, the negative control, ART IC10, CSP IC10, and ART + CSP IC10 presented higher means than the treatments that contained CSP in higher concentrations, differing statistically (p = < 0.001) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

ART and its derivatives have been effective against cervical (Jansen et al. 2011) and colorectal carcinoma (Krishna et al. 2014) without serious side effects. In a phase I clinical trial, 23 patients with metastatic breast cancer received daily oral administration of ART (100, 150, or 200 mg), and only four patients experienced treatment-related, non-serious adverse effects on the auditory system (König et al. 2016). Although there are no human clinical trials on the use of ART against OSCC, our results suggest that ART is well-tolerated and does not cause serious side effects. The clinical utility of CSP is hindered by adverse effects and toxicities, including protracted nausea, vomiting, ototoxicity, acute nephrotoxicity, myelosuppression, and chronic neurotoxicity (Shen et al. 2012; Oun et al. 2018). These occur due to the nonselective targeting of both healthy and malignant tissues (Oun et al. 2018).

We observed that ART, in vitro, did not cause a significant reduction in cell viability for OL and OSCC cell lines. In breast tumor and non-tumor cells, ART acted differently between them, indicating that the intrinsic properties of each cell type can lead to different responses to the compound (Tin et al. 2012). Furthermore, the effects of ART depend on the cell line and experimental design, such as dilution medium and tested doses (Posadino et al. 2023). This could explain our results obtained with ART, as we diluted CSP and ART in PBS, which is an aqueous medium. Although DMSO is commonly used for compound dilution, it can interact with platinum, rendering it unstable in this dilution medium, compromising the drug's cytotoxicity and promoting cell proliferation (Hall et al. 2014), potentially masking the results.

CSP isolated and combined with ART at IC50 concentrations were capable of reducing migration of HaCat and SCC-180 cells, indicating that ART, when employed with CSP, may control dysregulated cell growth and reduce excessive mobility, both of which contribute to metastasis processes (DeVries et al. 1999; Hood and Cheresh 2002). However, isolated ART did not reduce migration of the cells used in this study, contrary to findings by Yu et al. (2024), who observed reduced migration of OSCC cells treated with different doses of ART. Rao et al. (2022) found that the combination of CSP with dihydroartemisinin reduced the migration of human hepatoblastoma cells, and although we tested CSP in combination with ART, we found similar results in reducing OSCC cell migration. Our findings are also consistent with those of Semlali et al. (2023), who observed reduced migration of oral cancer cells treated with CSP.

ART is described as capable of inducing cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and ferroptosis in MN (Li et al. 2008; Wang et al. 2012; Slezakova and Ruda-Kucerova 2017; Cao et al. 2019). Yamachika et al. (2004), evaluating dihydroartemisinin treatment in vitro on OSCC cells, found that higher doses led to reduced cell viability and apoptosis, differing from our findings, which could be explained by methodological differences as we tested ART up to a dose of 200 µM; additionally, the authors did not clarify the dilution medium tested. Our results also contrast with those of Cao et al. (2019), who revealed that ART increased apoptosis of malignant cells in vitro.

Platinum-based compounds have been described to cause necrosis or apoptosis (Ober and Lippard 2007; Achkar et al. 2018), and some CSP derivatives, such as oxaliplatin, have been linked to cell cycle arrest (Martins et al. 2011; Hato et al. 2014; Bruno et al. 2017). In our study, we observed that treatment of the tested cell lines with CSP exhibited a dose-dependent relationship where increased dose led to reduced cell viability, although we found that apoptosis-related proteins were not expressed.

At IC50 concentrations, CSP and ART + CSP caused the opposite of cell cycle arrest, significantly reducing the expression of HMGB-1 in SCC-180, indicating that high doses of CSP cause non-immunogenic effects by acting in a way that does not maintain or reduce the expression of important proteins for the activation of inflammatory cells like HMGB-1 (Apetoh et al. 2007). HMGB-1 and CALR act as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), promoting recruitment and maturation of pro-inflammatory cells to recognize and eliminate dying cells; thus, when released by neoplastic cells, HMGB-1 and CALR contribute to the immune effects of the immune system against cancer (Ulloa and Messmer 2006; Apetoh et al. 2007; Kroemer et al. 2022). Our findings reveal that CSP reduces HMGB-1 expression, consequently dampening the activation of inflammatory cells against OSCC, potentially causing immunosuppression in patients with OSCC.

Dursun et al. (2006) found that CSP can also induce apoptosis initially and secondary necrosis after 18 h of treatment, which according to Lau (1999), leads to a reduction in CAS expression. This could be related to our findings, where treatment with CSP, resulting in a greater reduction of cell viability, did not show significant expression of CAS 3, 8, and 9 in the tested cell lines receiving this treatment, potentially causing initial apoptosis followed by necrosis of these cells, given that the treatment period we employed was longer than 18 h. The hypoexpression of apoptosis and ICD proteins in the tested cell lines could be attributed to the specific mechanisms through which the compounds interact with these cells. Other forms of cell death, such as necrosis, might have been triggered instead. Additionally, factors such as the duration of treatment utilized in the study and inherent properties of the cell lines, influencing their response to drug interaction, could also play a role.

During treatment with CSP at a weekly dose of 5 mg/Kg via intraperitoneal route, mice experienced pronounced weight loss, hair loss, and some became lethargic (Tsang et al. 2009; Tamura et al. 2017; Qi et al. 2019). Clinically, CSP is usually effective in the early stages of chemotherapy, similar to our results, but due to the gradual development of drug resistance or reduced tumor sensitivity, clinical application of CSP is greatly limited (Biddle et al. 2016). Despite the significant toxic effects, we observed that the groups treated with CSP did not show malignant progression of OL during the analyzed periods. To minimize these adverse effects, alternative methods of CSP delivery have been developed. PRV111, a nanotechnology-based system for local delivery of CSP-loaded chitosan particles, achieved complete resolution of oral pre-malignant lesions with no overt toxicity and improved CSP biodistribution compared to systemic administration. Additionally, a phase I/II trial found neoadjuvant PRV111 to be well tolerated in early-stage OSCC patients, with no dose-limiting toxicity, systemic toxicity, or severe adverse events, demonstrating remarkable technical success (Goldberg et al. 2022).

During weekly therapy with ART, animals demonstrated good general condition, but it did not prevent the progression of dysplasia to more advanced disease stages, contrasting with findings by Yu et al. (2024), who used ART at different doses (25 mg/Kg, 100 mg/Kg) daily for 21 days in nude mice xenograft tumors. ART induced vascular normalization of OSCC through the macrophage migration inhibitory factor signaling pathway and improved the transport efficiency of CSP (Yu et al. 2024).

Although no adverse effects were observed with the use of ART, in vivo, it was not able to prevent the malignant progression of OL in the periods and doses evaluated, unlike CSP. In vitro, ART was not demonstrated to be cytotoxic, ART and CSP did not increase the expression of proteins related to ICD and apoptosis in the cell lines tested. Higher doses of CSP and ART + CSP were able to reduce the migration of the SCC-180 cell line, although they significantly reduced the expression of HMGB-1 in this cell line, indicating that CSP causes non-immunogenic effects. We also emphasize that although CSP prevented the malignant progression of OL, important systemic adverse effects may reduce the benefits compared to the risks. this form of treatment.
