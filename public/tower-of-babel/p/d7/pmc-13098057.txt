Artificial Intelligence–Driven Colonoscopy: A Systematic Review and Network Meta‐Analysis on System Performance for Colorectal Neoplasia Detection
Authors: F N U Eman, F N U Gyaneshwari, Raveena Kumari, Afshan Jabeen, Karoona Kumari, F N U Mansha, Shagufta Sattar, Fatima Zehra, Sonika Hotwani, Muhammad Talha Kakar, Hira Riaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is a major global health issue, with adenomatous polyps as the main precursors. Standard colonoscopy is the gold standard, but its effectiveness is limited by operator variability and missed lesions. Artificial Intelligence (AI)‐assisted colonoscopy is emerging to enhance detection, but a comparative performance across different AI systems is unclear.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and Bayesian network meta‐analysis of 48 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (N = 34 106 participants), searched across PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar up to November 4, 2025. We evaluated five commercial systems—EndoAngel, EndoAID, CAD‐EYE, GI Genius, EndoScreener—and local platforms. Primary outcomes were Adenoma Detection Rate (ADR) and Adenomas Per Colonoscopy (APC).ResultsAll AI systems significantly improved ADR versus conventional colonoscopy. EndoAngel showed the largest effect (OR 1.84; SUCRA 0.9), followed by EndoAID (OR 1.64; SUCRA 0.7). CAD‐EYE (OR 1.46; SUCRA 0.5) and GI Genius (OR 1.45; SUCRA 0.5) also showed gains. APC gains were highest with EndoAID (MD 0.62). EndoAngel modestly increased withdrawal time (MD 1.14 min). Crucially, no AI system significantly improved the detection of high‐risk lesions. Evidence quality was moderate.ConclusionAI‐assisted colonoscopy improves adenoma detection over conventional methods. While EndoAngel and EndoAID show the largest gains, performance differences among systems are modest. Detection of high‐risk lesions remains uncertain, underscoring the need for future head‐to‐head trials and cost‐effectiveness studies to guide optimal implementation in CRC screening.Keywords: adenoma detection rate, artificial intelligence, colonoscopy, colorectal neoplasia

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major global health challenge, ranking as the second leading cause of cancer‐related deaths worldwide. In 2020 alone, an estimated 1.9 million new cases and 935 000 deaths were reported [1], emphasizing the urgent need for effective prevention strategies. Since most CRCs develop from adenomatous polyps, early detection and removal of these precancerous lesions remain the most powerful tools for reducing CRC incidence and mortality [2].

Conventional colonoscopy is the established gold standard for screening because it allows direct visualization and removal of neoplastic lesions [3]. Key performance indicators—such as Adenoma Detection Rate (ADR) and Adenomas Per Colonoscopy (APC)—help measure procedural quality. Their importance is well documented: a 1% increase in ADR is linked to a 3% reduction in CRC incidence and a 5% reduction in CRC‐related mortality [4]. However, colonoscopy performance varies widely. Miss rates range from 6% to 27% for adenomas and can reach up to 27% for serrated lesions [5, 6, 7]. Factors such as suboptimal bowel preparation, endoscopist fatigue, technique differences, and the subtle appearance of flat or sessile polyps all contribute to these missed lesions [7].

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has emerged as a promising approach to reduce these limitations. AI‐based Computer‐Aided Detection (CADe) systems use deep learning to provide real‐time alerts for potential polyps [8, 9]. Early meta‐analyses suggest that AI can increase ADR by 7%–10% over standard colonoscopy [10]. However, real‐world performance may be reduced by factors like poor mucosal exposure or image quality. Furthermore, multiple commercial systems—including EndoAngel, EndoAID, CAD‐EYE, and GI‐Genius—differ in design, capabilities, and clinical impact.

To address these gaps, this network meta‐analysis (NMA) systematically compares the efficacy of major AI‐assisted colonoscopy systems across primary outcomes (ADR, APC) and key secondary outcomes, including withdrawal time, SSLDR, AADR, lesion‐per‐colonoscopy metrics, and miss rates, using evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs). This evaluation aims to guide the informed and responsible adoption of AI technologies for CRC prevention.

Methodology

Protocol and Registration

To guarantee methodological transparency and repeatability, this network meta‐analysis was carried out in compliance with a predetermined procedure. Before the data were extracted and analyzed, the methodology was prospectively registered in the Open Science Framework (OSF). Complete information on the public can see the protocol, which includes the search strategy, eligibility requirements, and proposed statistical methodologies, at https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/CP467.

Eligibility Criteria and PICO

This analysis included only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to ensure a high level of evidence. Studies were eligible if they directly compared AI‐assisted colonoscopy with conventional colonoscopy in detecting adenomas. Trials were excluded if they lacked sufficient data on adenoma detection or focused primarily on advanced colorectal cancer. The study adhered to PRISMA guidelines to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar from inception to November 4, 2025. The search combined free‐text keywords related to adenoma detection, colonoscopy, and AI with Medical Subject Headings (MeSH). There were no language or publication restrictions. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened, but no additional records were identified.

The search strategy aimed to maximize sensitivity using MeSH terms and keywords, including the names of specific AI systems (GI Genius, EndoAngel, CAD‐EYE, EndoScreener, etc.), optimized with Boolean operators (AND/OR). Details of the search strategy for each database are provided in Table 1. Two independent reviewers conducted the search, and citations were managed using EndNote.

Data Management and Extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data from qualifying studies using a pre‐piloted, standardized data extraction form. Study characteristics (author, year, and country), participant demographics, the kind of AI system employed, the comparator, and sample size were among the variables that were extracted.

The extracted outcome data included both primary outcomes—adenoma detection rate (ADR) and adenomas per colonoscopy (APC)—and all secondary outcomes: withdrawal time, sessile serrated lesion detection rate (SSLDR), sessile serrated lesion detection per colonoscopy, advanced adenoma detection rate (AADR), and advanced adenoma per colonoscopy.

Any differences of opinion were settled by consensus or after speaking with a third reviewer. In order to gather missing or unpublished data, corresponding authors were contacted where needed.

Statistical Strategy and Data Synthesis

All statistical analyses were executed using a Bayesian Network Meta‐Analysis (NMA) with a random‐effects model (Stata software, version 17). This robust approach allows for the simultaneous comparison of all included AI systems, even those without direct head‐to‐head trials.

Summary measures: For binary outcomes (like ADR, AADR, etc.), the main effect metric was the Odds Ratio (OR), presented with its 95% Credible Interval (CrI).

Ranking and Heterogeneity Assessment

Treatment Ranking

To determine the relative clinical utility of the different AI systems, treatments were ranked using Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking Curve (SUCRA) scores. SUCRA scores, which range from 0% to 100%, indicate the probability that an intervention is the most effective therapy for a given outcome. Rank Plots were used to visually summarize these probabilities, providing an intuitive understanding of which system is most likely to be superior.

Consistency and Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity—the variability in treatment effects across the studies—was assessed using the I 2 statistic, guided by Cochrane Handbook guidelines. Values were used to determine the consistency of effects and inform the interpretation of results. Because our network primarily included comparisons of each AI intervention versus the control group (without many direct AI‐to‐AI comparisons), a formal statistical assessment of inconsistency using techniques like node‐splitting was not feasible. Instead, the critical assumption of transitivity was addressed by ensuring that key clinical and methodological factors (e.g., patient demographics, study design, and outcome definitions) were comparable across all included trials.

Evaluation of Bias in Publications

Confidence in the Evidence The GRADE approach modified for NMA was incorporated into the CINeMA (Confidence in Network Meta‐Analysis) framework, which was used to evaluate the overall certainty of the evidence. Within‐study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence are the six dimensions that CINeMA assesses. Based on these domains, each comparison received a confidence rating of high, moderate, low, or extremely low.

Results

Study Characteristics

The PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1) illustrates the study selection process. A total of 722 records were initially identified through database searches (PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar), with no additional records from other sources. After removing three duplicates, 719 records were screened by title and abstract, leaving 377 full‐text articles for detailed evaluation. Of these, 329 were excluded due to inappropriate document type (n = 126), lack of relevant data (n = 72), or an incorrect study population (n = 131). Ultimately, 48 studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising 34 106 participants.

Prisma flow diagram.

Five AI‐assisted colonoscopy systems were evaluated across these trials. GI Genius (Medtronic) was the most frequently studied, appearing in 12 trials with 9215 patients. CAD‐EYE (FujiFilm) was assessed in 8 studies, including 3126 patients. EndoScreener (Shanghai Wision AI) featured in 11 studies with 7643 patients, representing the largest single body of evidence. EndoAngel (Wuhan EndoAngel Medical Technology) was evaluated in 6 studies with 3110 patients, and EndoAID (Olympus Corporation) in 3 studies with 1024 patients. Additionally, 7 trials investigated locally developed AI systems, including 6643 participants across Asian and Western populations. Comparisions are summarized in Network plot Figure S1.

Study designs varied, including both parallel‐group and tandem trials, with settings across Asia, Europe, and North Americas, providing a broad and heterogeneous dataset for comparing AI‐assisted and conventional colonoscopy. While some studies had small sample sizes or less precise reporting, most were judged to have a low risk of bias. Detailed characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 2.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool was used to evaluate methodological quality across five domains: randomization, deviations from planned interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Two reviewers independently assessed each study, resolving disagreements through discussion.

Overall, the included studies demonstrated a low risk of bias in most domains. Randomization procedures, adherence to interventions, outcome measurement, and completeness of data were generally well conducted and reported. Selective reporting was not a significant concern, and most trials had protocols or registrations available, supporting the credibility of the dataset.

Effectiveness of AI Systems

Rate of Adenoma Detection

Among all evaluated systems, EndoAngel (Wuhan EndoAngel Medical Technology) showed the strongest improvement in adenoma detection rate (ADR), with an OR of 1.84 (SUCRA = 0.9), clearly outperforming standard colonoscopy. EndoAID (Olympus Corporation) ranked second (OR 1.64, SUCRA = 0.7), while CAD‐EYE (Fujifilm) and GI‐Genius (Medtronic) followed closely with similar effects (OR 1.46 and 1.45, SUCRA = 0.5). EndoScreener, although ranked low, still achieved a moderate improvement (OR 1.37, SUCRA = 0.4). As expected, conventional colonoscopy had the lowest ADR.

The league table analysis confirmed that EndoAngel (OR 1.84 [1.32; 2.56]) offered the greatest relative benefit. Other systems showed varying but generally positive effects, including EndoAID (OR 1.64 [1.20; 2.26]), CAD‐EYE (OR 1.46 [1.21; 1.75]), GI GENIUS (OR 1.45 [1.22; 1.73]), and EndoScreener (OR 1.37 [1.20; 1.56]). These results collectively demonstrate that all evaluated AI systems outperform conventional colonoscopy to varying degrees.

Details are shown in Figures S22 and S24 and Tables S22, S24, and S25.

Adenomas Per Colonoscopy (APC)

For APC, EndoAID demonstrated the largest improvement over standard colonoscopy (mean difference 0.62). EndoAngel, despite having the smallest mean difference (0.09), still showed a statistically meaningful improvement. Rankings for APC are visualized in Figure S5.

In contrast, the league table based on ORs indicated lower values for AI systems compared with control. EndoAngel (OR 0.54, 95% CI 0.39–0.76) and EndoAID (OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.44–0.84) performed best, though pairwise comparisons showed overlapping confidence intervals. For instance, CAD‐EYE vs. GI GENIUS (OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.77–1.28) and GI GENIUS vs. EndoScreener (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.76–1.17) indicated comparable effectiveness.

Full details are provided in Figures S23 and S25, and Table S23.

Withdrawal Time

EndoAngel produced the largest and statistically significant increase in withdrawal time (MD 1.14 min, 95% CI 0.70–1.59) (Figure S3). Local AI systems also significantly prolonged withdrawal time (MD 0.34 min, 95% CI 0.06–0.63). Other systems—CAD_EYE, EndoAID, EndoScreener, and GI‐Genius—showed small, non‐significant changes relative to control.

Ranking analyses presented in Table S3 consistently placed EndoAngel at the top (SUCRA = 98.7), followed by local systems (SUCRA = 66.01). Other systems demonstrated moderate effects, while conventional colonoscopy ranked lowest (SUCRA = 22.98). Additional analyses are shown in Figures S2 and S4, and summarized in Tables S1 and S2.

Sessile Serrated Lesion (SSL) Detection Rate

None of the AI systems significantly improved SSL detection compared with control (Figure S6). EndoScreener had the highest point estimate (OR 1.60, 95% CrI 0.23–12.04), followed by CAD_EYE (OR 1.15, 95% CrI 0.56–2.55) and GI‐Genius (OR 1.14, 95% CrI 0.70–1.71). EndoAngel and local systems also showed nonsignificant effects, with wide credible intervals signaling substantial uncertainty.

Although EndoScreener, GI‐Genius, and CAD_EYE ranked higher as presented in Table S6 (SUCRA 63.55, 55.38, and 53.05, respectively), no system demonstrated a reliable advantage. Further comparative details are provided in Figures S5 and S7, Tables S4 and S5.

Sessile Serrated Lesion Detection Per Colonoscopy

Similarly, no AI system significantly outperformed the control group for SSL detection per colonoscopy (Figure S9). CAD_EYE had the greatest mean reduction (MD –0.16, 95% CI –0.31 to −0.01), with other systems showing small, nonsignificant effects.

Ranking results presented in Table S9 placed CAD_EYE highest (SUCRA = 97.3), followed by local systems (55.87) and EndoScreener (48.07), while GI_GENIUS (14.52) and conventional colonoscopy (34.24) ranked lower. Despite these rankings, wide confidence intervals emphasize considerable imprecision. Further details are presented in Figures S8 and S10 and Tables S7 and S8.

Advanced Adenoma Detection Rate

Across all systems, none of the evaluated AI tools showed a statistically significant improvement over the control group (Figure S12). Local AI systems had the highest point estimate (OR 1.27, 95% CrI 0.85–1.84), followed by EndoAID (OR 1.23, 95% CrI 0.57–2.72) and CAD_EYE (OR 1.12, 95% CrI 0.80–1.56). GI‐Genius showed a modest, nonsignificant effect (OR 1.03, 95% CrI 0.69–1.53), while EndoAngel had the lowest estimate (OR 0.96, 95% CrI 0.26–3.76). The wide credible intervals across all systems highlight substantial uncertainty.

Direct and indirect comparisons showed no consistent advantage for any AI system, with overlapping credible intervals throughout. Rankings presented in Table S12 placed conventional colonoscopy highest (SUCRA = 67.75), followed by GI‐Genius (59.31) and EndoAngel (58.94). CAD_EYE (SUCRA = 46.02), EndoAID (38.57), and local systems (29.40) ranked lower. Despite these ranking differences, the absence of statistically significant effects indicates no clear benefit of AI systems for advanced adenoma detection. Further comparative details are provided in Figures S11 and S13 and Tables S10 and S11.

Advanced Adenoma Per Colonoscopy

Across all evaluated AI systems, no statistically significant improvement was observed compared with standard colonoscopy for advanced adenomas per colonoscopy (Figure S15). CAD‐EYE (MD 0.0209, 95% CrI –0.0272 to 0.0686) and GI‐Genius (MD 0.00784, 95% CrI –0.0266 to 0.0505) showed small nonsignificant increases, while EndoScreener (MD –0.00641, 95% CrI –0.0252 to 0.0179) and local systems (MD –0.00433, 95% CrI –0.0386 to 0.0266) demonstrated minimal nonsignificant differences. All credible intervals crossed the null, confirming no detectable clinical benefit.

Rankings presented in Table S15 placed EndoScreener highest (SUCRA = 72.65), followed by local systems (65.41) and conventional colonoscopy (53.14), while GI‐Genius (37.70) and CAD‐EYE (21.11) ranked lower. However, these should be interpreted cautiously given the nonsignificant effects. Further details are shown in Figures S14 and S16, and Tables S13 and S14.

Adenoma Miss Rate

All AI systems demonstrated numerically lower adenoma miss rates (AMR) than controls, but none reached statistical significance (Figure S18). Local systems (OR 0.249, 95% CrI 0.0551–1.12) and EndoScreener (OR 0.373, 95% CrI 0.122–1.08) showed the most favorable point estimates, followed by GI‐Genius (OR 0.378, 95% CrI 0.0820–1.72) and EndoAngel (OR 0.402, 95% CrI 0.0849–1.90). CAD‐EYE showed the least reduction (OR 0.479, 95% CrI 0.155–1.39).

Wide and overlapping credible intervals indicate substantial imprecision, with no reliable differences between systems. Rankings detailed in Table S17 placed local systems highest (SUCRA = 79.41), followed by EndoScreener (59.36), GI‐Genius (57.63), EndoAngel (53.96), and CAD‐EYE (43.96), with conventional colonoscopy lowest (SUCRA = 5.69). Despite favorable trends, no system can be considered definitively superior. Additional comparative analyses and plots are available in Figures S17 and S19, and Tables S16, S17, and S18.

Polyp Miss Rate (PMR)

The pooled results for polyp miss rate are presented in Figure S21. EndoScreener was the only system to demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in PMR compared with standard colonoscopy (OR 0.30, 95% CI 0.11–0.86). In contrast, Local systems (OR 0.24, 95% CI 0.06–1.05), GI‐Genius (OR 0.45, 95% CI 0.10–1.98), and EndoAngel (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.15–2.63) all displayed numerical reductions but did not reach statistical significance.

Ranking distributions and SUCRA probabilities for PMR are presented in Table S21. Local AI systems achieved the highest ranking (SUCRA = 74.37), driven by their large effect size, followed by EndoScreener (SUCRA = 68.83) and GI‐Genius (SUCRA = 52.19). EndoAngel displayed a lower relative ranking (SUCRA = 39.34), while conventional colonoscopy ranked the lowest overall (SUCRA = 15.28). These results suggest that while local systems had the highest probability of being the most effective based on point estimates, EndoScreener (ranked second) offers the most robust balance of high efficacy and statistical significance.

Heterogeneity and Publication Bias Assessment

The analysis showed low heterogeneity across most groups, with an I 2 of less than 50%. A funnel plot (Figures S6 and S7) was generated to assess potential publication bias among the included studies. The plot displays the log‐transformed odds ratios (log ORs) of each study against their standard errors. A symmetrical distribution of studies within the triangular region—bounded by 95% confidence limits—suggest a low likelihood of publication bias. In this analysis, the plot for ADR outcome appears reasonably symmetrical, while for APC outcome there is a slight asymmetry. However, due to the limited number of included studies (n = 5), the interpretive power of the funnel plot remains constrained, and results should be viewed with caution.

Certainty of Evidence

A CINeMA‐based risk of bias assessment was conducted across six key domains for all included AI‐assisted colonoscopy interventions. The evaluation revealed that all AI‐assisted colonoscopy interventions demonstrated a low risk of within‐study bias, reporting bias, and indirectness, with the exception. The domain of incoherence was marked as not applicable, as no direct head‐to‐head trials were available between the AI systems; all effect estimates were derived from indirect comparisons via conventional colonoscopy arms. Overall, the risk of bias assessment supports a moderate level of confidence in the evidence base, with the primary limitations stemming from small study sizes and the absence of direct AI‐vs‐AI comparisons.

Discussion

In the past, clinicians manually identified polyps during colonoscopies and determined whether the lesions were benign or potentially cancerous. However, this approach is vulnerable to human error and variations in detection rates due to operator fatigue, experience, or visualization technique [13]. Missed lesions remain a major challenge despite technological improvements, with studies reporting adenoma miss rates of up to 25% [14]. These challenges underscore the potential role of AI‐assisted detection systems in improving Adenoma Detection Rate (ADR) and Adenomas per Colonoscopy (APC) and, ultimately, reducing colorectal cancer (CRC) mortality.

Efficacy in Adenoma Detection (ADR and APC)

AI‐assisted colonoscopy devices provide real‐time visual alerts for suspicious lesions, helping reduce human error and enhance detection. In this network meta‐analysis, EndoAngel had the largest estimated effect on ADR (OR 1.84), outperforming conventional colonoscopy [15]. Its APC improvement was modest (MD 0.09), indicating that it effectively ensures at least one adenoma is detected per procedure rather than substantially increasing the total number of adenomas detected. EndoAID, by contrast, demonstrated a significant rise in ADR and was associated with the largest APC improvement (MD 0.62). These differing patterns highlight an important clinical question: should we prioritize systems that increase the number of adenomas detected per procedure (APC) or those that maximize overall detection (ADR)? While APC offers complementary insights into procedural performance, ADR remains the most reliable outcome metric because it is the key benchmark associated with reduced colon cancer risk.

SUCRA rankings indicate that EndoAngel is most likely to improve ADR, followed by EndoAID, CAD‐EYE, GI‐Genius, and EndoScreener. It is important to note that SUCRA values represent relative probabilities rather than absolute rankings, and overlapping confidence intervals caution against overinterpreting small differences.

Procedural Efficiency and Withdrawal Time

Enhanced detection came with trade‐offs in procedural efficiency. EndoAngel, the system with the highest ADR effect size, also showed the greatest increase in withdrawal time (MD 1.14 min, 95% CI 0.70–1.59), ranking highest in SUCRA (98.7). This suggests that while highly sensitive, EndoAngel may require longer procedures, an important consideration for workflow and cost. Other systems, including GI‐Genius, EndoScreener, and CAD‐EYE, showed nonsignificant changes in withdrawal time, indicating minimal impact on efficiency.

Adenoma and Polyp Miss Rates (AMR and PMR)

All AI systems showed numerically lower AMR and PMR compared with conventional colonoscopy, though none reached statistical significance. For AMR, local AI systems ranked highest (SUCRA = 79.41), followed by EndoScreener (59.36) and GI‐Genius (57.63). EndoAngel (53.96) and CAD‐EYE (43.96) had intermediate rankings, with conventional colonoscopy lowest (SUCRA = 5.69). For PMR, EndoScreener ranked highest (SUCRA = 81.2), followed by local systems (65.5) and GI‐Genius (52.1), with conventional colonoscopy lowest (SUCRA = 8.5). These results indicate a favorable directional effect for AI‐assisted systems, but the lack of statistical significance highlights uncertainty and prevents definitive claims about miss rate reductions.

High‐Risk Lesion Detection

AI systems did not show statistically significant improvements in detecting high‐risk lesions, including Sessile Serrated Lesion Detection Rate (SSLDR), Sessile Serrated Lesion per Colonoscopy (SSLPC), Advanced Adenoma Detection Rate (AADR), and Advanced Adenoma per Colonoscopy (AAPC).

Architectural Differences and Performance

Differences in AI performance likely reflect variations in architecture, training data, and real‐time feedback. EndoAngel uses a YOLOv3 CNN trained on a large and diverse dataset, which may explain its superior ADR and withdrawal time performance. EndoScreener, based on a SegNet CNN, and CAD‐EYE, using a skip‐connection CNN, achieved comparable ADRs, suggesting that dataset quality and clinical integration may outweigh architectural differences. EndoAID, though its CNN structure is unspecified, excelled at APC, demonstrating that some algorithms may prioritize detecting multiple adenomas per procedure.

Although heterogeneity values (I 2) were generally low across most outcomes, a more nuanced interpretation is warranted. Between‐study variability likely reflects differences in endoscopist expertise, bowel preparation quality, endoscope models, ADR baseline performance, and patient population characteristics (screening vs. diagnostic cohorts). These elements may influence system performance and contribute to the observed variation within effect estimates. For example, studies conducted in high‐ADR centers may show smaller incremental effects from AI systems, whereas those in settings with lower baseline detection may observe larger improvements.

From a clinical standpoint, understanding these heterogeneity drivers is important because the real‐world benefit of AI systems may depend on the operational environment. AI tools may perform optimally in settings with variable operator skill or challenging visualization, whereas in high‐performance endoscopy units the added relative improvement may be modest. This contextual interpretation helps clinicians determine where AI integration might yield the most meaningful improvements.

Clinical Interpretation of Divergence Between ADR and APC Across Systems

An important finding of this analysis is the observed divergence between adenoma detection rate (ADR) and adenomas per colonoscopy (APC) across different AI systems. EndoAngel ranked highest for ADR but showed only a modest improvement in APC, whereas EndoAID demonstrated the largest increase in APC despite ranking slightly lower in ADR.

This pattern suggests that different AI systems may be optimized for distinct clinical functions. EndoAngel's architecture, which emphasizes rapid segmentation and real‐time flagging, may increase the probability of detecting at least one adenoma per procedure—thus improving ADR. In contrast, EndoAID appears better suited for detecting multiple lesions, translating into a larger APC benefit.

Clinically, this distinction may guide implementation:

Screening colonoscopy, where finding at least one adenoma matters most, may benefit more from systems with strong ADR performance.

Limitations and Future Directions

Although this review provides detailed descriptions of most AI‐assisted colonoscopy systems, important technical information—especially for EndoAID—remains unavailable. Details such as model architecture, size of the training dataset, and validation processes were not publicly reported. These gaps limit our ability to fully compare systems and highlight a broader issue in the field: the lack of consistent, transparent reporting standards for AI development. Clearer technical documentation is needed so that differences in clinical performance can be better understood.

Interpreting the comparative rankings of AI systems also requires caution. While SUCRA scores may suggest which systems appear more effective, these rankings are probabilistic and not direct indicators of true superiority. Because no head‐to‐head randomized trials exist between AI systems, all comparisons rely on indirect evidence through the shared control arm. Future research should prioritize direct comparative trials to clarify whether observed ranking differences reflect meaningful clinical advantages.

The effectiveness of AI is influenced by many contextual factors, including study design, patient characteristics, and the experience and technique of the endoscopist. Standardizing AI training methods and ensuring high‐quality datasets could increase reliability across different settings. Practical considerations such as cost, workflow effects, and procedural time—particularly for systems like EndoAngel that increase withdrawal time—are also essential to evaluate. Long‐term outcomes, including colorectal cancer incidence, mortality, and detection of high‐risk lesions, remain understudied and warrant future follow‐up in large, multicenter trials.

This network meta‐analysis has limitations. Only 17 randomized trials were included, and some AI systems were evaluated in relatively few studies, reducing statistical power. Many comparisons depend on indirect evidence, and variability across trials—including endoscopist experience, equipment differences, and bowel preparation quality—may introduce clinical heterogeneity. Because performance differences between systems were modest and uncertain, conclusions should be interpreted carefully. Direct comparative trials are needed to better define the relative strengths of each AI platform.

Conclusion

This network meta‐analysis provides strong evidence that AI‐assisted colonoscopy systems can improve adenoma detection compared with conventional colonoscopy. Systems such as EndoAngel, EndoAID, CAD‐EYE, and GI‐Genius were all associated with higher adenoma detection rates (ADR) and adenomas per colonoscopy (APC). While EndoAngel showed the largest effect, overlapping confidence intervals and study variability suggest that differences between AI systems are generally modest and should be interpreted with caution.

These findings support the value of integrating AI into colonoscopy to enhance colorectal cancer screening. However, more head‐to‐head trials are needed to clarify the relative effectiveness of different AI systems. Future research should also explore cost‐effectiveness and practical implementation to guide clinical decision‐making and maximize the potential of AI in colorectal cancer prevention.
