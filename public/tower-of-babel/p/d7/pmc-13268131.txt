Temporal Lysophosphatidic Acid Supplementation Enhances Megakaryocyte Differentiation and Platelet Production From Human Hematopoietic Progenitors
Authors: Nittaya Jiamvoraphong, Chanchao Lorthongpanich, Praphasri Septham, Phatchanat Klaihmon, Pakpoom Kheolamai, Chuti Laowtammathron, Sumeth Imsoonthornruksa, Mariena Ketudat‐Cairns, Surapol Issaragrisil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlatelet shortages and limited storage stability restrict global platelet transfusion capacity, highlighting the need for efficient in vitro platelet production systems. This study establishes a simple, non‐genetic and cost‐effective system for efficient in vitro platelet‐like particle (PLP) production from human hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) using a Phase‐specific modulation of Hippo‐YAP/TAZ signaling modulation. Temporal control of Hippo‐YAP/TAZ signaling by lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), an activator of YAP/TAZ activity, significantly enhanced megakaryocyte differentiation, expansion and PLP production, resulting in an approximately 15‐fold increase in PLP yield at the end of the procedure. Furthermore, LPA extended the expansion period of HSPC‐derived megakaryocytes up to 8 days, resulting in a greater than 20‐fold increase in the number of HSPC‐derived CD41+ megakaryocytes. Moreover, replacement of expensive commercial recombinant human thrombopoietin (C‐rhTPO), one of the major cost‐driving components in in vitro PLP production, with recombinant human thrombopoietin produced in Escherichia coli (W‐rhTPO) further improved the cost‐effectiveness of the procedure. In conclusion, this study demonstrates that temporally controlled Hippo‐YAP/TAZ signaling, together with affordable cytokine supplementation, provides a robust and GMP‐compatible platform for large‐scale PLP manufacturing for future clinical applications. We believe that this system will enable scalable PLP generation, even in resource‐constrained settings, to increase human platelet supply for many life‐saving therapies in the future.Keywords: hematopoietic cell, lysophosphatidic acid, megakaryocyte, platelet, TPO

Introduction

Platelet transfusion continues to be a crucial treatment for various life‐threatening conditions, including hematologic malignancies, severe trauma, and surgical bleeding. However, the scarcity of suitable donors, the limited storage stability of platelets, and the potential risk of alloimmunization pose significant challenges to platelet transfusion worldwide Consequently, the development of an in vitro PLP production system from renewable cell sources, such as embryonic stem cells (ESCs), induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), and immortalized hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs), has emerged as a promising strategy for generating a limitless supply of platelets for transfusion (Nakamura et al. 2014; Chen et al. 2023; Sugimoto and Nakamura 2022; Takayama et al. 2008; Izady et al. 2022). Despite recent advances, most systems for deriving PLP from these renewable sources remain constrained by low yield, high costs, and scaling difficulties (Moreau et al. 2016).

The molecular process underlying megakaryocyte differentiation and platelet production is highly complex and tightly regulated by cytokine signaling, cytoskeletal remodeling, and mechanical forces, including shear stress within the bone marrow microenvironment (Machlus and Italiano 2013; Zaninetti et al. 2020). The high cost of thrombopoietin (TPO) and stem cell factor (SCF), which are indispensable for megakaryocyte differentiation, (Thon et al. 2017; Sitnicka et al. 1996; Ohmizono et al. 1997),], coupled with the challenges in replicating the appropriate mechanical forces, such as the hemodynamic shear stress that facilitates proplatelet elongation and platelet release (Roka‐Moiia et al. 2020). render the development of a large‐scale in vitro PLP production challenging.

Recent studies have highlighted the Hippo–YAP/TAZ signaling pathway as a crucial mechanosensitive regulator of megakaryopoiesis (Lorthongpanich et al. 2017, 2020; Roy et al. 2016). The Hippo kinases LATS1/2 act upstream of YAP/TAZ to modulate cytoskeletal tension, proliferation, and maturation. Lorthongpanich et al. demonstrated that inhibition of LATS1/2 enhanced megakaryocytic differentiation but reduced PLP production, indicating that appropriate dynamic alterations in Hippo pathway activity during various phases of megakaryocytic differentiation are essential for efficient PLP production (Lorthongpanich et al. 2020; Damkham et al. 2022). Consistent with this, it has been demonstrated that, while an increase in YAP level promotes the proliferation of megakaryocytes, its downregulation is necessary to facilitate efficient PLP production. Furthermore, Lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), an abundant serum phospholipid that activates RhoA‐dependent cytoskeletal remodeling and modulates YAP/TAZ activity (Sheng et al. 2015; Yasuda et al. 2019; Piccolo et al. 2014) has also been shown to affect megakaryocyte proliferation and differentiation by regulating actin polymerization and cell adhesion (Lorthongpanich et al. 2020). Collectively, these studies establish YAP/TAZ as biphasic regulators of megakaryopoiesis by promoting the expansion of megakaryocytes while restricting their terminal differentiation and PLP production. Given its biphasic effects on human megakaryocyte differentiation, we hypothesized that temporal control of YAP/TAZ activity using biochemical modulation, LPA treatment, could potentially enhance megakaryocytic differentiation and PLP production of CD34+ human HSPCs.

The discovery of thrombopoietin (TPO) in 1994 enabled significant advances in understanding and directing thrombopoiesis (Wendling and Tambourin 1991; Kato et al. 1995; Bartley 1994; Lok et al. 1994). By 1999, recombinant human TPO (rhTPO) was successfully produced in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells and purified for research and clinical use (Kaszubska et al. 2000), providing a critical cytokine for ex vivo megakaryocyte differentiation and in vitro PLP generation. However, despite its proven efficacy, rhTPO remains one of the costliest components in hematopoietic and megakaryocyte culture systems and constitutes a major driver of the overall production expense for stem‐cell‐derived PLPs. Thus, establishing efficient in‐house rhTPO expression and purification represents a practical strategy to reduce biomanufacturing cost and enhance scalability.

In this study, we establish a simple, non‐genetic, and cost‐effective platform for scalable platelet production from human cord blood–derived CD34+ cells using lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) supplementation. We further demonstrate that a locally manufactured recombinant thrombopoietin produced in Escherichia coli (W‐rhTPO) supports hematopoietic stem/progenitor cell differentiation into megakaryocytes with comparable efficiency to commercially available rhTPO. This approach substantially reduces production costs while maintaining biological efficacy, thereby providing a robust and GMP‐compatible framework for large‐scale platelet manufacturing and future clinical translation.

Materials and Methods

Isolation of Human Hematopoietic Stem/Progenitor Cells From Umbilical Cord Blood

Megakaryocytes were differentiated from HSPCs using the following procedure. Umbilical cord blood was diluted with phosphate buffer saline (PBS) at a 1:1 ratio, and gently layered onto LymphoprepTM (STEMCELL Technologies, Canada), and then centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 20 min with break. Mononuclear cells located between plasma and LymphoprepTM layers were harvested, washed twice with PBS, and centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min. HSPCs were isolated from mononuclear cells using a CD34 MicroBead Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). These CD34+ HSPCs were subsequently used in megakaryocyte‐inducing procedure.

Two‐Phase Megakaryocyte‐Inducing Procedure

A two‐phase cytokine‐based culture system was used to direct CD34+ HSPCs toward the CD41+ megakaryocyte lineage (Figure 1A). During Phase Ia (day 0–10), CD34+ HSPCs underwent initial expansion under high stem cell factor (SCF; 100 ng/mL, R&D Systems, USA) and low thrombopoietin (TPO; 30 ng/mL, R&D Systems, USA) conditions supplemented with 0.5 U/mL erythropoietin (EPO; Roche, Switzerland) to preserve progenitor potential. Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL, and the culture medium was completely refreshed with fresh Phase Ia medium every 3 days.

Phase Ib was designed to support lineage commitment and robust megakaryocyte proliferation through balanced supplementation with SCF and TPO (50 ng/mL each) together with 0.5 U/mL EPO. Cells were cultured in Phase Ib medium for an additional 3 days (day 10–13) at a starting density of 5 × 105 cells/mL following a complete medium change.

From day 13 onward (Phase II), a complete medium change was performed to promote terminal megakaryocyte maturation and platelet release using TPO alone (100 ng/mL). During this phase, megakaryocytes typically required 2–5 days to form proplatelets and release platelet‐like particles (PLPs). Half‐medium changes were subsequently performed every 2 days.

All cultures were maintained in StemSpan™ SFEM (STEMCELL Technologies, Canada) supplemented with 0.4% lipid‐ and cholesterol‐rich supplement derived from adult bovine serum (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) and 1% Penicillin–Streptomycin (HyClone™, USA). This stepwise culture strategy recapitulates the progressive phases of megakaryopoiesis observed in vivo and provides a defined platform for subsequent modulation experiments.

LPA Preparation and Supplementation

Lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) was purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich, USA (product number L7260) and prepared as 2.5 mM stock solutions in PBS with the present of 0.1% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA). The prepared solution was then aliquoted and stored at −20°C until use. Before supplementation, the stock solution was diluted in culture medium to a final concentration of 10 µM.

Sorting and Long‐Term Expansion of CD41+ HSPCs

At the end of Phase Ib of the megakaryocyte‐inducing procedure, CD41+ megakaryocytes were sorted to determine whether LPA can support the prolonged expansion of these cells in Phase Ib medium. The sorted CD41+ cells were washed twice with PBS, centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 5 min and resuspended with 100 μL PBS per 106 cells. The cells were then labeled with anti‐human CD41 antibody (BioLegend, USA), conjugated with APC (2 µL per 106 cells) and incubated for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. The cells were washed and resuspended with PBS supplemented with 2% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) and 50 µg/mL Gentamycin (Sartorius, Germany) and sorted for CD41+ cells by using BD FACSAria™ Fusion Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). These sorted cells were washed and re‐seeded in Phase Ib medium at the concentration of 2 × 104 cells/mL, cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C.

Flow Cytometric Analysis of CD41+/CD42b+ Cells and Platelet‐Like Particles (PLPs)

To observe the development of megakaryocytes during the differentiation process, the expression of megakaryocyte and platelet‐related markers could be determined by flow cytometry. The procedure was performed as previously described by Lorthongpanich et al (Lorthongpanich et al. 2020). Briefly, 100 µL of cell suspension was added to a BD Trucount™ Tube (BD Biosciences, USA) and 2 µL of APC‐conjugated anti‐human CD41 and/or CD42b antibody (BioLegend, USA), incubated for 30 min at 4°C, and then resuspended in 300 µL of PBS. Megakaryocyte and PLPs were evaluated by expression of CD41/CD42b cell‐surface expression, and were distinguished according to forward scatter (FSC‐A) and side scatter (SSC‐A) properties, as determined using human peripheral blood platelets as reference controls.

Functional Test of Platelet‐Like Particles (PLPs) Derived From CD34+ HSPCs

In the present study, the self‐aggregation ability of PLPs derived from megakaryocytes was evaluated without the addition of human peripheral blood platelets to enhance aggregation. Prior to the assay, PLPs were separated from residual megakaryocytes and cellular debris by sequential low‐speed centrifugation. Briefly, cultured cells were first centrifuged at 800 rpm for 5 min to pellet megakaryocytes and large cells, and the supernatant containing PLPs was carefully collected and transferred to a 5‐mL polystyrene round‐bottom tube (BD Biosciences, USA). The collected PLPs were then pelleted by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 10 min, as previously described (Lu et al. 2011). A total of 2 × 106 purified PLPs from each sample were resuspended in 300 μL of platelet‐depleted plasma containing 0.5 U/mL thrombin (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) and incubated at 37°C with shaking at 1,200 rpm to induce aggregation. After aggregation, the platelet aggregates were collected and weighed to compare aggregation efficiency among samples.

Production of a Human Recombinant TPO From Escherichia coli

The standard protocol for producing thrombopoietin (TPO) was adapted to produce a recombinant fusion protein (Imsoonthornruksa et al. 2015). The gene encoding human TPO (Genbank accession number: NM_001290003) was codon‐optimized for expression in E. coli and synthesized by GenScript Biotech Corporation (Singapore). This optimized sequence was subcloned into a pET32a prokaryotic expression vector, which was then used to transform ClearColi® BL21(DE3) E. coli expression strains (Lucigen).

An isolated colony was grown in 100 mL of LB broth with 0.1 mg/mL ampicillin at 37°C with shaking (200 rpm) overnight. A 25 mL aliquot of this culture was used to inoculate 1000 mL of LB broth containing ampicillin (0.1 mg/mL). The culture was grown until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 0.6. The protein expression was then induced by adding isopropyl‐β‐d‐thiogalactoside (IPTG) to a final concentration of 0.5 mM. The culture was incubated for an additional 6 h at 20°C with shaking (200 rpm) before the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8,000 g for 20 min.

Protein Extraction and Purification

The cell pellet was resuspended in a lysis/binding buffer containing 20 mM sodium phosphate (pH 8.0), 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonylfluoride (PMSF), 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X‐100, 2 mM imidazole, and 200 µg/mL lysozyme. Cells were disrupted by sonication on ice for 5 min using a GE 100 ultrasonic processor. The soluble and insoluble fractions were separated by centrifugation at 11,700 × g for 10 min. The crude supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 µm filter and loaded onto a Ni‐NTA column (GE Healthcare, USA) that had been pre‐equilibrated with binding buffer (20 mM sodium phosphate (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 10 mM imidazole). After loading, the flow‐through was collected at a rate of 0.5 mL/min, and the column was washed with 10 mL of binding buffer. The fusion protein, Trx‐6xHis‐hTPO, was eluted from the column using an elution buffer (20 mM sodium phosphate (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, and 250 mM imidazole). To remove residual imidazole, the purified protein solution was transferred to a dialysis bag and dialyzed overnight against PBS buffer at 4°C, with two changes of buffer. Then, the Trx‐6xHis‐hTPO fusion proteins were cleaved with enterokinase protease (produced in‐house) during an 8‐h reaction at 28°C, and the Trx‐6xHis and hTPO fragments were separated using a second Ni‐NTA column. Protein concentration was determined using a Bradford assay, while protein purity was assessed by SDS‐PAGE analysis. The rhTPO was subsequently sterilized by filtration through a 0.2 µm filter and stored at 4°C.

Statistical Analysis

The Mann‐Whitney U test was used to compare non‐parametric variations between groups. The independent paired Student's t‐test was used to compare platelet differentiation between the control and treatment groups on each day. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All results are presented as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM).

Results

LPA Treatment Increases the Differentiation of Umbilical Cord Blood‐Derived HSPCs Into Megakaryocytes

Initially, we investigated the impact of lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) on the differentiation of human umbilical cord blood‐derived hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (CD34+ HSPCs) into megakaryocytes. This investigation utilized a two‐phase differentiation protocol that has proven effective in deriving mature megakaryocytes from the human megakaryoblast cell line and peripheral mononuclear cells as demonstrated previously (Lorthongpanich et al. 2017; Salunkhe and Papadopoulos 2015). In this study, CD34+ HSPCs were cultured in Phase‐I medium for 13 days (10 days in Phase Ia and 3 days in Phase Ib medium) to induce their differentiation into CD41+ megakaryocytes. Subsequently, they were transferred to Phase‐II to facilitate platelet production on culture days 14–15 (Figure 1A).

As expected, distinct morphological alterations were observed during Phase I of the megakaryocyte induction procedure. In this phase, CD34+ HSPCs generated several clusters composed of refractile cells that progressively increased in size towards the end of the culture period (day 15) (Figure 1A). These morphologies have previously been associated with the differentiation of human megakaryoblast MEG‐01 cells in our previous publication (Lorthongpanich et al. 2017, 2020).

The results demonstrated that LPA treatment during Phase Ia significantly increased both the percentage (Figure 1B) and absolute number (Figure 1C) of CD41+ megakaryocytes compared with untreated controls at Day 15. Specifically, LPA treatment during Phase Ia increased the proportion of CD41+ cells from approximately 17% to 24%, indicating enhanced megakaryocytic purity in addition to increased cell yield. Furthermore, when the duration of LPA treatment was extended to the end of Phase Ib, the percentages and absolute number of CD41+ megakaryocytes at the end of Phase I and Phase II of the megakaryocyte induction procedure were even higher compared to those treated with LPA only in Phase Ia (Figures 1B and 1C). Under this optimal condition (LPA treatment during Phases Ia and Ib), approximately 2.8 ± 0.5 × 107 CD41+ megakaryocytes were generated by Day 15 from the initial seeded CD34+ HSPCs.

In contrast, when LPA treatment was extended through Phase II, the absolute number of CD41+ megakaryocytes at Day 15 (1.2 ± 0.1 × 107 cells) was markedly lower than that observed in cultures treated with LPA only during Phase I (2.8 ± 0.5 × 107 cells), although the percentage of CD41+ cells remained comparable between the two conditions (Figures 1B and 1C). These findings indicate that prolonged LPA exposure beyond the expansion and commitment phase does not further enhance megakaryocyte production and may instead reduce overall megakaryocyte yield.

While CD41 represents early megakaryocytic differentiation, CD42b is associated with terminal megakaryocyte maturation (Takayama et al. 2008; Nishikii et al. 2015). Based on the results in Figures 1B and 1C, the condition showing the highest megakaryocyte yield and purity (LPA supplementation during Phases Ia and Ib) was selected for further evaluation of megakaryocyte maturation. To further evaluate the effect of LPA on megakaryocyte maturation, the proportion of CD42b+ cells was examined during differentiation (Figure 3D). LPA supplementation during Phases Ia and Ib does not significantly effected the percentage of CD42b+ cells. This result suggesting that the primary effect of LPA is the enhancement of early megakaryocytic expansion rather than direct promotion of terminal maturation.

We further demonstrated that LPA treatment induced YAP expression (Figure 1E and Supporting Figure 1). However, the increase in YAP expression did not strongly correlate with terminal megakaryocyte maturation, as only modest changes in the CD42b+ population were observed. These findings are consistent with our previous studies showing that increased YAP expression is associated with enhanced proliferation of megakaryoblastic cells (Lorthongpanich et al. 2017, 2020).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that LPA exposure during Phase I of the megakaryocyte induction protocol markedly promotes the differentiation of CD34+ HSPCs into CD41+ megakaryocytes, at least in part via activation of YAP signaling. Importantly, the combined analysis of CD41+ cell frequency and absolute megakaryocyte yield demonstrates that LPA enhances both the purity and production efficiency of megakaryocytes derived from CD34+ HSPCs. However, prolonged exposure of LPA beyond Phase I does not further improve differentiation and instead reduces the number of CD41+ megakaryocytes derived from HSPCs.

Phase‐Specific LPA Treatment Increases PLP Production Yield From Umbilical Cord Blood‐Derived HSPCs

Next, we examined the effect of LPA on platelet production. Similar to its enhancing effect on the megakaryocytic differentiation of CD34+ HSPCs, LPA treatment throughout Phase I (both plases Ia and Ib), followed by withdrawing during phase II, of the megakaryocytic induction procedure (Figure 2A) resulted in an approximately 15‐fold increase in the number of CD34+ HSPC‐derived platelets (52,340 ± 5,400 PLP/ml) compared with the untreated control (3,740 ± 265 PLP/ml; Figure 2B). Although prolonged LPA treatment through the end of Phase II also increased the number of CD34+ HSPC‐derived PLPs (18,230 ± 5,405 PLP/ml) compared with the untreated control (Figure 2B), the PLP yield was significantly lower than that obtained from CD34+ HSPCs exposed to LPA exclusively during Phase I (Phases Ia and Ib) of the megakaryocyte induction protocol (Figure 2B). The maturation status of LPA‐derived PLPs was further evaluated by assessing CD41 and CD42b expression. PLPs generated from LPA‐treated cultures exhibited significantly higher CD41/CD42b expression compared with untreated controls, suggesting that LPA treatment improves platelet quality and maturation (Figure 2C). Consistently, microscopic observation revealed numerous mature megakaryocytes displaying extended proplatelet processes and releasing small platelet‐like fragments into the medium in the LPA‐treated CD34+ HSPC cultures (Figure 2D). These findings indicate that LPA treatment throughout Phase I of the megakaryocyte induction procedure significantly enhances the PLP production yield from CD34+HSPCs. However, prolonged exposure of LPA beyond this phase decreases the PLP production yield of CD34+HSPCs. Collectively, these results suggest that treating CD34+HSPCs with LPA throughout Phase I, followed by LPA withdrawal treatment during Phase II, may represent an optimal strategy for enhancing PLP production from CD34+ HSPCs.

LPA Treatment Can Extend the Expansion Period of HSPC‐Derived Megakaryocytes, Leading to a Significantly Higher Number of Megakaryocytes

Increasing megakaryoblast number is important for enhancing platelet production from HSPCs. Therefore, we investigated whether LPA could extend the expansion period of HSPC‐derived megakaryocytes beyond 13 days of Phase I culture before platelet production in Phase II. To examine this, HSPC‐derived CD41+ megakaryocytes were isolated at the end of Phase Ia (Day 10) by flow cytometry and subsequently expanded in Phase Ib for an additional 8 (Day 10 + 8), 16 (Day 10 + 16), or 21 days (Day 10 + 21) in the presence or absence of LPA (Supporting Figure 2).

The total cell number as well as the numbers of CD41+ and CD41− cells were determined over time. Total cell numbers progressively declined during extended culture regardless of LPA supplementation (Supporting Figure 3A). However, LPA‐treated cultures maintained a relatively higher proportion of CD41+ cells compared with untreated controls at all observed time points (Supporting Figure 3B–D), indicating enrichment of the megakaryocytic population during prolonged culture. To further visualize the effect of LPA on CD41+ cell enrichment, pie charts were generated to illustrate the proportion of CD41+ and CD41− cells during extended culture (Figure 3A). In the control group, CD41+ cells accounted for only 6 ± 0.7% at Day 10 + 8, 4 ± 0.5% at Day 10 + 16, and 2 ± 0.4% at Day 10 + 21. In contrast, LPA‐treated cultures contained significantly higher proportions of CD41+ cells, reaching 41 ± 1.8%, 26 ± 2.7%, and 18 ± 1.1% at the respective time points. Consistently, LPA treatment significantly increased the absolute number of CD41+ megakaryocytes during prolonged Phase I expansion, with the greatest effect observed at Day 10 + 8, resulting in an approximately 20‐fold increase compared with untreated controls (Figure 3B). Although both the percentage and absolute number of CD41+ cells remained significantly higher in LPA‐treated cultures at Days 10 + 16 and 10 + 21, these values progressively declined over time. Platelet production showed a similar trend and was proportional to megakaryocyte numbers following transfer into Phase II medium without LPA treatment (Figure 3C). These findings demonstrate that LPA treatment transiently promotes the expansion and maintenance of HSPC‐derived CD41+ megakaryocytes during extended culture, with the strongest effect observed at Day 10 + 8.

Functional Evaluation of Platelet Aggregation

To evaluate the functional competence of platelets obtained from our megakaryocyte‐inducing procedure, thrombin‐induced aggregation assays were performed using PLPs derived from CD34+ HSPCs compared to the untreated control (Figure 4A). Thrombin serves as a physiological agonist that triggers platelet activation and aggregation, both of which are essential for their function. Upon thrombin stimulation, PLPs derived from LPA‐treated CD34+ HSPCs formed visible aggregates that were comparable in size to those derived from the untreated control (Figure 4A). Similar aggregation was observed when donor‐derived platelets were subjected to thrombin stimulation (Supporting Figure 4). This finding suggests that PLPs derived from LPA‐treated CD34+ HSPCs exhibit normal physiological function. Consistent with this observation, platelet aggregation was not observed in platelet‐depleted plasma or in the absence of thrombin (Figure 4A).

To quantitatively compare platelet aggregation, platelet aggregates were separated from plasma and weighed to determine their mass (Figure 4B). The aggregate weight of PLPs derived from LPA‐treated CD34+ HSPCs was not significantly different from that of the untreated group (Figure 4C). Nevertheless, both groups showed significantly higher aggregate weights compared with the platelet‐depleted plasma and no‐thrombin controls, indicating successful thrombin‐induced aggregation. These findings confirm that PLPs derived from LPA‐treated CD34+ HSPCs are functionally active and capable of thrombin‐induced aggregation, highlighting their potential for future clinical applications.

A Recombinant Human Thrombopoietin from E. coli ClearColi Supports Efficient Megakaryocyte Differentiation and Platelet Production From CD34+ HSPCs

Building upon the optimized megakaryocyte‐inducing procedure established in previous experiments, we sought to further reduce the cost of platelet production by replacing commercial human recombinant thrombopoietin (C‐rhTPO) with a more cost‐effective recombinant human TPO produced from Escherichia coli. Given that TPO constitutes one of the most expensive components in an in vitro platelet production process, the development of a more affordable source of TPO is crucial to minimize the cost of large‐scale platelet production.

Recombinant human thrombopoietin (W‐rhTPO) was produced in Escherichia coli ClearColi using a pET‐based expression system (Figure 5A). The recombinant protein was expressed as a Trx‐hTPO fusion, purified by Ni2+ affinity chromatography, and enzymatically cleaved to obtain biologically active W‐rhTPO (Figure 5B). To compare the biological activity of W‐rhTPO with that of C‐rhTPO, CD34+ HSPCs were differentiated into megakaryocytes using the two‐phase megakaryocyte‐inducing procedure. The results demonstrated that W‐rhTPO robustly enhanced the megakaryocytic differentiation of CD34+ HSPCs, resulting in a comparable number of CD41+ megakaryocytes and platelet production yields compared to C‐rhTPO (Figure 5C‐D).

These findings confirm that our W‐rhTPO produced from Escherichia coli exhibits similar thrombopoietic activity compared to C‐rhTPO and could potentially be used to develop a more cost‐effective large‐scale platelet production from CD34+ HSPCs in the future.

Discussion

The shortage of blood platelets for clinical transfusions remains a significant challenge worldwide (Garraud et al. 2023; Stubbs et al. 2022). Although advances have been made in the manufacturing of human platelets from renewable stem cell sources, current procedures have been characterized by complexity, inefficiency, and high cost. This study presents an optimized strategy for cost‐effective in vitro platelet production from human hematopoietic stem cells (HSPCs) through a combination of phase‐specific chemical modulation, mechanical stimulation, and cost‐effective substitution of cytokines. In this study, modifications are introduced to the widely used three‐phase megakaryocyte induction procedure (Salunkhe and Papadopoulos 2015) to improve its efficiency and cost‐effectiveness in generating CD41+ megakaryocytes and platelets from human CD34+ HSPCs.

We modulate the activity of the Hippo‐YAP/TAZ signaling pathway during the first phase of the megakaryocyte‐inducing procedure to enhance the differentiation of CD34+ HSPCs into CD41+ megakaryocytes. This is achieved by using lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) which is an activator of YAP/TAZ activity (Yasuda et al. 2019). Several previous studies, including our own, have demonstrated the pivotal role of the Hippo‐YAP/TAZ signaling pathway in megakaryopoiesis (Lorthongpanich et al. 2017, 2020; Roy et al. 2016; Suraneni and Crispino 2016; Hu et al. 2024; Qiao 2023). Specifically, alterations in its activity during various phases of human megakaryocytic differentiation are crucial for efficient platelet production (Lorthongpanich et al. 2020). Our previous research indicates that while an increase in YAP/TAZ activity promotes the proliferation of megakaryoblast MEG‐01 cells, its downregulation is essential to facilitate efficient platelet production from these cells (Lorthongpanich et al. 2017).

Consistent with this, our present study demonstrates that increasing YAP/TAZ activity by LPA treatment throughout Phase I of the megakaryocyte induction procedure significantly enhances the differentiation of CD34+ HSPCs into CD41+ megakaryocytes, resulting in an approximately 15‐fold increase in platelet production yield at the end of the procedure. LPA likely acts through the YAP/TAZ–RhoA signaling axis, increasing cytoskeletal contractility during early expansion and allowing relaxation after its withdrawal, resulting in increased proplatelet formation (Nardone et al. 2017; Dasgupta and McCollum 2019). Mechanistically, LPA is known to activate RhoA signaling, which promotes actin polymerization and cytoskeletal tension, leading to suppression of Hippo kinase‐mediated YAP/TAZ phosphorylation and subsequent YAP/TAZ activation. Cytoskeletal remodeling plays a critical role in megakaryocyte maturation and proplatelet formation, processes that require dynamic actin and microtubule reorganization. In addition, Rho GTPases, including RhoA, have been reported to function as key regulators of megakaryocyte maturation and platelet biogenesis through modulation of cytoskeletal dynamics (Pleines et al. 2019; Heib et al. 2021). Furthermore, previous studies have suggested that Hippo‐YAP/TAZ signaling remains active during megakaryocyte polyploidization and maturation, potentially linking mechanotransduction pathways with megakaryopoiesis (Roy et al. 2016). Therefore, the enhanced megakaryocyte expansion observed following temporal LPA supplementation may, in part, be mediated through modulation of the RhoA‐actin‐YAP/TAZ axis. However, the precise molecular mechanisms linking LPA‐induced YAP activation to proplatelet formation and platelet biogenesis remain to be further investigated.

Similar to our previous research on MEG‐01 cells (Lorthongpanich et al. 2017), prolonged exposure of LPA to Phase II, a platelet production phase, reduced the platelet production yield of CD34+HSPCs. Consequently, treating CD34+HSPCs with LPA throughout Phase I of the megakaryocyte‐inducing procedure and discontinuing LPA treatment during Phase II may be the most effective strategy for enhancing the platelet production yield of CD34+HSPCs. Future transcriptomic and proteomic profiling will facilitate the identification of the molecular mechanisms underlying these transitions and the discovery of additional targets for optimization.

Beyond mechanistic optimization, it is also important to evaluate how the present strategy compares with existing in vitro thrombopoiesis platforms. Compared with previous in vitro thrombopoiesis platforms, the present two‐phases LPA strategy appears to improve overall platelet production primarily through expansion of the megakaryocyte pool rather than enhancement of platelet output per individual megakaryocyte. Earlier pluripotent stem cell–based approaches, including embryonic stem cell (ESC)‐, induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)‐, and forward‐programmed human pluripotent stem cell (hPSC)‐derived megakaryocyte systems, have generally been limited by low platelet yield, high production costs, and challenges in large‐scale manufacturing (Takayama et al. 2008; Lu et al. 2011; Takayama et al. 2010). Similarly, immortalized megakaryoblast cell lines such as MEG‐01 provide useful mechanistic models for studying Hippo‐YAP/TAZ signaling but remain limited in thrombopoietic efficiency (Lorthongpanich et al. 2017, 2020). In contrast, the present protocol achieved substantial expansion of the CD41+ megakaryocyte population together with an approximately 15‐fold increase in total platelet production following stage‐specific LPA supplementation. These findings suggest that temporal modulation of Hippo‐YAP/TAZ signaling may partially overcome an important bottleneck that has constrained previous differentiation systems lacking dynamic signaling control.

To explore whether the expansion phase of HSPC‐derived megakaryocytes could be prolonged, we established an extended LPA‐supported culture system following Phase Ia differentiation. Although the extended culture system did not generate higher platelet yields than the two‐phases differentiation protocol, the objective of this approach was distinct from maximizing cumulative platelet production. Instead, this strategy was designed to determine whether HSPC‐derived megakaryocytic progenitors could be maintained beyond the conventional differentiation period while preserving platelet‐producing capacity. Notably, total cell numbers progressively declined during prolonged culture regardless of LPA supplementation, suggesting that the reduced yield observed at later time points was primarily due to the limited proliferative capacity of megakaryocytic cells under extended in vitro culture conditions rather than the cell sorting procedure itself. Nevertheless, LPA treatment transiently maintained a higher proportion and number of CD41+ megakaryocytic cells, particularly at the Day 10 + 8 stage, indicating that LPA may help preserve the megakaryocytic population during extended culture. In contrast to the standard protocol, which requires re‐initiation of the full HSPC differentiation process ( ~ 15 days) for each production cycle, the extended culture system maintains a pool of expandable CD41+ megakaryocytic progenitors at the Day 10 + 8 stage that could potentially be cryopreserved for long‐term storage and subsequently thawed and cultured in Phase II medium for rapid platelet production when needed. Therefore, this approach may provide a practical platform for flexible or emergency platelet manufacturing rather than replacing the original high‐yield differentiation protocol. However, the progressive decline in CD41+ cell expansion and platelet production during prolonged culture suggests that LPA treatment alone is insufficient to sustain long‐term megakaryocyte progenitor expansion. Further optimization of the culture system, potentially through additional cytokines or signaling modulators, may be required to support prolonged megakaryocyte self‐renewal and improve downstream platelet production.

In this study, we replaced commercial recombinant human thrombopoietin (C‐rhTPO), one of the most expensive components in in vitro platelet production, with a recombinant W‐rhTPO produced from Escherichia coli to improve the cost‐effectiveness of the culture system. Our results demonstrated that the substantially less expensive W‐rhTPO exhibited thrombopoietic activity comparable to C‐rhTPO and effectively supported megakaryocytic differentiation and platelet production from CD34+ HSPCs. Although bacterial‐derived recombinant proteins may possess greater immunogenic potential due to the absence of mammalian post‐translational modifications and possible residual bacterial components, W‐rhTPO in this system was used only as a culture supplement during ex vivo platelet production, and downstream washing procedures would be expected to minimize residual contaminants in the final platelet product. These findings support the feasibility of W‐rhTPO as a cost‐effective alternative for scalable platelet manufacturing in the future.

While the protocol significantly improved total platelet yield, platelet productivity at the level of individual megakaryocytes remains substantially lower than physiological thrombopoiesis observed in vivo, where a single megakaryocyte can generate thousands of platelets. This limitation likely reflects the absence of critical bone marrow microenvironmental factors, including shear stress and vascular niche‐associated biomechanical signaling, which are difficult to fully reproduce in static culture systems. Furthermore, the current approach relies on primary umbilical cord blood‐derived CD34+ HSPCs, which possess finite proliferative capacity and donor‐dependent variability, potentially limiting scalability compared with renewable immortalized cell sources. Finally, characterization of the generated platelet‐like particles remained relatively limited and was primarily based on phenotypic marker expression and aggregation assays. Additional ultrastructural, transcriptomic, and in vivo functional studies will therefore be necessary to further evaluate platelet quality and translational potential.

Despite several countries having initiated clinical trials for in vitro‐generated platelets, high production costs and the reliance on genetic modification pose significant challenges to global implementation (Moreau et al. 2016; Sugimoto and Kanda 2022; Kayama and Eto 2024). Our optimized in vitro platelet production procedure, characterized by simplicity, robustness, and cost‐effectiveness, provides a promising framework for future adaptation toward large‐scale GMP‐compliant platelet manufacturing. This system may facilitate more scalable platelet generation, including in resource‐constrained environments, to increase human platelet supply for numerous life‐saving therapies.

Conclusion

In summary, we establish a phase‐specific Hippo–YAP/TAZ modulation strategy as a simple, non‐genetic, and cost‐effective platform for efficient in vitro platelet‐like particles production from human hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells. Precise temporal regulation of Hippo–YAP/TAZ signaling by lysophosphatidic acid markedly enhanced megakaryocyte differentiation, expansion, and platelet yield. Importantly, replacing commercially available recombinant thrombopoietin with a locally produced, Escherichia coli–derived W‐rhTPO substantially reduced production costs without compromising biological efficacy. Collectively, this optimized and scalable platform, defined by its robustness, simplicity, and economic feasibility, provides a practical foundation for large‐scale platelet manufacturing and supports future translational development.
