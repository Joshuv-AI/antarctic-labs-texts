Stroke‐Mimic: An Intracranial Dural Arteriovenous Fistula Revealed by New‐Onset Seizures and Post‐Ictal Deficit
Authors: Michael Van Haute, Mary Martin, Mark Harmon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntracranial dural arteriovenous fistulas (iDAVFs) are rare vascular malformations with diverse neurological symptoms depending on their venous drainage pattern. We describe a 59‐year‐old man who presented with transient right‐sided weakness and facial droop following new‐onset seizures. CT demonstrated asymmetric arterial‐phase enhancement of the left transverse sinus and cortical veins, while MRI revealed left occipitotemporal and cerebellar hemorrhages consistent with venous congestion. Digital subtraction angiography confirmed a high‐grade left anterior condylar iDAVF with cortical venous reflux (Borden II; Cognard IIa+b). Endovascular embolization was unsuccessful because of challenging vascular anatomy. Definitive management was achieved through surgical disconnection and targeted sinus coiling. This case highlights the diverse presentations of iDAVFs and emphasizes the importance of considering a vascular etiology in patients with atypical stroke‐like or postictal deficits.

Introduction

Intracranial dural arteriovenous fistulas (iDAVFs) are pathologic shunts wherein branches of the external carotid artery (ECA) drain directly into dural venous sinuses. They are typically acquired lesions presenting between the fifth and sixth decades of life. While they account for 10%–15% of intracranial vascular malformations [1], the annual incidence is low (0.15–0.29 per 100,000) [2], making diagnosis challenging and occasionally delayed or missed [3]. The presence of cortical venous drainage (CVD) indicates impaired dural sinus outflow due to high‐flow shunting or downstream stenosis, often associated with prior dural sinus thrombosis [4]. Resultant venous hypertension increases the risk of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) and non‐hemorrhagic neurologic deficits (NHND) [5, 6].

We report a patient who presented with headache progressing to seizure and stroke‐like presentation with transient right‐sided weakness and persistent right facial droop. Initial neuroimaging described asymmetric early enhancements of the left transverse sinus and associated cortical veins. Subsequent imaging demonstrated left occipitotemporal and cerebellar hemorrhages likely secondary to venous congestion. These findings were strongly suggestive of a high‐grade iDAVF supplied predominantly by the left ascending pharyngeal artery. Endovascular embolization was unsuccessful due to challenging access. A new left occipital lobe hemorrhage later developed, consistent with venous hypertension in the iDAVF's draining territory, necessitating surgical disconnection.

Case Presentation

Case History/Examination

A 59‐year‐old right‐handed male presented to a tertiary hospital in Dublin in November 2023 with right‐sided weakness and facial droop. Two weeks prior, he described a non‐traumatic frontal headache with vomiting, without fever or visual disturbance. The headache resolved spontaneously but recurred on the day of presentation after work, when he became unresponsive at the dinner table with a blank stare, prompting emergency services. During transport, he developed a generalized tonic–clonic seizure.

His medical history includes congestive heart failure, atrial fibrillation with previous direct current cardioversion, and long‐standing left‐sided exotropia. He had no cognitive impairment at baseline. His regular medications were apixaban 5 mg twice daily, bisoprolol 7.5 mg once daily, ramipril 5 mg once daily, and digoxin 125 μg once daily.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Upon arrival at hospital, his National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) was 11 (level of consciousness +1; month, age +2; open/close eyes, grip +2; horizontal extraocular movements +1; facial palsy +2; arm drift +1; language/aphasia +1; dysarthria +1), prompting urgent brain computed tomography (CT) with CT angiogram (CTA) ~1–2 h after seizure onset. No evolving territorial infarct or large‐vessel occlusion was identified. Arterial‐phase imaging demonstrated asymmetric early enhancement of the left transverse sinus and cortical veins with no appreciable nidus, raising suspicion of an arteriovenous malformation (AVM) or iDAVF (Figure 1). His right‐sided weakness eventually resolved. Apixaban was held without reversal and levetiracetam was initiated. He was clinically stable and repatriated the same day to our hospital, which serves the patient's catchment area, for ongoing care.

On arrival at our emergency department (~18 h after initial presentation), his Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) was 14 and NIHSS was 4 (month, age +1; horizontal extraocular movements +1; facial palsy +2). He was oriented to person but demonstrated spatial disorientation and partial temporal disorientation, being unable to state the current month or year. His vital signs remained stable. Although the right‐sided weakness resolved and he was independently mobile, the confusion and right facial droop persisted, prompting consultation of the acute stroke unit. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), performed ~23 h after the initial CT/CTA, demonstrated left occipitotemporal and left cerebellar hemorrhages, multiple microhemorrhages, and mild oedema with pressure in the left lateral ventricle and posterior left midbrain, on the background of moderate supratentorial white matter signal changes (Figure 2). Neuroradiologist review of imaging identified an iDAVF with associated venous congestion, accounting for the acute parenchymal hemorrhages.

MRI manifestations of venous congestion secondary to intracranial dural arteriovenous fistula. (A) T2/FLAIR image demonstrating temporo‐occipital white matter hyperintensity on the left (white arrows), with subtle effacement of the adjacent sulci and the temporal horn of the lateral ventricle compared with the contralateral side, consistent with venous congestion due to impaired venous drainage. (B) Gradient echo image demonstrating gyriform, rail‐like cortical siderosis (gray arrow) and multiple microhemorrhages (black arrow), in keeping with venous hypertension.

The patient was promptly transferred to a tertiary neurosurgical and interventional neuroradiology center. Urgent digital subtraction angiography (DSA), performed ~2 days after initial presentation, demonstrated a high‐grade left anterior condylar iDAVF (Borden II; Cognard IIa+b), supplied predominantly by branches of the ECA, including the ascending pharyngeal, middle meningeal (posterior branch), transosseous occipital, and meningohypophyseal arteries. The fistula drained retrogradely into a trapped isolated segment of the left transverse sinus with marked cortical venous reflux over the left parieto‐occipital lobe (Figure 3A). Left internal carotid artery injection demonstrated additional supply from the tentorial artery of Bernasconi and Cassinari (Figure 3B). Endovascular embolization under general anesthesia was attempted with a plan to access the fistulous point. The procedure was technically challenging because of bovine arch anatomy limiting transfemoral access to the left common carotid artery, preventing selective catheterization of the condylar vein at the fistulous point despite combined transvenous and trans‐arterial approaches. As the fistulous connection cannot be accessed, embolic agents were not deployed and complete embolization was unsuccessful. Following intraprocedural systemic heparinization, the procedure was terminated without complications. The patient awoke without new deficits.

The following day, aphasia with GCS 8 developed acutely. Emergency CT showed a new 4‐cm left occipital parenchymal hemorrhage with adjacent vasogenic oedema and petechial hemorrhages, consistent with venous hypertension in the iDAVF's draining territory. The patient underwent surgical disconnection via left suboccipital craniotomy and C1 posterior arch resection and was subsequently admitted to the ICU on ventilatory support. However, post‐operative angiography demonstrated persistent filling of the iDAVF with continued early opacification of the cortical veins and left transverse sinus, indicating residual shunt flow (Figure 4).

(A) Non‐contrast CT demonstrating interval intraparenchymal hematoma within the left temporo‐occipital region with surrounding oedema (black arrow). (B) Reformatted arterial‐phase CT angiogram with adjusted windowing demonstrating persistent early arterial filling of the left transverse sinus (white arrow) compared with the contralateral side, consistent with residual arteriovenous shunting.

Following urgent multidisciplinary neurovascular discussion, consensus was reached to sacrifice the isolated left transverse sinus, which was functionally excluded from normal cerebral venous drainage and remained a source of cortical venous reflux and venous hypertension despite prior surgery. Angiography confirmed that the affected transverse sinus represented a trapped, isolated venous segment without contribution to normal cerebral venous outflow, with adequate contralateral and collateral venous drainage through the remaining dural sinus system, supporting the safety of sacrifice. Repeat DSA and embolization were performed the next day, with coils deployed in the lateral left transverse sinus to occlude the fistulous draining vein. The procedure was completed without complications. Post‐coiling angiography demonstrated resolution of cortical venous reflux, with minimal sluggish residual filling of the fistulous vein that progressively regressed on final angiographic runs (Figure 5). An external ventricular drain was inserted for mild hydrocephalus that developed on the first post‐operative day. The patient was eventually extubated and transferred to the general ward where the drain was subsequently removed. Although his GCS improved to 11, aphasia persisted until transfer back to our hospital. No interval follow‐up DSA was performed, thus formal angiographic confirmation of durable occlusion is unavailable. However, serial CT imaging demonstrated progressive resolution of the ICHs without new hemorrhage or hydrocephalus. Anticoagulation was not restarted.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Prior to presentation, the patient worked as a farmer with no functional limitations (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] 0). Following repatriation to our hospital, he had right facial droop, right homonymous hemianopia, left sensory neglect, and inability to follow one‐step commands. Tone and power were normal throughout. He remained dysphasic, requiring nasogastric tube feeding, was incontinent of bowel and bladder, and initially required hoist‐assisted transfers.

He subsequently underwent prolonged inpatient rehabilitation, spending ~19 weeks in our hospital before transfer to a specialized rehabilitation facility for a further 12 weeks. He progressed to following two‐step commands and mobilizing with a four‐wheel walker under supervision, regaining a normal swallow and full continence. However, his right facial droop, right homonymous hemianopia, and left sensory neglect persisted. He also had residual expressive and receptive dysphasia with cognitive impairment (Abbreviated Mental Test Score 0/4 for location, date of birth, age, and year) and required supervision and prompting for activities of daily living (ADLs) and medication management. He continues to live at home with his brother, supported by his sister and a home care package, but has been unable to return to work since.

At follow‐up ~23 months after presentation, the patient's mRS was 3, reflecting moderate disability with independent ambulation under supervision and the need for assistance with some ADLs. Since the index admission, he has required only one subsequent hospitalization for urosepsis.

Discussion

IDAVFs are vascular shunts between dural arteries and venous sinuses or leptomeningeal veins that lack a parenchymal nidus [7]. Although considered rare, increasing use of multiphase CT/CTA and MRI has improved detection and recognition. In adults, iDAVFs most commonly involve the transverse‐sigmoid sinus (50%) and cavernous sinus (16%) [8, 9]. Most are thought to be acquired, with cerebral venous sinus thrombosis representing the principal pathogenic mechanism [7, 10, 11]. Less common causes include trauma, prior surgery, and inflammatory or infectious processes [12, 13]. Hypercoagulable states may predispose to the thrombotic subtype [14, 15]. Most adult‐onset iDAVFs are asymptomatic at the time of diagnosis. Clinical manifestations range from relatively benign symptoms such as pulsatile tinnitus and headache to aggressive presentations including ICH, seizures, and NHNDs [7, 16]. Lesion behavior is determined primarily by venous drainage architecture rather than arterial supply, with cortical venous reflux representing the principal predictor of hemorrhage and neurological morbidity [4]. Symptomatology also generally correlates with the anatomical location of the fistula (e.g., pulsatile tinnitus with lesions near the auditory apparatus; ophthalmologic findings with cavernous sinus involvement) [17, 18].

In the present case, neuroimaging demonstrated abnormalities that could account for the neurological deficits, yet the right‐sided weakness resolved within hours of presentation, mimicking a transient ischemic attack [19, 20]. Although Todd's paralysis following seizure is the most likely explanation, transient neurological deficits are well recognized in iDAVFs and may result from venous hypertension and altered venous drainage patterns [21, 22]. Unlike arterial ischemia, venous hypertension may cause reversible neurological dysfunction through impaired cerebral perfusion [23]. Fluctuations in venous hypertension may further contribute to transient symptoms [24]. The hemodynamic balance in iDAVFs is fragile, and even minor alterations in flow dynamics may exacerbate venous hypertension and precipitate hemorrhage. Unlike AVMs, which typically drain into cortical veins, iDAVFs often involve dural venous sinuses that can initially accommodate higher pressures, potentially delaying diagnosis until hemorrhagic risk is greater. Consequently, management is guided by lesion grade; high‐grade fistulas with cortical venous reflux generally require urgent treatment while low‐grade lesions may be managed conservatively. Another mechanism of transient neurological dysfunction is the “steal phenomenon,” whereby arterial blood is diverted from adjacent brain tissue towards the fistula [25].

Diagnosis of iDAVFs can be challenging because findings on routine neuroimaging may be subtle [26]. Accordingly, unexplained subarachnoid or lobar hemorrhage, as well as indeterminate radiological findings, should prompt consideration [7]. Initial assessment includes CT and MRI, although diagnosis may be challenging because non‐contrast CT primarily detects complications like hemorrhage and venous congestive oedema, while routine non‐vascular MRI may be unrevealing unless retrograde leptomeningeal venous drainage is present [27]. Asymmetric or enlarged occipital artery flow voids on T2‐weighted sequences are an important but often overlooked clue to iDAVFs. Caused by rapidly flowing blood, they are typically seen on T2‐weighted and fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences and may be accentuated on susceptibility‐weighted imaging/angiography (SWI/SWAN). Clustering adjacent to a dural venous sinus should prompt suspicion for iDAVF and further vascular imaging [28]. SWI/SWAN can detect characteristic venous congestion changes in iDAVFs, including the reversible “hypointense focal brain” sign within regions of CVD, supporting its role in the assessment of suspected lesions [29]. Imaging findings vary by lesion grade. Low‐grade iDAVFs typically demonstrate clustered flow voids, whereas high‐grade lesions more often exhibit dilated feeding arteries, enlarged draining veins, prominent pial vessels, and venous hypertension‐related changes, including white matter hyperintensity, oedema, hemorrhage, and venous infarction [7, 27, 30]. When iDAVFs are suspected, further evaluation with vascular imaging, including CTA, time‐of‐flight MRA (TOF‐MRA), and ultimately DSA, is recommended [31]. CTA and MRA may demonstrate dilated tortuous vessels, sinus enlargement, or sinus occlusion. CTA is particularly useful for treatment planning because it delineates the arteriovenous shunt relative to adjacent osseous and parenchymal structures [8, 32]. MRA may demonstrate early sinus filling and enlarged pial vessels, whereas venous hypertensive changes appear as T2/FLAIR hyperintensities on conventional MRI and are associated with cortical venous reflux, venous obstruction, and aggressive clinical behavior [28, 31]. Vasogenic oedema is a reversible process and an independent predictor of future neurological risk [28]. Despite its invasiveness, DSA remains the gold standard for diagnosis because it characterizes fistula anatomy, arterial supply, sinus patency, and retrograde leptomeningeal venous drainage, thereby guiding treatment planning [29, 32].

TOF‐MRA is particularly relevant in the evaluation of suspected iDAVFs. TOF‐MRA is a non‐contrast sequence commonly included in acute stroke MRI protocols. Hyperintense arterialized signal within a dural venous sinus or cortical vein strongly suggests pathological arteriovenous shunting and may provide an early clue to iDAVFs before contrast‐enhanced or catheter angiography is performed [33, 34]. Diagnostic performance showed excellent agreement with DSA for fistula localization (κ = 0.968) and good agreement for arterial feeders (κ = 0.809) and venous drainage patterns (κ = 0.837), with overall accuracy comparable to time‐resolved MRA [35, 36]. Recent compressed‐sensing techniques halve acquisition time while maintaining high sensitivity (97.3%) and specificity (94.7%) for detecting cerebral arteriovenous shunts [37]. Additionally, increased signal intensity within draining cortical veins correlates with aggressive iDAVF presentation and may provide prognostic information (AUC 0.909) [38]. Abnormal venous hyperintensity on TOF‐MRA may aid early recognition of high‐grade iDAVFs in patients presenting with seizures or stroke‐like symptoms [39, 40].

Arterial spin labeling (ASL) MRI is another useful non‐invasive adjunct, demonstrating abnormal venous signal and cortical hyperperfusion related to arteriovenous shunting, and may identify hemodynamic abnormalities when structural MRI is inconclusive [41]. Venous ASL signal shows high sensitivity (94%) and specificity (88%) for iDAVF detection and improves diagnostic performance when combined with conventional MRI sequences [41, 42, 43]. It also demonstrates signal changes that resolve following treatment, reflecting hemodynamic normalization and supporting its role in both diagnosis and post‐treatment surveillance [44, 45]. ASL additionally demonstrates high accuracy for detecting residual post‐treatment arteriovenous shunting relative to DSA, although interpretation requires awareness of mimics such as jugular venous reflux. In equivocal cases, asymmetric enlargement of ECA branches on TOF‐MRA may help distinguish true iDAVFs from benign reflux flow [46, 47, 48, 49].

CVD distinguishes high‐ from low‐grade lesions, is strongly associated with aggressive clinical behavior, and forms the basis of the Borden and Cognard classification systems [5, 6]. The Cognard system groups iDAVFs into seven categories based on fistula location, flow direction (anterograde or retrograde), presence of CVD, and venous outflow architecture, including venous ectasia [22]. CVD is absent in Cognard I and IIa; these lesions typically follow a benign course, although Cognard IIa is associated with a 20% risk of intracranial hypertension. In contrast, Cognard IIb–IV are high‐grade lesions characterized by CVD, with Cognard IV lesions demonstrating direct CVD with venous ectasia. Cognard V involves drainage into spinal perimedullary veins and is associated with progressive myelopathy in approximately 50% of cases. The Borden system is simpler and describes lesions based on the presence of CVD as well as site and direction of venous drainage [21]. Borden I (equivalent to Cognard I and IIa) iDAVFs are low‐grade lesions and have extremely low risk of ICH [50]. In contrast, Borden II and III are considered high‐grade lesions with aggressive behavior in 39% and 79% of cases, respectively [6]. The primary treatment objective is complete and durable obliteration of the arteriovenous shunt, particularly in high‐grade lesions with CVD [5, 6, 22]. Management is individualized based on angiographic grade, venous drainage pattern, symptomatology, and patient factors [24]. Endovascular therapy is first‐line for most lesions due to its minimally invasive nature and high efficacy. Trans‐arterial embolization using liquid embolic agents such as n‐butyl cyanoacrylate or ethylene‐vinyl alcohol copolymer is preferred when arterial feeders provide safe access to the shunt, while transvenous embolization is favored when the arterial route is unsafe or ineffective, particularly in cavernous sinus fistulas and isolated sinus lesions [6, 51]. In select cases, a combined approach may be required.

Microsurgical disconnection remains an important option when endovascular therapy is infeasible or unsuccessful [52], particularly in craniocervical junction iDAVFs where complex angioarchitecture may limit endovascular access. A systematic review and meta‐analysis of 266 patients reported higher complete obliteration (89.8% vs. 73.6%) and lower retreatment rates (46.7% vs. 6.2%) with microsurgery compared with endovascular therapy, with comparable complication rates [53]. Stereotactic radiosurgery has a limited role, mainly as adjunctive or salvage therapy for residual shunting or surgically inaccessible low‐flow lesions, reflecting its less predictable efficacy in iDAVFs than AVMs [54]. Conservative management with serial imaging may be appropriate for asymptomatic low‐grade lesions without CVD (Borden I, Cognard I–IIa), which have a low hemorrhagic risk despite possible progression [6, 22, 50]. Multidisciplinary management remains essential to optimize outcomes.

Prognosis is closely related to lesion grade, venous drainage pattern, and treatment success. Low‐grade lesions without CVD carry a negligible hemorrhagic risk, whereas untreated high‐grade lesions have annual rates of ICH or NHNDs of 7%–20%, increasing substantially after an initial hemorrhage [5, 6, 55]. Early treatment of high‐grade lesions is therefore essential. Neurological deficits may improve following successful fistula obliteration, although residual disability is common after hemorrhagic presentations. Durable occlusion significantly reduces recurrence risk and restores long‐term survival towards that of the general population [51], although recurrence may still occur even after apparently successful treatment, supporting ongoing clinical and radiological surveillance [51, 52].

Conclusion

IDAVFs should be considered in patients presenting with atypical stroke‐like or postictal deficits, particularly when neuroimaging is inconclusive. Early recognition, multidisciplinary assessment, and timely intervention are essential to reduce hemorrhagic risk and optimize neurological outcomes.
