CO₂ laser ablation versus conization for cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Shima Mohammadian, Mahin Seifi Alan, Hadith Rastad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to compare the efficacy of CO₂ laser ablation versus conization for treating cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2 (CIN2) and to identify predictors of treatment failure. A prospective observational cohort study was conducted on 146 women with histologically confirmed CIN2. Participants were allocated to either CO₂ laser ablation (n = 100) or loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP, n = 46) based on clinical decision. The primary outcome was short-term histopathological treatment response (regression to CIN1 or normal histology) at a 6-month follow-up. Propensity score matching was performed to adjust for baseline differences between groups. The overall treatment response rate was 81.5%, with no statistically significant difference between the conization (89.1%) and laser ablation (78.0%) groups (p = 0.108). This non-significant difference persisted after propensity score matching (OR = 0.66; 95% CI: 0.17–2.54; p = 0.548). For the entire cohort, abnormal cytology was an independent predictor of treatment failure, while younger age was independently associated with treatment success. In the laser group specifically, abnormal cytology, older age, and a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² were significant predictors of treatment failure. CO₂ laser ablation demonstrated acceptable short-term efficacy for managing CIN2, with no statistically significant difference compared with conization at six months of follow-up. While conization remains the preferred treatment for patients with high-risk features, CO₂ laser ablation may represent a reasonable fertility-sparing option in carefully selected low-risk patients. Careful patient selection remains crucial for optimizing outcomes.

Introduction

Cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2 (CIN2) represents a critical intermediate stage in the carcinogenic pathway to cervical cancer, characterized by a highly variable natural history encompassing spontaneous regression, persistence, or progression to malignancy [1].This prognostic uncertainty necessitates careful clinical management and remains a subject of ongoing debate.

Current management strategies for CIN2 are primarily divided into excisional and ablative modalities. Excisional procedures, including loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP) and cold knife conization, provide a histological specimen for definitive diagnosis and margin assessment [2]. Recent evidence comparing cold knife conization and CO₂ laser excision further highlights the importance of technique selection in balancing efficacy and reproductive outcomes [3]. However, these techniques are associated with an elevated risk of subsequent obstetric complications, such as preterm birth and cervical insufficiency, presenting a significant concern for patients of reproductive age [4]. In contrast, ablative therapies, such as CO₂ laser vaporization, are less invasive, preserve cervical anatomy, and demonstrate a more favorable profile regarding reproductive outcomes [5]. Reflecting this risk-benefit balance, the 2019 ASCCP Risk-Based Management Consensus Guidelines endorse that for patients with CIN2 in whom future pregnancy concerns are paramount, either treatment or observation is acceptable. For ablative treatment, a fully visualized transformation zone with no suspicion of endocervical involvement is required [6].

Despite established guidelines, a significant evidence gap exists regarding the comparative efficacy of ablation versus excision for CIN2. Existing literature is limited and reports heterogeneous success rates, influenced by factors including human papillomavirus (HPV) genotype persistence, lesion characteristics, and margin status [7, 8]. While some evidence suggests comparable efficacy in selected patients, robust predictors of treatment failure—such as specific high-risk HPV subtypes or high-grade cytology—remain poorly defined [9]. This lack of high-quality comparative data complicates clinical decision-making and underscores the need for further investigation.

This prospective observational cohort study aims to compare the short-term histopathological treatment response between CO₂ laser ablation and conization for CIN2 in a real-world clinical setting as the primary objective. The secondary objective is to identify clinical and virological predictors of treatment failure within each treatment modality. The findings from this study are intended to provide comparative short-term outcome data and contribute to the evidence base to inform clinical decision-making and guide personalized therapeutic strategies for patients with CIN2.

Method

Study design and population

This prospective observational cohort study with clinician-driven treatment allocation was conducted at Kamali Hospital, affiliated with Alborz University of Medical Sciences, Iran, from November 2023 to November 2024. The study evaluated the treatment response of CO₂ laser ablation compared to conization in women with histologically confirmed CIN2. A total of 146 women were enrolled and allocated into two groups based on clinical decision: a Laser Group (n = 100) receiving CO₂ laser ablation and a Control Group (n = 46) undergoing conization with LEEP. To ensure a homogenous and comparable cohort, inclusion criteria comprised women aged 25 to 50 years with biopsy-proven CIN2, a fully visualized squamocolumnar junction (no endocervical involvement), and a documented HPV status via Polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Exclusion criteria included active pregnancy, any immunodeficiency (e.g., HIV or immunosuppressive therapy), prior cervical treatment, suspicion of invasive carcinoma on colposcopy or cytology, and unwillingness to comply with follow-up protocols. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Alborz University of Medical Sciences (Approval No.: IR.ABZUMS.REC.1403.354), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Interventions

Treatment allocation was based on clinical decision-making. CO₂ laser ablation was offered to patients with a fully visible transformation zone, negative endocervical curettage (ECC), no colposcopic suspicion of glandular involvement or invasion, and those who expressed a preference for fertility preservation. Conization with LEEP was offered to patients with less favorable colposcopic features (e.g., lesion extending into the endocervical canal, non-visible transformation zone, or higher-grade cytology) or those who preferred excisional treatment.

Participants in the Laser Group underwent CO₂ laser ablation performed under colposcopic guidance. The procedure utilized standardized parameters (power: 40 to 60 W; spot size: 2 to 3 mm) to achieve complete vaporization of the abnormal cervical epithelium. The target depth of ablation was approximately 5–7 mm to ensure adequate destruction of involved cervical crypts, in accordance with standard clinical recommendations. Before laser ablation, all patients underwent ECC, and only those with negative ECC results were eligible. Colposcopy was performed by a single gynecologic oncologist, and targeted biopsies were taken from all acetowhite areas. Patients with any lesion extending into the endocervical canal, glandular lesions on biopsy, or any suspicion of invasion were excluded.

Participants in the Control Group underwent conization with LEEP. All conization specimens were sent for histopathological assessment to evaluate the completeness of excision margins.

Data collection and outcome measures

Baseline variables were collected for all participants and included demographic data (age, gravidity categorized as 0, 1, 2, or ≥ 3), clinical factors (Body Mass Index (BMI), smoking status, and drug history), and pathological variables (HPV status classified as high-risk or low-risk via PCR, and Pap smear results per the Bethesda System).

HPV testing was performed using a polymerase chain reaction–based assay, and high-risk HPV genotypes were defined according to established classifications, including HPV types 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, and 59. The primary outcome was short-term histopathological treatment response, assessed via follow-up colposcopy with directed biopsy at 6 months post-treatment. Response was categorized as either a Responder (regression to CIN1 or normal histology) or a Non-responder (persistence of CIN2 or progression to CIN3/carcinoma). BMI, smoking status, and drug history were collected prospectively for the laser group but were not available for the conization group, as these variables were not systematically recorded for conization patients during the study period.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data; continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons for continuous data were performed using independent t-tests, while Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical data. To control for potential baseline confounding, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using R Studio (version 4.3.1) with the MatchIt package, employing 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without a caliper based on age, HPV status, and cytology results. Standardized mean differences of < 0.1 post-matching indicated adequate balance between groups. BMI was dichotomized at ≥ 25 kg/m² based on the WHO classification for overweight in Asian populations. Binary logistic regression was employed to identify predictors of treatment response, with covariates including age (per-year increase), abnormal Pap smear (binary), BMI (dichotomized at ≥ 25 vs. <25 kg/m²), gravidity (> 0 vs. 0), and HPV status (positive/negative). The results of the regression are reported as adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). All other statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Confidentiality of participant data was rigorously maintained through de-identified data storage. Voluntary participation was ensured, and all participants provided written informed consent, which explicitly notified them of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any compromise to their clinical care.

Results

The study enrolled 146 patients with histologically confirmed CIN2, with a mean age of 36.6 years (SD = 9.4). Participants were allocated to two groups: the Laser Group (n = 100, 68.5%) and the Control Group (n = 46, 31.5%). Baseline characteristics revealed no significant difference in mean age between groups (Control: 37.2 ± 8.5 years; Laser: 36.4 ± 9.8 years; p = 0.089). The mean BMI in the Laser Group was 25.0 (SD = 2.9); BMI and smoking status were not available for the Control Group (Table 1).

HPV positivity was observed in 81.5% (119/146) of participants overall, with 87.0% (40/46) in the Control Group and 89.0% (79/100) in the Laser Group (p = 0.250). High-risk HPV subtypes were identified in 63.7% (93/146) of all participants, with comparable prevalence between the Control (67.4%, 31/46) and Laser (62.0%, 62/100) groups (p = 0.903). Smoking (16.0%, 16/100) and drug history (4.0%, 4/100) were reported only in the Laser Group, as these data were not collected for controls. Normal cytology was observed in 51.4% (75/146) of participants overall, with significantly higher rates in the Control Group (69.6%, 32/46) compared to the Laser Group (43.0%, 43/100; p = 0.009). High-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL) were present in 4.8% (7/146) of the total cohort, with a higher prevalence in the Laser Group (6.0%, 6/100) than in controls (2.2%, 1/46). Other abnormalities in the Laser Group included atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASC-US, 24.0%, 24/100), low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (LSIL, 22.0%, 22/100), atypical squamous cells-cannot exclude HSIL (ASC-H, 5.0%, 5/100), and atypical glandular cells (AGC, 0.0%). No ASC-H or AGC cases were reported in the Control Group. The overall treatment response rate was 81.5% (119/146), with 89.1% (41/46) in the Control Group and 78.0% (78/100) in the Laser Group (p = 0.108) (Table 1).

Unadjusted analysis showed no significant difference in treatment efficacy between CO₂ laser ablation and conization (OR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.15–1.23; p = 0.115). This non-significant association persisted after adjustment for Pap smear results (OR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.19–1.63; p = 0.282) (Table 2).

Logistic regression analysis for the entire cohort identified younger age (OR = 0.94 per year; 95% CI: 0.90 to 0.98; p = 0.003) and abnormal Pap smear results (OR = 3.37; 95% CI: 1.31 to 8.66; p = 0.012) as independent predictors of reduced treatment response. Gravidity (> 0 pregnancies) and HPV positivity showed no significant association with outcomes (analysis not shown in provided tables).

Analysis of the Laser Group, stratified by treatment response, revealed significant baseline differences between responders and non-responders (Table 3). Notably, non-responders were significantly older (mean age 41.2 ± 11.8 years) than responders (35.0 ± 8.7 years; p = 0.008) and had a higher mean BMI (26.9 ± 3.2 vs. 24.6 ± 2.7 kg/m²; p = 0.001). The distribution of Pap smear results also differed markedly between the groups (p < 0.001). A normal Pap smear was observed in 50.0% (39/78) of responders compared to only 18.2% (4/22) of non-responders. Conversely, HSIL were found exclusively in the non-responder group (27.3%, 6/22). No significant differences were observed between the groups regarding smoking status, drug history, or HPV positivity.

After propensity score matching, 91 patients were included in the analysis (Laser: n = 45, Conization: n = 46). Of the initial 146 patients, the 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching algorithm successfully matched 45 patients from the laser group to 46 patients from the conization group, resulting in a final matched cohort of 91 patients. The remaining patients were excluded from the matched analysis. The matching procedure successfully balanced the two groups on the key baseline characteristics of age, abnormal Pap smear status, and gravidity (all standardized differences < 0.1). We found no statistically significant difference in treatment response between the CO₂ laser and conization groups (OR = 0.66; 95% CI: 0.17 to 2.54; p = 0.548) (Fig. 1).

In the conization group, all resected specimens had negative margins, and no case of histopathological upgrade to CIN3 or worse was observed. No peri-procedural complications, including significant bleeding, infection, or cervical stenosis, were observed in either treatment group.

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study comparing CO₂ laser ablation to conization for CIN2, we found no statistically significant difference in the overall treatment response rate at six months (78.0% vs. 89.1%, p = 0.108). This finding remained consistent after adjustment for baseline differences using propensity score matching, supporting the robustness of the short-term comparison. It must be emphasized that the six-month follow-up provides only short-term data on histological response and does not reflect long-term recurrence or durable cure. The non-significant findings should be interpreted with caution due to wide confidence intervals and possible type II error. The point estimates consistently favor conization. Despite this comparable overall response, subsequent analyses identified specific patient factors that significantly predicted failure following laser ablation. In the laser group, older age, abnormal baseline cytology, and a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² were independent predictors of treatment failure.

Our findings align with existing literature comparing ablative and excisional treatments for CIN while also highlighting the importance of follow-up duration and patient selection. Zhang et al. [10], in a systematic review of CIN2/3, reported higher recurrence rates following ablative therapies compared with excisional procedures during longer follow-up periods. Importantly, this meta-analysis primarily evaluated long-term outcomes, whereas the present study focused on short-term histopathological response, which may explain the observed differences in reported success rates.

Kodama et al. [11] investigated long-term outcomes after CO₂ laser vaporization and demonstrated a progressive increase in recurrence rates over time, reaching nearly half of treated patients at five years. Notably, older age and higher body mass index were identified as significant predictors of recurrence in their cohort, findings that are concordant with our observation that these factors adversely influenced early treatment response in the laser group. This consistency across short- and long-term analyses supports the biological plausibility of patient-related risk factors affecting the effectiveness of ablative therapy.

The clinical implications of our findings are relevant for fertility-preserving management strategies. CO₂ laser ablation minimizes cervical tissue removal and is associated with a lower risk of adverse obstetric outcomes compared with excisional procedures [3]. However, our data suggest that such benefits should be weighed against patient-specific risk factors, particularly cytological severity, age, and BMI, to optimize treatment selection. In clinical practice, younger age, normal baseline cytology, and lower BMI may help identify patients more likely to achieve short-term success with laser ablation, supporting its use as a fertility-sparing alternative in carefully selected low-risk women. Additionally, Mariya et al. [12] highlighted the importance of colposcopic guidance during CO₂ laser vaporization, particularly for peripheral cervical lesions, which have been associated with higher failure rates. Their findings suggest that colposcopy-guided laser ablation may improve treatment accuracy and reduce the risk of residual disease, which is consistent with the procedural approach used in the present study, underscoring the critical role of precise technique in optimizing outcomes of ablative therapy.

Parallels can also be drawn with the management of other HPV-related lower genital tract intraepithelial neoplasias. Gallio et al. [13], in a large Italian cohort of patients with high-grade vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia (VaIN2/3), reported substantial recurrence rates over extended follow-up despite initial treatment success. Although VaIN represents a distinct anatomical entity, its shared HPV-driven pathogenesis with CIN underscores the importance of long-term surveillance following ablative therapies and cautions against interpreting early response as definitive cure.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The non-randomized, clinician-driven treatment allocation introduces potential selection bias, as patients in the laser group exhibited less favorable baseline cytological characteristics. Although propensity score matching was applied to adjust for key confounders (age, HPV status, and cytology), residual confounding due to unmeasured variables (such as lesion size, smoking, or gravidity) cannot be entirely excluded. BMI, smoking status, and drug history were not collected for the conization group, which represents a major limitation; therefore, comparative inference between groups is limited and findings related to these variables apply only to the laser-treated cohort. Repeat HPV testing at 6 months was not performed, although WHO recommends HPV testing as a test of cure. The subgroup analyses in the laser group (BMI, age, and cytology) are exploratory and hypothesis-generating and should not be interpreted as comparative or definitive. Due to the observational design and clinician-driven allocation, causal inferences cannot be made; our findings are hypothesis-generating and should be interpreted as associations, not as evidence of equivalence or causation. Finally, the six-month follow-up period limits assessment of long-term recurrence, which remains a critical outcome in CIN management.

Conclusion

In summary, CO₂ laser ablation demonstrated acceptable short-term histological response for the treatment of CIN2 at six months of follow-up, although the observed point estimates consistently favored conization. Given the non-randomized design and baseline differences between treatment groups, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence between modalities. While conization remains the preferred approach for patients with high-risk features, CO₂ laser ablation may represent a reasonable fertility-sparing option in carefully selected patients, particularly younger women with normal baseline cytology and lower BMI. However, the six-month follow-up period limits conclusions regarding long-term efficacy, and longer-term prospective studies with extended follow-up are warranted to better define durability of response and recurrence risk following ablative therapy.
