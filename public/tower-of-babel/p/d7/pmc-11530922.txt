Maximizing the environmental benefits of gas power development in China: A multidisciplinary modeling approach
Authors: Yan Chen, Michael Davidson, Yu Liu, Dachuan Liu, Huawei Zhang, Chaozi Wang, Xinyu Fan, Muzhen Ren, Pu Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryChina, holding nearly half of the global coal-fired power capacity, must shift from its coal-centric energy system to meet climate and air quality goals. This study explores the optimal utilization of natural gas by evaluating the environmental and economic costs of gas- and coal-fired power using plant-level data and a multidisciplinary modeling approach. We find that gas power has substantially lower air pollution costs (0.77 versus 12.2 cents/kWh) and moderately lower GHG costs (2.8 versus 4.5 cents/kWh), with an average environmental advantage of 12.1 cents/kWh and a social benefit of 10.4 cents/kWh when considering LCOE. The benefits vary spatially with the highest benefits in east-central China. Prioritizing substitution in regions with high environmental benefits could reduce SO2, NOx, and PM emissions by 28%, 15%, and 56%, respectively, offering a 52% higher environmental benefit than existing gas power plans. This study provides quantitative support for gas power development in China.Subject areas: Earth sciences, Environmental science, Energy policy

Introduction

China’s energy policy is of paramount significance as it shapes the energy landscape of the world’s largest carbon emitter. The power sector, which relies heavily on coal-fired power, plays a vital role in China’s transition toward a clean energy system.1 Unlike the Western developed countries, China does not have the opportunity to replace coal with natural gas first and then with renewable energies. While some Chinese cities and provinces have begun transitioning toward natural gas-fired power to mitigate regional air pollution and support intermittent renewable energy sources, China’s natural gas contribution to the overall energy mix remains substantially lower than the global average, standing at just 3% of China’s electricity generation in 2020,2 considerably below the world’s average of 23%.3 Notably, China grapples with limited domestic conventional natural gas resources and faces greater economic and technical challenges in developing its shale gas reserves compared to the United States.4 Therefore, expanding natural gas consumption might heighten China’s dependence on imported natural gas, which raises significant energy security concerns, especially in the wake of the Ukraine crisis.5 Consequently, it is anticipated that natural gas expansion in China will be realized only on a limited scale and, selectively, in regions capable of yielding the most significant benefits.

This raises a pivotal inquiry: What are the costs and benefits of substituting natural gas for coal in China’s power sector? Furthermore, what criteria should guide the planning of gas-fired power plants, and how can these benefits be maximized through strategic planning and regional prioritization?

Economic principles suggest that, to maximize social welfare, external costs should be internalized. In the context of the power sector, the external costs of fossil-fuel electricity mainly include health-related losses from air pollution and the costs of climate change caused by greenhouse gas emissions. Therefore, the planning criteria should be based on the maximization of substitution social benefits, including health benefits, carbon reduction benefits, and economic benefits.

After the introduction of China’s carbon peaking and carbon neutrality targets, different research teams have conducted various studies to explore China’s energy system transition pathways, as well as the coordination of air pollution reduction and carbon mitigation. For instance, Jiang et al.6 used the IPAC-AIM/Technology model to illustrate the necessity for China to achieve net-zero emissions between 2050 and 2060 under the global 1.5°C target. Duan et al.7 compared results from multiple integrated assessment models including AIM, GCAM, IMAGE, POLES, and IPAC, and indicated that under the 1.5°C target, China needs to approach zero emissions or achieve negative emissions by 2050, with the corresponding policy costs accounting for 3%–6% of GDP. Li et al.8 used the China TIMES model and GAINS model to evaluate the air quality improvement effects under China’s NDC and the well below 2°C target and pointed out that under the NDC scenario, the decrease in air pollution control costs brought by climate policies can completely cover the increase in climate policy costs. In these scenarios, natural gas played a moderate role in China’s energy transition and air pollution reduction, but its potential in the power sector can be significant due to its advantage in flexibility.

Previous studies have made significant progress in the assessment of greenhouse gas (GHG) and air pollutants emissions from the coal and natural gas industries. Liu et al.9 developed a fine-scale carbon and air pollutants emissions inventory for China’s coal-fired power plants. Zheng et al.10 used Liu et al.’s high-precision emission data to estimate the effects of China’s air pollution policies, finding that between 2010 and 2017, SO2, NOx, and primary PM2.5 emissions from coal-fired power plants in China decreased by 77%, 51%, and 25%, respectively. Tong et al.11 created the first global-scale power sector air pollutant emission database at the unit level, identifying high-pollution generating units and their contribution to global air pollutant emissions. Tang et al.12 analyzed online monitoring data (CEMS) from China’s coal-fired power plants using big data techniques, constructing a new emission inventory model that showed significant reductions in air pollutants due to ultra-low emission retrofits from 2014 to 2017.

In terms of natural gas-fired power, Skone et al. (2011) developed a life cycle inventory for the GHG emissions from extraction, delivery, and combustion of natural gas. Additionally, Howarth et al.13 and Alvarez et al.14,15 identified methane leakage rate as the most critical factor that determines the climate effect of natural gas, particularly during the process of shale gas extraction and transportation. Specifically for China, Gan et al.16 estimated the range of well-to-city-gate GHG emissions of China’s 104 gas-supplying sources, including domestic production, pipeline gas, and LNG. Most studies find that the shift from coal-to-gas can significantly reduce the life cycle emissions of GHG, SO2, and NOx, contributing to the decrease in national carbon emissions and air pollution.17,18,19,20,21 Furthermore, Mac Kinnon et al.22 argued that in addition to lower emission rates, flexible natural gas power plants can provide balancing services to renewable energy, and the gas infrastructure can support the future application of gaseous renewable fuels, though Zhang et al.23 warned that using natural gas as a “bridge” energy can delay the deployment of near-zero-carbon energy systems. Qin et al.24,25 compared life cycle air quality and carbon trade-offs in coal-to-gas switching in China’s power, industry, and residential sectors, finding that significant health and climate benefits could be achieved.

However, despite the generally supportive findings in the existing literature, several critical questions remain unanswered. First, with China’s ultra-low emission retrofitting initiatives significantly reducing air pollutants from coal-fired power plants,12,26 the advantages of transitioning from coal to gas could potentially diminish. Second, there is a lack of integrated policy analysis that considers both the external environmental costs and internal market costs of power generation. Finally, given the constrained supply and high costs of natural gas in China, the question of how to maximize benefits through strategic planning and regional prioritization remains unanswered.

In this study, we address the above questions using the latest power-plant and gas-field datasets and a multidisciplinary modeling approach. This approach integrates atmospheric models, environmental health assessments, spatial simulation, and scenario analysis. We take into account the location and technological specifications of each power plant, the life cycle GHG intensity of different gas sources, and the monetarized costs of GHG and air pollutants emissions, as well as the levelized costs of electricity generation (LCOE). Our quantitative analysis demonstrates that substituting gas for coal on a national scale can yield significant environmental benefits. Scenario modeling reveals that the strategic deployment of gas power, especially the prioritization of coal-to-gas transitions in central China, can maximize the environmental benefits of limited gas resources. Research framework

This article contributes to existing literature by integrating state-of-the-art atmospheric chemical models, environmental economic valuation methods, and high-resolution spatial simulations to address critical scientific and policy questions. While most previous studies on similar topics presented results at national or regional levels, our work provides external air pollution and GHG costs assessments at the power plant level, enabling a much more nuanced understanding of the feasibility of gas-fired power in every specific location and facilitating more precise policymaking in China’s energy development. By adopting a multidisciplinary modeling approach that considers various factors such as location, technological specifications, life cycle GHG intensity of different gas sources, and monetized costs of emissions, we offer a comprehensive analysis that accounts for both environmental and economic implications. This approach not only enhances the accuracy of our findings but also provides policymakers with invaluable insights for strategically planning the transition to a clean energy system in China.

Results

Emission factors of air pollutants and greenhouse gases

Based on our near-complete dataset of 208 operating and 286 planned gas plants and literature sources for coal plants after ultra-low emissions retrofitting, we calculated and compared the emission factors of gas-fired and coal-fired power plants in China. We also estimated the potential emission reductions from a coal-to-gas shift in power generation. Figure 2A shows the spatial distribution of China’s gas-fired power plants as of February 2024, with a total operational capacity of 121.3 GW and a planned capacity of 264.6 GW (Figure S1). Figure 2B presents the distribution of emission factors for gas plants in boxplots, and those of coal plants in scatter points. The values for gas power are from our own calculation, and the values for coal power are recalculated from the literature.9,12,26

(C) NOx emission factors are categorized by function types of gas-fired power plants.

The results show that gas plants have lower emission factors than coal plants for all pollutants, even after coal plants have undergone ultra-low emission retrofitting. The weighted mean emission factors of gas plants are 107.0, 6.6, and 4.8 mg/kWh for NOx, SO2, and PM, respectively (Figure 2B), whereas the lowest estimates for coal plants are 239, 165, and 28 mg/kWh.12 The emission factors of gas plants vary by plant function type: power-generation-only plants have the highest NOx emission factor (120.7 mg/kWh), combined-heat-and-power (CHP) plants have an intermediate value (105.1 mg/kWh), while CCHP plants have the lowest (78.1 mg/kWh) (Figure 2C). The life cycle CO2 equivalent (CO2-eq) emissions of gas plants range from 400 to 834 g/kWh, with a weighted mean of 553 g/kWh, while the average coal plant emits 902 g/kWh (Figure 2B). Therefore, transitioning from coal to gas in power generation can yield reductions of at least 55%, 96%, and 82% in NOx, SO2, and PM emissions, respectively. The life cycle GHG emissions per kWh from gas plants are also approximately 40% lower than those from coal plants (Figure 2B).27,28,29 We will discuss the economic and geographic aspects of this transition in the following sections.

Distribution of social costs and substitution benefits

We evaluated the social costs and substitution benefits of gas- and coal-fired power plants in China. The costs consist of three components: (1) air pollution costs, measured by the premature deaths due to increased PM2.5 exposure; (2) GHG emission costs, calculated by the social cost of carbon; and (3) economic costs, represented by the levelized costs of electricity generation (LCOE). We used detailed plant-level datasets and well-established methodologies to estimate these costs and converted them to U.S. cents/kWh. Figure 3 shows the maps of the results.

Distribution of air pollution costs

Figures 3A and 3B show the air pollution costs of generating one kWh of electricity by gas and coal power plants at different locations in China. The air pollution cost of gas power ranges from 0.1 to 1.6 cents/kWh, with a capacity-weighted mean of 0.77 cents/kWh (all mean values in this section are capacity-weighted). The air pollution cost of coal power ranges from 4.3 to 21.2 cents/kWh, with a capacity-weighted mean of 12.3 cents/kWh. The costs of both power sources are higher in northern and central China, where the pollutants affect a large proportion of the national population. The costs are lower in coastal regions due to more advanced power plants, more precipitation, and lower mortality rates from air pollution related diseases. The costs are also low in remote northeastern and western regions, where the population densities are low. Figure 3C shows the air quality benefits of replacing coal power with gas power. The benefits vary from 4.7 to 20.0 cents/kWh, with a capacity-weighted mean of 10.4 cents/kWh. Since the air pollution costs of gas power are much lower than those of coal power, the spatial pattern of substitution benefits in Figure 3C resembles that of coal power costs in Figure 3B.

Distribution of greenhouse gas costs and environmental benefits

We reviewed the key literature on the social costs of carbon (SCC) (Table S6) and chose 50 USD per ton CO2-eq to estimate the GHG emission costs. The GHG emission costs are 2.0–4.2 cents/kWh for gas power (Figure S2) and 4.1–5.2 cents/kWh for coal power (Table S7), with capacity-weighted means of 2.8 and 4.5 cents/kWh, respectively. The GHG costs for gas power vary by region, depending on the source of natural gas. The costs are lower in the western provinces, where conventional gas fields are nearby and higher in the eastern provinces, where natural gas is imported from overseas or transported over long distances from central Asia. Replacing coal power with gas power can bring a mean GHG reduction benefit of 1.7 cents/kWh, with the range of 0.3–2.9 cents/kWh (see Figure S3).

We define the “environmental costs” as the sum of the air pollution and GHG emission costs, and the “environmental benefits” as the reduction in environmental costs from substitution. The environmental costs per kWh are 2.6–4.8 cents for gas power (mean: 3.5, Figure 3D) and 8.8–25.3 cents for coal power (mean: 16.8, Figure 3E). The environmental benefits of substitution range from 6.4 to 21.3 cents/kWh (mean: 12.1, Figure 3F). The environmental benefit is higher in the east-central region of China with its focal point in Henan province.

Distribution of economic costs and social benefits

We calculated the LCOE of gas and coal power in each province using the method and assumptions of the International Energy Agency (IEA).30 The provincial LCOE is 4.6–7.4 cents/kWh for gas power, and 3.4–5.8 cents/kWh for coal power. We used “social costs” and “social benefits” to encapsulate the cumulative values of air pollution, GHG, and LCOE. The social costs are 7.4–12.0 cents/kWh (mean: 10.4) for gas power (Figure 3G), and 14.5–30.5 cents/kWh (mean: 21.6) for coal power (Figure 3F). The social benefits of replacing coal power with gas power are 4.3–19.7 cents/kWh (mean: 10.4) (Figure 3I). The spatial pattern of social benefits is similar to that of environmental benefits.

In summary, compared with coal power, gas power has a substantially lower air pollution cost (0.77 versus 12.2 cents/kWh), and a moderately lower GHG cost (2.8 versus 4.5 cents/kWh). The average environmental advantage resulting from the substitution of gas for coal (12.1 cents/kWh) significantly surpasses the Levelized Cost of Electricity (LCOE) disparity between gas and coal power, yielding an average net benefit of 10.4 cents/kWh.

Regional prioritization to maximize environmental benefits

Although gas-for-coal substitution can generate substantial environmental benefits, China’s limited domestic gas production and energy security concerns over dependence on imported natural gas restrict the scale of such a substitution. Figure 3 shows that the benefits of gas-for-coal substitution differ considerably across regions. Therefore, it is possible to gain additional benefits just through the spatial planning of gas power plants. We suggest a regional priority order for the substitution to allocate scarce gas resources to regions with higher environmental benefits.

We first integrated the information in Figure 3 and computed the capacity-weighted means of costs and benefits for all gas and coal plants within each province. Then, we ranked the provinces from high to low based on environmental substitution benefits (Figure 4). The order of the total social benefits (dashed line in Figure 4) slightly deviated from the order of the environmental benefits, because of variations in LCOEs. From an environmental perspective, the order in Figure 4 reflects the sequence of maximizing the environmental benefits of substituting gas for coal. However, in practice, the coastal provinces with more economic development have the most operating and planned gas power plants (Figure 5B).

We examined the implications of this mismatch by comparing the avoided air pollutants and GHG emissions and the corresponding environmental benefits under two different priority orders: the “Current order,” which replaced coal power capacity with gas power capacity in the order from the province with the highest gas power proportion to the lowest; and the “High-benefit order,” which replaced coal power capacity in the provinces with the highest environmental benefits first (the same order as in Figure 4). The orders in the two strategies differed considerably: the top ten provinces in the “Current order” case were mainly in coastal regions (Figure 5B), whereas the top ten provinces in the “High-benefit order” clustered in northern and central China (Figure 5C; method details; Table S10).

Furthermore, if gas power served as a flexible backup source rather than a baseload provider, its utilization hours could be reduced significantly, and more emissions could be avoided. To illustrate this point, we added a third case (Flexible utilization) based on the “High-benefit order” case, by assigning all newly added gas power plants an annual utilization of 2530 h, which was the actual utilization hours for gas power in 2020, instead of 4290 h, which was the actual utilization hours for coal power. We also added a fourth case (low-EF) based on the “Flexible utilization” case to apply the average emission factors in Beijing to other provinces, since Beijing had the strictest emissions standards for gas power (Table S8; Figure S4). We presented more details of the four cases in method details.

As of February 2024, China’s installed gas power capacity was 121.3 GW, and the planned capacity was 246.6 GW, adding up to a total of 368 GW. However, the plants were mainly clustered in three metropolitan regions: Beijing-Tianjin, Shanghai-Zhejiang-Jiangsu, and Guangdong (Figure 2). We therefore simulated a 30% substitution level, which replaced roughly 330GW coal power with gas power. In Figure 5, it is demonstrated that at a 30% substitution level, the annual avoided emissions of SO2, NOx, and PM in the “High-benefit order” scenario were 618, 1,243, and 222 thousand tons, respectively, which were 28%, 15%, and 56% higher than those in the “Current-order” scenario, respectively. The avoided CO2 emissions in the two scenarios were roughly the same, about 460 million tons. The resulting annual environmental benefit in the “High-benefit order” scenario was 214 billion U.S. dollars, marking a 52% improvement over the "Current-order" case. If the substitution level exceeds 30%, the construction of gas power plants would extend to provinces with moderate to low environmental benefits, (Table S11; Figure S5), and the environmental advantage of gas power over coal power would become less significant.

The findings presented in Figure 5 suggest that the current operational and planned gas power plants are not strategically located in optimal regions from an environmental perspective. Thus, improving regional prioritization in gas-for-coal substitution would significantly enhance the benefits. Furthermore, in the "Flexible Utilization" case, environmental benefits are projected to expand by an additional 10% compared to the "High-benefit order" scenario. The “Low-EF” scenario has the potential to further increase the environmental benefits by approximately 2%, indicating the benefits of stricter emission standards for future gas power plants.

Impacts of different gas sources

China has a diverse range of gas supply sources, and the life cycle GHG emissions vary significantly across sources. In 2018, about 60 domestic gas fields within China supplied 57% of the total consumed natural gas, with an additional 43% supplied by 40 gas fields from more than 20 countries.3 or import gas through long-distance pipelines from Central Asia and Russia, or in the form of liquefied natural gas (LNG) from overseas.

To elucidate the impacts of diverse gas sources, we turn our attention to Zhejiang province as a case study due to its exceptionally varied gas supply sources. Figure 6 presents the life cycle GHG emission factors associated with nine distinct gas sources supplying Zhejiang. The life-cycle emissions calculation framework is shown in Figure 7 in STAR Methods section. Gas from the West-East II pipeline has the highest emission factor (696 CO2-eq g/kWh), primarily attributed to the long transmission distance. In contrast, gas from the East China Sea’s Chunxiao field has the lowest emission factor of 449 CO2-eq g/kWh, which is 35% lower than that of gas from the West-East II pipeline. The superior environmental performance of Chunxiao gas can be attributed to its status as a conventional gas reservoir and its proximity to Zhejiang. LNG from the United States has the second-highest emission factor of 688 CO2-eq g/kWh, which is attributed to the large share of shale gas, additional liquefaction and regasification processes, and the long shipping distance. Although shale gas from China’s Sichuan basin has a high emission rate during extraction, primarily due to elevated methane leakage rate, its overall life cycle emissions fall within a mid-range. This is attributed to a shorter transmission distance, mitigating the cumulative environmental impact. These results indicate that the strategic planning of China’s gas infrastructure that matches gas consumption regions with their best-fit supply sources will help reduce the overall GHG emissions.

Sensitivity analysis for different social costs of carbon and value of statistic life values

Both the cost side and benefit side factors can influence our priority order results discussed in previous sections. On the cost side, gas price change and capital cost change could influence the LCOE and thus influence the market decisions on gas power development, but they will not change the priority order based on environmental costs shown in Figure 4. In contrast, on the benefit side, different choices for the values of social cost of carbon (SCC) and value of statistic life (VSL) can significantly change the priority order based on environmental costs. SCC and VSL in the main results are chosen based on middle-range values in the literature review. Specifically, we chose $50 per ton for SCC, which is the middle value based on the literature review in Table S6, and chose an income elasticity of 0.5 to calculate VSL following West et al.31 and Wang et al.32 Since the values of SCC and VSL can significantly affect the costs and benefits calculation, we conduct a sensitivity analysis to show the impacts of different parameter choices on our results.

First, we applied the lower estimate of SCC ($25 per ton) from Tol,33 and a high VSL value from Shindell et al. and Li et al. This combination gave lower weights to the impacts of GHG and higher weights to the impacts of air pollution, which slightly altered the ratios of air pollution and GHG costs, but did not affect the overall pattern of our results (Figure 8). Second, we used the higher estimate of SCC ($105 per ton) from Tol,33 and a low VSL value from Wang et al. This combination gave higher weights to the impacts of GHG and lower weights to the air pollution impacts, which considerably changed the order of regional priority (Figure 9). In practice, various socioeconomic factors and political discretion beyond our study determine the choice of these parameters, and our results based on environmental benefits can serve as a reference for actual policymaking.

Discussion

China, housing about half of the world’s coal-fired power plant capacity,3 is confronted with a pressing imperative to shift away from its coal-centric energy system. This transition is crucial to align with the climate and air quality objectives established by the nation. Our quantitative analysis demonstrates that substituting gas for coal on a national scale can yield significant environmental benefits. Despite the challenges posed by the limited and costly nature of natural gas resources in China, a noteworthy 265 GW of new gas power capacity was planned as of February 2024, with further increases anticipated in the future (Sichuan Provincial Development and Reform Commission, 2023). Therefore, strategic planning for these power plants to maximize their benefits becomes important.

Our study reveals that gas-fired power has substantially lower air pollution costs (0.77 versus 12.2 cents/kWh) and moderately lower GHG costs (2.8 versus 4.5 cents/kWh) compared to coal power, even after the ultra-low emission policy. The average environmental advantage resulting from this substitution is 12.1 cents/kWh, and the average social benefit is 10.4 cents/kWh when LCOE is considered. The substitution benefits are spatially heterogeneous with the highest benefits in east-central China.

Our modeling of different substitution scenarios emphasizes the importance of a strategic development plan for gas power, incorporating improved siting, infrastructure planning, and flexible utilization. Prioritizing substitution in key regions with high environmental benefits could lead to substantial reductions in SO2, NOx, and PM emissions—about 28%, 15%, and 56%, respectively—compared to existing gas power construction plans. This would result in an amplified annual environmental benefit of 52%. Additionally, optimizing gas infrastructure to match power plants with the best-fit gas sources can reduce life cycle GHG emissions by up to 35%, while utilizing gas power as a flexible backup to renewable energy enhances its overall advantages.

In conclusion, our high-resolution study provides quantitative support for strategic and precise policymaking in gas power development in China. It offers a roadmap to maximize environmental benefits while addressing challenges associated with finite gas resources.

This study has the following policy implications.

1)Optimizing Regional Prioritization of Future Gas Power Plants

Optimizing Regional Prioritization of Future Gas Power Plants

Our study advocates for a strategic regional prioritization approach in the planning of future gas-fired power plants. By maximizing environmental benefits, this approach ensures that limited gas resources are allocated to regions where the transition can yield the most substantial positive impact on air quality and reduce premature mortality.

2)Optimizing Gas Import Sources for GHG Reduction

Optimizing Gas Import Sources for GHG Reduction

Reaching carbon peak and neutrality are the major goals committed by China. Our study shows that different gas sources delivered to China have distinct GHG emission intensities. Therefore, as China diversifies its natural gas sources to improve energy security, it should also consider the life cycle greenhouse gas emissions associated with different supply sources. Strategic planning to align consumption regions with supply sources can contribute significantly to overall GHG reduction.

3)Stricter Emission Standards

Stricter Emission Standards

The study highlights the importance of enforcing and, where necessary, enhancing emission standards for gas-fired power plants. Stricter standards, particularly in regions with high environmental benefits, can amplify the positive impact of gas-for-coal substitution.

Limitations of the study

While our study provides valuable insights into the environmental advantages of gas-for-coal substitution in normal operating conditions, there are some limitations. First, we did not consider the changes in air pollutant emission intensities during star-up stages, when emissions tend to be higher than in normal working conditions. Second, we did not evaluate the flexibility advantages of gas power plants, particularly in terms of rapid start-stop capabilities. Future research could delve into a more comprehensive evaluation of gas power in the background of building a new energy system with the increased integration of wind and solar power. This would involve considering factors such as start-up times, emissions during transient phases, and the economic implications of frequent start-stop cycles in both gas and coal power units.

Method details

Development of national gas- and coal-fired power plants datasets

In this section, we construct comprehensive datasets for both national gas-fired and coal-fired power plants, which serve as the foundation for our analytical framework.

National Gas-Fired Power Plant Dataset

Our national gas-fired power plant dataset includes 208 operational plants and 286 planned plants as of February 2024 (Figure 3). The operational plants have a total capacity of 121.3 gigawatts (GW). The latest available data released by the China Electricity Council (2023) indicates that the total installed gas power capacity reached 115.7 GW by the end of 2022, marking a 6.2% increase from 2021. Extrapolating this growth rate suggests an installed gas power capacity close to 123 GW by the end of 2023. Therefore, our dataset encompasses the majority of gas-fired power units in China. Plant details, including plant name, operating status, address, gas pipeline and natural gas suppliers, and gas turbine model, were obtained from ARA Research and Publication's gas power project database and Global Energy Monitor (Table S1).34 Geo-coordinates, essential for spatial analysis, were collected from Google Earth on a plant-by-plant basis. Additional technical specifics, including function types (e.g., generating electricity only, combined-heat-and-power (CHP), and combined-heat-cold-and-power (CCHP)), turbine types (combined or simple cycle), thermal efficiency, heat-to-power ratio, and fuel-to-power efficiency, were extracted from environmental assessment reports, governmental publications, and power plant websites.

National Coal-Fired Power Plant Dataset

To construct our national dataset on coal-fired power plants, we integrated two data sources: the Global Energy Monitor’s Global Coal Plant Tracker35 and the Carbon Monitoring for Action (CARMA).36 The resulting dataset contains 2475 coal-fired power plants, each with its geographic coordinates, technology type, and installed capacity. This careful data collection process ensures the reliability and comprehensiveness of our datasets. It lays the groundwork for robust and insightful analyses in subsequent sections.

Computation of plant-level Emission Factors for Air Pollutants and life cycle greenhouse gases

Emission Factors for Air Pollutants

Air-pollutant Emission Factors for Gas-fired Power Plants

To estimate the pollutant emission factors for gas-fired power plants, we collected annual electricity generation and pollutant emission data for 65 plants, which represent 31% of the operating plants. We extracted the annual electricity generation data from the annual reports of listed companies and obtained the annual air pollutant emissions for SO2, NOx, and PM from the ref.37 We selectively used data from 2017 to 2019 to avoid possible influence by the COVID-19.

We calculated the per kilowatt-hour (kWh) emission factor EFp,iAir for individual plants p and specific pollutants i by dividing a power plant's annual emissions by its corresponding annual electricity generation. We then calculated the generation-weighted average emission factors for each pollutant, based on subgroups of emission standards and plant functions. We classified plant functions into three types: power only, combined heat and power (CHP), and combined cooling, heating, and power (CCHP). The average emission factors for each subgroup are shown in Table S2. We applied these average emission factors to the plants without annual generation data according to their emission standard and function type.

Air-pollutant Emission Factors for Coal-fired Power Plants

For coal-fired power plants, we used the Multi-resolution Emission Inventory for China (MEIC) developed by Tsinghua University9,10,32 to determine the grid-level pollutant emission factors. We computed the average emission factors for each pollutant for the six main regional electricity grids in China: North China, Northeast China, East China, Central China, Northwest China, and South China.

Estimation of Population Exposure to Air Pollutants from Power Plants

We used two methods to estimate the population exposure to air pollutants from coal- and gas-fired power plants. The first method was the intake fractions (IF) method, which is a time-efficient approach to estimate the impact of each individual power plant on the population. The second method was the atmospheric dispersion model (GEOS-Chem), which is a more accurate but computationally intensive approach to simulate the spatial distribution of air pollutants.

Intake Fraction Method

Intake fraction is defined as the fraction of a pollutant released from a source that is eventually inhaled by a population. Zhou et al.38 used statistical methods to estimate how different factors, such as atmospheric conditions, population density, and weather patterns, affect the intake fraction of polluting sources.

We applied the method of Zhou et al.,38 who estimated China-specific coefficients for intake fractions using the CALPUFF atmospheric dispersion model. They divided the regions affected by a power plant into four distance ranges: within 100 km, 100-500 km, 500-1000 km, and beyond 1000 km. They estimated the coefficients for intake fractions, treating them as the dependent variable, while employing the distance range from power plants, population density, and climate factors as independent variables. Precipitation was chosen as a proxy for climate factors, as it had a significant negative correlation with intake fraction and accounted for most of the residuals (Table S5). Zhou et al.38 provided the coefficients for primary fine particles, sulfur dioxide and sulfate, nitrogen oxides and nitrate. We then used intake fraction to examine the correlation between annual PM2.5 concentration and the emissions of SO2, NOx, and dust from coal and gas power plants, following the methodology in Wang et al.32

Atmospheric Dispersion Modeling

We used the GEOS-Chem model, a state-of-the-art high-resolution air quality model, to quantify the change in mortality attributed to coal-fired power plants in China ( ΔMortalityAllPlants). This step aims to validate the results obtained from the intake fraction model. The national emissions inventory for coal-fired power plants, sourced from the Multi-resolution Emission Inventory for China (MEIC) dataset (Zheng et al., 2018), was used as uniform input data for both the intake fraction method and the GEOS-Chem model. Emissions originating from various economic sectors were also derived from the MEIC dataset. The aggregated change in ambient PM2.5 concentration caused by all coal-fired power plants, ΔCAllPlants, was simulated with GEOS-Chem, and used to estimate the ΔMortalityAllPlants caused by coal-fired power plants. We then compared the ΔMortalityAllPlants derived from the GEOS-Chem model with the sum of mortality from each plant (∑p=1NΔMortalityp) derived from the intake fraction model for each of the 2761 Chinese counties (method details). The results revealed a robust correlation between the mortalities estimated with the two models, as depicted in Figures S6 and S7, along with Table S12.

Spatial simulation of emission costs and substitution benefits

After calculating air pollution and GHG costs for each of the 494 gas-fired plants and 2475 coal-fired plants, we used the Sequential Gaussian Simulation (SGS) method to model the spatial distribution of the costs and benefits in China. SGS is a conditional simulation method that based on a multivariate Gaussian field.42 It corrects the smoothing effect of the Kriging algorithm42 and is especially suitable for modeling unevenly distributed samples such as the coal and gas power plants in this study. We conducted the simulations using Petrel, a 3D modeling software developed by Schlumberger and widely used in the energy industry.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R and Excel software. The statistical details of each calculation, including the specific tests applied, the exact values of n, and the definitions of n (e.g., number of power plants, datasets used), are provided in the figure legends, figures, and results sections.

Appropriate statistical tests, including calculation of mean values, standard deviations (SD), standard error of the mean (SEM), and confidence intervals, were applied based on the type of data analyzed. Differences in emission factors between gas and coal plants were statistically analyzed and displayed using box plots, scatter points, and weighted averages (see Figures 2B and 2C).
