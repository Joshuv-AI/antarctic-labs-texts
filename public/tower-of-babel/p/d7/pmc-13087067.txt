Advancements and innovations in the molecular mechanisms and treatment of colorectal cancer
Authors: Xiaoyi Wang, Jianghua Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major global health challenge due to its high incidence and mortality rates. The development and progression of CRC are driven by complex molecular mechanisms involving genetic alterations in proto-oncogenes such as BRAF, PIK3CA, and K-ras, as well as tumor suppressor genes including APC, p53, and PTEN. These genetic changes are further compounded by the dysregulation of key signaling pathways such as Wnt/β-Catenin, PI3K/Akt, and STAT3, which collectively promote tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. Additionally, chronic inflammation mediated by pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8, along with disruptions in gut microbiota homeostasis, play critical roles in CRC pathogenesis by facilitating immune evasion and tumor progression.In recent years, significant advancements in therapeutic strategies have improved outcomes for CRC patients. Immunotherapy, including immune checkpoint inhibitors, tumor vaccines, and adoptive cell therapy, has shown promising results, particularly in patients with specific molecular profiles. Targeted therapies directed against biomarkers such as EGFR, VEGF, KRAS, and BRAF have also demonstrated efficacy, while complementary approaches like traditional Chinese medicine have gained attention for their potential to enhance treatment outcomes. However, challenges such as tumor heterogeneity, drug resistance, and the complex tumor microenvironment continue to hinder therapeutic success, underscoring the need for further research to overcome these barriers and improve patient survival.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04796-6.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Molecular mechanisms, Gene, Inflammation, Treatment

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) poses a substantial threat to global health and socioeconomic development due to its high incidence and mortality rates. According to the 2022 GLOBOCAN statistics, CRC accounts for 9.6% of global cancer cases, ranking as the third most commonly diagnosed cancer, and contributes to 9.3% of total cancer-related deaths, making it the second leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide [1]. Notably, the epidemiological patterns of CRC exhibit significant geographical variations. While countries such as the United States, Australia, and France have witnessed a decline in CRC incidence and mortality rates, attributed to advancements in cancer research and healthcare systems, many Asian and African nations, including China, South Korea, Japan, and Cape Verde, continue to experience a concerning upward trend in CRC cases [2, 3].

The pathogenesis of CRC is multifactorial, involving a complex interplay between genetic predisposition and environmental influences. Epidemiological studies have identified several modifiable risk factors strongly associated with CRC development, including smoking, alcohol consumption, elevated body mass index (BMI), impaired glucose metabolism, physical inactivity, excessive consumption of red and processed meats, and inadequate intake of calcium and dietary fiber [3–5]. Additionally, non-modifiable genetic factors, such as familial adenomatous polyposis syndrome, further contribute to CRC susceptibility. Given the substantial disease burden and economic impact imposed by CRC globally, there is an urgent need to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying its development and progression, as well as to establish effective preventive strategies and therapeutic interventions.

Molecular mechanism of colorectal cancer

The molecular mechanisms underlying colorectal cancer (CRC) are highly complex and multifaceted, involving a dynamic interplay of various biological processes that collectively drive tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis. At the genetic level, somatic mutations in key oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes disrupt normal cellular growth regulation, leading to uncontrolled proliferation and expansion of CRC cells, which ultimately contributes to tumor formation and growth. Concurrently, the aberrant activation of critical signaling pathways, such as Wnt/β-catenin, PI3K/Akt, and Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK), enhances the malignant potential of cancer cells by promoting their proliferation, migration, and invasive capabilities, thereby facilitating tumor dissemination and metastatic spread. Furthermore, the disruption of redox homeostasis within the tumor microenvironment creates a permissive niche for cancer cell survival and proliferation by mitigating oxidative stress and supporting metabolic adaptation. Additionally, dysregulation of immune-related molecular mechanisms, including the upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules and the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells, enables CRC cells to evade immune surveillance and destruction, further exacerbating tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. These interconnected molecular events highlight the intricate biological network that underpins CRC pathogenesis.

Proto oncogene mutations and abnormal expression

Studies have shown that the accumulation of mutations in oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes plays a pivotal role in driving genomic instability, which is a critical factor in the initiation and progression of CRC [6]. Proto-oncogenes, which encode proteins essential for regulating the cell cycle, promoting cellular proliferation and differentiation, and inhibiting apoptosis, can undergo mutations that transform them into potent drivers of tumorigenesis. These genetic alterations can lead to uncontrolled cell proliferation, enhanced metastatic potential, and ultimately contribute to malignant transformation. Among the proto-oncogenes implicated in CRC pathogenesis, several key players have been identified, including BRAF, PIK3CA, K-ras, c-myc, HER-2, and Gab3, each of which has been shown to exert significant influence on tumor development and progression through distinct molecular mechanisms.

Mutations in the Braf gene contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of CRC by disrupting apoptotic mechanisms and promoting tumor progression. Specifically, Braf mutations impair the function of Bcl-2 interacting mediator of cell death (Bim), a pro-apoptotic factor, thereby inhibiting apoptosis and facilitating tumor development. Studies [7] have identified that Braf mutations predominantly occur in exons 11 and 15, with the T1799A mutation in exon 15 being particularly notable. This mutation leads to the production of the BRAFV600E mutant protein, which exhibits a serine/threonine protein kinase activity approximately 500-fold higher than that of the wild-type protein. The hyperactivated BRAFV600E mutant constitutively activates the Ras/MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, resulting in downstream effects that drive tumorigenesis. These effects include the upregulation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and cytochrome C, as well as the suppression of E-cadherin expression, which collectively enhance tumor invasion and metastatic potential. Furthermore, the mutant protein modulates these processes through the activation of the Extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinase (ERK) and Nuclear Factor Kappa-Light-Chain-Enhancer of Activated B Cells (NF-κB) pathways and the inhibition of caspase activity, thereby reinforcing apoptotic resistance and promoting CRC progression [8].

The Phosphatidylinositol-4,5-Bisphosphate 3-Kinase Catalytic Subunit Alpha (PIK3CA) gene, which encodes the Phosphatidylinositol 3-Kinase(PI3K) protein, is frequently mutated in the early stages of CRC and plays a pivotal role in tumorigenesis. The PI3K protein exerts its biological functions primarily through the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, which regulates critical cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, migration, and glucose metabolism, thereby contributing significantly to cancer development and progression. Mutations in the PIK3CA gene are predominantly localized to two hotspot regions, exons 9 and 20, both of which enhance the phosphatidylinositol kinase activity of the PI3K protein. This gain-of-function mutation leads to the constitutive activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, driving oncogenic transformation and CRC initiation. Furthermore, mutations in the p110 catalytic subunit of mammalian class Ia PI3K proteins have been closely associated with CRC. Among the regulatory subunits, p85α exhibits the highest mutation frequency in CRC, further underscoring its critical role in the dysregulation of this pathway [9, 10].

K-ras, a member of the Ras gene family, is one of the most frequently mutated proto-oncogenes across various tumor types. It encodes the KRAS protein (p21 protein), which plays a critical role in regulating cell proliferation and differentiation through its involvement in key signaling pathways, including the Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR)/PI3K and EGFR/MAPK signaling Pathway. Under normal physiological conditions, K-ras functions as a molecular switch, cycling between an active GTP-bound state and an inactive GDP-bound state to tightly control downstream signaling. However, mutations in the K-ras gene result in the production of a constitutively active KRAS protein that lacks GTPase activity. This loss of regulatory function leads to the persistent activation of the EGFR/PI3K and EGFR/MAPK signaling pathways, independent of upstream EGFR signaling, thereby driving uncontrolled cell growth and contributing to tumorigenesis [11, 12].

The Cellular Myelocytomatosis oncogene (C-myc) gene, a member of the myc proto-oncogene family, plays a pivotal role in regulating gene expression, cell proliferation, and differentiation. Under physiological conditions, C-myc protein expression is minimal or absent in quiescent cells. However, its expression can be significantly upregulated by various growth factors and cytokines, including epidermal growth factor (EGF), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and mitogens. This upregulation facilitates the transition of cells from the G0/G1 phase to the S phase of the cell cycle, thereby accelerating cell cycle progression. Mechanistically, C-myc promotes malignant cell proliferation by enhancing the expression of cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) and cyclins while simultaneously suppressing the expression of cell cycle inhibitors such as p15, p21, and p27 [13]. Importantly, the amplification status of the C-myc gene and the expression levels of C-myc protein are closely associated with key clinicopathological features of CRC, including tumor differentiation, lymph node metastasis, liver metastasis, and Dukes staging, highlighting its critical role in CRC progression and aggressiveness [14].

HER-2, also referred to as neu or c-erbB-2, is a member of the ErbB receptor family and encodes a transmembrane tyrosine kinase receptor. It plays a critical role in regulating cell growth and proliferation by activating downstream signaling pathways, including the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR cascades [15]. While Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER-2) is typically absent or expressed at low levels in normal cells, its overexpression has been well-documented in breast and gastric cancers, where it contributes to tumor progression by inhibiting apoptosis, promoting angiogenesis, and enhancing cancer cell invasion. Recent studies have also identified HER-2 amplification in CRC, particularly in metastatic CRC (mCRC), where anti-HER-2 therapies have shown potential therapeutic benefits in patients with HER-2 overexpression. These findings suggest that HER-2 overexpression may play a significant role in the malignant transformation and progression of CRC [15, 16].

In addition to HER-2, the GRB2-Associated Binding Protein 3 (GAB3) gene has emerged as a potential regulator of CRC progression. A recent study [17] demonstrated that knockdown of GAB3 using targeted shRNAs significantly inhibits the proliferation of CRC cells. Mechanistically, GAB3 knockdown suppresses the activation of Ak strain Transforming (AKT) and Extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinase (ERK) signaling pathways, whereas GAB3 overexpression enhances their activation. Although these findings have thus far been validated only in animal models, they suggest that GAB3 may regulate CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by modulating AKT and ERK signaling, potentially through co-precipitation with specific protein partners. Further research is needed to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying GAB3’s role in CRC pathogenesis.

Mutations and abnormal expression of tumor suppressor genes

The common tumor suppressor genes associated with colorectal cancer are APC, DCC, p53, PTEN, Rb gene, rrunx3, NDRG2, p16, DPC4, KLF6, etc.

Mutations in the APC (Adenomatous Polyposis Coli) gene are a hallmark of the early stages of CRC development. The APC gene plays a pivotal role in regulating key signaling pathways, including the Wnt signaling pathway [18] and the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling pathway [19], through which it suppresses proto-oncogene expression and inhibits tumor cell adhesion. Beyond its role in signaling regulation, the APC protein interacts with microtubules to restrict tumor cell migration, directly modulates cell cycle components to arrest cell cycle progression, and inhibits tumor cell proliferation. However, when the APC gene undergoes deletions or other mutations, its tumor-suppressive functions are disrupted. This loss of function leads to the dysregulation of critical cellular processes, ultimately resulting in malignant transformation and tumor initiation.

The Deleted in Colorectal Carcinoma (DCC) gene encodes a single-pass transmembrane protein that belongs to the immunoglobulin superfamily. Research [20] has demonstrated that DCC gene expression can induce programmed cell death through the activation of caspase-3 or trigger G2/M cell cycle arrest, highlighting its role as a tumor suppressor. Notably, the DCC gene is deleted in over 70% of CRC cases, as well as in various other malignancies [21]. This frequent deletion strongly suggests that the loss of DCC function is closely associated with the initiation and progression of CRC, underscoring its importance in maintaining cellular homeostasis and preventing tumorigenesis.

The p53 gene, a well-known tumor suppressor, plays a critical role in maintaining genomic stability and preventing cancer development. Under normal conditions, p53 expression induces cell cycle arrest at the G1/G0 phase, preventing cells from entering the S phase and thereby inhibiting abnormal cell proliferation. Additionally, p53 promotes apoptosis in response to cellular stress or DNA damage, further contributing to its tumor-suppressive functions. However, mutations in the p53 gene disrupt these protective mechanisms, leading to the loss of cell cycle control and impaired apoptosis, which facilitate uncontrolled cell growth and tumor progression. In CRC, the expression levels of mutant p53 protein are closely associated with key clinicopathological features, including tumor differentiation, depth of invasion, lymph node metastasis, distant metastasis, and Dukes staging [22]. These findings underscore the significance of p53 mutations in driving CRC aggressiveness and highlight their potential as prognostic markers.

The Phosphatase and Tensin Homolog (PTEN) gene encodes a multifunctional tumor suppressor protein that plays a critical role in regulating cellular growth and survival. PTEN protein primarily exerts its anti-tumor effects by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, a well-established driver of tumorigenesis [23]. As a lipid and protein phosphatase, PTEN also modulates cell cycle progression by targeting the focal adhesion kinase (FAK) pathway, leading to growth arrest at the G2/M phase or, in some cases, the G0/G1 phase, thereby preventing entry into the S phase and suppressing tumor cell metastasis and adhesion [24]. Furthermore, PTEN inhibits the Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway by dephosphorylating SH2-containing protein (SHC) and insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS1), which disrupts the formation of the IRS1-Grb2-SOS complex and blocks downstream signaling [25]. Additionally, PTEN acts as an antagonist of IκB kinase (IKK), preventing the degradation of IκB and thereby inhibiting NF-κB-mediated malignant proliferation and tumor metastasis [26]. However, when PTEN gene mutations result in its inactivation, these tumor-suppressive functions are lost, leading to uncontrolled cell growth and cancer progression.

Research has demonstrated that the Solute Carrier Family 5 Member 8 (SLC5A8) gene plays a significant role in tumor suppression by promoting apoptosis and inhibiting tumorigenesis through its interaction with substrates such as dichloroacetic acid [27]. In colorectal cancer (CRC), the expression of SLC5A8 is markedly reduced or completely deleted, a phenomenon often attributed to the methylation-mediated silencing of the gene [28]. Additionally, SLC5A8 has been shown to exert anti-tumor effects by modulating immune responses, thereby contributing to the prevention of colon cancer [29]. These findings suggest that mutations or epigenetic alterations in the SLC5A8 gene may be closely associated with the development and progression of CRC, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target or biomarker.

Inflammation cancer transformation theory of colorectal cancer

The concept of inflammation-driven carcinogenesis, first proposed in the 19th century, has since been extensively studied and validated. Chronic inflammation contributes to CRC development through multiple mechanisms, including the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), which induce DNA damage, genomic instability, and mutations that drive tumor initiation and progression. In addition to these molecular alterations, a wide array of inflammatory mediators plays a critical role in promoting carcinogenesis. These include tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interferons (IFNs), interleukins (ILs), growth factors, granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), and chemokines such as CCL. Among these, TNF, a pro-inflammatory cytokine primarily secreted by monocytes and macrophages, is particularly noteworthy due to its multifaceted roles in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, inflammatory responses, autoimmune processes, and tumor formation. Its dysregulation is closely associated with the pathogenesis of CRC and other malignancies.

Tumor Necrosis Factor-α (TNF-α) is a critical mediator in the progression of CRC through its multifaceted roles in tumor biology. It promotes angiogenesis, tissue invasion, and metastasis by inducing oxidative stress-mediated DNA damage, stimulating endothelial cell proliferation, and regulating the expression of pro-angiogenic factors. Furthermore, TNF-α enhances epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) via Transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling and upregulates migration-associated adhesion molecules, facilitating cancer cell dissemination [30]. In patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), TNF-α contributes to the production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), which increase genomic instability and elevate the risk of carcinogenesis [31]. Additionally, TNF-α activates the NF-κB signaling pathway, a central regulator of inflammatory responses, thereby promoting tumor cell survival, proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [32]. Collectively, these mechanisms highlight the pivotal role of TNF-α in bridging chronic inflammation and CRC development, making it a promising target for therapeutic intervention.

Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) plays a significant role in promoting tumorigenesis, particularly in ulcerative colitis (UC)-associated cancer and IBD. It drives chronic inflammatory responses and upregulates the expression of inflammation-related genes, contributing to the development of enterocolitis and tumor formation. Studies have demonstrated that blocking IFN-γ signaling can effectively inhibit cancer progression [33], underscoring its critical role in inflammation-mediated carcinogenesis. These findings highlight IFN-γ as a key mediator linking chronic inflammation to tumor development and suggest its potential as a therapeutic target in inflammation-related cancers.

Interleukins (ILs) are a critical class of cytokines that facilitate communication between immune cells, regulate immune responses, and play pivotal roles in inflammation and tumorigenesis. While some interleukins, such as IL-10 and IL-22, exhibit anti-inflammatory and potentially anti-tumor effects, others, including IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, IL-13, IL-17 (e.g., IL-17 A), and IL-21, have been implicated in promoting inflammation-associated tumors, such as CRC. These pro-tumorigenic interleukins contribute to CRC development and progression by activating inflammatory signaling pathways, stimulating angiogenesis, and enhancing tumor cell proliferation and invasion. For instance, IL-1 drives early CRC growth through the IL-17 signaling pathway, and genetic deletion of its receptor, IL-1R1, has been shown to reduce tumor incidence [34]. IL-6 promotes CRC formation, metastasis, and angiogenesis by inducing oxidative stress and activating the Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling pathway [35]. IL-8, a potent pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic factor, enhances CRC progression by increasing tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [36]. IL-13 contributes to the formation of a tumor-supportive microenvironment by activating macrophages, thereby facilitating tumor invasion, proliferation, and metastasis [37]. The role of IL-17 in CRC remains complex and somewhat controversial. While some studies have reported overexpression of IL-17 in CRC, correlating it with angiogenesis and poor prognosis, the loss of the IL-17 A subtype has been associated with reduced colitis, suggesting a potential tumor-promoting role [38]. However, recent research has found no significant correlation between IL-17 A and CRC or IBD, leaving its role in CRC pathogenesis unresolved [39]. Finally, IL-21 promotes CRC progression through multiple mechanisms, including the enhancement of IL-6 and IL-17 A production, activation of STAT3 signaling, and increased tumor cell proliferation. Additionally, IL-21 impairs anti-tumor immunity by disrupting the function of cancer cell-specific CD103 + CD8+ cytotoxic T cells [40]. These findings underscore the diverse and context-dependent roles of interleukins in CRC, highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets while emphasizing the need for further research to clarify their mechanisms and clinical implications.

TGF-β and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) are pivotal regulators in the initiation and progression of CRC. TGF-β enhances tumor invasiveness and facilitates tumor growth by promoting EMT and establishing an immune-tolerant microenvironment during the later stages of tumor development [41]. Similarly, VEGF contributes to CRC progression by stimulating angiogenesis and amplifying inflammatory responses. Therapeutic strategies targeting VEGF signaling, such as blocking VEGFR2, have demonstrated efficacy in inhibiting tumor growth and angiogenesis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target [31]. Together, these findings underscore the critical roles of TGF-β and VEGF in CRC pathogenesis and their relevance in the development of targeted therapies.

Granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) plays a significant role in the pathogenesis and progression of CRC through multiple mechanisms. It promotes inflammatory responses, supports the formation of a tumor-favorable microenvironment, suppresses anti-tumor immunity, induces angiogenesis, and directly stimulates tumor cell proliferation. GM-CSF exerts its oncogenic effects by activating various cellular signaling pathways, including the NF-κB pathway, and upregulating the expression of anti-apoptotic proteins such as B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2). These actions collectively drive malignant transformation and facilitate cancer progression [42]. The multifaceted role of GM-CSF in CRC underscores its potential as a therapeutic target for disrupting tumor growth and enhancing anti-tumor immune responses.

Chemokines play a crucial role in the initiation and progression of CRC by modulating the tumor microenvironment and promoting oncogenic processes. Key chemokines such as C-X-C Motif Chemokine Ligand 1 (CXCL1), CXCL8/IL-8, and C-C Motif Chemokine Ligand 2 (CCL2) contribute to CRC development by recruiting immune cells, including neutrophils, macrophages, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). These cells enhance inflammatory responses, induce DNA damage, and promote cell proliferation and tumorigenesis. Additionally, CXCL12/SDF-1 facilitates tumor angiogenesis by directly or indirectly upregulating the expression of VEGF, thereby supporting tumor growth and metastasis. Furthermore, CXCL1 and CXCL5 activate critical signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK, to enhance tumor cell proliferation and survival. Collectively, these chemokines and their associated mechanisms highlight their significant roles in CRC pathogenesis and their potential as therapeutic targets for disrupting tumor progression.

The cancer promoting mechanism of intestinal microorganisms in colorectal cancer

Emerging research on intestinal microecology has revealed that disruptions in gut homeostasis significantly influence tumorigenesis, particularly in CRC. Analysis of fecal samples from CRC patients has demonstrated an increased abundance of harmful bacteria, such as Bacteroides, Fusobacterium nucleatum, and Enterococcus faecalis, alongside a decreased presence of beneficial probiotics like Bifidobacterium. Certain intestinal microorganisms contribute to CRC development by modulating host immune and inflammatory responses. For instance, enterotoxin-producing Bacteroides fragilis (ETBF) activates toll-like receptors, leading to the upregulation of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α. This, in turn, activates STAT3 and NF-κB signaling pathways, thereby promoting CRC progression [43]. Similarly, Fusobacterium nucleatum, frequently detected in highly dysplastic colorectal tissues and adenomas, induces the production of inflammatory mediators, including IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β, TGF-β, and TNF-α, further driving CRC development [44]. Fusobacterium nucleatum is not only the key pathogen of intestinal microecological imbalance in CRC, but also promotes the generation of therapeutic resistance through a variety of mechanisms. Its surface protein Fada can bind to E-cadherin of host cells, activate β-Catenin/Wnt signaling pathway, and enhance tumor cell proliferation and chemotherapy resistance [45]. In addition, Fusobacterium nucleatum can weaken the effect of immunotherapy such as PD-1 inhibitors by inducing the infiltration of immunosuppressive cells (such as M2 macrophages and regulatory T cells) in the tumor microenvironment [46, 47]. The latest research also shows that the bacterium can promote the resistance of CRC cells to 5-fluorouracil by secreting exosomes to transmit miRNA (such as miR-21) [48].

On the other hand, certain probiotics exhibit protective effects against CRC by modulating immune responses and exerting anti-tumor activities. For example, Lactobacillus casei demonstrates immunomodulatory properties by downregulating IL-22 and mediates anti-proliferative effects through the upregulation of Caspase-7 (casp-7), casp-9, and BCL2-Interacting Killer (Bik), while reducing the expression of the proliferation marker Kiel 67 (Ki67) [49]. Similarly, Clostridium butyricum has been shown to inhibit the progression of chronic colitis to cancer in murine models, as evidenced by decreased Ki67 expression and increased TUNEL-positive epithelial indices. This probiotic also reduces levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, and COX-2 [50]. Additionally, Prevotella enhances anti-inflammatory responses by upregulating ovalbumin-specific T cells and reducing IFN-γ + T cell populations, while F. fischeri amplifies its anti-inflammatory activity by increasing IL-10 production [51]. The reduced abundance of these beneficial bacteria weakens their anti-inflammatory capabilities, thereby elevating the risk of CRC.

The metabolites produced by intestinal microbiota play a significant role in the pathogenesis of CRC. Compounds such as ammonia, phenols, and hydrogen sulfide, which are byproducts of microbial metabolism, contribute to CRC development by inducing chronic inflammation and causing DNA damage. Additionally, N-nitroso compounds (NOCs), nitrogen-containing metabolites generated by microbial activity, exert carcinogenic effects through DNA alkylation, leading to genomic instability and tumor initiation [52]. Furthermore, gallic acid, a microbial metabolite, has been shown to disrupt the inhibitory function of mutant p53 on the Wnt signaling pathway. This disruption promotes the malignant proliferation of intestinal cells driven by Wnt activation, further facilitating CRC progression [53].

These findings highlight the critical role of microbiota-derived metabolites in CRC development and underscore the importance of understanding their mechanisms to identify potential therapeutic targets.

Epigenetic regulation

CpG island methylation phenotype (CIMP) is common in CRC, which leads to hypermethylation inactivation of tumor suppressor genes (such as MLH1, CDKN2A) promoter, and is related to MSI-H/dmmr phenotype and immune therapy response [54]. Aberrant methylation can also drive the activation of Wnt pathway. For example, methylation of APC gene promoter can be detected in the early stage of sporadic CRC [55].

Overexpression of histone deacetylases (HDACs) promotes the proliferation of CRC cells by inhibiting the acetylation of tumor suppressor proteins such as p53 and Rb [56]. HDAC inhibitors (such as vorinostat) can enhance the antitumor effect of 5- fluorouracil in preclinical models [57]. The mutation of ARID1A, a member of SWI/SNF complex, leads to abnormal chromatin structure, which is related to the microsatellite stability (MSS) phenotype and immunotherapy resistance of CRC [58].

Treatment based on molecular mechanism of colorectal cancer

CRC remains a major global health challenge due to its high incidence and mortality rates. The disease is characterized by complex molecular mechanisms and significant heterogeneity, which often limit the efficacy of conventional treatments such as surgery combined with radiotherapy and chemotherapy. In recent years, however, the landscape of CRC treatment has evolved considerably with the advent of novel therapeutic approaches. The introduction of neoadjuvant therapies, particularly immunotherapy and targeted therapy, has significantly enhanced patient outcomes and survival rates. These advancements highlight the critical need for ongoing research and innovation to address the diverse and intricate nature of CRC, ultimately improving therapeutic efficacy and patient quality of life.

Immunotherapy

Tumor immunotherapy has emerged as a groundbreaking approach in cancer treatment, primarily targeting mechanisms of tumor immune escape. This innovative strategy encompasses immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), tumor vaccines, and adoptive cell therapy [59]. ICIs function by blocking immune checkpoints (ICs) expressed on tumor cells or immune cells, thereby disrupting co-inhibitory signaling pathways and reactivating anti-tumor immune responses to achieve tumor cell elimination [60]. Common targets of ICIs include programmed death-1 (PD-1), programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1), and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4). Based on the DNA mismatch repair (MMR)/microsatellite instability (MSI) classification system, CRC can be categorized into dMMR/MSI-H and pMMR/MSS subtypes. dMMR/MSI-H CRC, characterized by high levels of neoantigens and lymphocyte infiltration, is often referred to as a “hot tumor.” This subtype typically exhibits a high tumor mutation burden and is considered highly suitable for ICI therapy. In contrast, pMMR/MSS CRC, which demonstrates lower sensitivity to conventional immunotherapy, is classified as a “cold tumor.” To address this limitation, clinical strategies often combine ICIs with other treatments to modify the tumor microenvironment, thereby enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy [61–63]. These advancements underscore the potential of immunotherapy in transforming CRC treatment, particularly for patients with specific molecular profiles.

Tumor vaccines represent a novel and promising approach in cancer immunotherapy, designed to elicit an immune response by introducing tumor antigenic substances into patients. This process enhances the level of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), leading to the targeted destruction of antigen-expressing tumor cells and providing sustained immune surveillance [64]. Tumor vaccines primarily include dendritic cell (DC) vaccines, oncolytic virus vector vaccines, and tumor peptide vaccines. DC vaccines function by introducing tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) into dendritic cells, which process and present these antigens to CD8 + T cells in the form of peptide-major histocompatibility complexes (pMHC). This presentation promotes the proliferation and differentiation of T cells into cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) while simultaneously activating B lymphocytes, thereby engaging both cellular and humoral immune responses to achieve potent anti-tumor effects. Additionally, DCs can activate T cells through surface co-stimulatory molecules, enabling them to identify and eliminate tumor cells [65]. Oncolytic virus vector vaccines utilize engineered viruses that selectively infect and lyse tumor cells. Upon infection, oncolytic viruses (OVs) release immunogenic factors that recruit and mature antigen-presenting cells (APCs), ultimately activating both CD4 + and CD8 + T cells to generate a robust anti-tumor immune response. For example, clinical studies have demonstrated reduced treatment-related adverse events in CRC patients following the targeted intravenous infusion of a novel Ad11p/Ad3 chimeric adenovirus (enadenotucirev) [66]. In vitro studies have also identified a novel oncolytic virus, NGOVM (M1), derived from the directed evolution of refractory CRC tumor cells (HCT-116). This virus exhibits potent oncolytic activity and high safety profiles, although further clinical validation is required [67]. Peptide vaccines, a form of molecular vaccine, work by internalizing tumor antigens into APCs after administration. These antigens are then transported to lymph nodes, where they activate cytotoxic T lymphocytes through the antigen presentation system, leading to tumor cell killing. Alternatively, peptide vaccines can stimulate B cells to produce antibodies, thereby achieving anti-tumor effects. The efficacy of peptide vaccines has been validated in clinical trials for colorectal cancer [68, 69]. Collectively, these tumor vaccine strategies highlight the potential of immunotherapy to harness the immune system for targeted and sustained anti-tumor responses, offering new avenues for CRC treatment.

Immune cell therapy, also referred to as adoptive cell therapy, is an innovative treatment approach that achieves active immunity by administering tumor-specific cytotoxic live cells to patients [70]. Based on the methods used to manipulate immune cells ex vivo, this therapy can be categorized into non-genetically engineered immune cell therapy—including tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy, cytokine-induced killer (CIK) cell therapy, and dendritic cell (DC)/CIK cell therapy—and genetically engineered immune cell therapy, such as T-cell receptor (TCR)-T cells, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells, and CAR-NK therapy. Among these, CIK therapy has been the most extensively studied in the context of colorectal cancer (CRC). CIK cells exert their anti-tumor effects through multiple mechanisms: they can directly induce tumor cell death via death ligands such as Fas-L or release cytotoxic granules containing perforin and granzymes. Additionally, CIK cells secrete cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), which contribute to indirect tumor killing by modulating the tumor microenvironment [71]. Adoptive cell therapy represents a promising therapeutic strategy for CRC; however, its full potential remains to be realized through further clinical validation. By optimizing pre-treatment regimens and targeting novel tumor antigens, this approach holds significant promise for achieving clinical translation and improving outcomes in CRC treatment.

Targeted therapy

Targeted therapy for CRC focuses on specific molecular targets involved in tumorigenesis and progression, aiming to inhibit tumor growth and metastasis. Compared to traditional chemotherapy, molecular targeted therapy offers higher efficacy and lower toxicity, making it a cornerstone of modern CRC treatment. The development of targeted therapies for CRC primarily stems from inhibitors of the EGFR and VEGF signaling pathways, which have demonstrated significant benefits in first-line treatment.

The Wnt signaling pathway, whose dysregulation is a key driver of CRC, includes critical components such as secreted Wnt family proteins, casein kinase 1, dishevelled proteins, and β-catenin. Mutations in these components lead to the overexpression of oncogenes, and inhibition of the Wnt pathway has shown promise in preventing CRC development. Current strategies targeting this pathway include porcupine inhibitors (such as lgk974), β-Catenin degradation agents and tankyrase inhibitors (such as xav939), but their efficacy may be limited by bypass activation or β-Catenin mutation. Additionally, inhibitors targeting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway have been effective in controlling tumor growth by modulating the expression of tumor-related factors. For instance, studies [72] have identified deubiquitinase 46 (USP46) as a crucial regulator of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Downregulation of USP46 enhances the sensitivity of CRC cells to oxaliplatin, inhibits proliferation and metastasis, and promotes apoptosis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.

The dual role of the TGF-β signaling pathway in CRC is particularly noteworthy. While TGF-β initially acts as a tumor suppressor, it can transition into a tumor promoter during metastasis. Research [73] has shown that TGF-β promotes tumor invasion and metastasis by enhancing interactions between cancer cells and the extracellular matrix, stimulating angiogenesis, and suppressing immune responses. A key mechanism underlying this process is EMT, which is primarily driven by TGF-β. Consequently, inhibitors of the TGF-β pathway hold significant potential for suppressing tumor metastasis.

KRAS mutations, particularly in the KRAS gene, are prevalent in CRC and have historically been challenging to target. However, the development of KRAS inhibitors, such as sotorasib, marks a historic breakthrough. In the CodeBreaK 100 study, the combination of sotorasib and EGFR inhibitors achieved a disease control rate of 90%. A phase III randomized controlled trial (CodeBreaK 300) is currently underway, with promising preliminary results at a median follow-up of 7.8 months [74].

For patients with BRAF mutations, who often have poor prognoses, multi-target combination therapies have shown efficacy. In clinical studies, patients with BRAF-mutant CRC were randomized to receive either a triple-target regimen (encorafenib + binimetinib + cetuximab), a double-target regimen (encorafenib + cetuximab), or a control treatment. The triple-target regimen demonstrated superior efficacy in second-line treatment, particularly for patients with high tumor burden, extensive metastasis, and severe tumor-related symptoms [75, 76].

Furthermore, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines [77] recommend anti-HER2 therapy for CRC cases with HER2 amplification in palliative settings. The combination of trastuzumab and pertuzumab has demonstrated robust anti-tumor activity in refractory CRC, as evidenced by the MyPathway and TAPUR studies.Among the new targeted therapies, bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) and antibody drug conjugates (ADCs) are representative in the treatment of CRC. Bispecific antibodies can simultaneously target two kinds of tumor antigens or antigen immune cell molecules to overcome the limitations of single target therapy. For example, egfr/cd3 bispecific antibody can induce the specific killing of T cells to tumors by connecting EGFR on the surface of CRC cells with CD3 molecules on the surface of T cells, and has shown the efficacy on KRAS mutant CRC in preclinical models [78]. ADCs combine cytotoxic drugs with targeted antibodies through linker to achieve precise tumor killing [79].

In summary, targeted therapies have revolutionized CRC treatment by addressing specific molecular pathways, offering personalized and effective options for patients. Ongoing research and clinical trials continue to expand the therapeutic landscape, providing hope for improved outcomes in CRC management.

Adjuvant therapy with traditional Chinese medicine

With the growing integration of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) into the treatment of malignant tumors, research into potential TCM-based therapies for CRC using network pharmacology has gained significant momentum. For instance, Salvia miltiorrhiza has demonstrated promising anti-tumor properties in CRC. Through network pharmacology analysis, 39 active compounds were identified from Salvia miltiorrhiza, targeting 544 genes associated with CRC. Among these, three key targets—SRC, IL6, and INS—were identified as critical mediators. Molecular docking studies revealed strong binding affinities between Salvia miltiorrhiza and these targets, while in vitro experiments confirmed its ability to inhibit CRC progression by modulating the INS/SRC/IL6 signaling pathway [80]. Berberine, sanguinarine, and chelerythrine, three natural alkaloids, exhibit significant inhibitory effects on colorectal cancer tumor growth and EMT by targeting crucial signaling pathways including mTOR, MAPK, EGFR, PI3K/Akt, and NF-κB. They possess the ability to induce apoptosis and autophagy in cancer cells, along with exhibiting immunoregulatory properties. When combined with chemotherapy drugs such as 5-fluorouracil and oxaliplatin, they demonstrate synergistic enhancement effects, with berberine additionally capable of reversing cisplatin resistance. These alkaloids offer promising new potential options for the treatment of colorectal cancer; however, further research and validation are required for their clinical application [81]. Calebin A and curcumin, as polyphenolic components extracted from turmeric (Curcuma longa), augment the sensitivity of colorectal cancer cells to chemotherapeutic agents such as 5-FU and reverse drug resistance by modulating signaling pathways involved in inflammation, proliferation, and apoptosis. Their multitarget mechanism offers a supplementary approach to traditional chemotherapy and holds clinical translation potential in addressing tumor drug resistance [82]. In the study by Efferth et al. artesunate demonstrated broad-spectrum antitumor activity by inducing iron-dependent reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, leading to DNA damage, cell cycle arrest, and caspase-mediated apoptosis in various cancer cell lines. Although CRC was not specifically examined, the proposed mechanism—particularly its reliance on intracellular iron levels—has direct implications for CRC, as later studies confirmed its efficacy in CRC models through ROS-p53 activation and Wnt/β-catenin pathway inhibition. Additionally, artesunate’s ability to overcome drug resistance suggests potential for combination therapies with conventional CRC treatments like 5-FU. While this foundational study did not focus on CRC, its findings paved the way for subsequent research validating artesunate’s role in CRC suppression via oxidative stress and apoptosis induction [83].

These studies underscore the value of network pharmacology in elucidating the mechanisms of TCM-based therapies and their potential applications in CRC treatment. TCM offers a holistic approach to cancer therapy, paving the way for further exploration and clinical validation. However, TCM encounters three pivotal challenges in the clinical translation of colorectal cancer treatment: the mechanism of action of its complex components requires in-depth pharmacological validation, standardization in preparation and quality control poses difficulties, and clinical research designs must adhere to international standards. Moving forward, it is imperative to leverage multi-omics technologies to advance evidence-based medical research and achieve breakthroughs.

Summary

Through in-depth research into the molecular mechanisms and treatment strategies of CRC, we have identified key molecules, including oncogenes such as BRAF, PIK3CA, and K-ras, as well as tumor suppressor genes like APC, p53, and PTEN. Their mutations and abnormal expressions drive the abnormal activation of signaling pathways such as Wnt/β-Catenin and PI3K/Akt, which are closely associated with tumor cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and immune evasion. Additionally, cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 in the chronic inflammatory microenvironment, along with intestinal microbial dysbiosis (such as overproliferation of Fusobacterium nucleatum), significantly contribute to the carcinogenic process. These discoveries offer potential targets for precision treatment, including EGFR, VEGF, and HER2. Immunotherapy targeting these tumor-associated molecules (such as PD-1 inhibitors), molecular targeted therapy (such as anti-HER2 bispecific antibodies and ADC drugs), and adjuvant therapy with traditional Chinese medicine are strategies that can effectively curb the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of tumor cells, while augmenting the anti-tumor immune response. Despite these advancements, significant challenges persist, including variability in treatment sensitivity due to tumor heterogeneity, acquired drug resistance (such as KRAS mutant tumors becoming resistant to EGFR inhibitors), and the intricate tumor microenvironment (characterized by immunosuppressive cell infiltration and disrupted cytokine networks). Tumor cells may evade targeted therapy through mutations or epigenetic modifications (such as DNA methylation and histone modifications), and the presence of immunosuppressive cells and disrupted cytokine networks within the tumor microenvironment may further compromise therapeutic efficacy. In addition, the implementation of individualized treatment strategies is impeded by the technical barriers in biomarker screening, such as the costs involved in single-cell sequencing and multi-omics analysis, as well as the high costs associated with advanced treatments like CAR-T cell therapy and novel ADC drugs. To address these challenges, future efforts should concentrate on integrating multi-omics analysis (genome, transcriptome, microbiome) with innovative immunotherapy approaches (such as bispecific antibodies and personalized tumor vaccines). Furthermore, exploring the modulatory role of intestinal microbiota regulation (including probiotics intervention and fecal microbiota transplantation) on treatment response is crucial, aiming to overcome current treatment limitations and enhance the prognosis for colorectal cancer patients.
