Multi-scale analysis of urban forests and socioeconomic patterns in a desert city, Phoenix, Arizona
Authors: Zhe Wang, Chao Fan, Xiang Que, Felix Haifeng Liao, Xiaogang Ma, Hui Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Understanding the relationship between various socioeconomic factors and urban forest structure is essential for directing resources to ensure equitable distribution of green space. Through a case study of a desert city, i.e., Phoenix, AZ, this study provides a novel application of Multiscale Geographically Weighted Regression (MGWR) in which we explore the spatially variable relationships between a wide array of socioeconomic indicators and urban forest attributes. Through the computation of various scales of influence for different explanatory variables, MGWR enhances our analysis’s precision and stresses the association between socioeconomic status and urban forest structure at local and regional scales. Our results indicate that although there has been a pattern of green inequality where minority and low-income communities have less access to urban forests, education levels were mostly insignificant based on the MGWR results. In some instances, higher incomes are negatively correlated with tree canopy coverage. Additionally, the stem density model outperformed the canopy coverage model in terms of prediction accuracy. This research adds a new dimension to urban forestry literature and emphasizes the value of customized urban planning strategies and the environmental justice implications of urban forestry, particularly in arid environments.

Introduction

Urban forests, including parks, gardens, street trees, and greenways1, are essential to sustainable urban development. Urban forests provide vital ecosystem services that benefit both humans and the urban environment. They help improve air quality, conserve energy, reduce carbon emissions, and mitigate the urban heat island effect2–6. Furthermore, urban forests strengthen social cohesion and add economic value to urban communities5,7.

As a critical urban forest attribute, tree canopy cover is widely investigated in urban forest studies8–10. Tree canopy cover is defined as the ground area that is covered by a layer of tree leaves, branches, and stems11 Stem density, defined as the number of tree stems in a unit area12, is another important attribute for defining forest structure Stem density is frequently used to assess spatiotemporal patterns of tree mortality and forest succession dynamics13. It is often used in silvicultural studies to assess forest structure and health and has been increasingly adopted in urban forestry research to quantify tree distribution patterns and understand their relationship with various ecological and socioeconomic factors14,15. The combination of tree canopy cover and stem density offers a comprehensive assessment of urban forest structure, enabling more precise management by distinguishing areas with sufficient canopy but low stem density that may require replanting, from areas with high stem density yet insufficient canopy that may benefit from tree thinning and maintenance16.

Existing literature has highlighted key linkages between urban forests and the socioeconomic status of residents. Socioeconomic indicators can affect the quality and quantity of nature in an urban environment where localized benefits of trees, parks, and other green spaces are often considered a key aspect of environmental justice17–19. For example, residents with higher socioeconomic statuses are found to live in neighborhoods with higher plant diversity, and these residents also tend to live in areas with more local parks that have natural remnant ecosystems18. Peterson et al. (2008) pointed out that residents who live in neighborhoods with more green space are older, better educated, and more environmentally conscious than those who live in less vegetated areas20. Many studies have identified a positive relationship between urban tree canopy cover and income14,21–23.

Further, ethnicity was associated with the pattern of urban forest distribution. Neighborhoods with higher proportions of racial minorities had lower levels of urban tree canopy coverage and were less likely to gain tree canopy over time24. Watkins et. Gerrish (2019) conducted a meta-analysis to particularly discuss the relationship between urban forests and race and concluded that urban forest is inequitably distributed by race, with less cover in minority communities25. Martin et al. (2004) noted that the percentage of Hispanic/Latino residents was positively related to the abundance of Oleander – an evergreen plant in Phoenix, AZ26. Schwarz et al. (2015) identified low tree canopy cover in communities with a high concentration of racial and ethnic minorities in Sacramento and Los Angeles, California10. Troy et al. (2007) discovered a positive association between the percentage of African-American households and the percentage of tree canopy cover in Baltimore, MD27.

Previous studies mostly focused on using correlation analysis and global regression models to understand the relationships between urban forest characteristics and socioeconomic composition10,28–31. Grove et al. (2014) found a positive correlation between the urban tree canopy and housing density in New York City32. However, Fan et al. (2019) and Iverson and Cook (2000) reported a negative association in Chicago14,33. Moreover, Sorrensen et al. (2013) indicated that home age was positively associated with urban tree cover in Lubbock, Texas30. However, Conway and Hackworth (2007) found an opposite association in Toronto, Canada34. The physical benefits of urban forests are more regional35. It is recognized that the relationship between urban forest attributes and socioeconomic determinants is not constant and tends to vary widely over space. Conventional global models are no longer sufficient to explain the spatial distribution of urban forest and the relationships between these factors.

Geographically weighted regression (GWR) was a popular spatial statistics tool that allowed the relationship to vary spatially36. GWR allows a more nuanced understanding of the relationships in the equity of tree distribution in Phoenix35. However, in a complex urban environment like Phoenix, the impact of socioeconomic status on urban forestry can vary across different neighborhoods, districts, or even block groups. For instance, the median household income shows a local association with urban tree coverage locally but will be less apparent citywide21. Similarly, cultural preferences for landscaping, which can influence tree planting and maintenance, might vary greatly within small communities but could be less distinct at a larger scale37.

Considering these varying scales, the basic GWR was expanded and refined in its subsequent variant, the Multiscale Geographically Weighted Regression model (MGWR), which allowed for a more flexible approach to addressing local, regional, or even uniform spatial variations. The variations of spatial associations between the dependent variable and any independent variable can be explored by employing MGWR38, leading to improved model accuracy and comprehensive analysis, which is crucial for effective urban planning and policymaking.

Hence, by coupling remote sensing and MGWR modeling, this paper examines the varying spatial scales of relationships between the spatial pattern of urban forests and socioeconomic factors at a neighborhood scale in Phoenix, Arizona. Specifically, we aim to (1) explore urban forest patterns and socioeconomic inequity; (2) understand the relationships between urban forest characteristics and socioeconomic indicators at varying spatial scales; and (3) compare the impacts of different urban forest characteristics on various socioeconomic groups.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study area is the city of Phoenix, located in Maricopa County, Arizona, which encompasses 911 block groups (Fig. 1, created by ArcGIS Pro 3.339). Phoenix has a typical desert climate with extremely long, hot summers and short, mild winters. As of 2019, Phoenix is the 5th most populous city in the United States, with an estimated population of around 1.63 million40. The city is located within one of the sunniest regions in the world, with more than 300 days of sunshine annually. Being one of the fastest-growing regions in the US, Phoenix is known for its urban heat island (UHI) effect, the size and intensity of which have mounted rapidly in recent years (e.g., Hoehne et al.(2022)41).

Data processing

Tree canopy cover was extracted from the 2015 land use and land cover (LULC) map created by the Central Arizona–Phoenix Long-Term Ecological Research (CAPLTER)42. The LULC map was originally generated from the 2015 National Agriculture Imagery Program (NAIP) with a spatial resolution of 1 m43. The percentage of tree canopy cover (PTCC) was calculated at the block group level. The Arizona State University Map and Spatial Hub provided tree location data44. The tree location data were generated from the September/October 2014 LiDAR dataset with a spatial resolution of 0.5 m. The LiDAR data were sourced from the United States Geological Survey (USGS) three-dimensional elevation program (3DEP)45. Stem density (SD) was calculated as the number of trees in a block group over its area. Table 1 shows the PTCC and SD summary statistics at the block group level. Tree canopy cover ranges from 0 to 36% across 911 block groups in Phoenix. The mean PTCC was only 8.2. The SD ranges from 0 to 23.33/acre, with an average of 6.95 stems /acre.

Socioeconomic indicators

The socioeconomic data at the block group level were sourced from the 2010–2014 American Community Survey (ACS) 5-year estimate46. Table 2 shows the descriptive statistics of all socioeconomic variables collected. We included factors reflecting wealth (median household income, percent of poverty), demographics (population density, median age, percent associate degree or above), minority information (percent of residents that are Black, Hispanic, or Latino), ethnic information (percent of residents that are White) and building characteristics (density of housing units, median building age, or median housing value). The density of housing units was calculated as the total number of housing units in a block group divided by the block group area. The median building age in 2014 was obtained by subtracting the median year the house was built from 2014, and the median resident age was the median age of all residents in a block group.

Thirdly, the GWR models were used to understand and visualize the local variations in the relationships. Results

Urban forest structure

Most urban forest studies have used canopy cover to represent forest structure. The term “Urban Tree Canopy” (UTC) refers to the layer of tree leaves, branches, and stems that cover the ground1. The PTCC was calculated as the proportion of land surface covered by treetops within each block group area. SD is another key characteristic of forest structure, calculated as the density of the number of stems in each block group area.

Figure 2 shows the spatial distribution of PTCC and SD (tree/acre) by block group. The block groups with a higher PTCC (higher than 8.83%) were mainly located on the east side of the study area. Even though the highest PTCC is 35%, nearly one-third of block groups had a PTCC under 5%. In 2010, tree canopy was estimated to cover 9.0% of the study area47. In 2015, based on the data we collected, the tree canopy cover was 6.12% in the study area. The tree canopy cover had a 2.88% decrease, which accounts for ~ 6702 acres. The SD ranges from 0 to 23.3 trees/acre in 2014. Figure 2 indicates that areas with a high SD also tend to have a high PTCC. However, some high-PTCC areas in the west side had a very low SD (< 10 trees/acre).

Spatial patterns of the MGWR estimates

The MGWR model’s incorporation of different bandwidths for each explanatory variable is a novel computational approach that allows nuanced spatial analyses. Bandwidths serve as a tuning parameter that defines the spatial context in which local regression coefficients are estimated. By enabling each predictor to have a distinct bandwidth, the MGWR model becomes highly adaptive, adjusting to the spatial heterogeneity inherent in the data. This adaptability is essential for capturing the complex, multi-scale spatial relationships between predictors and the response variable.

In this study, the bandwidth values varying from 1 to 911 offer a spectrum of spatial resolutions from very local to essentially global, where lower bandwidth values capture finer, local variations, and higher values encompass broader, more global patterns across the study area. The use of a bandwidth of 911 effectively transforms the MGWR into a global regression model, smoothing out local variations and focusing on overarching spatial trends. The division of variables into local, regional, and global categories based on their bandwidths is a methodological advancement that aids in interpreting spatial processes. By setting a bandwidth threshold of 100 to demarcate local from regional variables, this study offers a pragmatic and context-sensitive approach to categorizing spatial influences.

Figure 3’s depiction of the spatial distribution of significant coefficient estimates for the percentage of Black residents within the PTCC and SD models provides critical insights into the spatial dynamics in effect. The more significant areas in the PTCC model than in the SD model suggest a more pronounced and widespread relationship between tree canopy cover and the percentage of Black households. The universal significance across all block groups in the PTCC model underscores a strong and pervasive negative association, in line with the average coefficients presented in Table 5. Conversely, despite an overall negative mean coefficient, the SD model’s presentation of positive relationships in the city’s northwest illustrates spatial inconsistencies that may indicate underlying socio-environmental factors or data idiosyncrasies.

Figure 4 delineates the varying relationships among the percentage of Hispanic or Latino residents, displaying a uniform negative association across significant cases in both the SD and PTCC models. This uniformity echoes the findings tabulated in Tables 3, 4 and 5, reinforcing the robustness of the observed relationships. The bandwidths employed in the PTCC and SD models reveal different scales of spatial influence for the percentage of Hispanic or Latino residents. According to the PTCC model, a bandwidth of 43 suggests that the percentage of Hispanic or Latino residents operates as a local variable, influencing the tree cover in a more immediate and narrowly defined spatial context. This is visually supported by Fig. 4a, where significant cases are concentrated within a limited number of block groups, underscoring the localized nature of the variable’s impact.

Conversely, the SD model’s bandwidth of 440 positions the percentage of Hispanic or Latino residents as a regional variable. This broader bandwidth indicates that the percentage of the Hispanic or Latino population affects SD over a more extensive area, which is evident in Fig. 4b as the coefficient estimates span many block groups with similar value ranges. The distinction between the PTCC model’s local influence and the SD model’s regional effect highlights the spatial complexity of demographic impacts on SD. The contrast in the number of significant block groups between the SD and PTCC models further accentuates the importance of selecting an appropriate bandwidth for each predictor. With its regional bandwidth, the SD model captures a more expansive pattern of significance, potentially reflecting underlying regional trends or broader socio-spatial dynamics. In contrast, the PTCC model, which focuses on local variation, might reflect more immediate environmental or social factors specific to smaller areas.

Figure 5 elucidates the spatial distribution of significant estimates for median household income, revealing nuanced differences between the PTCC and SD models. The designation of median household income as a local variable in both models suggests a focused spatial impact on two urban forest variables. Yet, the number of significant cases is greater in the SD model, indicating a more pronounced effect within its framework.

Correlation analysis alongside OLS findings indicates a positive relationship between PTCC and median household income, hinting at a potential socioeconomic gradient in urban forestry distribution (Tables 3 and 4). Despite this upward positive trend, Fig. 5a presents a complex local reality. At the same time, the east of the study area exhibits a pocket of positive relationships, and the mean estimate across the study area remains negative. This is attributed to the stronger negative effects observed in the northwest, pointing to localized disparities that may reflect an uneven distribution of economic and environmental resources. In contrast, the SD model consistently aligns with the correlation analysis, manifesting positive associations throughout all examined cases. This uniform positivity correlates with the aggregate mean coefficient found in Table 5, suggesting that the SD model may capture broader socioeconomic factors influencing median household income. The divergence between the two models indicates the importance of considering spatial scale when interpreting the relationship between socioeconomic variables and urban environmental features.

Discussion

The current state of Phoenix’s urban forest is a pressing concern due to its low tree canopy coverage compared to the urban tree cover average globally (26.5%)48. In response, the City of Phoenix Parks and Recreation Department formulated a master plan in 2010 to achieve a 25% tree canopy cover by 203049. However, in 2005, the estimated tree canopy cover was only 13%, declining to 9.0% in 201050, and reaching 6.12% in 2015 based on collected data. Figure 2illustrates that despite the highest PTCC being 35%, over a third of block groups have a tree cover percentage of less than 5%. However, until now, the report estimated that only 9% of Phoenix has the tree canopies51. Furthermore, in most low-PTCC block groups, the tree density is only 0–6 trees per acre. Conversely, SD is higher than six trees per acre in block groups within the city center, while tree cover is lower than 7%. These findings echoed what has been discussed recently that wealthier neighborhoods, particularly in central and east Phoenix, have a higher percentage of tree canopy coverage compared to lower-income areas in south and west Phoenix52. In response to this, The City of Phoenix has partnered with American Forests to promote Tree Equity, intending to achieve a minimal standard of tree canopy cover for all Phoenix’s neighborhoods by 203053.

In Phoenix, urban heat island effects disproportionately impact low-income communities, while affluent neighborhoods experience lower temperatures54. Increasing tree cover can significantly reduce summer daytime temperatures by potentially 1.97 °C if implemented citywide54. Access to green spaces in Phoenix shows disparities based on age and income rather than race or ethnicity55. Urban heat island (UHI) effects in dryland regions differ from wetter areas, often producing daytime “urban cool islands” and nighttime UHIs. Urban forests and irrigated landscapes have been found to cool daytime temperatures significantly, while xeriscaping tends to amplify heating. These findings suggest that shade-maximizing urban forestry strategies may be more effective in reducing heat islands in dryland cities than xeriscape approaches56. In desert environments, where more trees don’t always mean larger canopy areas, such detailed analysis is key to achieving effective and more targeted greening strategies. Therefore, in urban forestry, especially in desert cities like Phoenix, it’s vital to consider a combination of SD and PTCC.

In this investigation, we employed correlation analysis, OLS, GWR, and MGWR models to examine the interplay between urban forest characteristics and socioeconomic indicators within the diverse metropolitan area of Phoenix, Arizona. The correlation and OLS outcomes offer a comprehensive overview of the associations between PTCC and SD and the socioeconomic variables across the study area. Notably, marginalized and low-income communities need more access to natural amenities. Furthermore, in the MGWR results, communities with a greater abundance of trees and shrubs demonstrated a positive correlation with higher educational attainment, housing densities, and median household income. Except for the educational background, these observations align with prior studies, underscoring the unequal distribution of trees, with neighborhoods characterized by lower income or predominantly comprising people of color exhibiting diminished tree cover22,25,35,57,58.

MGWR gives a more detailed view of the coefficient estimates and their spatial variation at different scales. Figures 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7 shows that the magnitude and significance of the coefficients vary across the study area. Not every variable significantly predicts PTCC and SD within each block group. Minority populations have been discussed in many other papers; all of which have limited access to more trees10,14,24,59. However, MGWR results in this study indicate positive associations between the percent of Blacks and SD but negative associations with PTCC in the northwest corner of Phoenix (Fig. 3b). That region has a higher percentage of White, fewer black residents, and fewer Hispanic or Latino residents (Fig. 8). Compared to people who lived in other parts of the city, black residents and Hispanic or Latino residents in those areas had greater access to green space. However, the variation of the magnitude of PTCC is not consistent with SD in that specific region (Fig. 2). The shift of the magnitude of PTCC and SD are primarily similar in other areas. The anticipated finding is that the relationships between the percent of Hispanic or Latino and PTCC are only significant in several block groups, even though the OLS provides a significant result globally. In contrast, much more significant relationships are found in the SD model. This inconsistency may be due to the unique urban forest structure in Phoenix that is different from other cities.

Income is another essential indicator that has been discussed a lot in previous studies. Urban forests are unequally distributed, with lower-income communities having less urban tree cover. Gerrish and Watkins (2018) conducted a study to discuss the relationship between urban forest and income. Their results revealed significant income-based inequity in urban forest and the inequity was robust across most method choices60. Similarly, multiple studies have found a strong positive correlation between urban tree canopy cover and median household income across various U.S. cities10,21,61. Correlation analysis and OLS results in this study support evidence from these earlier findings. However, in MGWR results, contrary to global relationships, a small number of significant cases were observed in the PTCC model result, while some negative associations were found in the northwest corner (Fig. 5a). In the context of Phoenix, which is characterized by its desert climate and unique socioeconomic dynamics, the negative association between income and PTCC may stem from some localized factors. Higher-income residents may opt for xeriscaping with drought-resistant plants over tree canopies in Phoenix’s desert environment to conserve water62. This could lead to a negative association between income and tree cover. Urban development patterns and property values in the northwest may also drive a preference for built-up spaces, restricting available land for tree planting. Additionally, cultural shifts towards minimalist landscaping and the urban heat island effect might influence wealthier communities to choose alternative cooling methods over traditional green spaces63,64.

As our results suggest, housing density positively correlates with both PTCC and SD. It is reasonable that trees can increase the properties’ aesthetic and economic values7,65. Therefore, most residents and real estate developers would like to plant more trees surrounding their property. Both models also observed significant cases in the south and north of the city. In other areas, the spatial distribution of housing density was more irregular, however, a regular distribution of PTCC and SD can be seen according to Fig. 2. This explains why there were no significant cases in these areas.

Previous literature has consistently shown a positive correlation between higher education levels and PTCC, suggesting that areas with a more educated population tend to have more tree coverage14,58,66. This relationship is often interpreted as reflecting greater environmental awareness and the higher socioeconomic status that comes with better education, leading to more demands for and the ability to maintain urban green spaces. In contrast, our study’s correlation analysis and OLS results indicate that the relationship between education level and two key urban forest variables, PCTT and SD, is weaker than previously reported14,67,68. The OLS method provides a global measure of association, which might dilute the localized effects in the spatial data.

By applying MGWR, which considers spatial heterogeneity, we found the education-PTCC association vanishes, highlighting Phoenix’s unique spatial dynamics. However, significant correlations between education level and SD in certain block groups were observed, suggesting that while education does not influence overall canopy coverage, it impacts tree density in specific neighborhoods. MGWR’s ability to detect these localized patterns emphasizes its utility in studying urban forestry, aiding targeted interventions in city planning.

This study’s findings suggest a strategic approach to tree planting in Phoenix is necessary, especially given a decline in tree cover from 2010 to 2015. The Phoenix government’s plan to educate residents about the benefits of urban trees is crucial, as it recognizes that simply increasing tree cover is not a perfect solution for disparity. A differentiated strategy, informed by this study’s results and mindful of local financial and policy constraints, can lead to more cost-effective interventions.

Spatial scale is a fundamental concept in geographical studies, yet there needs to be more methodological advancements for measuring geographic scale across different processes. Previous research highlights GWR as vital for understanding local relationships61,67. MGWR improves upon this by accommodating spatial heterogeneity more effectively, allowing for varied scales in processing each factor, thereby enhancing its suitability for policy recommendations. MGWR has shown superiority in representing parameter surfaces with varying spatial heterogeneity levels, offering insights into the scales at which different processes operate38.

In the MGWR analysis, the substantial variation in bandwidths for the independent variables across both PTCC and SD models signifies that each socioeconomic factor interacts with the urban forest attributes at different spatial extents. Several studies discuss the relationships at varying scales using the definition ‘global, regional, and local’49,50. Using these definitions to distinguish the results of MGWR models is more intuitive. In this study, the percentage of Black residents is a regional variable, while income and housing density are local variables in both PTCC and SD models. The percentage of Hispanic or Latino is local in the PTCC model but regional in the SD model. Education level is globally insignificant in the PTCC model but regionally significant in the SD model. OLS results demonstrate only global correlation relationships. GWR can provide local results, but GWR handles the same scale for all variables. By employing MGWR, researchers can discern local and regional effects, revealing the multi-scaled nature of urban ecological phenomena. For example, the PTCC might correlate with socioeconomic variables like household income or educational attainment at a neighborhood level, highlighting areas where targeted policies could enhance urban canopy coverage. Similarly, SD variations may emerge as significant in certain locales due to specific demographic or economic factors, suggesting opportunities for localized urban greening initiatives.

MGWR’s varied bandwidths underscore the need for tailored urban forestry policies. Localized bandwidths indicate the necessity for targeted interventions to combat environmental injustice, whereas broader bandwidths call for more general policy implementation. This highlights the heterogeneity of socioeconomic factors that may impact on urban forests and the importance of multi-scaled approaches in urban forestry research and planning. The significant bandwidth variations between socioeconomic variables in the PTCC and SD models emphasize considering diverse indicators in assessing and addressing urban forest structure and distribution.

Additionally, while multiple spatial scales are well-addressed, temporal scales are limited. Current models, like spatiotemporal weighted regression (STWR)69, consider variables’ temporal change rates, but a mature multiscale spatiotemporal model is lacking. This gap highlights a potential direction for further research, emphasizing the need for dynamic spatial coefficient surfaces to support multi-time scale exploration and analysis and longitudinal analysis of urban trees’ loss or gain (e.g., Foster et al. (2024)24).

Conclusion

This study focused on the socioeconomic indicators influencing urban forest structure in a desert city like Phoenix, which revealed several significant insights into the interplay between urban greenery and socioeconomic composition at the neighborhood level. This research is particularly pertinent given the unique environmental context of desert cities, which are currently understudied in urban forestry literature. One of the key contributions is the novel application of MGWR, a computational spatial statistical method, in urban forest management and planning. Through applying MGWR, we could discern the varying spatial relationships between socioeconomic factors and urban forest attributes. This nuanced approach allows different explanatory variables to operate at different scales and has led to more accurate and detailed insights than possible with traditional, single-scale methods. The MGWR analysis has highlighted the complex, multi-scale nature of urban socio-ecological systems, especially in a desert urban setting like Phoenix. Furthermore, the inclusion of SD alongside the more commonly used PTCC as indicators of urban forest structure has provided a more comprehensive understanding of urban greenery. This underlines the importance of considering multiple facets of urban forests in environmental justice studies. Nevertheless, the availability of SD data varies significantly from city to city. While some cities have comprehensive tree inventories, others may lack such high-resolution datasets, necessitating primary data collection efforts to ensure spatiotemporal consistency and reliability in future analyses.

Our findings indicate a pronounced inequality in urban tree distribution, with minority and low-income communities having reduced access to urban trees at multi-scales. This is a critical finding, emphasizing the need for equitable urban planning and policy-making that ensures all residents benefit from urban greenery while reiterating the presence of environmental injustice (e.g. Nelson et al. (2021)35). Intriguingly, we observed that education level was insignificant in most block groups from MGWR results. Additionally, higher income was negatively correlated with PTCC in some block groups, a finding contrary to common assumptions and previous studies. This suggests that socioeconomic influences on urban forest structure can be highly context specific.

The study conclusively demonstrates the effectiveness of MGWR in urban forest analysis, especially in understanding the multi-scale and localized nature of socioeconomic impacts on urban greenery. It reiterates the need for urban forestry research to adopt multi-scale analytical approaches to accurately capture urban ecosystems’ complex dynamics.

In summary, this study contributes to urban forestry literature by introducing new insights into the socioeconomic influences on urban forests in an arid urban setting. It highlights the necessity for context-specific, multi-faceted, and equitable approaches in urban forestry planning and policy. The unique findings from Phoenix reveal the importance of extending urban forestry research to diverse environmental contexts, ensuring a comprehensive understanding of the challenges and opportunities in urban green space management statement aided by using remotely sensed imagery and advanced spatial modeling methods (e.g., MGWR).
