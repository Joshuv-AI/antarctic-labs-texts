Modeling of the effect of gradation and compaction characteristics on the california bearing ratio of granular materials for subbase and landfill liner construction
Authors: Majed Alzara, Kennedy C. Onyelowe, Ahmed M. Ebid, Shadi Hanandeh, Ahmed M. Yosri, Talal O. Alshammari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The California bearing ratio (CBR) of a granular materials are influence by the soil particle distribution indices such as D10, D30, D50, and D60 and also the compaction properties such as the maximum dry density (MDD) and the optimum moisture content (OMC). For this reason, the particle packing and compactibility of the soil play a big role in the design and construction of subbases and landfills. In this research paper, experimental data entries have been collected reflecting the CBR behavior of granular soil used to construct landfill and subbase. The database was utilized in the ratio of 78–22% to predict the CBR behavior considering the artificial neural network (ANN), the evolutionary polynomial regression (EPR), the genetic programming (GP), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Random Forest (RF) and the response surface methodology (RSM) intelligent learning and symbolic abilities. The relative importance values for each input parameter were carried out, which indicated that the (CBR) value depends mainly on the average particle size (D30, 50 & 60). They showed a combined influence index of 66% of the considered parameters in the model exercise. This further shows the importance and structural influence of the particles within the D50 and D60 range in a granular material consistency in the design and construction purposes. Performance indices were also used to study the ability of the models. The ANN model showed the best performance with accuracy of 88%, then GP, EPR and RF with almost the same accuracies of 85% and lastly the XGBoost with accuracy of 81%. Also, the RSM produced an R2 of 0.9464 with a p-value of less than 0.0001. These values show that the ANN produced the decisive model with the superior performance indices in the forecast of CBR of granular material used as subbase and waste compacted earth liner material. The results further show that optimal performance of the CBR depended on D50 and D60 for the design of subgrade, subbase, and liner purposes and also during the performance monitoring phase of the constructed flexible pavement foundations and compacted earth liners.

1. Introduction

The particle distribution of granular materials used in landfill liner construction can significantly influence the California Bearing Ratio (CBR) of the material1,2. CBR is a measure of the relative strength of the granular material and its ability to support loads.The particle distribution refers to the size and gradation of particles within the granular material2. It includes the range of particle sizes and the proportion of different-sized particles present in the material. The particle distribution affects the overall packing and interlocking of particles, which in turn affects the material’s strength and load-bearing capacity. The distribution of particle sizes within the granular material affects its compaction characteristics. A well-graded material with a diverse range of particle sizes tends to have better interlocking and improved compaction compared to a poorly-graded material with a narrow range of particle sizes. Well-graded materials tend to have higher CBR values. The particle distribution can influence the optimum moisture content of the material during compaction. Different particle sizes require different moisture conditions to achieve maximum compaction density. The moisture content affects the interparticle friction and the ability of the material to resist deformation3–5. Improper moisture content can lead to inadequate compaction and lower CBR values. The shape of particles, such as angularity or roundness, can affect the interlocking and frictional characteristics of the granular material. Angular particles tend to interlock better, providing higher shear strength and CBR values compared to rounded particles. The gradation envelope represents the upper and lower limits of particle sizes allowed for a specific application. It ensures that the material has sufficient voids for drainage while preventing excessive particle movement. The gradation envelope specified for landfill liner construction is designed to meet the required permeability and strength criteria. In some cases, granular materials used in landfill liner construction may have a small proportion of fines (silt and clay particles). The presence of fines affects the plasticity index of the material. Higher plasticity index values indicate higher fines content, which can reduce the CBR value due to increased compressibility and reduced shear strength4. It is important to note that the influence of particle distribution on CBR is typically evaluated through laboratory testing and analysis. Engineers and geotechnical experts perform tests like the Proctor compaction test and CBR test to assess the material’s behavior and determine its suitability for landfill liner construction.

To optimal-model the CBR (California Bearing Ratio) of granular materials used in landfill liner construction, several factors should be considered3. The particle size distribution, or gradation, of the granular material plays a crucial role in CBR optimization. A well-graded material with a range of particle sizes allows for better compaction and interlocking, resulting in improved CBR values. The gradation should be within the specified gradation envelope that meets the project requirements. The angularity or shape of particles can affect the interlocking and frictional characteristics of the granular material. Angular particles tend to provide better interlocking and shear strength, leading to higher CBR values. Therefore, selecting granular materials with angular or rough surfaces can contribute to CBR optimization. The moisture content during compaction significantly affects the CBR of granular materials. It is important to determine the optimum moisture content (OMC) specific to the material being used. The OMC ensures that the material achieves maximum compaction density, resulting in improved CBR values. Careful control of moisture content during compaction is necessary to optimize the CBR. The compaction energy applied during construction affects the CBR of granular materials. Adequate compaction energy ensures better particle rearrangement and improved interlocking, leading to higher CBR values. It is important to use appropriate compaction equipment and techniques to achieve the desired level of compaction energy for optimal CBR. The fines content, including silt and clay particles, can affect the CBR of granular materials. Excessive fines content can increase compressibility and reduce shear strength, resulting in lower CBR values. It is important to control the fines content within the allowable limits specified for the landfill liner construction. The use of geosynthetic reinforcement, such as geogrids or geotextiles, can enhance the mechanical properties and CBR of granular materials. Geosynthetics provide additional tensile strength and confinement, improving the overall stability and load-bearing capacity of the liner system. Regular quality control and testing throughout the construction process are essential to ensure that the granular materials meet the required specifications. Testing methods such as Proctor compaction tests and CBR tests should be performed to monitor and verify the material’s compaction characteristics and CBR values.It is important to note that the optimization of CBR involves a comprehensive understanding of the project requirements, material properties, and construction techniques. Consulting with geotechnical engineers and following industry best practices can help ensure the successful optimization of granular materials for landfill liner construction in terms of CBR.

Granular materials are commonly used in landfill liner construction as part of waste management and disposal systems3,6,7. Landfills are engineered facilities designed to safely contain and manage solid waste. The liner system is a critical component of a landfill and is designed to prevent the migration of contaminants into the surrounding environment. Granular materials are used as a base or foundation layer for the landfill liner system. This layer, often referred to as the bottom liner or basal liner, is typically constructed using a compacted layer of granular material such as gravel or sand. The base liner provides a stable and permeable foundation for the subsequent liner layers. Above the base liner, a drainage layer is often incorporated into the landfill liner system. This layer consists of granular materials with high permeability, such as coarse sand or gravel. The drainage layer facilitates the collection and removal of leachate, which is the liquid that forms as water interacts with waste in the landfill. The granular drainage layer allows for efficient leachate flow and helps prevent the buildup of excess water within the landfill. The barrier layer, also known as the composite liner, is designed to prevent the migration of contaminants from the waste into the underlying soil and groundwater. It typically consists of a geomembrane liner and a compacted clay liner. Granular materials are used as protective layers above and below the clay liner to prevent damage during construction and provide stability. Granular materials, such as sand or geotextile fabric, are placed above the compacted clay liner to protect it from punctures or damage during construction activities. This layer acts as a cushioning material and provides additional stability. Granular materials are also used as a transition layer between the clay liner and the geomembrane liner. This layer, known as a geosynthetic clay liner (GCL), consists of a layer of clay bonded to a layer of geotextile. The granular materials within the GCL help distribute loads and provide a transition between the clay liner and the geomembrane liner. Granular materials are used in the final cover system of a landfill to provide a protective and aesthetically pleasing layer. The cover system helps minimize the infiltration of rainwater into the waste and prevents the release of odors or the attraction of vectors (such as pests). The cover system typically includes a layer of topsoil or engineered soil, which may contain granular materials such as sand or gravel to enhance its properties.In landfill liner construction, the selection and placement of granular materials are crucial to ensure proper compaction, stability, and permeability. The materials used should meet specific engineering and regulatory requirements to ensure the effective containment and management of waste, as well as the protection of the environment and public health6.

Optimizing the compaction behavior of granular materials for landfill liner construction and waste management can be a complex task. Machine learning (ML) techniques can be used to assist in this optimization process8. A high-level overview of how ML can be applied to optimize the compaction behavior of granular materials for landfill liner construction and waste management is as follows. (i) Data Collection: Gather data related to the granular materials, compaction process, and desired outcome. This may include information such as material properties, compaction parameters, and the resulting compaction behavior of the materials. (ii) Feature Selection: Identify relevant features from the collected data that may have an impact on the compaction behavior. These features could include particle size distribution, moisture content, compaction energy, and other relevant parameters. (iii) Data Preprocessing: Clean and preprocess the collected data to handle missing values, outliers, and normalize the data if necessary. This step ensures that the data is in a suitable format for ML algorithms. (iv) Algorithm Selection: Choose an appropriate ML algorithm based on the problem at hand. Some common algorithms suitable for regression tasks, which may be applicable in this case, include linear regression, decision trees, random forests, support vector machines (SVM), or neural networks. (v) Model Training: Split the preprocessed data into training and testing sets. Use the training set to train the ML model by fitting the selected algorithm to the data. The model learns the relationship between the input features (e.g., particle size distribution, moisture content) and the desired output (e.g., compaction behavior). (vi) Model Evaluation: Evaluate the trained model’s performance using the testing set. Metrics such as mean squared error (MSE), root mean squared error (RMSE), or coefficient of determination (R-squared) can be used to assess the model’s accuracy and predictive capability. (vii) Model Optimization: Adjust the model parameters or explore different algorithms to improve the model’s performance. This may involve fine-tuning hyperparameters, such as learning rate, regularization, or tree depth, to achieve better results. (viii) Prediction and Optimization: Once the model is trained and evaluated, it can be used to make predictions on new, unseen data. The model can help optimize the compaction behavior of granular materials by suggesting appropriate compaction parameters or identifying potential issues in the compaction process. (ix) Iterative Refinement: As more data becomes available or the compaction process evolves, the model can be periodically retrained and refined to incorporate new information and improve its predictions.It’s important to note that the success of ML optimization depends on the quality and representativeness of the data collected, as well as the choice of appropriate features and algorithms. Consulting with domain experts in waste management and geotechnical engineering can provide valuable insights and guidance throughout the optimization process.

2. Literature reviews

The most commonly utilized materials in pavement construction are unbound granular aggregates. They are employed in the sub-base and base layers beneath the certain course, and their qualities are critical to the pavement structure’s quality. An important part of pavement design is the design of the granular material to ensure its sufficient performance and stability. The stability is typically used to compare the crushing strength of base course materials to that of standard rock9.

The California bearing ratio (CBR) is the resistance ratio to the sinking of a 0.05 in/min (1.27 mm/min) entry piston into the soil to the resistance shown by a typical crushed rock sample for the similar penetration depth. CBR resistance is described as the ratio between the applied stress and the applied standard tension10. This ratio is based on a specific amount of energy that is used to compress the soil to a certain level of moisture. The CBR is determined using a penetration test, which indirectly assesses soil strength11, whereas optimum water content and maximum dry density are calculated by the modified Proctor test. The California bearing ratio experiment can be done in a lab or the real world. ASTM D1883-9912talks about the CBR test in the lab, and ASTM D1883-9912talks about the CBR test in the field. In the lab, the CBR test is usually done on samples of earth that have been packed down. In the field, the CBR test is done on the level surface or a ground surface dug out in a trench, test pit, or bulldozer cut13.These parameters are influenced by a variety of grain-related factors, including roundness, grain sphericity, compaction energy, smoothness, and grain size distribution14. Patel & Desai15stated that the CBR is influenced by the soil type and other soil parameters. An alternative to time-consuming tests like CBR is to forecast it using the index qualities of soils. According to Carter & Bentley16, CBR is significantly affected by factors such as density, soil type, moisture content, and procedure of sample preparation. The optimal moisture content, maximum dry density, plastic limit, liquid limit, type soil, plasticity index, the soil permeability, etc. are just a few of the variables that affect CBR, as described by Talukdar17. Yildirim & Gunaydin18utilized statistical methods to evaluate CBR based on Atterberg limits, sieve analysis, optimal moisture content, and maximum dry unit weight of soils.Several researchers have attempted to forecast soil CBR using elementary soil variables/parameters (compaction qualities, grain sizes, OMC, AL, MDD, etc.) by employing both simple and multiple regression algorithms. Bello19developed linear regressions associate CBR values with several lateritic soil indicator parameters such as (specific gravity, AL, and MDD depend on eight separate borrow pits within Oshogbo town in South Western Nigeria. As an additional means of estimating OMC, CBR, Los Angeles Abrasion (LAA), and MDD were incorporated into a regression model20. To estimate the CBR value using the plasticity index, OMC, and MDD. Shirur & Hiremath21developed both single and multiple linear regression models. The best linear link between MDD value and CBR was discovered using SLR and MLR analyses. Using eight distinct fine-grained soil samples, Nguyen & Mohajerani22 examined the influence of soil physical parameters on CBR values and discovered that CBR values were highly influenced by PI, moisture content, and MDD.

Numerous studies have been conducted over the years to develop a relation between CBR values23and soil index factors such as gradation properties, Atterberg limits, and compaction characteristics and many other important soil properties24–30. Using data from 57 soil samples, Kin31established a relationship between CBR and index parameters of fine-grained soil in Malaysia. Maximum dry density and optimal moisture content were found to have a positive relationship with CBR. Using compaction and index parameters of fine-grained soils in India, Ramasubbarao & Sankar32developed multiple linear regression (MLR) and simple linear regression (SLR) models to predict CBR values. The statistical metrics of the goodness of fit suggest that an MLR model with an R2 of 0.92 will get the best results. CBR values in Pakistani coarse-grained soils were estimated using MLR models established by Ul-Rehman et al33.. The optimal model connecting CBR values with D60 and MDD achieved an R2 of 0.88 and a validation error of 14.1%. Fine-grained soils in India were studied by Rakaraddi & Gomarsi34 who found a correlation between CBR and several soil characteristics. Predicting soaking CBR values using LL, OMC, MDD, and PI yielded R2of 0.96, making LL the most important parameter. CBR values of clayey soils can be accurately predicted using the regression models proposed by Aderinola35. These models take into account soil index features and compaction characteristics.

Yildirim & Gunaydin18examined the implementation of various techniques (artificial neural networks and multiple–simple analysis) for the assessment of the California bearing ratiofrom Atterberg limits, sieve analysis, optimum moisture content, and maximum dry unit weight of the soils. CBR, an archaic and still widely used experiment, is typically used to evaluate the resistance of granular soils in the subgrade layers and superstructure foundation. The data was collected from the various regions’ public highways in Turkey. It has been demonstrated that the regression analyses-derived correlation equations are in acceptable agreement with the test results. It is suggested that the proposed relationships will be beneficial for the primary design of a project with budgetary and time restrictions. Yideti et al36. examined how aggregate packing affects unbound material CBR values. A packing theory-based paradigm identifies the aggregate skeleton’s load-carrying portion. The experimentally obtained CBR values of 20 granular unbound materials in the literature are used to evaluate aggregate packing parameters that affect CBR performance. Granular materials’ CBR values are mostly determined by their load-carrying skeletal packing. Also, Khasawneh et al37. established a validated model for predicting California Bearing Ratio (CBR) values in subgrade soil by making use of soil index features through the application of machine learning and regression analysis methods. Artificial neural networks, K-nearest neighbor, and M5P Model tree are the machine-learning approaches that are incorporated into this model. Additionally, the model makes use of two standard modeling techniques, namely nonlinear regression (NLR) and multiple linear regression (MLR). Material properties predict CBR best, with R2 of 90.46 and RMSE of 7.89. Vu et al38. estimated the CBR using 10 input parameters using a Machine Learning (ML) strategy and Random Forest (RF) model. Standard statistical methods were applied to an experimental database consisting of 214 soil samples categorized according to AASHTO M 145 to assess the model’s efficacy. According to the findings, the RF model successfully predicted the soil’s CBR. Various other machine learning techniques have been applied in different areas of civil engineering to favorably optimize the behavior of materials and systems24–30, but none has combined five (5) machine learning techniques and an additional symbolic regression technique such as the response surface methodology (RSM) to predict the behavior of a granular soil CBR considering the gradation and compaction factors like it is reported in this present research paper.

An artificial neural network (ANN) is a computational model inspired by the biological neural networks of the human brain. It consists of interconnected nodes (neurons) organized in layers. Each neuron receives inputs, processes them through an activation function, and produces an output that can be passed to other neurons. ANN is a fundamental component of machine learning and has found widespread applications in various fields due to its ability to learn complex patterns and relationships from data.Neurons are the basic units of an ANN. They receive inputs, apply a weighted sum operation, and then pass the result through an activation function to produce an output.

ANNs are typically organized into layers: the Input Layer that receives the initial data or features. The intermediate layers (hidden layer) between the input and output layers where most of the computation occurs. Each layer consists of multiple neurons. The Output Layer produces the final output or prediction based on the input and the computations performed in the hidden layers. Each connection between neurons has an associated weight that scales the input value. Additionally, each neuron typically has a bias term that allows it to adjust its output independently of the inputs. The activation function determines the output of a neuron given its weighted inputs. Common activation functions include sigmoid, tanh, ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit), and softmax (for multi-class classification).ANNs learn by adjusting the weights and biases based on the error between predicted outputs and actual targets during training. The process of adjusting these parameters is often achieved through techniques like backpropagation and optimization algorithms such as gradient descent.

In summary, artificial neural networks are powerful computational models that have revolutionized many fields by enabling machines to learn from data and perform tasks that were previously thought to be too complex for traditional algorithms. Their flexibility and ability to capture intricate patterns make them a cornerstone of modern machine learning and artificial intelligence research.

Genetic programming (GP) is a technique within the field of evolutionary computation that uses principles inspired by biological evolution to automatically solve complex problems. It belongs to the broader category of evolutionary algorithms, which includes genetic algorithms, evolutionary strategies, and evolutionary programming.In genetic programming, solutions to a problem are represented as computer programs or mathematical expressions. This representation is typically in the form of tree structures, known as expression trees or syntax trees. The GP employs an evolutionary process similar to natural selection to iteratively improve solutions over generations; Initialization: Generate an initial population of random programs.Evaluation: Evaluate each program’s fitness based on how well it solves the problem. Selection: Select programs from the current population based on their fitness to serve as parents for reproduction. It works with the Genetic Operators such as Crossover: Exchange parts of two parent programs to create offspring with combined characteristics.Introduce random changes in the genetic material (e.g., altering parts of a program) to explore new solutions. Create a new population by combining offspring generated through crossover and mutation with the existing population.Stop the process when a termination condition is met (e.g., a sufficiently fit program is found, or a maximum number of generations is reached). A fitness function quantifies how well a program solves the problem at hand. It evaluates the program’s output or behavior against a desired outcome or objective.Through generations of evolution, genetic programming adapts and learns to produce better solutions by favoring programs with higher fitness scores.Genetic programming is applied to a wide range of problem domains, including: Finding mathematical expressions that best fit given data.Control systems design: Optimizing parameters for controlling complex systems.Data mining and pattern recognition: Extracting useful patterns and knowledge from data.Game playing: Creating strategies for games based on evolutionary principles. GP can discover novel and unexpected solutions that may not be evident through traditional programming or heuristic methods.Versatility: It can handle complex, non-linear problems where relationships are not well-defined or understood.Adaptability: Genetic programming can adapt to changing environments and requirements by continuously evolving solutions. Some challenges are associated with the Genetic Programming such as Computational Cost: Evolutionary algorithms, including GP, can be computationally expensive, especially for large-scale problems.Difficulty in Representation: Designing a suitable representation and fitness function for a specific problem domain can be challenging. Premature Convergence: The search process may converge prematurely to suboptimal solutions if the parameters are not properly tuned or if the diversity of the population is not maintained. In summary, genetic programming is a powerful technique for solving complex optimization and search problems by simulating the process of natural evolution. Its ability to explore large solution spaces and generate diverse and potentially innovative solutions makes it a valuable tool in various fields of research and application.

Here, β0​,β1​,…,βp​are coefficients of the polynomial terms, and ϵ represents the error term.EPR uses principles inspired by evolutionary algorithms to optimize the selection and combination of polynomial terms. Conclusively, EPR combines the flexibility of polynomial models with the adaptive nature of evolutionary algorithms to efficiently and effectively model complex relationships in data. It is particularly useful in scenarios where traditional regression methods may struggle to capture the underlying patterns or where a high degree of flexibility in model specification is required.

Response Surface Methodology (RSM) is a collection of mathematical and statistical techniques used to model and analyze the relationship between one or more independent variables (factors) and a dependent variable (response) in order to optimize the response. It is widely used in experimental design and optimization processes, particularly in fields such as engineering, chemistry, and manufacturing. RSM typically involves designing a series of experiments where different combinations of factor levels are tested. The goal is to explore the response surface, which represents how the response variable changes with variations in the factors.RSM uses mathematical models, often quadratic or higher-order polynomial models, to approximate the relationship between the factors and the response. The general form of a quadratic response surface model for a single response variable Y and two factors X1 and X2 is

10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y=\beta_0+{\beta_1}{X_1}+{\beta_2}{X_2}+{\beta_{11}}{X_{12}}+{\beta_{22}}{X_{22}}+{\beta_{12}}{X_1}{X_2}+\epsilon$$\end{document}

Here, β0​,β1​,β2​,β11​,β22​,β12​ are coefficientsrepresenting the intercept, linear effects, quadratic effects, and interaction effects, respectively, andϵis the error term.

Once the response surface model is constructed, optimization techniques are used to find the optimal settings of the factors that maximize or minimize the response variable. Optimization can be done using numerical methods or by solving the equations derived from the response surface model. RSM employs various statistical tools such as analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess the significance of the factors and their interactions, as well as diagnostics to check the adequacy of the model.In conclusion, Response Surface Methodology is a powerful approach for systematically optimizing processes and products by exploring the relationships between factors and responses through experimental design, modeling, and optimization techniques. Its application spans various industries where improving efficiency, quality, and performance are paramount.

Accordingly, the (SI) values are; 0.03, 0.58, 0.86, 0.86, 0.46 and 0.84 for; D10, 30, 50, 60, MDD and OMC, respectively. A sensitivity index of 1.0 indicates complete sensitivity, a sensitivity index less than 0.01 indicates that the model is insensitive to changes in the parameter.

6. Limitations and recommendation

The study investigates how the gradation (particle size distribution) and compaction characteristics of granular materials influence their California Bearing Ratio (CBR). The CBR is a key parameter used to evaluate the strength of subbase materials in road construction and the stability of landfill liners. The study focused on specific gradation values of granular materials, which could limit the generalizability of the findings to other materials commonly used in subbase or landfill liner construction. The models used to represent gradation and compaction characteristics have been simplified, potentially overlooking complex interactions between particle shapes and compaction techniques. The CBR tests are typically conducted in a controlled laboratory environment, which may not fully replicate field conditions where factors like moisture content, temperature, and load variability could significantly impact the material performance. The study may not fully account for the impact of varying moisture content on CBR values. Since moisture content significantly affects the compaction and strength of granular materials, its role should be thoroughly examined. The scale of the experiments or simulations might be limited, meaning the results might not represent large-scale construction projects. Small-scale tests might not capture all the complexities of real-world scenarios. If the study data did not explore a wide range of compaction methods, the results might not be applicable to all construction practices. Different compaction techniques can have varying effects on CBR.

However, future studies should include a broader range of granular materials, including those with different mineralogical compositions, to enhance the generalizability of the findings. Integrating field data into the modeling process could improve the relevance of the results. Conducting CBR tests under actual field conditions or using field-calibrated models could offer more practical insights. Employing additional sophisticated, nonlinear modeling approaches could help capture the complex interactions between gradation, compaction, and CBR. This should involve using metaheuristic machine learning techniques. Studies should explore a wider range of field compaction techniques and their impact on CBR. This could include different types of rollers, compaction energies, and moisture conditioning practices. Assessing the long-term performance of granular materials, particularly in varying environmental conditions, could provide valuable information on how gradation and compaction characteristics affect durability and service life. Models developed in the study should be validated and calibrated using additional datasets from different regions or project types. This could enhance the accuracy and applicability of the models. Future research could also consider the environmental and economic aspects of using different granular materials and compaction methods. This could lead to more sustainable construction practices. These recommendations could help address the limitations of the study and provide more comprehensive insights into the impact of gradation and compaction characteristics on the CBR of granular materials in subbase and landfill liner construction.
