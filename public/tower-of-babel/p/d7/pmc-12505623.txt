Treatment-focused clinical reasoning in medical students: relationship with academic success and professional commitment
Authors: Ender Tekes, Murat Tekin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical reasoning is a fundamental skill for medical students, enabling them to integrate knowledge and make informed treatment decisions. While previous studies have primarily focused on diagnostic reasoning, this study examines the relationship between treatment-focused clinical reasoning, academic performance, and professional commitment.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with final-year medical students (n = 53). Clinical reasoning skills were assessed using a structured case-based evaluation involving diabetes mellitus and hypertension management scenarios. Professional commitment was measured using the commitment to the profession of medicine scale, and academic performance was determined by cumulative grade point average. Correlation and multiple regression analyses were conducted to examine relationships among these variables.ResultsThe mean clinical reasoning score was 13.06 out of 54, indicating substantial challenges in treatment-based reasoning. Students performed better in the hypertension case (7.15/28) compared to the diabetes mellitus case (5.91/26). The most frequent difficulties included identifying hypoglycaemia-inducing drugs, recognizing contraindicated medications in renal impairment and heart failure, and selecting appropriate individualized therapies. While academic performance did not show a significant correlation with clinical reasoning scores (p > .05), a strong positive relationship was observed between professional commitment and clinical reasoning ability (r = .722, p < .05). Regression analysis indicated that both professional commitment and academic achievement significantly predicted clinical reasoning performance (R² = 0.560, p < .05), collectively explaining 56% of the variance.ConclusionThese findings highlight the need for enhancing treatment-based reasoning education in medical curricula, as students exhibited difficulties in pharmacotherapy decision-making, especially in integrating contraindications and individualized treatment considerations. Professional commitment appears to be a stronger predictor of clinical reasoning than academic performance alone. Medical education strategies that reinforce patient-centred reasoning and professional engagement may improve preparedness for real-world therapeutic decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07989-9.Keywords: Clinical reasoning, Medical education, Academic success, Professional commitment, Pharmacotherapy decision-making

Introduction

Clinical reasoning is a fundamental component of medical education and essential for effective patient care [1, 2]. This complex cognitive process enables healthcare professionals to gather and interpret patient data, formulate diagnoses, and, crucially, devise and implement appropriate treatment plans [1, 3]. The ability to accurately diagnose, make informed treatment decisions, and anticipate the trajectory of a clinical scenario are all essential for high-quality, patient-centred care [4]. While developing robust clinical reasoning abilities is paramount, research suggests a need for more effective educational and assessment strategies in this critical area [5, 6]. This study addresses this need by investigating the relationship between medical student performance on case-based clinical reasoning assessments focused on treatment, their academic performance, and their commitment to the medical profession.

Much existing research emphasizes diagnostic reasoning—the identification of diseases based on clinical findings—while treatment-focused reasoning receives comparatively less attention [7]. This oversight is significant, as effective treatment management is paramount for optimizing patient outcomes. Treatment-focused reasoning encompasses therapeutic decision-making, follow-up planning, further testing, and resource allocation [7]. Inadequate treatment-focused reasoning can lead to suboptimal treatment choices, adverse drug events, increased healthcare costs, and diminished patient satisfaction. Despite the clear importance of clinical reasoning in medical practice, existing literature has yet to thoroughly examine the association between students’ clinical reasoning skills, academic performance outcomes, and their broader professional commitment [8].

In contrast to diagnostic reasoning, often described as a “fast” cognitive process relying on pattern recognition and hypothesis generation, treatment-focused reasoning necessitates a more deliberative, “slow” approach [7]. It involves carefully weighing patient-specific variables, conducting thorough risk-benefit analyses of various treatment options, anticipating potential complications, and incorporating patient preferences and values into the decision-making process [9]. Moreover, treatment-focused reasoning requires clinicians to navigate uncertainty, adapt their strategies as new information emerges, and acknowledge the limitations of medical knowledge [10].

The relationship between clinical reasoning and academic performance (as measured by Grade Point Average) remains a complex area of inquiry. While Grade Point Average (GPA) reflects knowledge acquisition, it may not fully capture the nuanced application of that knowledge in clinical contexts, as noted by Gold et al. (2022) [8]. Indeed, Tekin et al. (2022) found no significant correlation between GPA and clinical reasoning skills among medical students [11]. This underscores the importance of experiential learning, active application of knowledge, and other factors in developing clinical reasoning expertise. Angus et al. (2018), in their qualitative study of experienced clinical teachers, highlights the critical role of active learning and authentic clinical experiences over traditional didactic methods in fostering clinical reasoning [12]. Furthermore, the learning environment, feedback quality, and individual student characteristics all contribute to the development of these skills, as highlighted by Fonseca et al. (2024) [13]. Finally, as Wingelaar et al. (2012) point out, some assessment methods for clinical reasoning, such as pattern recognition, may be more suitable for advanced learners, further complicating the relationship between GPA and reasoning skill development [14].

The Commitment to the Profession of Medicine Scale (Aytuğ-Kosan & Toraman, 2020) used in this study reflects attitudinal elements such as emotional commitment to the profession, a sense of responsibility, and adherence to ethical values. In treatment-focused clinical reasoning, these characteristics may foster a more comprehensive assessment of patient-specific factors, stronger adherence to safe prescribing principles, and closer alignment with patient-centred care principles. Given the limited research on this topic, our study aims to contribute to this area. Specifically, we examine how professional commitment may be related to students’ ability to make well-founded treatment decisions [15].

This study examines the potential relationship between commitment to the medical profession and treatment-focused clinical reasoning. The multidimensional nature of academic performance may influence the extent of this relationship. Academic success encompasses not only knowledge accumulation but also learning approaches and examination skills [16]. The study evaluated the extent to which commitment to the profession predicts clinical reasoning skills and the nature of the relationship between academic performance and treatment-focused reasoning. The aim was to gain a clearer understanding of the factors that contribute to the development of strong clinical reasoning skills in medical students. The findings of this study may contribute to curricular design, particularly in light of ongoing discussions on competency-based medical education [17]. Emphasizing the integration of pharmacological knowledge into treatment decisions could help better prepare students for the complex situations they will face in real-world clinical practice [18].

Methods

This research is a correlational study examining the relationships between clinical reasoning levels, academic achievement and commitment to the profession of medicine among sixth-year medical students.

Participants

The participants of this study were sixth-year students of Canakkale Onsekiz Mart University Faculty of Medicine. 53 students who gave their consent and volunteered to participate in the study were included.

Data collection tools

Data was collected from three sources: the Clinical Reasoning Form, the Commitment to the Profession of Medicine Scale (CPMS), and Academic Achievement Scores.

Clinical reasoning form

A structured Clinical Reasoning Form (Appendix 1) was developed by the researchers to assess the clinical reasoning skills of sixth-year medical students. The form included two clinical cases, each presented in a three-stage structured format, requiring students to interpret given information, findings, and symptoms to reach a final decision. The first case focused on the treatment management of a diabetic patient, while the second case addressed the treatment management of a hypertensive patient. These conditions were selected based on their high prevalence and the likelihood of general practitioners encountering them frequently upon graduation. The cases were presented as stepwise scenarios, requiring students to identify appropriate and inappropriate treatment approaches based on patient comorbidities, contraindications, and adverse effects. A total score of 54 points could be obtained from both cases. When structuring the content of the clinical reasoning forms, the forms were created taking into account the National Core Curriculum for Undergraduate Medical Education in Turkey (UÇEP 2020) and the learning objectives that students are expected to transform into knowledge and skills at the end of the courses. These processes were performed to ensure content validity.

The clinical cases were developed by researchers and reviewed by a panel of five experts from the relevant medical fields. During the content validity process, opinions obtained from experts were evaluated in terms of both the suitability of the case scenarios to professional reality and their capacity to demonstrate students’ treatment-focused reasoning. To assess inter-rater reliability, Krippendorff’s alpha coefficient was calculated, yielding a value of 0.861 [19]. Based on expert feedback, necessary revisions were made, and the final versions of the cases were established. With this process, even though it is a basic level of validity proof, basic content validity evidence based on expert judgement has been provided.

To ensure validity of the response process, students were asked to answer all questions individually under conditions similar to an exam environment. At each stage, they were asked to write not only the decision but also the rationale for that decision. This method allowed for the assessment of not only the students’ knowledge level but also their cognitive efforts in the clinical decision-making process.

The scoring system used in this study was developed specifically for the Clinical Reasoning Form and includes point deductions for clinically unsafe treatment choices. For example, prescribing a thiazide diuretic to a hypertensive patient with a history of gout or a thiazolidinedione to a diabetic patient with heart failure resulted in a deduction of one point in both cases. This approach aimed to reflect patient safety considerations in the assessment process rather than replicating an existing assessment format.

Two independent raters scored each student’s form separately during the evaluation process. Because the scoring was conducted using the same instructions, no additional inter-rater calculations were made at this stage; inter-rater reliability was tested using Krippendorff’s alpha (0.861), calculated from the content validity assessments of five experts.

Commitment to profession of medicine scale (CPMS)

The Commitment to the Profession of Medicine Scale (CPMS) (Appendix 2), developed and validated by Aytuğ-Koşan and Toraman (2020), was used to measure students’ professional commitment [15]. The scale consists of nine items under a single-factor structure. Responses are rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree). The total possible score ranges from 9 to 45, with higher scores indicating greater commitment to the profession of medicine. The scale’s validity and reliability were previously confirmed, with an internal consistency coefficient (Cronbach’s alpha) of 0.876 [20].

Academic achievement

To examine the relationship between students’ clinical reasoning levels and their overall academic success, their cumulative Grade Point Average (GPA) as of July 2024 was obtained from the Faculty of Medicine’s student affairs office.

Data analysis

Given the study’s objectives, the relationships between clinical reasoning, professional commitment, and academic achievement were analyzed. Additionally, the effects of professional commitment and academic achievement on clinical reasoning were modeled using multiple regression analysis. Before selecting parametric or non-parametric statistical methods, normality assumptions were tested, confirming that the data followed a normal distribution. Based on this, Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine relationships between variables. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to determine the predictive effects of professional commitment and academic achievement on clinical reasoning performance. Since multiple regression analysis falls under parametric methods, the following assumptions were tested: (a) The dependent variable (clinical reasoning score) followed a normal distribution. (b) There was no multicollinearity among the independent variables (commitment to the profession of medicine and GPA), assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) [20, 21].

The VIF values were close to 1, indicating no multicollinearity between predictors. Therefore, the model was deemed appropriate for interpretation [21, 22].

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Canakkale Onsekiz Mart University Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2023/14-67, Date: 23.11.2023). All participants were asked to fill out the informed consent form before the study. All participants were included in the study after obtaining the informed consent form.

Results

Analysis of student performance in treatment-focused clinical reasoning

The analysis of student performance in treatment-focused clinical reasoning revealed significant challenges, particularly in managing diabetes mellitus cases. The mean total score across both cases was 13.06 out of a maximum of 54 points.

Diabetes mellitus case (Mean score: 5.91/26)

Step 1: identifying hypoglycaemia-inducing drugs

While most students (81%) correctly identified insulin as a potential cause of hypoglycaemia and 70% provided correct justification, identification and justification rates were considerably lower for other agents. Only 36% correctly identified sulfonylureas (21% with correct justification), 8% recognized meglitinides (6% with correct justification), and 68% identified metformin (42% with correct justification). This suggests a potential knowledge gap regarding the mechanisms of action of various hypoglycaemic agents.

Step 2: discontinuing medications with comorbidities

A significant proportion of students struggled to justify their choices for discontinuing medications in the context of renal dysfunction and heart failure. Although 79% correctly discontinued sulfonylureas, only 17% linked this decision to severe hypoglycaemic episodes. Similarly, while 23% and 58% correctly stopped metformin and thiazolidinediones, respectively, only 11% and 30% provided appropriate justifications based on renal impairment and heart failure. These results indicate that while students recognize some contraindicated medications, many struggle to articulate the pathophysiological basis for these decisions.

Step 3: selecting appropriate antidiabetic therapy

Student selection of appropriate antidiabetic therapy revealed a preference for SGLT-2 inhibitors (47% correct identification with 43% correct justification) and insulin (34% correct in both identification and justification). However, there was a limited understanding of GLP-1 receptor agonists (11% and 9% correct identification and justification, respectively) and DPP-4 inhibitors (4% with correct justification). Notably, no student selected alpha-glucosidase inhibitors. This suggests a potential area for improvement in understanding the clinical application of newer antidiabetic agents, particularly their benefits in cardiovascular and renal protection.

Hypertension case (Mean score: 7.15/28)

Step 1: initiating treatment for newly diagnosed hypertension

While students generally recognized first-line antihypertensive agents, their justifications were often lacking. Selection rates for first-line antihypertensive medications were generally high, ranging between 62% and 77%. However, correct justification rates for these choices were significantly lower, ranging from 4% for calcium channel blockers to 23% for ACE inhibitors. This discrepancy highlights a need for greater emphasis on understanding the specific indications for each drug class [23].

Step 2: adjusting therapy based on comorbidities

Students demonstrated better performance in recognizing contraindications related to comorbidities. 70% correctly identified and 66% justified the contraindication of ACE inhibitors in pregnancy. Similarly, 58% correctly identified and 51% justified avoiding ARBs in pregnancy. However, while 64% recognized the contraindication of thiazide diuretics in patients with gout, only 28% provided the correct rationale. Furthermore, although 57% recognized the appropriateness of beta-blockers for stable angina, only 25% could justify this choice.

Step 3: identifying and managing adverse effects

In the final step, focusing on adverse effects and medication adjustments, only 30% of students correctly identified calcium channel blockers as the likely cause of peripheral oedema, with 25% providing a correct explanation. Furthermore, while 32% selected beta-blockers as a suitable alternative, only 17% provided adequate justification fort his choice. These findings highlight challenges in recognizing drug-related adverse effects and selecting appropriate alternative medications.

Overall performance and implications

Across both the diabetes mellitus and hypertension cases, the results indicate that while students demonstrate a basic understanding of treatment-focused clinical reasoning, they often struggle with integrating pathophysiology, contraindications, and patient-specific factors into their decision-making process. Enhancing education on individualized pharmacotherapy and reinforcing clinical application through case-based learning may improve their ability to apply clinical reasoning effectively.

Discussion

Our study investigated the role of treatment-focused clinical reasoning among medical students and its relationship with academic performance and professional commitment. Unlike most existing research [7, 9], which predominantly emphasizes diagnostic reasoning, this study uniquely focuses on therapeutic decision-making. Tekin et al. (2022) define the components of clinical reasoning as information gathering, hypothesis generation, problem representation, differential diagnosis, selecting a leading or working diagnosis, providing diagnostic rationale, and developing a management or treatment plan [11]. This shift in focus is crucial because effective treatment decisions are essential for positive patient outcome [7]. This distinction is critical because appropriate treatment selection, modification based on individual patient responses, and careful consideration of risks and benefits are all vital for delivering high-quality, patient-centred care [7]. Our findings reinforce this perspective by demonstrating that students encounter significant difficulties in treatment-focused reasoning, particularly in cases requiring individualized medication adjustments and recognition of contraindications.

One of the key findings is that students demonstrated substantial challenges in treatment-based reasoning, as reflected in their low mean clinical reasoning score (13.06/54). Students performed better in the hypertension case (7.15/28) than in the diabetes mellitus case (5.91/26), suggesting that certain treatment scenarios were more challenging than others. Students struggled particularly with recognizing contraindications, identifying drug-related adverse effects, and making individualized treatment choices. To address these difficulties, integrating clinical reasoning skills into medical education requires early and structured exposure, as students benefit from iterative learning experiences that reinforce decision-making strategies in diverse clinical contexts [2]. Case-based reasoning education could potentially bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and clinical application, providing students with opportunities to practice and refine their skills in a simulated environment [24].

Strengthening both clinical reasoning and professional commitment is most effective when introduced early in medical training. In the first and second years, small-group sessions using patient-centred scenarios to design individualized treatment plans can help students connect pharmacological knowledge with professional values, ethical questions and patient safety (Cruess et al., 2019) [25]. During the third and fourth years, supervised case discussions can prompt students to justify their treatment choices and engage directly with professional role models (Dornan et al., 2019) [26]. Activities such as reflective writing or group discussions at the end of each rotation can further reinforce learning and support the growth of professional identity.

At the same time, fostering professional commitment requires more than individual awareness; it depends on structured opportunities that connect clinical decisions with ethical responsibility and patient-centred care. In this way, the strategies described above not only strengthen reasoning skills but also nurture values of accountability and dedication to the profession. To make this integration meaningful, schools could also include simple ways of evaluating its impact, such as pre- and post-reflective exercises, feedback from peers and faculty on professionalism, or tracking how students justify their treatment choices in ethical terms. These steps would ensure that the link between reasoning and commitment is not only discussed in theory but also visible in practice.

Another significant finding is the lack of a strong correlation between academic performance and clinical reasoning ability. While it might be intuitively assumed that high academic achievement translates to strong clinical reasoning, this was not the case in our study. In fact, Tekin et al. (2022) found that the final grades of internship and general success grades did not show a correlation with clinical case assessments [11]. This aligns with other research that reports inconsistent relationships between traditional academic metrics and clinical decision-making [27, 28]. Students with higher GPA scores did not necessarily demonstrate better reasoning performance, suggesting that knowledge-based assessments alone do not capture the ability to apply treatment-focused reasoning in real-world patient care. These findings raise important questions about the argument that high academic achievement does not equate to proficiency in therapeutic reasoning, reinforcing the need for direct instructional approaches rather than reliance on implicit learning through conventional assessments. Although GPA’s zero-order correlation with clinical reasoning was not significant, it emerged as a significant predictor in the multivariate regression analysis. This pattern suggests a suppression effect, meaning that GPA’s predictive role became evident only after controlling for the shared variance with professional commitment. This difference suggests that, despite the limited explanatory power of GPA alone, its contribution becomes more pronounced when considered in conjunction with the CPMS, and that clinical reasoning is shaped by a multivariate structure. As Connor et al. (2020) note, despite calls for improved teaching and assessment of clinical reasoning, the creation of explicit, theory-informed clinical reasoning curricula has proceeded slowly [27]. Shea and Chan (2023) highlight that medical schools often place insufficient emphasis on teaching students how to think about clinical problems, with the accumulation of medical knowledge overriding the development of clinical reasoning skills [29].

Although this study addresses the relationship between professional commitment and clinical reasoning for the first time, it offers a preliminary observation that these two concepts may develop together, rather than being causal. The scale used has a multidimensional structure encompassing emotional dedication to the profession, a sense of responsibility, and adherence to ethical values (Aytuğ-Kosan & Toraman, 2020) [15]. Our justification for including professional commitment as a variable is that these attitudinal dimensions could plausibly influence how carefully and thoroughly students approach clinical decision-making. This may be particularly relevant in patient-centred, treatment-focused scenarios. However, whether this relationship is mediated by academic achievement or occurs independently was not tested in this study. Therefore, our findings are hypothesis-generating and require further analysis.

However, academic abilities are not the only factor influencing clinical reasoning; our results indicate that professional commitment plays a crucial role in clinical reasoning development. Students who reported higher commitment to the medical profession demonstrated significantly better clinical reasoning performance (r =.722, p <.05). This aligns with prior research indicating that motivation and professional identity influence clinical decision-making [2]. This is further supported by the assertion that development of students’ thinking and learning skills that contribute to capability in clinical reasoning should be a priority, not just for academic and clinical educators but for all practitioners [2]. Regression analysis further illuminated these relationships. Both GPA and professional commitment emerged as significant positive predictors of clinical reasoning performance, collectively explaining 56% of the variance in clinical reasoning scores. These results highlight that while GPA may not be directly correlated with clinical reasoning, it does contribute, along with professional commitment, to predicting clinical reasoning success. This finding resonates with research emphasizing the multifaceted nature of clinical reasoning and the influence of factors beyond pure academic knowledge, such as personal values, motivation, and professional identity [24]. The significant contribution of professional commitment in our model underscores the importance of fostering a strong professional identity in medical education [30]. Students who are more deeply committed to the medical profession may be more invested in developing their clinical reasoning abilities, as they recognize the critical role it plays in providing high-quality patient care. This heightened investment might explain why students were motivated to participate in our study as an extracurricular activity and dedicate time to improve their clinical reasoning skills. This is consistent with findings from other studies, which suggest that factors beyond academic metrics, such as interview scores and the stage of progression through a program, can be predictive of clinical reasoning skill [18, 31].

Consequently, the lack of a significant correlation between academic achievement and clinical reasoning skills suggests that traditional measures of academic performance, such as GPA, may not fully capture the cognitive processes and clinical competencies required for effective treatment-focused clinical reasoning [31]. This aligns with the idea that clinical reasoning involves more than just recalling facts; it requires the application of knowledge to specific patient scenarios. Clinical reasoning performance is highly context-specific, implying that general academic abilities may not directly translate to clinical problem-solving [2]. Tekin et al. (2022) also found that clinical case evaluations and problem-solving perception were not correlated with GPA, reinforcing the idea that academic success doesn’t always equate to strong clinical reasoning [11].

While academic achievement is undoubtedly important, it does not necessarily translate directly into the ability to integrate complex clinical information, consider patient-specific factors, and make appropriate treatment decisions. Effective clinical reasoning involves the ability to synthesize data, communicate effectively, and reflect on experiences [12]. These skills may not be adequately assessed by traditional academic metrics. Furthermore, communication skills are necessary for building rapport with patients, conducting medical interviews, and discussing cases with supervisors [24]. Williams et al. (2011) suggest that medical schools with early, integrated clinical and basic science teaching might produce sustained superior gains in clinical reasoning, although their study did not confirm this hypothesis across five different medical schools [32].

The strong positive correlation between clinical reasoning and commitment to the medical profession highlights the potential importance of professional identity formation and intrinsic motivation in the development of clinical reasoning skills. Students who feel a stronger commitment to the medical profession often show greater motivation to develop their clinical reasoning skills. This drive appears to come not only from the impact on patient outcomes but also from an internalized sense of responsibility and dedication to providing quality care. In our study, this was evident in their voluntary participation and active engagement with case-based activities.

The assessment tool was deliberately designed to mirror real-world clinical reasoning tasks. Using structured response formats aligned with expert opinion supported both content and response process validity. These steps were also guided by the UÇEP 2020 framework and relevant learning objectives, ensuring that the findings carry significance from an educational as well as a statistical perspective.

As Kirch et al. (2015) state, “ensuring competence in professionalism requires the concerted efforts of many” and is foundational to the practice of medicine [33]. Higgs et al. (2008) support this, arguing that the relationship between patient and practitioner is critical for ethical conduct and is an essential foundation for novice development [9].

The regression analysis further supports this notion, demonstrating that both academic achievement and professional commitment are significant predictors of clinical reasoning performance, explaining a substantial portion of the variance in clinical reasoning scores. This reinforces the idea that a multifaceted approach to medical education, which fosters both academic excellence and a strong professional identity, may be key to enhancing students’ clinical reasoning abilities. Unfortunately, many medical students arrive on clerkships without understanding key clinical reasoning concepts [5, 27] Explicit, theory-informed clinical reasoning education and assessment are needed not only in graduate medical education but also across the spectrum of medical education, beginning in undergraduate medical education and persisting into physicians’ continuing professional development activities [27].

Ultimately, while this study provides valuable insights into the factors that influence medical students’ clinical reasoning skills, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted at a single institution, with a relatively small volunteer sample (n = 53), which may limit the generalizability of the findings [12]. Future research should aim to replicate the study across multiple medical schools to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between clinical reasoning, academic performance, and professional commitment. Additionally, the study relied on self-reported measures of professional commitment, which may be subject to social desirability bias. Future studies could incorporate more objective measures of professional identity and commitment, such as observations of clinical behaviors or peer/faculty assessments [4].

Although inter-rater reliability was calculated for the expert review process (Krippendorff’s alpha = 0.861), no additional statistical analysis was performed for the reliability of student response scoring, since both raters used the same scoring guideline. This remains a limitation, as further reliability evidence from the actual scoring process could have strengthened our findings.

It is important to recognize the outcomes of the clinical decisions practitioners make rest in their commitment to quality, relevant and accountable decision making [9]. The evaluation form demonstrated content validity, response process validity and basic validity evidence based on expert judgement; however, the absence of further construct validity testing and additional reliability analysis represents a limitation of this research.

In addition, the study was conducted at a single medical school with a relatively small sample (n = 53), which constrains the generalizability of the findings. Institutional characteristics such as curriculum design, the timing of the transition to clinical training, and faculty involvement in clinical decision-making are likely to influence the development of students’ clinical reasoning. For example, in settings where clinical exposure is introduced earlier, or where faculty mentoring is more intensive, students might perform differently in similar assessments. Cultural factors, such as hierarchical structures or varying expectations for student participation, may also shape decision-making and perceptions of professional commitment. Replicating the study across different institutions and cultural contexts will therefore be important to test the robustness of these findings.

Conclusions

In summary, this study found that while academic achievement, as measured by GPA, was not significantly correlated with medical students’ treatment-focused clinical reasoning skills, a strong positive relationship was observed between clinical reasoning and commitment to the medical profession. The CPMS score, used in the study to measure professional commitment, explained a significant portion of the variance in clinical reasoning scores. These findings align with the broader understanding that clinical reasoning extends beyond the acquisition of knowledge and involves the application of skills, integration of information, and consideration of patient-specific factors [34]. Notably, our study specifically focused on treatment-centred clinical reasoning, an area often less explored than diagnostic reasoning, as highlighted by Cook et al. (2018) [9]. This emphasis allowed us to identify the distinct role of professional commitment in shaping students’ abilities to make effective treatment decisions. Therefore, a multifaceted approach to medical education, which fosters both academic excellence and professional identity formation, may be essential for developing robust clinical reasoning abilities in future physicians. The evaluation form used in our study was structured with expert opinions and the scenarios were prepared in accordance with the UÇEP 2020 and learning objectives, strengthening its content validity. Furthermore, allowing for detailed analysis of students’ response processes increases the educational validity of the results. Such an approach should emphasize not only knowledge acquisition, but also communication skills and professional socialization (Higgs et al. 2008), to prepare future physicians for the complexities of treatment-focused decision-making [9]. Given the predominantly theoretical instruction of pharmacology in early years, bridging the gap between classroom learning and clinical application is paramount.
