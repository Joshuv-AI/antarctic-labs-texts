Rationally and in silico guided APOBEC3F-directed CBE for enhanced PDAC genetic therapy
Authors: Qingxiao Fang, Jin Zhang, Keshan Wang, Zhe Wang, Zongjing Lv, Xiaoping Zhang, Changhao Bi, Xueli Zhang, Jun Yu, Yukuan Feng, Tianxing Zhou, Jihui Hao, Chao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCytosine base editors (CBEs) mediate precise C-to-T conversion and hold considerable therapeutic promise, yet their editing function and utility in oncology remain underexplored. Here, we integrate evolutionary scale modeling (ESM) with structure-guided mutagenesis to remodel human APOBEC3F (A3F), yielding a panel of high-performance CBEs. Our high-efficiency A3F-CBEs achieve up to 1.9- and 3.3-fold higher on-target editing within the canonical editing window than A3A- and Anc689-BE4max, respectively. The high-accuracy A3F-CBEs deliver up to 3.0-fold improvement over haA3A-G at the majority of surveyed loci without compromising specificity. To demonstrate therapeutic potential, we deploy a dual-AAV platform packaging A3F-BE4max and dual gRNAs co-targeting KRAS and MYC in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) models. It elicits robust oncogene silencing and inhibited PDAC cell proliferation both in vitro and patient-derived organoids (PDOs). In a PDAC mouse model, it markedly suppresses tumor burden and extends survival. Our work establishes ESM-guided A3F-based CBEs as a versatile, precise platform for cancer genetic therapy.Subject terms: Biological techniques, Cancer therapy

Introduction

The advent of base editors (BEs) has revolutionized genome editing by enabling precise single-nucleotide modifications without the need for double-strand breaks1–3. Among these, adenine base editors (ABEs)1 and cytosine base editors (CBEs)2, as well as emerging variants like CGBEs4–7 and AYBEs8,9, have been widely adopted across biomedicine and agriculture for their high precision and efficiency. Despite this, CBEs continue to have space to improve: their deaminase domains can catalyze Cas9-independent off-target deamination of both DNA and RNA10–12; bystander conversions within the activity window undermine target fidelity13,14; and sequence-context dependencies15,16. For example, the poor performance of rAPOBEC1 in GC-rich regions limits their generalizability17. Although A3A-derived CBEs exhibit robust activity in GC-rich contexts and have been finely tuned for both precision and efficiency14,17, optimizing CBE platforms to function reliably across diverse genomic loci and in clinical settings remains an important area for further exploration.

Protein engineering has long addressed such limitations through rational design, structure-guided mutagenesis and directed evolution, yielding editors with enhanced activity and reduced off-target profiles. More recently, advances in computational protein design, powered by large-scale language models and artificial intelligence, have accelerated these efforts. Although deep-learning-based de novo Cas9 redesign illustrates AI’s transformative potential, it remains constrained by high computational cost and algorithmic complexity18. In contrast, protein language models like ESM offer a computationally efficient, data-driven alternative for optimizing core components of gene editing tools19–21. For instance, ESM2-guided refinement of uracil-DNA glycosylase in TSBE systems has yielded marked gains in efficiency20, yet analogous approaches to deaminase domains in CBEs remain largely unexplored.

The therapeutic promise of base editing is underscored by successes such as CBE-mediated silencing of TRBC and CD52 for universal CAR-T cell engineering22 and ABE targeting of PCSK9 to lower cholesterol23. Preclinical studies further demonstrate efficacy in neuro-oncological, cervical and hepatocellular carcinoma models24–26. However, their application in PDAC remains largely unexplored, despite the disease’s dismal prognosis and the lack of effective targeted therapies27–29. The near-universal presence of KRASG12D mutations and frequent MYC overexpression underlie the aggressive nature of PDAC, yet both remain challenging to directly target30. Although small-molecule inhibitors such as MRTX1133 targeting KRASG12D have shown early preclinical promise28,31,32, no clinically approved therapies directly target these two oncogenic drivers. Moreover, pioneering studies have demonstrated the feasibility of using base editing and prime editing to correct KRAS mutations in PDAC models, underscoring the therapeutic potential of gene editing approaches33,34. Given the therapeutic potential of base editing and the challenges of drugging KRAS and MYC30, leveraging BE-based gene editing tools in PDAC might offer a novel intervention strategy.

In this study, we report the ESM-assisted and structure-guided engineering of human APOBEC3F to create novel CBEs with markedly improved efficiency, precision and reduced off-target activity. We further demonstrate the therapeutic potential of our optimized A3F-BE4max via a dual-AAV delivery system, achieving efficient co-targeting and silencing of KRAS and MYC in PDAC genetic therapy. Our work establishes a versatile framework for ESM-guided deaminase engineering to expand the utility of CBEs in cancer genetic therapy.

Results

Structural and rational engineering of APOBEC3F for enhanced cytosine base editing

Deaminases from the APOBEC3 family, including A3A, A3B, and A3G, have demonstrated broad utility in cytosine base editors (CBEs)17,35,36. Nonetheless, opportunities remain to further improve their editing efficiency and precision. In particular, the potential of APOBEC3F (A3F) in base editing has been underexplored, despite prior studies indicating that A3F possesses robust deaminase activity and specificity in vitro37. Notably, within the BE3 framework, A3F exhibits the third-highest editing efficiency after A3A and A3B and shows minimal preference between methylated and unmethylated GC sequences17, suggesting it may serve as a promising scaffold for engineering new CBEs. To investigate this, we constructed a panel of A3F-based editors using both full-length A3F (FL) and its C-terminal domain (CTD). Guided by structural data, rational design and previously reported beneficial mutations38–41, we generated 15 candidate A3F variants (Fig. 1a). These were individually co-transfected with gRNA expression vectors targeting three genomic loci in HEK293T cells. Editing efficiencies and indel frequencies were quantified using high-throughput sequencing and CRISPResso2 analysis42. Among the candidates, several variants, particularly those harboring W310 mutations, exhibited enhanced editing efficiency compared to wild-type A3F and its CTD (Fig. 1b-c). One variant, A3F-CTD_W310D-BE4max (hereafter referred to as A3F-W310D; BE4max is omitted in the following context), demonstrated editing efficiency on par with or exceeding that of BE4max and A3A-BE4max, with a slightly narrowed editing window (Fig. 1c). Importantly, indel rates remained comparable to BE4max (Fig. 1d).

Given the promising performance of the W310D mutation, we further screened a set of additional substitutions at this site. W310D consistently showed the highest editing activity across three loci (Supplementary Fig. 1a). To assess broader applicability, we benchmarked A3F-W310D at nine additional loci and found it outperformed both BE4max and A3F-CTD across all targets, without sequence-context preference (Fig. 1e). While A3F-W310D modestly expanded the editing window for higher editing at PAM-distal cytosines (Fig. 1f), editing specificity and indel frequencies showed no significant difference compared to BE4max (Fig. 1g–h). Together, these data establish A3F-W310D as a potent and broadly applicable CBE with high efficiency and support the utility of A3F as a potential candidate for further base editor development.

In silico-guided engineering of APOBEC3F enhances C-to-T editing

To further augment the editing performance of A3F-W310D, we leveraged in silico modeling to guide the functional optimization. Specifically, we applied Evolutionary Scale Modeling (ESM), a protein language model (PLM) trained on comprehensive sequence databases including UniProt and UniRef50, to identify beneficial mutations. Three independent models including esm-1v, esm-msa-1b, and esm-if143–45, were employed to generate residue-wise mutation scores across the A3F-CTD sequence (Fig. 2a). The top 10% of high-scoring substitutions was subjected to further prioritization using position-specific scoring matrix (PSSM) filtering, resulting in 10 candidate point mutations predicted to enhance function (Fig. 2a). These variants were introduced into the A3F-W310D backbone, and their editing activity was evaluated in HEK293T cells across two genomic loci. Among them, several mutations, particularly H198D, S216T and G285R, further increased C-to-T editing efficiency compared to the W310D variant alone (Fig. 2b). To assess potential additive effects, we generated combinatorial constructs, and identified the triple mutant W310D + S216T + H198D as the top-performing variant, hereafter termed heA3F (high-efficiency A3F) (Fig. 2c). We next benchmarked heA3F across multiple endogenous loci. Compared to A3F-W310D, heA3F consistently exhibited higher editing efficiencies with a modestly expanded editing window and no discernible sequence context bias (Fig. 2d, e). Editing specificity and indel frequencies remained comparable to the parental construct (Fig. 2f, g). To evaluate performance in an additional cell line, we also tested heA3F in HeLa cells at three genomic loci. Consistent with results in HEK293T cells, heA3F outperformed A3F-W310D in editing efficiency while maintaining comparable specificity and indel profiles (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). Together, these data establish that ESM-based engineering of APOBEC3F enables the development of a more efficient and broadly applicable cytosine base editor.

Rational design of A3F-directed CBEs with improved fidelity

Base editors with improved fidelity, characterized by a confined editing window and reduced off-target activity, are critical for safe and precise therapeutic genome editing. Previous studies have demonstrated that modifying the substrate-binding interface of cytidine deaminases can fine-tune their nucleotide interaction dynamics, thereby enhancing editing specificity. Building on this rationale, we sought to refine the heA3F system by engineering high-accuracy variants of APOBEC3F. Using reported structural data and homology-based alignments between APOBEC3F and other cytidine deaminases, particularly APOBEC3A, we identified key residues potentially involved in substrate coordination10,14,46,47. Based on these insights, we designed a panel of eight heA3F point mutants predicted to restrict the editing window or modulate DNA binding affinity (Fig. 3a). Among these, mutations such as N240G (corresponding to N57G in A3A) and Y307G (Y130G in A3A), derived through homology inference, led to a marked increase in editing precision (Fig. 3b). Interestingly, the Y359A predicted to destabilize nucleotide binding slightly enhanced editing activity at two tested loci (Fig. 3b). We next explored combinatorial strategies to consolidate fidelity-enhancing effects. Constructs such as heA3F-N240G, heA3F-N240G + Y359A and heA3F-Y307G emerged as lead variants with favorable editing profiles (Fig. 3c). Targeted sequencing across eight additional genomic loci revealed that these haA3F (high-accuracy A3F) variants retained robust C-to-T editing activity while sharply restricting the editing window to positions 5-6 (Fig. 3d, e), a feature desirable for therapeutic applications. In parallel, analysis of editing byproducts demonstrated that the heA3F-N240G (haA3F-G) and heA3F-N240G + Y359A (haA3F-GA) exhibited reduced C-to-A/G conversions and lower indel frequency relative to heA3F (Fig. 3f, g). Importantly, validation in HeLa cells confirmed that these trends in editing precision were maintained in cancer-relevant contexts (Supplementary Figs. 3a–c). Collectively, these findings highlight that structure-guided rational design and cross-deaminase homology alignment can be leveraged to engineer A3F-based base editors with superior fidelity.

Benchmarking A3F-directed base editors against state-of-the-art CBEs

To benchmark our engineered A3F base editors, HEK293T cells were transfected with indicated gRNAs and constructs encoding heA3F and heA3F-Y359A (hyA3F) alongside several widely used CBEs14,48–51. Within the canonical editing window (C4-C8), hyA3F variants achieved up to 1.9-fold higher C-to-T editing at TP53 (C4) compared with A3A, and 1.4-fold at EMX1 site2 (C6) versus Anc689 (Fig. 4a). At the HEK4 site, hyA3F and CBE6b exhibited distinct positional preferences, while CBE6b was more efficient at C5, hyA3F showed higher editing activity at C8 (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Outside the canonical window, for example hyA3F enhanced editing by up to 7.3-fold at ABL1 (C10) and PDCD1 site2 (C3). In contrast, haA3A-A displayed superior precision, albeit with reduced editing levels (Fig. 4a, b). We next assessed editing specificity and indel byproducts to evaluate editing fidelity. The overall profiles of C-to-A/G conversions and indel frequencies were comparable among these CBE variants (Fig. 4c, d).

To further interrogate editing specificity, we compared haA3F-G, haA3F-GA, haA3A-G, eA3A46 and sdd652 across multiple endogenous loci. Notably, haA3F variants achieved higher editing efficiencies than haA3A-G at the majority of tested loci, with up to a 3.0-fold increase observed at EMX1-2 (C6), while sdd6-derived CBE showed a lower editing efficiency (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 4b). At the TP53 site, haA3F displayed exceptional editing specificity at CC5, whereas haA3A-G and eA3A preferentially edited TC7 with robust activity (Fig. 4e). Byproduct profiling across these high-fidelity CBEs revealed no significant differences (Fig. 4f, g). Among them, haA3F-GA achieved the optimal balance between efficiency and specificity, underscoring the robustness of the engineered haA3F variant (Fig. 4h). Further, our engineered A3F variants were systematically characterized, revealing that high-efficiency A3Fs displayed a modest PAM-distal shift in their editing windows, whereas haA3F-GA consistently exhibited a narrowed window (C5-C6) across all tested CBEs (Supplementary Fig. 5a). We then assessed sequence-context preferences and observed no detectable bias toward 5′-NC motifs for A3Fs (Supplementary Fig. 5b). To evaluate performance at methylated genomic loci17, we examined sites with differing methylation levels and found that high-efficiency A3Fs generally surpassed A3A-derived BEs at most loci, achieving maximal efficiencies of 79.4% and 80.5% (Supplementary Fig. 6a). In addition, NG-Cas9 and SpRY-Cas9 versions paired with A3Fs retained robust activity, further expanding their targetable scope (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Finally, validation in HeLa cells confirmed that both heA3F and haA3F outperformed existing CBEs across diverse loci, highlighting their broad therapeutic potential (Supplementary Figs. 7a, b). These findings establish the A3F-based editing system as a highly versatile and powerful addition to the base editing toolbox for both research and potential clinical translation.

Off-target profiling of heA3F and haA3F in HEK293T cells

Given that CBEs can induce both gRNA-dependent and deaminase-dependent off-target effects, we comprehensively evaluated the off-target profiles of heA3F and haA3F variants in HEK293T cells. For gRNA-dependent DNA off-target analysis, we quantified cumulative C-to-T conversions at previously reported off-target loci corresponding to each guide RNA. HeA3F exhibited off-target editing levels comparable to those of Anc689 and A3A (Fig. 5a, b). Notably, heA3F variants demonstrated equal or improved specificity across these loci (Fig. 5b). At the potential off-target loci of HEK3 locus, both haA3F and haA3A-G variants induced background editing, with haA3Fs consistently showing enhanced specificity relative to haA3A-G at the majority of loci (Fig. 5c, d). To further assess deaminase-dependent off-target activity, we employed an orthogonal R-loop assay53 at two genomic loci for high-efficiency variants. HeA3F demonstrated high DNA off-target activity comparable to A3A, whereas haA3F-GA exhibited reduced off-target levels and showed a tendency toward higher editing at CG contexts at the tested R-loop sites (Fig. 5e, f and Supplementary Fig. 8a). To further examine potential RNA off-target effects, we performed transcriptome-wide RNA-seq in base editor-expressing cells. Quantification of unintended C-to-U conversions revealed that both A3F variants induced significantly fewer RNA off-target edits than A3A-BE4max (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 8b). Together, these results indicate that while heA3Fs display elevated off-target activity relative to haA3A-A, comparable to that of A3A, haA3Fs maintain high fidelity across both DNA and RNA substrates, supporting their potential as precise and safe genome engineering tools.

Identification of potential heA3F-gRNA combinations for efficient silencing of KRAS and MYC in PDAC cells

Despite advances in KRASG12D-targeted therapies in PDAC, resistance remains a significant challenge31,54. MYC, frequently co-activated with KRAS mutations, contributes to resistance by promoting tumor cell survival and genomic instability55. Targeting MYC has been shown to restore sensitivity to KRAS inhibitors through modulation of MAPK/ERK signaling or disruption of the PLK1/MYC axis56,57. Moreover, targeting either KRAS or MYC individually suppresses PDAC progression28,55, suggesting potential synergy in co-targeting these oncogenic drivers. However, no clinically approved therapies directly inhibit either MYC or KRASG12D, underscoring the need for innovative strategies. The functional dependence on these oncogenes was preliminarily confirmed by the marked growth inhibition observed upon their individual disruption, supporting their roles as key drivers in our PDAC models. Given the therapeutic potential of base editors in cancer therapy, we sought to simultaneously silence KRAS and MYC using A3F-mediated editing as a precise approach to suppress PDAC growth. To this end, we first screened a panel of candidate gRNAs targeting KRAS and MYC loci in HEK293T cells co-transfected with hyA3F-BE4max (Fig. 6a). Editing efficiency was assessed by high-throughput sequencing (HTS) across several candidate CBEs, identifying gRNA-3# and -5# for KRAS and gRNA-1# and -5# for MYC as the most effective with hyA3F (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 9a–c), both of which were further validated by western blot analysis. Ultimately, KRAS_gRNA-3 and MYC_gRNA-1 were selected for their proximity to the 5’ exons, which minimizes the likelihood of producing partially functional truncated proteins, and for their high silencing efficiency (Fig. 6c). Next, we evaluated the functional impact of these gRNAs in PDAC cell models. Lentiviral delivery of heA3F-BE4max and the selected gRNAs into KRASG12D-mutant PANC-1 and SW1990 cells resulted in robust editing and effective suppression of target gene expression, consistent with the HEK293T findings (Fig. 6d, e). Functional assays demonstrated that disruption of either KRAS or MYC significantly impaired cell proliferation, while dual targeting elicited a synergistic anti-proliferative effect (Fig. 6f, g). To evaluate gRNA specificity, we examined the top predicted off-target (OT) sites for KRAS_gRNA3 and MYC_gRNA1. While MYC_gRNA1 showed minimal off-target activity, KRAS_gRNA3 induced substantial off-target editing accompanied by notable indel formation at its OT1 site (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Functional assays showed that KRAS-edited clone reduced proliferation in PANC-1 and HEK293T cells, whereas the OT1-edited clone displayed no growth effects in either PANC-1 or HEK293T, indicating that this off-target site is not phenotypically relevant (Supplementary Fig. 10b, c). These results highlight the therapeutic potential of A3F-mediated base editing in concurrently silencing KRAS and MYC, leading to marked inhibition of PDAC cell growth in vitro.

AAV-mediated delivery of heA3F-BE4max and dual gRNAs targeting MYC and KRAS for enhanced PDAC genetic therapy

Encouraged by the in vitro findings, we next evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of hyA3F-mediated editing in more clinically relevant models. Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) harboring the KRASG12D mutation and showing marked MYC overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 11a–c) were transduced with lentiviral constructs encoding heA3F-BE4max along with individual gRNAs for five days (Fig. 7a). High-throughput sequencing confirmed robust editing at both KRAS and MYC loci, with editing efficiencies reaching up to 53% and 40%, respectively (Fig. 7b). Consistently, western blotting showed marked reductions in KRAS and MYC protein expression in edited PDOs compared to non-targeting controls (Fig. 7c). In addition, organoid growth was significantly impaired upon editing of either gene, with dual targeting leading to a further reduction in proliferation (Fig. 7d, e). To assess in vivo efficacy, we developed a dual-AAV system58 for systemic delivery of heA3F-BE4max and dual gRNAs targeting KRAS and MYC (Fig. 7f-g). Orthotopic xenografts were generated by implanting unedited PDOs into the pancreas of nude mice, followed by intraperitoneal AAV administration every three days for a total of three doses (Fig. 7h). Mice receiving AAVs targeting either KRAS or MYC exhibited marked tumor regression and prolonged survival compared to control groups (Fig. 7i–k). Notably, co-targeting both oncogenes yielded a synergistic anti-tumor effect, further enhancing survival benefit relative to single-gene targeting (Fig. 7i–k). And targeted sequencing of tumor tissues in combined group at day 38 revealed on-target editing efficiencies of 23% for KRAS and 17% for MYC, respectively (Fig. 7l). To investigate potential adaptive responses to genetic therapy, RNA-seq analysis of PDO-derived tumors with KRAS, MYC, or dual knockout revealed largely overlapping pathway changes, with most oncogenic pathways downregulated (Supplementary Fig. 12a–c). Wnt signaling was among the upregulated pathways, suggesting a potential compensatory mechanism supporting partial tumor survival (Supplementary Fig. 12a–c). Together, these findings establish that systemic delivery of heA3F-BE4max and selected gRNAs enables efficient editing of KRAS and MYC in patient-derived PDAC models, resulting in tumor suppression and survival benefit in vivo.

Discussion

The clinical landscape of gene editing therapy is rapidly evolving, driven by continuous advances in editing technologies and delivery systems. As the demand for highly efficient and precise editing tools intensifies, we report the development of a base editor derived from APOBEC3F (A3F) with enhanced activity and reduced off-target effects. Through rational design and PLM-guided optimization, we engineered heA3F and haA3F variants with improved deaminase function, offering new additions to the base editing toolbox.

Structural and literature-guided analyses identified mutation sites predicted to enhance A3F catalytic activity. Consistent with prior studies on APOBEC3B36, we found that the A3F-CTD is the primary determinant of deaminase function. Among several candidates, the W310 mutation significantly boosted editing efficiency, underscoring its value in editor optimization. While this site was not predicted by the protein language model, the model identified additional mutations, such as S196, that also enhanced editing activity. These findings highlight the complementary nature of structure-based design and AI-driven predictions in protein engineering.

To expand the versatility of A3F editors, we further developed haA3F variants with narrowed editing windows and reduced off-target effects. Compared to existing high-specificity CBEs, both heA3F and haA3F demonstrated superior on-target efficiency across diverse loci, with minimal sequence context bias. Interestingly, the hyA3F mutation enhanced editing at multiple targets but markedly reduced activity at PD-L1 loci, which are hypomethylated14,17. This suggests that Y359A may interfere with A3F-mediated deamination of nonmethylated cytosines. By contrast, the combined N240G/Y359A variant consistently exhibited enhanced editing efficiency, underscoring the complex, context-dependent interplay among A3F mutations. Notably, our high-performance A3F variants demonstrated reduced or comparable off-target effects on both DNA and RNA substrates. In orthogonal R-loop assays, haA3F-GA showed site-dependent sequence context effects in R-loop assays, which are discussed with appropriate caveats. This favorable specificity profile may be attributed to the intrinsic properties of A3F-CTD or the impact of specific mutations, such as Y359A43.

To explore therapeutic applicability, we developed a dual-AAV system delivering heA3F with gRNAs targeting KRAS and MYC, two major oncogenic drivers in PDAC. This strategy effectively suppressed both targets and impaired tumor growth in cell lines, patient-derived organoids, and orthotopic mouse models, establishing a proof-of-concept for precision genetic therapy in PDAC. Notably, co-targeting KRAS and MYC produced a synergistic anti-tumor effect, providing phenotypic support for combined therapeutic targeting of these oncogenes. Despite these promising results, in vivo editing efficiencies remained modest (~20%) compared to those reported in normal tissues14,23,58. The low measured editing efficiency likely reflects the strong proliferative disadvantage of edited cells, which grow slowly and undergo apoptosis, preventing them from becoming a dominant population. Nevertheless, PDAC is strongly driven by KRASG12D and MYC, so even partial editing that induces death or growth arrest in a subset of tumor cells can reduce tumor burden. Suppression of KRAS and MYC signaling could also remodel the immunosuppressive, CAF-rich tumor microenvironment59, enhancing sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade and improving chemotherapeutic drug uptake30. We further explored potential adaptive responses to gene editing therapy and observed upregulation of the Wnt signaling pathway, which has been reported in various contexts of cancer therapy resistance60,61. We speculate that this upregulation may reflect a feedback mechanism in response to proliferative stress imposed by KRAS and MYC suppression. However, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that this observation is influenced by the timing of PDO collection.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the current protein language model lacks structural resolution, which may constrain its predictive accuracy. Future iterations incorporating structural-aware algorithms, such as SaProt62, may further refine editor design. Second, long-term in vivo safety and comprehensive off-target assessments remain essential for clinical translation. Lastly, the therapeutic window could be further optimized through more selective delivery strategies. Pancreas-tropic AAV serotypes63 and programmable lipid nanoparticle (LNP)64 and virus-like particles (VLP)65 capable of surface modification with pancreatic cancer-targeting peptides or antibodies may provide more precise options for future improvement.

In summary, we present heA3F and haA3F as robust, high-precision base editors with broad applicability. Their successful deployment in PDAC models demonstrates the feasibility of targeting undruggable oncogenes via gene editing and lays the groundwork for translational development. These advances expand the gene editing toolkit and underscore the promise of engineered editors for treating complex malignancies such as PDAC.

Method details

Cell culture and transfection

Cell lines used in this research were purchased from the ATCC. HEK293T, HeLa, PANC-1 and SW1990 were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS in the humidified incubator equilibrated with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. For transfected experiments, cells were seeded in 24-well plates (Corning, USA) and performed using polyethylenimine (Polysciences, USA) based on the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 600 ng of BE and 300 ng of sgRNA-expressing plasmid were together transfected with 50 μl of Opti-MEM (Gibco, USA) containing PEI for 24 h. After transfection, cells were cultured in fresh medium containing 5 μg/ml puromycin (Merck, USA) for 3 days. Finally, genomic DNA was extracted via QuickExtract DNA Extraction Solution (Epicentre, USA). On-target genomic regions (200 bp~300 bp) of interest were amplified by PCR for high-throughput DNA sequencing.

Plasmid construction

Compact A3F systems were synthesized by AZENTA. PCR products were gel-purified, digested with DpnI restriction enzyme (NEB, USA), and assembled via Gibson or GoldenGate assembly based on the manufacturer’s instructions. All gRNA-expression plasmids were assembled via Golden Gate with the protospacer sequence embedded in the primers, and RNF2 sgRNA expression plasmids were used as the template2. The main primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Strains and culture conditions

Escherichia coli Trans5α served as the cloning host, maintained in lysogeny broth (LB; containing 1% (w/v) tryptone, 0.5% (w/v) yeast extract, and 1% (w/v) NaCl) with incubation at 37 °C. For plasmid selection, ampicillin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was supplemented in the medium at a concentration of 100 mg/L to identify positive clones.

High-throughput DNA sequencing of genomic DNA samples and data analysis

Next-generation sequencing libraries were constructed and analyzed following a previously described methodology14. Briefly, purified PCR fragments were subjected to end repair, 5’-phosphorylation, and dA-tailing using the End Prep Enzyme Mix in a single reaction. Subsequently, T-A ligation was performed to attach adapters to both ends of the fragments. The resulting PCR products were purified, quantified, and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq platform in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols.

Amplicon sequencing data were analyzed with CRISPResso2 (v.2.0.45) in batch mode using window parameters set to -wc 10 -w 20. Base conversion frequencies were extracted from the output file “Nucleotide_percentage_summary.txt”, while indel rates were derived from “CRISPRessoBatch_quantification_of_editing_frequency.txt” for base editor experiments. All genomic loci and deep-sequencing primers for sgRNA are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

RNA editing analysis

Transcriptome-wide C-to-U RNA editing analysis was performed as previously described66, with minor modifications. HEK293T cells were transfected with either the base editor construct or nCas9-GFP control (15 µg plasmid DNA) and cultured for 48 h. Cells were then harvested for total RNA extraction and subjected to RNA sequencing. Raw FASTQ files were processed using Fastp to trim adapter sequences, remove low-quality bases (Q < 20), and discard unpaired reads. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38, UCSC) using STAR v2.7.11b with default parameters. SAM files were sorted and indexed using SAMtools.

Aligned reads were analyzed using REDItools2, and all nucleotides other than cytosines were excluded from the analysis. Cytosine positions with read coverage below 10 were also removed to minimize potential errors arising from low sequencing depth. Variant calling was performed using the FreeBayes software package, which applies a probabilistic framework to account for sequencing and mapping errors. The resulting VCF files were further filtered to retain only C-to-U variants detected on both the forward and reverse strands. For base editor overexpression experiments, variant loci were further filtered to exclude sites lacking high-confidence reference genotype calls in the corresponding control samples. Specifically, loci were required to meet the following criteria: (i) read coverage in the control experiment exceeded the 90th percentile of coverage across all single-nucleotide variants in the corresponding overexpression experiment, and (ii) at least 99% of reads supported the reference allele in the control sample. For controls, RNA editing sites were retained only if they had a minimum coverage of ten reads and exhibited a nonzero proportion of reads containing the alternate allele.

PLMs evolution for APOBEC3F

The evolutionary screening process mainly consists of two rounds. In the first round, we evaluate the protein structure and fitness landscape to screen potential efficient mutation sites. Alphafold2 is used to generate the overall structure of enzymes, determining the binding sites of enzymes with substrates and the amino acid sites in disordered regions. Subsequently, we use three models, ESM-1v, ESM-MSA-1b and transformer-inverse fold (ESM-if1), to evaluate the functional scores of given amino acid sequences with saturation mutations. We combine the results from the first round to comprehensively select the top 10% mutation sites. In the second round, we filter through the PSSM scoring matrix, considering the higher score compared to existing amino acids, to obtain potential efficient mutation sites for A3F. The top variants of A3F were further rationally selected based on their functional domains. The screened data of PLMs are listed in the Supplementary Data 2.

The establishment and culture of PDOs

PDOs were established and cultured as previously reported67. Fresh PDAC specimens were obtained from patients who provided written informed consent prior to tissue collection, and the study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute & Hospital (Approval No.: EK20240132). Briefly, fresh PDAC specimens were washed three times with cold PBS containing 10% penicillin-streptomycin. The specimens were dissected into small fragments (<1 mm³) using sterile blades, followed by digestion with a cocktail of 5 mg/mL collagenase II, 5 mg/mL collagenase IV, and 5 mg/mL collagenase XI for 30 min at 37 °C with vigorous shaking. Digestion was terminated by adding advanced DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 7.5% BSA. Tumor cells were harvested by centrifugation at 400 ×g for 5 min, then embedded in Matrigel (Corning, USA) and cultured in Human Complete Feeding Medium (HCPLT). Organoid medium was refreshed every 2 days based on growth status, with passaging performed every 14-20 days. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Lentivirus infection and base editing of MYC and KRAS in PDAC cells and PDOs

Lentivirus production was performed according to established protocols68. Briefly, constructed vectors containing the base editor (BE) system and target-specific gRNA were co-transfected with psPAX2 and pMD2.G packaging plasmids into HEK293T cells at a 3:2:1 mass ratio. Viral supernatants were collected 48 h post-transfection, filtered through 0.45 μm membranes, and concentrated via ultracentrifugation (100,000 × g, 4 °C, 2 h). Concentrated viral particles were used to infect 5×10⁵ PANC-1/SW1990 cells or PDOs (20-30% confluence) in 60 mm dishes with 5 μg/mL polybrene (MOI = 20). Transduced cells were selected using 2 μg/mL blasticidin (Solarbio, China) for 5 days. Genomic DNA from harvested cells was analyzed by Sanger sequencing to quantify editing efficiency.

Colony formation assay

As previously described69, cells were seeded in triplicate into 6-well plates at cell densities of 1000 cells per well for PANC-1 and SW1990, pretreated with virus infection. Following incubation for 10–14 days, the cell colonies were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and then counted. The plating efficiency was calculated as the ratio of the number of colonies to the number of cells plated, and the survival fraction was calculated as follows: plating efficiency of edited cells/plating efficiency of control cells without editing.

CCK-8 assay

As previously described69, the cell survival rate was determined using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (Shandong Sparkjade Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shandong, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, cells (2000 cells/ well) were seeded into 96-well plates and pretreated with virus infection. After a further 3-day incubation period, CCK8 reagent was added to each well at a final concentration of 10% and incubated in the dark for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was detected using a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, USA).

Western blot analysis

Whole-cell extracts were prepared by lysing cells with SDS protein lysis buffer supplemented with proteinase inhibitor cocktail (Bimake, B14001). Proteins in the lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE, and then, the target proteins were detected by immunoblotting with anti-KRAS (ab275885) at 1/1000 dilution, anti-MYC (ab32) at 1/10,000 dilution. β-actin was used as a loading control. Anti-rabbit secondary antibody was used at a 1:5000 dilution (Abmart).

Organoids proliferation analysis

Organoids were plated in 96-well cell culture plates and microscopically verified to form uniformly distributed clusters. Systematic morphological monitoring was performed at 48-h intervals using phase-contrast microscopy with digital image acquisition. Following photography, an equivalent volume of CellTiter-Glo 3D Cell Viability Reagent (G9683, Promega, USA) was added to each well containing culture medium. The plate was protected from light and subjected to horizontal orbital shaking at 300 rpm for 5 min to ensure complete lysis, followed by a 15-min incubation period in a humidified 37 °C/5% CO2 incubator. Luminescence signals were quantified using a PerkinElmer EnSight multimode plate reader, with luminescence serving as a quantitative indicator of organoid metabolic activity through ATP bioluminescence measurement.

Orthotopic mouse model and AAV therapy

NSG (NOD-scid-IL2Rγc⁻/⁻) female mice aged 5–6 weeks were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Prior to orthotopic implantation of patient-derived pancreatic cancer organoids into the pancreas, mice were anesthetized with 2–3% isoflurane in oxygen delivered via a precision vaporizer. The surgical procedure was minimally invasive, and no postoperative analgesia The authors declare the following competing i was administered, as it was well tolerated by the animals. Following orthotopic injection of pancreatic cancer organoids into NSG mice, a mixture of adeno-associated virus serotype 8 (AAV8, commercially produced by Vector Builder) carrying two vectors-one encoding A3F-SpCas9_573-N and sgRNA targeting KRAS or MYC; the other containing A3F-SpCas9_573-C and sgRNAs targeting KRAS or MYC. It was an intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 1 × 1011 genomic copies per mouse. This dual-AAV system enables in vivo reconstitution of functional heA3F and simultaneous delivery of both sgRNAs. One week post-viral delivery, mice were transitioned to a Western diet. Tumor progression and survival were monitored in a blinded manner, and all tumor volumes were maintained within the maximum limit permitted by institutional ethical guidelines (Approval No.: NSFC-AE-2025373). At study endpoint or upon reaching predefined humane endpoints, mice were humanely euthanized by controlled CO₂ inhalation followed by secondary confirmation of death via cervical dislocation, in accordance with AVMA guidelines.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experimental data, unless specifically stated, are expressed as mean±SD from three biological replicates. Statistical comparisons between control and experimental groups were performed using Student’s t-test or log-rank test (GraphPad Prism 8). Differences with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
