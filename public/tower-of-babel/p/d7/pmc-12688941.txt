Cerebrospinal fluid volume: the overlooked determinant in brain and spinal stroke
Authors: Vishal Chavda, Creed M Stary, Bipin Chaurasia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Emerging evidence suggests that reduced cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) volume may contribute to the onset and progression of both brain and spinal strokes. Traditionally under recognized, low CSF states can alter cerebral perfusion, compromise venous compliance, and exert mechanical strain on critical neural structures. This commentary integrates clinical observations, imaging findings, and biomechanical data to highlight the role of CSF depletion in stroke pathophysiology. Recognizing this mechanism may refine current models of cerebrovascular disease and inform novel strategies for prevention and management.

Stroke remains a major global health burden and a leading cause of disability. Conventional models emphasize arterial pathology thrombosis, embolism, and perfusion failure as central mechanisms of ischemia. However, this vascular paradigm alone does not explain all clinical scenarios, particularly posterior circulation infarcts, cryptogenic events, and spinal cord ischemia. Mounting evidence suggests that cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics, especially CSF volume depletion, represent an underappreciated but clinically relevant factor in stroke pathogenesis[1].

CSF, produced mainly in the choroid plexus, plays vital roles in mechanical cushioning, metabolic exchange, and intracranial pressure regulation. According to the Monro-Kellie hypothesis, the intracranial compartment is a fixed volume shared by brain, blood, and CSF; changes in one component inevitably influence the others[2]. While this framework is usually discussed in the context of edema or hemorrhage, reductions in CSF volume whether from spontaneous leaks, surgical drainage, or degenerative processes also carry profound neurological consequences. Spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) is the best-studied low-CSF-volume condition. Beyond its hallmark orthostatic headache, SIH has been linked to infarcts in the cerebellum, brainstem, and spinal cord, often without arterial occlusion[3,4]. In such cases, ischemia seems to arise from biomechanical and hemodynamic disturbances induced by CSF loss. “Brain sag,” or downward displacement of neural structures, can stretch vessels, impair venous compliance, and distort the vertebrobasilar system[5]. Neuroimaging has corroborated these mechanisms. MRI in SIH frequently shows cerebellar tonsillar descent, cisternal effacement, dural enhancement, and pituitary enlargement features of low CSF volume[6]. Some patients present with focal neurological deficits mimicking stroke, and perfusion-weighted imaging has demonstrated regional hypoperfusion consistent with mechanical stress[7]. Similarly, the spinal CSF reservoir cushions the cord and buffers vascular dynamics. The depletion of spinal CSF can provoke venous congestion, compromise arterial inflow, and predispose to cord infarction, particularly in patients with tenuous perfusion[8]. Clinical reports of spinal strokes after anesthesia, lumbar drainage, or inadvertent dural puncture exemplify this link[9]. CSF depletion also perturbs venous return. Reduced CSF volume elicits compensatory venous dilation to preserve intracranial equilibrium, but this paradoxically elevates venous pressure, diminishes cerebral perfusion pressure, and promotes interstitial congestion[10]. Watershed and brainstem territories are especially vulnerable. Importantly, reduced CSF volume may occur even when CSF pressure appears near normal, particularly in chronic or insidious leaks[11]. Such cases may escape recognition unless clinicians employ advanced imaging, such as CSF volumetry or elastance measurements.

Beyond stroke initiation, CSF volume may influence outcomes. Post-stroke autoregulation is often impaired; if CSF volume remains low, the brain is more susceptible to hypoperfusion, complicating recovery. Conversely, restoration of CSF volume for instance, with epidural blood patch has been associated with rapid symptom resolution in selected patients lacking vascular occlusion[12]. This raises the possibility that unrecognized CSF hypovolemia may underlie some cryptogenic strokes, particularly in younger patients without conventional risk factors. Findings such as dural enhancement or pituitary engorgement should prompt consideration of this mechanism[13]. Preventive and therapeutic implications are notable. Clinicians should exercise caution with unnecessary CSF drainage in high-risk patients and maintain vigilance for subtle presentations of low-CSF states. Imaging protocols that include CSF assessment may improve diagnostic precision, especially when vascular studies are inconclusive[14]. Identifying patients who might benefit from CSF-targeted interventions alongside established reperfusion or neuroprotective therapies represents a promising frontier. Although still underexplored, animal models indicate that CSF volume directly interacts with perfusion pressure and infarct size[15–17]. Greater awareness and interdisciplinary collaboration among neurologists, neuroradiologists, and neurosurgeons are essential to clarify this relationship.

Conclusion (take-home message)

CSF volume is not merely a passive component of intracranial physiology but an active determinant of cerebrovascular stability. Low CSF states can precipitate or exacerbate ischemia in both the brain and spinal cord through mechanical displacement, venous congestion, and impaired perfusion. Recognizing this missing link may refine stroke models, enhance diagnostic accuracy, and inspire novel therapeutic strategies. Future research must explicitly integrate CSF dynamics into stroke science to bridge current gaps and improve patient outcomes.
