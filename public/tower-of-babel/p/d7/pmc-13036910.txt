Emergence of blaNDM−5 and international dissemination of an ST26 Salmonella Thompson resistance gene-enriched sublineage
Authors: Haiyan Qi, Chunxin Xu, Xifeng Sun, Xiaowei Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to retrospectively analyze Salmonella Thompson isolates collected at our research center and to investigate the genetic characteristics of Salmonella Thompson by integrating isolates from the present study with publicly available global genomic data, with a particular focus on the ST26 clone.MethodsThis retrospective genomic epidemiological study investigated Salmonella Thompson isolates collected from a tertiary hospital in northern China between 2018 and 2023. We employed antimicrobial susceptibility testing, short-read and long-read whole-genome sequencing, phylogenomic analysis, and plasmid structure characterization. All Salmonella Thompson genomes from the NCBI GenBank database were included in comparative genomic analysis.ResultsAll Salmonella Thompson strains in this study belonged to ST26. Strain WFS122 carried blaNDM−5, which was located on a common IncX3-type plasmid found in NDM-positive Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae strains (100% homology). Analysis of the public database revealed that ST26 is the globally predominant lineage of Salmonella Thompson, accounting for 89.57% of strains and harboring the overwhelming majority of resistance genes and Class 1 integrons. We further identified Clade D4 as a high-risk evolutionary branch with elevated resistance rates. Within this study, 96.15% of Clade D4 strains harbored Class 1 integrons, which were pivotal in resistance gene carriage, primarily encoding the aminoglycoside resistance gene aadA and the trimethoprim resistance gene dfrA.ConclusionWe hypothesize that ST26 Salmonella Thompson exhibits global expansion, with the high-risk clone Clade D4 showing a strong association with Class 1 integrons and resistance genes. This high-risk clone has demonstrated potential for transmission in China, the United States, and the United Kingdom, necessitating expanded research scope and scale. Additionally, this study represents the identification of NDM-5-carrying ST26 Salmonella Thompson. The emergence of carbapenem-resistant Salmonella Thompson poses a significant challenge for clinical management of Salmonella infections caused by this strain.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04841-y.Keywords: blaNDM−5, Salmonella thompson, IncX3; ST26; High-risk clone

Salmonella is a widespread and important zoonotic pathogen, and Salmonella infections represent a major global public health concern, particularly in developing countries [1]. Salmonella spp.are intracellularly parasitic Gram-negative Enterobacteriaceae widely distributed in nature. They primarily infect animals and humans through oral ingestion of contaminated food and water sources, with contaminated animal meat, eggs, and dairy products being the main sources of human Salmonella infection.

Currently, over 2,600 internationally recognized Salmonella serotypes exist. Clinically, the Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Thompson (Salmonella Thompson) has recently emerged as a growing concern. This strain has now ranked among the top ten prevalent serotypes reported in both China and the United States [2, 3]. Salmonella Thompson was first isolated in 1924 during an outbreak among the Thompson family in England [4].

The blaNDM−5 carbapenemase gene was first identified in 2011 in Escherichia coli (EC405) isolated from a patient in the United Kingdom who had been hospitalized in India. blaNDM−5 differs from blaNDM−1 at two amino acid positions: valine (Val) at position 88 is replaced by leucine (Leu), and methionine (Met) at position 154 is replaced by leucine (Leu) [5]. Compared to blaNDM−1, blaNDM−5 exhibits higher hydrolase activity against carbapenems, cefotaxime, cefotetan, and cefotiam. Currently, blaNDM−5-carrying CRE strains are predominantly Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae [6]. Reports of carbapenemase-producing Salmonella strains in domestic and international literature remain scarce, with occasional detections of blaVIM−2, blaVIM−1, blaNDM−1, and blaIMP−4 types [7–10]. blaNDM−5 has been identified in Salmonella Typhimurium [11, 12], Salmonella Typhi [13], Salmonella London [14], and Salmonella 1,4 [5], , 12: i:- [15].

Currently, treatment for Salmonella infections involves three classes of first-line antimicrobials: fluoroquinolones (e.g., ciprofloxacin), third-generation cephalosporins (e.g., cefotaxime and ceftriaxone), and macrolides (e.g., azithromycin) [16]. Reports indicate their efficacy is diminishing due to rising resistance rates. An 8-year study in China found clinical Salmonella Thompson resistance to these antimicrobials exceeding 25% [16]. Notably, sporadic cases in some Chinese regions exhibit co-resistance to three first-line antibiotics. The resistance patterns observed in Salmonella Thompson in Zhai’s study [2] may enable these dominant clones to transmit directly from the food supply to humans, thereby exacerbating the public health crisis associated with antibiotic resistance.

In this study, we characterized the distribution of Salmonella Thompson using a global dataset, focusing on the high-risk clone Clade D4 of ST26 Salmonella Thompson. Additionally, we analyzed the genetic environment and plasmids of blaNDM−5-carrying Salmonella Thompson isolates in this study, aiming to reveal the potential transmission risk of blaNDM−5 in ST26 Salmonella Thompson.

Methods

Materials and methods bacterial isolation and identification

From 2018 to 2023, five Salmonella Thompson strains were isolated from a tertiary hospital in northern China. All strains were identified using Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS).

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

The BD Phoenix™ M50 System (Becton, Dickinson and Company, New Jersey, USA) and the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method (Oxoid, Hampshire, UK) were used to test the susceptibility of the strains to 22 antimicrobial agents commonly used in clinical practice. Antimicrobial sensitivity results were interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines (M100-S32) [17]. Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 was used as quality control for the antimicrobial susceptibility tests.

Whole-genome sequencing and assembly

Genomic DNA was extracted using a bacterial DNA kit (OMEGA, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. WGS was performed using both the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, CA, USA) and Nanopore PromethION (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, OX, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A hybrid assembly was then performed using Unicycler v0.4.9 [18], incorporating both the short paired-end Illumina reads and the long Nanopore reads.

Whole-genome sequencing analysis

Antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) were identified using NCBI Blast with ABRicate 0.8 [19]. PlasmidFinder [20] was employed to determine the plasmid replicon type. WGS-based Salmonella serotyping was performed using SeqSero [21]. The obtained sequences were submitted to the PubMLST database (https://pubmlst.org/) to determine the allele number and specific sequence type (ST). DANMEL was used for analyzing sequenced mobile genetic elements in bacteria [22]. BLASTN was employed for the alignment and comparison of the plasmid sequences analyzed in this study with highly homologous plasmid sequences publicly available in NCBI.

The sequences of 1721 Salmonella Thompson strains were obtained from the NCBI database for constructing a global distribution map (up to JUN 1, 2025). A phylogenetic tree was jointly constructed using 1546 ST26 Salmonella Thompson strains. Subsequently, core single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were identified by MUMMER 3.25 [23], and a total of 114,517 core SNPs were identified. The core SNPs were extracted and used to build a phylogenetic tree using FastTree v2.1.10 [24] using the GTR model of nucleotide substitution, and visualized by Interactive Tree Of Life (iTOL) [25]. The population structure of each phylogenetic tree was defined using hierBAPS v6.0 [26]. The plasmid alignment was generated using BRIG v0.95 [27].

Results

Clinical information of strains

Four isolates (WFS35, WFS79, WFS112, WFS411) were obtained from pediatric patients aged between 1 and 10 years, with all isolates derived from fecal specimens. The blaNDM−5-carrying Salmonella Thompson strain WFS122 was collected from the blood sample of a 62-year-old female patient in December 2020. The patient had cerebrovascular disease (Table 1).

Clinical information, resistance genes, and MLST analysis of Salmonella Thompson

All five Salmonella Thompson strains belonged to ST26. WFS35, WFS79, and WFS411 were predicted to carry 11–14 resistance genes each, primarily including aminoglycoside resistance genes, β-lactamase resistance genes, fluoroquinolone resistance genes, sulfonamide resistance genes, and trimethoprim resistance genes. WFS35 carried the ESBL resistance gene blaCTX−M−65, while WFS79 harbored blaTEM−1. No resistance genes were predicted for WFS112. WFS122 was predicted to carry only blaNDM−5.

The results of the drug susceptibility tests demonstrated that strains WFS35 and WFS79 exhibited resistance to 9 and 12 antimicrobial agents, respectively, with resistance predominantly directed against β-lactam antimicrobial agents. In contrast, strain WFS112 was susceptible to all tested antimicrobial agents, whereas strain WFS411 showed resistance to 4 antimicrobial agents. WFS122 exhibited resistance to 11 antimicrobial agents. Specifically, it demonstrated simultaneous resistance to all three carbapenems (imipenem, meropenem, ertapenem). Among the β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor agents, resistance was observed to all except aztreonam. It exhibited susceptibility to polymyxin, aminoglycosides (gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin), chloramphenicol, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, tetracyclines (tetracycline, tigecycline), and fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin) (Supplemental Table 1).

Whole-genome sequencing analysis of WFS122 strain

To analyze the genomic context of blaNDM−5, we performed long-read sequencing (Table 2). Results revealed that WFS122 possesses a circular chromosome of 4,737,450 bp. It carries three plasmids, designated pWFS122-1, pWFS122-NDM, and pWFS122-3. WFS122 carries only one antibiotic resistance genes: blaNDM−5. blaNDM−5 is located on the pWFS122-NDM plasmid, while no resistance genes were predicted on the other plasmids. pWFS122-NDM belongs to the IncX3 plasmid family. The full-length pWFS122-NDM plasmid is 46,161 bp in length with a CG content of 46.65%.

Global distribution of Salmonella Thompson

This study analyzed 1,726 Salmonella Thompson strains (1,721 downloaded from NCBI and 5 from our laboratory) (Supplemental Table 3). Based on ST typing analysis, ST26 Salmonella Thompson accounted for an absolute dominance (1546/1726, 89.57%), followed by ST2125 (80/1,726, 4.63%). A global map was constructed using 1709 Salmonella Thompson strains (17 strains without national information). Results indicated these strains originated from 14 countries, with the United States and the United Kingdom being the most well-represented countries within the Salmonella Thompson isolate dataset. ST26 is the predominant ST type globally, and all Salmonella Thompson isolates from China belong to ST26. ST2125 primarily originates from the United States and the United Kingdom (Fig. 2).

Analysis of antimicrobial resistance genes in ST26 Salmonella Thompson

We conducted resistance gene analysis on global Salmonella Thompson isolates and found that 99 (99/1726, 5.74%) strains carried resistance genes (Supplemental Table 3). Among these, 89 (89/99, 89.90%) strains belonged to ST26, while 10 (10/99, 10.10%) strains belonged to other ST types. Heatmap analysis of resistance genes revealed that resistance was primarily mediated by genes conferring resistance to aminoglycosides, sulfonamides, and quinolones (Fig. 3). Among the 99 strains harboring resistance genes, 37 strains were found to carry Class 1 integrons, which were subsequently analyzed (Fig. 4). Eleven subtypes (A-K) of Class 1 integrons were predicted, with the A-type combination being the most prevalent (IntI1-dfrA12-aadA2-qacED1-sul1). Most Class 1 integrons 3’-CS harbored the qacED1-sul1 resistance gene. Among the resistance gene cassettes carried by Class 1 integrons, aadA and dfrA were the most prevalent. Of these 37 strains, 36 belonged to ST26, while only one (H subtype) belonged to ST2125. Three strains of this experiment carry Class 1 integrons, with WFS35 and WFS411 belonging to the D subtype and WFS79 belonging to the A subtype.

ST26 Salmonella Thompson phylogenetic tree

As a globally prevalent ST type, ST26 was analyzed using 1,546 ST26 Salmonella Thompson isolates to construct a phylogenetic tree (Fig. 5) (Supplemental Table 3). The results revealed four distinct clades. These 1,546 ST26 Salmonella Thompson isolates were collected from 13 countries, with the majority originating from the United States (971/1,546, 62.81%), Canada (296/1546, 19.15%), and the United Kingdom (217/1546, 14.04%). Host sources were predominantly human (532/1546, 34.41%) and animal (109/1546, 7.05%). The isolation dates of the strains primarily covered the interval between 2011 and 2020. All five strains in this study belonged to Clade D. WFS35 and WFS79 showed close phylogenetic relationships with human strains from China. WFS112 was closely related to strains isolated from the United Kingdom between 2011 and 2020. WFS411 exhibited high similarity to animal-derived strains from China. WFS122 shared close phylogenetic relationships with strains from the United States and China.

We screened strains carrying ≥ 3 antimicrobial resistance genes, identifying a total of 38 strains. Of these, 26 (26/38, 68.42%) were concentrated within the Clade D4. Notably, 25 of these 26 Clade D4 strains carried IntI1, resulting in a IntI1 carriage rate of 96.15%. Among Clade D4 isolates harboring resistance genes, the majority originated from China (13/26, 50.00%), followed by the United States (6/26, 23.08%) and the United Kingdom (6/26, 23.08%). Furthermore, of the 38 strains carrying ≥ 3 resistance genes, 34 concurrently carried IntI1, indicating a strong correlation between IntI1 and the dissemination of resistance genes in Salmonella Thompson.

Discussion

This study revealed the antimicrobial resistance and global spread of Salmonella Thompson. We also provided a genetic environment and plasmid analysis of the blaNDM−5 resistance gene in carbapenem-resistant Salmonella Thompson isolates. The isolates in this study all belonged to ST26, and we found that ST26 is a globally prevalent ST type. We speculate that ST26 has the potential for global expansion. Furthermore, our study identified one strain carrying blaNDM−5. These observations suggest that ST26 carrying resistance genes, including carbapenem resistance genes, poses significant challenges to healthcare and public health.

Salmonella Thompson has recently garnered increasing attention [16]. Similar to other non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) serotypes, Salmonella Thompson infection typically causes gastroenteritis [28], which is usually self-limiting. However, vulnerable populations such as the elderly, immunocompromised individuals, and children are at risk for severe complications including bacteremia and meningitis [29, 30]. Consistent with our findings, four pediatric patients and one elderly woman with underlying conditions were infected with Salmonella Thompson. WFS122 did not undergo invasive procedures, yet Salmonella Thompson was cultured from blood samples. We hypothesize that the pathogen translocated into the bloodstream as a result of the patient’s immunocompromised status, ultimately leading to bacteremia. Studies indicate increasing resistance in Salmonella Thompson [2]. This study identified a blaNDM−5-carrying Salmonella Thompson strain exhibiting resistance to all three carbapenem antibiotics. It also demonstrated resistance to all β-lactam antibiotics and β-lactam inhibitors except aztreonam, which remained susceptible. This further underscores the severe resistance profile of blaNDM−5-positive Salmonella Thompson, warranting widespread attention.

Within the Enterobacteriaceae family, plasmids such as IncX3, IncFII, and IncN have been reported to harbor blaNDM−5, but IncX3 remains the predominant plasmid type [31]. However, in Salmonella species, there are slight variations in the plasmids carrying blaNDM−5. Tan’s study found that blaNDM−5 in Salmonella enterica Serovar London was located on an IncFIB plasmid [14]. Ke’s research found that blaNDM−5 in Salmonella 1,4 [5], ,12:i:- was located in a 208,610 bp IncHI2-type plasmid [11]. However, consistent with Wu’s findings, Salmonella enterica blaNDM−5 was found on a 46,161 bp IncX3 plasmid [32], indicating diverse plasmid types carrying blaNDM−5 in Salmonella species. Multiple plasmid types may serve as vectors for transmitting blaNDM−5 resistance genes. IncX3-type plasmids carrying blaNDM−5 are primarily found in Escherichia coli [33]. In this study, the IncX3 plasmid from Salmonella Thompson showed 100% homology with IncX3 from Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli. As IncX3 is the most prevalent IncX3-type plasmid carrier in E. coli and K. pneumoniae, this indicates that IncX3 carries the risk of transmitting carbapenem resistance genes to Salmonella.

Studies have demonstrated that the IncX3 plasmid exhibits a significantly higher conjugative transfer frequency compared with other plasmid types. This serves as the primary mechanism for the rapid global dissemination of blaNDM−5-carrying IncX3, suggesting that IncX3 has become a crucial vector for blaNDM−5 transmission. The blaNDM−5 gene is usually located on Tn125 [34]. In this study, ISAba125 was inserted or truncated by IS5, and there was an insertion sequence IS3000 downstream of the blaNDM−5 gene. The transfer of the blaNDM−5 gene may be mediated by it. The transfer of the blaNDM−5 gene may be mediated by IS3000. Furthermore, our analysis of the genetic environment surrounding blaNDM−5 revealed additional mobile elements capable of mediating resistance gene transfer, such as IS26, Tn3-like, and ISKox3. The presence of these mobile elements may promote the dissemination of the blaNDM−5 gene. The insertion and recombination of multiple mobile genetic elements result in diverse and polymorphic environments surrounding the blaNDM−5 gene. This genetic diversity is closely associated with its role in mediating broad-spectrum resistance in host bacteria and facilitating inter-bacterial transfer, while also conferring additional biological phenotypes to host organisms.

This study analyzed the global distribution of Salmonella Thompson and found that ST26 represents the most prevalent sequence type, constituting a high-risk clone widely distributed worldwide. ST26 Salmonella Thompson exhibits a higher likelihood of carrying ARGs and Class 1 integrons. Phylogenetic tree analysis of ST26 Salmonella Thompson revealed that most strains in this study shared high homology with Salmonella Thompson strains from other regions of China. Notably, strain WFS411 showed a close phylogenetic relationship with animal-derived strains from China. Strain WFS112 demonstrated a close relationship with strains from the United Kingdom, while WFS122 was closely related to strains from the United States-findings that point to the potential for global transmission of ST26 Salmonella Thompson. Furthermore, strains harboring resistance genes were predominantly concentrated in the United Kingdom, the United States, and China. Phylogenetic analysis revealed closer evolutionary relationships between strains from the United States and China, suggesting that resistance genes may have spread across regions along with the bacterial strains. Zhai et al. [2] analyzed Salmonella Thompson in China and identified ST26 as the predominant ST type, consistent with the ST designation of the strains in this study. Compared to other global Salmonella Thompson isolates, the isolate WFS122 is the only Salmonella Thompson carrying blaNDM−5. The identification of carbapenem-resistant Salmonella Thompson highlights the emergence of a clinically relevant resistance mechanism in this serovar, which limits treatment options.

As demonstrated in this study, the high-risk clone Clade D4 carries more resistance genes and Class 1 integrons. Our findings indicate that Clade D4 represents a high-risk clone associated with ARGs. Class 1 integrons act as genetic platforms for capturing and expressing resistance gene cassettes; their dissemination is mediated by their location on mobile genetic elements such as plasmids and transposons. In this study, the predominant gene cassettes detected in Salmonella Thompson were dfrA and aadA, with the most common array being IntI1-dfrA12-aadA2-qacED1-sul1. This specific cassette array is also prevalent in Klebsiella pneumoniae [35], this gene cassette configuration is a conserved and widely disseminated structure among Enterobacteriaceae. Additionally, one strain carried blaTEM, a β-lactam resistance gene harbored within an integron; this inevitably elevates the risk of resistance emergence and multidrug resistance in clinical strains. Notably, the high-risk clone Clade D4 comprises isolates distributed across different countries and hosts, with the spread of their resistance genes closely linked to Class 1 integrons. This observation points to the occurrence of long-distance transmission and underscores the potential risk of clonal spread driven by the dissemination of this integron-carrying lineage.

Overall, we performed genomic analyses on ST26 Salmonella Thompson isolates from this study, with a particular focus on strain harboring blaNDM−5, and provided a detailed characterization of the blaNDM−5-carrying plasmid. Using a global dataset, we further characterized the distribution patterns of Salmonella Thompson, revealing ST26 as the predominant ST type circulating widely across the globe. Additionally, resistance gene analysis identified a high-risk clone Clade D4, which may disseminate resistance genes via Class 1 integrons. Given these findings, more comprehensive studies on this emerging high-risk clone are warranted to prevent the further spread of this clinically significant multidrug-resistant bacterium.
