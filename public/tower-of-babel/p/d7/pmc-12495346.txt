The experience of individuals living with alcohol use disorder within palliative care and end of life services: A scoping review
Authors: Amélie Deschamps, Andrée-Anne Légaré, Anne-Marie Auger, Natalia Muñoz Gómez, Magaly Brodeur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividuals with alcohol use disorder (AUD) often face stigma and fragmented care, contributing to the underutilization of palliative care (PC), despite overlapping clinical needs. Research on the specific experiences of AUD patients in PC and end of life (EOL) settings remains limited.MethodsA scoping review following PRISMA guidelines was conducted across five databases. From 865 initial results, eight studies met inclusion criteria after full-text screening. Five of them were case reports. Eligible studies focused on the experiences of individuals living with AUD receiving PC or EOL services. Data extraction and narrative synthesis were then conducted.ResultsPatients with AUD in PC often experienced significant symptom burden, including poorly managed pain, depression, and alcohol withdrawal symptoms. AUD was frequently underrecognized by healthcare professionals, and details of alcohol use were inconsistently reported. Transitions to PC were often delayed, with limited patient involvement in EOL decision-making. Stigma, clinician discomfort, and lack of integrated addiction and PC approaches were recurring barriers to optimal care.ConclusionGiven the limited research on the management of AUD within PC settings, this review highlights the need for further investigation. While the findings suggest potential priorities for future research and clinical practice, including improved AUD screening, enhanced training for PC providers, and earlier, patient-centered EOL planning. These findings should be interpreted with caution, as they are based on a small number of studies, most of which are case reports. Addressing these gaps may enhance the quality of care and outcomes for individuals with AUD at the EOL.Keywords: Palliative care, Alcohol use disorder, End of life, Scoping review, Problematic alcohol use, Substance use disorder

Background

General context and definitions

Alcohol-related morbidity and mortality have been rising in the last few decades and are expected to continue increasing (Witham et al., 2019, World Health Organization, 2018). In a study by Glantz et al. (2020) comparing the prevalence and associated factors of alcohol use disorders (AUD) across a wide range of countries, it was reported that the average lifetime prevalence of alcohol use across all countries was 80 %, with rates varying from 3.8 % in Iraq to 97.1 % in Peru. Additionally, among non-abstainers, the lifetime prevalence of AUD across all countries was 10.7 %. According to the American Psychiatric Association, AUD is associated with higher chances of developing health issues such as cancers, gastritis, ulcers, cardiomyopathy, alcohol-related seizures, neurocognitive disorders, and liver cirrhosis, among others (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Throughout this article, the term 'AUD patients' will be used to refer collectively to individuals with problematic alcohol use, alcohol misuse, or AUD. Despite the growing global impact of AUD, there remains a gap in understanding how these individuals are managed and supported in the context of severe illness and at the end of life (EOL).

Given that alcohol is linked to numerous illnesses, individuals with AUD may require palliative care (PC) when recovery-focused treatment is no longer viable, according to Cook et al. (2022). PC focuses on quality of life, pain management, and optimization of the person’s sense of well-being at the EOL while using a patient-centered holistic approach (Chan et al., 2021, Chan et al., 2023, Cook et al., 2022). It aims to provide comfort and support to the patient throughout the challenges they face in the EOL process (Collins and Small, 2019). However, individuals with AUD may be underserved in these settings due to stigma, lack of integration between addiction services and PC, and limited clinical guidance specific to this population (MacCormac, 2017, Witham, 2023, Witham et al., 2019).

Context of alcohol use disorder and palliative care in the last few years

In the literature focusing on AUD and PC, there appears to be a distinct perception of each concept. Chan et al. (2021) explain in their article that PC is often viewed as a compassionate approach aimed at improving quality of life and is sometimes even regarded as having spiritual significance. Meanwhile, individuals with AUD are often viewed in a more negative light and frequently face stigma, as some health professionals assume patients can simply cease alcohol consumption to improve or restore their health (MacCormac, 2017, Witham, 2023). Others may see the disorder as complex and unconsciously dismiss or avoid the AUD population (MacCormac, 2017). This stigmatization is consistent with broader findings in substance use research, which show that negative provider attitudes can result in fragmented or delayed care, particularly in non-addiction-focused health systems (Berry, 2023, MacCormac, 2017, Witham, 2023). Alternatively, some health professionals may perceive a separation between addiction services and their patient’s other health condition, despite the interconnected nature of both issues, highlighting the necessity to address them in tandem (Berry, 2023). Finally, PC for those with AUD remains relatively underutilized and not fully understood (Lucey, 2019).

Previous reviews have examined PC needs among individuals with substance use disorders (SUDs) more broadly, but none have focused specifically on the experiences of patients with AUD. For example, Mundt-Leach (2016), Witham et al. (2019), and Cook et al. (2022) each wrote a review article focusing on the SUD population in PC, while Crooks et al. (2023) addressed access barriers and facilitators among individuals living with homelessness, SUD, and incarceration or sex work. However, these reviews did not isolate AUD-specific experiences or challenges. While overlapping with other SUD populations, individuals with AUD face distinct PC issues, including distinct symptom management challenges, withdrawal risks, and psychosocial dynamics that require tailored investigation (Cook et al., 2022, MacCormac, 2017, Mundt-Leach, 2016). One exception is MacCormac’s (2017) review, which explored clinical identification and management needs of alcohol-dependent patients in PC. That review highlighted the importance of routine screening, identified gaps in care, and called for further research on effective treatment strategies in EOL settings.

However, we still have a limited understanding of the experiences of patients with AUD in PC. Gaining deeper insights into their unique challenges and needs is crucial for improving their care and support. This population is often excluded from mainstream EOL research, and their experiences are rarely captured in addiction-focused literature (Cook et al., 2022, MacCormac, 2017, Mundt-Leach, 2016). Thus, this present review aimed to report on the experience of AUD patients receiving PC and EOL services in the current scientific literature. We chose to focus on the experience of people with AUD at the EOL to capture not only the clinical aspects of care, but also the emotional, relational, and challenges reflected in the literature.

Methods

Identification and justification of the method

This study is a scoping review that follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines for scoping reviews (see Additional file 1) (Tricco et al., 2018). It is also based on the methodological framework established by Arksey and O’Malley (2005) which has been refined by Levac et al. (2010). Scoping reviews are “exploratory projects that systematically map the literature available on the topic, identifying the key concepts, theories, sources of evidence, and gaps in the research” (CIHR, 2010). This method was chosen to answer the research question in this review to gather and analyze a wide range of worldwide information and obtain a portrait of the current situation relating to the experience of AUD patients in PC (Fortin, 2022, Levac et al., 2010).

Data sources and literature search

A comprehensive electronic search was developed by AD under the supervision of MB and in collaboration with an academic health librarian to create a selection of keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) to capture the literature on AUD within PC and EOL services (Medical Subject Headings, 2024). Then, two categories of keywords were developed, which are presented in Fig. 1. After consulting with the information expert, five databases were searched: Academic Search Complete, APA PsychINFO, CINAHL Plus with Full Text, MEDLINE with Full Text, and SocINDEX with Full Text. These databases were chosen because the research question targeted medical and psychosocial elements. Google Scholar and references in the included articles were also searched and yielded no other eligible studies. The search results were retrieved on August 21, 2023, and the complete search strategy can be found in Fig. 2. Finally, a second and final search was performed on July 19, 2024, to verify whether there were new or missing admissible sources, and no other sources could be included.

Study selection

The following inclusion criteria were used to determine the eligibility of the sources. First, the studies had to be available in English. Second, they had to focus on the experiences of patients with problematic alcohol use or AUD who were also receiving PC and EOL services. Our focus on “experience” was intended to capture studies reporting patient- or provider-level insights related to the lived realities of AUD in the context of PC and EOL services. These criteria were selected to assess the number of studies focused on the topic, to understand the existing literature about the relevant population, and to identify gaps in research regarding the issue.

The search yielded 865 results, of which 577 remained once duplicates were removed. Afterward, Covidence (2014) was used to better keep track of the screening process and the reasons why certain sources were excluded or why we needed further discussion to decide. This software found 7 duplicates, which resulted in a total of 570 remaining articles. Following the title and abstract review performed by AD, 17 articles were retained. These articles were then read in full and validated by AD, A-MA, and NMG under the supervision of MB. Uncertainties were deliberated within the team, leading to a decision on whether to include or exclude each source. Ultimately, a total of 8 articles remained and were consequently incorporated into this review. Five of the eight studies were case reports.

Data extraction and analysis

For the data extraction process, AD developed a structured data extraction grid using Microsoft Excel to systematically capture relevant information from each included article. The grid included predefined categories such as: 1) bibliographic details (authors, year, journal), 2) study objectives, 3) study design and methodology, 4) population and sample characteristics, and 5) main findings. After the data extraction, AD used narrative synthesis methods to report the findings. This method allows the synthesis of findings from numerous studies, using words and text to summarize and analyze findings (Popay et al., 2005). Both the data extraction and the narrative synthesis processes were validated by all authors.

Results

The following sections summarize study characteristics and key findings across five domains: symptom severity and management, alcohol use, patient beliefs and emotions, ceilings of care, and transitions to PC.

Study characteristics

The eight selected sources were published between 1993 and 2023, reflecting a limited yet geographically diverse body of literature on the experience of AUD patients in PC (see Table 1). Most originated from the United Kingdom (n = 4) (Berry, 2023, Berry et al., 2017, Webber and Davies, 2012, Witham, 2023), with others from the United States (n = 1) (Parsons et al., 2008), Canada (n = 1) (McCorquodale et al., 1993), Australia (n = 1) (Irwin et al., 2005), and Italy (n = 1) (Mercadante et al., 2020). Among the included studies, five were case reports or series (Berry, 2023, Berry et al., 2017, Irwin et al., 2005, McCorquodale et al., 1993, Witham, 2023), while three used quantitative methods (Mercadante et al., 2020, Parsons et al., 2008, Webber and Davies, 2012). Combined, the studies included 790 participants (455 males, 335 females), generally middle-aged or older (Berry et al., 2017, Irwin et al., 2005, McCorquodale et al., 1993, Witham, 2023), though one study (Parsons et al., 2008) included participants as young as 16. Primary diagnoses included cancer (Irwin et al., 2005, McCorquodale et al., 1993, Mercadante et al., 2020, Parsons et al., 2008, Webber and Davies, 2012) and alcohol-related liver disease (Berry, 2023, Berry et al., 2017, Witham, 2023). Due to limited large-scale research on this population, case reports were included for their detailed clinical insights and to illustrate the complexity of care in individual cases.

Symptom severity and management

Across all sources, AUD patients in PC experienced significant symptom severity. Pain, distress, depression, anxiety, and sleep-related concerns were consistently reported (see Fig. 3). Some patients also experienced symptoms related to alcohol withdrawal, such as tremors (Irwin et al., 2005), cognitive impairment (McCorquodale et al., 1993), hallucinations (McCorquodale et al., 1993), and restlessness (Irwin et al., 2005).

Common symptom management approaches included opioids and other analgesics (Irwin et al., 2005, McCorquodale et al., 1993, Mercadante et al., 2020, Parsons et al., 2008, Witham, 2023) and counseling or psychotherapy (Irwin et al., 2005, McCorquodale et al., 1993, Witham, 2023) (see Fig. 4).

illustrates the number of studies reporting on each symptom management treatment.

While AUD patients were often described as having higher symptom severity, findings were mixed. Parsons et al. (2008), which included patients with suspected AUD (based on the CAGE screening), found that CAGE-positive patients experienced higher levels of pain, sleep disturbances, dyspnea, anxiety, depression, fatigue, and overall symptom distress. These patients were also more likely to use opioids at the time of referral, with a higher median morphine-equivalent daily dose (MEDD). However, the study does not mention opioid use disorder (OUD). Although opioid doses decreased at follow-up in both groups (CAGE-positive and CAGE-negative), the difference was not statistically significant. The authors noted that this early opioid use may reflect greater symptom expression among CAGE-positive patients. Meanwhile, they also highlighted concerns about stigma and the risk of inappropriate pharmacologic management in this population.

In contrast, Mercadante et al.’s study (2020) found that CAGE-positive patients showed similar improvements in symptom control compared to controls when given comprehensive PC, despite initially lower expectations. Webber and Davies (2012) also found no clear association between AUD and greater symptom distress, though high-risk AUD was linked to depression.

Alcohol use

Half of the studies reported on patterns of alcohol use beyond diagnostic status (Berry, 2023, Irwin et al., 2005, McCorquodale et al., 1993, Witham, 2023). Continued or relapsing alcohol use near EOL was documented, often linked to coping with emotional distress or chronic pain. Several patients reduced or stopped drinking due to physical decline or not enjoying it anymore, while others maintained high consumption until late in the disease trajectory (Berry, 2023; Irwin, 2005; Webber and Davies, 2012). Some reasons for alcohol consumption included the self-management of pain or symptoms related to post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety, and stimulus-seeking (McCorquodale et al., 1993, Witham, 2023). Quantitative data showed CAGE-positive prevalence rates between 3 % and 18 %, though contextual detail regarding alcohol use was generally sparse (Mercadante et al., 2020, Parsons et al., 2008; Webber and Davies, 2012).

Patient beliefs, emotions, aspirations, and choices

Six articles reported information regarding patients’ beliefs, feelings, desires, or decisions, though the depth of patient voice varied (Berry, 2023, Berry et al., 2017, Irwin et al., 2005, McCorquodale et al., 1993, Mercadante et al., 2020, Witham, 2023). Berry et al. (2017) and Berry (2023) described individual cases where patients expressed a desire for abstinence, awareness of poor prognosis, or determination to pursue treatment. One patient hoped for a liver transplant and to return home for Christmas, while another refused spiritual support but accepted life-extending procedures.

McCorquodale et al. (1993) reported a patient prioritizing relief from psychological distress over physical pain, while Irwin et al. (2005) mentioned choices regarding alcohol intake near EOL in four cases. Mercadante et al. (2020) found that CAGE-positive patients began with lower care goal expectations but showed comparable improvements in satisfaction with care once symptoms were managed.

More extensive reflection on patient beliefs came from Witham (2023), who highlighted how stigma, fear of discrimination, and prior negative experiences shaped patients’ reluctance to seek care. Some patients delayed help, avoided difficult conversations, or self-managed symptoms. Others hoped sobriety might allow for more meaningful interactions with family. Emotional barriers such as shame, isolation, and self-blame were also noted.

Ceilings of care and transition to palliative care

Five studies discussed transitions into PC and limitations of care (Berry, 2023, Berry et al., 2017, Irwin et al., 2005, McCorquodale et al., 1993, Parsons et al., 2008). Delayed referrals were common, often occurring late in disease progression or after multiple hospitalizations. Decisions to transition to PC were influenced by clinical decline, poor treatment response, or unmanageable symptoms. For example, Berry et al. (2017) reported that a patient with liver impairment was transferred to PC following repeated alcohol intoxication. Similarly, Irwin et al. (2005) described transfers to PC due to cancer progression, including cases with metastases and difficult-to-manage symptoms such as pain and depression. More recently, Berry (2023) noted that patients are referred to PC when their condition does not improve, with decisions influenced by both patients/family needs and healthcare resource considerations.

Finally, regarding the discussion of care ceilings or limits with patients, Berry et al. (2017) reported that the medical team suggested to a patient’s mother that her daughter’s care should transition to PC, employing honesty. The mother was asked on multiple occasions to say her goodbyes to her daughter because they anticipated her passing. Similarly, in his other study, Berry (2023) underscored the importance of approaching these discussions with gentleness and sensitivity while maintaining honesty and clarity. He also mentioned that with their first patient, the do-not-resuscitate decision and limits of care were discussed and agreed upon.

Discussion

This scoping review mapped existing literature on the experience of individuals with AUD receiving PC, highlighting clinical and psychosocial factors relevant to SUD management at EOL. Research within the field of AUD patients in PC or EOL services is minimal. This review encompassed the types of research articles on the subject, methodologies employed, patients’ overall experiences encompassing alcohol intake, beliefs, desires, decisions, emotions, symptom severity, treatment approaches, ceilings of care, transitions to PC, and various recommendations and challenges highlighted by the authors.

Our analysis led us to the following conclusions. First, there is a lack of studies on the subject, which is consistent with MacCormac’s review (2017). There is limited direct reporting of patient perspectives, with an overrepresentation of healthcare professional accounts. The overrepresentation of healthcare professional perspectives, rather than direct patient narratives, limits our understanding of patient autonomy, values, and substance-related choices in EOL settings, which is a critical area for future addiction-focused clinical research. As illustrated in the results section, most studies had under five participants, while only three quantitative studies included sixty or more participants (Mercadante et al., 2020, Parsons et al., 2008, Webber and Davies, 2012).

Although Parsons et al. (2008) involved a relatively large cohort, they focused on patients with AUD, identified through the CAGE screening tool, and did not report on the presence of OUD or other SUDs. While the study explored opioid prescribing patterns in relation to symptom severity, it did not include diagnostic data on OUD or other SUDs. As a result, the study’s findings on opioid use, such as higher rates and doses among CAGE-positive patients, must be interpreted in the context of symptom severity rather than substance use comorbidity.

Given the clinical overlap and complexity often observed between AUD and other SUDs, including OUD, the lack of OUD screening represents a limitation in both the study’s reporting and the interpretation of its findings. The potential presence of undiagnosed or unreported comorbid OUD may also contribute to clinicians’ hesitancy to initiate or escalate opioid therapy, as concerns about misuse and addiction risk often shape pain management decisions at EOL. These considerations highlight the importance of comprehensive diagnostic assessment and balanced, evidence-informed opioid prescribing in PC settings. The interaction between AUD, symptom expression, and opioid prescribing may be influenced by broader issues such as stigma, addiction-related bias, and clinical discomfort, all of which can further complicate pain management in this population.

However, in these larger-scale studies (Mercadante et al., 2020, Parsons et al., 2008, Webber and Davies, 2012), in-depth qualitative data from patients were lacking. Potential explanations for this could include the vulnerability of this patient population, their difficult access, and reluctance or inability to participate in studies due to various health factors or negative past experiences with healthcare workers, and being afraid of stigmatization.

Second, patients with AUD in PC face substantial symptom severity, including the difficult management of withdrawal symptoms, which is also concordant with MacCormac’s review (2017). Some of the studies in this present review discuss reasons for the high symptom severity, including inadequate identification and management of withdrawal symptoms, physicians’ hesitance to prescribe certain medications due to addiction concerns, and challenges in distinguishing between symptoms caused by AUD and those from other health conditions (Irwin et al., 2005, McCorquodale et al., 1993, Witham, 2023). The combined challenges of complex symptom presentation, frequent underdetection of AUD, and poor coordination between addiction treatment and PC approaches highlight a significant gap in care continuity, which is at heart of efforts to improve clinical approaches for SUD (Berry, 2023, MacCormac, 2017, Witham, 2023). Moreover, other published articles may give possible reasons for the challenges associations with managing symptoms in patients with AUD, as they report that symptom management is complicated by medication metabolism influenced by organ function, cross-tolerance, and its interactions with alcohol (MacCormac, 2017, Mundt-Leach, 2016). Meanwhile, regarding differences in symptom severity between AUD patients and control groups, Mercadante’s (2020) and Webber and Davies’s (2012) findings show contradicting results, as few significant disparities were reported.

Third, there is a lack of detection of AUD by healthcare professionals and a lack of reporting of details regarding the alcohol use of patients in scientific articles on the matter. Only half of the studies reported some information regarding the alcohol intake of their patients, other than the fact that these patients had alcohol-related issues (Berry, 2023, Irwin et al., 2005, McCorquodale et al., 1993, Witham, 2023). In addition, for the studies that reported on choices of use at the EOL, most of their patients consumed alcohol until the last few days or weeks of their lives, which highlights the need for harm reduction and other low-threshold approaches (Berry, 2023, Irwin et al., 2005, Witham, 2023). This gap in documentation and detection aligns with the broader literature on the underdiagnosis of AUD in medical settings (MacCormac, 2017), underscoring the need for routine screening and integration of AUD assessments in PC protocols. Finally, possible reasons for this issue could include insufficient knowledge of AUD signs and symptoms, how to identify and intervene with potential signs, and lack of awareness regarding how AUD detection and consideration can affect symptom management and the patient’s care plan.

These findings suggest that while the treatments themselves may align with standard PC protocols, the planning and delivery of EOL care for patients with AUD often appear inadequate. Several studies described late or crisis-driven transitions to PC, with patients frequently transferred only in the final days or weeks of life (Berry, 2023, Irwin et al., 2005; McCorquodale et al., 2993). EOL decisions were largely clinically initiated, with little evidence of early, shared decision-making or advanced care planning. In terms of symptom management, although standard interventions were often used, some studies noted clinical hesitancy to adequately address distress or withdrawal due to concerns about addiction, stigma, or lack of training (Irwin et al., 2005, Witham, 2023). These patterns highlight structural and attitudinal barriers to optimal EOL care, reinforcing the need to integrate addiction-informed approaches within PC services.

Additionally, a fourth conclusion pertains to the insufficient amount of information that is available in the current scientific literature regarding the transfer of AUD patients to PC and the processes involved in decision-making and discussions. Of the two studies that did, the authors mentioned that doctors initiated the discussion (Berry, 2023, Berry et al., 2017). This may reflect both patient-related factors, such as avoidance of death-related discussions, and systemic dynamics, including physician-led decision-making and limited shared decision-making models in PC. This dynamic may also reflect patients’ trust in physicians to make appropriate medical decisions on their behalf, including the timing of the transition to PC. Some patients reportedly consented to the decision to receive PC services, while others expressed a strong desire to continue with treatments and procedures, such as an organ transplant, in hopes of recovery (Irwin et al., 2005). As a result, the decision to transition to PC was sometimes not mutually agreed upon or planned in collaboration with the patients while they were still conscious (Berry, 2023, Berry et al., 2017).

Concerning the delays in transferring patients to PC, most individuals died within days or weeks after transfer, though a minority survived up to two months. This suggests that earlier PC integration could potentially reduce unnecessary suffering and avoid useless procedures. Several factors may contribute to delayed transfer. First, patients may struggle to accept their terminal prognosis, often maintaining goals or unresolved issues they wish to address before death. Additionally, patients may perceive PC as “a place of no return,” reinforcing avoidance of EOL discussions (Witham, 2023). Second, the prevailing medical culture prioritizes life preservation and prolongation, which can delay PC referral. Third, healthcare professionals outside PC services may lack confidence or training in navigating EOL decision-making, further postponing transitions (Berry, 2023, Berry et al., 2017, Witham, 2023). These factors highlight the critical need for enhanced provider education and support; a theme echoed throughout the literature.

Beyond patient experiences, the reviewed studies identify key challenges such as the underdetection of AUD in PC and EOL contexts (Irwin et al., 2005, McCorquodale et al., 1993, Parsons et al., 2008), and the essential role of multidisciplinary collaboration in improving care (Berry, 2023, Berry et al., 2017, Witham, 2023). Challenges in symptom management and inadequate EOL planning were also recurrent concerns (McCorquodale et al., 1993, Witham, 2023). Several authors express the need for targeted training programs to equip healthcare providers across disciplines with the skills necessary to address both AUD and PC needs effectively (Berry, 2023, Berry et al., 2017, MacCormac, 2017, Witham, 2023). Finally, delays in EOL planning may stem from healthcare providers’ discomfort with death, a strong focus on life-prolonging treatments, knowledge gaps about AUD in PC, and patient reluctance to confront EOL realities.

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review has several key strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first scoping review to comprehensively synthesize what is known about the experiences of AUD patients in PC. It offers a broader understanding of patients’ symptoms, beliefs, and care experiences, including transitions to PC and the challenges related to care planning. By addressing a significant gap in the literature, this review contributes to a limited evidence base and provides useful insights to help guide future research, clinical training, and policy development.

Regarding the limitations of this study, first, the search strategy included only articles published in English, which might have excluded pertinent studies. Second, there is the possibility that we may have missed relevant studies due to the exclusion of gray literature and language limitations. Similarly, our limitation to five databases could have excluded studies on our research subject and consequently affected the accuracy of the findings reported in this review. Finally, five of the eight studies included were case reports, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first scoping review to comprehensively synthesize the experiences of individuals with AUD receiving PC and EOL services. It is also the first review to detail participants’ different symptoms, desires, beliefs, and decisions, as well as their experiences regarding ceilings of care, transitions to PC, and inadequate end-of-life planning and training for healthcare professionals in managing AUD cases. By summarizing the limited available evidence, this review adds to the small amount of literature on the subject. However, given the scarcity of research and the predominance of case reports among included studies, the findings of this review should be interpreted with caution. There is a clear need for further studies involving multiple participants living with AUD and direct reporting of their perceptions and experiences within PC services. The findings suggest potential areas for development in clinical practice, including structured AUD training programs for providers in PC settings, systematic screening protocols, and earlier, collaborative EOL planning to improve the quality of care and reduce patient suffering.
