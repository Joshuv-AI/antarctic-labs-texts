Psychiatric and Psychological Approach in Patients with Atopic Dermatitis, Hidradenitis Suppurativa and Psoriasis: A Propensity-Matched Real-World Cohort Study
Authors: Marta Szepietowska, Piotr K Krajewski, Przemyslaw Pacan, Anna Wojas-Pelc, Jacek C Szepietowski, Andrzej K Jaworek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic inflammatory dermatoses—atopic dermatitis (AD), hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), and psoriasis (PsO)—are linked to elevated rates of psychosocial consequences. Beyond controlling cutaneous inflammation, contemporary care should address psychosocial well-being, yet real-world evidence on psychiatric and psychological service use in these diseases is limited. To quantify the prevalence and risk of psychiatric evaluation, psychotherapy, and psychiatric pharmacotherapy among adults with AD, HS, and PsO versus matched controls, and to examine sex-specific differences.MethodsUsing deidentified electronic health records from the international TriNetX network, we identified adults newly diagnosed with AD, HS, or PsO and matched each disease cohort 1:1 to control patients without the index dermatosis or prior mental disorders. Outcomes over 5 years were psychiatric evaluation, psychotherapy, and psychiatric medications (sedatives/hypnotics, anticonvulsants, antidepressants, antipsychotics). Risks were estimated with generalized linear models (risk ratios (RRs)) and Cox models (hazard ratios (HRs)), with sex-stratified analyses.ResultsAfter matching, the study included 447,821 AD pairs, 123,443 HS pairs, and 443,598 PsO pairs with balanced demographics and comorbidities. All dermatoses showed higher utilization than controls. In AD, psychotherapy displayed the largest relative increase (RR≈3.0); psychiatric evaluation and treatment were also elevated (RRs ≈2.6 and ≈1.35). In HS, risks were higher for psychiatric evaluation, psychotherapy, and especially psychiatric pharmacotherapy (RR ≈1.75). In PsO, psychiatric evaluation, psychotherapy, and psychiatric treatment were all increased (RRs ≈1.9, ≈1.9, and ≈1.34, respectively). Sex-stratified analyses showed women with AD had greater risks across all outcomes; in HS, only psychiatric pharmacotherapy was higher in women; in PsO, men had slightly higher pharmacotherapy use.ConclusionsAdults with AD, HS, and PsO have substantially greater psychiatric and psychological care utilization than matched controls, with meaningful sex differences. Findings support integrated, multidisciplinary management that proactively addresses mental health alongside dermatologic treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01703-3.Keywords: Chronic dermatoses, Atopic dermatitis, Hidradenitis suppurativa, Psoriasis, Psychological intervention, Psychiatric treatment

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Chronic inflammatory dermatoses such as atopic dermatitis, hidradenitis suppurativa, and psoriasis are known to be associated with psychiatric comorbidities, yet real-world data on the actual utilization of psychiatric and psychological care in these populations are scarce.Previous studies have mainly focused on prevalence of mental disorders rather than on health care utilization patterns.Large, multinational, real-world evidence on the utilization of psychiatric and psychological care comparing different chronic dermatoses using matched control populations is lacking.What was learned from the study?Adults with atopic dermatitis, hidradenitis suppurativa, and psoriasis show a significantly higher utilization of psychiatric evaluation, psychotherapy, and psychiatric pharmacotherapy compared with matched controls from the general population.The magnitude and pattern of psychiatric and psychological care differ across diseases, with psychotherapy being particularly increased in patients with atopic dermatitis and pharmacological treatment most pronounced in patients with hidradenitis suppurativa.Meaningful sex differences were identified, with women generally showing higher utilization of psychiatric and psychological care in atopic dermatitis, while disease-specific patterns were observed in hidradenitis suppurativa and psoriasisThese findings support the need for integrated, multidisciplinary management strategies that proactively address mental health alongside dermatologic treatment in chronic inflammatory skin diseases.

Introduction

Numerous chronic inflammatory dermatoses are associated with an elevated prevalence of psychiatric comorbidities [1–5]. Several studies have demonstrated that patients with atopic dermatitis (AD), hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), and psoriasis (PsO) experience substantial psychiatric sequelae, particularly depression and anxiety, that contribute markedly to their overall psychosocial burden [5–9]. Contemporary treatment goals for chronic skin diseases should therefore include not only effective control of cutaneous inflammation to alleviate signs and symptoms but also a holistic strategy aimed at restoring psychosocial well-being [10]. Many patients with chronic inflammatory dermatoses require psychiatric consultation and psychological and/or psychiatric interventions, and the benefits of such approaches have been documented by multiple groups [11–17].

To our knowledge, real-world data on the prevalence and determinants of these interventions in patients with chronic inflammatory dermatoses are lacking or very limited. To address this gap, we conducted a large, propensity score–matched cohort study to quantify the incidence and risk of receiving psychiatric and psychological care among patients with AD, HS, and PsO, including an exploration of potential sex differences.

Methods

Study Design and Ethical Considerations

This retrospective cohort analysis utilized data from the TriNetX Analytics network, an international federated research platform that compiles deidentified electronic health records (EHRs) from more than 180 healthcare institutions worldwide. The TriNetX database provides continuously updated, real-world clinical information from both inpatient and outpatient encounters, encompassing demographic characteristics, diagnostic codes, medical procedures, prescriptions, and healthcare utilization patterns. This comprehensive dataset enables longitudinal investigations across diverse and heterogeneous patient populations.

As all information accessed for this study was deidentified within the TriNetX platform, institutional review board (IRB) approval and informed consent were not required. The access for the TriNetX platform was obtained by Dr. Piotr K. Krajewski who works as a guest scientist at the University of Lübeck. No additional permissions were required. The study adheres to ethical guidelines for the secondary use of anonymized patient data and complies with all relevant data protection and research integrity standards. All patient data in the TriNetX database is certified as anonymized under Section §164.514(a) and §164.514(b)(1) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule and, when relevant, the GDPR of the European Union.

Study Population

The study included three distinct cohorts of adults aged 18 years and older, each newly diagnosed with one of the following inflammatory skin disorders: atopic dermatitis (AD), hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), and psoriasis (PsO). All index diagnoses were recorded for the first time between 24 May 2015 and 24 May 24, 2025. The date of the first diagnosis was designated as the index date for each participant. Patients with AD were identified using the ICD-10-CM code L20, those with HS using L73.2, and individuals with PsO using L40. To ensure a clean baseline, participants with any prior record of mental, behavioral, or neurodevelopmental disorders (ICD-10-CM codes F01–F99) before the index event were excluded.

Comparator groups (Non-AD, Non-HS, and Non-PsO) consisted of adults who underwent a routine general health examination, identified by the ICD-10-CM code Z00, without any prior diagnosis of the corresponding dermatologic condition or mental health disorder. The index event for these individuals was the date of the general examination. To reduce potential confounding by psychiatric comorbidity, all participants with any documented mental, behavioral, or neurodevelopmental condition before the index date were excluded from both disease and control cohorts. A minimum baseline period of 6 months was required for both cohorts. Subjects were excluded from the analysis if they had: incomplete or implausible demographic information—such as missing or out-of-range values for age or sex—or if they had missing or invalid dates pertaining to the index event or follow-up period.

To further control for confounding, 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) was performed separately for each disease cohort (AD versus Non-AD, HS versus Non-HS, and PsO versus Non-PsO). Propensity scores were calculated using logistic regression models that incorporated baseline characteristics, including age at the index date, sex, and a comprehensive set of significant comorbidities defined by ICD-10 categories. These included: migraine (G43), fibromyalgia (M79.7), low back pain (M54.5), asthma (J45), vasomotor and allergic rhinitis (J30), other chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases (J44), noninfective enteritis and colitis (K50–K52), other rheumatoid arthritis (M06), diabetes mellitus (E08–E13), overweight and obesity (E66), other hypothyroidism (E03), gastroesophageal reflux disease (K21), irritable bowel syndrome (K58), chronic kidney disease (N18), neoplasms (C00–D49), chronic ischemic heart disease (I25), and heart failure (I50). Socioeconomic and psychosocial determinants (Z55–Z65) were also included as covariates.

Matching was conducted using a greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm without replacement, applying a caliper width of 0.1 pooled standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score, in accordance with established methodological recommendations. The success of the matching procedure was evaluated by calculating standardized mean differences (SMDs) for all covariates, with values close to zero indicating optimal balance between the matched groups.

Outcome Measures

All individuals were followed longitudinally for up to 5 years from their respective index dates. The primary outcomes were psychiatric evaluation (CPT codes: 90791 and 90792) psychotherapy (CPT codes: 90834 90832 90837 90833 90836 90839 90838 90847 90846 90840 90849 90853), and psychiatric treatment (CN300—sedatives/hypnotics, CN400—anticonvulsants, CN600—antidepressants, CN700—antipsychotics). Psychiatric pharmacotherapy was defined according to medication classes recorded in the database. Because these drug categories may also be prescribed for nonpsychiatric indications (e.g., pain management, sleep disturbances, or neurological conditions), particularly in patients with HS, this outcome should be interpreted as psychiatric-related pharmacotherapy rather than as a direct proxy for psychiatric diagnosis. All outcomes were classified as incident if they occurred for the first time during the 5-year follow-up period. In addition, the number of instances for each outcome was calculated across all studied groups and compared within each patient cohort.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were described using means with standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for skewed data, and counts with percentages for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous measures, and the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical data, as appropriate. The quality of propensity score matching was assessed by examining standardized mean differences (SMDs) across all covariates before and after matching, with values below 0.1 considered indicative of satisfactory balance.

Risk ratios (RRs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed for all outcomes within the matched datasets. These estimates were derived using generalized linear models with a log-link function. To evaluate the association between each inflammatory skin disease (AD, HS, and PsO) and the subsequent development of psychiatric disorders, Cox proportional hazards regression models were fitted to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for time-to-event data. The follow-up period was defined as the interval between the index date and the first recorded diagnosis of any psychiatric condition. The proportional hazards assumption was verified through the inspection of Schoenfeld residuals. In addition, sex-stratified analyses were conducted to explore potential gender-specific differences in psychiatric morbidity and healthcare utilization among individuals with inflammatory skin diseases. For each participant, follow-up started the day after the index event and continued for up to 5 years. Participants who did not develop a psychiatric disorder during follow-up were right censored at the date of their last clinical record or at the end of the 5-year observation period. Observation could have ended earlier if one of these events occurred: (1) the first documented outcome, (2) death, or (3) loss to follow-up (last available EHR encounter). This censoring approach was used for all time-to-event analyses.

All statistical models underwent assessment for goodness of fit. Patterns of missing data were examined, and records with substantial data incompleteness were excluded from analyses. For each psychiatric outcome, incidence proportions (number of new cases per at-risk population) were reported along with 95% CIs. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Groups’ Characteristics

In the initial dataset, adults newly diagnosed with AD, HS, and psoriasis PsO and their respective control groups were identified. Specifically, 510,836 individuals with AD were compared against 2,115,785 adults without AD; 127,725 individuals with HS were compared with 2,183,933 adults without HS; and 468,959 individuals with PsO were compared with 2,143,225 adults without PsO. Following 1:1 PSM performed separately within each disease cohort, well-balanced study populations were obtained, consisting of 447,821 matched pairs in the AD cohort, 123,443 pairs in the HS cohort, and 443,598 pairs in the PsO cohort. Following matching, each patient cohort (AD, HS, and PsO) and its respective control group exhibited well-balanced distributions of age, sex, and key aforementioned comorbidities. Comprehensive demographic and clinical data are summarized in Supplementary Tables 1–3 respectively.

Differences in the Incidence and Risk of Psychiatric and Psychological Approach

Within the AD cohort, the cumulative incidence of psychiatric evaluation was significantly elevated compared with non-AD subjects (0.9% versus 0.4%, p < 0.001), corresponding to a relative risk (RR) of 2.629. Similarly, a higher proportion of patients with AD underwent both psychotherapy (0.8% versus 0.3%, RR = 3.044) and psychiatric treatment (15.6% versus 11.6%, RR = 1.345) compared with non-AD individuals (Table 1). In addition, the mean number of instances per patient for psychiatric evaluation, psychotherapy, and psychiatric treatment was significantly greater in the AD cohort relative to the non-AD population (p < 0.001 for all measures), as shown in Fig. 1A.

Sex-Stratified Analysis

Sex-stratified analysis was conducted on the basis of the following number of PSM pairs (female patients versus male patients) for each chronic dermatosis: 182,758 for AD, 30,074 for HS, and 196,723 for PsO. This analysis revealed significant differences in measured outcomes between female patients and male patients suffering from various chronic skin diseases.

In the cohort of patients with AD, female patients demonstrated a significantly higher risk of undergoing psychiatric evaluation (RR = 1.330; HR = 1.267), engaging in psychotherapy (RR = 1.370; HR = 1.304), and receiving psychiatric treatment (RR = 1.190; HR = 1.154) as compared with male patients (Table 4). The total number of prescribed psychiatric treatment episodes was also statistically greater in female patients (mean ± SD: 4.03 ± 7.16) than in male patients (3.83 ± 7.53), with a p-value of 0.004. However, no significant gender differences were observed in the frequency of psychiatric evaluations and psychotherapy sessions (Fig. 2A).

Discussion

Although AD, HS, and PsO are chronic, recurrent inflammatory skin diseases, they present with distinct clinical manifestation [18–20]. AD is a common condition affecting both children and adults. Its prevalence has been estimated at 1–2% in adults, being much more common in children [21, 22]. A recent meta-analysis of epidemiological studies in HS reported a global prevalence of approximately 1% [23, 24]. Psoriasis is likewise common, affecting about 1–3% of the general population [25]. Adult AD typically presents with erythematous lesions on the face and neck, often involving flexural areas and the hands [18]. The clinical picture of HS includes abscesses and inflammatory nodules, with sinus tracts/tunnels and scarring predominantly located in intertriginous skin [19]. Typical psoriasis is characterized by erythematous, scaly plaques most frequently found on the scalp, elbows, knees, and sacrum [20]. These dermatoses are associated with prominent subjective symptoms, notably itch and cutaneous pain. Itch, the most common subjective symptom in dermatology, is reported by nearly all patients with AD [26]. In HS, more than 40–50% of patients report itching [27, 28]. Psoriatic itch has long been underrecognized; current evidence indicates that approximately 80% of individuals with psoriasis experience pruritus of varying severity [29–31]. Cutaneous pain is reported by over 80% of patients with HS [32, 33] and is also present in a substantial proportion of those with AD and PsO [34, 35].

The signs and symptoms of these chronic, recurrent conditions contribute to a considerable psychosocial burden [2, 3, 36–39]. Health-related quality of life is markedly impaired; patients are at increased risk of stigmatization, and rates of depression, anxiety, and suicidality are elevated [5, 40]. A holistic approach to managing chronic dermatoses is therefore essential throughout the therapeutic process. This is clearly emphasized in actual guidelines for AD, HS, and PsO [10, 41–43].

In our study, we clearly demonstrated that psychiatric evaluations, psychiatric treatment visits, and psychotherapy were significantly more frequently conducted in all studied patients with chronic inflammatory skin conditions, namely AD, HS, PsO, compared with their matched controls. Although we showed that utilization of psychological and psychiatric care is significantly higher in those subjects, it is worth mentioning that the observed incidence, especially psychiatric evaluations and psychotherapy sessions, is rather low. In our opinion this, at least partly, contrasts with the vast psychosocial burden of the studied conditions and could be of importance for physicians in their daily clinical practice. We do believe that the close cooperation between dermatologists and psychologists/psychiatrists could enhance final therapeutic outcome.

A study conducted in Korea also reported a higher risk of psychiatric outpatient visits in patients with a history of AD (HR = 3.70), which was even higher than the HR observed in our AD group [44]. Moreover, the authors suggested that the HR for psychiatric visits increased with the complexity of AD treatment. Specifically, patients who received only topical treatment had an HR of 4.50, those who received a combination of topical treatment and systemic steroids had an HR of 4.88, and patients treated with topical therapy, systemic steroids, and a systemic calcineurin inhibitor had the highest risk (HR = 9.56) [44].

Although direct comparison between the two studies is not possible due to differences in methodology and the ethnic composition of the study populations, the increased incidence of psychiatric visits among patients with AD appears to be a global phenomenon. It is observed both in adults and in children with AD. For instance, a Danish study of children diagnosed with AD in hospital settings found an increased risk of psychiatric treatments, particularly the use of antidepressants (HR = 1.19), anxiolytics (HR = 1.72), and centrally acting sympathomimetics (HR = 1.29). In addition, these subjects were at increased risk for both psychiatric and psychological consultations (HR = 1.33 and HR = 1.25, respectively) [45].

The Korean researchers suggested that the observed increase in psychiatric interventions among patients with AD may be attributed to frequent sleep disturbances and the negative cosmetic effects of the disease, which heavily affect patients’ self-esteem. It is also plausible that shared pathogenic mechanisms underlie both chronic skin inflammation and psychiatric disorders [44]. Furthermore, psychiatric comorbidities in patients with moderate-to-severe AD have been shown to markedly contribute to the increased economic burden on healthcare systems [46].

Data regarding psychiatric and psychological care in patients with PsO and HS are relatively scarce. One study, based on a cohort of nearly 10,000 individuals with PsO, found that 66.2% had at least one visit with a diagnostic code for mental illness, significantly higher than the 56.0% observed in the control group [47]. Specifically, 36.5% of patients with PsO were diagnosed with anxiety, compared with 28.95% in controls. Similar significant differences were documented for depression (37.04% versus 30.19%) and adjustment disorder (6.89% versus 5.48%) [47]. The increased number of psychiatric visits may lead to greater overall healthcare utilization. Shalom et al. [48] found that, in addition to an increased number of annual dermatology clinic visits, individuals with PsO also had a significantly higher number of emergency room visits.

Although the psychological burden in patients with HS, including increased indirect self-destructiveness, has been well-documented [7, 8, 19, 49], we were unable to find clear studies in the literature reporting the number of psychiatric and psychological visits in this patient population. Interestingly, in our study, HS appeared to present a somewhat different profile compared with AD and PsO. While patients with HS, similar to those with AD and PsO, had an increased risk for all three analyzed aspects of psychiatric and psychological care, only the number of prescribed psychiatric treatments per patient was significantly elevated in patients suffering from HS.

This profile aligns with established HS care pathways. Our definition of pharmacotherapy includes sedatives, hypnotics, and anticonvulsants—medications very often employed in HS management to address sleep disturbances and pain, rather than solely for primary psychiatric reasons [32, 33]. In addition, medication data are captured more consistently across different sites than CPT codes for psychotherapy, which suggests prescriptions are recorded more reliably than psychotherapy sessions. Furthermore, HS treatment typically involves surgeons, dermatologists, and primary care providers, who are more likely to initiate pharmacologic symptom relief rather than refer patients for psychotherapy. Collectively, these factors plausibly account for the observed increase in prescription counts without a corresponding rise in psychotherapy visit numbers. This pattern also reflects the significant pain and psychosocial burden associated with HS [8, 19, 32, 33, 49].

In the current study, we also attempted to analyze differences in outcomes between female patients and male patients. As reported in the results, some differences were found, suggesting that female patients with chronic inflammatory skin conditions are generally at a higher risk of receiving psychiatric and psychological care compared with male patients. Several authors, including our group, have previously reported that women with chronic dermatoses experience greater psychosocial suffering than men [6]. In addition, there is clear evidence that the psychosocial burden is influenced by disease severity [50–55]. Therefore, we believe that firm conclusions regarding sex differences can only be drawn when disease severity is considered as a key factor in such analyses. The observed sex-stratified differences should be interpreted with caution. These analyses are exploratory in nature and may reflect not only biological factors but also differences in healthcare-seeking behavior, prescribing practices, access to care, and sociocultural gender norms. Therefore, the observed patterns should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of causal sex-specific biological effects.

We underline several notable strengths alongside important limitations of our study. This work draws on a large, multinational, real-world dataset; implements extensive PMS across key comorbidity domains; and relies on prespecified outcome definitions that include both psychiatric and psychological approaches. To the best of our knowledge, such an analysis has not been conducted in relation to psychiatric and psychological care in any of the chronic inflammatory conditions to date. An important limitation of this study is the lack of standardized disease severity measures (e.g., EASI, PASI, or Hurley stage), which are not captured within the TriNetX database. Disease severity likely influences both psychiatric burden and healthcare utilization and therefore may represent a source of residual confounding. Similarly, use of new therapeutics, such as biologics, age of disease onset, disease duration, socioeconomic stressors, and healthcare utilization could not be fully accounted for. PMS balanced numerous demographic and clinical covariates; however, unmeasured confounding may still have influenced the observed associations, particularly in sex-stratified analyses. Consequently, sex-specific differences should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as hypothesis-generating. Moreover, the comparator cohorts were defined using individuals attending routine general health examinations. While this approach ensured a clear index event and baseline observation period, it may introduce a potential “healthy user” effect, as these individuals may differ from dermatology patients in healthcare-seeking behavior. In addition, generalizability may be limited to the healthcare systems represented in the database, potentially underrepresenting uninsured or marginalized populations.

Conclusions

We showed that individuals with studied chronic inflammatory skin diseases as AD, HS, and PsO exhibited a significantly higher cumulative incidence of psychiatric evaluation, psychotherapy, and psychiatric treatment, with relative risks of around twofold for psychiatric evaluation and psychotherapy, and a moderate increase for pharmacological treatment. Collectively, these findings underscore the strong association between chronic inflammatory skin diseases and psychiatric/psychological approaches, highlighting the necessity of integrated, multidisciplinary care addressing both dermatological and mental health aspects.
