Genomic Analysis of Ocular Pseudomonas aeruginosa Isolates: Insights From a Predominantly Asian, Multi-Regional Dataset
Authors: Hui Gao, Jiaqi Ma, Yan Gao, Zhirong Liu, Li Ge, Yuzhe Shen, Xian Wei, Lu Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study characterized the genomic features, population structure, and evolutionary mechanisms of antimicrobial resistance and virulence in ocular Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) isolates, with a primary focus on the predominantly represented Asian strains within an international context.MethodsA dataset of 102 human ocular P. aeruginosa whole-genome sequences, comprising 67 isolates from Asia and 35 from other international regions, was retrieved from the National Center of Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. Isolates were categorized by geographic origin and, where metadata were available, by clinical or environmental source. Systematic bioinformatic analyses were conducted to determine sequence types, phylogenetic relationships, mobile genetic elements, and pangenome functional characteristics.ResultsOcular isolates exhibited high genetic diversity with 60 distinct sequence types identified. Reflecting the dataset’s geographic composition, sequence type (ST)308 emerged as a dominant high-risk lineage in Asia, whereas the emerging ST1203 displayed the highest antimicrobial resistance gene burden. Multidrug-resistant clones carrying the blaNDM gene were detected, and their dissemination was significantly associated with IncR and IncFIA plasmids. A significant negative correlation was observed between the counts of resistance and virulence genes. Phylogenetic analysis indicated that, within the current collection, clonal clusters are geographically restricted, with no evidence of international transmission detected.ConclusionsOcular P. aeruginosa possesses complex evolutionary potential. The rise of high-risk lineages like ST1203 and critical resistance-carrying plasmids pose severe clinical challenges. Given the observed regional dominance of certain resistant clones, the evolutionary plasticity of these lineages warrants proactive cross-regional molecular surveillance to monitor the potential for future international dissemination.

Ocular infections represent a significant public health concern, manifesting in diverse clinical forms such as blepharitis, conjunctivitis, keratitis, dacryocystitis, and preseptal cellulitis.1 Notably, severe keratitis can progress to corneal melting and perforation, culminating in irreversible vision loss or even the necessity for evisceration.2,3 Whereas the etiology of ocular infections is multifactorial, bacteria remain the primary causative agents. In particular, Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) warrants intensive investigation as it is among the most prevalent pathogens in bacterial ocular infections worldwide.4–6 As a ubiquitous opportunistic pathogen, its environmental persistence poses a continuous threat to public health.

Gram-negative bacteria account for approximately 11% to 50% of ocular bacterial infections, with P. aeruginosa identified as the dominant pathogen, particularly in several developing nations.5 Its pathogenicity is closely linked to contact lens wear, likely due to the microenvironment between the lens and cornea that facilitates microbial colonization.5 Evidence suggests that P. aeruginosa can survive in reusable soap dispensers and various ophthalmic preparations (e.g. artificial tears and dry eye treatments), posing a latent infection risk to users, especially immunocompromised individuals.3 Previous studies have documented bacterial contamination, including P. aeruginosa, in refillable liquid soap dispensers and eye drop bottles,7 with concentrations in certain preservative-free ophthalmic solutions exceeding 106 CFU/mL.8

Currently, antibiotics remain the primary frontline therapy for bacterial ocular infections.2 However, the widespread use and potential misuse of antimicrobials have accelerated bacterial adaptive evolution, leading to the emergence of resistant strains that may impede the corneal healing process.9 Over recent decades, the escalation of antimicrobial resistance has emerged as a formidable challenge in managing ocular infections.1,10,11 For instance, a 25-year longitudinal surveillance of ocular isolates in Canada reported chloramphenicol resistance rates as high as 88.7% in P. aeruginosa, alongside an upward trend in moxifloxacin resistance.12 Concurrently, the Asian Corneal Society Infectious Keratitis Study (ACSIKS) reported that multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains accounted for 40.68% of ocular P. aeruginosa isolates in India.13 The proliferation of such MDR phenotypes is increasingly attributed to the global expansion of “high-risk clones.” These lineages—exemplified by canonical clones such as sequence type (ST)235, ST111, and ST175—are formally defined by their widespread geographic distribution, an extensive-drug resistance (XDR) profile typically involving the acquisition of carbapenemases, and enhanced biological fitness that allows them to outcompete sporadic strains in clinical settings.14,15 Although these established lineages are well-characterized in systemic infections, their specific role and evolutionary dynamics within the ocular microenvironment remain inadequately explored.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) has become an indispensable tool in pathogen epidemiology.16 Compared to conventional antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST), WGS enables rapid identification of resistance genes and provides high-resolution characterization of virulence factors, plasmid replicons, and overall genetic diversity. Furthermore, bioinformatic approaches, such as phylogenetic, Bayesian, and pangenome analyses, facilitate a comprehensive evaluation of population structure, genetic variation, and niche-specific evolution.17,18 Although large-scale regional surveillance based on conventional AST has been conducted in specific areas, such as Canada and Asia,13 these phenotypic studies offer limited insights into the global genomic architecture and evolutionary trajectories of ocular P. aeruginosa across different geographic regions. Regional snapshots fail to capture the complex interplay between locally adapted clones and the potential for cross-continental dissemination of high-risk lineages. Furthermore, the relative contribution of clinical versus environmental sources to the ocular resistome remains poorly defined in a genomic context. To bridge this knowledge gap, the present study leverages currently available international genomic data to systematically analyze the multilocus sequence typing (MLST) distribution, antimicrobial resistome, and virulome. By integrating single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based phylogenetics with pangenome analysis, we aim to elucidate both the population structure across major regions—with a primary focus on the dominant Asian isolates—and the evolutionary mechanisms governing these critical ocular pathogens.

Materials and Methods

Search Strategy and Isolate Selection

The National Center of Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Pathogen Detection database was queried (last accessed on August 8, 2025) using the keywords “eyes,” “keratitis,” “ocular,” and “Pseudomonas aeruginosa.” A total of 102 P. aeruginosa genomes were retrieved and curated based on the availability of definitive geographic metadata (Supplementary Table S1). This resulted in a final dataset comprising 102 high-quality ocular P. aeruginosa assemblies.

Genome Annotation and Characterization

MLST was performed using mlst version 2.23.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/mlst). Antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs), virulence factors, and plasmids were identified via ABRicate version 1.0.1 (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) by querying the ResFinder, VFDB, and PlasmidFinder databases, respectively, applying a minimum identity and coverage threshold of 80%. This threshold was selected as a widely accepted standard in comparative genomics to provide a robust balance between the sensitivity required to detect sequence variants and the specificity needed to exclude distant homologs.19,20 Genome annotation was conducted using Prokka version 1.14.6.21 A minimum spanning tree based on the MLST profiles of 102 representative isolates was constructed using GrapeTree version 1.5.0.22 Alpha diversity of STs was calculated using the vegan R software package version 2.6–4. A t-SNE plot based on the pangenome gene presence/absence matrix was generated using the Rtsne R software package version 0.16.

Phylogenetic Analysis

P. aeruginosa PAO1 (GenBank: NC_002516.2) served as the reference genome. Core-genome alignment and variant calling were performed using Snippy version 4.6.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy). Recombination regions were identified and masked with Gubbins version 2.4.1.23 A maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was inferred using FastTree version 2.1.1124 and visualized via Chiplot.25 Pairwise core-genome SNP distances were calculated with SNP-dists version 1.2.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snp-dists), isolates with <10 SNPs were defined as belonging to the same clone. The population structure was further delineated using RhierBAPS version 1.1.4 (https://github.com/gtonkinhill/rhierbaps).

Pan-Genome Analysis

Pan-genome analysis was executed with Panaroo version 1.5.2 (https://github.com/gtonkinhill/panaroo). Core genes were defined using a threshold of “99% ≤ strains ≤ 100%” under the “clean-mode strict” option, and core alignments were generated using the MAFFT algorithm. Both core and accessory genes were functionally annotated using Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) categories via eggNOG-mapper version 2.1.12 (https://github.com/eggnogdb/eggnog-mapper).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed in R software version 4.5.2 (http://www.r-project.org). Sampling maps and Sankey diagrams were utilized to illustrate the spatiotemporal distribution of isolates. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to evaluate differences in ARG, plasmid replicon, and virulence gene counts across different sampling periods and geographic locations. Hierarchical clustering heatmaps were generated based on these genomic features. Correlations among ARGs, virulence genes, and plasmids were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation. Differences between core and accessory genomes were evaluated via Pearson's chi-square test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Metadata Characteristics and Source Distribution

A total of 102 ocular P. aeruginosa isolates were included in this study. To address the clinical relevance of the dataset, we categorized the isolates based on their specific isolation sources (see Supplementary Table S1). The majority of the isolates (n = 56, 54.90%) were derived from direct clinical infections, including corneal scrapings from patients with keratitis, vitreous biopsies from endophthalmitis cases, and conjunctival swabs. A smaller subset (n = 3, 2.94%) was associated with ophthalmic devices or environmental sources, such as contact lens solutions and bandage contact lenses. The remaining isolates (n = 43, 42.16%) were identified as ocular-associated specimens without further sub-classification in the NCBI metadata. This diverse composition allows for an exploratory analysis of P. aeruginosa across various niches within the ocular environment.

Analysis Based on Multilocus Sequence Typing

MLST analysis identified 60 distinct sequence types (STs) among the 102 isolates (Figs. 1A, 2A). Asian isolates exhibited the highest allelic diversity with 42 STs, an observation that is likely correlated with the greater sampling depth of the Asian cohort, followed by Oceania (18 STs), Europe (6 STs), and North America (2 STs). Regionally, ST308 was the most frequent lineage in Asia, whereas ST1203 predominated within the limited North American cohort available in this dataset (Fig. 1B). Overall, the most frequent lineages were ST308 (n = 10), ST1203 (n = 10), ST3755 (n = 7), ST316 (n = 3), ST664 (n = 3), and ST2012 (n = 3).

To elucidate the genetic background and diversity of these ocular isolates, genomic variations were further analyzed in relation to geographic origin and ST profiles. The genetic composition of the isolates displayed prominent ST-specific clustering (Fig. 2B), while showing no significant correlation with geographic sources (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, the relationship between ST diversity (Shannon index) and the prevalence of ARGs, plasmids, and virulence genes was assessed. Significant negative correlations were observed between ST diversity and the counts of both plasmids and virulence genes (P < 0.05). This suggests that the enrichment of these genetic elements may be associated with a reduction in overall ST diversity, potentially reflecting the clonal expansion of specific high-risk lineages characterized by specialized genetic cargo (Figs. 2D–F).

Antimicrobial Resistance Genes and Plasmids

Genome-wide analysis identified 66 distinct ARGs across 11 antibiotic classes (Fig. 3A). The most diverse categories were beta-lactams (n = 21) and aminoglycosides (n = 19). The most prevalent ARGs included aph(3′)-IIb (100.00%), blaPAO (100.00%), catB7 (100.00%), and fosA (99.02%), whereas the prevalence of all other ARGs remained below 50%. Notably, the carbapenemase genes blaNDM-1 and blaNDM-5 were detected in Asian isolates (one ST1203 and one ST357) and a European isolate (ST234), respectively.

(B) The total number of ARGs in P. aeruginosa across different time intervals. (C) Prevalence of ARGs in P. aeruginosa across different time intervals. (D) The total number of ARGs in P. aeruginosa across different geographic locations. (E) Prevalence of ARGs in P. aeruginosa across different geographic locations.

Subsequent analysis examined the distribution of ARG abundance by sampling time and geographic origin. Isolates from 1992 to 1999 and 2020 to 2024 exhibited significantly higher ARG counts compared with those from 2005 to 2009 (P < 0.05; Fig. 3B). A clear longitudinal expansion in ARG diversity was observed: the number of ARG types increased from 13 in 1992 to 1999 to 37 and 36 in 2010 to 2014 and 2015 to 2019, respectively, peaking at 52 types in the 2020 to 2024 cohort (Fig. 3C). North American isolates harbored a significantly higher ARG burden than those from Asia, Europe, and Oceania (P < 0.05; Fig. 3D); this pattern, however, is largely attributable to the predominance of the ST1203 lineage within that small cohort rather than representing a generalized regional resistance profile. Specifically, the prevalence of aph(3″)-Ib, crpP, ant(3″)-Ia, aph(6)-Id, blaGES-9, blaOXA-10, blaOXA-395, bla VIM-2, dfrA5, ere(A), floR, rmtF, sul1, and tet(G) was markedly higher in North American isolates than in the other three regions (Fig. 3E). This enrichment is primarily attributed to the high frequency of these determinants within the ST1203 lineage, which predominates in the North American cohort of this dataset.

Plasmid analysis identified 11 distinct replicons across the 102 isolates. The majority of strains (74.51%) carried no plasmids, whereas 22.55% harbored one, 1.96% harbored 2, and 0.98% carried 4 plasmids (Supplementary Table S2). IncQ2 was the most prevalent replicon (15.69%). Although no significant temporal differences in plasmid counts were observed (P > 0.05; Figs. 4A, 4B), Asian isolates exhibited significantly greater plasmid abundance and diversity than those from North America and Oceania (Figs. 4C, 4D). Co-occurrence analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between the IncQ2 plasmid and the blaOXA-488 resistance gene (P < 0.05), suggesting a stable non-random co-existence of these elements in ocular P. aeruginosa.

Virulence Genes

A total of 242 virulence-related genes spanning 12 categories were identified, with 106 genes constituting the core virulome across all isolates (Supplementary Table S3, Supplementary Fig. S1). Consistent with the antimicrobial resistance and plasmid findings, no significant temporal variation was observed in the number of virulence genes (P > 0.05; Fig. 5A). However, geographic distribution analysis showed that isolates from Asia and Oceania harbored significantly higher virulence gene counts compared with North American isolates (P < 0.05; Fig. 5B). Specifically, North American isolates displayed a reduced repertoire of genes associated with type IV pili (fimT, fimU, pilA, pilC, pilE, pilV, pilW, pilX, pilY1, and pilY2), type III secretion systems (exoS, exoU, and exoY), and iron uptake systems (fpvA, pvdD, pvdE, pvdI, and pvdJ) relative to isolates from other continents (see Supplementary Fig. S1). This observation likely reflects the specific genetic characteristics of the predominant high-resistance lineages (e.g. ST1203) within the North American cohort rather than a broad regional trait.

Relationship Among ARGs, Plasmids, and Virulence Genes

Correlation analysis revealed distinct association patterns between ARGs and either virulence factors or plasmids within the P. aeruginosa isolates. Specifically, ARGs exhibited a mild positive correlation with plasmid counts, whereas a significant negative correlation was observed between ARGs and virulence gene abundance (Figs. 6A, 6B).

Correlation analysis between ARGs, plasmid replicons, and virulence genes in P. aeruginosa isolates. (A) ARGs and plasmid replicon counts. (B) ARGs and virulence gene counts. (C) Heatmap of Spearman rank correlation between ARGs and plasmid replicons. The color scale represents the Spearman correlation coefficient (R).

Further, Spearman's rank correlation analysis demonstrated that plasmids IncFIA(HI1), Col440I, IncFII, and IncR clustered together and showed significant positive correlations (P < 0.05) with a variety of resistance genes, including mph(A), qnrB1, dfrA12, blaNDM-5, aadA2, and blaCTX-M-5. Regarding the interplay between virulence and resistance, most virulence genes exhibited either negative or nonsignificant correlations with ARGs. Notably, a cluster of flagellar-related genes (e.g. fliF, fliG, fliH, fliE, fliJ, and fliI) displayed a positive correlation trend with specific resistance genes (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Phylogenetic Analysis

Phylogenetic analysis indicated that the clustering of P. aeruginosa isolates primarily aligned with their Bayesian Analysis of Population Structure (BAPS) groups rather than country, continent, or sampling year. Hierarchical BAPS (hierBAPS) divided the 102 isolates into 5 distinct clusters: BAPS1 (n = 24), BAPS2 (n = 56), BAPS3 (n = 10), BAPS4 (n = 2), and BAPS5 (n = 10). Notably, BAPS1 and BAPS2 exhibited a broad international distribution, whereas BAPS3 (predominantly ST308) and BAPS5 (predominantly ST1203) were localized to Asia and North America, respectively (Fig. 7). Based on pairwise core-genome SNP distances, eight clonal clusters (SNP ≤ 10) were identified. Notably, these clusters were restricted to individual countries (India, the United States, Australia, and Portugal), with no evidence of cross-border clonal transmission (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Phylogenetic analysis of 102 P. aeruginosa isolates from human ocular sources worldwide. The color of the branch nodes indicates population classification based on HierBAPS. The heatmap sequentially displays metadata (i.e. continent, country, and time).

Analysis of genomic features across BAPS groups revealed significant variations in ARG abundance (P < 0.05), with BAPS5 harboring the highest count, followed by BAPS3 (Fig. 8A). Genes such as crpP, fosA, catB7, aph(3’)-IIb, and blaPAO were ubiquitous across all clusters. This highlights that the high resistance burden observed in specific regions is intrinsically linked to the expansion of these BAPS-specific lineages. Whereas BAPS1 and BAPS2 shared similar ARG profiles, blaOXA-48 was prevalent in BAPS3, and blaOXA-50 was observed in both BAPS4 isolates (Fig. 8B). In terms of mobile genetic elements (MGEs), BAPS3 exhibited a significantly higher average plasmid count, primarily carrying IncQ2-type plasmids. BAPS1 and BAPS2 displayed greater plasmid diversity, whereas no plasmids were detected in the BAPS4 and BAPS5 cohorts (Figs. 8C, 8D), suggesting that the extensive ARG repertoire in BAPS5 (ST1203) may be primarily integrated into the chromosome.

Pan-Genomic Analysis

Pan-genomic analysis of the 102 ocular P. aeruginosa isolates identified a total of 4915 core genes and 11,397 accessory genes. The high proportion of accessory genes underscores significant genomic diversity, likely driven by MGEs, horizontal gene transfer (HGT), and adaptive evolution across diverse ecological niches. To evaluate the impact of geographic origin on genomic plasticity, pangenome accumulation curves were constructed. Asian isolates exhibited a higher number of accessory genes compared with those from other continents, whereas the size of the calculated core genome decreased as a function of increasing genetic diversity within the Asian group. This suggests that the Asian cohort captures a more extensive “open” pangenome within this dataset; however, the relatively “conservative” profile of isolates from other regions should be interpreted with caution, as it is likely constrained by the smaller sample sizes available for those areas (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Functional enrichment analysis using COG categories revealed distinct functional profiles between the core and accessory genomes. Approximately 43% of the accessory genes remained uncharacterized. The core genome was significantly enriched in categories related to “Cellular processes and signaling,” “Metabolism,” and “Multi-function” (P < 0.05). Conversely, the accessory genome showed significant enrichment in functions associated with “Information storage and processing” (P < 0.05; Fig. 9), highlighting the role of the accessory genome in facilitating rapid adaptation through the acquisition of diverse genetic information.

Discussion

P. aeruginosa is a primary etiologic agent of infectious keratitis, posing a substantial threat particularly to contact lens wearers, where infection can lead to severe visual impairment or permanent blindness.26 Previous studies have identified P. aeruginosa as the most frequently isolated Gram-negative bacterium in microbial keratitis cases. Its management is exceptionally challenging due to its complex regulatory networks, extensive virulome, and escalating antimicrobial resistance, representing a persistent risk to the safe use of contact lenses.4,26,27 Given these global public health challenges, the present study systematically characterized the genomic landscape and population structure of ocular P. aeruginosa using publicly available international WGS data from the NCBI database.

Our findings reveal high ST diversity among ocular isolates, with 60 distinct STs identified from only 102 genomes. However, apart from Asia and North America, no predominant STs were observed in other regions. Further analysis indicated that the genomic composition of the isolates was strongly associated with specific STs rather than geographic clustering. This aligns with previous reports suggesting that P. aeruginosa STs in ocular infections are highly diverse, with minimal phylogeographic influence.28–30 It is noteworthy that the higher diversity observed in the Asian cohort may partially stem from its greater sampling depth, suggesting that the ocular P. aeruginosa population in other regions remains under-sampled in current genomic repositories.

Notably, ST308 emerged as the most prevalent lineage in Asia. Consistent with the criteria for international high-risk clones, ST308 frequently harbors multiple beta-lactamase genes and exhibits potent multidrug resistance, warranting stringent clinical surveillance.31 However, it is important to note that the dominance of ST308 in our findings is likely a reflection of the dataset's geographic composition, as isolates from Asian countries constitute the majority (65.7%) of the study population. This sampling imbalance may lead to an over-representation of ST308, which is an established endemic lineage in certain Asian regions, whereas potentially masking the importance of other lineages that might be more prevalent in under-represented areas such as Europe or North America.

WGS analysis further elucidated the distribution of ARGs. The most prevalent ARGs—aph(3′)-IIb, blaPAO, catB7, and fosA—reflect a resistome dominated by beta-lactam and aminoglycoside resistance, primarily mediated by the blaOXA family. This profile is consistent with P. aeruginosa isolates from other clinical sources.28,30,32 Moreover, we observed a longitudinal expansion in the diversity of ARGs over time, which may partially explain the rising phenotypic resistance observed in ocular isolates in certain regions.12,13 Carbapenems serve as critical last-line therapies for MDR P. aeruginosa; however, strains harboring blaNDM genes typically exhibit pan-resistance to these agents.33,34 In this study, three isolates were found to carry blaNDM, highlighting the emergence of high-risk MDR clones in ocular infections. The significantly higher ARG burden observed in North American isolates compared with other regions (see Fig. 3D) is primarily driven by the dominance of the ST1203 lineage within that small cohort, rather than representing a generalized regional resistance profile. Whereas MGEs such as pO111 and IncC plasmids are known drivers of blaNDM dissemination,35 the majority of our isolates lacked plasmids, with IncQ2 being the most frequent replicon. This suggests that large-scale horizontal transfer of blaNDM may not yet be established in the ocular population. Nevertheless, the role of IncQ2 remains complex; our co-occurrence analysis revealed a significant link between IncQ2 and blaOXA-488, consistent with previous reports linking IncQ2 to the dissemination of blaCTX-M-3.36,37 Furthermore, although the detection rates of plasmids such as IncFIA(HI1), Col440I, IncFII, and IncR were relatively low, they exhibited significant positive correlations (P < 0.05) with a variety of resistance genes, including mph(A), qnrB1, dfrA12, blaNDM-5, aadA2, and blaCTX-M-5. Notably, IncR and IncFIA(HI1) plasmids demand heightened vigilance, as multiple studies have corroborated their critical role in the dissemination of blaNDM genes.38–40

A significant negative correlation was identified between ARG burden and virulence gene counts. Interestingly, although most virulence factors were inversely related to resistance, a suite of flagellar-related genes (e.g. fliE, fliF, fliG, fliH, fliI, and fliJ) displayed a positive correlation trend with specific resistance markers. This suggests that whereas high-resistance clones may shed redundant virulence factors to conserve metabolic energy, they likely retain or even co-select for motility-related genes to facilitate colonization and biofilm formation in the ocular environment. Despite this trade-off, critical virulence genes, such as toxA, lasA, lasB, aprA, plcH, rhlA, rhlB, rhlC, and exoT, remained ubiquitous (near 100% prevalence). Previous research has demonstrated that isolates harboring this core virulome exhibit extreme toxicity.41 Consequently, future surveillance should prioritize the monitoring of P. aeruginosa isolates that simultaneously harbor IncR/IncFIA(HI1) plasmids, blaNDM-related genes, and the aforementioned high-prevalence virulence factors. Such lineages may possess a synergistic combination of multidrug resistance, hypervirulence, and enhanced HGT potential, posing a severe threat to patient outcomes.

Phylogenetic analysis further elucidated the genetic relatedness and potential transmission patterns of the isolates. WGS provides high resolution at the single-nucleotide level, significantly enhancing the capacity for phylogenetic traceability of pathogenic bacteria.42 Previous studies typically define strains with core-genome SNP distances ≤10 as closely related potential clones.43 In the present study, we identified eight such clonal clusters, each composed of a single ST and restricted within the borders of four countries: India, the United States, Australia, and Portugal. No transnational clonal clusters were detected. This suggests that the transmission of ocular P. aeruginosa may currently be confined to national or regional levels, with no evidence of widespread international clonal dissemination yet. However, given that high-risk lineages such as ST244 have been reported to spread globally,43 continuous and intensified surveillance of ocular high-risk clones remain paramount.

In-depth analysis of BAPS groupings revealed that BAPS1 and BAPS2 exhibited high genetic diversity, comprising 18 and 39 STs, respectively. BAPS4 consisted of ST4735 and ST2420, whereas BAPS3 and BAPS5 were each composed of a single lineage (ST308 and ST1203, respectively). Notably, the ST308-dominated BAPS3 group, recognized as a high-risk lineage, already harbored elevated counts of ARGs and plasmids. Even more concerning is that the ST1203-constituted BAPS5 group possessed an ARG burden exceeding even that of the ST308 group. Although ST1203 was previously considered a rare lineage, it has garnered significant attention since early 2022 following the detection of MDR strains in clinical samples and imported artificial tears in the United States.44,45 Our results indicate that lineages represented by ST1203 exhibit an extremely high ARG load, suggesting its emergence as a formidable new high-risk lineage that necessitates prioritized monitoring and infection control in the future.

Although detailed longitudinal patient outcomes were not available in the public metadata, the inclusion of isolates derived from “eviscerated content” and “necrotic materials” in our clinical infection group (see Supplementary Table S1) underscores the devastating visual impact of these pathogens. The presence of emerging lineages like ST1203 in such severe clinical contexts suggests a high potential for adverse outcomes, necessitating rapid intervention. The clinical translation of these genomic insights is vital for optimizing ophthalmic management. Our identification of ST1203 as an emerging high-risk lineage with an extreme ARG burden—particularly involving carbapenemases like blaNDM—suggests that empirical treatment with traditional beta-lactams or carbapenems may be increasingly prone to failure in severe keratitis cases. Consequently, these findings advocate for a pivot toward personalized antibiotic therapy guided by rapid molecular diagnostics or genomic screening in high-risk patients. Furthermore, given the recent association of ST1203 with contaminated ophthalmic preparations, our results emphasize the urgency of integrating genomic surveillance into quality control programs for over-the-counter eye care products. Finally, because these resistant lineages appear to retain critical flagellar-related genes, infection control strategies must remain focused on preventing biofilm-mediated colonization on contact lenses and surgical instruments. Implementing WGS-based surveillance could provide the high-resolution data necessary to track the dissemination of these specific clones and intercept potential outbreaks before they culminate in irreversible visual loss.

Pan-genome analysis further highlighted the geographic characteristics of genomic composition within the current dataset. Isolates from Asia harbored a larger repertoire of accessory genes, suggesting higher genomic plasticity. Although this observation points toward a high capacity for acquiring exogenous genes, it must be interpreted alongside the larger sample size of the Asian cohort, which naturally facilitates the detection of a broader accessory gene pool.46 COG functional enrichment analysis revealed significant functional divergence between the core and accessory genomes. The core genome was primarily enriched in fundamental categories such as “Cellular processes and signaling” and “Metabolism,” whereas the accessory genome was significantly enriched in functions related to “Information storage and processing.” This indicates that the accessory genome may enhance genomic plasticity and evolutionary potential through the acquisition of genes related to DNA recombination, repair, and translation, thereby allowing for more flexible responses to external selective pressures.19 Notably, approximately 43% of accessory genes remain functionally uncharacterized, indicating that the accessory genome of P. aeruginosa harbors a vast reservoir of unresolved genetic information. These genes may encode yet-to-be-discovered virulence factors, resistance mechanisms, or environmental adaptation factors, representing a valuable genetic resource for future investigation.

Notwithstanding the insights provided, several limitations must be acknowledged. The relatively small number of available genomes in the NCBI database, coupled with the inherent geographic imbalances—specifically the predominance of Asian isolates (65.7%)—constrains the precision of characterizing the genomic landscape on a truly global scale. This skew may introduce bias into analyses of antimicrobial resistance and virulence profiles across regions, as the smaller sample sizes from other regions (e.g. Europe and North America) may not fully capture their local genetic diversity. Consequently, our findings should be interpreted as a preliminary framework based on a high-resolution snapshot of currently available public data, rather than a definitive global epidemiological profile.

In summary, this study elucidated the population structure and evolutionary dynamics of ocular P. aeruginosa through a cross-regional analysis of available international whole-genome data. Our findings indicate that despite high ST diversity, high-risk lineages—represented by ST308 in Asia and the emerging ST1203—exhibit substantial ARG burdens and have become focal points for clinical prevention and control. Notably, since its detection in clinical samples and artificial tears in 2022, the ST1203 lineage has demonstrated a resistance trend that surpasses conventional high-risk clones, suggesting its potential to become a dominant next-generation epidemic clone. From an evolutionary perspective, we identified a significant negative correlation between ARG burden and virulence factor counts. Whereas core virulence genes, such as toxA, lasB, and exoT, maintained near-universal prevalence, isolates may have undergone an evolutionary fitness trade-off between acquiring resistance and maintaining virulence intensity under escalating antibiotic selective pressure. It is also critical to note that although the horizontal transfer of key resistance genes like blaNDM has not yet reached a large-scale dissemination phase, the significant correlation between specific plasmids, such as IncR and IncFIA(HI1), and multiple ARGs underscores the potent evolutionary potential for resistance expansion in ocular isolates. Furthermore, the identified accessory genome—of which approximately 43% remains functionally uncharacterized—represents a vast genetic reservoir for understanding the environmental adaptation of this pathogen. Although current clonal clusters remain largely restricted geographically within the analyzed dataset, the intensification of cross-border movement renders continued molecular surveillance of high-risk lineages an urgent priority. Future research should integrate functional genomics to validate the specific mechanisms by which these uncharacterized genes contribute to adaptation within the ocular microenvironment and the pathogenic process, thereby providing a scientific foundation for developing targeted antimicrobials and precision infection control strategies.
