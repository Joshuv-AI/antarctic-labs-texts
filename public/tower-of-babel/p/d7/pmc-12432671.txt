Current evidence and ongoing trials for surgery versus stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer: a narrative review
Authors: Lauren Drake, Robert Timmerman, Hiran C Fernando
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivePulmonary resection with mediastinal lymph node dissection is the standard of care for standard-risk operable patients with early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), while stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) is the mainstay treatment for inoperable patients. Within the last decade, SBRT has become increasingly used to treat high-risk operable patients who may otherwise be offered a compromise operation such as wedge resection, as well as standard-risk operable patients who would be able to tolerate lobectomy. The aim of this review is to discuss the current available data comparing SBRT and surgery with an emphasis on the ongoing randomized JoLT-Ca Sublobar Resection (SR) Versus Stereotactic Ablative Radiotherapy (SABR) for Lung Cancer (Stablemates) and veterans affairs lung cancer surgery or stereotactic radiotherapy (VALOR) studies.MethodsA search for studies comparing SBRT to surgery in early-stage NSCLC was conducted on PubMed. An emphasis was made on selecting publications between 2020 to 2024 to include the most recent studies on the topic. Meta-analyses, systematic reviews, propensity matched studies, retrospective reviews and national database analyses were included. ClinicalTrials.gov was searched for information pertaining to current randomized trials.Key Content and FindingsThe majority of current data supports surgery over SBRT based on overall survival (OS), however, a direct comparison between the two has been challenging. Definitions for locoregional control, requirements of biopsy proven malignancy, the extent of surgical resection and mediastinal lymphadenectomy, and primary end points vary by study. Previous randomized controlled trials have failed to accrue, though two ongoing randomized studies, Stablemates (NCT02468024) and VALOR (NCT02984761), are nearing accrual which will better inform clinicians which treatment may be preferable to which patients.ConclusionsThe current evidence favors surgery over SBRT for early-stage NSCLC in terms of OS, especially for standard-risk operable patients. For high-risk operable patients, surgery should still be considered standard of care, however the evidence is less clear, since many studies show similar recurrence rates. Based on the current evidence, we recommend surgical resection with mediastinal lymph node dissection for all patients with early-stage NSCLC who are operable. For patients medically unfit to undergo surgery, SBRT should be considered the standard of care.Keywords: Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT), surgery, early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (early-stage NSCLC)

Introduction

Approximately one in four patients diagnosed with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), will have early-stage disease (1). Surgical resection with mediastinal lymph node dissection is the standard of care for standard-risk operable patients (2). Stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR), also known as stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT), has been studied and validated for medically inoperable patients, but has been increasingly used to treat both high-risk operable patients (who could receive a compromise procedure such as wedge or segmentectomy) as well as standard-risk operable patients (who would usually be considered for lobectomy). Several phase I and II studies have found SBRT to be safe and effective with local control rates >90% (3-10). Given SBRT has resulted in improved outcomes compared to conventionally fractionated radiotherapy, SBRT has become the standard of care for medically inoperable patients and has gained momentum for both standard-risk and high-risk operable patients (11-13). Whereas 16% of patients were treated with SBRT in 2012, up to 26% of patients were treated with SBRT in 2018 (14). Specialists interpret the literature comparing SBRT and surgery differently. These differences were emphasized in a 2018 report of 959 surveys completed by various lung cancer specialists (15). When asked a series of questions assessing how they felt SBRT compared with surgery given the current state and strength of the evidence, 80% of radiation oncologists responded that SBRT was the same or better than surgery. In contrast, only 28% of non-radiation oncologists surveyed felt that SBRT offered the same or better benefits as compared with surgery. There are several concerns about accepting this view based on currently available literature. It is often wrongly stated that local control is better with SBRT as compared with sublobar resection and probably equivalent to that of lobectomy. However, the definitions of local control have been different between several surgical and radiation studies. In many radiation studies, local control refers to recurrence only at the irradiated tumor site, not capturing nearby but separate recurrence (e.g., intra-pulmonary lymph node recurrence). In addition to local recurrence, surgical studies will usually include recurrence in other areas of the lung (e.g., in the same lobe but away from the resection site after a wedge resection). In radiation studies, tumor recurrence in another lobe, but in the same lung, will often be considered a distant recurrence, whereas several surgical studies would consider this a locoregional recurrence. In addition, many SBRT studies have included patients who do not have a tissue diagnosis. If a patient never had a malignant tumor, to begin with, and was treated with radiation, estimates of recurrence and cancer-specific survival are inflated.

The best comparison of SBRT to surgery would be randomized studies where patients have similar baseline characteristics and also similar definitions of adverse event reporting and recurrence. The best metric for comparison of these modalities is overall survival (OS). However, as will be discussed below, this has been a challenging issue. Other options include single or multi-institution case control comparisons of SBRT to surgery, or large database comparisons. Both of these approaches have been used, with often contradictory outcomes, with some studies favoring SBRT and others favoring resection. We previously published an analysis comparing the modalities of ablation, SBRT and resection for high-risk operable patients (16). Three- and 5-year OS outcomes favored surgery. In the current review, we examine the evidence comparing SBRT to surgery for early-stage lung cancer in standard and high-risk operable patients with a focus on studies published after 2020. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2025-387/rc).

Methods

PubMed was searched in December 2024 using the following terms: “surgery vs SBRT early stage NSCLC”, “surgery vs radiation early stage NSCLC”, “surgery vs radiation early stage lung cancer”, and “surgery vs SBRT early stage lung cancer”. Publications delete from 2020 onwards were prioritized to review the most recent data pertaining to the topic. Meta-analyses, systematic reviews, propensity matched studies, retrospective reviews, national database analyses and clinical trials were reviewed. One hundred twenty-one articles were retrieved from our search, of which 13 were chosen based on study design and generalizability of results, inclusion of operable and inoperable patients and patient populations with stage I and II lung cancer only. ClinicalTrials.gov was reviewed for information pertaining to ongoing randomized trials. Randomized reviews prior to 2020 were included for context in addition to two randomized trials that are currently ongoing. Only articles originally written in English were considered. Discussion

Non-randomized studies comparing SBRT and surgery

Several studies have reported a survival advantage associated with surgery, though differences in recurrence rates following SBRT or surgery have not been consistent. In a study of 206 propensity score matched patients, Snider et al. reported significantly higher OS and progression-free survival associated with lobectomy or sublobar resection compared to SBRT (17). Interestingly, there was no significant difference in the rate of new malignant nodules or distant recurrence (17). Similarly, analysis of 538 patients by Miki et al. reported significantly higher 3-year OS in patients undergoing surgery though local and distant recurrence rates as well as lung cancer-specific survival (LCSS) rates were not significantly different between groups (18). Propensity score matched analysis by Park et al. of 48 pairs of patients ≥75 years old identified surgery to be associated with significantly higher 5-year OS (19). However, there was no difference in cancer-related recurrence or death despite significantly higher regional recurrence rates in patients undergoing SBRT (19). It is worthwhile noting that every surgical patient in this study underwent a thorough lymph node dissection with a mean number of 20 lymph nodes resected per patient. As up to 12% of patients with early-stage NSCLC are upstaged based on nodal disease, lack of lymph node evaluation with SBRT remains a concern (14,20-23). In addition, with similar patterns of cancer recurrence, these studies may suffer from inaccurate propensity matching favoring surgical selection.

A handful of studies have reported analysis using large databases such as the National Cancer Database (NCDB). Not surprisingly, short term survival is higher in patients undergoing SBRT, and this is likely related to surgery-related rather than cancer-related mortality. However, given the nature of data collection, analysis of comorbidities and factors determining selection of SBRT or surgery are not available. Analysis by Udelsman et al. identified 30,658 patients who underwent treatment with surgical resection or SBRT between 2012 to 2018 (14). The majority of patients underwent lobectomy though wedge resection was included. Not every patient in the SBRT cohort had pathologic confirmation of malignancy. Ninety-day mortality was significantly higher in patients who underwent surgery compared to SBRT though 5-year OS was significantly higher in patients who underwent surgery (14). Five-year OS was 73% following lobectomy, 72% following segmentectomy, 62% following wedge resection and 56% after SBRT (14). Regardless of the extent of surgical resection, operation resulted in significantly higher OS compared to SBRT. Khorfan et al. queried the NCDB between 2004 to 2016 and also reported significantly higher survival outcomes with surgery (24). The majority of patients underwent lobectomy, though patients undergoing sublobar resection and wedge resection were also included. Five-year OS was 57% for patients undergoing surgery vs. 38% for patients receiving SBRT (24). Again, wedge resection was associated with superior survival outcomes compared to SBRT, with 5-year OS of 49% and 38%, respectively (24). However, up until the first 2 years, survival was higher for patients treated with SBRT. Rosen et al. analyzed 13,562 co-morbidity free patients undergoing lobectomy vs. SBRT (23). Five-year OS was significantly higher for patients treated with lobectomy compared to SBRT, with OS rates of 59% vs. 29%, respectively (23). Interestingly, survival was higher following SBRT up until 7.5 months, at which time survival following surgery became superior (23). The initial survival advantage observed with SBRT in these three studies is likely related to procedural morbidity, however, subanalysis is limited due to the nature of data collection (14,23,24). While results from these studies report superior long-term survival associated with surgery, better long-term survival in surgical patients is somewhat expected as this cohort of patients presumably have preserved cardiopulmonary function allowing them to tolerate surgery, whereas patients unfit to undergo surgery are likely to negatively impact survival in the SBRT cohort. These underlying health discrepancies are challenging to compare across hospitals and may confound results in national database analyses. It is also important to note that these studies included patients who refused to undergo surgery despite clinical recommendation for surgical resection in the SBRT cohort. However, based on current literature, these patients comprise a smaller proportion of patients undergoing SBRT.

Results from retrospective studies are challenging to apply to the general population given selection bias, differences in criteria of determining whether a patient is operable, and nebulous reasons for patient decision to undergo one treatment over the other. Despite these challenges, systematic review and meta-analyses have pooled available data to compare SBRT to surgery, though interpretation of results is limited due to high heterogeneity among studies. In collection of data up through 2021, Peng et al. compared survival outcomes following wedge resection compared to SBRT (25). Though wedge resection was associated with significantly higher OS, disease-free survival (DFS) was not significantly different between both cohorts (25). However, heterogeneity was high among studies and 5-year OS and DFS were not reported by all studies. Viani et al. analyzed 29,511 patients and reported 3-year OS and LCSS to be significantly higher in patients undergoing surgery (26). Viani et al. did note that local control was not significantly different between groups regardless of extent of surgical resection and heterogeneity of data for this analysis was considerably lower (26). In a systematic review and meta-analysis by Ijsseldijk et al., 100 studies up through 2018 were analyzed, the majority of which were noncomparative studies (27). In comparative studies, the authors attempted to reduce heterogeneity by including only clinically staged surgical patients so that pathologic staging and therefore survival would be more comparable to patients receiving SBRT as these patients did not undergo mediastinal staging. In noncomparative studies, only SBRT cohorts with a minimum of 85% of patients receiving biopsies were included to reduce comparison of survival outcomes with patients who may be treated with SBRT to benign lesions. While OS and DFS favored surgery over SBRT, the authors still note considerable heterogeneity of pooled analyses in estimating survival (27). Park et al. evaluated short and long term outcomes associated with quality of life and survival following SBRT and resection (28). Not surprisingly, short term morbidity and quality of life favored SBRT, however, OS and LCSS significantly favored surgery (28). Despite high heterogeneity in these studies, surgery continues to result in more favorable OS compared to SBRT. A summary of these studies can be found in Table 2.

A significant issue when trying to interpret these studies is the heterogeneity of the patient populations. It is likely that patients selected for operation have lower comorbidity, and better performance status. Propensity matching can help improve comparison of patient groups, but the closeness of matching will be limited by the reservoir of potential matches and the available number of measured covariates between groups from retrospective and larger database studies. However, even with propensity score matching, all factors that go into determining operability and respectability cannot be accounted for. Another factor is the relative distribution of high-risk operable and standard risk operable patients when comparing modalities. A high-risk patient, with impaired cardiopulmonary function may die from co-morbid disease rather than cancer. It could be argued that SBRT would provide lower short-term impact on quality of life and physical function compared to operation. A standard-risk operable patient is more likely to tolerate operation, and more likely to die from cancer if inadequately treated. Prospective randomized studies are really the best way to compare patients as these will truly compare similar patient groups, and have uniform methods of data collection and outcome classification.

Randomized studies comparing SBRT and surgery

There were three previous attempts at randomized trials aimed at comparing OS for SBRT versus surgical resection for early-stage NSCLC. Two of these trials included standard risk (lobectomy candidate) patients. These were the STARS, randomized study to compare cyberknife to surgical resection in stage I NSCLC (NCT00840749), and the ROSEL, trial of either surgery or stereotactic radiotherapy for early stage (IA) lung cancer (NCT00687986). Both studies included patients up to T2N0M0. However, there were some differences in eligibility criteria. Patients enrolled in ROSEL included patients who demonstrated radiographic evidence of malignancy without pathologic confirmation of cancer whereas patients in STARS were all required to have a tissue biopsy confirming malignancy. ROSEL excluded patients with tumors <2 cm from hilar structures whereas STARS did not. Patients treated with SBRT received 50–54 gray (Gy) in STARS compared to 54–60 Gy in ROSEL. Notably, surgical resection was similar in both trials and patients underwent a minimum of a lobectomy or sleeve resection with mediastinal lymph node dissection. The third trial, ACOSOG Z4099 study (RTOG1201; NCT01336894), focused on high-risk operable patients (29). This study included patients who were considered too high risk for lobectomy, but were still considered eligible for sublobar resection.

Unfortunately, all three trials closed due to failure to accrue. Only 68 of the combined target of 2,410 patients (2.8%) were ever successfully enrolled in three of these planned randomized trials. There are several factors that likely accounted for this. It is challenging to randomize patients to two disparate treatments such as operation or radiation. Patients will question why they are being asked to randomly receive an invasive procedure such as lobectomy compared to a non-invasive procedure such as SBRT. Physicians also need to have equipoise regarding these very different therapies, and be committed to the time consuming process of counselling and educating patients to allow successful enrollment to these studies.

Given similar inclusion criteria, Chang et al. pooled data from the 36 and 22 patients enrolled in STARS and ROSEL studies, respectively (30). OS was significantly lower for patients who underwent surgical resection compared to SBRT, with a 3-year OS of 95% and 79% for patients who underwent SBRT vs. resection, respectively (30). There was no clinically meaningful difference in recurrence-free survival. There are several concerns raised with this pooled analysis. Only 2.9% of the planned patients for the two studies were ultimately enrolled. Complications after surgery were higher than would be expected. For instance operative mortality was 4%, which is significantly higher than the 1.4% reported from a Society of Thoracic Surgeons database study (31). Additionally, 80% of operations were by thoracotomy, and 11% did not even undergo lobectomy, which is noteworthy, since eligible patients for these two studies included standard risk surgical patients. Additionally, in the ROSEL study, tissue diagnosis was not required.

Ongoing studies

Currently, there are two ongoing multicenter, randomized trials comparing surgery to SBRT that are nearing accrual. The JoLT-Ca Sublobar Resection (SR) Versus Stereotactic Ablative Radiotherapy (SABR) for Lung Cancer (Stablemates; NCT02468024) is a randomized phase III trial comparing sublobar resection to SABR. The Stablemates study is limited to high-risk operable patients. This study uses an approach called “randomized consent”. This differs from a traditional randomized study in that the patient is screened and randomized. The study is then presented to the patient. Patients have the option to continue treatment according to the arm they were randomized to, or to be treated and then followed if they reject the randomization arm. Although this approach is subject to some biases, patients should have similar baseline characteristics and all will have similar definitions of outcome and follow-up schedule. This approach was successfully used in the NSABP B-06 study that compared mastectomy with lumpectomy and radiation and allowed that trial to be successfully completed, leading to a change in the standard of care for breast cancer patients (32). In that study there was no difference in outcome in patients who were randomized or selected their treatment arm. Total radiation dose for Stablemates is 54 Gy in 3 fractions. There are currently 233 patients out of 272 patients enrolled. Additional eligibility criteria includes patients over the age of 18 years, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status 0–2, tumors with radiographic findings consistent with NSCLC including ground glass opacities with a minimum of 50% or more solid component, biopsy proven NSCLC, clinical stage I with tumor ≤4 cm, peripheral tumors located at a minimum of 2 cm from the proximal bronchial tree, no history of radiation to the ipsilateral lung, and no malignancy <3 years prior to registration. Primary endpoint is 3-year OS. Secondary outcomes include 5-year progression-free survival and OS and toxicity associated with each treatment.

Veterans affairs lung cancer surgery or stereotactic radiotherapy (VALOR) is another multicenter randomized trial designed to compare surgical outcomes to those following SBRT. Goal enrollment is 670 patients. Eligibility criteria includes patients greater than 18 years of age, tumors ≤5 cm, Karnofsky performance status ≥70, pathologic confirmation of NSCLC, tumors >1 cm from proximal bronchial tree and peripheral structures, pathologic assessment of all level 10 and above nodes that are >1 cm and have a standardized uptake value (SUV) >2.5, no history of radiation to the thorax, and no prior thoracic surgery excluding cardiac procedures. SBRT will be given over 3–5 fractions with a total of 54–57.5 Gy for peripheral tumors and 50 Gy for central tumors. Unlike the Stablemates trial, VALOR includes standard risk patients. Patients undergoing surgical resection will undergo lobectomy or any anatomic pulmonary resection. Primary endpoint is OS. Secondary end points are 5-year follow-up of the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Quality of Life Questionnaire (EORTC QLQ-C30) and Lung Cancer (LC 13) surveys, respiratory function based on St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire, EQ-5D-5L (EuroQOL-5D) assessment of quality of adjusted life years, cause of death, disease recurrence, and forced expiratory volume at 1 second.

Conclusions

The current evidence favors surgery over SBRT for early-stage NSCLC in terms of OS, especially for standard-risk operable patients. For high-risk operable patients, surgery should still be considered standard of care, however the evidence is less clear, since many studies show similar recurrence rates. There are likely sub-groups of patients who may benefit more, with one approach or another. Based on the current evidence, we recommend surgical resection with mediastinal lymph node dissection for all patients with early-stage NSCLC who are operable. For patients medically unfit to undergo surgery, SBRT should be considered the standard of care. Randomized trials such as Stablemates and VALOR will help to inform us better on optimizing therapy when these trials are completed.
