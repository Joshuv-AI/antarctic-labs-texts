Ginsenoside Rg1 attenuates experimental periodontitis via modulating the PI3K/AKT pathway and TLR2 expression
Authors: Yifan Pu, Liping Chen, Danni Wang, Yanzhong Pan, Ling Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeriodontitis is a persistent inflammatory disease caused by the interplay between periodontal pathogens and the host immune response, leading not only to inflammatory infiltration of periodontal tissues but also to resorption and destruction of alveolar bone. Ginsenoside Rg1, a major pharmacologically active constituent of ginseng, exhibits anti-inflammatory, osteogenic, and immunomodulatory properties. This study investigated the therapeutic potential of Rg1 in an experimental periodontitis model in rats and explored its underlying molecular mechanisms.MethodsAn experimental periodontitis model was established in 8-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats by ligating the maxillary first molars and inoculating with Porphyromonas gingivalis suspension. Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) and hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining were used to evaluate inflammatory bone destruction. Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining and immunofluorescence (IF) were performed to assess osteoclast numbers and osteogenic marker expression. Gene and protein expression levels of inflammatory cytokines and the immune receptor Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) were measured using RT-PCR and IF. Western blotting was conducted to evaluate the phosphorylation status of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.ResultsRg1 at a dose of 500 µg/kg exhibited optimal pharmacological activity, effectively alleviating inflammatory damage and bone destruction in periodontitis rats. Rg1 treatment significantly decreased the expression of TLR2 and IL-1β, while increasing that of TGF-β1. Additionally, Rg1 upregulated the expression of osteogenic markers and inhibited osteoclast formation. Notably, co-treatment with PI3K/AKT pathway inhibitor LY294002 significantly reduced the protective effects of Rg1, suggesting that the PI3K/AKT pathway is indispensable for its protective effects.ConclusionGinsenoside Rg1 attenuates experimental periodontitis by regulating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and suppressing TLR2 expression. This study provides a novel reference for pharmacological mechanism and new drug development of Rg1 in the therapeutic applications of periodontitis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-07950-2.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by progressive destruction of periodontal supporting tissues, and it is ranked as the sixth most common disease worldwide [1]. Its pathological features include persistent inflammatory infiltration and progressive alveolar bone resorption, ultimately leading to tooth loosening and loss, which severely compromises the patients' masticatory function and psychosocial well-being [2, 3]. The current clinical management primarily relies on mechanical debridement (scaling and root planning) combined with adjunctive antibiotic therapy. However, the efficacy of these conventional methods is often limited by incomplete biofilm removal, the emergence of antibiotic resistance, and microbiota dysbiosis [4, 5]. It is noteworthy that periodontal bone destruction is primarily mediated by an excessive host immuno-inflammatory response to microbial challenge [6]. In particular, Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) is regarded as a keystone pathogen in the progression of periodontitis, through virulence factors such as gingipains, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and extracellular vesicles, it promotes bacterial adhesion, host matrix degradation, and hyperactivation of the host immune response [7]. Consequently, a sustained inflammatory microenvironment is established, which disrupts bone homeostasis, enhances osteoclast activity, and aggravates alveolar bone loss [8]. Hence, there is an immediate necessity of exploring optimized therapeutic approaches that modulate the immuno-inflammatory response to improve the management of periodontitis.

The phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) pathway is central to the modulation of immune responses and inflammation. It has emerged as a critical target for periodontitis immunomodulation [9, 10]. Activation of the pathway requires phosphorylation of downstream substrates. The lipid kinase PI3K is composed of two distinct subunits: a regulatory component (p85) and a catalytic component (p110), which together contribute to structural stability and enzymatic activity. However, gingipain of P. gingivalis inhibits pathway activity by interfering with the binding of the p85 subunit to the membrane protein [11]. Several studies have indicated that the restoration of AKT signaling is associated with reduced oxidative stress and lower levels of pro-inflammatory mediators [12–14]. In addition, Liu Y and colleagues discovered that activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway significantly upregulated osteogenic markers, including RUNX2, osteocalcin (OCN), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and promoted osteogenesis [15, 16]. Despite these findings, the specific regulatory mechanism of PI3K/AKT in periodontitis remains to be fully elucidated.

Activation of the host immune-inflammatory response by pathogens such as P. gingivalis typically begins with pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on host cells. Among these, Toll-like receptors (TLRs) stand out as the best-known PRRs and play a central role in innate immunity [17]. The inflammatory reaction induced by P. gingivalis primarily relies on the identification of its pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) via Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) [18]. Epidemiological studies have shown that TLR2 expression in periodontal tissues of chronic periodontitis patients is significantly higher than in healthy individuals, and positively correlates with disease severity [19]. Therefore, TLR2 can be considered an important biomarker of inflammation and tissue destruction.

Ginsenosides are the most well-studied natural bioactive compounds in ginseng [20]. Ginsenoside Rg1, which has been widely used in traditional Chinese medicine, has been demonstrated to exhibit diverse pharmacological properties, including immunomodulatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic activities [21]. Recent investigations have highlighted the therapeutic value of Rg1 in bone-related diseases [22], both in vivo and in vitro studies have demonstrated that Rg1 regulates osteoclast and osteoblast activity and promotes H-type angiogenesis in osteoporosis [23, 24]. Nevertheless, evidence regarding the impact of Rg1 on periodontitis remains scarce.

Rg1 has also been reported to target TLR2, thereby inhibiting downstream inflammatory signaling and improving the tissue microenvironment [25]. However, its effects on TLR2 in the periodontal microenvironment require further investigation. Additionally, our prior research has shown that Rg1 can effectively mitigate H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in human periodontal ligament cells through PI3K/AKT activation [26]. The PI3K/AKT pathway is a key signaling pathways regulated by Rg1, mediating its protective effects in various pathological processes [27]. However, whether Rg1 can alleviate inflammation in periodontitis in vivo by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway remains to be verified.

In this study, an experimental periodontitis model in rats was established to simulate disease-associated inflammatory responses and bone resorption. We further investigated whether Rg1 exerts anti-inflammatory and osteoprotective effects through the regulation of TLR2 expression and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway activity.

Materials and methods

Animal models

Forty-eight 8-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats (weighing 250 ± 10 g) were supplied by the Laboratory Animal Center of Southwest Medical University (Luzhou, China). All animals were acclimatized for one week in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility with controlled environment (standard temperature, humidity, and a 12-h light/dark cycle).

The Porphyromonas gingivalis (ATCC 33277) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). The frozen glycerol stock was revived and streaked onto Columbia blood agar plates. Following anaerobic incubation at 37 °C, single colonies were inoculated into liquid broth and cultured anaerobically to the logarithmic growth phase ( 1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL). 1 mL of the bacterial suspension was reserved for subsequent use. For ligation, 100 µL of the suspension was applied to the gingival margin and the ligature wire around the maxillary first molar to ensure subgingival colonization.

To induce experimental periodontitis, rats were anesthetized with 4% (v/v) isoflurane, and a 3 − 0 silk ligature soaked in a suspension of Porphyromonas gingivalis was tied around the right maxillary first molar. The ligature was checked daily and repositioned if necessary, with fresh P. gingivalis suspension reapplied accordingly. The ligature remained in place for three weeks to facilitate biofilm accumulation and disease development before removal.

Rg1 dose screening and treatment protocol

The animal experiments were conducted in two stages. The first stage aimed to screen for an effective dose of Rg1 (purchased from Weikeqi Biotech, Sichuan, China; dissolved in sterile saline, filtered, aliquoted, and stored at 4 °C). Rats were randomly divided into four groups (n = 4/group) using an online random number generator: Periodontitis (ligation + saline), Rg1-250 (ligation + 250 µg/kg Rg1), Rg1-500 (ligation + 500 µg/kg Rg1), and Rg1-1000 (ligation + 1000 µg/kg Rg1) group. Rats in the treatment groups received multi-site intrasulcular injections of Rg1 solution (0.05 mL) at the mesial, distal, buccal, and palatal gingival margins of the maxillary first molar (depth: 1–2 mm). The periodontitis group received the same volume of physiological saline using the same injection protocol.

The second stage served as the formal experiment (n = 8/group). The determination of sample size was based on a power analysis conducted with PASS software (Supplementary Material 1). Rats were randomly divided into four distinct groups: Control (without ligation+ saline), Periodontitis (ligation + saline), Rg1-500 (ligation + 500 µg/kg Rg1), and LY294002 group (ligation + LY294002 + 500 µg/kg Rg1). LY294002, an inhibitor of the PI3K/AKT pathway, was administered at the same volume and via the same route as Rg1. Rats in the LY294002 group were injected with 0.05 mL LY294002 (MedChemExpress, USA) 30 min before the 0.05 mL Rg1 injection. The administration method and injection volume for the remaining groups were consistent with those used in the preliminary experiment. All injections were performed daily for four weeks at the same location and time.

Assessment of Systemic Safety: To evaluate potential systemic toxicity of Rg1, body weights of all rats were recorded weekly throughout the experimental period. At the study endpoint, major visceral organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested, fixed, and processed for histopathological examination using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT)

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) analysis was performed on rat maxillary bones using a SkyScan 1176 micro-CT scanner (Bruker, USA). The scanning parameters were set to 65 kV X-ray source voltage, 385 µA source current, and 18 μm resolution. Three-dimensional reconstruction was performed using CTvox software (Bruker, USA). The region of interest (ROI) was defined as the area surrounding the first molar. The following parameters were quantified: cementoenamel junction-alveolar bone crest distance (CEJ-ABC, mm), bone volume/total volume ratio (BV/TV), and bone mineral density (BMD).

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining

Maxillary specimens from rats were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and demineralized for 40 days. The samples were then dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. Sections were mounted on adhesive glass slides and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). For osteoclast detection, additional sections were stained using a tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining kit (Servicebio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis

The expression of IL-1β and TGF-β1 was visualized via immunohistochemistry (IHC). Briefly, tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies targeting IL-1β (1:500, GB11113) and TGF-β1 (1:500, GB11179). Following a wash step, the sections were incubated for 50 min at room temperature with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:200, GB23303). All antibodies were obtained from Servicebio (Wuhan, China). Antibody binding was visualized using a DAB chromogenic reagent (G1212, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin (G1040, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Five random fields within the periodontal connective tissue apical to the alveolar bone crest surrounding the first molar were examined under a Nikon E100 microscope. Brown-yellow staining in these areas was considered positive. The mean optical density (MOD) was then quantified using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was conducted on paraffin-embedded rat maxillary sections. Following deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed using EDTA buffer (pH 8.0). The sections were then permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 solution and blocked with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). For primary antibody incubation, sections were treated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against RUNX2 (GB115631, 1:200), osteocalcin (GB11233, 1:500), or TLR2 (GB11518, 1:200), respectively. Following PBS washes, the sections were incubated with an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:400) at 37 °C for 1 h. After quenching tissue autofluorescence and counterstaining nuclei with DAPI, the sections were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium. All antibodies were obtained from Servicebio (Wuhan, China). Immunofluorescence images were acquired employing an IX73 microscope system (Olympus, Japan). Fluorescence intensity was quantified from five randomly selected fields within specific regions of interest (ROI) surrounding the first molar: for RUNX2 and OCN, the ROI was defined as the alveolar bone crest and its adjacent bone surface; for TLR2, the ROI encompassed both the inflamed connective tissue and the alveolar bone surface in the peri-crestal region.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

As instructed by the manufacturer, TRIzol reagent (Ambion, USA) was used to extract total RNA from gingival tissues. This was followed by reverse transcription and real-time PCR. The CFX Connect™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) was used for all qRT-PCR analyses. The amplification protocol was as follows: 95 °C for 3 min (initial denaturation), followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s (denaturation), 56 °C for 10 s (annealing), and 72 °C for 25 s (extension). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Western blotting

Periodontal tissues were minced and lysed in RIPA buffer (R0010, Solarbio) supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (PC0020, Solarbio). Equal amounts of protein (20 µg) were separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA). Prior to transfer, PVDF membranes were activated in methanol for 5 min. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against PI3K (1:1000; PAB31074, Bioswamp), p-AKT (Ser473) (1:1000; AF4369, Affinity), and β-actin (1:1000; PAB36265, Bioswamp) overnight at 4 °C (complete blots are presented in Supplementary Material 3). Following three 5-min washes with PBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:10000; SAB43706, Bioswamp) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, protein bands were visualized using a Tanon-5200 automated chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, China) with an ECL reagent (H31500, Tiandirenhe) and quantified with ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data processing and statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 10.0 and SPSS 27.0. All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed across various groups, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. For comparisons between two specific groups, an unpaired t-test was used. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Dose optimization of ginsenoside Rg1 reveals maximal anti-inflammatory and osteoprotective effects in experimental periodontitis

After three weeks of ligature placement, alveolar bone resorption was observed in SD rats (Fig. 1D). The CEJ–ABC distance at the maxillary first molar increased more than threefold compared to baseline, reaching approximately 1.5 mm (p < 0.05; Fig. 1E), confirming the successful establishment of the periodontitis model. To evaluate the therapeutic potential and determine the optimal dose of ginsenoside Rg1, rats received gingival sulcus injections of Rg1 at 250 µg/kg, 500 µg/kg, and 1000 µg/kg for four weeks. Histological analysis by hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining (Fig. 2A, B) revealed that all Rg1-treated groups exhibited significantly fewer inflammatory cells and osteoclasts compared to the periodontitis group (p < 0.05). Notably, there was no significant difference between the 250 µg/kg and 1000 µg/kg groups; however, both exhibited higher levels of inflammation and osteoclast numbers than the 500 µg/kg group (Fig. 2C). These findings indicate that the intermediate dose of 500 µg/kg provides the most pronounced anti-inflammatory and osteoprotective effects, and was thus selected for subsequent experiments. Furthermore, histopathological examination of key organs revealed no signs of toxicity in the Rg1-500 group, supporting its excellent biocompatibility (Supplementary Material 4).

Local Rg1 injection alleviates periodontal inflammation and suppresses TLR2 expression

To further investigate the regulatory effect of ginsenoside Rg1 on periodontal inflammation, histopathological analysis was performed four weeks after treatment. HE staining showed that, compared with the periodontitis group, the Rg1-500 group exhibited markedly reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and more organized collagen fibers arrangement, closely resembling normal tissue architecture (Fig. 3B). Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis revealed a significant reduction in IL-1β-positive signals and an enhancement of TGF-β1 expression in the Rg1-500 group (Fig. 4A). These findings were further supported by RT-PCR, which demonstrated significantly decreased IL-1β and increased TGF-β1 mRNA expression in the Rg1-treated group compared to the periodontitis group (p < 0.05; Fig. 4B). These observations suggested that Rg1 effectively suppresses periodontal inflammation and promotes tissue repair. Notably, these regulatory effects were abolished by the PI3K/AKT pathway inhibitor LY294002, suggesting that the anti-inflammatory effects of Rg1 are mediated via PI3K/AKT signaling pathway activation. Given the anti-inflammatory ability of Rg1, we further examined its impact on TLR2, a key receptor for Porphyromonas gingivalis in gingival tissues. Immunofluorescence and RT-PCR analysis revealed significantly elevated TLR2 expression in the periodontitis group relative to the control group. Conversely, treatment with Rg1 markedly suppressed TLR2 expression (Fig. 3C, E, F), resulting in a significantly lower TLR2 expression level in the Rg1-500 group. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that local Rg1 administration alleviates inflammatory infiltration in periodontal tissues, modulates expression of inflammatory cytokines, and downregulates expression of TLR2.

Local administration of Rg1 attenuates alveolar bone loss and regulates bone remodeling balance

To evaluate the protective effects of ginsenoside Rg1 on periodontitis-associated alveolar bone resorption, we performed micro-CT analysis and assessed the expression of osteogenesis-related markers. Micro-CT results revealed that Rg1 treatment significantly reduced the CEJ–ABC distance compared to the periodontitis group (Fig. 5A, B), whereas LY294002 administration reversed this protective effect. This trend was in line with histological findings from H&E staining. Furthermore, bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and bone mineral density (BMD) were significantly higher in the Rg1-500 group than in both the periodontitis and LY294002 groups (p < 0.05; Fig. 5C, D), indicating enhanced bone preservation. Given that alveolar bone integrity relies on the dynamic coupling of osteoblast and osteoclast activities, we further investigated the cellular mechanisms underlying the bone-protective effects of Rg1. According to TRAP staining, osteoclast counts in the Rg1-500 group were much lower than those in the periodontitis group. However, LY294002 treatment increased osteoclast presence, with levels comparable to those in untreated periodontitis rats (Fig. 5E, F). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated decreased expression of osteogenic markers RUNX2 and OCN in the periodontitis group, whereas Rg1 administration markedly enhanced both fluorescence intensity and mRNA expression of these markers (Fig. 6A-C). Notably, the LY294002 group showed lower expression of RUNX2 and OCN compared to the Rg1-500 group, yet remained elevated relative to the periodontitis group (Fig. 6B, D-F). Collectively, these findings suggested that local administration of Rg1 promotes alveolar bone preservation by reducing osteoclast activity and enhancing osteoblast function, thereby maintaining bone remodeling homeostasis. This effect was likely mediated via the PI3K/AKT pathway, as it was abrogated by LY294002 inhibition.

Ginsenoside Rg1 attenuates inflammatory alveolar bone destruction in periodontitis via the PI3K/AKT pathway

As described above, Rg1 effectively alleviated inflammatory damage in the periodontitis animal model and reduced alveolar bone resorption caused by the imbalance between osteogenic and osteoclastic activity. Our previous study revealed that Rg1 engages the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway to attenuate oxidative stress in vitro. Therefore, to gain deeper insight into the molecular mechanisms behind the therapeutic effects of Rg1 in vivo, this study examined the expression changes of key molecules within the PI3K/AKT pathway. The Rg1-500 group displayed higher PI3K and AKT mRNA levels than the control group (Fig. 6F). Moreover, protein expression levels of phosphorylated PI3K (p-PI3K) and phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT) were significantly elevated in the Rg1 group compared to the periodontitis group (Fig. 6D, F). Importantly, co-administration of the PI3K/AKT inhibitor LY294002 reversed the Rg1-induced upregulation of p-PI3K and p-AKT, indicating that activation of this pathway was suppressed. These results collectively suggested that activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is essential for the osteoprotective function of Rg1 against periodontitis-induced bone loss.

Discussion

Periodontitis is a major focus in both basic and clinical dentistry, and has increasingly drawn attention from multidisciplinary fields due to its bidirectional association with systemic diseases [28]. The progression of periodontitis involves a complex biological process, characterized by dysregulated inflammatory responses, disrupted bone immunity, and microbial dysbiosis marked by the overgrowth of pathogens such as P. gingivalis, often resulting in irreversible alveolar bone destruction [29]. Recent studies have confirmed the protective effects of ginseng extracts, including Rg1, on periodontal tissues [30]. However, the underlying mechanisms by which Rg1 acts its effects in periodontitis remain unclear.

In this study, an experimental periodontitis model was established in rats by ligation around the first molar teeth combined with inoculation of Porphyromonas gingivalis. The effect of different concentrations of Rg1 on periodontitis was evaluated for the first time. Histological analysis using H&E and TRAP staining showed that the number of inflammatory and osteoclast cells was significantly reduced in all Rg1-treated groups compared to the periodontitis group (p < 0.05). Among these, the Rg1-500 group showed the greatest reduction in both inflammatory cells and osteoclasts. Therefore, 500 µg/kg was selected as the optimal dose for follow-up experiments. This concentration also falls within the reported safety range [31]. Besides, we found that both lower (250 µg/kg) and higher (1000 µg/kg) doses showed attenuated efficacy. This phenomenon of an “optimal intermediate dose” suggests that Rg1 may have a narrow therapeutic window in the treatment of periodontitis. Such a dose-response pattern aligns with the reported nonlinear or hormetic effects of Rg1 in other systems [32, 33], suggesting that careful dose determination is crucial for its therapeutic application.

Recently, Rg1 has been shown to regulate bone immune homeostasis by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway and promoting osteogenic differentiation in zebrafish models [34, 35]. Notably, our study found that Rg1 inhibited the expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β and increased the secretion of the transforming growth factor TGF-β1, consistent with its known anti-inflammatory effects in other chronic inflammation models [36, 37]. Topical administration of Rg1 also significantly upregulated the gene and protein levels of osteogenic markers OCN and RUNX2 (p < 0.05), accompanied by a reduction in osteoclast numbers. This may explain the mild alveolar bone loss observed by micro-CT after Rg1 treatment. Together, these results indicate that Rg1 alleviates periodontal tissue destruction by modulating the bone immune microenvironment and restoring the balance between bone resorption and formation. Furthermore, in vivo experiments showed that Rg1 treatment further increased the phosphorylation of PI3K/AKT compared to the periodontitis group (p < 0.05), whereas the corresponding mRNA levels did not show a parallel elevation. This suggests that Rg1 may activate PI3K/Akt signaling primarily through post-transcriptional mechanisms [38]. Co-treatment with LY294002, a pan-PI3K inhibitor, reversed the protective effect of Rg1 against inflammatory bone loss, confirming the involvement of this pathway. These results are consistent with a recent study, which demonstrated that kaempferol reduces alveolar bone loss and rescued impaired osteoblast differentiation by upregulating EphrinB2 and activating the PI3K/AKT/p38 pathway [39]. Taken together with our study and the aforementioned literature, we propose that Rg1 may exert anti-periodontitis effects in the periodontal microenvironment by activating PI3K/AKT to improve host immunity and maintain bone homeostasis, and ultimately provide a novel reference for the pharmacological mechanism and new drug development of Rg1 in the treatment of periodontitis.

TLR2 is primarily located on the membrane surface of host immune cells. As a key pattern recognition receptor for the pathogenic factor of Porphyromonas gingivalis, it plays a crucial role in mediating inflammatory bone destruction in periodontitis [40]. It is generally recognized that TLR2 resides upstream of the PI3K/AKT pathway. This study revealed that local administration of Rg1 elevated PI3K/AKT phosphorylation levels while markedly reducing TLR2 expression. Consequently, we hypothesize that p-PI3K/p-AKT may exert negative feedback to suppress TLR2 expression. Following the addition of the PI3K/AKT inhibitor LY294002, the suppression of TLR2 and bone-protective effects by Rg1 were reversed, further suggesting the existence of a feedback loop within the TLR2-PI3K/AKT signaling axis. The effects of ginsenosides on the PI3K/AKT pathway are bidirectional and highly context-dependent. Research on the mutual influence of TLR2 and PI3K/AKT in the periodontitis microenvironment remains limited [41]. Within these constraints, this study demonstrates that Rg1 may exert therapeutic effects against periodontitis by influencing bidirectional regulation between PI3K/AKT and TLR2, offering a potential strategy for modulating the bone-immune microenvironment.

Modern medicine recognizes that the development of periodontitis is primarily driven by dysregulation between microbial pathogens and host immunity [42]. This study focused on the host immune response to periodontitis induced by P. gingivalis and local ligation, as well as the therapeutic effects of Rg1 intervention. Previous studies have confirmed the osteogenic efficacy and safety of Rg1 in both in vitro and in vivo models [24, 43]. Moreover, our earlier work demonstrated the good biocompatibility of Rg1 and its capacity to promote tissue repair by modulating the immune microenvironment [44, 45]. Collectively, these findings support the therapeutic potential and clinical translational prospects of Rg1, providing a stronger theoretical foundation for the application of ginsenosides in periodontitis treatment.

This study has some limitations. First, the rat model of periodontitis used in this experiment does not fully replicate the pathological progression of human periodontitis [46]. Second, although this study demonstrated that ginsenosides inhibit bone resorption by osteoclasts and promote bone formation by osteoblasts, further studies are required to clarify their role in enhancing the interaction between osteoclasts and osteoblasts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that local administration of ginsenoside Rg1 attenuates experimental periodontitis by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway and downregulating TLR2 expression, thereby restoring immune-bone homeostasis. This study not only elucidates a novel mechanism of Rg1 but also supports its potential as a promising host-modulatory agent for periodontitis treatment.
