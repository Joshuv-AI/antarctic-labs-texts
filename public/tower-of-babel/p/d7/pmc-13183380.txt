A Multicenter Phase II Study on Atezolizumab plus Bevacizumab Combination Therapy in Patients with Unresectable Hepatocellular Carcinoma and Child-Pugh Classification B Cirrhosis: CHALLENGE Trial
Authors: Takeshi Terashima, Masafumi Ikeda, Taro Yamashita, Takuji Okusaka, Hiroshi Imaoka, Tomokazu Kawaoka, Satoshi Kobayashi, Sadahisa Ogasawara, Atsushi Hiraoka, Masahito Nakano, Naohiro Okano, Rie Sugimoto, Michihisa Moriguchi, Yoshitaka Inaba, Satoru Shinoda, Makoto Ueno, Masatoshi Kudo, Junji Furuse, Hiroaki Nagano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAtezolizumab plus bevacizumab combination therapy (atezo + bev) has been successfully developed as a standard of care for patients with unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and Child-Pugh A; however, there is no established treatment for those with Child-Pugh B. This study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of atezo + bev in patients with advanced HCC and Child-Pugh B.MethodsThis multicenter, open-label, single-arm, phase II study enrolled patients with advanced HCC and a Child-Pugh score of 7 or 8 who had not received prior systemic therapy. Patients were administered atezolizumab (1,200 mg) plus bevacizumab (15 mg/kg) every 3 weeks. The primary endpoint was the frequency of serious adverse events (SAEs), assuming a threshold SAE of 68.5%. A binomial test with a one-sided significance level of 2.5% was employed for the primary analysis.ResultsAmong the 31 patients enrolled between December 2021 and April 2023, we set 30 eligible patients as the population for the safety and efficacy analyses. The frequency of SAEs was 23.3% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 9.9–42.3, p < 0.0001). Objective response rates according to RECIST v1.1 and the modified RECIST were 40.0% and 46.7%, respectively. The median progression-free and overall survival were 240 days (95% CI: 176–526 days) and 470 days (95% CI: 256–576 days), respectively. The mean ± SD Child-Pugh scores were 7.2 ± 0.4 at the start of treatment, maintained 7.0 ± 1.0 and 7.0 ± 1.2 at the third and sixth cycles, respectively, and was 7.6 ± 1.7 at the end of treatment.ConclusionThe atezo + bev regimen can be safely administered with demonstrable antitumor effects in patients with unresectable HCC and a Child-Pugh score of 7 or 8. Additional studies are warranted to confirm the usefulness of atezo + bev in this patient group.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is well-known to commonly develop in a background of chronic liver disease, and impaired liver functional reserve secondary to chronic liver disease is another critical factor that significantly affects patient outcomes [1]. Successful development of antiviral therapy suppresses the progression of fibrosis and consequently contributes to the preservation of liver functional reserve in certain patients [2]. Moreover, expansion of indications to transplant patients with mildly impaired liver functional reserve is expected to increase the number of patients receiving radical treatment [3]. Despite advances in these various areas, liver functional reserve may deteriorate in patients with more advanced HCC stage [4], possibly due to progressive tumor burden directly affecting liver function or gradual worsening of the background liver due to repeated locoregional treatment [5].

Even after curative treatment for HCC, patients frequently experience multicentric recurrence and intrahepatic and extrahepatic metastases. Consequently, recurrent HCC becomes refractory to locoregional therapies because of intrahepatic vasculature invasion or extrahepatic metastasis [6]. In the last decade, randomized phase III trials have proven the efficacy of several multikinase inhibitors as first-line treatment for such patients with preserved liver functional reserve [7, 8]. However, these treatments have yet to be regarded as the standard of care for patients with impaired liver functional reserve because of safety concerns and lack of clarity regarding efficacy [9, 10]. Subsequent studies have demonstrated the efficacy of three combination immunotherapies, such as atezolizumab plus bevacizumab (atezo + bev) [11], durvalumab plus tremelimumab [12], and ipilimumab plus nivolumab [13], over these multikinase inhibitors. However, these clinical trials included only patients with preserved liver functional reserve, and the benefit in patients with impaired liver functional reserve remains unclear.

In a phase I/II study that evaluated the efficacy and safety of nivolumab monotherapy in patients with advanced HCC, the primary endpoints of objective response rate (ORR) and duration of response were 12% and 9.9 months, respectively, and the incidence of adverse events was similar to that in patients with Child-Pugh A [14]. Unfortunately, nivolumab is not widely available, because its superiority over sorafenib could not be verified in a phase III trial on patients with advanced HCC and preserved liver functional reserve [15]. On the other hand, atezo + bev was demonstrated to be efficacious in the IMbrave150 trial and has been established as the standard of care for such patients [9]. Therefore, we considered atezo + bev to be one of the promising development candidates for patients with impaired liver functional reserve, namely Child-Pugh B. This study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of atezo + bev in patients with advanced HCC with Child-Pugh B.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patients

This open-label, single-arm, prospective phase II trial (CHALLENGE trial, jRCTs031210355) comprised two parts. The feasibility confirmation part was planned to assess tolerability to atezo + bev among the first 6 patients with Child-Pugh B. If treatment-related serious adverse events (SAEs) occurred in ≤3 patients, atezo + bev was considered tolerable, and we moved to phase II part of the trial. In the phase II part of the trial, the primary analysis to assess the primary endpoint was scheduled at 6 months after enrollment of the last patient. The final analysis was scheduled to assess the secondary and other endpoints after a sufficient period of follow-up to evaluate overall survival (OS). The study protocol was approved by the National Cancer Center Hospital East Certified Review Board (CRB3180009), granted permission by the directors of each participating institution, and conducted in accordance with the international ethical recommendations of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients were provided with comprehensive information about the risks and benefits of these treatments, including the known safety and efficacy, and they provided written informed consent before study enrollment. This study was conducted at 16 Japanese hospitals that were participating in the Japan Oncology Network in Hepatobiliary and Pancreas. The eligibility criteria were patients with pathologically confirmed or radiologically diagnosed HCC who were unsuitable for local therapy; had a Child-Pugh score of 7 or 8; no history of hepatic encephalopathy within 3 months prior to enrollment, no or mild ascites, total bilirubin ≤3.0 mg/dL, and prothrombin time international normalized ratio ≤2.3; were aged ≥20 years; had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status of 0, 1, or 2; had one or more measurable lesions; did not previously receive systemic therapy; had preserved major organ function; were expected to survive for at least 12 weeks; and provided written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were patients who had double cancer, active infection, fever >38°C, or autoimmune disease; were pregnant or lactating or had childbearing potential; were taking antiplatelet or anticoagulant agent; had past history of encephalopathy within 3 months, moderate or massive ascites, esophageal or gastric varices with high risk of bleeding, central nervous system metastases, interstitial pneumonitis, pulmonary fibrosis, or severe emphysema; had severe complication, unstable angina, or myocardial infarction within 6 months; and were regarded by the investigator as unfit for enrollment.

HCC was diagnosed according to radiological findings of hyperattenuation in the arterial phase and hypoattenuation in the late phase on triple-phase contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or gadolinium ethoxybenzyl diethylenetriamine penta-acetic acid-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), following the guidelines of the American Association for the Study of Liver Disease [16]. All patients underwent CT scan of the chest and whole abdomen to assess the extent of HCC.

Treatment

Treatment comprised atezolizumab (atezo) 1,200 mg plus bevacizumab (bev) 15 mg/kg every 3 weeks, which was based on the treatment schedule used in the IMbrave150 trial [11]. No dose reduction of both agents was permitted. If grade 3 or 4 toxicity was observed, either or both drugs was postponed until the predefined criterion of resolution to grade ≤1 was met. Treatment was continued until radiological or clinical tumor progression, unacceptable toxicity, patient refusal, or death.

Assessment and Data Collection

The physiological and laboratory findings were assessed during each patient visit. The albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) score, which is a simple assessment criterion for liver functional reserve, was calculated as previously reported [17]. For safety assessment, treatment-emergent adverse events were evaluated according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events ver. 5.0. SAE was defined as any adverse event occurring at any dose that resulted in death, life-threatening illness, hospitalization or prolongation of current hospitalization, persistent or significant disability/incapacity, congenital anomaly or birth defect, or a medically important event.

For efficacy assessment after treatment initiation, we used dynamic CT or MRI every 6 weeks until 24 weeks and 9 weeks thereafter. The antitumor effects were assessed by the investigators according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) ver. 1.1 and modified RECIST [18, 19]. ORR was defined as the sum of the complete and partial response rates. Disease control rate (DCR) was defined as the sum of the rates of complete response, partial response, and stable disease. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the duration from the date of enrollment until tumor progression or death. Time to progression (TTP) was defined as the duration from the date of enrollment until tumor progression. OS was defined as the duration from the date of enrollment until death. OS was censored at the time of the last follow-up if there was no documentation of death.

Statistical Analysis

The primary endpoint was the frequency of SAEs. The secondary endpoints included the ORR, PFS, TTP, OS, frequency of adverse events, and trends in Child-Pugh and ALBI scores during treatment. Assuming a threshold SAE of 68.5%, an expected SAE of 42.0%, in the primary analysis detection power of 80%, and one-sided α error of 2.5%, the minimum required sample size was 27 patients; therefore, we planned to enroll 30 patients. Sample size calculations and assumptions were based on data of safety profiles from GIDEON [20], observational study evaluating safety of sorafenib in a largest cohort, and IMbrave150 trial [11].

The frequency of SAEs during the treatment period was analyzed as the primary endpoint. The main decision was made in the primary analysis based on the frequency of SAEs evaluated using exact binomial methods with a one-sided significance level of 2.5%, based on a predefined threshold of 68.5%. The frequency of SAEs was evaluated also in final analysis as with other adverse events. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for the frequencies was also calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method. Adverse event frequencies were summarized using descriptive statistics.

ORR and DCR were summarized with 95% CIs calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method. PFS, TTP, and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Median survival times were reported with 95% CIs calculated by the Brookmeyer and Crowley method, and survival rates at 6, 12, and 18 months were accompanied by 95% CIs estimated using Greenwood’s formula. Changes in Child-Pugh and ALBI scores were illustrated using frequency tables and Sankey diagrams. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 software.

Results

Feasibility Confirmation

Study enrollment started on October 1, 2021, and the first 6 patients were enrolled for feasibility confirmation. In the feasibility confirmation part, two SAEs (i.e., lung infection and hepatic failure) occurred in 1 patient and were considered to be not associated with the protocol treatment. Because the predefined criteria were met, the date and safety monitoring committee judged that atezo + bev was tolerable, and we proceeded to phase II part of the trial.

When data collection terminated on August 26, 2024, 28 (93%) patients had discontinued treatment because of disease progression (n = 12), adverse events (n = 9), patient refusal (n = 3), death (n = 3), and other reasons (n = 1). The median follow-up duration was 517 days (95% CI: 443–638 days). The adverse events leading to treatment discontinuation were gastric ulcer, pneumonitis, worsening general condition, hyperammonemia, spinal fracture, hypothyroidism, malaise, proteinuria, and bleeding esophageal varices in 1 patient each (Table 2).

Adverse Events

The primary analysis revealed that the frequency of SAEs was 23.3% (95% CI, 9.9%–42.3%) (Table 2), with the upper limit of the 95% CI being significantly lower than the predefined threshold (p < 0.0001). In the final analysis, SAEs occurred in 30.0% (95% CI: 14.7%–49.4%) and included esophageal varices (n = 2), gastric ulcer (n = 2), hepatic failure (n = 1), lung infection (n = 1), anorexia (n = 1), encephalopathy (n = 1), pneumonitis (n = 1), upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage (n = 1), and peritoneal infection (n = 1). Grade ≥3 adverse events occurred in 14 patients (46.7%), and the most frequent were blood bilirubin increased (n = 3), proteinuria (n = 3), hypoalbuminemia (n = 2), and hypertension (n = 2). Based on RECIST ver. 1.1 and modified RECIST, the ORRs were 40.0% (12 of 30, 95% CI: 22.7%–59.4%) and 46.7% (14 of 30, 95% CI: 28.3%–65.7%), respectively, and the DCRs were 80.0% (24 of 30, 95% CI: 61.4%–92.3%) and 80.0% (24 of 30, 95% CI: 61.4%–92.3%), respectively (Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of atezo + bev in patients with advanced HCC with Child-Pugh B. As a result of the primary analysis, the primary endpoint was achieved based on the predefined hypothesis. In addition, efficacy results including an ORRs of 40.0% and 46.7% based on RECIST ver1.1 and modified RECIST, respectively, a median PFS of 240 days, and a median OS of 470 days could indicate that atezo + bev is well tolerable and effective in such patient.

Originally, the selection and inclusion of patients in whom treatment efficacy could be maximized were important for the efficient development of new agents in clinical trials. In this context, there were two concerns regarding the inclusion of patients with HCC and Child-Pugh B [21]. First was the expected poor prognosis in such patients. The causes of death in patients with cirrhosis are not limited to tumor progression. Unfortunately, various complications of cirrhosis, including the liver failure, esophageal variceal hemorrhage, compromised status, and encephalopathy, are often fatal or seriously affect treatment continuation [22]. In a previous systematic review, the estimated 1- and 2-year survival rates were worse at 80% and 70%, respectively, in patients with Child-Pugh B, despite the absence of HCC, than the 95% and 90% rates, respectively, in patients with Child-Pugh A [1]. In addition, nonliver-related mortality events can affect OS and occur at the same frequency between the control and new agent groups, thereby, masking the antitumor effect of the new agents and making developments less successful. Another concern was the potential impact of decompensated liver function on pharmacokinetics and safety profiles. In a pharmacokinetic study on lenvatinib for patients with HCC, different recommended doses were given because of the significantly higher blood concentrations in those with Child-Pugh B than in those with Child-Pugh A [23]. Several prospective studies investigating the efficacy and safety of sorafenib reported more frequent SAEs and liver-related adverse events and inferior OS and PFS in patients with Child-Pugh B than in those with Child-Pugh A [20, 24–27]. A recent prospective, observational study (REFINE) that included patients with Child-Pugh B cirrhosis, evaluated the efficacy and safety of regorafenib and reported that the starting dose of regorafenib in clinical practice was lower and the OS in patients with Child-Pugh B cirrhosis was inferior to those with Child-Pugh A cirrhosis (median OS 15.5 vs. 6.3 months) [28]. Based on these findings, no agent development trials have been conducted for patients with Child-Pugh B, and a large unmet need for data to support the risk-befit balance in patients with Child-Pugh B remains [9].

The predominant prognostic factor in patients with decompensated liver cirrhosis, whether it is tumor progression or liver failure related to underlying liver disease or treatment toxicity, has long been discussed but remains a challenging issue [29]. Notably, the situation is gradually changing. There has been a growing understanding of the importance of maintaining liver functional reserve during systemic treatment [30, 31]. In addition, recent advances in assessment and innovative interventions for liver cirrhosis complications have steadily improved the prognosis and quality of life of patients [32–35]. Supportive care can provide sufficient survival time for the antitumor effects of treatment and make patients with Child-Pugh B an evaluable population. In addition, previous concerns on the safety impact of impaired liver function have been reduced because the main systemic treatment has shifted from tyrosine kinase inhibitors to immunotherapy using antibody agents [36], which have pharmacokinetics that are independent of liver function. This study indicated that the liver functional reserve appeared to be maintained throughout the treatment period because of these factors. In addition, the cause of death for most of the patients enrolled in this study (14/18 patients) was tumor progression, which has rarely been addressed in retrospective studies.

The results of this study suggested that atezo + bev has a good safety profile in patients with Child-Pugh B. In the primary analysis, the upper limit of the 95% CI of the primary endpoint of SAEs was significantly lower than that of the predefined threshold. Furthermore, the frequency of SAEs in this study was comparable with that in patients with preserved liver functional reserve in the IMbrave150 trial [11]. However, the frequency of adverse events with special interest was noteworthy. For example, grade ≥3 and liver-related adverse events were more frequent in this study than in the CheckMate 040 cohort 5 phase I/II study on nivolumab monotherapy for patients with Child-Pugh B (24% and 6%, respectively) [14]. In particular, the SAEs of esophageal variceal hemorrhage, gastric ulcer, encephalopathy, and upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage that occurred in this study were suspected to be related to the addition of antivascular endothelial growth factor antibody to the immune checkpoint inhibitor. In addition, the grade ≥3 liver-related adverse events of blood bilirubin increased, esophageal varices hemorrhage, liver failure, and portal vein thrombosis were also occurred in this study, and they are assumed to occur more frequently in patients with Child-Pugh B than in patients with preserved liver functional reserve because these events are complications of impaired liver function. Based on the results of this study, administration of atezo + bev, especially bev, to such patients would require an additional caution, including consideration of performing an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy for following esophagogastric varices or gastrointestinal mucosal injury.

The efficacy results in our cohort were better than expected and comparable with those in patients with preserved liver functional reserve in the IMbrave150 trial. The reasons for this were unclear, but good management of liver functional reserve is one possibility. Some retrospective studies and meta-analyses showed inferior PFS and OS in patients treated by atezo + bev with Child-Pugh B than in those with Child-Pugh A [37–40]. They considered that TTP could minimize mortality due to the underlying liver dysfunction without tumor progression, showed that atezo + bev achieved comparable TTP across all Child-Pugh classifications, and poor PFS and OS in patients with Child-Pugh B disease may be attributed to the impact of liver dysfunction on mortality [37]. Conversely, none of the patients in this cohort died of liver dysfunction without tumor progression; this can explain the consistent results between TTP and PFS and the preserved liver functional reserve during treatment. Previous studies have reported the efficacy of tyrosine kinase inhibitors in patients with Child-Pugh B cirrhosis with a median PFS ranging from 2.1 to 6.9 months and median OS ranging from 3.2 to 7.7 months [20, 23–28]. The results of the current study showed a median TTP and median PFS of 240 days and a median OS of 470 days, demonstrating the superiority of atezo + bev compared with TKIs, even in patients with preserved liver function. Interpretation of these results should take into account the effect of different selection biases between this prospective study and previous retrospective real-world studies. Although definitive conclusions cannot be drawn from the results of a single-arm study, this novel successful experience indicated that preservation of liver functional reserve was an important strategy for improving the outcomes of atezo + bev therapy in this cohort mostly composed of patients with Child-Pugh score 7. Of note, we excluded patients with a Child-Pugh score of 9 from this study at the study planning stage because of tolerability concerns. Therefore, the safety and efficacy of atezo + bev in patients with a Child-Pugh score of 9 remain unclear. Other limitations included the relatively small sample size restriction to a Japanese population, selection bias, and short observation period. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, this was the first prospective study to evaluate the safety and efficacy of atezo + bev in patients with advanced HCC with Child-Pugh score 7 or 8 based on a predefined hypothesis.

In conclusion, this phase II study showed that atezo + bev was well-tolerated and had demonstrable antitumor effects in patients with advanced HCC with Child-Pugh score 7 or 8. Further investigations are warranted to confirm the outcomes of atezo + bev in patients with impaired liver function.
