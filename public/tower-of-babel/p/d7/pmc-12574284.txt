Development of a prediction model to estimate short- and long-term clinical outcomes in arteriovenous grafts for hemodialysis patients
Authors: Bin Zhao, Shen Zhan, Lihong Zhang, Hui Wang, Gang Fu, Shanshan Guo, Yuzhu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prognosis and quality of life for patients undergoing hemodialysis (HD) are significantly compromised by dysfunctions in arteriovenous grafts (AVG). This study aimed to develop and validate a risk prediction model for factors influencing both short- and long-term primary patency rates in HD patients with AVG.MethodsThe single-center retrospective study comprised 354 HD patients who had undergone AVG creation. Using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and Cox regression models, we evaluated the patency rates of AVG and identified associated independent risk factors based on retrospectively collected clinical data.ResultsThe primary patency rates of the patients with AVG at 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years were 82.49%, 50%, and 16.95%, respectively. The multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that the brachial arterial blood flow and draining venous diameter at 1 month post-operatively were independent predictors of newly created AVG patency at 6 months. Furthermore, at 1 month post-operatively, the brachial artery blood flow, draining vein diameter, and history of central venous catheter (HR 0.480 (0.281, 0.820), p = 0.007) were independent risk predictors for failure of newly created AVG at 12 months.ConclusionThis study revealed that these parameters, including brachial arterial blood flow, draining vein diameter, and history of central venous catheter, could be applied to predict short- and long-term risks for AVG patency loss.Keywords: Hemodialysis, Arteriovenous graft, Patency rate, Prediction model, Prognosis

Introduction

Hemodialysis serves as the primary treatment modality for end-stage renal disease, with vascular access constituting its essential lifeline [1–3]. While the autogenous arteriovenous fistula (AVF) represents the gold standard, a substantial proportion of patients, particularly the elderly, diabetics, and those with exhausted peripheral vasculature, require arteriovenous graft (AVG) placement due to high AVF maturation failure rates [4–8]. Nonetheless, AVG is plagued by inferior long-term patency, with one-year primary patency rates as low as 25% in contemporary reports, primarily attributable to stenosis and thrombosis [9, 10]. These complications not only compromise dialysis efficacy but also contribute significantly to hospitalization rates and healthcare burdens [11, 12].

Current evidence regarding predictors of AVG failure remains inconclusive. Although factors such as forearm location, female sex, and cardiovascular disease have been implicated in AVF failure [13–15], no consistent clinical or demographic predictors have been established for AVG dysfunction in multicenter settings. This knowledge gap persists despite well-documented pathophysiological mechanisms, particularly neointimal hyperplasia at the graft-vein anastomosis. We therefore propose a central hypothesis: the long-term patency of AVG is predominantly determined by the functional capacity of the venous outflow tract, which can be accurately assessed through early postoperative hemodynamic and morphological parameters. This hypothesis challenges the conventional view of AVG failure as unpredictable, instead positioning it as a consequence of measurable venous characteristics and their hemodynamic sequelae.

To test this hypothesis, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to identify independent predictors of AVG patency and develop a validated prediction model. We specifically investigated whether early postoperative measurements of brachial artery blood flow and draining venous diameter, potential surrogates for venous outflow capacity, could reliably stratify patency risk. Furthermore, we constructed a clinical nomogram to translate these predictors into an actionable tool for personalized risk assessment and management.

Patients and methods

Study design

This research study included 354 patients treated with AVG surgery from August 2018 to March 2022. All the patients were supervised monthly, tested with color Doppler ultrasound, and interviewed via telephone. The potential laboratory and clinical indicators were extracted from electronic medical records (EMRs) and historical paper records from the Beijing Haidian Hospital (Haidian Section of Peking University Third Hospital), where this retrospective, single-center study was conducted.

Inclusion criteria: (1) patients diagnosed with stage 5 or 5D chronic kidney disease (CKD), (2) all patients received initial AVG placement in the forearm, (3) patients had receipt of ultrasound monitoring in the first months after AVG placement, (4) patients received standardized ultrasound examinations before surgery, (5) poor-quality native vessels identified on preoperative ultrasound, including severe arteriosclerosis or vessels with inadequate diameter for AVF creation, (6) preoperative ultrasound confirmation of: anastomotic vein diameter ≥ 3 mm with patent venous outflow; anastomotic artery diameter ≥ 3 mm; (7) absence of active local or systemic infection in the patient. Exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) individuals under 18 years of age, (2) individuals treated with both peritoneal dialysis and hemodialysis, and (3) individuals unable to participate regularly.

Surgical Procedure: The procedure was performed under local infiltration anesthesia with preoperative prophylactic antibiotics (Cefuroxime Sodium 1.5 g; Clindamycin Phosphate 1.2 g). Expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (e-PTFE r = 6 mm) grafts (W.L. Gore & Associates) were used in all cases. The arterial anastomosis was created at either the elbow or upper arm brachial artery, while the venous anastomosis was performed at the median cubital vein, basilic vein, or brachial vein. Intraoperatively, the target vessels were adequately exposed and dissected approximately 2 cm in length for anastomosis. The graft was tunneled through subcutaneous tissue using a dedicated tunneler device, forming a U-shaped loop configuration. End-to-side anastomoses were performed using CV-7 suture, with the venous anastomosis completed first, followed by the arterial anastomosis. Upon completion of the anastomoses, blood flow was reestablished with intraoperative thrill confirmed by palpation. No routine postoperative antiplatelet or anticoagulation therapy was administered.

Follow-up Protocol: Following AVG creation, patients underwent routine follow-up at 1 month postoperatively, with subsequent follow-ups scheduled every 2–4 months based on clinical findings. Follow-up assessments included physical examination and Doppler ultrasound evaluation. Arterial measurements were obtained 2–4 cm proximal to the arterial anastomosis in the brachial artery, while venous measurements recorded the minimum internal diameter of the outflow vein within 5 cm distal to the venous anastomosis. All measurements were performed by a dedicated vascular access surgeon who also participated in the original procedure. Three consecutive measurements were obtained in the short-axis plane and averaged for analysis.

Predictive factors of AVG patency after its placement

Demographic variables included comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and vascular disease, as well as age, smoking status (categorized as ever vs. never), sex, and duration of dialysis. Laboratory assessments comprised measurements of albumin (ALB), serum calcium, hemoglobin (Hb), and serum phosphorus, with test results obtained within three days prior to surgery. Vascular characteristics were documented, including the type of AVG vessel anastomosis and catheter positioning. Postoperative ultrasound data were collected one month following surgery, encompassing brachial artery blood flow (F1), brachial artery diameter (B1), anastomotic diameter of the graft-vein (V1), draining vein diameter (D1), and anastomotic diameter of graft-artery (A1).

Standards and definition

The definition of AVG dysfunctions: (1) Thrombosis: lack of thrill or murmurs on physical examination or imaging results. (2) Significant hemodynamic stenosis necessitating endovascular intervention is characterized by a local stenosis rate exceeding 50% of the adjacent normal vascular diameter and a normal internal fistula blood flow of less than 500 mL/min, a reduction of the blood flow required for the dialysis prescription, difficulty in puncture, elevated dialysis venous pressure, decreased dialysis adequacy, and internal fistula anomalies.

Primary patency: the interval from access creation to any intervention for maintaining or restoring patency. The primary objectives included monitoring all participants until the occurrence of AVG dysfunction, kidney transplantation, death, loss of follow-up, or study endpoint. The main endpoint event was the initial instance of AVG dysfunction occurring within 6 to 12 months post-surgery. In survival and Cox regression analyses, death, renal transplant, loss of follow-up, or AVG patency at the end of follow-up was considered truncated data.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical assessments were carried out via SPSS v26.0 and R v4.1.2 software. The original data has no missing values and does not require statistical imputation for missing data. Univariate analysis was carried out for primary patency of AVG at 6 and 12 months to estimate the associations of patient demographics, dialysis parameters, preoperative biochemical examinations, and postoperative vascular ultrasound findings with AVG dysfunction at 6 and 12 months. Furthermore, the risk factors affecting AVG dysfunction identified in the univariate Cox models were also analyzed using multivariate Cox models to identify independent risk factors influencing the outcome. The area under the ROC curve was used to identify the risk factors that could predict AVG dysfunction. Statistical significance was attributed to a p-value less than 0.05.

Primary patency rate of AVG at 6 months

At 6 months, 62 patients indicated dysfunction. Of those, 17 had occlusion (27.42%), and 45 had stenosis (72.58%). Furthermore, the smallest internal diameter at the narrowest stenosis point was < 1 mm in 5 of the 45 patients with stenosis (11.11%). Moreover, 19 patients (42.22%) indicated single-site stenosis, while 26 (57.78%) had complex stenosis. In addition, single-site stenosis was observed in 8 cases (42.11%) due to graft-venous anastomosis, whereas in 11 cases (57.90%), it occurred because of a draining vein. Complex stenosis patients indicated the presence of graft-venous anastomosis stenosis and draining venous (Fig. 3).

Independent predictors of AVG patency with univariate Cox regression analysis at 6 and 12 months

Univariate analyses revealed that AVG patency for 6 months was independently correlated with brachial arterial blood flow (HR 0.998 (0.997, 0.999), p < 0.001) and draining venous diameter (HR 0.625 (0.483, 0.808), p < 0.001) at 1 month post-operation. These findings suggest that early postoperative hemodynamic and morphological conditions are critical factors influencing short-term AVG patency. For every 1 ml/min increase in brachial artery blood flow at 1 month postoperatively, the 6-month patency rate of AVG increased by 0.2%. Similarly, each 1 mm increase in outflow vein diameter at 1 month postoperatively was associated with a 37.5% higher 6-month AVG patency rate, indicating that the diameter of the draining vein, as measured 1 month post-operation, serves as a strong and independent predictor for subsequent AVG patency. Furthermore, at 12 months, the AVG patency rate was independently correlated with brachial arterial blood flow at 1 month (HR 0.999 (0.999, 1.000), p < 0.001), draining venous diameter at 1 month (HR 0.808 (0.692, 0.942), p = 0.007), and history of central venous catheter (HR 0.474 (0.279, 0.805), p = 0.006) (Table 2). The sustained significance of these parameters at 12 months highlights their importance as indicators of long-term AVG function. For every 1 ml/min increase in brachial artery blood flow at 1 month postoperatively, the 12 month patency rate of AVG increases by 0.1%. Each 1 mm increase in the diameter of the outflow vein at 1 month postoperatively is associated with a 19.2% improvement in AVG patency at 12 months. Conversely, patients with a history of central venous catheterization exhibit a 52.6% reduction in 12 month AVG patency compared to those without prior catheterization, confirming that a history of central venous catheterization stands as an independent risk factor compromising long-term AVG patency.

Independent predictors of AVG patency with multivariate Cox regression analysis at 6 and 12 months

Multivariate Cox regression analysis, after adjusting for potential confounding factors, confirmed that AVG patency at 6 months remained independently associated with brachial arterial blood flow (HR 0.998 (0.997, 0.999), p < 0.001) and draining venous diameter (HR 0.639 (0.493, 0.827), p < 0.001) measured at 1 month post-operation. Specifically, each 1 mL/min increment in brachial arterial blood flow was associated with a 0.2% reduction in the risk of 6 month patency loss, while each 1 mm increase in draining vein diameter was associated with a 36.1% risk reduction.

At 12 months follow-up, the multivariate model further established brachial arterial blood flow (HR 0.999 (0.999, 1.000), p = 0.002), draining venous diameter (HR 0.798 (0.682, 0.933), p = 0.005), and history of central venous catheter (HR 0.480 (0.281, 0.820), p = 0.007) as independent predictors of AVG patency. The analysis demonstrated that each 1 mL/min increase in brachial arterial blood flow at 1 month was associated with a 0.1% decrease in 12 month patency loss risk, while each 1 mm increase in draining vein diameter was associated with a 20.2% risk reduction (Table 3). These multivariate findings strengthen the evidence that early postoperative hemodynamic and morphological parameters provide independent prognostic value for both short-term and long-term AVG outcomes, with the draining venous diameter demonstrating particularly strong predictive capacity across both timepoints.

The area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was evaluated for diagnostic ability

ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive utility of early postoperative parameters for AVG patency. For 6 month outcomes, both the 1 month draining vein diameter (AUC 0.629, 95% CI 0.576–0.679, p = 0.002) and brachial artery blood flow (AUC 0.664, 95% CI 0.612–0.713, p < 0.001) demonstrated statistically significant predictive capacity. The integration of these two parameters yielded a combined index with enhanced predictive value (AUC 0.695, 95% CI 0.568–0.672, p < 0.001) (Table 4, Fig. 4A). For 12-month patency prediction, the analysis revealed a distinct pattern. While the draining vein diameter alone showed limited discriminative ability (AUC 0.544, 95% CI 0.490–0.597, p = 0.152), brachial artery blood flow provided meaningful predictive information (AUC 0.590, 95% CI 0.537–0.642, p = 0.003). The history of central venous catheter also served as a significant predictor (AUC 0.556, 95% CI 0.503–0.609, p = 0.002). More importantly, composite models demonstrated improved performance, with the combination of catheter history and blood flow achieving an AUC of 0.621 (95% CI 0.568–0.672, p < 0.001), and the comprehensive three-parameter model reaching an AUC of 0.631 (95% CI 0.579–0.682, p < 0.001) (Table 5, Fig. 4B). The consistent statistical significance across multiple parameters and their combinations supports their collective value in AVG patency prediction. The observed improvement in AUC values with parameter integration underscores the complementary nature of hemodynamic measurements and clinical history in assessing AVG prognosis.

Construction of the predictive nomogram for AVG patency rate at 6 and 12 months

Based on the independent predictors identified in the multivariate Cox regression model, a nomogram was constructed to provide a practical tool for individualized prediction of AVG patency loss risk (Fig. 5). This nomogram quantifies and visually represents the contribution of each predictive factor, allowing for the calculation of a total risk score. The model integrates brachial arterial blood flow and draining venous diameter for 6 month prediction, with the additional incorporation of central venous catheter history for 12 month risk stratification. In clinical application, the nomogram enables risk quantification through a points-based scoring system. For example, in assessing 12 month primary patency risk, a patient with a brachial artery flow rate of 2400 ml/min would receive approximately 35 points, while a venous diameter of 6.0 mm at the anastomotic site would contribute 20 points. The summation of these individual scores (total 55 points) corresponds to predicted patency probabilities of approximately 88% at 1 year and 80% at 2 years. The developed nomogram successfully translates the identified prognostic factors into an accessible clinical tool, providing quantifiable risk estimates that support individualized patient management and follow-up planning. This model represents a practical application of our findings, demonstrating how early postoperative parameters can be integrated to inform clinical decision-making for AVG patients.

Discussion

Arteriovenous graft (AVG) remains the second-line option for hemodialysis access, particularly for elderly and diabetic patients with compromised vascular resources. At our center, we perform approximately 200 AVG creations annually to accommodate this growing patient population with challenging vascular conditions. However, it has a limitation of lower long-term patency and thus requires more frequent treatments for maintaining its patency, which results in a higher incidence of stenosis and thrombosis. Several studies have revealed that the primary patency for AVG at 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years is 58%, 33.9–90%, and 25.3–85%, respectively. Halbert et al. performed a systematic literature review and meta-analysis including 3381 AVG accesses (38 study arms) analyzing a timeframe from 2004 to 2019; the mean primary, primary-assisted, and secondary patency rates at 1 year were 41%, 46%, and 70% [15, 16]. The primary patency rates of the present study were 82.49%, 50%, and 16.95% at 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years, respectively, which were higher than previous reports.

Based on our findings, we propose that the long-term patency of arteriovenous grafts is significantly influenced by the functional capacity of the venous outflow tract, which can be assessed through early postoperative hemodynamic and morphological parameters. This concept is strongly supported by our data showing that brachial artery blood flow and draining venous diameter at 1 month post-operatively serve as independent predictors for AVG patency at both 6 and 12 months. Previous literature lacked the analysis of specific clinical or demographic features linked with AVG survival. Notably, no clinical factors—including age, body mass index, race, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, sex, or access location—have been reliably associated with early AVG failure in multicenter studies [16]. This contrasts with the well-established pathophysiology of AVG failure; experimental models and human studies have demonstrated neointimal hyperplasia at graft-vein or graft-artery anastomoses as the primary contributor to AVG stenosis. Research by Michael Allon and colleagues further confirmed that pre-existing vascular abnormalities (arterial medial fibrosis, intimal hyperplasia, and arterial calcification) may paradoxically reduce AVG failure rates. Our results provide substantial evidence for this concept through several key findings. Here, based on the multivariable risk adjustment of possible confounding factors, it was found that brachial artery blood flow (HR 0.998 (0.997, 0.999), p < 0.001) and draining vein diameter (HR 0.731 (0.573, 0.934), p = 0.012) at 1 month post-operatively were independent risk factors for patency at 6 and 12 months. These findings raise an important question: Does the preoperative venous status determine the fate of AVG by influencing these indicators? Although this study did not directly evaluate the preoperative venous features, the existing data strongly suggest this possibility.

The pathophysiological mechanism underlying our findings involves a complex interplay between venous anatomy and hemodynamic factors. Specifically, a smaller diameter of the drainage vein may reflect preoperative venous dysplasia or pre-existing lesions; reduced brachial artery blood flow may be a secondary manifestation of poor venous outflow tract. These morphological and hemodynamic alterations likely create a vicious cycle that exacerbates AVG dysfunction through two synergistic pathways. First, the presence of pre-existing vascular pathologies in the draining vein to which an AVG is anastomosed can accelerate neointimal hyperplasia and restrictive vascular remodeling, leading to early AVG failure. Second, the implantation of an AVG generates non-physiological oscillatory wall shear stress in the vicinity of the graft-vein anastomosis. This condition may contribute to the development of intimal hyperplasia and progressive luminal narrowing, ultimately leading to thrombosis and loss of graft patency. Additionally, high blood flow can maintain normal endothelial cell function through laminar shear stress (where the direction of blood flow aligns with the direction of shear stress), reducing endothelial injury and intimal hyperplasia. The diameter of reflux veins may also influence reflux resistance, generating oscillatory shear stress, which can damage endothelial cells and lead to intimal hyperplasia.

Stenosis most commonly occurs at the venous anastomosis, but it can also be detected in the central vein, draining vein, feeding artery, or within the AVG itself [17–19]. The present study revealed that the most frequent locations of stenosis were graft-vein anastomosis (51.72%) and draining vein (48.28%), consistent with the above studies. The observed phenomenon may be attributed to the pronounced severity of neointimal hyperplasia (NIH) at the graft-vein anastomosis, which is exacerbated by aberrant blood flow and non-physiological wall shear stresses in the venous region [20–22].

These findings collectively substantiate our central hypothesis that venous outflow tract characteristics fundamentally determine AVG patency outcomes. The observed hemodynamic disturbances appear to be significantly amplified by pre-existing venous wall abnormalities, revealing a crucial interplay between intrinsic venous properties and acquired hemodynamic stressors. This pathophysiological understanding not only validates our hypothesis but also establishes a mechanistic framework for interpreting AVG failure patterns.

The critical importance of venous outflow capacity is further evidenced by the strong predictive value of postoperative surveillance. Given that most thrombosed grafts develop from underlying stenosis, our findings align with previous studies emphasizing the value of clinical monitoring [23–25]. We demonstrate that systematic ultrasonography surveillance following surgery provides essential data for predicting AVG patency, corroborating the reports by Krivitski, Gantela et al. [26]. The predominance of duplex ultrasonography as the preferred imaging modality stems from its unique ability to provide both anatomical and hemodynamic assessment. Clear ultrasound criteria enable precise differentiation between evolving and borderline stenoses, allowing early detection of lesions that could progress to AVG thrombosis.

Our hypothesis gains additional support from computational fluid dynamics research [27–30], which demonstrates that strategic modifications to graft geometry or the graft–vein interface can generate favorable flow patterns and hemodynamic conditions. These engineering approaches, potentially reducing NIH incidence, are further validated by meta-analytical evidence suggesting that geometric optimization of the graft–vein interface enhances AVG patency [21, 31, 32]. This convergence of clinical observations and engineering principles strongly reinforces our proposition that venous outflow characteristics dictate AVG fate.

The detrimental impact of central venous catheterization provides compelling ancillary evidence for our hypothesis. Central venous stenosis, well-established as a factor reducing vascular access lifespan [33, 34], creates upstream resistance that compromises venous outflow capacity. Our multivariate analysis confirms that a history of central venous catheterization (HR 1.939, 95% CI 1.137–3.304, p = 0.015) independently predicts reduced 12 month patency, consistent with Shingarev et al.'s conclusions [35]. The association between ipsilateral central catheters and diminished cumulative access survival further underscores how pre-existing venous compromise adversely affects AVG outcomes.

While pharmacological interventions represent a potential avenue for improving AVG patency, current evidence remains mixed. Although conventional anticoagulants or dual antiplatelet therapy have failed to demonstrate efficacy while increasing bleeding risk, some regimens like adenosine diphosphate plus aspirin show modest benefits in reducing stenosis or thrombosis [35, 36]. The promising results with fish oil supplementation suggest potential avenues for future pharmacological strategies. However, the absence of systematic drug intervention data in our study highlights the need for prospective investigations with standardized medication recording and long-term follow-up to establish evidence-based pharmacological management.

The clinical application of our hypothesis is embodied in the developed nomogram, which translates theoretical concepts into practical tools for personalized risk assessment. By integrating key predictors—brachial arterial blood flow, draining vein diameter at 1 month, and central venous catheter history—this model enables clinicians to generate individualized patency probabilities for 6 month, 1 year, and 2 year intervals. Beyond its clinical utility for risk stratification, the nomogram serves as an effective educational instrument, helping patients visualize their personal risk profiles and make informed decisions regarding treatment options and follow-up adherence. The model's demonstrated capacity to discriminate between different risk strata validates its practical implementation for personalized AVG management.

This study has several important limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its single-center, retrospective design introduces potential selection bias and limits the generalizability of our findings. The lack of randomization and relatively small cohort size may also affect the robustness of our predictive model. While our results demonstrate good discriminative performance, external validation in larger, multicenter populations is essential to confirm their applicability across diverse clinical settings. To address these limitations, we are actively collecting prospective data from multiple centers, which will allow for rigorous external validation in future studies. Additionally, our analysis did not include “preoperative vessel diameter” as a potential predictor of AVG patency, which warrants further investigation. Ultimately, prospective, multicenter studies with standardized protocols are needed to strengthen the clinical validity of our findings and enhance their broader applicability.

Conclusions

In summary, this study establishes that the functional capacity of the venous outflow tract is a primary determinant of arteriovenous graft (AVG) patency. We identified brachial arterial blood flow, draining vein diameter at 1-month post-operation, and a history of central venous catheter as robust, independent predictors of short- and long-term AVG outcomes. These findings support the integration of early postoperative hemodynamic monitoring into clinical practice for the early identification and management of patients at high risk of AVG failure. The predictive nomogram developed herein translates these factors into a practical tool for personalized risk stratification, offering a novel strategy to improve AVG management. Ultimately, our work provides a comprehensive framework for understanding AVG failure, centered on venous outflow integrity. Future research integrating detailed preoperative venous mapping with longitudinal postoperative monitoring will be crucial to validate this paradigm and uncover opportunities for preemptive intervention.
