P waves marching through QRS during supraventricular tachycardia: What is the mechanism?
Authors: Hiroshi Mannoji, Koichi Nagashima, Yasuharu Matsunaga-Lee, Takeru Ikenaga, Masatsugu Nozoe, Nobuhiro Suematsu, Toru Kubota
Published: 2026

Introduction

Dual atrioventricular (AV) nodal physiology may substantially alter the ventricular response during supraventricular tachycardia through shifts between fast- and slow-pathway conduction. Under certain conditions, this interaction can mask the expected manifestations of Wenckebach conduction, resulting in the appearance of a relatively regular narrow QRS tachycardia. We present an unusual tachycardia in which P waves appeared to march through the QRS complexes without obvious dropped beats on the surface electrocardiogram.

Case report

An 81-year-old woman presented with palpitations. The 12-lead electrocardiogram recorded during narrow QRS tachycardia is shown in Figure 1. The P-wave rate was 187 beats per minute, and the QRS rate was 150–165 beats per minute, such that the P waves marched through the QRS complexes. Discussion

During the narrow QRS tachycardia, the atrial cycle length was shorter than the ventricular cycle length, with P waves appearing to overtake the QRS complexes. In addition, the ventricular cycle length varied despite a stable atrial cycle length. In such a situation, Wenckebach-type AV block would generally be expected. However, because no obvious dropped QRS complex was observed in this case, the diagnosis was somewhat challenging.

The tachycardia was reproducibly terminated by a low dose of adenosine triphosphate (2 mg) without occurrence of AV block (Figure 2). This observation suggests adenosine-sensitive atrial tachycardia (AT).Figure 2Termination of the tachycardia without atrioventricular block after injection of the low-dose adenosine triphosphate (2 mg).

Electrophysiological study demonstrated anterograde dual AV nodal physiology without evidence of preexcitation. An atrial extrastimulus induced the clinical tachycardia with an atrial cycle length of 334 ms. The earliest atrial activation (EAA) was observed in the His bundle region. During the tachycardia, gradual prolongation of the atrial–His interval was observed (Figure 3). In particular, the atrial–His interval prolonged by ≥50 ms at A5 and A6. This phenomenon suggests not only AT with a shift in anterograde conduction from the fast pathway (solid line) to the slow pathway (dashed line), but also that the slow pathway itself exhibits marked decremental properties. Furthermore, at A8, conduction block in the slow pathway occurred, but was immediately followed by recovery of fast pathway conduction at A9, thereby masking the overt AV block.Figure 3Intracardiac electrograms during the clinical tachycardia. After development of a 1:1 AV relationship with isoproterenol infusion, the diagnosis of reentrant AT was established based on a V-A-A-V response following ventricular overdrive pacing, and the last entrainment sequence showing an A2-H∗-V∗ sequence following atrial overdrive pacing from high right atrium (HRA) (Figure 4).1, 2, 3 The last entrainment sequence can be assessed only after orthodromic atrial capture is confirmed by demonstrating that A2, in addition to A1, is accelerated to the pacing cycle length. After confirmation of orthodromic atrial capture, the sequence of A2 and the last His (H∗) and right ventricular (V∗) electrograms accelerated to the pacing cycle length is analyzed. The A2-H∗-V∗ sequence was diagnostic of reentrant AT. Because this adenosine-sensitive reentrant AT originated from the perinodal region, direct ablation at the EAA site carried a risk of AV block. Importantly, the last entrainment sequence was key not only for the diagnosis of reentrant AT but also for determining the ablation strategy, which targeted the entrance of the slow conduction zone while avoiding ablation at the EAA site.4 Orthodromic capture of the EAA site during atrial overdrive pacing from the HRA suggested that the entrance to the slow conduction zone was located approximately 1 cm from the EAA site toward the pacing site in the HRA. Radiofrequency application at this site (the 12- to 1-o’clock position of the tricuspid annulus) immediately terminated the AT and rendered it noninducible. Successful elimination of the AT without ablation at the EAA site suggests that interruption of the slow conduction zone was sufficient to abolish the critical limb of the reentrant circuit. Not all adenosine-sensitive ATs are reentrant. In typical Wenckebach conduction, progressive prolongation of AV conduction is most pronounced at the beginning of the cycle, with the degree of R–R interval prolongation gradually diminishing until AV block occurs.5 However, in the present case, the decremental properties of the fast pathway, switching from the fast pathway to the slow pathway, and the marked decremental properties of the slow pathway itself counteracted the expected diminishing prolongation of ventricular intervals. This interplay effectively buffered variations in AV conduction time, maintaining a relatively constant ventricular cycle length despite ongoing Wenckebach conduction. Furthermore, because conduction over the slow pathway exceeded the tachycardia cycle length, recovery of fast pathway conduction at the time of slow pathway block made the AV block appear concealed. This phenomenon created the illusion of a relatively regular narrow QRS tachycardia without dropped QRS complexes, even though the underlying rhythm was AT with Wenckebach-type AV block.

Electrophysiologists should be aware that a relatively regular narrow QRS tachycardia with P waves marching through the QRS complexes may represent AT under the following conditions: (1) the presence of both fast and slow pathways, (2) marked decremental properties of the slow pathway, and (3) the slow pathway conduction time that exceeds the tachycardia cycle length.
