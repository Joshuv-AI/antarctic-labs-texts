Analysis of the clinical characteristics of warfarin-associated purple toe syndrome
Authors: Can Shi, Siyi Zhang, Songhua Chen, Ren Guo, Tian Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purple toe syndrome (PTS) represents a critical yet underrecognized complication of warfarin therapy. To characterize this condition, we conducted a retrospective analysis of all reported warfarin-associated PTS cases through April 30, 2025, without language restrictions. A total of 26 patients (17 male, 9 female) with a median age of 68 years were identified. Of these, 76.9% had pre-existing atherosclerotic disease and 66.7% showed elevated serum creatinine. Symptom onset occurred within three months of warfarin initiation in 53.9% of cases, with 84.0% exhibiting classic bilateral lower extremity involvement characterized by painful violaceous discoloration. Management strategies included warfarin discontinuation (88.5%), dose reduction (7.7%), renal replacement therapy (15.4%), LDL apheresis (11.5%), surgical or endovascular interventions (7.7%), and transition to alternative anticoagulant therapy. Notably, no cross-reactivity between anticoagulant classes was observed, and warfarin rechallenge was not categorically contraindicated in all cases. Outcomes were favorable in 69.2% of patients (symptom resolution), though 3.8% required amputation and 19.2% died within one year, suggesting this complication carries substantial morbidity despite therapeutic intervention. These finding suggest that warfarin-associated PTS warrants clinical awareness, particularly in elderly patients with pre-existing atherosclerotic disease and renal impairment. Early detection, warfarin cessation, and initiation of alternative anticoagulation appear important to help prevent irreversible outcomes.

Introduction

Warfarin, a cornerstone oral anticoagulant with seven decades of clinical use, remains widely employed in modern thrombosis management due to its well-characterized pharmacokinetics and reversible anticoagulant effect [1, 2]. Despite the increasing use of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), warfarin remains irreplaceable in patients with (i) mechanical heart valves [3, 4], (ii) antiphospholipid syndrome [5–7], (iii) atrial fibrillation and moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis [3, 4], and (iv) severe renal dysfunction [8, 9], owing to its unparalleled multivalent inhibition of vitamin K-dependent clotting factors (II, VII, IX, and X).

While warfarin provides essential anticoagulation, its use carries risks beyond bleeding complications, including rare but potentially severe complication purple toe syndrome (PTS). First described by Di Cato MA in 1975 among patients receiving oral anticoagulants [10], PTS represents a distinct form of cholesterol microembolization syndrome characterized by painful violaceous discoloration of the toes and potential tissue necrosis [11]. Despite its clinical importance, the incidence and clinical characteristics of warfarin-associated PTS have not yet been clarified. Most of the literature on warfarin-associated PTS is in the form of case reports. To understand the clinical characteristics of warfarin-associated PTS and to strengthen the prevention of adverse reactions of warfarin, we collected cases of warfarin-associated PTS for retrospective analysis.

Methods

Search strategy

We searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), VIP Database, and Wanfang Data from their inception through April 30, 2025, and identified literature regarding warfarin-associated PTS using the search terms ‘warfarin’, ‘coumadin’, ‘purple toe syndrome’, ‘blue toe syndrome’, ‘cholesterol microembolism’, ‘cholesterol embolization syndrome’ and ‘digital ischemia’. There was no language restriction. Review articles, commentaries, animal studies, duplicate reports, and non-full-text literature were excluded.

Data extraction

We used a self-designed table to extract the following patient characteristics: sex, age, nationality, indication, dosage, and duration of warfarin, underlying comorbidities, concomitant medications, clinical presentation, laboratory parameters (serum creatinine, coagulation profile), histopathological results (when available), treatments, and clinical outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as percentages (%), while continuous variables were presented as medians with ranges. The causality of warfarin-associated PTS was assessed using the Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction (ADR) Probability Scale.

Results

Patients’ information

The flow diagram of the study is presented in Fig. 1. A total of 23 articles [11–33] (26 patients) met the eligibility criteria upon full-text evaluation (Table 1). The baseline characteristics of these patients are summarised in Table 2. Of these, 9 (34.6%) were from the United States, 6 (23.1%) from Japan, 5 (19.2%) from United Kingdom, and 3 (11.5%) from China. The median age of the 26 patients (17 males and 9 females) was 68 years (range 61–75). Warfarin was used for atrial fibrillation in 8 patients (30.8%), venous thromboembolism in 7 patients (26.9%), coronary heart disease in 7 patients (26.9%), cerebral infarction or transient ischemic attack in 6 patients (23.1%), peripheral vascular disease in 4 patients (15.4%), cardiovascular disease in 2 patients (7.7%), and valvular heart disease in 2 patient (7.7%). Pre-existing atherosclerotic disease was present in 20 cases (76.9%). Hypertension (46.2%) was the most frequent comorbidity, followed by diabetes (19.2%), hypercholesterolemia (15.4%), chronic kidney disease (11.5%), and aortic aneurysm (11.5%).

Clinical features

The clinical characteristics of the 26 included patients with warfarin-associated PTS were summarized in Table 3. 53.9% of patients experienced symptom onset within 3 months of starting warfarin. All patients presented with purple toes, and 69.2% of cases involved pain. Twenty-five patients had documented laterality, of which 21 (84.0%) were bilateral, while unilateral involvement was equally distributed with 2 cases (8.0%) each on the left and right sides. Typical cholesterol crystals were found in all 13 patients with definitive biopsy results (50% of the total), yielding a 100% detection rate among reported biopsies. Based on the Naranjo ADR Probability Scale, the scores were distributed as follows: 25 patients (96.2%) scored 5 ~ 8 points (indicating a probable ADR), and 1 patient [20] (3.8%) scored 9 points (suggesting a definite ADR).

Laboratory tests

Of the 15 patients measured, 10 (66.7%) had serum creatinine levels above 110 µmol/L, including 7 cases (70.0%) with severe elevated creatinine (>250 µmol/L). Fourteen patients had documented International Normalized Ratio (INR) values, of which 8 (57.1%) had INR values between 2 ~ 3, and only 3 (11.4%) exceeding the therapeutic threshold of 3.0. Details are shown in Table 3.

Treatments and outcomes

The treatment and prognosis of the 26 included patients are summarized in Table 4. Twenty-three patients (88.5%) immediately stopped warfarin, and 2 (7.7%) decrease warfarin. Among them, 4 patients (15.4%) required hemodialysis or hemofiltration, 3 patients (11.5%) underwent LDL apheresis, and 2 patients (7.7%) experienced surgical or endovascular intervention. Statins was provided to 3 patients (11.5%), glucocorticoids to 2 patients (7.7%), low‑molecular‑weight heparin (LMWH) or unfractionated heparin (UFH) or Fondaparinux to 8 patients (30.8%), DOAC to 1 patient (3.8%) and aspirin to 4 patients (15.4%). Warfarin was restarted in six patients, resulting in recurrent purple toes in three (50%) and no recurrence in three (50%). Eighteen patients (69.2%) had favorable outcomes with improved purple toes, 1 patient [23](3.8%) required amputation of the right third and fourth digits, and 5 patients [11, 12, 24, 27, 32] (19.2%) died within 1 year.

Discussion

Warfarin remains a cornerstone of anticoagulation therapy for thromboembolic disorders, yet its association with PTS represents a clinically significant but underrecognized complication. Our study characterized warfarin-associated PTS as typically developing within weeks of therapy initiation, predominantly affecting male patients, elderly individuals, and those with pre-existing atherosclerotic disease. The syndrome presented with bilateral lower extremity involvement (without lateralization tendency) accompanied by characteristic painful violaceous discoloration. Notably, most cases occurred at therapeutic warfarin levels, and an elevated serum creatinine levels was observed in 66.7% of patients. Clinical management generally required warfarin discontinuation and transition to alternative anticoagulant therapy. Importantly, no cross-reactivity between anticoagulant classes was noted, and warfarin rechallenge was not categorically contraindicated in all cases.

The pathogenesis of warfarin-associated PTS primarily involves cholesterol microembolization secondary to anticoagulant-induced disruption of vulnerable atherosclerotic plaques [34]. Our study provides histopathological evidence supporting this mechanism: cholesterol crystals were identified in all 13 patients who had definitive biopsy results, yielding a 100% detection rate. The temporal association between warfarin initiation and symptom onset (typically within weeks) suggests rapid plaque destabilization may be the initiating event. Furthermore, our demographic findings - particularly the predominance of elderly patients with established atherosclerotic disease - further suggest an important role of pre-existing vascular pathology in this condition.

A critical finding in our study was the variability in warfarin dosages at PTS onset, with some patients developing symptoms even at subtherapeutic INR levels. This suggests that factors beyond anticoagulant intensity—such as individual vascular susceptibility or plaque instability—may influence PTS development. The rapid resolution of symptoms upon warfarin discontinuation in most patients further underscores the importance of early recognition and drug cessation.

The mechanism linking warfarin to PTS involves vitamin K antagonism. Warfarin inhibits vitamin K-dependent proteins, including matrix Gla protein (MGP), thereby promoting vascular calcification [35, 36]. This reduces vascular compliance and facilitates microvascular entrapment of cholesterol crystals. When atherosclerosis is present, a synergistic “double-hit” effect occurs: atherosclerotic plaque rupture releases cholesterol emboli, while calcified vessels lose compensatory vasodilation, collectively exacerbating end-organ ischemia [37, 38]. Unlike warfarin, which acts by inhibiting vitamin K-dependent synthesis of coagulation factors (II, VII, IX, X) as well as endogenous anticoagulants proteins C and S, DOACs such as dabigatran (direct thrombin inhibitor) and rivaroxaban/apixaban (direct factor Xa inhibitors) target single active coagulation factors without affecting vitamin K-dependent pathways [39]. Consequently, DOACs do not interfere with MGP function and have not been associated with cholesterol microembolization syndromes. Similarly, LMWH and fondaparinux, which potentiate antithrombin‑mediated factor Xa inhibition, do not affect vitamin K‑dependent proteins and are not known to cause PTS. Therefore, based on mechanistic reasoning, the absence of vitamin K antagonism may render DOACs and heparins safer alternatives for patients with pre‑existing atherosclerosis or prior warfarin‑associated PTS.

However, recent clinical evidence challenges this assumption for certain DOACs. A multicenter randomized controlled trial [40] involving 132 patients with atrial fibrillation on hemodialysis (mean follow-up 18 months) found that rivaroxaban was associated with similar progression of vascular calcification as warfarin, despite warfarin’s well-established pro-calcific effect. Our cohort study [41] of 1,527 patients identified rivaroxaban as an independent risk factor for vascular calcification, increasing its incidence, severity, and progression rate. Likewise, the IRIVASC trial [42] showed that rivaroxaban and warfarin had comparable effects on coronary calcification progression, consistent with our findings. Thus, while vitamin K antagonism is a key mechanism underlying warfarin-induced vascular calcification and PTS, direct factor Xa inhibitors like rivaroxaban may also exert pro-calcific effects through alternative pathways. The assumption that all DOACs are uniformly safer for patients at risk of calcification or PTS requires re-evaluation, and further studies are needed to clarify the differential effects among individual DOACs.

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample (n = 26) and lack of a control group restricted the analysis to descriptive summaries, preventing hypothesis testing, risk estimation, confounder adjustment, and any statistical inference. Consequently, we could not distinguish warfarin-related risks from those attributable to underlying conditions, concurrent illnesses, recent procedures, or chance. Second, heterogeneity in case definitions, reporting completeness, and management strategies limited meaningful subgroup comparisons. Given these constraints, our findings are exploratory and should serve primarily to guide the design of future large-scale prospective studies.

Conclusions

PTS represents a critical yet underrecognized complication of warfarin therapy. Clinicians should be aware of its potential occurrence, particularly in elderly patients with pre-existing atherosclerotic disease and elevated serum creatinine levels. Enhanced monitoring, early recognition, and prompt switching to alternative anticoagulants could be considered to help prevent irreversible tissue damage. Future research is needed to explore early biomarkers and optimal therapeutic strategies for high-risk populations.
