Microbial Profile Alteration as a Potential Biomarker and Therapeutic Target in Children with Celiac Disease: A Narrative Review
Authors: Fahimeh Sadat Mostafaei, Nikoo Hossein-Khannazer, Somayeh Jahani Sherafat, Asal Noruzpur, Amir Sadeghi, Mohammad Rostami Nejad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Celiac disease (CD) is an autoimmune disorder triggered by gluten ingestion in genetically susceptible individuals. While gluten exposure is a necessary environmental trigger, its interaction with the intestinal microbiota has recently been recognized as a critical factor in modulating the onset and progression of the disease. Emerging evidence suggests that compositional and functional alterations in the microbial community may contribute to CD’s pathogenesis by influencing intestinal barrier integrity, antigen presentation, and the balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory immune responses. Consequently, detailed characterization of microbial signatures holds promise for the identification of biomarkers for early diagnosis and development of microbiota-targeted interventions. This narrative review comprehensively analyzed observational human studies and controlled clinical trials to assess the role of microbiota in pediatric CD. Moreover, dysbiosis patterns across gastrointestinal niches in children with CD were compared and discussed. These findings have the potential for use in novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Celiac disease (CD) is an immune-mediated chronic enteropathy triggered by dietary gluten in genetically predisposed individuals and is characterized by intestinal inflammation and mucosal damage. Although specific human leukocyte antigen (HLA) haplotypes, particularly HLA-DQ2 and HLA-DQ8, are well-established genetic risk factors, their presence alone is insufficient to confirm CD development. These alleles occur in approximately 20–40% of Western populations, yet only a small fraction (≈1%) of carriers develop CD, indicating the essential contribution of non-genetic, environmental co-factors [1]. Emerging evidence suggests that the gastrointestinal (GI) microbiota is a key environmental modifier capable of shifting the immune response from tolerance to gluten reactivity. Recently, it has been hypothesized that microbial alterations contribute to the pathogenesis of CD. Dysbiosis, characterized by a reduction in beneficial commensals and an expansion of pro-inflammatory pathobionts, regulates CD through several interconnected mechanisms, including the direct modulation of gluten peptide immunogenicity via microbial enzymatic activity, disruption of intestinal barrier integrity, and activation of host immune responses that induce local and systemic inflammation (Fig. 1).

Notably, the GI microbiota is not a homogeneous entity; its composition varies substantially across different anatomical sites owing to regional differences in pH, oxygen tension, nutrient availability, and peristalsis. A critical unresolved question is which part of the GI niche plays the crucial role in the initiation and progression of CD, particularly in the pediatric population [2]. While numerous studies have analyzed the fecal microbiota in adults [34], data regarding age-specific microbial alterations and the dynamic interplay between the host genotype and the developing microbiome in children remain limited.

This narrative review discusses current evidence from human studies to evaluate the role of the microbiota in the pathogenesis of pediatric CD and assesses its potential as a target for novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. We systematically compared microbial profiles from multiple pediatric sample types, including saliva, duodenal mucosa, feces, and blood, to identify site-specific signatures of dysbiosis. Furthermore, we discuss how these insights can inform future microbiota-directed interventions for CD management in children.

SEARCH STRATEGY AND SELECTION CRITERIA

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify studies relevant to this narrative review. The PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases were searched for articles published between January 2000 and July 2024. The search utilized a combination of keywords and MeSH terms, including: “celiac disease,” “coeliac disease,” “children,” “pediatric,” “microbiota,” “microbiome,” “dysbiosis,” “gluten-free diet,” “probiotics,” “fecal microbiota transplantation,” and “biomarkers.” The inclusion criteria were as follows: original research or review articles, studies involving humans, a focus on pediatric populations, or containing a pediatric subgroup.

Oral microbial composition

Recently, the oral microbiota has emerged as a critical player in both health and disease, suggesting new possibilities for diagnosing certain conditions. Researchers have identified over 1,000 different types of bacteria inhabiting various regions of the oral cavity. Several strains of oral bacteria have been linked to the development of oral and dental diseases, while others have been implicated in non-oral conditions, such as GI cancer, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [5], heart disease [6], and pneumonia [7]. Although gluten in foods remains in the oral cavity for a short time, patients with CD experience oral dysbiosis. The oral microbiota plays a role in gluten metabolism and protects the oral mucosa by enhancing immune function [8]. Studies have suggested that certain bacterial genera, such as Rothia, Neisseria, Actinomyces, and Streptococcus, may be involved in the initial metabolism of gliadin within the oral cavity [89]. Additionally, Lactobacillus species exhibit a high ability to degrade gluten fragments; however, their abundance is significantly decreased in the saliva of children with CD compared to healthy children [10].

Dental plaque bacteria have been found capable of breaking down the immunogenic 33-mer α-gliadin peptide, potentially reducing its immunogenicity. However, other studies have offered different perspectives, suggesting that bacterial enzymes in the oral cavity may only partially degrade gluten, potentially increasing the concentration of immunogenic peptides. This partial degradation triggers inflammation in the intestinal tract. Both perspectives must be considered when evaluating the impact of dental plaque bacteria on gluten degradation and the subsequent immunological consequences [1112].

A study conducted by Francavilla et al. [10] analyzed the saliva of children with CD and compared it with that of healthy controls. Their findings indicated significant differences in the composition of certain bacterial groups, specifically the Firmicutes and Actinobacteria to Bacteroidetes ratios, as well as their metabolites. These differences may serve as microbial signatures of CD. Interestingly, this study revealed that even after adherence to a strict gluten-free diet (GFD) for >2 years, the salivary microbiome of patients with CD did not fully normalize. Patients who adhered to the GFD exhibited a high frequency of Bacteroidetes and a low frequency of Actinobacteria in their saliva. Among Actinobacteria, Rothia mucilaginosa was the most abundant species in children adhering to the GFD. Conversely, the levels of Corynebacterium durum and species of Actinomyces and Atopobium were reduced in children with CD adhering to a GFD [10]. Moreover, Veillonella parvula, which belongs to the phylum Firmicutes and is associated with oral health [13], was more abundant in the saliva of healthy controls [10]. Another study of the oral microbiota in 14 Saharawi children with CD with an African-style GFD revealed a significant increase in the levels of Firmicutes, Tenericutes, and Actinobacteria compared to children with an Italian-style GFD and healthy children [14]. These results suggest that the type of GFD can influence the microbial composition in patients with CD.

Senicar et al. [15] showed a partial overlap in salivary gluten-degrading microorganisms between healthy adolescents and those with CD. Another study by Acar et al. [16] found that despite a higher prevalence of dental enamel defects and recurrent oral aphthae in children with CD, the abundance of Streptococcus mutans and lactobacilli in their saliva was significantly decreased.

However, existing studies on the salivary microbiota in children are limited, making it challenging to draw definitive conclusions. Further research is required to explore the potential of the salivary microbiome in the diagnosis and treatment of CD. This will require a detailed analysis of the oral microbiota and an investigation into strategies for correcting dysbiosis in patients with CD.

Fecal microbial composition

Understanding the intestinal microbial composition and its functions in host immune regulation and preservation of the intestinal epithelial layer is vital for diagnosing and developing treatments for immune-mediated GI conditions, such as CD [17]. Fecal samples represent the colonic environment, which harbors diverse and abundant microbiota.

Several studies have indicated that the intestinal microbiota of patients with CD differs from that of healthy individuals. However, the exact roles of these microbial differences in CD’s development and manifestation remain unclear. Limited research has been conducted on the frequency of specific microbiota and their relationship with CD symptoms in children at the time of diagnosis and after GFD adherence. For instance, a study of pediatric patients with CD with abdominal pain found a higher prevalence of Bacillaceae and Enterobacteriaceae. In contrast, patients with diarrhea exhibited a lower frequency of Clostridium clusters XIVa and Akkermansia, along with an increase in Bacillaceae and Fusobacterium [18]. Another study reported no association between the microbial composition and symptom presentation. This study also showed that patients with CD on a GFD had a lower frequency of fiber-degrading bacterial genera such as Lactobacillus, Prevotella, Blautia, and Dorea than healthy individuals [19]. A study by Colladeo et al. [20] showed that Bacteroides and Clostridium leptum were increased in the fecal samples of pediatric patients with CD compared to healthy children, regardless of the pathological stage, whereas Bifidobacterium spp. were lower in both patient groups (active and under GFD). Studies have reported that some genera belonging to Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium exert anti-inflammatory effects in the host intestine, but children with CD have lower levels of these bacteria compared to controls [20]. Furthermore, the frequencies of E. coli and Staphylococcus were higher in fecal samples from non-adherent patients than in those from controls. However, these levels normalized following a GFD [21]. Moreover, a cohort study of children susceptible to CD conducted before disease development showed an increase in pro-inflammatory bacteria and a decrease in anti-inflammatory bacteria over time [22].

Duodenal microbial composition

Several studies have reported increased microbial diversity in duodenal biopsies from pediatric patients with CD [2123]. Children with active CD exhibited an increased abundance of Proteobacteria and a decreased frequency of Firmicutes in duodenal biopsies compared to other pediatric groups (GFD and healthy) [24]. Collado et al. [21] determined that Bacteroides and Clostridium leptum were more prevalent in duodenal biopsies of children with CD. Furthermore, the abundance of E. coli and Staphylococcus was higher in patients with active CD than in controls [21]. Another study demonstrated that Enterobacteriaceae were dominant in the duodenal samples of children with CD. Mucosal inflammation can be exacerbated by members of the Enterobacteriaceae family, which function as pathobionts by releasing pro-inflammatory antigens and metabolites [18]. Moreover, infants at genetic risk of developing CD (HLA-DQ2 haplotype) showed a higher frequency of Enterobacteriaceae, indicating a potential role in disrupting gluten homeostasis [25].

An analysis of microbial diversity using DGGE banding patterns in 11 children with CD and six healthy controls showed no significant differences between the groups, although Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus were predominant in healthy controls. The study further revealed a 98% similarity in the intestinal microbiota of individuals with Marsh 3c lesions, which differs from other histologic lesion types such as Marsh 2, 3a, and 3b [26]. According to Nadal et al. [23], a higher prevalence of pro-inflammatory and gram-negative bacteria in children with CD was associated with symptom manifestations and might have been involved in the development of pathological changes. A key finding was the increased prevalence of Bacteroides and Escherichia coli in patients with active CD compared to healthy controls [23]. However, several studies have reported no significant differences in microbial diversity between patients with CD and healthy controls [272829].

There are few studies on the duodenal microbiota in children, and they often have small sample sizes owing to technical and ethical challenges. The invasive nature of sampling is a major challenge in evaluating the mucosal microbiota in children. Moreover, the validity of healthy pediatric duodenal microbiomes is questionable, as reference children were themselves being investigated for GI complaints.

Blood microbial composition

Although blood has traditionally been considered sterile, scientists have used 16S ribosomal RNA sequencing to identify bacterial DNA in blood samples of healthy individuals [3031]. Circulating microbial DNA is associated with chronic conditions, such as cardiovascular diseases, cirrhosis, diabetes mellitus, and CD [32]. Most studies linking the blood microbiota to GI diseases have focused on adult populations [3334]. However, analyses of blood microbiota have revealed clear compositional differences between pediatric and adult patients. Mehrotra et al. [35] observed that beta diversity was increased in the blood microbiota of pediatric patients with active CD compared with controls. In pediatric patients, the blood microbiota was dominated by Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria, a profile that was consistent across patients with active CD, those on a GFD, and the control group. Taxonomic analysis identified the depletion of Odoribacter at the genus level as a key differentiator between patients with active CD and healthy controls. The study also found that the bacterial families Odoribacteraceae and Helicobacteraceae were less abundant in the pediatric active CD group than in healthy controls [35]. Research on adult patients with CD revealed that the abundance of Gemellaceae was the highest in those on a GFD and was significantly reduced in the blood of patients with active CD [36].

The literature suggests that increased intestinal permeability in children with CD may allow the intestinal microbiota to translocate into the bloodstream, potentially altering the blood microbial composition. Consequently, the systemic circulation of dysregulated blood microbes may contribute to multiple extraintestinal manifestations in children with CD. Therefore, profiling the blood microbiome could reveal a distinct microbial signature that is potentially useful as a diagnostic biomarker for CD management.

MICROBIAL COMPOSITION AS A TARGET FOR THERAPEUTIC IMPLICATIONS

Dysbiosis has been documented in various GI disorders, including CD [33738]. Although associations between microbial imbalance and autoimmunity have been established, the specific mechanisms by which microbiota contribute to immune dysregulation remain underexplored. Advancing our knowledge of host-microbial immune crosstalk is critical for leveraging therapies, such as fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) [3940].

Current Standard Therapy (GFD) affects microbial composition

Diet plays a defining role in shaping microbial community structure and functional output within the GI tract [41424344]. Children with CD require adherence to specific dietary regimens, including a GFD [45]. A GFD is a standard recommended treatment for managing this condition [46]. Based on current evidence, there is a strong age-dependent response to the GFD in CD, with approximately 95% effectiveness in children after 2 years, compared with significantly lower effectiveness in adults aged 30–60 years [47]. GFD can modulate immune responses by regulating the balance of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines produced by peripheral T and B lymphocytes [48]. In addition, it may alter the composition and functional profiles of the gut microbiome. The gut microbiota of patients with CD following a GFD more closely resembles that of healthy patients than that of untreated patients [4950]. The specific composition of the GFD directly influences the microbiota. A study by Ercolini et al. [14] illustrated this by showing that a shift from an African-style to an Italian-style GFD remodeled the salivary microbiota, characterized by an increase in Neisseria, Porphyromonas, and Granulicatella and a decrease in Clostridium, Veillonella, and Prevotella [14]. Therefore, the impact of a GFD on bacterial richness appears to be context-dependent. While it may reduce inflammation and improve symptoms in patients with CD, it can also deplete beneficial bacteria and favor opportunistic pathogens in healthy individuals [515253]. Despite its efficacy, a GFD has limitations, including nutritional inadequacies and incomplete restoration of the healthy microbiome, prompting investigations into adjuvant therapies.

Microbial proteolytic enzymes can degrade gluten peptides

The limitations of a GFD have spurred research into alternative approaches, particularly the enzymatic degradation of gluten peptides [54]. One promising strategy involves the use of bacterial proteolytic enzymes to cleave immunogenic gluten peptides. Although prolyl endopeptidases from various microbial sources (Myxococcus xanthus, Sphingomonas capsulata, Aspergillus niger, and Flavobacterium meningosepticum) have been investigated, concerns regarding their toxicity have limited their application [5556]. Notably, proteolytic enzymes are produced by bacteria located at various sites in the GI tract. For example, the oral cavity is the first site of enzymatic gluten degradation. Key active species include Actinomyces odontolyticus, Neisseria mucosa, Rothia spp., and Capnocytophaga sputigena [957]. In the duodenum, species from the genera Lactobacillus, Actinomyces, Prevotella, Bacillus, Stenotrophomonas, and Pseudomonas produce gluten-degrading enzymes [58]. Caminero et al. [59] identified numerous bacterial strains in human stool samples with gluten proteolytic and peptidase activities against immunogenic 33-mer peptides, including various species of Clostridium, Bacillus, Staphylococcus, Bifidobacterium, and Enterococcus. However, as most immunogenic gluten reactions occur in the duodenum, the therapeutic relevance of enzymatic activity by the colonic microbiota remains questionable.

Although enzymatic approaches directly target gluten, other strategies aim to modulate the gut environment through microbial supplementation.

Probiotics target symptom-related dysbiosis

Several studies have demonstrated that patients with CD show a depletion of beneficial intestinal bacteria, specifically reduced abundances of anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory species within the Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus genera, concurrent with an elevated abundance of opportunistic species such as Bacteroides spp. and Escherichia coli compared with healthy individuals [60]. These microbial alterations may manifest as various clinical symptoms, including diarrhea, weight loss, abdominal pain due to malabsorption, and extraintestinal symptoms [61]. Few studies have specifically evaluated the relationship between bacterial abundance and symptom presentation in children with CD. A pediatric study of CD found that dysbiosis of the fecal microbiota, but not of the duodenal microbiota, was significantly associated with clinical symptoms, including diarrhea and abdominal pain [18]. Unlike the pediatric study, research in adults found that duodenal microbiome changes were linked to symptoms. Adults with CD experiencing GI symptoms or anemia showed reduced microbial diversity in duodenal samples compared with those with dermatitis herpetiformis. Moreover, the subgroup with GI symptoms displayed increased Proteobacteria levels and distinct microbiota profiles [62], as shown in Table 1. Over the past decade, researchers have extensively explored the potential advantages of probiotics. Probiotics have been shown to influence the intestinal barrier function and immune activity in children with CD. Clinical trials support their potential benefits, which include restoring microbiota balance, alleviating GI symptoms, improving GFD effectiveness, boosting the immune response, and increasing body mass index and anthropometric indicators such as weight and height [6364]. Probiotic supplementation, primarily involving Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus species, has shown potential in the management of pediatric CD. In a randomized controlled trial, Tremblay et al. [65] reported that a multi-strain probiotic significantly improved stool consistency and reduced GI symptoms, specifically bloating and abdominal pain, in children who remained symptomatic despite adherence to a GFD. Similarly, a large trial involving 170 pediatric patients with CD demonstrated that probiotics effectively reduced diarrhea [66]. The proposed mechanisms of action include restoration of healthy gut microbiota, degradation of gluten peptides, and immunomodulation. In vitro studies have shown that certain Bifidobacterium strains stimulate the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines and mitigate gliadin-induced toxicity. Clinically, supplementation with B. breve has been shown to help restore microbial balance and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α in children following a GFD [676869]. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study demonstrated that Bifidobacterium longum CECT 7347 improved the intestinal microbial composition and immune responses in 33 children following a GFD [64].

Immunological effects have also been observed for specific Lactobacillus strains. A double-blind trial indicated that daily administration of L. plantarum HEAL9 and L. paracasei 8700:2 significantly decreased IgA-tTG levels and modulated peripheral immune responses [70]. Notably, although one study reported no direct correlation between bacterial genera and tTGA levels, it reported a relative increase in Lactobacillus abundance following probiotic intake [71]. Despite evidence linking dysbiosis to CD development, the precise therapeutic role of probiotics, particularly in pediatric patients, remains to be fully established. Future research should focus on identifying optimal probiotic strains, dosages, treatment durations, and potential risks associated with long-term supplementation in children with CD.

More extensive microbiome restoration strategies, such as FMT, are emerging as promising areas of investigation for refractory or complex cases.

FMT restores intestinal microbiota imbalance

FMT, the transfer of healthy donor stools to restore the gut flora, is a recognized treatment for conditions such as recurrent Clostridium difficile infections [397273]. Its potential application in CD is currently under investigation [74]. Although a single case report noted symptomatic improvement in an adult with refractory CD and Clostridium difficile [75], major limitations and obstacles remain. The procedure is complex, and finding suitable donors is difficult because of microbiome differences between patients with CD and healthy individuals. In children, unsuitable transplants carry the risk of worsening dysbiosis, infections, and symptom flare-ups. Therefore, the safety and efficacy of FMT in pediatric CD should be evaluated before clinical adoption.

CONCLUSION

Intestinal microbiota has emerged as a critical focus of research over the past decade, with growing evidence linking GI dysbiosis to a spectrum of disorders, including CD. Current findings underscore a dual role of bacteria in CD pathogenesis. While certain bacterial taxa may promote inflammation by disrupting epithelial barrier integrity and increasing gluten peptide translocation, others exert protective effects through the enzymatic degradation of immunogenic gluten peptides. Nevertheless, the significant heterogeneity in study populations, methodologies, and sampling sites has thus far precluded the identification of a consistent predictive microbial signature for pediatric CD. Disentangling these complex interactions remains a key challenge, particularly in children. Precise characterization of microbiota alterations in pediatric CD could unlock new diagnostic and therapeutic avenues. Potential applications include the identification of microbial biomarkers for early detection, the development of targeted interventions such as gluten degradation by bacterial strains, and the production of new generations of probiotics. Furthermore, although this review focused on bacterial communities, the contributions of fungi, viruses, and other components of the microbiome to CD pathogenesis warrant further investigation to fully elucidate the role of the microbial ecosystem in this immune-mediated enteropathy.
