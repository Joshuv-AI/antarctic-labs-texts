Profiles, needs and satisfaction with healthcare among women with psychoactive substance use disorder in France: the Calliope mixed-methods study protocol
Authors: Adelaide Pladys, David Fonte, Deborah Loyal, Danièle Bader, Marie-Lou Stefanowski, Ilhame Anwar, Vincent Estellon, Perrine Roux
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite having more medical and social vulnerabilities than men with substance use disorder (SUD), women with SUD are underrepresented in addiction healthcare structures. This is because they experience double stigma—from society (including the medical community) and from themselves—which hinders them from accessing healthcare.Methods and analysisUsing a mixed-methods approach, the main objective of the Calliope study is to explore and compare healthcare satisfaction among women with SUD according to the category of healthcare structure they attend: specialised addiction centres versus primary care centres called ‘microstructures’. A total of 240 women receiving care for SUD will be included in the study, with equal representation from specialised addiction centres and ‘microstructures’. All participants will complete a standardised questionnaire to collect quantitative data. Additionally, a subsample of 40 women will be selected to participate in in-depth, semistructured interviews to gather qualitative data. Additionally, focus groups will be conducted with healthcare professionals to explore their perspectives and practices.Ethics and disseminationCalliope was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committee of Paris Cité University (IRB: 00012024–02). The study’s findings will provide a greater understanding of the characteristics, needs and satisfaction with healthcare of women with SUD living in France. Moreover, they will help guide the development of tailored therapeutic interventions for this population.

Introduction

Historically, men have been more likely than women to use psychoactive substances. In recent decades, however, this trend has changed, as women’s behaviours—especially among younger women—are becoming increasingly similar to those of men with regard to alcohol, cannabis and other psychoactive substance use.13 Although the gender difference in psychoactive substance use is decreasing, associated biological, social and psychological side effects are significantly different between both genders. In terms of biological side effects, hormonal fluctuations and metabolic differences in women lead to more severe health outcomes.13 Then, compared with men, women are significantly more likely to initiate substance use in response to trauma, psychological distress and psychiatric disorders such as post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety and depression.48 Experiencing physical, sexual or emotional abuse— which frequently occurs during childhood or within intimate relationships—also contributes significantly to substance use initiation in women.9

Moreover, women with substance use disorder (SUD) experience double stigma: first due to their substance use itself, and second, their behaviours do not reflect traditional hetero-patriarchal moral expectations of female gender roles.10 Specifically, women who use drugs are often viewed as less attractive, less hygienic and less capable of being good mothers. This stigmatisation not only comes from the general population (ie, social stigma), but also from women with SUD themselves (ie, self-stigma). Both these types of stigma significantly hinder their decision to access healthcare, and consequently, they hinder recovery efforts.81013 Moreover, these challenges are often exacerbated by social stigma from healthcare professionals. Despite the French National Plan on Addictions which prioritises the reduction of health inequalities for vulnerable populations, including women,14 there is still a considerable gap between these recommendations and both healthcare professionals’ training on addiction/SUD and their efforts to combat the stigmatisation of individuals who use drugs. Indeed, many healthcare professionals continue to see individuals with SUD (ie, women and men) in a negative light.15 All the above elements reflect why women are underrepresented in substance abuse treatment programmes compared with men.8 12

In France, various healthcare structures manage patients with SUD; these include specialised addiction centres (called Centre de Soins, d’Accompagnement et de Prévention en Addictologie or CSAPA), hospital addiction units and primary care centres which offer healthcare through an innovative service called ‘microstructures’. CSAPA provides free, anonymous, multidisciplinary outpatient care, including medical, psychological and social assessments, referrals and harm reduction services. Hospital-based addiction units offer varied services, ensuring continuity of care through interdisciplinary teams and providing both outpatient consultations and inpatient treatment for detoxification or complications. A microstructure comprises a multiprofessional care team (general practitioner, social worker and psychologist) offering medical, social and psychological follow-up to each patient in a general practitioner’s office.16 The team is centred on the general practitioner. In 2023, women with SUD represented an average of 23% of all patients attending specialised addiction services17 in France, and 50% of patients followed in microstructures.18

There is a lack of recent data on the profiles of women with SUD in France, including their characteristics and overall health status. Furthermore, although studies have explored patient satisfaction with SUD treatment,1922 none have specifically focused on women in primary care settings, including microstructures. This article aims to describe the innovative design of the mixed-methods study Calliope, whose objective is to explore and compare healthcare satisfaction among women with SUD in France according to the category of healthcare structure they attend.

Objectives

The primary objective of Calliope is to compare satisfaction with healthcare among women with SUD followed in two different categories of healthcare structure providing specialised care in addiction medicine: category 1: CSAPA & hospital addiction units; 2: primary care centres (ie, microstructures).

Methods and analysis

Multidisciplinary team

The originality of this study lies in its multidisciplinary approach, which incorporates experts in psychology and psychometrics (CRPMS team), epidemiology and social sciences (SESSTIM team), and its community-based participatory perspective. More specifically, the National Coordination of Microstructures Networks (CNRMS association) will be involved, as will women with SUD affiliated with a community-based association for people who use drugs (Nouvelle Aube association).

Study design and setting

Calliope is a prospective multisite study which uses a mixed-methods approach. Combining quantitative and qualitative data will provide us with a comprehensive understanding of the research outcomes. Specifically, quantitative data will provide statistical insights and information on the generalisability of results, while qualitative data will ensure that findings are based on context, meaning and experiences.23 Mixed-methods studies allow for triangulation, whereby results from the quantitative and qualitative components can be compared and validated against each other. Quantitative data will be collected using a questionnaire and qualitative data using semidirective interviews and focus groups. Data will be collected by two independent investigators over a 6 month period, from November 2024 to April 2025, in four French regions.

Population and eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria are as follows (a) female gender (cisgender and transgender), (b) age ≥18 years, (c) basic French proficiency, (d) at least 6 months of medical follow-up for SUD in the addiction healthcare structure (ie, whether category 1 or 2; see below), and (e) living in one of the four French regions included in the study (Occitania, Grand Est, Ile-de-France, Provence-Alpes-Côte-d’Azur).

Two groups of women with SUD will be distinguished according to category of healthcare structure they attend:

Category 1: centres specialising in addiction medicine (including CSAPA) and hospital addiction units).

Category 2: primary care centres (microstructures).

In the quantitative study, we aim to include 120 women per group to reach the final objective of 240 women. In the parallel qualitative study, 20 women from each group, recruited in the quantitative study, will be invited to participate in semi-structured interviews.

Patient and public involvement

The study, including the development of the questionnaire and interview framework, was designed in collaboration with members of a community-based association for people who use drugs. Their involvement was instrumental in refining the study materials to ensure relevance and clarity. Additionally, with their support, the questionnaire was tested on four women to assess its comprehensibility and applicability.

Quantitative study

Outcomes and variables

Satisfaction with healthcare

The primary outcome is participant satisfaction with healthcare; this will be measured in the survey using the Consumer Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ).24 The CSQ is a standardised tool used to measure client satisfaction with healthcare services. It was originally developed to assess the satisfaction of consumers in various health and mental health services, but has been adapted for other service settings. The CSQ provides feedback on how individuals perceive the quality, effectiveness and accessibility of the services they receive. Different versions exist depending on the context and length of the questionnaire. The 8-item CSQ (CSQ-8) is the most commonly used short version25 and it has been validated in the French language.26

Substance use patterns

To analyse participants’ psychoactive substance use behaviours, the survey questionnaire will also gather general information on both licit and illicit substance use (eg, substances, methods, dosage and frequency of use). Additionally, two standardised tools will assess nicotine and alcohol use: (1) the Fagerström Test for nicotine dependence; this six-item tool is widely used to assess the level of physical addiction to nicotine, and higher scores indicate greater dependence;27 (2) the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test-Concise (AUDIT-C); this is a three-item shortened version of the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT).28 The AUDIT-C is widely used in healthcare settings; higher scores indicate a greater risk of problematic alcohol use.

Health issues

To analyse healthcare trajectories, specific questionnaire items were developed to gather other information on participants’ including: (a) medical follow-up history, (b) the professionals they consult, (c) the medical services they attend, and (d) screening for infectious diseases.

In addition, the questionnaire will also contain specific items about sexual health, taken from the French national Baromètre survey,29 to assess the broader health implications of participants’ psychoactive substance use.

Quality of life

To assess quality of life, we will use the WHOQOL-BREF;30 this 26-item questionnaire, which assesses physical health, psychological health, social relationships and a person’s social environment, is a shortened version of the WHO’s Quality of Life questionnaire (WHOQOL-100).

Life events

Items focusing on negative life events (eg, gender-based and sexual violence, domestic violence and foster child placement) will also be included in order to assess any past history of physical or sexual violence.

Desired effects associated with substance use

The questionnaire will also include items on perceived effects of substance use, in order to assess the impact that substance use has on the lives of women with SUD (eg, pain management, anxiety reduction).

The questionnaire has already been tested on a sample of four women (two in microstructures and two in specialised addiction centres) and adjusted based on their feedback. The questionnaire will be completed on a dedicated tablet with a trained interviewer present.

Procedure

Prior to data collection, healthcare teams in each participating region will be invited to a presentation of the study. Study information will then be shared with eligible women through care teams and distributed flyers. Interested women may contact their healthcare provider or the study investigator directly to schedule an appointment. While there are no restrictions on the number of participants per structure, recruitment will aim for balanced sampling across settings and regions, with 120 women recruited from microstructures and 120 from specialised addiction, that is, a total of 240 participants equally distributed across the four study regions.

An information leaflet will be provided to each eligible woman with SUD invited to participate in the quantitative study. Women who agree will be included in the study after providing informed consent.

All collected data will be anonymised, and no identifying or confidential information will be included in the questionnaire. The interviewer will be available while the participant completes the questionnaire on the tablet, in order to clarify any items that are not understood. The interviewer will in no way influence the participants’ responses. In the event that completing the questionnaire brings up painful feelings or memories, the interviewer will address the participant to a relevant care professional or service.

At the end of the questionnaire, participants will receive a €15 gift voucher as a token of appreciation for the time spent answering the questions and for their contribution to the study.

Statistical analysis

First, participants’ characteristics will be described. Chi-square tests or means’ comparisons will be used to compare women with SUD attending category 1 structures with those attending category 2 structures.

The main study outcome is satisfaction with healthcare (CSQ-8). To study the association between participants’ characteristics, addiction care structure category and satisfaction with healthcare, we will perform univariate and multivariate analyses (logistical and/or linear regressions). A sample size of 120 women per group, for a total of 240 participants, will provide sufficient statistical power to detect meaningful differences between groups. Indeed, with 120 women per group (n=240), the study will have 80% power at a 5% significance level to detect a difference of approximately 20% between groups.31

Qualitative study

Two qualitative data collection methods will be used: (a) Semistructured interviews with women to enable an in-depth exploration of individual care trajectories, conducted in a setting that ensures safety, confidentiality and emotional comfort. (b) Focus groups with healthcare providers to foster interaction and shared reflection, making them well suited for examining collective practices and team dynamics.

Semistructured interviews

Semistructured interviews will encourage women to articulate and interpret their life experiences related to psychoactive substance use across several themes. The interviewer will use an ad hoc guide developed by the Calliope team to explore their: (a) history of substance use, reasons for using and personal impact of use; (b) healthcare experiences and perception of quality of care received; (c) social relationships and support systems; and influence of substance use on professional and sexual lives. Sensitive topics, such as experiences of violence or trauma, will be addressed with prior consent. The interview will also explore participants’ perspectives of perinatal healthcare received and their ideal care models, thereby providing insights into their needs and preferences.

Interviews will be audio recorded only if participants agree. Any potentially identifying information leaked during audio recordings will not be transcribed. The interviews will start with an unstructured phase featuring broad questions; this will encourage participants to talk in general about their substance use history. A semi-structured phase will then address more specific topics related to addiction and health.

Focus groups

Eight focus groups with healthcare professionals will be conducted.32 Focus groups are effective in fostering social interaction; they encourage participants to build on each other’s comments33 and address any disagreements or conflicts that emerge. Discussions will explore not only healthcare providers’ perceptions of women with SUD, including the latter’s health, behaviours and access to care, but also their own professional practices in terms of receiving and supporting these women. The focus groups will examine the professionals’ perspectives of the needs of women with SUD, the challenges they (ie, the professionals) face delivering care to this population, and the resources available to them to provide adequate care in their daily practice. By gathering these insights, the study will uncover systemic and institutional elements that may not be evident to the women themselves but which may influence their care more generally.

Moreover, by juxtaposing the perceptions of women with SUD, gathered through semistructured interviews, with those of healthcare providers from focus groups, we hope to highlight discrepancies in views regarding service accessibility, appropriateness and patient needs.

Procedure

Women participating in the qualitative component will be recruited from those who completed the quantitative questionnaire. Interested participants will be invited to take part in a semistructured interview. Recruitment will be equally balanced between specialised addiction centres and microstructures, and across the four study regions, until a total of 40 women is reached (ie, 10 per region, with 5 from each type of care structure). Quantitative and qualitative data collection will occur in parallel. An information leaflet outlining the qualitative study and participants’ rights will be provided to all eligible women.

Questionnaire and interview will be conducted on-site within the healthcare structure, depending on room availability and women’s schedules, and will be organised following a medical assessment.

At the end of the interview, each participant will receive an additional €15 gift voucher as a token of appreciation for their taking the time to return for another survey, and for sharing their experiences in greater depth.

Concurrently, eight focus groups with healthcare practitioners will be conducted—one in each structure type per region—with a maximum of eight participants per group. There will be four focus groups (ie, one in each region) for healthcare practitioners from microstructures (ie, general practitioners, psychologists and social workers) and four (ie, one in each region) for healthcare practitioners from specialised addiction centres (addictologists, psychiatrists, psychologists and social workers). Participation will be based on team interest and availability during fieldwork.

Triangulation

A thematic content analysis will be performed34 35 on the semistructured interviews and focus groups to examine key themes, such as women’s substance use trajectories, healthcare experiences, perceptions of care quality, as well as professionals‘’ perceptions of women with SUD.

Qualitative data will then be triangulated with the quantitative data from the questionnaires as part of a lexicometry analysis. The software package IRaMuTeQ will be used to conduct this analysis, in order to reveal key discourse themes as a function of a number of variables.36 37 A descending hierarchical classification, as proposed by Reinert,38 39 will be conducted to extract significant similarities and oppositions between the words from the corpus, with a view to exploring associations between women’s perspectives and variables such as socio-demographic factors and the category of healthcare addiction structure they attend.

Discussion

The Calliope study uses a mixed-methods approach with two key objectives: to assess satisfaction with addiction healthcare structures among women with SUD, who are currently receiving medical care, in France, and to investigate the sociodemographic and medical profiles, substance use behaviours and needs of this vulnerable population. A novel aspect of this study is its focus on the psychosocial context of drug use, including an investigation of participants’ access to care and their perceived benefits of substance use, a topic that has been relatively underexplored.

The study’s findings will provide healthcare professionals with valuable insights into how to provide tailor-made medical, psychological and social care which meets the needs of women with SUD. One very important element in this population is stigmatisation. Calliope will examine key moments in women’s lives, such as drug use initiation, motherhood and mental health issues, with a view to shedding light on how stigma affects their SUD treatment journeys. Our findings can be used to inform training programmes in stigmatisation for healthcare professionals and can be used to advocate harm reduction approaches in women with SUD, with the double aim of increasing access to care and the quality of care they receive.

Microstructures were developed based on the principle of harm reduction; studies on their effectiveness have highlighted positive results and the fact that women are more likely to engage with such programmes than with specialised care centres. Calliope will investigate why this is the case and will assess how the care offered may be adapted to better suit their specific needs.

In addition to evaluating the perspectives of women with SUD, Calliope will explore the perceptions and practices of healthcare providers regarding women they treat, focusing on their approaches to care, the challenges they encounter and their understanding of the specific needs and treatment barriers faced by these women. Comparing the perspectives of women with SUD with those of healthcare professionals will highlight perceptual differences, which in turn will inform the development of strategies to reduce prejudice and improve mutual understanding.

This multisite research study spans four French regions, each with distinct cultural and demographic characteristics that shape substance use patterns. For instance, the prevalence of opioid use and overdose-related deaths is high in the Grand Est region, while alcohol and tobacco consumption is higher in Occitania. In the Île-de-France region, the prevalence of psychoactive substance use is lower than the national average, but social precarity is a major issue. These regional differences are compounded by growing disparities in healthcare access, particularly in rural and underserved urban areas, affecting not only life expectancy but also quality of life and the prevalence of chronic diseases.40

However, the main limitation of this study is that it only includes women who are currently receiving care in addiction treatment facilities. Women who are not engaged in these structures will not be represented, preventing analysis of the reasons for their non-engagement or refusal of medical treatment. Nevertheless, the results of the Calliope study may help the development of targeted prevention and outreach strategies for women with SUD who are not currently engaged in healthcare structures.

Improving access to healthcare for vulnerable populations, especially women with SUD, is a major public health issue. Women with SUD often enter treatment with complex, damaged life pathways, including poly-drug use and limited previous engagement with healthcare.5 6 12 13 Calliope seeks to fill a critical gap in research, addressing the need for ongoing exploration of professional practices related to the care of women with SUD,13 with the ultimate aim of improving access to care and the quality of care in this vulnerable group.

Ethics and dissemination

All procedures performed were in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments. The project and data management process is governed by the ethics committee of Paris Cité University, which has given its approval for the study. All participants in the survey will receive an information leaflet. Written, informed consent is necessary to participate.

All relevant results will be published in peer-reviewed international scientific journals and presented at conferences, nationally and internationally.
