Multimodal image fusion guidance for the treatment of structural heart disease and aortic dissection in hybrid operation room: a single-center case series in a hybrid operating room
Authors: Liang Zhao, Jidong Liu, Zhiqing Qiao, Yi Qiu, Yan Zhou, Ji Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAngiography and transesophageal echocardiography are limited by the lack of providing detailed three-dimensional (3D) cardiovascular stereo-anatomy intraoperatively. Currently, numerous researchers have utilized advanced functionalities from various imaging manufacturers to integrate multimodal images for intraoperative navigation. Nevertheless, the clinical application of such techniques is often compromised by suboptimal registration accuracy, primarily attributable to positional changes of the patient across preoperative imaging and intraoperative settings. This study aimed to propose a novel multimodal image fusion method to guide cardiovascular interventions.MethodsIn this retrospective study, we introduce the image fusion method that integrates preoperative computed tomography angiography (CTA) and intraoperative cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) to improve 3D real-time fusion navigation in treating structural heart diseases and aortic dissection. Furthermore, this study provides a comprehensive description of the technical details for this method and retrospectively reviews the specific applications of this method in a single-center setting.ResultsSeven cases demonstrated the effectiveness of this technique in enhancing procedural accuracy and outcomes. In these cases, the 3D fusion roadmap provided important navigational information for minimally invasive procedures to accurately implant prosthetic valves and stents. All procedures achieved satisfactory outcomes with reduced fluoroscopic radiation exposure.ConclusionsThe multimodal image fusion roadmap offers detailed and stereoscopic guidance of cardiovascular minimally invasive treatments. It has great potential to help improve procedural efficiency and reduce complication rates.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases, such as structural heart diseases (SHDs) and aortic diseases, are becoming increasingly prevalent due to the aging population and the worsening of unhealthy lifestyles among citizens (1,2). In recent years, with the global dissemination of new minimally invasive techniques and the development of advanced medical equipment, the field of cardiovascular intervention has experienced rapid growth (3). In current clinical practice, intraoperative navigation for cardiovascular minimally invasive procedures typically relies on angiography, digital subtraction angiography (DSA), and fluoroscopy (4-6). However, these techniques are limited by the constraints of two-dimensional (2D) imaging and the invisibility of anatomical tissue. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is often required as an assisting tool during cardiac interventional procedures (7). Several studies have demonstrated the utility of TEE/fluoroscopy fusion images in guiding cardiac interventions (8-10). However, TEE-based fusion roadmaps usually offer restricted visibility and structural information, especially for less experienced cardiologists. In addition, some studies have applied computed tomography angiography (CTA)/fluoroscopy fusion guidance in thoracic endovascular repair (TEVAR) (11-14) and transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) (15) procedures to enhance visualization of cardiac and adjacent vascular anatomy. Building upon these studies, we further optimized the registration process of multimodal images and then applied the multi-volume technique to generate a more precise three-dimensional (3D) fusion roadmap in clinical practice.

The aim of this study is to introduce a multi-volume-based fusion method and demonstrate its application in 3D real-time fusion navigation in the treatment of SHD and aortic diseases. We present this article in accordance with the AME Case Series reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2024-2966/rc).

Methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by institutional review board of Renji Hospital (No. 102 K) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Preprocedural preparation

All patients underwent CTA imaging (SOMATOM Force, Siemens Healthineers, Germany; uCT 960+, United Imaging Healthcare, China) as a routine diagnostic examination upon arrival at Shanghai Renji hospital. CTA examinations were performed using an ABA injection protocol (contrast-saline-contrast), with contrast agent administered at 1.5 mL/kg adjusted according to body weight and a 30 mL saline flush in between, both injected at a flow rate of 4–5 mL/s. For cardiac imaging, the scan range extended from the level of the aortic arch to the diaphragm. For cardiovascular imaging, the scan range covered from the mandible to the greater trochanter of the femur. The scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage, 90 kV; slice thickness, 0.625 mm; slice interval, 0.5 mm; and gantry rotation time, 0.25 s per rotation. After the computed tomography (CT)-scans, the cardiologist made the clinical diagnosis and planned the procedure. The radiologist then performed the 3D CTA reconstruction with 1.0 mm-slice thickness and segmentation of the relevant cardiac anatomical structures based on the planned procedure. This step was completed for all cases on the CT post-processing workstation. The 3D CTA images and segmented image were uploaded to the hospital Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) and re-imported before the procedure.

Intraprocedural imaging

All procedures were performed in a hybrid operation room (HOR) equipped with a robotic C-arm angiography system (Artis Zeego, Siemens Healthcare, Forchheim, Germany). The radiologist loaded the segmented CTA image onto the DSA workstation (syngo X Workplace, Siemens Healthineers) and adjusted it in the coronal, sagittal, and axial planes [multiplanar reconstruction (MPR)] to identify and mark the calcification or soft tissue structures such as the vessel ostium, aortic sinus. All landmarks were annotated before the completion of pre-procedural anesthesia preparation. After anesthesia, a 10 s cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scan was performed, and the images were immediately reconstructed on the DSA workstation. Correspondingly, the radiologist also marked landmarks or regions of interest (ROIs) on the CBCT images.

Image registration and fusion

3D CTA and CBCT reconstructed images were fused (syngo 3D/3D fusion, Siemens Healthineers) on the DSA workstation. Initially, the algorithm identified bony structures such as the spine to achieve automatic registration. Subsequently, the radiologist performed more precise manual alignment by adjusting the images in three planes based on soft tissue structures, marked landing zones, calcification points and other anatomical landmarks. Typically, before manual adjustments, radiologist set the display color and transparency for the two imaging modalities to achieve clearer differentiation and better visual effect (Figure 1, Video S1). Following registration, the segmented CTA images were able to accurately fuse with the CBCT images. In further processing, another segmented 3D CTA image can be loaded onto the workstation to achieve 3D CTA to 3D CTA dual-volume image fusion. The fused 3D virtual model can then be overlaid with 2D fluoroscopic images to create a 3D real-time navigation roadmap. Finally, the radiologists evaluated the accuracy of the 3D fusion by rotating the C-arm and assessing it from multiple angles.

Illustration of the workflow for the 3D fusion roadmap. (A) Preoperative CTA: the CTA scan is a routine examination performed upon patient admission. After image reconstruction, the radiologist segmented the relevant cardiac regions and sent them to the DSA workstation. (B) Intraoperative CBCT: the radiologist performed 3D image reconstruction immediately after a 10-second CBCT scan. (C) Post-processing: the radiologist first marked the ROI on the CTA images, followed by initial automatic registration using the fusion function. Results

Case 1

A 77-year-old male with a history of transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement presented with recurrent dyspnea, chest discomfort, and significant lower limb edema without apparent triggers. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed severe tricuspid regurgitation. Preoperative TEE demonstrated existing tricuspid prosthesis failure with significant right atrial enlargement. After group discussion, a decision was made to proceed with the transcatheter tricuspid valve-in-valve replacement (TViV). Based on preoperative CTA images, the radiologist segmented the anatomical structures of the patient’s right atrium, right ventricle, and connected vessels. The soft tissues such as pulmonary veins and the pulmonary artery were considered as landmarks for registration and fusion. The overlaid roadmap (Figure 2A,2B, Video S2) clearly demonstrated that the position of the prosthetic valve in the segmented CTA image closely matched that in the fluoroscopic image, further confirming the accuracy of the prior registration. After optimal positioning under 3D roadmap guidance, the new prosthetic valve was accurately deployed. Postoperative TEE and angiography demonstrated that the newly implanted prosthetic valve was functioning well, with no regurgitation or paravalvular leak. The total procedural fluoroscopy time was 26.2 minutes, with a total cumulative dose-area product (DAP) of 25,816 µGym2.

Case 2

A 65-year-old female patient with a 19-year history of diabetes mellitus and over 10 years of atrial fibrillation had previously undergone mitral valve replacement. TTE revealed severe mitral stenosis and severe tricuspid regurgitation. After evaluation by the cardiology team, a decision was made to perform the transcatheter mitral valve-in-valve replacement (TMViV) procedure. Preoperative TEE indicated failure of the existing mitral bioprosthetic valve, characterized by stenosis and regurgitation. The radiologist registered segmented 3D CTA images with intraoperative CBCT images, and subsequently overlaid them onto fluoroscopic images. The overlaid images demonstrated alignment between the valve positions in the CTA and fluoroscopic images, which confirmed the registration was precise (Figure 2C,2D, Video S2). Under the guidance of the fused roadmap, the cardiologist successfully completed the atrioventricular septal puncture and valve deployment. Postoperative TEE and angiography showed that the new prosthetic valve functioned well (mean pressure gradient of 4 mmHg), with optimal positioning, no regurgitation, and no paravalvular leak. The total procedural fluoroscopy time was 29.8 minutes, with a total cumulative DAP of 25,262 µGym2.

Case 3

A 48-year-old male patient with a history of tetralogy of Fallot repair presented with lower extremity edema and anuria. Upon admission, TTE revealed severe pulmonary regurgitation, aneurysmal dilatation of the pulmonary artery, severe tricuspid regurgitation, and moderate mitral regurgitation. The patient underwent transcatheter pulmonary valve replacement (TPVR) under general anesthesia. The radiologist delineated the landing zone on a pre-segmented 3D model of the pulmonary artery (Figure 3A). The 3D model was then rotated to select the optimal DSA-gantry angle and procedural position. During the procedure, the cardiologist utilized the anatomical marker on the CTA model to guide the positioning and deployment of the balloon and prosthetic valve (Figure 3B-3D, Video S3). Postoperative angiography demonstrated the prosthetic valve was well-positioned with good function, and left coronary angiography indicated no compression of the left coronary artery (LCA). The total procedural fluoroscopy time was 24.0 minutes, with a total cumulative DAP of 12,057 µGym2.

Case 4

A 78-year-old female patient, with a history of percutaneous coronary intervention, was admitted with a diagnosis of severe aortic stenosis and calcification, accompanied by coronary artery atherosclerosis. The patient underwent transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) under general anesthesia. The radiologist segmented the left ventricle, aortic root, ascending aorta, and coronary arteries preoperatively. For the 3D segmentation model, the LCA ostium, right coronary artery (RCA) ostium, and aortic sinuses were color-traced to provide reference landmarks for the cardiologists during the implantation of the prosthetic valve (Figure 4A,4B, Video S4). Postoperative aortic root angiography and TEE demonstrated that the prosthetic valve was well-positioned without paravalvular leakage (mean pressure gradient of 5 mmHg). Coronary angiography showed no changes compared to preoperative findings. The total procedural fluoroscopy time was 34.1 minutes, with a total cumulative DAP of 41,917 µGym2.

Colored tracings were used intraoperatively as reference position for valve deployment to prevent coronary artery involvement. Case 5

An 80-year-old female patient presented with a 2-week history of recurrent mild chest pain in the precordial region. TTE revealed a thickened, calcified aortic valve with severe stenosis, while coronary angiography indicated mild to moderate coronary artery stenosis. Additionally, the patient had hypertension, poor baseline physical condition, and complex aortic anatomy, leading the procedural team to opt for transapical transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TA-TAVR). In transapical cases, the catheter, balloon, and prosthetic valve were all precisely positioned under the guidance of the fusion roadmap (Figure 4C,4D, Video S4). Postoperative angiography and TEE demonstrated proper positioning of the prosthetic valve, with minimal paravalvular leak and no regurgitation. The coronary angiography result was satisfactory and unchanged from preoperative findings. The total procedural fluoroscopy time was 25.6 minutes, with a total cumulative DAP of 36,763 µGym2.

Case 6

A 72-year-old female patient was admitted with severe mitral regurgitation detected by TTE, along with reduced left ventricular diastolic and systolic function, pulmonary hypertension, and moderate tricuspid regurgitation. Preoperative CTA indicated sclerosis of the aorta and coronary arteries in the patient. The patient underwent TEER under general anesthesia. Radiologists segmented the left heart and mitral valve leaflets based on preoperative CTA images as independent datasets, and then fused them using dual-volume techniques (Figure 5A). The fusion roadmap further highlighted the reference position and 3D visualization of the mitral valve during the procedure, thereby enhancing the guidance for clip-positioning (Figure 5B,5C). Post-release evaluation via TEE indicated the satisfactory closure morphology of the clip and a reduction in mitral regurgitation to mild, with the mean transvalvular pressure gradient of 5.4 mmHg (Figure 5D,5E, Video S5). The total procedural fluoroscopy time was 27.3 minutes, with a total cumulative DAP of 24,067 µGym2.

Case 7

A 73-year-old male patient was admitted for treatment after the detection of an aortic dissection (AD) during a routine health examination. Preoperative diagnosis identified a Stanford type B dissection with the entry tear located at the orifice of the left subclavian artery (LSA). Under general anesthesia, the patient underwent TEVAR and LSA stenting. The thoracic aorta and AD, also referred to as the “true lumen” and “false lumen”, were independently segmented and fused, then overlaid onto the fluoroscopic images (Figure 6A,6B). During the introduction of guidewire into the aorta arch or after its placement, contrast agent was injected to assess and confirm whether the guidewire was within the true lumen (Figure 6C). After stent graft deployment, angiographic images demonstrated that the contrast agent and stent were substantially overlapped with the “true lumen” in the segmented model, confirming the accuracy of this multimodal image registration (Figure 6D, Video S6). Subsequently, under the guidance of the 3D roadmap, the cardiologists completed the needle fenestration and deployment of the fenestrated stent graft via the left brachial artery access. Postoperative angiography demonstrated optimal stent positioning with satisfactory coverage of the dissection and good blood flow within the fenestrated stent in LSA. The total fluoroscopy time was 34.1 minutes, with a total cumulative DAP of 87,949 µGym2 and injected contrast agents of 90 mL.

The dual-volume CTA image consisted of a set of red “true lumen” and another set of blue transparent “false lumen” CTA images. (C) Intraoperative angiography confirmed that the contrast agent and catheter were both positioned within the “true lumen”. (D) Post-stent deployment angiography assessed the status of stent expansion and contrast agent flow (Video S6). 3D, three-dimensional; CTA, computed tomography angiography; TEVAR, thoracic endovascular repair.

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive review on the clinical application of 3D multimodal fusion roadmaps in the treatment for SHD and AD. Moreover, it innovatively proposes the usage details of multi-volume and volume rendering techniques to provide the operator with more stereoscopic and detailed intraoperative guidance. Since 2023, Shanghai Renji Hospital has applied image fusion techniques for intraoperative navigation, leading to the successful treatment of 132 patients. Compared with conventional 2D image-guided procedures, 3D fusion navigation can reduce fluoroscopy time by approximately 25–30%. Benefiting from the advantages of the HOR, including its surgical environment and equipment configuration, cardiologists can not only perform minimally invasive procedures via transcatheter and transapical approaches under fusion image navigation but also seamlessly transition to open surgery in the event of emergencies. In addition, the robotic C-arm combined with the iso-centric synchronized operation table (Artis OP Table, Siemens Healthineers) provided surgeons with the optimal procedure angles and positioning. Based on our single-center experience, we further believe that 3D fusion roadmap not only provides accurate navigation for patients with complex anatomical structures, but also offers significant advantages in the education and procedural guidance of junior operators.

In previous studies, several applications of fused image in cardiac intervention have been reported, including ultrasound-fluoroscopy fusion (9,10,16-18) and CT-fluoroscopy fusion (18-20). In these studies, the primary approach involved either directly fusing segmented CTA images with fluoroscopy or utilizing planning software to segment ROIs on intraoperative CBCT images, which were then overlaid onto fluoroscopy. In comparison, our proposed fusion method aligns the preoperative CTA and intraoperative CBCT images before overlay. This method enhances the accuracy of multimodal image registration while ensuring the high-quality of segmented images. In our further investigations, we referenced cases of bubble reconstruction in aortic (21), achieving valve reconstruction, which also appeared as low signal intensity on CTA images. Combined with multi-volume technique, the valves can be highlighted between the atrioventricular chambers or between the chambers and blood vessels, thereby allowing the novel fused roadmap to be more advantageous in valve interventional procedures. On the other hand, since the heart is an organ that is highly susceptible to deformation and spatial position change, a perfect multimodal registration is nearly impossible. Sometimes, it is necessary to make compromises in the registration area based on the procedural requirements. However, with the proposed registration method, the accurate registration of localized cardiac regions, such as the left atrial appendage and the left superior pulmonary vein, can be achieved.

There are several limitations in this study. First, the quantitative clinical benefits of this fusion method and comparative studies with control groups are necessary, requiring the inclusion of a larger number clinical cases and data. Second, the accuracy of this method is highly dependent on the manual adjustment steps in the registration process. This requires the operating radiologist to possess extensive imaging experience and anatomical knowledge. Particularly in cases where the patient lacks calcifications, stenosis, or other existent radiopaque markers such as mechanical valves, the radiologist must rely solely on soft tissue for registration, which is often prone to alignment errors.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates the feasibility and clinical potential of 3D multimodal image fusion in guiding cardiovascular interventions. The proposed method based on calcification and soft tissues registration and multi-volume image fusion, can significantly enhance the accuracy and visualization of the real-time roadmap.
