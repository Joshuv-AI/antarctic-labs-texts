Seroprevalence of hepatitis B virus markers of infection and immunity among people living in Libreville, Gabon
Authors: Samira Zoa-Assoumou, Hervé M'boyis-Kamdem, Pélagie Mougola-Bissiengou, Guy Francis Nzengui-Nzengui, Claudine Kombila-Koumavor, Marina Mbani-Okouma, Gael Mourembou, Angélique Ndjoyi-Mbiguino
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The high hepatitis B virus (HBV) exposure rate highlights a significant public health concern.•The vaccination coverage for hepatitis B needs to be improved.•Younger individuals and health care workers are particularly susceptible to HBV infection.•There is a need for increased efforts to prevent vertical transmission of HBV.

Background

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) is a viral infection that attacks the liver and can cause either an acute and self-resolving, or a chronic disease [1]. This is an enveloped virus found in both the blood and body fluids of infected individuals, and can be transmitted to the mucous membrane or bloodstream of noninfected individuals [1]. HBV infection leads to the expression of various serological markers of variable epidemiological and clinical relevance [2]. These HBV markers serve as serological evidence of exposure, current infection (acute or chronic), immunity, susceptibility, and infectivity status. The combination of these HBV markers has diverse health implications and provides HBV serological patterns that could better complement the clinical evaluation to better manage this infection [2].

According to the latest data from the World Health Organization (WHO), nearly 2 billion people worldwide have been infected with HBV, and approximately 296 million of them have a chronic infection and are carriers of HBV surface antigen (HBsAg), with a high risk of progression to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [3]. Owing to the complications of chronic hepatitis (cirrhosis or liver cancer), nearly 820,000 people will die each year from this infection [3]. Approximately 25% of these deaths are attributable to adults who are chronically infected during childhood [4].

In sub-Saharan Africa, the data are just as alarming. Indeed, 81 million people are chronically infected with HBV, and the prevalence varies between 5% and 35%, despite the existence and availability of a safe and effective vaccine for more than two decades [3].

Previous studies in Gabon, mainly based on HBsAg or HBV DNA detection, have reported varying seroprevalence rates of HBV, ranging between 9.2% and 19%. These studies focused mainly on specific populations, such as pregnant women (9.2%) [5], people living with HIV (17.3%) [6], or people living in a semiurban area (town of Oyem in the north of the country: 12.9%) [7] or rural area (19%) [8]. However, it is recognized that the prevalence of HBV infection varies not only between countries but also between cities and between urban and rural areas [9].

To date, no studies have investigated various HBV serological markers other than HBsAg (HBsAg, anti-HBc, anti-HBs, anti-HBe, and hepatitis B e antigen [HBeAg]) to evaluate exposure, natural immunity (past or resolved infection), infectivity, vaccination (acquired immunity), and susceptibility in the country. Thus, the objective of this work was to determine serological markers of HBV infection between February 2002 and February 2022 among people living in Libreville, Gabon.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective laboratory-based study of the records of people screened for HBV infection at the Bacteriology-Virology Laboratory of the University of Health Sciences (Libreville, Gabon). It is one of the first laboratories in Gabon, a small country with fewer than 2 million inhabitants. Data from January 2002 through December 2022 were included in this study. All of these patients were requested by clinicians to undergo HBV marker screening (HBsAg, anti-HBc, anti-HBs, anti-HBe, HBeAg). Information on age, sex, occupation, pregnancy status (for women), and the results of marker screening were reported in our laboratory register. The inclusion criteria were complete sociodemographic data and the presence of all of the HBV markers. Those who did not meet the eligibility criteria were excluded. A total of 518 HBV tests (all markers) occurred during the study period. A total of 496 inpatients who were screened for HBV markers with complete data were enrolled in the study. A small percentage (4.25%; 22 inpatients) were excluded because of missing or incomplete data. All data were analyzed anonymously with respect to the results of the serological screening for HBV infection.

Case definition

In this study, exposure was defined as positivity for anti-HBc only or HBsAg only. A resolved infection with postinfection immunity was defined as positivity for both anti-HBc and anti-HBs. Isolated anti-HBc was defined as positivity for anti-HBc and negativity for hepatitis B antigens and surface antibodies. HBV infection was defined as HBsAg positivity. Infectivity was defined as positivity for HBeAg.

Postvaccination immunity was defined as having tested positive for anti-HBs only, while susceptible people were those who tested negative for all HBV serological markers.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the OpenEpi statistical software (available at http://openepi.com/Menu/OE_Menu.htm). All the data are presented as percentages for basic descriptive purposes. The chi-square test (χ2) was used to assess the significance of differences among study variables. The odds ratio (OR) was calculated using binary logistic regression. P-values <0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Among the 201 individuals who had never been exposed to HBV, 75 (37.3%) were vaccinated, representing 15.1% of the 496 participants. Workers had a significantly greater probability of being vaccinated than HCWs (P = 0.0373). Compared with male patients, female patients were more likely to test positive for HBsAb (P = 0.0001). Most of those who were not vaccinated were in the 26-35-year age group (P = 0.0104) (Table 4).

Discussion

Hepatitis B is a major global public health problem, particularly in developing countries [10]. This study aimed to evaluate the seroprevalence of different HBV profiles among the general population in Gabon. To our knowledge, this study is the first in the country to cover such an extended period (2002-2022) and to focus specifically on the general population in urban areas.

The prevalence of HBV exposure was high (59.5%) and significantly associated with age, sex, pregnancy status, and social status. Lower exposure was found in low-prevalence countries such as Iran and Spain (4.9% and 7.3% respectively) [11,12]. In contrast, higher exposure was found in high-prevalence countries such as Nigeria, Togo, and Mauritania (32%, 53.9%, and 76.5%, respectively) [[13], [14], [15]].

HBV infection is a major cause of chronic hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma [16]. HBsAg is the main marker indicating the endemicity of HBV infection in the general population of a particular geographical area [17]. Levels of HBV endemicity based on HBsAg prevalence have been classified into four categories: low (2%), lower-intermediate (2-4.99%), higher-intermediate (5-7.99%), and high (≥8) [18]. In sub-Saharan Africa, carrier rates range from 9-20% [19]. In this study, the HBsAg prevalence was 21.8%, which supports the classification of Gabon as a highly endemic area. In a previous rural study in Gabon, the HBsAg prevalence was 19% [7]. These data support the difference in HBV seroprevalence between rural and urban zones. Our findings were similar to those reported by the neighboring country of Cameroon, where the prevalence was 11.2% [20]. These findings emphasize that HBV infection remains an important public health problem, and efforts will be made to achieve the WHO targets for the elimination of viral hepatitis.

The prevalence of HBV infection was high among young people in the 16-25-year (35.1%) and 26-35-year (30.6%) age groups (P<0.05). However, it decreased in the oldest age groups. The reason behind these findings could be the sexual transmission of HBV [21]. Therefore, some young people may present with acute hepatitis that can heal spontaneously [22]. Thus, it is likely that in the oldest age groups, HBsAg prevalence was low, possibly because of spontaneous clearance, treatment, or the death of carriers of the virus due to complications [23].

Maternal–fetal transmission of HBV represents an essential link in the maintenance of infection, especially in areas of high endemicity. Blocking mother-to-child transmission remains a significant challenge. In our study, we found an overall seroprevalence of 14.8% among pregnant women. This finding was consistent with the results of Makuwa et al. [5], who reported that 9.2% of pregnant women in Gabon were pregnant. This rate is lower than those reported by Kishk et al. [24] among pregnant women in Egypt (18.3%), Sidibe et al. [25] in Mali (15.5%), and Lohouès-Kouacou et al. [26] in the Ivory Coast (19%). In Gabon, HBV vaccination was first introduced in 2004 into the expanded program on immunization. The vaccine was administered for free to infants at 6, 10, and 14 weeks. However, under this program of infant vaccination, children may not be protected during the first month of life, suggesting that the first dose should be administered at birth in addition to the first dose used for seroconversion [27].

There was a significantly lower prevalence of HBsAg among male (19.6%) than female patients (23.3%). In most studies, male patients were more prevalent, possibly because of higher exposure to the virus through sexual behavior [[28], [29], [30], [31]]. A change in sexual behavior could explain the difference observed in our study.

A few people (15.1%) were positive for HBV. Moreover, only 6% of the HCWs were vaccinated. Vaccination remains the cornerstone in the fight against HBV, as it prevents chronic hepatitis B in 95% of cases and limits the occurrence of hepatocellular carcinoma [32]. Health care personnel should be vaccinated before exposure to blood or body fluids. In Gabon, medical institutions have not previously asked for proof of HBV vaccination. The low prevalence of vaccination among HCWs might be due to insufficient sensitization to HBV.

In Brazil, anti-HBV vaccination coverage for children aged <1 year started in 2004. Therefore, we can easily assume that in the coming years, most adults will be vaccinated, leading to a significant decrease in the number of HBV-positive individuals.

In the present study, 25.4% of patients were negative for all hepatitis B markers. This is relatively high but not very surprising given that the impact of the national immunization program would not have been observed in the studied age groups. This finding likely reflects the lack of voluntary adult vaccination outside the national vaccination program.

In this study, we identified a profile that could eventually predict occult hepatitis (anti-HBc only). Occult HBV infection is defined as the isolation of HBcAb via serological methods and detection of HBV DNA in serum and/or liver tissue. In this study, HBcAb alone was observed in a high percentage (19.8%) of patients. This finding is supported by other studies [33,34]. Moreover, a study conducted in Gabon among people living with HIV revealed a high rate of occult hepatitis [35].

In this study, isolated HBcAb markers were mainly found among women and those without a defined social status. The essential question about this profile pertains to its clinical impact. Some studies have shown that patients with HBcAb alone may have chronic liver injury, which is associated with oncogenic potential. Unfortunately, occult hepatitis cases were not confirmed by HBV DNA PCR. This is a limitation of our study, as the use of molecular tools for the confirmation of occult hepatitis cases is recommended.

Conclusion

In Gabon, hepatitis B infection is a significant public health issue. A high prevalence of HBV infection was detected in our study. Moreover, the high prevalence among women of childbearing age highlights the risk of mother-to-child transmission. There is a need for multicenter studies in Gabon, as these studies can provide a more comprehensive understanding of the burden of this infection in the country. This improved understanding would help to emphasize the urgent need for the implementation of preventive and control measures that will help to reduce infections.
