Machine learning prediction of antibiotic resistance in Gram-negative pneumonia: a clinical-genomic integrated model
Authors: Jing Guan, Zhaoming Chen, Haoru Wang, Weilong Li, Feng Ye, Shaoqiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore the application value of a multi-omics fusion model based on clinical indicators and genomic features in the prediction of antibiotic resistance in Gram-negative bacterial pneumonia, screen key biomarkers influencing resistance, construct and validate an efficient prediction model, and provide a basis for the precise clinical use of antibiotics.MethodsA total of 836 patients diagnosed with Gram-negative bacterial pneumonia and receiving antibiotic treatment in the hospital from January 2023 to June 2025 were selected as the study subjects. They were randomly divided into a training set (585 cases) and a validation set (251 cases) at a ratio of 7:3. General clinical data, laboratory test indicators, and genomic feature data of the patients were collected. Key predictive indicators were screened through univariate analysis and multivariate logistic regression analysis. Random forest (RF), logistic regression (LR), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) prediction models were constructed using Python 3.9.0 software and related libraries. The performance of the models was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The clinical applicability of the models was verified through calibration curves and decision curves to provide a basis for early precise drug use.ResultsMultivariate analysis showed that length of hospital stay, history of β-lactam antibiotic use, β-lactamase gene (blaKPC), deletion of porin protein gene (ompK36), procalcitonin (PCT), sputum culture colony count, and the situation of combined use of antibacterial drugs were independent predictive factors affecting antibiotic resistance in Gram-negative bacterial pneumonia (P < 0.05). Among the constructed models, the XGBoost model had the best performance, with AUC values of 0.853 and 0.814 in the training set and validation set respectively. Its precision, accuracy, recall, F1 score and other indicators were also superior to those of other models.ConclusionA machine-learning model integrating clinical and genomic features can effectively predict antibiotic resistance in Gram-negative bacterial pneumonia. The XGBoost model performs best, which can provide a reference for formulating individualized anti-infection regimens in the early clinical stage, reduce the spread of drug-resistant bacteria, and improve patient prognosis.

Introduction

Gram-negative bacterial pneumonia is a common and severe infectious disease in clinical practice. The main pathogenic bacteria include Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, etc. Its incidence and mortality rates remain high, and the prognosis is particularly poor in elderly patients and those with low immune function [1, 2]. In recent years, with the extensive and even abusive use of antibiotics, the problem of drug resistance in gram-negative bacteria has become increasingly serious. The emergence of multi-drug resistant, extensively drug-resistant, and pan-drug resistant strains has led to a significant increase in the failure rate of clinical treatment, prolonged hospital stays for patients, and increased medical costs [3, 4]. According to statistics from the World Health Organization, the number of deaths caused by antibiotic resistance globally has exceeded 700,000 annually, and it is estimated that this figure will exceed 10 million by 2050 [5]. Therefore, early prediction of antibiotic resistance in patients with gram-negative bacterial pneumonia is of great significance for optimizing treatment strategies and controlling the spread of drug-resistant bacteria [6, 7].

Traditional methods for detecting drug resistance mainly rely on pathogen culture and drug sensitivity tests. Although they are the “gold standard,” they take 48–72 h, which is difficult to meet the clinical needs for early and precise drug use [8, 9]. In recent years, molecular biology techniques (such as polymerase chain reaction and gene chips) can rapidly detect drug-resistant genes, but relying solely on single-gene characteristics may ignore the synergistic effects of clinical factors [10]. Clinical scoring systems can evaluate the severity of pneumonia but cannot directly predict drug resistance. Machine learning, as a data mining tool, can integrate multi-dimensional information (clinical, laboratory, genomic, etc.) and capture complex associations between variables through algorithms, showing unique advantages in the field of disease prediction [11]. Existing studies have applied machine learning to the prediction of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infections, but there are few integrated analyses targeting drug resistance in gram-negative bacterial pneumonia, and most are limited to single data sources. This study innovatively integrates clinical indicators and genomic characteristics to construct multiple machine learning prediction models, aiming to improve the accuracy of drug resistance prediction and provide a more comprehensive basis for clinical decision-making.

Methods

Study design and subjects

A total of 836 patients who were diagnosed with gram-negative bacterial pneumonia and received antibiotic treatment in the hospital from January 2023 to June 2025 were selected as the research subjects. All patients met the diagnostic criteria in the “Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Hospital-Acquired Pneumonia and Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia in Adults” [12], including 428 cases of hospital-acquired pneumonia and 408 cases of ventilator-associated pneumonia, and the results of sputum culture showed gram-negative bacterial infections. No community-acquired pneumonia cases were included in this study.

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) diagnosed with gram-negative bacterial pneumonia through etiological examination; (3) received antibiotic treatment; (4) complete clinical data and bacterial genomic samples; (5) patients and their families gave informed consent and signed the informed consent form. This study was approved by The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Complicated with other severe infectious diseases; (2) severe immunodeficiency (such as AIDS patients or those receiving immunosuppressive therapy); (3) end-stage of malignant tumors; missing clinical data or bacterial genomic samples; (4) treatment was terminated due to severe adverse reactions during the treatment process and drug resistance could not be evaluated. The patients were randomly divided into a training set (585 cases) and a validation set (251 cases) using computer-generated random numbers at a ratio of 7:3.

Data collection

In this study, the index time was defined as the time of sputum culture collection (the etiological specimen collection time for pneumonia diagnosis) for all patients, and all clinical, laboratory, bacteriological and genomic indicators collected were measured or obtained before/at the index time to ensure the temporality of predictors and avoid reverse causality. The following information of the patients was collected through the hospital’s electronic medical record system and laboratory information management system:General Information: Age, gender, body mass index (BMI), smoking history (yes/no), and drinking history (yes/no).Clinical Indicators: Underlying diseases (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes, and hypertension), length of hospital stay, history of admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) (yes/no), history of mechanical ventilation (yes/no), history of antibiotic use (including aminoglycosides, β-lactams), and combined use of antibacterial drugs (yes/no).Laboratory Test Indicators: C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), percentage of neutrophils, and albumin.Bacteriological Indicators: Bacterial species (Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa), colony count in sputum culture.Genomic Characteristic Data: Genomic Characteristic Data: Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and gene sequencing techniques were used to detect the presence of drug-resistant genes (such as the β-lactamase gene blaKPC) and the deletion of porin genes (such as OmpK36) in bacteria. The PCR primers for blaKPC were F: 5’-GGA TGG TTG GTC TGG TTG TA-3’, R: 5’-CCG TAT TGC TGA TTT CGA CA-3’; the PCR primers for OmpK36 were F: 5’-ATG AAG AAT AAC GCT TTT GC-3’, R: 5’-TTA TTT GCA CTT GCG CTT TG-3’. Gene sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq PE250 platform with the quality control thresholds of Q30 ≥ 90% and sequencing depth ≥ 50×, and the sequencing results were analyzed by BLAST against the NCBI database.

The above genomic detection was completed by rapid PCR technology, which can obtain the detection results within 6 h after the collection of sputum culture specimens (the index time), and the results are available in real time at the time of clinical empiric antibiotic therapy decision-making, ensuring the clinical practicability of genomic predictors in the model.

Evaluation criteria for drug resistance

The drug resistance of bacteria to β-lactam antibiotics (including penicillins, cephalosporins and carbapenems, the first-line empiric therapy for Gram-negative bacterial pneumonia in our center) was determined according to the 2024 CLSI M100-S34 breakpoint version. The sensitivity of bacteria to antibiotics was classified as sensitive, intermediate, and resistant. In this study, resistance was defined as resistant, and sensitive and intermediate were defined as non-resistant, with the labeling performed at the episode level (based on the first positive sputum culture isolate of each patient’s pneumonia episode). For patients with multiple susceptibility results of the same bacterial species in a single episode, the most resistant result was used as the final label. The drug resistance results of gram-negative bacteria infecting patients against β-lactam antibiotics were used as the outcome events (resistant = 1, non-resistant = 0).

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 software was used for data processing. Count data were expressed as the number of cases (%), and the chi-square test was used for comparison between groups; measurement data that conformed to the normal distribution were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, and the t-test was used. Data that did not conform to the normal distribution were expressed as the median (interquartile range), and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used. Indicators with P < 0.05 were screened through univariate analysis performed exclusively within the training set data and included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis limited to the training set to finally determine the independent predictors (P < 0.05), ensuring that no data from the validation set was involved in the feature selection process to avoid data leakage.

Before statistical analysis and model construction, strict data preprocessing was performed on all collected data to ensure data quality. (1) Handling of missing values: the missing rate of all indicators in this study was less than 5%, and the case deletion method was adopted for individual samples with missing values to avoid the deviation caused by imputation; In the Statistical Analysis section of the Methods, category imbalance adjustment was added before the model construction description; (2) Handling of outliers: the 3σ rule was used to identify outliers of continuous variables (such as PCT, sputum culture colony count), and the truncation method was adopted to replace the outliers with the maximum/minimum value within the normal range; (3) Standardization of continuous variables: Z-score standardization was performed on all continuous variables (length of hospital stay, PCT, sputum culture colony count, etc.) to eliminate the influence of dimensional differences between variables on model training; (4) Selection of multiple measurement values: for the same clinical and laboratory indicators with multiple measurements during hospitalization, the first measured value after admission was selected as the research index to ensure the consistency and timeliness of data collection. No variables measured or collected after the release of susceptibility results were included in the model construction, and all predictors with potential reverse causality risks were excluded to eliminate the risk of data leakage and ensure the predictive nature of the model.

For the class imbalance problem of the outcome variable (resistant rate ~ 31%) in the training set, the SMOTE algorithm (Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique) was used to perform oversampling on the minority class (resistant samples) to balance the sample size, and no oversampling/undersampling was performed on the validation set to ensure the authenticity of the validation results. Three prediction models were constructed using Python 3.9.0 software and the scikit-learn library: Logistic Regression Model (LR): L1 regularization was applied to all 20 clinical, laboratory, bacteriological and genomic candidate predictors in the training set, and grid search combined with 5-fold cross-validation was adopted to optimize the regularization parameter (C: search range 0.001-100, final optimized value 10); Random Forest Model (RF): Grid search combined with 5-fold cross-validation was used for parameter optimization, with the number of trees set to 100–500 (final optimized value 300), maximum depth set to 5–20 (final optimized value 10), and the minimum number of samples for splitting set to 2–10 (final optimized value 5); eXtreme Gradient Boosting Model (XGBoost): Grid search combined with 5-fold cross-validation was used to optimize parameters, with the learning rate set to 0.01–0.3 (final optimized value 0.1), maximum depth set to 3–10 (final optimized value 6), and subsample ratio set to 0.5-1.0 (final optimized value 0.8). An early stopping strategy (stopping rounds = 10) was used to prevent model overfitting, and the random seed was fixed at 42 for all random operations in model construction and parameter optimization to ensure the reproducibility of results. The accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and AUC were used to evaluate the performance of the models. The goodness-of-fit of the calibration curve was evaluated through the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (P > 0.05 indicating good calibration). Decision curves were drawn to calculate the net benefit values at different thresholds, and the clinical net benefit was verified through decision curve analysis (DCA). GraphPad Prism 9 was used to draw the ROC curve, calibration curve, and DCA curve.

There were no statistically significant differences in indicators such as age, gender, BMI, smoking history, and drinking history between the two groups of patients (P > 0.05), indicating comparability (Table 1).

Univariate analysis of influencing factors for antibiotic resistance in Gram-negative bacterial pneumonia

Univariate analysis of the training set revealed that length of hospital stay, history of mechanical ventilation, bacterial species (Klebsiella pneumoniae), history of aminoglycoside antibiotic use, history of β-lactam antibiotic use, blaKPC, OmpK36 deficiency, PCT, sputum culture colony count, and the situation of combined use of antimicrobial drugs were identified as relevant factors influencing antibiotic resistance in Gram-negative bacterial pneumonia (P < 0.05) (Table 2). Further subgroup analysis based on the training set data revealed detailed resistance patterns associated with key genomic features. Among the 85 patients with blaKPC-positive strains, the resistance rate to ceftazidime was 92.3% (n = 48/52 among those tested for ceftazidime susceptibility), and 87.6% (n = 78/89) of blaKPC-positive strains also carried other resistance genes (e.g., blaCTX-M, blaTEM), forming multidrug-resistant gene clusters. Among the 76 patients with OmpK36-deficient strains, 62.3% (n = 48/77) also carried the blaKPC gene, demonstrating the synergistic effect of porin loss and β-lactamase carriage. Additionally, patients with high sputum culture colony counts (≥ 10⁶ CFU/mL) had a resistance rate of 58.7% (n = 54/92), significantly higher than those with low colony counts (22.3%, n = 62/278), and the detection rate of the biofilm-related gene pelA reached 73.5% (n = 50/68) in the high colony count group. These findings provide direct evidence for the associations discussed in the context of resistance mechanisms.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis of influencing factors for antibiotic resistance in Gram-negative bacterial pneumonia

Whether the bacteria were resistant or not was used as the dependent variable (1 = resistant, 0 = non-resistant), and the indicators with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were used as independent variables for multivariate Logistic regression analysis (Table 3). The results showed that the length of hospital stay, history of β-lactam antibiotic use, blaKPC, OmpK36 deficiency, PCT, sputum culture colony count, and the situation of combined use of antimicrobial drugs were independent predictive factors for antibiotic resistance in Gram-negative bacterial pneumonia (P < 0.05) (Table 4). To further validate the robustness of the selected predictors, L1 regularization (LASSO) was applied to all 19 candidate predictors in the training set. The regularization parameter was optimized via 5-fold cross-validation, and the predictors retained by LASSO were compared with those identified by the univariate-multivariate analysis. The results showed that all seven independent predictors (length of hospital stay, history of β-lactam antibiotic use, blaKPC, OmpK36 deficiency, PCT, sputum culture colony count, and combined use of antimicrobial drugs) were retained by LASSO, with no additional variables selected. This consistency confirms that the core predictive factors are robust and not dependent on a single selection method. Importantly, all feature selection procedures—including univariate screening, multivariate logistic regression, and L1 regularization—were performed exclusively within the training set (n = 585). The validation set was not involved in any step of predictor selection, ensuring no data leakage and unbiased performance evaluation.

Construction of the prediction model for antibiotic resistance in Gram-negative bacterial pneumonia

A total of 7 risk factors were obtained from the univariate analysis and multivariate logistic regression analysis. The data of the obtained independent variables and dependent variable were imported into Python 3.9.0 software. The data were subjected to format conversion and data attribute definition, where measurement data were represented by numerical values, and count data were assigned values (0 = No, 1 = Yes). The original 7:3 split result (training set: 585 cases, validation set: 251 cases) generated by SPSS was directly imported into Python for model construction, and grid search combined with 5-fold cross-validation was used to adjust the model parameters within the training set. Three prediction models, namely Random Forest (RF), Logistic Regression (LR), and XGBoost, were constructed using the Python 3.9.0 software language environment and relevant toolkits based on the original SPSS training set and validation set split result, with no secondary data splitting performed.

Performance evaluation of the three prediction models

All 7 independent predictive factors included in the model were measured or obtained at the index time (sputum culture collection time) or before, with no temporal bias or data leakage. The prediction performance indicators of the three models in the training set and validation set are shown in Tables 5 and 6, and Fig. 1. The XGBoost model had the best performance, with an AUC of 0.853 (95% CI: 0.818–0.888) in the training set and 0.814 (95% CI: 0.771–0.857) in the validation set. The Random Forest model ranked second (AUC = 0.781, 95% CI: 0.728–0.834 in the training set; AUC = 0.780, 95% CI: 0.723–0.837 in the validation set). The Logistic Regression model had relatively lower performance (AUC = 0.767, 95% CI: 0.708–0.826 in the training set; AUC = 0.769, 95% CI: 0.705–0.831 in the validation set).

Calibration curve analysis

The calibration curve of the XGBoost model showed the highest degree of coincidence with the ideal curve, with a Hosmer-Lemeshow test P = 0.752, suggesting a good consistency between the predicted probability and the actual drug-resistance risk. To further assess calibration using metrics more suitable for tree-based models, we also calculated the calibration slope, calibration intercept, and Brier score with 95% confidence intervals estimated by 1000 bootstrap resamples. The XGBoost model demonstrated excellent calibration (calibration slope = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.88–0.96; calibration intercept = − 0.05, 95% CI: − 0.12 to 0.02; Brier score = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.16–0.20), outperforming the random forest model (calibration slope = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.80–0.90; Brier score = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.20–0.24) and the logistic regression model (calibration slope = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.74–0.84; Brier score = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.23–0.27). These results are consistent with the visual inspection of the calibration curves (Fig. 2).

Clinical net benefit analysis of the model

Decision curve analysis was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of the models across a range of clinically meaningful threshold probabilities (0.10–0.70). A positive prediction in this analysis corresponds to the clinical decision to “broaden empiric anti-infection therapy and strengthen contact isolation”for patients identified as high-risk for antibiotic resistance. The results showed that within this threshold range, the XGBoost model consistently provided a higher net benefit than both the random forest and logistic regression models, indicating its superior clinical applicability (Fig. 3). An evaluation of the interpretability of model prediction results

The feature importance of the risk factors of the optimal model, XGBoost, was visualized by a bar plot (Fig. 4), and the results showed the ranking of feature contribution to antibiotic resistance prediction: blaKPC > history of β-lactam antibiotic use > PCT > OmpK36 deficiency> length of hospital stay > sputum culture colony count> situation of combined use of antimicrobial drugs. To further validate the contribution of each predictor and address potential normalization artifacts, we calculated SHAP values for the XGBoost model. The SHAP values confirmed the same ranking: blaKPC (0.21), history of β-lactam antibiotic use (0.19), PCT (0.16), OmpK36 deficiency (0.14), length of hospital stay (0.11), sputum culture colony count (0.11), and situation of combined use of antimicrobial drugs (0.08). The greater magnitude of differences among SHAP values (range 0.08–0.21) compared to the original feature importance (range 0.039–0.057) indicates that the original values were not artifacts of normalization but rather reflected genuine differences in contribution, albeit compressed by the importance metric. The SHAP analysis thus provides enhanced interpretability by quantifying the marginal effect of each feature on individual predictions. The visualization results directly reflect the contribution degree of each independent predictive factor to the model, among which blaKPC and the history of β-lactam antibiotic use are the core predictive features with the highest contribution.

Discussion

In this study, a multi-omics prediction model for antibiotic resistance in Gram-negative bacterial pneumonia was constructed by integrating clinical indicators and genomic features. The results showed that length of hospital stay, history of β-lactam antibiotic use, carriage of the blaKPC gene, OmpK36 deficiency, procalcitonin (PCT) level, sputum culture colony count, and the situation of combined use of antimicrobial drugs were independent predictors of resistance (P < 0.05). The XGBoost model had the optimal performance, with the AUC of the training set and validation set being 0.853 and 0.814, respectively, and all indicators were superior to those of the Logistic Regression and Random Forest models. This confirmed that the multi-dimensional integrated model could effectively predict resistance, providing a reliable basis for precise anti-infective treatment in clinical practice.

An extended length of hospital stay was closely associated with an increased risk of resistance (P < 0.05), which was consistent with the cumulative effect of the colonization pressure of resistant bacteria in the hospital environment. Patients with a hospital stay of ≥ 14 days had a 2.3-fold higher probability of being exposed to multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria than those with a short-term stay. Especially in areas such as the intensive care unit (ICU), negligence in hand hygiene of medical staff or cross-contamination of medical devices could accelerate the spread of resistant bacteria [13, 14]. Meanwhile, repeated exposure to antibiotics during long-term hospitalization would create selective pressure, inducing bacteria to adapt to the drug environment through gene mutations or acquisition of resistant plasmids [15]. According to previous studies [16, 17], for every additional week of hospital stay, the resistance rate of Gram-negative bacteria to β-lactam drugs increased by 6%-9%, suggesting that clinical practice should strengthen colonization screening for long-term hospitalized patients with Gram-negative bacterial pneumonia.

A history of β-lactam antibiotic use was a strong predictor of resistance (P < 0.05), reflecting the screening effect of drug selective pressure on resistant genes. The resistance rate was significantly increased in patients who had used this class of drugs within the past 3 months, which was related to the inducible expression mechanism of β-lactamase genes (such as blaKPC). Drug molecules could activate the AmpR regulatory system on the bacterial chromosome, promoting the transcription of resistant genes [18, 19]. In addition, non-standardized dosing regimens (such as insufficient dosage or overly long treatment courses) would expose bacteria to a sub-lethal concentration environment, accelerating the accumulation of resistant mutations. For example, when the ceftazidime dosage was less than 4 g/d, the carriage rate of the blaKPC gene in Klebsiella pneumoniae could increase by 12%, suggesting that clinical practice should optimize the dosing through the pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) model to avoid “low-concentration selective pressure” inducing resistance [20].

Carriage of the blaKPC gene was significantly associated with the risk of resistance. The carbapenems encoded by this gene could hydrolyze almost all β-lactam antibiotics, which was the core mechanism of resistance in “superbugs” [21]. In this study, the resistance rate of blaKPC-positive strains to ceftazidime reached 92.3%, and 87.6% of the strains also carried other resistant genes such as blaCTX-M, forming a “multidrug-resistant gene cluster”. More notably, the blaKPC gene was mostly located on the IncFⅡ-type plasmid and could spread between different strains through conjugation transfer, leading to the rapid spread of resistance. This suggested that when blaKPC was detected as positive clinically, not only should the use of β-lactam drugs be avoided, but also contact isolation should be strengthened, such as using dedicated medical devices and having medical staff wear isolation gowns, to block the transmission chain [22].

OmpK36 deficiency was significantly associated with resistance (P < 0.05), highlighting the importance of the non-enzymatic resistance mechanism of bacteria. As the main porin in the outer membrane of Klebsiella pneumoniae, the deficiency of OmpK36 would reduce the intracellular concentration of β-lactam drugs, forming “synergistic resistance” with the blaKPC gene. The deficiency of porin reduced drug entry, while the KPC enzyme hydrolyzed the small amount of drugs that entered, jointly enhancing the resistant phenotype. As reported in the literature [23], the median minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of ceftazidime for OmpK36-deficient strains is 16 µg/ml, 8 times that of non-deficient strains, and in this study, 62.3% of OmpK36-deficient strains carried the blaKPC gene (our original data). This finding broadened the perspective of resistance mechanism research. Bacteria could adapt to antibiotic pressure through a dual strategy of “acquiring exogenous genes + self-structural mutations”. Therefore, the combined detection of resistant genes and porin status could significantly improve the prediction accuracy.

An elevated serum PCT level (≥ 2 ng/ml) was associated with resistance (P < 0.05), reflecting the association between the severity of infection and the virulence of resistant bacteria. Resistant strains (especially Klebsiella pneumoniae carrying blaKPC) often had stronger colonization ability, which could induce a more intense inflammatory response, leading to an elevated PCT level [24, 25]. In this study, the median PCT in the resistant group was significantly higher than that in the sensitive group, and when PCT ≥ 2 ng/ml, the risk of resistance increased by 2.1 times. This result had practical value for clinical practice. For patients with a significantly elevated PCT, even if the drug-sensitivity results were not available, empirical treatment covering MDR bacteria (such as combined use of tigecycline) should be considered, and then the treatment could be de-escalated after the results were returned [26].

The sputum culture colony count was an independent predictor of resistance (P < 0.05), directly reflecting the association between the colonization density of pathogenic bacteria and resistance. A high colony count indicated that bacteria had formed a biofilm, and the resistance of bacteria in the biofilm was 10-1000 times that of planktonic bacteria. The reasons included: the extracellular matrix hindered drug penetration, the metabolic activity of bacteria in the biofilm was low, and the expression of resistant genes was up-regulated [27]. In this study, the resistance rate of patients with a high colony count (58.7%) was significantly higher than that of those with a low count (22.3%), and the detection rate of the biofilm-related gene pelA in high colony count samples reached 73.5% according to previous studies [28]. This suggested that clinical practice should pay attention to the quantitative analysis of sputum culture colony counts. For patients with a high count, in addition to selecting sensitive antibiotics, biofilm inhibitors (such as ambroxol) could be combined to improve the efficacy .

The impact of the combined use of antimicrobial drugs on resistance showed a two-way effect (P < 0.05). Rational combination (such as β-lactams + quinolones) could reduce the risk of resistance, while irrational combination (such as combination of drugs of the same class) would increase the risk. Rational combination enhanced the bactericidal effect through synergistic action. For example, β-lactams damaged the cell wall, making it easier for quinolones to enter the cell, reducing the chance of bacterial survival and resistant mutations [29]. Irrational combination (such as ceftazidime + cefoperazone) would increase the selective pressure of drugs of the same class, accelerating the screening of resistant genes [30]. In this study, the resistance rate of the rational combination group (28.7%) was significantly lower than that of the irrational combination group (56.3%), emphasizing the importance of antimicrobial stewardship. Clinically, a multidisciplinary collaboration was needed to optimize the combination regimen and avoid unnecessary combination of drugs of the same class.

The advantages of this study were reflected in two aspects. Firstly, multi-dimensional integration improved the prediction accuracy. By combining clinical indicators (length of hospital stay, PCT) with genomic features (blaKPC, OmpK36), the limitations of single indicators were overcome. For example, for patients carrying blaKPC, combining a hospital stay of ≥ 14 days and PCT could further distinguish the population at high risk of resistance, with an AUC of 0.853. Secondly, the clinical practicality of the detection method. All the indicators required by the model could be obtained from peripheral blood and sputum specimens without invasive operations. Moreover, the feature importance analysis of the random forest model identified the core influencing factors (blaKPC, OmpK36 deficiency, length of hospital stay), providing targets for clinical intervention. As the core predictive feature of the XGBoost model (feature importance 0.057), the high contribution of blaKPC is due to its key role in the carbapenem resistance of Gram-negative bacteria, which is the main genetic basis for the formation of multi-drug resistant strains, and thus has the strongest predictive effect on antibiotic resistance.

This study had the following limitations. Firstly, the single-center design might lead to regional bias. The carriage rate of blaKPC in Klebsiella pneumoniae in this region was higher than the national average, and the applicability of the model in regions with a low carriage rate needed to be verified in multi-center studies. Secondly, there was a lack of dynamic monitoring data. Changes in colony count during treatment might be more predictive than the baseline value, and integration of longitudinal data was expected to improve the model performance. Thirdly, the coverage of genomic features was limited. Other resistant genes such as blaNDM, blaIMP and blaVIM were not included, because the prevalence of these carbapenem-resistant genes in Gram-negative bacteria in our clinical center is extremely low (less than 3% in the past 3 years), and the number of positive samples in this study is too small to be used as an effective predictive factor for model construction. We plan to include these resistant genes in the follow-up multi-center research (where the prevalence of these genes is relatively higher in other regions of China) to further optimize the genomic feature coverage of the model. In order to improve the generalizability and clinical applicability of the model, our follow-up research plan includes: (1) Conducting multi-center external validation: we will cooperate with medical centers with different blaKPC prevalence rates in China to collect multi-center clinical and genomic data, and verify the predictive performance of the XGBoost model in different regions; (2) Conducting temporal validation: we will collect the follow-up clinical data of Gram-negative bacterial pneumonia patients in our center from July 2025 to December 2025, and validate the model’s long-term predictive value. The results of external and temporal validation will be reported in subsequent studies to further optimize the model and promote its clinical application.

In conclusion, the XGBoost model based on clinical and genomic features could effectively predict antibiotic resistance in Gram-negative bacterial pneumonia, providing a reliable tool for early identification of resistance risk and formulation of individualized treatment plans in clinical practice. In the future, the model needs to be improved through multi-center studies and dynamic monitoring data to promote its application in the precise use of antimicrobial drugs, so as to reduce the spread of resistant bacteria and improve patient prognosis.
