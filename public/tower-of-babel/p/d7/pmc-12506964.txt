Generating theranostic bacteria by depositing a protective visual therapeutic nanocoating via in situ sequential mineralization
Authors: Shixiong Chen, Beibei Sun, Lingling Lin, Xinxin Zhao, Yan Zhou, Feng Wu, Huilin Zhao, Jinyao Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Oral bacteriotherapy is increasingly exploited but faces challenges in simultaneously monitoring treatment process and achieving desirable efficacy. Here, we develop theranostic bacteria by depositing a protective visual therapeutic nanocoating via in situ sequential mineralization. Using two-step metal ion adsorption and nucleation, bacterial surface is successively mineralized with an inner manganese dioxide/ferric oxide nanoparticle layer and an outer calcium carbonate layer. Upon ingestion, the outer layer neutralizes gastric acid through double-decomposition reaction, protecting bacterial viability and the inner layer. During intestinal passage, the inner layer enables T1/T2 dual-mode magnetic resonance imaging for real-time tracking, whereas the lesion-triggered release of manganese ions allows T1 single-mode pathological detection. The inner layer also exhibits superoxide dismutase and catalase activities, synergizing therapeutic effects with bacteria. Further supported by satisfactory performances in two murine models of Salmonella-induced colitis and apolipoprotein E–deficient atherosclerosis, this work provides a platform to generate innovative theranostic agents for advanced bacteriotherapy.

INTRODUCTION

Bacterial therapy has been increasingly explored for treating a wide range of diseases, due largely to the rapid proliferation, niche-specific colonization, and vigorous metabolic activity of bacteria (1–3). In view of the existence of the gut microbiota that plays essential roles in maintaining host immune and metabolic homeostasis, oral delivery of beneficial bacteria to the intestine has emerged as an attractive approach to treat different intestinal and extraintestinal diseases (4–6). For instance, Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) has been orally administered to intervene inflammatory bowel disease, whereas oral ingestion of Clostridium butyricum has been reported to prevent the transition from prediabetes to type 2 diabetes, by the cross-talk of bacteria with the host (7, 8). However, current bacterial therapy faces major challenges in tracking the ingested bacteria within the complex in vivo environment and detecting the progression of the treated disease. This real-time information is critical for timely understanding the therapeutic process and adjusting treatment regimen to advance bacterial therapy. On the other hand, the harsh intraluminal conditions of the gastrointestinal tract, particularly the strong acidity of gastric fluid, can severely reduce the viability of orally ingested bacteria (9, 10). Meanwhile, the multiplicity of mechanisms and targets involved in disease pathogenesis often causes inadequate therapeutic responses when using a single-mode of bacterial therapy (11, 12). These factors inevitably reduce the therapeutic effectiveness of bacteria, ultimately resulting in suboptimal treatment outcomes. Therefore, the design and preparation of bacterial agents with real-time monitoring and multimodal therapeutic capabilities is crucial for developing next-generation bacterial therapy.

Synthetic bioengineering is the most commonly used approach to modify bacteria (13–15). With the help of gene editing techniques, bacteria have been engineered to express diverse fluorescent proteins, bubble proteins, ferritins, and other imaging reporters for visualization (16–20). Similarly, bacteria can be encoded with the genes of internally expressing or externally secreting therapeutic proteins or antibody fragments, integrating bacterial therapy with a chemotherapy or immunotherapy mode (21, 22). However, genetic engineering of bacteria to combine imaging and therapeutic functional motifs remains several difficulties, such as the requirement of constructing complex genetic circuits, limited types of genetically modifiable strains, and potential safety risks associated with the introduction of foreign genes. As an alternative, physicochemical approaches, including chemical conjugation and physical modification, have been recently used to introduce exogenous functional elements on bacterial surface (23). Given its simplicity and versatility to modify different bacterial species and incorporate designable components, physicochemical engineering of bacteria has achieved considerable progress, particularly with aims to improve the efficacy of bacterial therapy (24). Along with other groups, we have developed multiple bacteria with enhanced viability against external insults by individually enveloping with a nanocoating (25–27). Apart from cellular protection, functional nanocoatings have been fabricated to combine with specific ligands or small-molecular drugs for targeting therapy or synergistic treatment (28, 29). Despite these achievements, physicochemical modification of bacteria to simultaneously incorporate real-time monitoring and synergistic therapeutic functions has been rarely reported. Note that contrast agents, such as metal nanoparticles, have been chemically conjugated to bacterial surface but solely to enhance their accumulation at disease sites by leveraging the inherent targeting ability of bacteria (30). The capability to visualize the applied bacteria or examine the therapeutic responses has not been explored yet.

Here, we describe a strategy of using a protective visual therapeutic nanocoating generated via in situ sequential mineralization to develop theranostic bacteria (Fig. 1). By a two-step metal ion adsorption and nucleation procedure, the surface of bacteria is first deposited with an inner mineral layer composed of manganese dioxide (MnO2) and ferric oxide (Fe2O3) nanoparticles, which are further deposited with an outer calcium carbonate (CaCO3) layer. We show that both the preparation process and the formed nanocoating present negligible influence on the viability of coated bacteria. After oral delivery, the CaCO3 layer is rapidly destructed via an autonomous double-decomposition reaction, which neutralizes gastric acid to protect the activities of bacteria and the remaining MnO2/Fe2O3 layer. During intestinal transit, the MnO2/Fe2O3 layer serves as a contrast agent for real-time visualization via T1 and T2 dual-mode magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), thereby allowing one to track the intraluminal distribution of the delivered bacteria. Meanwhile, the lesion-triggered derivation of Mn2+ ions from MnO2 nanoparticles can be detected via T1 monomode MRI, enabling the examination of the pathological changes. Owing to its nanozyme properties including superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activities that eliminate reactive oxygen species (ROS) to reduce inflammation, the MnO2/Fe2O3 layer can also act as a therapeutic agent to combine with bacteria for synergistic treatment. We demonstrate that oral ingestion of coated bacteria exhibits favorable therapeutic potentials in two mouse models of Salmonella-induced colitis and apolipoprotein E–deficient (apoE−/−) atherosclerosis (AS). Considering the flexibility of using mineralization to deposit diverse functional components, this work opens a window to prepare theranostic agents for bacterial therapy of various intestinal and extraintestinal diseases.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Design, preparation, and characterization of theranostic bacteria

As a proof-of-concept study, we investigated the use of metal oxides to form a visual therapeutic layer on bacterial surface. Metal oxide nanoparticles, such as MnO2 and Fe2O3, are typical MRI contrast agents widely used for disease diagnosis (31). Specifically, MnO2 nanoparticles function as a paramagnetic agent that shortens the longitudinal relaxation time (T1) of water protons to enhance bright signal intensity, whereas Fe2O3 nanoparticles act as a negative contrast agent by reducing the transverse relaxation time (T2) to produce dark contrast-enhanced signals (32). Also, MnO2 nanoparticles can be triggered by ROS and undergo reductive decomposition to release Mn2+ ions, which can be captured by T1 monomode MRI (33). As the level of ROS is highly related to inflammatory reaction, a circumstance associated with a variety of diseases, the MRI signal of Mn2+ ions is able to reflect the therapeutic response (34). On the other side, these metal oxide nanoparticles are appealing therapeutic agents due to their nanozyme properties, including SOD and CAT activities that mitigate oxidative stress by scavenging ROS (35). Thus, we hypothesized that decorating bacterial surface with both MnO2 and Fe2O3 nanoparticles could not only track the administered bacteria and detect pathological changes via MRI but also provide a synergistic therapeutic efficacy with bacteria. Despite this rationality, the challenge is how to protect bacteria and the MnO2/Fe2O3 nanoparticle layer following oral delivery, as which are susceptible to gastric acid (36). Given the fact that CaCO3, an approved antacid, can neutralize gastric acid by a spontaneous double-decomposition reaction (37), we further covered the MnO2/Fe2O3 nanoparticle layer with a CaCO3 layer. As a result, after oral ingestion, both the activities of bacteria and the MnO2/Fe2O3 layer could be protected from acidic insult. In addition, EcN, a clinically used oral therapeutic known for its potency to restore intestinal homeostasis and long-standing safety in humans (38), was chosen as a model probiotic strain to prepare theranostic bacteria.

We developed an in situ sequential mineralization strategy by using a two-step metal ion adsorption and nucleation procedure. In the first step, manganese chloride (MnCl2) was introduced before ferric chloride (FeCl3) into the EcN suspension, allowing a balanced electrostatic adsorption of both Mn2+ and Fe3+ ions onto the negatively charged bacterial surface (termed Fe3+Mn2+@EcN) (39). This sequential addition could avoid excessive adsorption of Fe3+ ions with a high valency and strong coordination capacity. Subsequently, sodium hydroxide (NaOH) was added to facilitate the growth of MnO2 and Fe2O3 on the EcN surface, resulting in an inner MnO2/Fe2O3 layer (Fig. 2A). The morphology of the resulting bacteria (termed FeMn@EcN) was observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), showing a much rougher surface compared to naked EcN (Fig. 2B). Energy-dispersive x-ray (EDX) elemental mapping indicated the coexistence of six elements across the entire surface of the bacteria, with manganese and iron signals consistently aligned with bacterial outlines (Fig. 2C and fig. S1). Element contents of manganese and iron were separately determined to be 49.8 and 3.9 μg/ml by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) (table S1). X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra showed that the diffraction peaks of FeMn@EcN closely matched the crystal phases of pure MnO2 and Fe2O3, which verified the crystalline structure of the inner layer (fig. S2). In the second step, FeMn@EcN was dispersed in a solution of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and calcium chloride (CaCl2), allowing Ca2+ ions to be adsorbed onto the bacterial surface (termed Ca2+FeMn@EcN) as the deposited PVP could capture Ca2+ ions by the complexation with the pyrrolidone pendant groups (37). Followed by the addition of sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), an outer layer of CaCO3 could be generated via heterogeneous nucleation and deposition on the FeMn@EcN surface, forming the target theranostic bacteria (termed CaFeMn@EcN) (Fig. 2D). TEM imaging of CaFeMn@EcN presented a rough surface with much higher contrast in comparison to that of FeMn@EcN (Fig. 2E). As expected, three different lattice fringes could be observed under high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) (Fig. 2F), suggesting that CaCO3, MnO2, and Fe2O3 were nucleated separately, instead of forming into composite nanocrystals. Characteristic signal peaks assigned to calcite, manganese oxide, and iron oxide phases were observed in the XRD patterns of CaFeMn@EcN (Fig. 2G), verifying the formation of a triple mineral nanocoating. As shown in Fig. 2H, fig. S3, and table S1, EDX element mapping and ICP-OES tests revealed that additional calcium signals were uniformly distributed across the entire bacterial surface, with a calcium loading content of 50.1 μg/ml in the coated bacteria. Viability assessment using plate counting across different stages of the mineralization process disclosed that the counts of living bacteria in the Fe3+Mn2+@EcN, FeMn@EcN, Ca2+FeMn@EcN, CaFeMn@EcN, and bare EcN groups remained consistent (fig. S4). In addition, growth curve analysis showed that the overall growth profiles of the mineralized bacteria resembled that of EcN, whereas the engineered strains exhibited varying degrees of lag phases due to the shielding effect of the mineral coatings. Specifically, the triply mineralized CaFeMn@EcN displayed a more pronounced delay than the dual-mineralized FeMn@EcN. Notably, all groups eventually reached growth plateaus comparable to native EcN with extended incubation duration (fig. S5). These indicated that both the preparation procedures and the formed mineral nanocoating had a negligible influence on bacterial viability. Briefly, these results demonstrated the successful preparation of coated bacteria under cytocompatible conditions.

Resistance of CaFeMn@EcN against gastric acid

The ability to resist gastric acid following oral ingestion is essential for theranostic bacteria as both EcN and the MnO2/Fe2O3 layer are susceptible to acid (36). We first studied the capability of the nanocoating to resist the insult of gastric acid and protect the survival of coated EcN. In principle, after oral administration, the outer CaCO3 layer on CaFeMn@EcN can spontaneously react with gastric acid through a double-decomposition reaction, simultaneously enabling rapid neutralization of gastric acid and the release of FeMn@EcN (Fig. 3A). To verify this, CaFeMn@EcN was incubated directly with simulated gastric fluid (SGF; pH 2) to examine the resistance of theranostic bacteria against gastric acid. As controls, FeMn@EcN and bare EcN were similarly exposed to SGF for 30 min and then subjected to Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plate counting to calculate the number of survived bacteria. As displayed in Fig. 3 (B and C), EcN had a survival ratio of only 3.22%, and the deposition of a MnO2/Fe2O3 layer slightly enhanced the survival of EcN by five times. Notably, the triple mineral nanocoating provided a marked protection effect, with a 17-fold increase in the number of survived bacteria. The morphology of exposed bacteria visualized by TEM displayed that the triple mineral coating could protect EcN from cell membrane destruction compared to uncoated bacteria (Fig. 3D). EDX element mapping images showed that the Ca signal of CaFeMn@EcN was nearly indistinguishable from the background signal after 30-min exposure to SGF (Fig. 3E). This suggested the removal of Ca from coated bacteria, due to the decomposition of CaCO3 layer after exposure to gastric acid. We further investigated the protection of the MnO2/Fe2O3 layer by the CaCO3 layer during exposure in SGF. Different from the disappearance of Ca signal, the signals of both Fe and Mn remained detectable in the EDX element mapping images (Fig. 3E). The ICP-OES test was further conducted to analyze the change in element contents of CaFeMn@EcN after incubation in SGF (table S1). As calculated, the element contents of Ca, Mn, and Fe in CaFeMn@EcN decreased from 50.1, 50.6, and 4.6 μg/ml to 0.02, 44.9, and 4.1 μg/ml, respectively. However, as a control, FeMn@EcN without an outer CaCO3 layer showed large decrements in the element contents of Mn and Fe, which were separately dropped from 49.8 and 3.9 μg/ml to 10.8 and 0.7 μg/ml. This confirmed that the CaCO3 layer of CaFeMn@EcN decomposed during SGF incubation and effectively enhanced the stability of the inner Fe2O3/MnO2 layer. Collectively, the triple mineral nanocoating presented satisfactory capabilities of protecting both coated bacteria and the MnO2/Fe2O3 layer from harsh gastric environment.

MRI performance of CaFeMn@EcN

We assessed the MRI capability of coated bacteria considering the broad use of MnO2 and Fe2O3 nanoparticles for T1/T2 MRI (31). To simulate the in vivo encounters experienced in the gastrointestinal tract, CaFeMn@EcN was first incubated in SGF for 15 min, followed by suspension in physiological saline (pH 7.4) before MRI analysis. As shown in Fig. 4 (A and B), the longitudinal relaxation rate (r1) of CaFeMn@EcN achieved a level of 5.34 mM−1 s−1, comparable to clinically used gadolinium-based contrast agents (40), and its transverse relaxation rate (r2) reached 1139.77 mM−1 s−1. This suggested that coated EcN could exhibit T1/T2 dual-mode MRI performance in the gut. To validate the MRI ability of coated bacteria at intestinal inflammation sites, CaFeMn@EcN was subjected to a simulated inflammatory environment containing H2O2 and weak acid (pH 6.8) after SGF incubation. Markedly, the r1 value of coated bacteria increased markedly under inflammatory conditions, probably due to the boosted release of Mn2+ ions (Fig. 4C and fig. S6). We then investigated the in vivo MRI performance of CaFeMn@EcN by oral gavage in healthy mice (Fig. 4, D and E). FeMn@EcN and a mixture of free Fe2O3 and MnO2 nanoparticles (designated as FeMn) were used as controls. As expected, CaFeMn@EcN exhibited a strong T1/T2 signal in the transverse colon at 1 hour after oral gavage, with signal intensity increasing over time, peaking at 3 hours, and declining to undetectable levels at 8 hours. This observation suggested that CaFeMn@EcN could aggregate and settle in this location over time, benefiting from the colonization ability of EcN. The subsequent signal diminution might be attributed to bacterial proliferation, coating degradation or detachment, and the clearance through intestinal peristalsis. In contrast, mice administered with FeMn@EcN showed negligible enhancement in MRI signals in corresponding intestinal segments under the same experimental conditions. This could be ascribed to the harsh gastric environment that damaged the imaging contrast properties of the Fe2O3/MnO2 layer as well as the viability of EcN. The FeMn group displayed a similar signal, which could be explained by the damage and random distribution of Fe2O3 and MnO2 nanoparticles following oral delivery. We also explored the temporal kinetics of CaFeMn@EcN to detect disease progression in mice with intestinal infection. Figure 4F visually presented the MRI images of the intestine under three different conditions, including healthy, 5 days after Salmonella infection (termed Infected), and consecutive 5-day treatment with CaFeMn@EcN (one dose per day) after Salmonella infection (termed Treated). T1 MRI was conducted on these mice before administration and at 1, 2, 3, and 4 hours after oral gavage of 1 × 108 colony-forming units (CFUs) of CaFeMn@EcN. The T1 signal in the colon of the Infected group presented enhanced diffuseness and brightness, peaking at 3 hours with a contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) of 2.14 times higher than that of healthy mice, followed by a rapid decline to undetectable levels at 4 hours (Fig. 4G). This could be attributed to the high ROS level of the infected colon (41), which promoted the release of Mn2+ ions from the nanocoating. Following consecutive 5-day daily treatment, the brightness of the T1 signal within 4 hours postadministration markedly decreased to a level comparable to that of healthy mice, suggesting that the intestinal inflammation was effectively remitted by CaFeMn@EcN. These results confirmed that coated bacteria not only showed a T1/T2 dual-mode MRI capability but also demonstrated a potential for dynamically detecting the process of disease treatment.

Nanozyme activities of CaFeMn@EcN

Next, we evaluate the valence states and multienzyme activities of CaFeMn@EcN as the presence of Fe2O3 and MnO2 nanoparticles on the surface. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectrum of CaFeMn@EcN illustrated the characteristic peaks of Ca 2p, Mn 2p, and Fe 2p (Fig. 5A). Specifically, the Ca 2p spectrum appeared two characteristic peaks at 347.1 and 350.6 eV, separately corresponding to Ca2+ (2p3/2) and Ca2+ (2p1/2) (Fig. 5B) (42). The Mn 2p spectrum displayed two peaks at 641.7 and 653.1 eV, referring to the spin-orbit peaks of Mn4+ (2p3/2) and Mn4+ (2p1/2), respectively (Fig. 5C) (43). The Fe 2p spectrum exhibited two peaks at 710.8 and 724.5 eV, assigning to Fe3+ (2p3/2) and Fe3+ (2p1/2) (Fig. 5D) (44). These results further supported that the nanocoating was mainly composed of CaCO3, MnO2, and Fe2O3. The multienzyme activities of the nanocoating, particularly the SOD-like and CAT-like activities, were investigated with or without SGF treatment. Note that SOD can catalyze the dismutation of superoxide anions (·O2−) to produce hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and oxygen (O2), whereas CAT is able to catalyze the conversion of H2O2 into water and O2 (Fig. 5E) (45, 46). SOD-like activity of the nanocoating was estimated by using a nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) assay (Fig. 5F). Expectedly, native EcN showed negligible SOD activity comparable to the blank group as it failed to scavenge ·O2− to inhibit the reduction of NBT. In contrast, both FeMn@EcN and CaFeMn@EcN demonstrated a high SOD activity with ·O2− scavenging ratios reaching up to 68.3 and 64.2%, respectively. However, upon SGF treatment, the SOD activity of FeMn@EcN substantially dropped to 15.11%, due to the disruption of the MnO2/Fe2O3 layer by SGF. Differently, CaFeMn@EcN maintained its SOD activity at 48.53%, owing to the protective effect of the CaCO3 layer. CAT-like activity of the nanocoating was performed by a CAT assay, and similar results were obtained (Fig. 5, G and H). Both FeMn@EcN and CaFeMn@EcN presented a strong H2O2 scavenging ability. After treatment with SGF, the H2O2 scavenging ability of FeMn@EcN largely decreased, whereas that of CaFeMn@EcN was nearly unaffected. The production of O2 was also measured by using a dissolved oxygen meter to further assess the CAT-like activity of the nanocoating (Fig. 5I). FeMn@EcN and CaFeMn@EcN were found to produce up to 32.61 and 29.43 parts per million (ppm) of dissolved oxygen after coincubation with H2O2, respectively, exceeding the level in regular water (47). Following SGF treatment, the dissolved oxygen level of FeMn@EcN decreased to 15.72 ppm, whereas CaFeMn@EcN maintained at a level of 25.48 ppm. These results indicated that the triple mineral nanocoating had remarkable SOD-like and CAT-like nanozyme activities, even after exposure to SGF.

Potential of CaFeMn@EcN for intervening colitis

Having confirmed its stable MRI capability and multienzyme activities, we then turned our attention to evaluate the potential of CaFeMn@EcN for therapeutic applications. Colitis, with a characteristic inflammation of the inner lining of the colon, influences millions of people worldwide (48, 49). EcN, a clinically used living agent for treating colitis, exerts its beneficial effects primarily by suppressing the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, reinforcing the intestinal mucosal barrier, and enhancing epithelial integrity (7). However, its application suffers from low oral availability and a unitary therapeutic modality (50, 51). In light of the ability of the nanocoating to protect EcN and scavenge ROS accompanied with inflammation, we hence assessed the efficacy of CaFeMn@EcN in treating colitis. Mice were orally gavaged with 5 × 107 CFUs of attenuated Salmonella to develop an animal mouse model of infection-induced colitis. Five days after infection (set as day 1), daily oral administration of 1 × 108 CFUs of CaFeMn@EcN for 5 days was conducted and mice were euthanized at day 6 for sample collection and analysis (Fig. 6A). Infected mice treated with free EcN, MnO2-modified EcN (termed Mn@EcN), or FeMn@EcN with an equivalent amount of EcN, as well as infected mice treated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and healthy mice were used as controls. As expected, mice encountered a pronounced reduction in body weight upon Salmonella infection. Treatment with CaFeMn@EcN effectively restored the body weight of infected mice to a level comparable to healthy mice, whereas the PBS group showed a slightly increased body weight (fig. S7). The colon length of mice treated with CaFeMn@EcN (72.4 ± 3.6 mm) was notably longer than those in the PBS group (57.7 ± 2.4 mm), the EcN group (66.5 ± 2.0 mm), the Mn@EcN group (64.7 ± 2.2 mm), and the FeMn@EcN group (63.0 ± 2.2 mm), proving that CaFeMn@EcN could effectively recover the colon length of colitis mice (Fig. 6, B and C). Instead of all other treated groups, the expressions of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) in the serum of mice treated with CaFeMn@EcN, were greatly reduced to levels equal to those of healthy mice (Fig. 6, D and E). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining images of the collected colon tissue revealed that CaFeMn@EcN treatment achieved the minimum crypt damage and architectural alteration among all treated groups (Fig. 6F). EcN failed to achieve clinically comparable outcomes, which might be attributed to the low dose and short-term intervention used in our experimental design. Together, the above results demonstrated the potency of CaFeMn@EcN in alleviating inflammation-related symptoms in the intestine, underscoring its therapeutic potential for treating colitis.

Value of CaFeMn@EcN for treating AS

Previous studies have reported that AS is an extraintestinal metabolic disorder closely associated with gut dysbiosis. Disruption of the intestinal microbiota can compromise gut barrier integrity, induce metabolic and immune dysregulation, and lead to systemic chronic low-grade inflammation, thereby accelerating AS progression (52, 53). Oral administration of probiotics such as EcN offers a promising intervention by restoring gut microbial homeostasis and intestinal barrier function while rebalancing dysregulated serum metabolites such as homocitrulline (54), thus playing systemic anti-atherosclerotic effects. Therefore, we also explored the value of CaFeMn@EcN for treating AS. Six-week-old apoE−/− mice were fed with a high-fat diet for 8 weeks to induce AS. Then, the mice were orally administered with 1 × 106 CFUs of CaFeMn@EcN three times a week along with the high-fat diet. After 12 weeks of treatment, the extent of AS was to assess and then samples were collected for further analysis (Fig. 7A). Healthy mice, AS mice treated with PBS, and AS mice treated with an equivalent amount of EcN or FeMn@EcN were used as controls. As displayed in Fig. 7B, similar to the healthy group, CaFeMn@EcN treatment showed no notable thickening of the arterial wall and no obvious narrowing of the carotid bifurcation lumen bilaterally. In contrast, the FeMn@EcN group exhibited exacerbated lesions, characterized by localized mild thickening of the carotid bifurcation wall bilaterally but without apparent lumen narrowing. The most severe lesions were observed in the EcN and PBS groups, which presented eccentric or circumferential thickening of the carotid bifurcation bilaterally, with mild stenosis of corresponding lumen. These observations suggested that CaFeMn@EcN was able to restore the aorta to a nearly healthy level. Oil red O (ORO) staining was performed on the sampled aortic specimens to analyze lipid deposition (Fig. 7C). The aortas of mice in both the PBS and EcN groups showed extensive dark red areas after ORO staining, indicating a heavy burden of atherosclerotic plaques. Both FeMn@EcN and CaFeMn@EcN effectively reduced plaque formation, with the reduction being more pronounced in the CaFeMn@EcN group. Quantitative analysis of the ORO-stained areas revealed that the average plaque area in the aortas of mice in the PBS and EcN groups accounted for as high as 44.69 and 32.98% of the total aortic area, respectively (Fig. 7D), whereas the aorta of CaFeMn@EcN-treated mice presented the least plaque level, with an average plaque area reduced to 19.67%. We also found that the total cholesterol (TC) level in the blood of CaFeMn@EcN-treated mice nearly matched the level observed in healthy mice, which was substantially lower than that in the PBS group (Fig. 7E). This implied that CaFeMn@EcN was capable of repairing the intestinal barrier, which inhibited the intestinal permeability to reduce the entry of AS-related metabolites into the circulation. Furthermore, vacuolar structures with larger lipid droplets that were formed by increased lipid accumulation were present in the PBS, EcN, and FeMn@EcN groups, indicating the occurrence of steatotic degeneration in liver cells (Fig. 7F). Notably, the liver sections of the CaFeMn@EcN group were found similar to the healthy group, without obvious steatosis. H&E staining of aortic root sections depicted that the increased subendothelial plaques in the PBS, EcN, and FeMn@EcN groups prominently projected into the lumen and contained numerous lipid necrotic cores, whereas the plaques in the CaFeMn@EcN group were notably smaller and had minimum lipid deposits (Fig. 7G). Meanwhile, Masson staining results indicated that, compared to the PBS group, CaFeMn@EcN treatment exhibited stronger positive collagen expression in the plaques, signifying a higher collagen content (Fig. 7H). Namely, CaFeMn@EcN could reduce the formation of lipid necrotic cores and increase collagen content to enhance plaque stability. In brief, coated bacteria verified a potent effect toward AS, as supported by reduced aortic plaque formation, diminished hepatic steatosis, enhanced plaque stability, and lowered blood lipid levels.

In summary, we have reported the utilization of a protective visual therapeutic nanocoating to integrate oral bacterial therapeutics with real-time monitoring and synergistic treatment modalities. Bacteria deposited with a nanocoating of CaCO3, MnO2, and Fe2O3 are fabricated by in situ sequential mineralization via a two-step metal ion adsorption and nucleation procedure. As reflected by uninfluenced viability of the obtained coated bacteria of CaFeMn@EcN, both the preparation process and the formed nanocoating are cytocompatible. Upon oral ingestion, this triple mineral nanocoating acts as a physical barrier, with the outer CaCO3 layer spontaneously reacting with gastric acid and decomposing to release MnO2/Fe2O3-mineralized bacteria. This reaction can cause the neutralization of gastric acid to simultaneously protect EcN and the remaining MnO2/Fe2O3 layer from acidic insult. After entering the gut, the resulting MnO2/Fe2O3-mineralized EcN can serve as a theranostic agent. On one hand, the remaining MnO2/Fe2O3 layer not only acts directly as an MRI contrast agent to track the intraluminal distribution via T1 and T2 dual-mode imaging but also release Mn2+ ions to enable the detection of therapeutic responses through T1 single-mode imaging. On the other hand, this MnO2/Fe2O3 layer functions as a nanozyme with SOD and CAT activities to relieve inflammation via eliminating ROS, thereby playing a synergistic role with EcN to enhance therapeutic efficacy. In two mouse models of Salmonella-induced colitis and apoE−/− AS, the use of CaFeMn@EcN through oral delivery demonstrates superior therapeutic potentials. In short, this work proposes (i) a concept of theranostic bacteria, (ii) a cytocompatible strategy to generate multicomponent mineral nanocoating on bacterial surface via in situ sequential mineralization, and (iii) the application of functionalized bacteria as oral theranostic agents for treating intestinal and extraintestinal diseases. Future works will explore the use of this preparation strategy to modify other bacterial strains and deposit bacteria with different functional mineral nanocoatings and the employment of theranostic bacteria to treat other intestine-related diseases and beyond.

METHODS

Materials and strains

Sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), ferric trichloride (FeCl3), manganese chloride (MnCl2), calcium chloride (CaCl2), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), and PVP (44 kDa) were obtained from Adamas-beta (Shanghai, China) and used as received. PBS (1×, pH 7.4) was provided by Sigma-Aldrich (USA). EcN and attenuated Salmonella were obtained from China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (Beijing, China). LB broth and agar powders were purchased from Hope Bio-Technology Co. Ltd. (Shandong, China) and used for bacterial culture and plate counting.

Growth of bacteria

Bacteria were grown in liquid LB medium with supplement of kanamycin (50 μg/ml) overnight at 37°C and then diluted 1:50 (v/v) to fresh LB medium for a further 3-hour growth. Bacteria were collected by centrifugation at 3500g for 10 min, washed three times, and resuspended in PBS. Bacterial counts were determined by colony-forming assay on LB agar plates.

Preparation of mineralized bacteria

CaFeMn@EcN was fabricated through an in situ sequential mineralization via a two-step metal ion adsorption and nucleation procedure, resulting in the deposition of MnO2, Fe2O3, and CaCO3 on the surface of bacterial cells. Briefly, 1 × 109 CFUs of EcN were dispersed in 1 ml of deionized water, followed by the addition of 1 ml of 1 mM MnCl2 solution at room temperature. After stirring for 30 min, an equal volume of 0.05 mM FeCl3 solution was introduced, and the suspension was stirred for an additional 5 min. Subsequently, 0.3 ml of 0.1 M NaOH solution was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37°C for 1 hour. The resulting FeMn@EcN was collected by centrifugation at 10,000g for 1 min, followed by washing three times with PBS. CaFeMn@EcN was prepared by further depositing an additional CaCO3 layer on FeMn@EcN via the secondary mineralization. Specifically, 1 × 109 CFUs of FeMn@EcN were dispersed in 1 ml of deionized water with PVP (0.2 mg/ml). A volume of 0.02 ml of 0.33 M CaCl2 solution was added at room temperature, and the mixture was stirred for 20 min. Then, an equal volume of 0.33 M Na2CO3 solution was introduced, and the mixture was stirred for an additional hour to complete the CaCO3 deposition. Last, triply mineralized bacteria CaFeMn@EcN was isolated by centrifugation at 10,000g for 1 min and washed three times with PBS.

Characterization of mineralized bacteria

Morphological analysis of native and coated bacteria was conducted using TEM (HT7700). Structural analysis and lattice spacing determination were performed by HRTEM (JEM-2100F). EDX elemental mapping was used on the same HRTEM to determine elemental composition and spatial distribution. XRD spectra were performed on a Thermo Fisher Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi spectrometer to further characterize the chemical composition and elemental valence states of the mineralized coating. Elemental content was quantified via ICP-OES on an Agilent 725 instrument. Briefly, all glassware was presoaked in 1% nitric acid for 24 hours to eliminate interference from residual ions. The dried CaFeMn@EcN samples were precisely weighed and digested in a 2-ml mixture of concentrated nitric acid and 30% H2O2 (v/v = 9:1) overnight. The mixture was then heated at 95°C in a water bath until a clear solution was obtained. After cooling, the digest was diluted with deionized water to a final volume of 10 ml using a volumetric flask and filtered through a 0.45-μm membrane before elemental analysis by ICP-OES. Chemical valence states of Ca, Mn, and Fe in mineralized bacteria were determined through XPS using a Thermo Fisher Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi spectrometer.

Viability assessment of mineralized bacteria

Bacterial viability was determined by plate counting using a standard colony-counting method. Briefly, equal volumes of CaFeMn@EcN, FeMn@EcN, Ca2+FeMn@EcN, Fe3+Mn2+@EcN, and naked EcN were resuspended in liquid LB medium, and 50 μl of the suspension was withdrawn. After washing with PBS, the sample was serially diluted with PBS and plated on LB agar plates. The plates were incubated overnight at 37°C, and colony counts were recorded on the following day.

Growth curves of mineralized bacteria

To assess the influence of mineralized coating on bacterial growth, coated bacteria at different stages of mineralization, including CaFeMn@EcN, FeMn@EcN, Ca2+FeMn@EcN, and Fe3+Mn2+@EcN, were individually resuspended in LB medium and adjusted to an OD600 nm (optical density at 600 nm) of 0.15. A volume of 200 μl from each suspension was aliquoted into 96-well plates and incubated at 37°C under gentle shaking. OD600 values were recorded every 30 min over a 24-hour period using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). Each sample was assayed in triplicate, and native EcN served as the control group.

Resistance of CaFeMn@EcN against acidic insults

SGF was prepared by dissolving 0.32 g of pepsin and 0.2 g of sodium chloride in 100 ml of deionized water, followed by the addition of 700 μl of 36.5% hydrochloric acid solution, and adjusting the pH to 2. The resulting solution was then filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane. To evaluate the resistance of CaFeMn@EcN against acidic insult, equal amounts of CaFeMn@EcN, FeMn@EcN, and native EcN were suspended in 1 ml of SGF and incubated at 37°C with gentle shaking. After 30 min of incubation, the bacterial suspensions were centrifuged at 5000g for 5 min and resuspended in PBS. Bacterial viability was determined by spreading on LB agar plates and incubating overnight at 37°C. In addition, bacteria exposed to SGF were also collected for elemental analysis using EDX element mapping and ICP-OES quantification.

T1/T2-weighted MRI performance of CaFeMn@EcN in vitro

The MRI performance of coated bacteria was evaluated in vitro by a BioSpec70/20 USR analyzing and imaging system. Repetition time (TR), echo time (TE), and radio frequency dump (RFD) were 8000, 1, and 0.2 ms, respectively. Specifically, to simulate the in vivo experience of coated bacteria postoral administration, CaFeMn@EcN with different Mn contents were exposed to SGF for 30 min and then transferred into either a normal physiological environment (PBS, pH 7.4) or a simulated inflammatory environment (PBS, 10 mM H2O2, pH 6.8) for imaging to obtain longitudinal relaxation time (T1) and longitudinal relaxation rate (1). Similarly, CaFeMn@EcN at varied Fe contents were exposed to SGF for 30 min and then transferred into a normal physiological environment (PBS, pH 7.4) for imaging to determine transverse relaxation time (T2) and transverse relaxation rate (r2).

Animal studies

Female C57BL/6 mice (6 to 8 weeks old, 17 to 20 g) were provided by Jiesijie Laboratory Animal Center (Shanghai, China). apoE−/− C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were supplied by East China of Normal University Laboratory Animal Centre. All mice were housed and fed under specific pathogen–free (SPF) conditions on a 12-hour/12-hour dark/light cycle at an ambient temperature of 25°C and a humidity of 55%. All animal experiments were performed under the guidelines evaluated and approved by the ethics committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Affiliated Renji Hospital (KY2023-023-B, IACUC-2022-Mi-204).

T1/T2-weighted dual-mode MRI performance of CaFeMn@EcN in vivo

The in vivo MRI performance of mineralized bacteria was assessed in the intestine of healthy C57BL/6 female mice (6 to 8 weeks old). Mice were fasted for 24 hours and underwent enema cleansing before the experiment. Following gas anesthesia, each mouse was positioned in a small animal MRI scanner (Bruker BioSpec 70/20 USR) for abdominal imaging, and the images were analyzed using a magnetic resonance workstation. CaFeMn@EcN, FeMn@EcN, and a mixture of free Fe2O3 and MnO2 nanoparticles (designated as FeMn) were orally administered, and T1 and T2 imaging was conducted at specified time points (1, 2, 3, 4, 8, and 12 hours) to measure the CNR. The CaFeMn@EcN, FeMn@EcN, and FeMn groups were prepared with equivalent amounts of EcN (1 × 108 CFUs) and equal dosages of Fe2O3 (0.006 mg/ml) and MnO2 (0.08 mg/ml).

T1-weighted single-mode MRI of CaFeMn@EcN for the detection of pathological changes in vivo

C57BL/6 female mice (6 to 8 weeks old) were prepared under three different conditions: healthy, 5 days after Salmonella infection (termed Infected), and consecutive 5-day treatment with CaFeMn@EcN (one dose per day) after Salmonella infection (termed Treated). Initial T1-weighted MRI scans were conducted on all groups. Then, the mice were orally administered with 1 × 108 CFUs of CaFeMn@EcN as a contrast agent and imaged again at 1, 2, 3, and 4 hours postadministration. The resulting images were analyzed to evaluate CNR signals in the relevant intestinal segments before and after contrast administration.

Nanozyme activities of CaFeMn@EcN

SOD-like activity was assessed by measuring the inhibition of NBT photoreduction. Solutions containing NBT (75 μM), methionine (13 mM), riboflavin (20 μM), and different samples (EcN, FeMn@EcN, and CaFeMn@EcN with or without SGF exposure, equivalent amounts of 1 × 109 CFUs bacteria) were prepared in PBS at pH 7.4, respectively. The mixtures were illuminated with a constant intensity for 5 min. After illumination, the absorbance at 560 nm was immediately measured. The inhibition percentage was calculated as inhibition (%) = [(A0 − A)/A0] × 100, where A0 represented the absorbance of the blank control, and A was the absorbance of the sample.

CAT-like activity was assessed by measuring residual H2O2 levels and dissolved oxygen production. Solutions containing H2O2 (10 mM) and different samples (EcN, FeMn@EcN, and CaFeMn@EcN with or without SGF exposure, equivalent amounts 1 × 109 CFUs bacteria) were prepared in PBS (pH 7.4), respectively. After a 15-min incubation, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation, and the residual H2O2 content was determined by measuring the absorbance at 240 nm using an ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer. Dissolved oxygen production, reflecting the catalytic decomposition of H2O2, was quantified by dispersing these samples in 5 ml of PBS with H2O2 (1 mM final concentration). The dissolved oxygen levels were continuously monitored for 5 min using a JPBJ-610 L portable oxygen meter and recorded at the fifth minute.

Intervention efficacy of CaFeMn@EcN in a Salmonella-induced mouse model of colitis

For treatment evaluation, C57BL/6 female mice (6 to 8 weeks old) were orally administered with 5 × 107 CFUs of Salmonella to induce colitis 5 days before randomly dividing to different treatment groups. Then, mice were daily orally gavaged with 100 μl of PBS, free EcN, Mn@EcN, FeMn@EcN, or CaFeMn@EcN at a dose of 1 × 108 CFUs (except for the PBS group) for 5 consecutive days. Healthy mice were used as a control. Mouse body weight was recorded daily, and the intestinal tract and blood samples were collected after treatment. The colon samples were collected for length measurement and subsequently fixed in 4% formalin for blinded histopathological analysis by H&E staining. Serum was isolated by centrifuging the collected blood at 4000g for 5 min, followed by inflammatory cytokine assessment using ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) kits (MultiSciences Biotech, China).

Treatment of CaFeMn@EcN in an apoE−/− murine model of AS

Six-week-old female apoE−/− mice were fed with a high-fat diet containing 21.2% lard, 49.1% carbohydrate, 19.8% protein, and 0.2% cholesterol for 8 weeks to induce AS. The mice were then randomly grouped (n = 5) and orally administered with 1 × 107 CFUs of CaFeMn@EcN three times a week along with the high-fat diet. After 12 weeks of treatment, MRI was used to assess the extent of atherosclerotic lesions in mice, and the aorta, liver, and blood samples were collected for further analysis. The free EcN and FeMn@EcN groups with an equivalent amount of EcN as well as the health and PBS groups were used as controls. The degree of pathological changes was evaluated by measuring the lesion area in the aorta from the heart to the iliac bifurcation. For AS assessment at the aortic root, aortic arch, and brachiocephalic artery, ORO staining was performed to confirm the lipid accumulation and plaque formation. The aorta was dissected from the heart to the iliac bifurcation, cleared of surrounding tissue, opened longitudinally, pinned on black wax, and fixed for 24 hours in 10% buffered formalin before ORO staining. Digital imaging of the fixed aortas was performed against a black background, and lesion areas within the aorta were calculated using image analysis software (Image-Pro Plus 6.0). Liver and aortic root samples from mice under various treatments were subjected to histological analysis using H&E and Masson staining. Blood TC levels were also measured through commercial assay kits (Solarbio, BC1985) to assess systemic lipid levels.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. The results were expressed as means ± SD. Statistical analysis was assessed by a one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) test for multiple comparisons, giving P values. Significance was defined as a value of P < 0.05. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001; NS, not significant.
