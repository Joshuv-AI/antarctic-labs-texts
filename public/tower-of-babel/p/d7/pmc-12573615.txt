A deep learning algorithm to detect proximal humerus fractures on radiographs
Authors: John W Sperling, Linjun Yang, Miguel M Girod, Sami Saniei, Elizabeth S Kaji, Austin F Grove, Monty Khela, Rodrigo de Marinis, Joaquin Sanchez-Sotelo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProximal humerus fractures are one of the most common fractures in the elderly. Management of these injuries varies depending on the fracture pattern. It has become recognized that agreement is poor when these fractures are classified according to the most traditional schemes. Deep learning (DL) offers the promise to improve recognition of specific fracture patterns. A first, the necessary step in the development of DL pipelines to automatically classify fractures into specific patterns is to automatically detect that the proximal humerus is actually fractured, so that a fracture pattern DL classifier is not applied to nonfracture x-rays (normal, arthritic, and other). The purpose of this study was to develop a reliable and trustworthy DL approach to detect proximal humerus fractures on radiographs.MethodsAfter obtaining a patient cohort and reviewing and labeling their associated shoulder images, radiographs of fractured (n = 996) and non-fractured (n = 607) proximal humerus were used for this study. For model training, a random search was performed to fine-tune and determine the training hyperparameters. All radiographs were split into six sets. The first five sets were used for the model development using five-fold cross-validation, and the sixth set was used for internal model testing. The best-performing model was determined using the F1 score on the sixth set and was further validated using an external test set of 116 separate additional radiographs obtained after proximal humerus fractures. Saliency maps were generated for visual understanding of the DL model.ResultsThe best-performing model from the five-fold cross-validation achieved an accuracy of 0.972 and an F1 score of 0.969 on the hold-out test set. When further validated using the external test set of 116 radiographs of fractured proximal humeri, the model achieved an accuracy/sensitivity of 0.966, misclassifying only 4 of the 116 fractures. The saliency maps showed that the model focused on the perimeter of the humeral head, including the greater tuberosity and the surgical neck, when detecting the fracture.ConclusionThe DL algorithm developed in this study displayed robust and trustworthy performance when detecting the presence of a proximal humerus fracture on radiographs. This algorithm could be implemented in busy emergency department practices so that proximal humerus radiographs with a high probability of fracture can be flagged for dedicated review. In addition, this algorithm provides the first step to further develop other artificial intelligence tools to better understand and manage proximal humerus fractures.Keywords: Proximal humerus fracture, Artificial intelligence, Machine learning, Deep learning, Image analysis, Convolutional neural networks

Proximal humerus fractures represent the third most common fracture in the elderly after proximal femur and distal radius fractures.2 The incidence of these fractures is likely to rise further with the aging of the population. Although proximal humerus fractures are one of the most frequent types of fractures sustained in this population, their detection can be challenging, especially for the nonspecialist.6,11,13 In some studies, up to 60% of nondisplaced fractures were missed on initial evaluation.5,10,12 The rate of missed one-part proximal humerus fractures (64%) was significantly higher than the rate of missed two-part fractures (27%).10 Notably, a fracture also has a higher chance of being misdiagnosed if it is limited to the supraspinatus insertion on the greater tuberosity. Although nondisplaced fractures that are missed at the time of initial evaluation may do well with minimal to no treatment, timely and accurate diagnosis is essential for more substantial fractures to reduce the risk of potential adverse outcomes; eg, an incorrect diagnosis often leads to a secondary fracture displacement of at least 10 mm in 25% of patients.5 One additional challenge related to the management of proximal humerus fractures is the poor agreement when health care providers classify these injuries according to most traditional schemes. The lack of agreement when classifying these fractures is associated with worse outcomes.7

Recently, artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning (DL) have been shown to be a practical approach for use in orthopedic surgery studies.9,15,16,18 DL models, eg, convolutional neural networks, have demonstrated accuracy and efficiency in diagnosis and radiographic measurement, presenting a promising avenue to detect fractures from shoulder radiographs.1 In certain studies, these DL algorithms have achieved up to 87% specificity and 91% sensitivity.17 Although promising, there has been limited experience using DL to identify fractures in the proximal humerus.3,8 Although those existing studies have achieved high detection performance, they were not externally validated, and hence, that performance may not be generalized to practical uses. Fracture detection on radiographs not only provides the potential to aid in the practice of busy emergency departments, but also represents the first step before the development of other elements of an AI pipeline that can assist in fracture classification based on pattern recognition. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to develop and evaluate a DL tool to detect proximal humerus fractures on plain shoulder radiographs, separating them from normal radiographs or shoulders with alternative pathology (ie, arthritis).

Materials and methods

Study cohort

On institutional review board approval, a retrospective query of our institutional databases was conducted to identify patients who eventually underwent shoulder arthroplasty for nonfracture indications and patients who were treated for an acute proximal humerus fracture. All shoulder diagnosis for both the fracture and the nonfracture cohort were labeled using computed tomography (CT) imaging. The Digital Imaging and Communication in Medicine files of the associated shoulder anteroposterior (AP) radiographs acquired around the same time as the CT imaging for these shoulders were retrieved from our institutional shoulder imaging data registry built using a previously published DL model.16

Dataset preparation

All retrieved shoulder radiographs were initially labeled as “fractured” vs. “nonfractured” according to the CT diagnosis. Two trained medical student human observers manually reviewed all the retrieved shoulder radiographs to confirm or modify the initial label as needed (Fig. 1). Questionable radiographs were further reviewed by a board-certified orthopedic shoulder surgeon to confirm the label. Radiographs that contained pacemakers, tumors, or malunions that were close to the humeral head were excluded. Radiographs that showed a fracture at the humeral shaft were outside the scope of this study and hence also excluded. The resulting image cohort consisted of 607 fractured and 996 nonfractured radiographs. Of those 996 non-fracture radiographs, 441, 331, and 224 were of glenohumeral arthritis, rotator cuff tear without arthropathy, and other nonfracture pathologies (eg, rheumatoid arthritis), respectively. With the numbers available, there were no differences in demographic characteristics, including gender, between the patients belonging to the fractured and nonfractured cohorts.

A you-only-look-once object detection model developed internally was run to detect a bounding box around the proximal humerus region on each radiograph (Fig. 2, A), which was then manually reviewed and adjusted as needed by the two human observers. The proximal humerus region on each original radiograph was cropped using the reviewed bounding box. The cropped image was resized to the final image size of 224 × 224 pixels for the DL classification model development (Fig. 2, B).

(B) A DL classification model with the EfficientNetB3 backbone was trained to classify the cropped radiograph as nonfractured vs. fractured. (C) The whole image data cohort was split into 6 folds, where the first five folds were used for model development using five-fold cross-validation and the sixth fold as the internal holdout test set. AI, artificial intelligence; YOLO, you-only-look-once.

The dataset of cropped images was then split into six sets with approximately equal proportions of fracture and nonfracture images (Fig. 2, C). To prevent data leakage, the split was performed at the patient level, where images belonging to the same patient were split into only 1 of the six sets. The first five sets (hereafter referred to as the developmental set) were used to train and validate the models using five-fold cross-validation, whereas the final set was reserved as a holdout test set after the models were trained (Fig. 2, C).

AI models

Five-fold cross-validation was performed for model training using the developmental set. To implement this, five training runs were conducted to train five DL classification models using the five sets. Each run used 1 set as the validation set, whereas the other four were used as the training sets. The following model training hyperparameters were determined using a random search of thirteen sets of parameters: batch size of 128, epochs number of 200, and a learning rate of 0.0005 with a cosine annealing function. A pretrained EfficientNetB3 model for shoulder radiograph classification was used as the starting point for each training run to facilitate the model learning, and the model was saved when its F1 score on the validation set reached the highest during the training. The F1 score is the harmonic mean of the sensitivity and precision (positive predictive value) and ranges from 0 to 1.0, with an F1 score of 1.0 indicating perfect model performance.4 Random data augmentations, including image zooming, flipping, and rotation, were applied to the training images during training to prevent overfitting. All five trained models were evaluated using the holdout test set (sixth set) to score the models against each other. The model that achieved the highest F1 score on the test set was considered the best and further evaluated using an external dataset (described below). All training runs were conducted on an NVIDIA A100 accelerator GPU (NVIDIA, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

External validation

In addition to the institutional image data used for model training and evaluation above, a set of 116 radiographs from an external institution was collected and used for validation. All radiographs were acquired in the standard AP view and were from patients diagnosed with proximal humerus fractures as confirmed by a board-certified orthopedic shoulder surgeon. The same data preparation was used; the region of the proximal humerus of those radiographs was cropped using the same you-only-look-once model and manually reviewed and adjusted before running the final preferred model on this set of radiographs.

Statistical and evaluation methods

To report demographic information, categorical variables are reported using numbers and the associated percentage in the parenthesis, and continuous variables are reported using means and the associated standard deviation in the parenthesis. To compare demographic characteristics between the development set and the holdout test set, the chi-square test was used to compare the gender distributions in the two sets. The level of statistical significance was set at P < .05.

The performance of the five trained models from the five-fold cross-validation on the holdout test set was evaluated by calculating the accuracy, F1 score, and confusion matrixes. When evaluating the best-performing model (the one achieving the highest F1 score on the holdout test set) using the external test set, as all radiographs show fractures, the sensitivity was calculated to evaluate the performance. A higher sensitivity value indicates a better model performance in detecting the fracture, with a value of 1.0 meaning perfect detection. The saliency map based on the method of integrated gradient was used to provide a visual estimation of what region/feature in the image the model used to predict the binary classification.14

Results

It took approximately 25 minutes to train each of the five classification models during the five-fold cross-validation. All five models achieved good to excellent classification on the internal holdout test set. The models trained using fold 1 to 5 as the validation set (hereafter referred to as model 1 to model 5) achieved average accuracies of 0.968, 0.941, 0.972, 0.965, and 0.954, respectively (Fig. 3, A). Only model 3 was more accurate in classifying fractured radiographs than nonfractured radiographs. This indicated that model 3 was more sensitive and less likely to miss the fracture in the radiograph. The average F1 score of model 1 to model 5 were 0.968, 0.944, 0.969, 0.968, and 0.956, respectively (Fig. 3, A). All models achieved a higher F1 score in the nonfractured class than the fractured class. All models resulted in fewer than 10 incorrect predictions on the nonfractured and fractured classes combined, as shown by the confusion matrices (Fig. 3, B).

Model 3 achieved the highest accuracy in detecting fracture cases, average accuracy, and F1 score, and hence was considered the best model to be subjected to external validation. The accuracy/sensitivity of model 3 on the external test set (which only contained fracture cases) was 0.966, where the model missed 4 of the 116 fractures. The model performed very efficiently: it analyzed all 116 radiographs in less than 14 seconds.

Saliency maps based on the method of integrated gradients were applied to evaluate model 3 further using six radiographs (three nonfractured and three fractured) from the holdout test set. When classifying radiographs as “no fracture” (Fig. 4, A-C), the saliency maps indicate that the model focuses on the perimeter of the humeral head, including the greater tuberosity. When classifying radiographs as “fracture” (Fig. 4, D-F), the saliency maps indicated the model emphasized whether the surgical neck was intact, regardless of the specific fracture pattern. These maps also showed that the model paid attention to different details according to the different fracture patterns.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to develop an AI/DL model to accurately identify proximal humerus fractures on shoulder radiographs, which is important in the evaluation of patients after injury. The results demonstrate that the trained DL models could accurately identify the presence of a proximal humerus fracture on radiographs. Our model 3 was particularly effective and validated with external radiographs.

To train DL models to accurately detect proximal humerus fractures, the patient cohort was selected to include radiographs from patients with a known fracture as well as radiographs from patients who had eventually undergone shoulder arthroplasty due to nonfracture indication. Those shoulders included a mixture of both radiographically abnormal (arthritis) and nonradiographically abnormal (cuff tears without arthropathy) shoulders, representing a relatively comprehensive spectrum of nonfractured shoulders. This method helped create a balanced dataset of fracture and nonfracture shoulder radiographs to train the DL models, allowing the model to learn to analyze various representations of the proximal humerus. The model performance was excellent even when trained using only AP radiograph. This can be helpful in the situation when it is challenging to obtain radiograph of axillary view, which can be difficult to obtain. Data split was performed at the patient level to ensure model development fairness, where the model was trained and tested using radiographic data of different patients. Training from the previously trained shoulder radiographic DL model which has learned features of shoulder radiographs, facilitated the model learning. This is usually referred to as transfer learning.19 The random search of the model training hyperparameters also helped model learning. The reported metrics on the holdout internal test set, including accuracy, F1 score, and confusion matrices, suggest that the trained models can effectively determine the presence of a proximal humerus fracture on radiographs. The best model achieved the best accuracy in detecting the fracture among all five trained models from the five-fold cross-validation. It also achieved the highest F1 score among all five models, making it the best model for further evaluation.

Previous studies by Magnéli et al and Chung et al have reported accurately constructing DL models to identify fractures present in shoulder radiographs.3,8 However, prior studies have failed to validate their models externally. The model developed in the present study was validated using an external image set of 116 radiographs of proximal humerus fractures and achieved high sensitivity. This indicates that our preferred model is robust and less likely to miss fractures when used to predict unseen radiographs. In addition, the DL model interpretability also matters. Our saliency maps demonstrated that the trained model used certain key features (proximal humerus neck and greater tuberosity) when classifying the data as nonfractured vs. fractured. Saliency maps provide a better understanding of the model's decision-making and can assist clinicians in detecting fractures by identifying certain features of images that should be particularly scrutinized.

The following limitations should be considered in the present study. First, radiographs that contained malunions, pacemakers, or tumors were excluded. These exclusions might limit the generalizability of the model in practice. Second, although the model was validated using an external dataset and achieved excellent performance, this dataset size was relatively small and hence may compromise the power of the result. Finally, the task the model was trained to perform was relatively simple: providing a binary classification of nonfractured vs. fractured, where classifying specific fracture patterns is also crucial for planning surgeries for fracture fixation. However, the resulting saliency maps indicated that the model could accurately identify the fracture regardless of its pattern. In addition, this model serves as an initial but important step for future work: it can help identify radiographs of proximal humerus fracture on a large scale, which can be used to develop more advanced DL models to further recognize more detailed information about the fracture.

Conclusion

A DL-based AI algorithm to detect proximal humerus fractures in shoulder radiographs was developed and externally validated. Initial binary classification of radiographs can be used to support clinicians in establishing the diagnosis as well as to assist in imaging registry curation and construction. This model serves as the first step in creating a more advanced shoulder radiograph fracture classifier which will be able to recognize more specific fracture patterns (one-, two-, or multi-part fracture) from the radiographs. Greater generalizability of this model can occur with further external validation datasets across different institutions before incorporation into patient care.
