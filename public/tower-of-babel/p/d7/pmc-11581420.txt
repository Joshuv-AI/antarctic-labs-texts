A Dual Action Platinum(IV) Complex with Self‐assembly Property Inhibits Prostate Cancer through Mitochondrial Stress Pathway
Authors: Muhammad Nafees, Muhammad Hanif, Raja Muhammad Asif Khan, Faisal Faiz, Piaoping Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlatinum(IV) prodrugs are highly promising anticancer agents because they can selectively target tumors and minimize the adverse effects associated with their PtII congeners. In this study, we synthesized dual action PtIV complexes by linking oxoplatin with lithocholic acid. The synthesized compounds, designated as PL‐I, PL‐II, and PL‐III, can spontaneously self‐assemble in water, resulting in the formation of spherical shape nanoparticles. Among the developed complexes, PL‐III appeared to be the most potent compound against all the tested cancer cell lines, with 10 fold higher cytotoxicity compared to cisplatin in PC3 cells. The complex arrests the cell cycle in the S and G2 phases and induces DNA damage. Additional mechanistic investigations demonstrate that PL‐III predominantly localizes within the mitochondria and cytoplasm. Consequently, PL‐III disrupts mitochondrial membrane potential, increases ROS production, and perturbs mitochondrial bioenergetics in PC3 cells. The complex induces apoptosis through the mitochondrial pathway by upregulating pro‐apoptotic protein expression and downregulating anti‐apoptotic protein expression from the BCl‐2 protein family. These results demonstrate that higher cellular uptake and reduction of PL‐III by biological reductants in PC3 cells resulted in a synergistic effect of lithocholic acid and cisplatin, which can be easily observed due to its unique cytotoxic mechanism. This further underscores the significance of dual‐action PtIV complexes in enhancing the efficacy of cancer therapy.

Introduction

The fortuitous discovery of cisplatin by Rosenberg et al. in 1965 and its subsequent approval in 1978 marked the beginning of the prospective use of metal‐based compounds in cancer treatment.[ 1 , 2 ] However, despite the notable achievements of cisplatin in combating various types of cancer, its clinical use is still restricted due to dose‐dependent toxicities and a narrow spectrum of activity.[ 3 , 4 ] Efforts have been made to mitigate the occurrence of systematic side effects by employing a strategy that involves the conjugation of tumor targeting groups or carrier ligands with cisplatin or its analogs (carboplatin, oxaliplatin). This approach aims to achieve the selective accumulation of the metal complex in targeted organs or tissues. [5] Another approach is to move from square planar PtII to kinetically inert octahedral PtIV complexes. From the chemical point of view, two additional axial ligands in the coordination sphere of octahedral PtIV complexes offer further possibilities to fine tune their lipophilicity, solubility, and redox properties.[ 6 , 7 ] This may mitigate the undesired side effects of PtII complexes and enhance the safety profile of these compounds. Once internalized into the tumor cells, the axial ligands will be released following reduction of the PtIV complex with cellular reducing agents such as glutathione and ascorbic acid. This mechanism, known as ‘activation by reduction’, allows these ligands (say bioactive) to work synergistically with the resulting PtII complexes in effectively eliminating cancer cells. [8]

Converting a drug into a prodrug is a promising approach to reduce systematic toxicity. However, prodrugs still face the drawbacks of being rapidly cleared by the kidneys and hepatic metabolism, which is commonly associated with small molecules. [9] An alternative approach involves employing delivery vehicles made from modified drugs that can spontaneously form well‐defined nanostructures. These are known as self‐assembling prodrugs. The relatively large size of these self‐assembled prodrugs can reduce their rapid removal through the kidneys, leading to an extended duration in the bloodstream and allowing for higher concentration at the site of the disease. Furthermore, the drug can be effectively shielded from degradation processes, such as hydrolysis and metabolism, by being confined within the nanostructure.[ 10 , 11 , 12 ] Moreover, carrier‐free nanostructures of prodrugs offer several advantages in terms of high solubility, improved stability, controlled release, and higher cellular uptake. However, there is limited literature on carrier‐free nanostructures based on PtIV prodrugs.[ 13 , 14 , 15 ]

Bile acids (BAs) are an important class of signaling molecules present in bile and associated with the imperative paracrine and endocrine functions. [16] They are biosynthesized de novo from cholesterol in the liver and subsequently secreted into the intestinal lumen, where the gut microbiota convert primary BAs (chenodeoxycholic acid, cholic acids) into secondary BAs (lithocholic acid, deoxycholic acid, and ursodeoxycholic acid).[ 17 , 18 ] Among secondary BAs, lithocholic acid (LCA), also known as 3α‐hydroxy‐5β‐cholan‐24‐oic acid, gains much attention as an anti‐aging molecule because of its ability to delay chronological lifespan of budding yeast cells and alter the metabolic processes such as respiration and production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the mitochondria. [19] It has been reported that LCA and its derivatives have the potential to induce diverse biological effects by selectively activating vitamin D receptors, farnesoid X receptor, and G‐protein‐coupled TGR5 receptor, which are expressed in both epithelial and mesenchymal cells.[ 20 , 21 ] Earlier studies report the conjugation of bile acids to PtII precursors, resulting in the generation of compounds named Bamet‐H2, Bamet‐R1, and Bamet‐R2. Preliminary in vitro assays show that these conjugates were very effective against L1210 murine leukemia and displayed enhanced uptake by the liver as compared to cisplatin.[ 22 , 23 ] It is uncertain whether the increased cellular uptake reported for these complexes is due to specific interactions with membrane receptors or if their inherent lipophilicity merely improves trans‐membrane diffusion.

We herein report the synthesis and mechanistic insights into the anticancer activity of LCA conjugated PtIV complexes (PL‐I, PL‐II, and PL‐III) (Figure 1). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirms that all the complexes can spontaneously self‐assemble in aqueous solution. The antiproliferative effect of these complexes was evaluated against a panel of human cancer (lung, ovarian, prostate, and liver) cell lines. We studied the cytotoxic mechanism of the most active complex PL‐III, which includes its cellular uptake, intracellular localization, mode of cell death, interaction with DNA and mitochondria, interference with proteins, and ability to generate ROS and disrupt energy pathways. We demonstrate that administration of PL‐III leads to a substantial increase in mitochondrial mediated apoptosis of prostate cancer cells.

Results and Discussion

The syntheses of LCA conjugated PtIV complexes (PL‐I, PL‐II, and PL‐III) are summarized in scheme S1. The synthesized PtIV complexes were characterized by NMR spectroscopy (1H, 13C{1H} and 195Pt), and by ESI‐MS spectrometry (Figures S1–S13). HPLC analysis was used to confirm the purity of the compounds (Figure S14–S16). Oxoplatin was synthesized by reacting cisplatin with 30 % hydrogen peroxide. The initial PtIV precursors (PAC‐OH, and PHP‐OH) were prepared using a literature procedure with minor modifications in terms of solvent choice, reaction time, and temperature. [24] To prepare PL‐I, LCA was first reacted with dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC) and N‐hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) in a solvent mixture (THF:ACN) and reaction left on stirring at room temperature for 24 h. Afterward, the resulting dicyclohexylurea (DCU) was filtered off. The filtrate was washed with water to get LCA‐NHS ester as a white solid, which was used in the next step without further purification. In the next step, LCA‐NHS ester was reacted with oxoplatin in DMSO at 60 °C for 12 h. PL‐I was then extracted from the reaction mixture by repetitive washing with diethyl ether. PL‐II and PL‐III were prepared by direct treatment of LCA with PAC‐OH and PHP‐OH in the presence of 2‐(1H‐benzotriazole‐1‐yl)‐1,1,3,3‐tetramethyluronium tetrafluoroborate (TBTU) and triethylamine (TEA) in DMF at room temperature for 48 h. Progress of the reactions was monitored by mass spectrometry, and the final products were purified by HPLC. The 1H, 13C{1H}, and 195Pt NMR spectra confirmed the identity of new complexes. The proton resonances of the amine coordinated to the PtIV centers appear over a wide range from 5.8 to 6.6 ppm. In the 195Pt NMR spectra, the mono‐ (PL‐I) and di‐ (PL‐II, PL‐III) substituted PtIV complexes show a single resonance peak at 1046.77, 1232.55, and 1227.60 ppm respectively, which correlates with analogous complexes. [25] The stability of the complexes was explored in a PBS buffer (pH 7.4) that was used to mimic a physiological condition. HPLC studies confirm that all the synthesized complexes were stable (>97 %) in PBS at 37 °C for 24 h (Figure S17). We also confirmed the stability of the most potent complex (PL‐III, vide infra) in cell cultural medium (DMEM). HPLC study confirms that the complex is stable at 37 °C for 6 h (Figure S18).

Since bile acids are well‐known for their self‐assembly properties, we utilized a nanoprecipitation approach in order to assess the capacity of the synthesized compounds to generate PtIV nanoassemblies. Specifically, 1 mg of the synthesized compounds dissolved in 0.5 mL of DMSO was added dropwise into 5 mL of deionized water. All three synthesized compounds demonstrated the ability to undergo self‐assembly, resulting in the formation of nanoparticles without the need for external excipients. The morphology was detected by TEM, whereas hydrodynamic diameter was determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS).

As shown in Figure 2, all three complexes reveal the existence of spherical nanostructures. DLS analysis further reveals that the particle size was approximately 105 nm–110 nm (PDI=0.20, Figure S19) with a narrow, monomodal size distribution. TEM and DLS results are in accordance with the previous reports where, among other bile acids, LCA also displays the property of spontaneous self‐assembly in water. [26]

TEM images of PL‐I, PL‐II, and PL‐III self‐assembled nanoparticles.

Next, the lipophilicity of synthesized complexes was measured prior to the biological evaluation, as it will influence the anticancer activities of the developed compounds. The lipophilicity (log PO/W) of PL‐I (−0.88), PL‐II (−0.53), and PL‐III (−0.29) were measured using the shake flask method in a 1‐octanol/phosphate buffer system (pH 7.4). The obtained outcome indicated that the monosubstituted complex (PL‐I) exhibits lower lipophilicity than the disubstituted complexes (PL‐II and PL‐III). Lipophilicity is generally considered one of the characteristics of higher cellular uptake. However, there are exceptions where Pt complexes with low lipophilicity demonstrated higher cellular uptake. For example, cisplatin (log P −2.3) had a higher propensity for cellular accumulation in cancer cells compared to the more lipophilic dicarboxylato PtIV complex (log P +0.6). [27] Therefore, the cellular uptake can be influenced by the intrinsic characteristics of the drug molecule.

The cytotoxicity of the synthesized complexes was then measured at 72 h on a panel of human cancer cell lines, including HeLa (cervical), A549 (lung), PC3 (androgen negative, prostate), LNCaP (androgen positive, prostate) and HepG2 (liver) as well as against RWPE−I (normal prostate) cells using the MTT assay (Table 1). All three complexes exhibited a half maximum inhibitory concentration (IC50) within the low micromolar range. It was observed that ligand LCA displayed moderate activity with an IC50 value from 50 μM to 70 μM in various cancer cell lines. Notably, PL‐III exhibited the highest sensitivity toward PC3 cells and demonstrated a 10.37‐fold increase in cytotoxicity compared to cisplatin. This may correlate with its higher cellular uptake (vide infra). PL‐I and PL‐II exhibit a more favorable cytotoxicity profile in comparison with cisplatin. However, the cytotoxic effect of PL‐I and PL‐II is comparatively less pronounced than that of PL‐III, which may account for the decreased cellular uptake of these compounds. To compare, all complexes were evaluated for their potential toxicity against a prostate epithelial cell line. It was found that the complexes exhibited a slightly lower level of toxicity in comparison to their cytotoxicity against cancer cell types.

The internalization of PtIV complexes within cells is commonly acknowledged to have a substantial impact on amplifying the cytotoxic effects of these complexes. [28] So, we first determined the whole cells uptake of synthesized compounds in PC3 cells using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS). As expected, the uptake of PL‐III is significantly higher than PL‐I, PL‐II, and cisplatin (Figure 3A). The results obtained are in line with the observed cytotoxicity data. Due to the broad spectrum of activity and higher potency of PL‐III against PC3 cells, it has been selected for further mechanistic investigations. Figure 3B illustrates the cumulative amount of platinum present in PC3 cells following exposure to PL‐III and cisplatin. Cellular uptake of cisplatin is much lower in PC3 cells when compared with PL‐III (47 vs 239 ng Pt in 106 cells). Subcellular distribution studies revealed that most of PL‐III was distributed between the mitochondria and mitochondrion‐free cytoplasm, while only a minimal quantity was detected inside the nucleus. Since cisplatin mainly resides in nucleus, a distinct cytotoxic mechanism of PL‐III would be observed.

Content of Pt (ng/106 cells) in PC‐3 cells after treatment with 20 μM of PL‐I, PL‐II, PL‐III, and cDDP for 6 h. (A) Whole cell uptake of platinum compounds. (B) Subcellular distribution of cDDP and PL‐III.

Most of the disubstituted PtIV complexes are stable in the presence of reducing agents such as ascorbic acid and glutathione.[ 29 , 30 ] Therefore, instead of extracellular conditions, we examined the intracellular reduction behavior of PL‐III complex in PC3 cells using HPLC over a period of 6 h (Figure S20). The ratio between the peaks corresponding to PL‐III and LCA in the PC3 cell lysate was 1:0.9, indicating a reduction of roughly 50 % of PL‐III. It is possible that other forms of PL‐III metabolites were also present in PC3 cell lysate which cannot be detected by HPLC. Previous studies have shown that small proteins such as metallothionein can reduce PtIV complexes. [31] Identifying the specific biological molecule responsible for the intracellular reduction of PL‐III is challenging in cell lysates because numerous cellular components can reduce PtIV complexes. Furthermore, the components responsible for this reduction may differ across different cell lines.

The primary mechanism of Pt based drugs mainly relies on the foundations of DNA damage. Upon entering the cells, they were known for undergoing an aquation reaction prior to forming DNA lesions. [32] Intracellular distribution studies reveal that a small amount of PL‐III enters the nucleus. Therefore, we first determine the impact of PL‐III treatment on nuclear morphology of PC3 cells using DAPI staining. Figure 4A demonstrates that control cells roughly retain a constant ratio of nuclear volume with spindle‐like morphologies. Exposure to PL‐III results in enlarged, clear, and swollen nuclei, and the intensity of this impact increases with time (36 h vs 48 h). We hypothesize that the significant dystrophic alterations observed in the nuclei of PC3 cells may be attributed to the synergism caused by LCA and cDDP after disintegration of PL‐III. We also determine the reactivity of PL‐III with cellular DNA in PC3 cells. As shown in Figure 4B, PL‐III binds to nuclear DNA in both time and concentration dependent manner. Moreover, the expression of DNA damage biomarker y‐H2AX was also increased (Figure 4C) following treatment with PL‐III in comparison with the control. These results demonstrate that a small portion of PL‐III in the nucleus is enough to cause significant DNA damage.

(A) Confocal microscopy images of nuclear swelling in DAPI labelled or PL‐III (1 μM) treated PC3 cells for the indicated times at 37 °C. (B) Platination of cellular DNA. (C) Western blot analysis of DNA damage marker γ‐H2AX in PC‐3 cells after incubation with PL‐III at indicated concentrations for 48 h.

Next, we examined the mode of cell death triggered by PL‐III, employing annexin V/propidium iodide (PI) dual staining technique, which enables differentiation of intact cells, early apoptotic cells, late apoptotic cells, and necrotic cells (Figure 5A). Following exposure to PL‐III (1 and 2 μM), there was a notable rise in the proportion of early and late apoptotic cells, which exhibited a positive correlation with the concentration of PL‐III. The increased potency of PL‐III in inducing apoptosis is consistent with the cytotoxicity results. Subsequently, we assess the influence of PL‐III on the cell cycle distribution in order to ascertain the potential mechanism underlying the inhibition of cell proliferation (Figure 5B). Interestingly, treatment with PL‐III induces cell cycle arrest in both the S and G2 phases, which is unlikely in comparison with cDDP, which arrests the cell cycle only in the S phase. In untreated cells, the proportion of cells in the S and G2 phases was 19.34 and 12.27 %, respectively. Whereas, over increasing concentration of PL‐III (2 μM), the population in both the S and G2 phases increases (36.16 and 28.74 %, respectively). This rise is accompanied by a decrease in the G1 phase. This strongly indicates that PL‐III demonstrates a distinct cytotoxic mechanism when compared to cisplatin.

(B) Cell cycle arrest of PC3 cells after treatment with PL‐III at indicated concentrations for 48 h. Data are presented as the means±SD of triplicate experiments.

Mitochondria have essential roles in both physiological and pathological scenarios. They are critical regulators of energy production and crucial regulators of cell death pathways.[ 33 , 34 ] Given that PL‐III mainly accumulates both in mitochondria and mitochondrial free cytoplasm. Therefore, our initial investigation focuses on the effect of PL‐III treatment on the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) of PC3 cells by 5,5,6,6′‐tetrachloro‐1,1′,3,3′‐ tetraethyl imidacarbocyanine iodide (JC‐1) staining using flow cytometry. MMP in control cells is high, where JC‐1 forms aggregates to give red fluorescence (Figure 6A). The fluorescence of JC1 shifts from red to green after treatment with PL‐III, implying a marked decrease in MMP. The proportion of cells exhibiting depolarized mitochondria increases from 5.5±2.4 % (control) to 63.9 %±4.1 % following treatment with PL‐III (2 μM). We next examine the impact of PL‐III treatment on mitochondrial morphology of PC3 cells using CLSM. Figure 6B demonstrated that untreated cells display a balance between mitochondrial fission (fragmented) and fusion (elongated) events, which is in accordance with the previous reports. [35] Whereas upon treatment with PL‐III (1 μM), a decrease in fission or an increase in fusion is observed in a time‐dependent manner. These results clearly dictate that mitochondrial functions of PC3 cells are compromised after treatment with PL‐III.

(A) Detection of MMP by JC‐1 staining after treatment with PL‐III at indicated concentrations. (B) Confocal microscopic images of PC3 cells mitochondria after treatment with PL‐III (1 μM) at indicated time intervals (visualized by staining with MitoTracker Deep Red).

There is a strong correlation between damage to mitochondria and an increase in the quantity of ROS. Therefore, we determined the intracellular ROS levels in PC3 cells treated with PL‐III by flow cytometry using 2’,7’‐dichlorofluorescein diacetate (H2DCF DA) staining. The generation of ROS stimulated by PL‐III is significantly increased than that of the control group after 48 h of treatment (Figure 7A). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) increased from 120±5.7 (control) to 321±5.3 following PL‐III treatment. The electron flux along with the mitochondrial respiration determines the ROS generation, which can damage the cellular component including mitochondria. Therefore, we next determine the impact of PL‐III treatment on the mitochondrial respiration of PC3 cells by quantifying the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) using a Seahorse XF24 Extracellular Flux analyzer. The OCR profile provides information on basal respiration, ATP production, and maximal respiration levels during oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). Following treatment with PL‐III, a notable reduction in the basal respiration, ATP synthesis, and mitochondrial respiration of PC3 cells was observed (Figure 7B and C) in comparison with the control. These findings indicate that PL‐III treatment significantly impacts the mitochondrial bioenergetics of PC3 cells. It is widely accepted that LCA can inhibit glycolysis. [36] Hence, the impact of PL‐III treatment on the glycolysis of PC3 cells was assessed by measuring the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR). As shown in Figure 7D, treatment with PL‐III leads to a considerable reduction in ECAR. Changes in ECAR in response to sequential injection of glycolysis modulators (glucose, oligomycin, and 2‐deoxy‐D‐glucose (2‐DG)) to PC3 cells were defined to determine the indices of glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserves. As expected, all these indices measured for PL‐III treated cells were substantially lower than those for the control samples under the same conditions (Figure 7E).

The BCl‐2 family proteins play a critical role in regulating the mitochondrial death pathway. This family includes both pro‐apoptotic proteins, which facilitate the release of cytochrome c from the mitochondrial intermembrane space into the cytosol to promote apoptosis, and anti‐apoptotic proteins, which prevent the release of cytochrome c and maintain the integrity of mitochondria. The mechanism of cell death is effectively associated with the up‐regulation of pro‐apoptotic (Bax) and the down‐regulation of anti‐apoptotic (BCl‐2) proteins. [37] Hence, we studied the impact of PL‐III treatment on the BCl‐2 protein family. Figure 8 illustrates that treatment with PL‐III leads to a reduction in the expression of BCl‐2 and an increase in the expression of Bax protein in a concentration dependent manner. Furthermore, administration of PL‐III also results in the translocation of cytochrome C from mitochondria to cytoplasm. This also confirms the damage to mitochondria. Nevertheless, cisplatin did not induce any alterations in the pro‐ and anti‐apoptotic BCl‐2 protein family, hence proving its lack of sensitivity at such a low concentration. Given that, apoptosome complex assembly and activation of procaspase‐9 is initiated in the cytosol after the release of cytochrome c from mitochondria. Once active, caspase‐9 cleaves the downstream effector caspases, leading to the initiation of apoptotic cell death. [38] We found that a higher concentration of PL‐III significantly activates and cleaves caspase 9, thereby validating its capacity to initiate intrinsic apoptotic cell death (Figure 8).

Changes in the expression of BCl‐2, Bax, cytochrome C, and caspase 9 in PC3 cells after treatment with cDDP and PL‐III at the indicated concentrations for 48 h.

Conclusions

In recent years, PtIV complexes have emerged as promising candidates for pharmacological applications due to their enhanced cytotoxicity and capacity to reduce the adverse effects associated with PtII based clinical drugs. In this study, we develop a dual‐action PtIV complexes by anchoring LCA at the axial position of PtIV precursor. Their in vitro anticancer activity was evaluated against a panel of human cancer cell lines, which was correlated with their cellular uptake. PL‐III exhibits higher cellular uptake among the developed complexes. The higher lipophilicity of PL‐III is likely the reason for its higher cellular uptake. However, the intracellular distribution of PtIV complexes is influenced by multiple factors, such as the characteristics of the axial co‐ligands, cellular physiology, and interaction with biomolecules within the cells. The complex displayed enhanced sensitivity against PC3 cells. Cellular localization studies indicated that the complex is primarily found in the mitochondria and cytoplasm of PC3 cells.

We conducted detailed investigations to understand the molecular mechanisms involved in PL‐III induced apoptosis. Treatment with PL‐III results in a marked decrease in MMP as observed in the JC‐1 fluorescence shift from red to green. The complex also inhibits mitochondrial respiration, which causes cellular stress and increases ROS production. Western blot analysis demonstrates that PL‐III treatment drastically changes the expression of the BCl‐2 family of proteins, leading to the release of cytochrome C from mitochondria to the cytoplasm. In addition, western blot analysis confirms the activation of caspase 9 after treatment with PL‐III. This finding is consistent with earlier reports that suggest the release of cytochrome C into the cytosol triggers the activation of caspase 9. In summary, this study has demonstrated that by optimizing the lipophilicity and carefully selecting the bioactive ligands, we can design dual action PtIV complexes as potent anticancer agents.
