Predicting patient satisfaction in digital orthodontics using a KAC based model integrating AI support and risk stratification
Authors: Xie Xiaoting, Nurul Azira Ismail, Ying Liu, Rabab Alayham Abbas, Qing Song, Li Ruiting
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding and predicting patient satisfaction in digital orthodontics is essential for ensuring treatment effectiveness and optimizing human–AI collaboration. However, structured behavioral models for satisfaction prediction remain limited. This study aimed to develop and validate a patient satisfaction prediction model based on the Knowledge–Attitude–Challenge (KAC) framework in digital orthodontics, and to evaluate its discriminative performance and clinical utility in risk stratification. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 500 orthodontic patients and 500 orthodontists across 31 provinces in China. Two KAC-based questionnaires were developed and validated to quantify the three dimensions. High satisfaction was defined as a score of ≥ 4 on item Q6. A multivariate logistic regression model was constructed and evaluated using area under the ROC curve (AUC), calibration curve, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Patients were stratified into low-, medium-, and high-risk groups based on predicted probabilities. Higher Knowledge (OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.09–1.21) and Attitude scores (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.06–1.15) were positively associated with high satisfaction, whereas Challenge scores were negatively associated (OR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.90–0.98). The model demonstrated good discriminatory power (AUC = 0.79) and yielded notable clinical net benefit within the threshold range of 0.2–0.6. Satisfaction rates varied significantly across risk strata: 89.1% in the low-risk group versus 51.3% in the high-risk group (P for trend < 0.001). The KAC-based model effectively predicts patient satisfaction in digital orthodontics and offers strong interpretability and clinical relevance. Introduction

With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) in dentistry, digital orthodontics has emerged as a major trend in modern clinical practice1,2. AI technologies have demonstrated substantial potential in enhancing diagnostic precision, treatment planning, three-dimensional modeling, and automation of orthodontic workflows3,4. Amid this technological shift, patient satisfaction has gained increased recognition as a critical outcome metric, reflecting both clinical acceptance and service quality in digitally assisted orthodontic care5.

However, whether AI-enabled orthodontic interventions truly enhance patient satisfaction remains debatable. While clinicians generally perceive AI positively and report growing acceptance6,7, patients often exhibit heterogeneous levels of understanding, trust, and expectation, resulting in inconsistent satisfaction outcomes8,9. Recent research has indicated that satisfaction is influenced not only by technical performance but also by human factors such as knowledge, attitude, and psychological barriers to AI implementation10.

Existing behavioral models, including the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), have been widely used to explain AI adoption in healthcare. However, these models predominantly emphasize provider perspectives and focus on behavioral intention or usage6,7. In digital orthodontics, few studies have explored structured behavioral predictors of satisfaction from the patient’s standpoint. This lack of patient-centered modeling limits the development of early warning tools and individualized communication strategies.

To address these gaps, this study proposes a structured framework based on the Knowledge–Attitude–Challenge (KAC) model to predict patient satisfaction in digital orthodontics. This model incorporates patients’ cognitive understanding, emotional receptiveness, and perceived barriers toward AI-assisted treatment. Unlike traditional models that emphasize general acceptance, the KAC structure allows for individualized assessment of satisfaction risk grounded in behavioral and psychological dimensions.

Specifically, we aimed to (1) develop and validate a KAC-based satisfaction prediction model using large-scale patient-reported data; (2) evaluate its predictive accuracy and clinical utility through multivariate logistic regression, calibration, and decision curve analysis (DCA); and (3) stratify patients into satisfaction risk tiers based on model-derived probabilities. We hypothesized that higher knowledge and attitude scores would independently increase satisfaction, while higher challenge scores would reduce it.

By enabling early identification of patients at risk of dissatisfaction, the proposed model is expected to support targeted communication strategies, risk-specific counseling, and optimized implementation of AI-assisted orthodontic workflows.

Materials and methods

Study design and Ethical approval

This study adopted a cross-sectional observational design focused on patient-reported outcomes in digital orthodontics. A structured questionnaire survey was conducted to evaluate three behavioral dimensions—Knowledge, Attitude, and Challenge (KAC)—related to AI-assisted orthodontic treatment. The primary objective was to develop a predictive model of patient satisfaction based on the KAC framework.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of North Sichuan Medical College (Approval No. [NSMC-LLWYH-20240023]). All participants provided written informed consent. This study adhered to the STROBE guidelines for observational studies and to the TRIPOD statement for prediction model development and validation, in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki11.

Participants and sample sources

Orthodontist sample (questionnaire A)

Data from orthodontists were collected via the online survey platform “Wenjuanxing” (www.wjx.cn) between April and June 2025. Participants were members of the Chinese Orthodontic Society (COS) and its provincial branches. A total of 500 valid responses were received, covering all 31 provincial-level administrative regions in China. Respondents included practitioners from urban hospitals, county-level clinics, and township dental institutions, representing a broad geographic and institutional spectrum.

Patient sample (questionnaire B)

Patient data were collected from the Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College and its partner institutions. A total of 500 orthodontic patients aged 12–35 years were enrolled. All participants had undergone at least 6 months of digital or AI-assisted orthodontic treatment prior to survey completion. Digital orthodontics was defined as orthodontic treatment incorporating at least one of the following: intraoral digital scanning, CAD/CAM appliance design, 3D printing of appliances or retainers, AI-assisted diagnosis or treatment simulation, and/or remote monitoring systems. Treatment modality was further categorised into (i) clear aligner therapy and (ii) digitally customised fixed appliances (e.g., customised brackets, indirect bonding, robot-bent archwires). Treatment modality was also included as a covariate in the regression model. Inclusion criteria were: (1) basic cognitive and communicative competence; (2) voluntary participation; and (3) absence of psychiatric disorders or cognitive impairment. The questionnaires were administered either in paper format or via digital devices under researcher supervision.

Questionnaire design and pilot testing

Based on the KAC theoretical framework, the research team developed two structured questionnaires. Questionnaire B (patient version) consisted of 15 items: 5 each for knowledge, attitude, and challenge. Questionnaire A (clinician version) contained 20 items: 2 on knowledge, 6 on attitude, 5 on perceived experience, and 7 on challenge. All items were rated on Likert-type scales. The item pool was developed with reference to recent literature on AI applications in dentistry1,2,6, and adapted linguistically to reflect real-world orthodontic workflows in China.

In February 2025, pilot testing was conducted at the Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College in Nanchong, Sichuan Province, involving 50 patients and 30 orthodontists. Evaluations focused on item comprehension, logic flow, and completion time. Based on pilot results, revisions were made to terminology, item order, and skip logic, resulting in the finalized version of both questionnaires.

Both questionnaires were originally developed in Chinese. Forward translation into English was conducted independently by two bilingual orthodontists, followed by back-translation by an independent bilingual researcher. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion until semantic equivalence was achieved. The full Chinese and English versions of the questionnaires are provided in the Supplementary Materials, together with detailed scoring rules (Supplementary Tables S1–S3). Although both instruments were developed under the same KAC framework, they were designed as parallel forms tailored to the perspectives of patients and clinicians, respectively. Only the patient questionnaire (Questionnaire B) was used in the predictive model, while the clinician questionnaire (Questionnaire A) was analysed descriptively.

Questionnaire scoring rules

Scoring rules followed predefined Likert-type coding. For Questionnaire A, Knowledge, Perception, and Attitude scores reflected more positive orientation, whereas higher Challenge scores indicated greater perceived barriers. The Challenge dimension was operationalised to capture perceived barriers in five domains: (i) cost and financial burden, (ii) technical burden and usability, (iii) privacy and data security, (iv) logistical compliance (time and convenience), and (v) expectation-related anxiety. All Challenge items were coded such that higher scores consistently represented stronger perceived barriers. The full item content and scoring keys are provided in Supplementary Table S1 (Questionnaire A, full), with the domain-level Challenge items listed separately in Supplementary Table S1a.

For Questionnaire B, Knowledge (Q1–Q5) was scored 0–3, Attitude (Q6–Q10) was scored 1–5, and Challenge (Q11–Q15) was scored 1–3. Dimension scores were converted to percentages, and the composite percentage score was calculated as the mean of the three dimension percentage scores. An example calculation is provided in Supplementary Table S3. The full item content and scoring keys for Questionnaire B are provided in Supplementary Table S2 (Questionnaire B, full), with the domain-level Challenge items listed separately in Supplementary Table S2a. Cross-group score comparisons were avoided, as measurement invariance between patient and clinician forms was not formally established.

Reliability and structural validity

All three dimensions of Questionnaire B demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α values of 0.78 (Knowledge), 0.81 (Attitude), and 0.76 (Challenge). McDonald’s ω coefficients also exceeded 0.80, meeting established psychometric criteria in medical psychology research12.

Structural validity was assessed using principal axis factoring with Promax rotation. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.89, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (P < 0.001). The scree plot supported a three-factor structure. All item loadings exceeded 0.60 and cross-loadings were below 0.30, indicating a stable and interpretable factor structure13.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was patient satisfaction, assessed via item Q6 in Questionnaire B using a 5-point Likert scale. Responses of “satisfied” or “very satisfied” were defined as high satisfaction. To ensure robustness, sensitivity analyses were also performed using a stricter cut-off (Q6 ≥ 5) and by treating satisfaction as a continuous outcome; the results are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Predictors included standardized scores (Z-scores) for Knowledge, Attitude, and Challenge dimensions, along with demographic variables such as age, sex, education level, and hospital category. Treatment modality (aligners vs. digitally customised fixed appliances) was also included as a covariate to account for potential heterogeneity in digital orthodontic workflows.

Sample size estimation

Based on pilot data, assuming a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.3) for differences in KAC scores between satisfaction groups, and setting α = 0.05 and β = 0.20, the required sample size was 432. To ensure adequate power accounting for nonresponse, the target sample was increased to 500 participants.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.2) and SPSS (version 29.0). Internal consistency of the scales was assessed using Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s ω coefficients14. Structural validity was evaluated via exploratory factor analysis with Promax rotation15.

Group comparisons were conducted using independent t-tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test, depending on data type and distribution6,16. Correlation analyses were conducted using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ).

Missing data were minimal (< 5%) and handled by multiple imputation (m = 20) using chained equations under the missing-at-random assumption. To identify predictors of high satisfaction, a binary logistic regression model was constructed using the enter method17. Model performance was assessed by evaluating discriminative ability (receiver operating characteristic curve and area under the curve, AUC)18,19, calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test20, calibration slope and intercept, and calibration plot), overall accuracy (Brier score), and clinical utility (decision curve analysis, DCA)10. The calibration slope was 0.98 and the intercept was − 0.02, indicating minimal miscalibration. Internal validation was performed using 1,000 bootstrap resamples to obtain optimism-corrected performance estimates (AUC and calibration).

For risk stratification, patients were categorised into three groups based on predicted probabilities of high satisfaction: low risk (< 0.33), medium risk (0.33–0.66), and high risk (≥ 0.66). These cut-points were selected using tertiles of the predicted probability distribution to ensure balanced group sizes and a clinically interpretable gradient17.

All R syntax and SPSS output files used for logistic regression, bootstrap validation, calibration analyses, Brier score, and DCA are provided in Supplementary Code S1 to ensure reproducibility. All hypothesis tests were two-tailed, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For regression models, odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and P values were reported. Bonferroni correction was applied where multiple comparisons were performed.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 500 orthodontic patients and 500 orthodontists were included in the final analysis, with questionnaire response rates of 96.2% and 94.8%, respectively. The mean age of patients was 22.8 ± 5.1 years; 61.4% were female, and 52.6% held a bachelor’s degree. Among orthodontists, participants were distributed across all 31 provincial-level regions in China, with 58.2% being female and a median clinical experience of 7 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 3–12 years). In the patient group, 84.2% had received digital orthodontic treatment. Digital orthodontics was defined as treatment involving intraoral digital scanning, CAD/CAM appliance design, 3D printing, AI-assisted diagnosis or simulation, or remote monitoring. Treatment modality was further classified as (i) clear aligner therapy (43.6%) and (ii) digitally customised fixed appliances, including customised brackets, indirect bonding, or robot-bent archwires (56.4%).Detailed demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Distribution of KAC dimension scores

Among patients, the mean scores for the three KAC dimensions were as follows: Knowledge, 13.2 ± 2.6; Attitude, 17.8 ± 3.9; and Challenge, 10.3 ± 2.8. All scores followed an approximately normal distribution (Shapiro–Wilk test, P > 0.1 for all). Age-group comparisons revealed that Knowledge scores increased significantly with age (P < 0.001), whereas no significant differences were observed in Attitude or Challenge scores across age groups (Fig. 1).

Boxplots of Knowledge, Attitude, and Challenge (KAC) dimension scores among patient subgroups. This figure displays the distribution of KAC dimension scores across different age groups and gender in the patient population. Knowledge scores increased significantly with age (P < 0.001), while Attitude and Challenge scores showed no significant difference.

This may reflect greater health literacy, increased information-seeking behavior, or more digital engagement among older patients, which can enhance their understanding of AI-assisted orthodontics.

Among orthodontists, similar trends were observed. Notably, clinicians with over 10 years of practice experience scored significantly higher in the Knowledge dimension compared to those with less than 5 years of experience (P = 0.012). In addition, Challenge scores were elevated among clinicians working in primary-level hospitals, indicating stronger perceived barriers to AI implementation.

Patient satisfaction and its association with KAC dimensions

In Questionnaire B, item Q6 was used to assess overall treatment satisfaction using a 5-point Likert scale. Responses of ≥ 4 were defined as high satisfaction, accounting for 71.4% of patients (357/500). Patients in the high-satisfaction group had significantly higher scores in the Knowledge and Attitude dimensions (P < 0.001), and significantly lower scores in the Challenge dimension (P = 0.002) compared to those in the low-satisfaction group.

In multivariate logistic regression analysis, higher scores in the Knowledge (OR = 1.15) and Attitude (OR = 1.10) dimensions were independently associated with greater odds of high satisfaction (both P < 0.001), while the Challenge score was inversely associated with satisfaction (OR = 0.94, P = 0.004). Only patient-reported KAC scores (from Questionnaire B) were included in the regression model; clinician-reported data were analysed descriptively and not entered into predictive analyses. Multivariate logistic regression model for predicting satisfaction

A multivariate logistic regression model was constructed to predict high satisfaction, using scores from the Knowledge, Attitude, and Challenge (KAC) dimensions as well as demographic variables including age, gender, and education level. The model showed good calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow test, P = 0.57), with a calibration slope of 0.98 and intercept of − 0.02. Discriminative ability was strong, with an apparent area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.79 (95% CI: 0.74–0.83). Internal validation using 1,000 bootstrap resamples yielded an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.78. The overall accuracy of the model, as measured by the Brier score, was 0.16. A calibration plot is shown in Fig. 2, indicating close agreement between predicted and observed probabilities. This indicates moderate-to-high accuracy in distinguishing between patients with high versus low satisfaction. Compared to models using only basic demographic variables (AUC typically ranging from 0.65 to 0.7217, the inclusion of KAC dimensions significantly improved predictive performance (Fig. 3).

The results showed that for each additional point in the Knowledge score, the odds of high satisfaction increased by 29% (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.18–1.41, P < 0.001). Similarly, each point increase in the Attitude score was associated with a 42% increase in satisfaction odds (OR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.30–1.56, P < 0.001). In contrast, higher Challenge scores were negatively associated with satisfaction (OR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.68–0.90, P = 0.001). Higher education level (bachelor’s degree or above) also emerged as an independent protective factor (OR = 1.55, P = 0.014). Risk stratification based on the regression model

Based on the linear predictors derived from the regression model, patients were stratified into three groups according to predicted probabilities of high satisfaction: low risk (< 0.33), medium risk (0.33–0.66), and high risk (≥ 0.66). The observed satisfaction rates were 89.1% in the low-risk group, 70.2% in the medium-risk group, and 51.3% in the high-risk group. A significant trend was observed across groups (χ2 trend test, P < 0.001), confirming the discriminative capacity of the tertile-based stratification approach17. Discussion

This study leveraged large-scale data from 500 patients and 500 orthodontists to validate the predictive value of the Knowledge–Attitude–Challenge (KAC) framework for satisfaction in digital orthodontics. A logistic regression model (AUC = 0.79) was developed to stratify patients by predicted satisfaction level. The results revealed that both Attitude and Knowledge were significant positive predictors of satisfaction, while perceived Challenge was negatively associated.

Attitude emerged as the strongest predictor, aligning with recent findings in AI-related healthcare adoption, which suggest that satisfaction is largely dependent on patients’ willingness to engage with new technologies8. This aligns with systematic reviews showing that orthodontic satisfaction often exceeds 90% when clear aligner therapy is combined with effective patient–clinician communication22. Knowledge was the second most influential dimension, consistent with findings that a clearer understanding of digital orthodontic technologies correlates with higher satisfaction17. A recent clinical comparison jointly conducted by institutions in China and Malaysia further demonstrated that AI-assisted diagnostic pathways, especially those incorporating visual simulations, led to significantly improved patient comprehension and satisfaction outcomes23.

A key strength of our study lies in integrating dual-source data—from both patients and clinicians—into a probability-based risk model. Unlike traditional AI perception surveys, our model enhances clinical interpretability and translational relevance6. More importantly, the KAC framework captures affective and risk-related dimensions—such as perceived challenges—that are not included in classic TAM or UTAUT models, providing deeper behavioral insight. In addition, this study adhered to TRIPOD reporting standards, including transparent handling of missing data, internal bootstrap validation with optimism-corrected AUC, and detailed reporting of calibration (slope, intercept, calibration plot) and Brier score, which further strengthens the methodological rigor of our prediction model. By dividing predicted satisfaction probabilities into three risk strata, the model captures the gradient nature of satisfaction and equips clinicians with guidance for preemptive communication.

The logistic regression model showed robust discrimination (AUC = 0.79), good calibration, and favorable net clinical benefit within the 0.2–0.6 threshold range (Fig. 3). This aligns with prior studies that used logistic regression to predict patient satisfaction and adherence19. Although machine learning methods like XGBoost were evaluated during model development, they did not significantly outperform logistic regression. Given the importance of interpretability and ease of clinical deployment, logistic regression was retained as the primary modeling strategy. By explicitly distinguishing clear aligner therapy from digitally customised fixed appliances, our study addressed potential heterogeneity within digital orthodontics. Prior studies have compared patient satisfaction between these treatment modalities, with some reporting better comfort, aesthetics, and lower anxiety for aligners24,25, while others found no significant difference in overall satisfaction26. In our analysis, treatment modality was included as a covariate, and although aligner patients showed slightly higher odds of reporting high satisfaction (OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 0.88–1.74, P = 0.21), the association was not statistically significant. This finding suggests that the predictive value of the KAC dimensions is robust across different digital workflows, thereby enhancing the clinical interpretability and applicability of the model.

Recent evidence suggests that machine learning techniques such as XGBoost and random forests may outperform logistic regression in capturing non-linear interactions and complex feature patterns—for instance, in predicting lip protrusion (R2 = 0.75) and treatment outcome consistency27,28. Future work could integrate KAC variables with objective treatment data and explainable AI tools like SHAP to construct more precise and adaptive satisfaction prediction models29.

The Challenge dimension also deserves further exploration. In our operationalisation, Challenge encompassed five domains of perceived barriers: (i) cost and financial burden, (ii) technical burden and usability, (iii) privacy and data security, (iv) logistical compliance, and (v) expectation-related anxiety. This multidimensional structure aligns with prior studies that highlighted economic cost, technical unfamiliarity, and information insufficiency as major barriers to AI adoption in dentistry30,31. Early identification of patients with high Challenge scores could enable targeted risk alerts and personalized counseling—crucial steps for improving human-centered care in digital orthodontics32.

Limitations of this study include the self-reported nature of data, which may introduce social desirability bias; the current model’s linear structure, which excludes nonlinear modeling; and the study’s geographic concentration in China, limiting external generalizability. Future research should incorporate objective treatment outcomes (e.g., PAR improvement, treatment duration) and AI usage behavior markers to develop a dynamic, real-time satisfaction prediction system. Another limitation is the use of a single-item satisfaction endpoint. Although this approach is widely adopted in orthodontic research for simplicity and high response rates29,33, it may not directly capture adherence, PAR score, or treatment duration. We addressed this by performing sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S3), and future longitudinal studies will validate associations with objective clinical outcomes. A key limitation is that the model has not yet undergone external validation, and thus its generalizability to other populations and clinical contexts remains uncertain. Nevertheless, internal validation with 1,000 bootstrap resamples demonstrated stable model performance. Future prospective and multi-center studies are planned to externally validate and refine the KAC-based model. In addition, measurement invariance between the patient and clinician questionnaires was not formally assessed (e.g., configural, metric, scalar). Therefore, cross-group comparisons should be interpreted with caution, and future studies should establish invariance using multi-group CFA and DIF/IRT approaches.

Conclusion

This study established a patient satisfaction prediction model in digital orthodontics using the Knowledge–Attitude–Challenge (KAC) framework. Knowledge and attitude emerged as strong positive predictors, whereas perceived challenges had a negative influence, highlighting the behavioral dimensions underpinning satisfaction.

The KAC-based model provides practical clinical value by enabling early identification of patients at risk of dissatisfaction. Its risk-stratified outputs can help clinicians personalize communication, address barriers, and improve patient experiences within AI-assisted orthodontic workflows.

Future research should validate this model externally, incorporate objective treatment outcomes, and apply explainable machine learning approaches to further enhance precision, interpretability, and generalizability across diverse populations.
