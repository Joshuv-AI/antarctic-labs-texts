Dynamic response of optic nerve sheath diameter to PaCO2 fluctuations: A prospective clinical monitoring study of the lung-brain axis
Authors: Büşra Pekince, Yeşim Şerife Bayraktar, Yasemin Cebeci, Dilara Cari Güngör, Jale Bengi Çelik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to compare optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) measurements obtained during hypercapnic and normocapnic periods in mechanically ventilated patients and to describe changes in ONSD in relation to ventilation-associated carbon dioxide levels. The study included sixty intubated patients admitted to the intensive care unit with a diagnosis of pneumonia and who underwent mechanical ventilation. ONSD measurements were performed during periods of hypercapnia identified during routine monitoring. After adjustment of ventilator settings, arterial blood gas analysis (ABG) was obtained one hour later, and ONSD measurements were repeated when normocapnia was achieved. The mean age of the patients was 61.6 ± 17.9 years. During the hypercapnic period, the mean ONSD (average of both eyes) was 4.88 ± 0.31 mm. Following the achievement of normocapnia, the mean ONSD decreased significantly to 4.61 ± 0.24 mm (p < 0.001). Furthermore, a significant positive correlation was found between the change (∆) in PaCO2 and the reduction in mean ONSD (r = 0.538, p < 0.001). ONSD measurements differed significantly between hypercapnic and normocapnic periods defined by PaCO₂ levels. These findings suggest that ONSD may reflect physiological responses across different ventilatory CO₂ states.

Introduction

Alterations in blood carbon dioxide (CO2) levels are known to exert significant effects on cerebral circulation through changes in vascular tone. CO2 readily diffuses across the blood–brain barrier, and variations in CO2 tension influence cerebrovascular resistance primarily via pH-dependent mechanisms, thereby modulating cerebral blood flow and cerebral blood volume [1].

Hypocapnia induces cerebral vasoconstriction and has traditionally been used as a temporizing strategy to reduce elevated intracranial pressure (ICP), particularly in patients with acute brain injury [2]. In contrast, hypercapnia promotes cerebral vasodilation, increases cerebral blood flow and blood volume, and may consequently elevate ICP [1, 3]. Thus, fluctuations in CO2 levels—whether arterial or venous—represent an important physiological determinant of intracranial dynamics.

While hypocapnia is often used therapeutically, hypercapnia is frequently an unintended consequence of respiratory failure or protective lung ventilation strategies [4], leading to potential increases in ICP even in non-neurological patients [5]. Recent evidence suggests that such fluctuations in CO2 can be dynamically tracked via ONSD measurements, highlighting its role as a surrogate marker for cerebral hemodynamic changes [6].

Accurate monitoring of ICP is crucial in critically ill patients, especially those at risk for secondary brain injury. Although invasive ICP monitoring remains the gold standard, it carries procedural risks and is not feasible in all patient populations. Therefore, noninvasive surrogate methods for assessing ICP have gained increasing interest in critical care practice. Ultrasonographic measurement of ONSD is one of the most widely studied noninvasive techniques, based on the anatomical continuity between the intracranial subarachnoid space and the optic nerve sheath [7]. Because the dural sheath surrounding the optic nerve is distensible, elevations in cerebrospinal fluid pressure are transmitted to the retrobulbar optic nerve sheath, resulting in measurable expansion—most reliably detected approximately 3 mm posterior to the globe [8]. Regardless of the underlying etiology, increases in ICP are reflected by an increase in ONSD [7].

Patients receiving invasive mechanical ventilation in the intensive care unit (ICU) constitute a particularly suitable population for studying ICP surrogates. Ventilator settings directly influence CO₂ elimination, allowing for controlled and clinically relevant fluctuations in CO₂ levels. Moreover, critically ill patients frequently experience alterations in cerebral physiology even in the absence of primary neurological injury, making the assessment of secondary cerebral effects clinically meaningful.

Given the established effects of CO₂ on cerebral hemodynamics and the sensitivity of ONSD to changes in ICP, evaluating the relationship between PaCO₂ fluctuations and ONSD measurements may offer valuable insights into noninvasive cerebral monitoring. The selection of patients with pneumonia—who lack primary neurological injury—serves as a crucial physiological model to isolate the systemic effects of CO2 on cerebral dynamics. In the ICU, patients with respiratory failure are frequently exposed to PaCO2 fluctuations that may trigger ‘silent’ elevations in ICP, potentially leading to secondary brain injury that goes undetected in the absence of invasive monitoring. By focusing on a non-neurological cohort, this study aims to clarify whether ONSD can serve as a sensitive indicator of cerebrovascular reactivity to CO₂. Therefore, we aimed to compare ONSD measurements obtained during hypercapnic and normocapnic periods, providing insights into the dynamic responsiveness of the optic nerve sheath in a general ICU population.

Materials and methods

After obtaining local ethics committee approval, this prospective, observational study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Since the patients enrolled in the study were intubated and sedated, written informed consent was obtained from their legal representatives. The study was approved by the Selçuk University Faculty of Medicine Ethics Committee (Number: E-70632468-050.04-760993).

Sixty patients, aged 18–90 years, receiving invasive mechanical ventilation in the ICU of Selçuk University Faculty of Medicine Hospital were enrolled. Although ventilator settings were individualized according to patient-specific clinical needs, all measurements were conducted under standardized conditions. Ventilator mode, tidal volume, Positive End-Expiratory Pressure (PEEP), and FiO₂ were kept constant during the stabilization and measurement periods. Ventilator parameters including tidal volume, PEEP, FiO₂, and ventilator mode were maintained constant between measurements, while respiratory rate was the only parameter adjusted to achieve normocapnia. These parameters were managed according to standard ICU practice, although they were not systematically tabulated at each measurement time point. Sedation was maintained using midazolam and fentanyl to achieve a Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale (RASS) score of − 3/–4.

Exclusion criteria included conditions potentially affecting ICP: acute ischemic or hemorrhagic cerebrovascular disease; subdural, epidural, or subarachnoid hemorrhage; acute head trauma; intracranial space-occupying lesions; and a history of ocular surgery, trauma, or infection. Additionally, patients with ocular diseases affecting the posterior chamber (e.g., glaucoma or optic nerve pathologies) and those with chronic CO2 retention, such as Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease, were excluded to ensure the accuracy of ONSD responsiveness. Furthermore, patients whose blood glucose, electrolyte levels, or mean arterial pressure were outside physiological ranges were excluded. Finally, patients in whom normocapnia could not be achieved within one hour of ventilator adjustment were excluded from the final analysis.

ONSD measurements were performed when hypercapnia (defined as PaCO₂ > 45 mmHg) was detected via routine arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis upon ICU admission. Following the detection of hypercapnia, ventilator settings (primarily respiratory rate) were adjusted. During the one-hour stabilization period, continuous end-tidal CO2 (ETCO2) monitoring was utilized via waveform capnography to follow the downward trend and ensure a steady transition toward the target range. A follow-up ABG was obtained one hour later, and ONSD measurements were repeated once normocapnia (PaCO₂ 35–45 mmHg) was confirmed. This one-hour stabilization period was chosen to allow for both arterial CO2 equilibration and the attainment of a new intracranial steady state, while minimizing the potential influence of external clinical confounders.

All ultrasound examinations were conducted with patients in a semi-recumbent position (20°–30°). Using a high-frequency linear probe (L12-4 MHz, Mindray DC-60), a thick layer of coupling gel was applied to the upper eyelids. Images were frozen during the end-expiratory phase to ensure consistency, and measurements were taken from the outer-to-outer borders of the sheath. To minimize observer bias, all ONSD measurements were performed using a standardized protocol 3 mm posterior to the globe, and the final value was recorded as the average of three consecutive measurements. To further assess intra-observer reliability, variability across these three consecutive measurements was evaluated using the coefficient of variation and inter-measurement range. Measurements were obtained from both the right and left eyes, and the average of these bilateral measurements was used for the final statistical analysis to represent each patient’s mean ONSD. Furthermore, ultrasound images were archived, and internal consistency was verified by re-evaluating representative images to ensure measurement accuracy (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R version 4.2.1 (www.r-project.org). Sample size calculations were performed a priori using G*Power. It was determined that a minimum of 54 patients would be required to detect a moderate effect size (Cohen’s 𝑑 = 0.5) in ONSD changes between normocapnic and hypercapnic states, with a power of 80% and a significance level (α) of 0.05. To account for a potential 10% attrition or data loss, 60 patients were ultimately enrolled.

Normality of continuous data was assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances with Levene’s test. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum–maximum), and categorical variables as frequency (n) and percentage. The primary outcome variable was the mean ONSD, calculated as the average of the measurements from both eyes for each patient.

Changes in the mean ONSD over time were evaluated by paired t-tests. Additionally, the relationship between the change in PaCO₂ and the change in mean ONSD (calculated as the difference between measurements, ∆) was assessed using Pearson correlation analysis. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sixty patients (42 men, 18 women) diagnosed with pneumonia and receiving mechanical ventilation were included in the study (Table 1). During the hypercapnic period, patients’ vital signs were as presented in Table 2.

Blood gas analysis during hypercapnia yielded the following results: pH: 7.35 ± 0.11, partial oxygen pressure (PaO2): 70.24 ± 10.78 (mmHg), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2): 55.44 ± 8.44 (mmHg), bicarbonate (HCO3): 27.73 ± 6.39 (mmol/L), Na: 141.47 ± 3.76 (mmol/L), K: 3.96 ± 0.46 (mmol/L), glucose: 157.7 ± 32.03 (mg/dL), hemoglobin (Hb): 10.72 ± 2.01, and hematocrit (HCT): 33.25 ± 6.33 [Table 3].

After adjusting the ventilator settings and achieving normocapnia, control ABG revealed the following results: pH: 7.44 ± 0.07, PaO2: 69.76 ± 27.6 (mmHg), PaCO2: 40.76 ± 5.72 (mmHg), HCO3: 28.07 ± 5.27 (mmol/L), Na: 141.01 ± 4.11 (mmol/L), K: 3.89 ± 0.41 (mmol/L), glucose: 160.5 ± 33.77 (mg/dL), Hb: 10.25 ± 1.51, and HCT: 31.65 ± 4.44 (Table 3).

During the hypercapnic period, the mean ONSD (average of both eyes) was 4.88 ± 0.31 mm. After normocapnia was achieved, the mean ONSD decreased significantly to 4.61 ± 0.24 mm (p < 0.001) (Table 4). The overall coefficient of variation (CV) was approximately 1.5–2.0%, with an inter-measurement range typically between 0.1 and 0.2 mm, indicating excellent intra-observer consistency. Notably, this variability was well below the magnitude of the observed ONSD change.

To further investigate the dynamic relationship between respiratory changes and ocular morphology, a correlation analysis was performed on the change (∆) between hypercapnic and normocapnic states. A significant positive correlation was observed: as the PaCO2 levels decreased, there was a proportional and statistically significant reduction in the mean ONSD (r = 0.538, p < 0.001). These findings indicate that the mean ONSD reflects acute fluctuations in arterial CO2 tension in a predictable manner (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, changes in ONSD during hypercapnic and normocapnic phases were evaluated in patients monitored in intensive care due to pneumonia. Our findings demonstrated that ONSD increased significantly during hypercapnia and decreased markedly following the restoration of normocapnia. These results indicate that changes in PaCO₂ are reflected in ONSD measurements obtained noninvasively, suggesting a relationship with cerebral hemodynamic changes. However, in the absence of concurrent invasive ICP monitoring, these findings should be interpreted as reflecting physiological responsiveness rather than definitive ICP monitoring. Notably, the mean ONSD values observed in our study (4.88 mm during hypercapnia and 4.61 mm during normocapnia) remained below commonly cited diagnostic thresholds for intracranial hypertension. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating elevated ICP in a diagnostic sense, but rather as reflecting physiological variation in ONSD within a likely normotensive ICP range. This distinction is clinically important, as it indicates that ONSD may capture dynamic cerebrovascular responsiveness to CO₂ fluctuations even in the absence of overt intracranial hypertension. Accordingly, ONSD may be better interpreted as a trend-based physiological parameter rather than a standalone diagnostic marker in non-neurological ICU populations.

Numerous studies have established the reliability of ONSD in detecting elevated ICP in various neurological conditions, particularly traumatic brain injury [9]. Soldatos et al. reported that ONSD measured by ultrasound is both sensitive and specific for predicting increased ICP in traumatic brain injury patients [10]. Geeraerts et al. found a significant correlation between computer tomography–confirmed ICP elevations and ultrasound-measured ONSD values [11]. This relationship has been shown to extend beyond traumatic brain injury to other patient populations [12, 13]. Moretti et al. demonstrated that ONSD measurements reflect ICP increases in non-neurological critical illnesses such as sepsis, liver failure, and cardiac arrest [6,21,22]. Robba et al. evaluated 45 intensive care patients followed with sepsis and highlighted that elevated ONSD may signal sepsis-associated brain dysfunction [14]. By focusing on a cohort of patients with pneumonia—individuals devoid of primary neurological injury—our study isolates the systemic impact of respiratory-induced CO₂ fluctuations on cerebral hemodynamics. This contributes to the growing understanding of the so-called ‘lung–brain axis’ in critical care. In patients with respiratory failure, hypercapnia may induce subclinical changes in cerebral hemodynamics that may not be readily detectable without invasive monitoring. Our results suggest that even in non-neurological populations, systemic respiratory changes can exert secondary cerebral effects, and ONSD may reflect these subclinical changes. In this context, our findings should be interpreted as reflecting a predominantly unidirectional, CO₂-mediated cerebrovascular response (ventilation → ONSD), rather than a bidirectional lung–brain interaction. Thus, our results may be considered as contributing to one aspect of the lung–brain axis rather than fully characterizing it. This observation appears to be consistent with the ESICM head-to-toe ultrasound competency framework, which supports the integration of optic nerve ultrasonography as a useful component of the holistic assessment of critically ill patients [15]. Furthermore, the Brussels consensus on non-invasive intracranial pressure monitoring suggests that while no single non-invasive method can currently replace invasive gold standards, ONSD remains one of the most promising parameters for trend monitoring of intracranial dynamics [16]. Consistent with these reports, our data suggest that ONSD changes may also be observed in patients hospitalized due to pneumonia, thus supporting its potential relevance in non-neurological ICU populations. These results underscore the potential relevance of ONSD monitoring in systemic conditions where cerebral autoregulation (AR) is impaired or variable [9, 17]. Our findings suggest that ONSD changes are associated with alterations in cerebral physiology in ICU patients and may provide insight into these physiological processes, without implying a direct causal relationship with ICP.

The vasodilatory effect of CO₂ on cerebral circulation is well established: elevated CO₂ induces cerebral vasodilation and increases cerebral blood flow, which, in the setting of impaired autoregulation, may raise ICP [12]. Crucially, CO₂ is a potent modulator of cerebrovascular compliance and AR. Hypercapnia-induced vasodilation reduces cerebrovascular resistance and increases cerebral blood volume, which can shift the intracranial pressure-volume relationship and alter vascular compliance [18, 19]. Therefore, the ONSD changes observed in our study likely represent a composite physiological response; reflecting dynamic alterations in vascular tone and autoregulatory capacity associated with PaCO₂ fluctuations [20]. This suggests that ONSD may reflect early physiological changes in cerebrovascular status during respiratory adjustments, especially in critically ill patients where AR may be fragile or impaired. Dinsmore et al. emphasized that changes in ETCO2 can be used as a simple, noninvasive tool to detect acute and fluctuating ICP alterations via ONSD measurements in both awake and anesthetized patients [5]. A critical observation in our study is the rapid responsiveness of the ONSD to physiological shifts. The significant regression of the sheath diameter within only one hour of ventilator adjustment underscores the high elasticity and dynamic nature of the optic nerve sheath. This finding suggests that ONSD is not merely a marker of established or static intracranial pathology, but rather a dynamic physiological parameter reflecting short-term changes. This rapid responsiveness may provide supportive information for future research on ventilatory management strategies and cerebral effects of CO₂ management in critically ill patients.

Bala et al. concluded that acute and dynamic changes in ONSD occur in response to hyperventilation and PEEP in patients undergoing surgery under general anesthesia [23]. Consistent with these findings, we observed a significant increase in ONSD during hypercapnia in our patients. Differently from the study by Bala et al., our patients were in the ICU, and we monitored CO₂ levels via ABG. In our study, consistent with the literature, we detected an increase in ONSD values when hypercapnia was detected in the patients’ blood gas. Our findings indicate that the decrease in ONSD is significant not only during hypercapnia but also during the transition to normocapnia. A critical strength of our study lies in the analysis of dynamic shifts rather than absolute values. As the absolute diameter of the optic nerve sheath is subject to individual anatomical variation, correlating static measurements across time points may provide limited physiological insight. Instead, we focused on the correlation between the magnitude of PaCO2 reduction and the magnitude of ONSD regression (∆vs.∆). The significant positive correlation (r = 0.538, p < 0.001) demonstrates a dose-dependent relationship between systemic CO2 levels and cerebral hemodynamic markers, reinforcing the role of ONSD as a dynamic surrogate for cerebrovascular reactivity. This may support the concept that ONSD is not a static indicator of absolute ICP values, but rather a dynamic parameter sensitive to respiratory and physiological changes. From a clinical perspective, the observed mean reduction of 0.27 mm in ONSD—representing a dynamic shift of approximately 6% in response to a 15 mmHg decrease in PaCO2 — may reflect a measurable physiological response. This magnitude of change suggests that the intracranial compartment is likely responding to ventilation adjustments. For the clinician, such a measurable regression could provide non-invasive insights into preserved cerebrovascular reactivity and might indicate a trend toward the restoration of the intracranial steady-state after hypercapnic stress.

Numerous studies in the literature have shown that ONSD can vary substantially during different pathophysiological processes and is therefore considered a dynamic and reversible parameter, capable of responding to clinical interventions. Lochner et al. reported that ONSD responds rapidly to changing physiological conditions, exhibiting measurable alterations over short time intervals [24]. Duyan et al., in hypercapnic COPD patients undergoing noninvasive ventilation, documented a parallel decrease in ONSD with reductions in arterial partial pressure CO₂ [25]. Similarly, Kim et al. observed ONSD reduction during induced hypocapnia in a cohort of 14 patients [26]. These findings support the potential relevance of ONSD monitoring in acute clinical settings. Consistent with the literature, our results suggest that ONSD may provide insight into physiological changes during treatment.

Previous studies have reported high sensitivity and specificity of ONSD measurements for detecting elevated ICP in selected patient populations. In our study, the observed increase in ONSD during hypercapnic periods and its subsequent decrease following restoration of normocapnia indicate that ONSD is responsive to changes in systemic and cerebral physiology. These findings support the role of ONSD as a noninvasive parameter that reflects dynamic physiological alterations, rather than directly confirming its diagnostic accuracy or specificity for ICP. Accordingly, ONSD may be considered a complementary monitoring tool that could assist clinical assessment by tracking trends over time, particularly in response to therapeutic interventions, without implying a definitive measure of ICP.

Furthermore, the dose-dependent relationship observed between PaCO₂ reduction and ONSD regression suggests that ONSD may provide insight into the physiological effects of hypercapnia on cerebral dynamics. While PaCO₂ is often permissively elevated to prevent lung injury, its potential impact on cerebral physiology should be considered, and ONSD may offer a non-invasive approach for exploring these effects.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the absence of concurrent invasive ICP monitoring precludes direct validation of ONSD measurements against the gold standard, limiting its interpretation to a physiological surrogate rather than an absolute measure of ICP. Second, as a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, our results may have limited generalizability. While our a priori power analysis targeted a medium effect size, certain subtle physiological shifts might require larger cohorts for fuller characterization. Additionally, our measurements were obtained at discrete time points; although this allowed for a clear comparison of physiological states, it may not capture the continuous kinetics of ONSD changes during the stabilization period. Third, the heterogeneous nature of the study population and unmeasured differences in lung compliance or respiratory mechanics may have influenced PaCO₂ responsiveness. Although ventilator parameters such as tidal volume, PEEP, FiO₂, and ventilator mode were maintained constant, they were not systematically recorded at each measurement time point, which may represent a potential confounding factor, particularly given the known influence of PEEP on ONSD. While we maintained constant ventilator settings and sedation, the presence of mild hypoxemia or subtle changes in vascular compliance within the one-hour interval could also serve as potential confounders. Furthermore, while the primary outcome (ONSD change) demonstrated a clear effect, several secondary parameters—including arterial blood gas components such as pH, PaO₂, and HCO₃⁻—showed comparatively smaller or non-significant changes, which may require larger cohorts for more precise characterization. Fourth, all measurements were performed by a single observer to ensure high internal consistency, an approach supported by the seminal work of Tayal et al. [27]; however, this limits the assessment of interobserver variability. Finally, while baseline severity was assessed via SOFA scores, clinical parameters were not reassessed between measurements due to the short study duration. Furthermore, integrating other non-invasive modalities, such as Transcranial Color-Coded Doppler (TCCD), in future research could complement ONSD findings and provide a more comprehensive interpretation of cerebrovascular reactivity.

Conclusion

The present study shows that PaCO₂ fluctuations are associated with significant and reversible changes in ONSD in mechanically ventilated patients. Beyond the observed expansion during hypercapnia and regression during normocapnia, the significant positive correlation between the magnitude of PaCO₂ reduction and ONSD regression supports the interpretation that ONSD behaves as a dynamic and sensitive indicator of respiratory-induced cerebral hemodynamic shifts. Our findings suggest that ONSD may reflect cerebrovascular reactivity and provide insight into the physiological effects of ventilation on cerebral dynamics. These results support the potential relevance of ONSD as a repeatable parameter for assessing trends in cerebral physiology in critically ill patients. However, in the absence of validation against invasive ICP monitoring, ONSD should be regarded as a complementary tool reflecting cerebral hemodynamics rather than a substitute for the gold standard. Future investigations incorporating multimodal monitoring, such as Transcranial Doppler, alongside invasive measurements are warranted to further define the clinical role of ONSD in monitoring the secondary cerebral effects of systemic CO₂ fluctuations.
