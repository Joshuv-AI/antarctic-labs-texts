A Genetic Landscape of Euploid Miscarriages From Couples With Recurrent Pregnancy Loss Through Whole Exome Sequencing
Authors: Fanjuan Kong, Zhaochu Yin, Haiyan Zhou, Zhiyu Liu, Wanqin Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThis study aimed to identify candidate genes for recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL).MethodsTrio‐whole exome sequencing (WES) analysis was performed with blood samples and euploid miscarriage tissues from 55 couples.ResultsOverall, 117 de novo protein‐altering variants of 83 genes from 38 miscarriages were identified, with three alterations per embryo on average. A vast majority of the de novo variants were missense/indel and classified as variants of uncertain significance. The de novo variants preferentially hit the intolerant genes and impacted the functional pathways such as Pol II transcription, heart development, and p53 signaling. In search of recessive variants associated with embryo lethality, six autosomal and four X‐chromosomal genes, which are functionally clustered into the pathways such as cilium assembly and regulation of cell morphogenesis, were prioritized for 12 family trios. Of the recessive variants, a large majority were missense and of uncertain significance. Notably, there were miscarriages having inherited biallelic variants and coincidental de novo variants. In perspective of maternal effect genes, homozygous or compound heterozygous missense variants of the MEI4 gene were identified.ConclusionsOur findings reveal a significant heterogeneity and complexity in exomic variants contributing to RPL.Keywords: maternal effect gene, recurrent miscarriage, recurrent pregnancy loss, unexplained pregnancy loss, whole exome sequencing

Introduction

Recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) refers to ≥ 2 not necessarily consecutive pregnancy losses before 24 weeks of gestation (including biochemical pregnancy) according to the definition from the European Society for Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE Guideline Group on RPL et al. 2023). It is estimated that RPL approximately affects 3%–5% fertile age couples (Tise and Byers 2021). The risk factors of RPL may include advanced maternal age, previous pregnancy failures, antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), uterine anomalies, endocrine disorders, infection, genetic variation, lifestyle risk factors (e.g., tobacco, alcohol) (Nielsen et al. 2024; Ticconi et al. 2023). However, when referencing the diagnostic guidelines of the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM), ESHRE or the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (RCOG), over 50% of cases are still unexplained (Colley et al. 2019; Vomstein et al. 2021). In recent years, our understanding of the etiology of RPL has advanced greatly with the utility of clinical genetic testing, particularly chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) of miscarriage tissue, which indicates that aneuploidy is a common cause of RPL (Eliwa et al. 2024).

In the era of next generation sequencing technologies, the decreasing cost of high‐performance whole exome sequencing (WES) and whole genome sequencing (WGS) promotes further investigation on the genetic etiology of RPL with respect to unexplained cases after excluding chromosomal aberrations and copy number variations (CNVs) (Rajcan‐Separovic 2020). In a previous study, WES followed by bioinformatics analysis on eight family trios with unexplained recurrent miscarriage identified six novel candidate genes (Wang et al. 2023). It is anticipated that more monogenic causes can be identified in a larger cohort of family trios with RPL.

In the present study, we report the genetic findings of 55 couples with RPL and their euploid miscarriages based on WES analysis. The findings are meaningful to patients and clinicians from both genetic counseling and pregnancy management perspectives.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Approval

This prospective study was conducted with the approval of the Medical Ethics Committee of Hunan Provincial Maternal and Child Health Care Hospital (No. EC20180319). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Subjects

The couples newly diagnosed as a miscarriage meeting the definition of RPL (ESHRE, 2023) were recruited from obstetric outpatients of the hospital. The exclusion criteria were extremely maternal advanced age (≥ 45 years), abnormal utero anatomy, thrombophilic disorders, endocrine disorders, uterine infection, and unhealthy lifestyle risk factors (e.g., tobacco, alcohol). The couples were further screened for inclusion into the study based on normal karyotypes in the couples as revealed by conventional karyotyping and no pathogenic CNVs identified from chromosome microarray analysis (CMA) of miscarriage tissues.

Clinical Samples

Two ml of peripheral venous blood samples were drawn from the couples into K2EDTA tubes. Expelled or retained products of conception (POC) from early pregnancy loss, or fetal (skin) tissue from pregnancy loss at mid‐gestation were collected. The tissues were washed in buffered saline to remove any blood and were placed into a 15 mL falcon tube for storage at −80°C.

Whole Exome Sequencing

To generate sequencing libraries, firstly, genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood or miscarriage tissue using a QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen GmBH, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's protocol. Maternal cell contamination (MCC) was determined using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. An amount of 50 ng DNA was interrupted to 200 bp around by fragmentation enzymes. The DNA fragments were then end repaired, and an adenine base was added to the 3′end. Secondly, the DNA fragments were ligated with barcoded sequencing adaptors, and fragments about 320 bp were collected using XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). After PCR amplification, the DNA fragments were hybridized and captured by NanoWES V2.1 (Berry Genomics, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol as described elsewhere (Duan et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024). The hybrid products were eluted and collected, and then subjected to PCR amplification and subsequent purification. Next, the libraries were quantified by qPCR and size distribution was determined using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Finally, Novaseq6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) with 150 bp pair‐end sequencing mode was used for sequencing the genomic DNA. Raw image files were processed using Illumina CASAVA v1.82 software for base calling and generating raw data (fastq files). The fastq files underwent quality control. The FASTX‐Toolkit and Cutadapt were used to remove primers, adapters, and low quality sequence. Variant call format (VCF) files were generated using the GATK software (https://software.broadinstitute.org/gatk/).

Variant Analysis

Quality control for VCF files was performed using Bcftools software to remove variants of depth of coverage (DP) < 10 or genotype quality score (GQ) < 20. The Exomiser application (Smedley et al. 2015) was used to search for candidate variants in VCF files of parent‐POC trios.

To identify the de novo variants related to pregnancy loss, variants presenting only in the miscarriage tissues and with MAF < 1% were picked out, and then screened in terms of a list of 6289 genes associated with embryonic/prenatal/perinatal lethality in Mouse Genome Informatics (www.informatics.jax.org) (Data S1). Further, the ‘definitive’ protein‐altering variants, including missense, indel, frameshift, canonical splicing, stop‐gain and start‐lost alterations were considered.

Regarding the recessive candidate variants, the inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) MAF in gnomAD < 1%; (ii) being associated with at least one of the Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) annotations including miscarriage (HP: 0005268), intrauterine growth retardation (HP: 0001511), akinesia (HP: 0002304), fetal akinesia sequence (HP: 0001989); (iii) compatibility with autosomal/X‐linked recessive inheritance. Over‐represented variants (≥ 3 counts in the study cohort) were discarded. The prioritized variants were further contrasted with the data of 200 healthy controls to filter out the homozygous and hemizygous variants that were also present or at high frequency (≥ 4 counts) in the controls. Online tools including SIFT (http://provean.jcvi.org/), PolyPhen2 (http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/), MutationTaster (http://www.mutationtaster.org/), CADD (https://cadd.gs.washington.edu/snv), and GERP (http://mendel.stanford.edu/SidowLab/downloads/gerp/) were used to predict the damage of candidate variants. A CADD score of > 15 was anticipated. Two clinical geneticists independently reviewed the final variant lists for pathogenic significance to identify the candidate variants for each family trios being tested.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical description and analysis were performed using Microsoft Excel. One‐sided binomial test was conducted with an alpha (α) value set at 0.05 and alternative = “greater”.

Results

Clinical Features of the Study Cohort

A total of 55 family trios consisting of married couples and euploid miscarriages were finally included in the study. The average maternal age and gestational age were 31.4 ± 4.0 years (range: 24–42 years) and 9.9 ± 4.6 weeks (range: 2–24 weeks), respectively (Table S1).

De Novo Variants in Miscarriages

Following the aforementioned methodology, we identified 117 de novo variants of 83 coding genes from the miscarriage tissues (Data S2). The functional classes of the variants included missense (65.8%), indel (23.9%), frameshift (5.1%), splice acceptor/donor (1.7%), stop‐gain (2.6%), and start‐lost (0.85%). The de novo variants were from 38 miscarriages, with three alterations per embryo on average (range: 1–8). The large majority (81.1%) of the variants were classified as VUS, whereas nine (7.6%) variants of eight genes (ANK2, CHD2, CTCF, HTT, KMT2C, NF2, TBXT, UROD) were annotated as pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP). Previously, a ranking list of Residual Variation Intolerance Score (RVIS) indicative of gene intolerance to a de novo mutation has been established for 16,956 coding genes (Petrovski et al. 2013). Assuming a variant will randomly occur to these intolerant genes, the probabilities to hit the first quartile and percentile of the intolerant genes is 0.38 and 0.04, respectively, based on the total length of the first quartile/percentile of intolerant genes divided by the total coding length of all genes (Kataoka et al. 2016). We looked into whether there was an enrichment of the ‘intolerant’ genes within the candidate genes. Interestingly, 93 of the 117 de novo variants can be assigned to the intolerant genes, with 45 and 9 variants hitting the first quartile and percentile, respectively. Hence, the de novo genetic variations were significantly enriched in the intolerant genes as suggested by one‐sided binomial test (α = 0.05, p = 0.0263 for the first quartile and p = 0.0123 for the first percentile). Further, a cluster and network analysis revealed enrichments of the intolerant genes hit by de novo alterations in functional pathways such as RNA polymerase II‐specific DNA‐binding transcription factor binding, heart development, and signal transduction by p53 class mediator (Figure S1), suggesting the roles of impacted genes in embryogenesis.

Recessive Variants From Couples

In search of embryonic lethal genetic variants of autosomal or X‐linked recessive inheritance, we prioritized 10 candidate genes, including six autosomal genes (CENPF, ADAMTS9, CEP290, RPGRIP1L, ANKRD11, POLG) and four X‐chromosomal genes (NAA10, MAGED2, FLNA, BRWD3) for 12 family trios with RPL. The candidate variants either were absent or had an allele count less than four from the controls (n = 200). Among the candidate genes, variants in CENPF and BRWD3 were recurrently detected in at least two family trios, whereas ANKRD11, POLG, and FLNA are present in the most recently curated PLoV (Pregnancy Loss Variants) database (Maksiutenko et al. 2025). Of the recessive candidate variants, 77.8% (14/18) were missense, alongside with 5.6% (1/18) nonsense, 11.1% (2/18) one‐codon deletion, and 5.6% (1/18) synonymous change that may alter splicing. Following the ACMG guidelines, 11.1% (2/18) of the recessive candidate variants were classified as pathogenic/likely pathogenic, whereas the remaining were variants of uncertain significance (Table 1). Interestingly, the majority of the genomic sites related to the recessive variants are highly conserved as reflected by their evolutionary conservation scores calculated using the phastCons program (Siepel et al. 2005). Results of binomial test showed that the identified biallelic genotypes of candidate genes all can occur with a probability of 4.12 × 10−3 or less based on MAFs in gnomAD (Data S3), suggesting that these biallelic genotypes were significantly enriched in our study cohort.

Apart from the candidate genes listed above, two genes encoding high‐molecular‐weight proteins gained our attention as potential candidate genes for RPL. Compound heterozygous missense (or missense and splice‐site) variants of the SYNE1 gene were recurrently observed in pregnancy losses from three family trios (case ID: 0511, 4498, and 4360) (Table S2). This gene is also known as Nesprin 1, correlated with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita 3 (AMC3, OMIM #618484). SYNE1‐deficient AMC manifests decreased fetal movements and indicates a high intrauterine mortality (Beaudin et al. 1993). We also found miscarriages from three family trios (case ID/history of pregnancy: 4355/G4P0, 4448/G2P0, and 7577/G3P0) carrying compound heterozygous missense variants of the ZFHX4 gene (Table S2). This gene is linked with ptosis, hereditary congenital 1 (OMIM #178300). Interestingly, Zfhx4 −/− mice died within 1 day after birth and exhibited cleft palate, skeletal malformations, and inability to initiate respiration (Nakamura et al. 2021). However, currently it is difficult to predict the effects of single amino acid substitutions on the function of large proteins. Hence, the implications of variants of SYNE1 and ZFHX4 remain to be further investigated.

Maternal Effect Genes

Our analysis strategy also took maternal effect genes into consideration by investigating the presence of homozygous or compound heterozygous variants from the mothers of family trios. Homozygous missense variant NM_001322247.2:c.401C>G (p.Pro134Arg) of the MEI4 gene was identified from the mother of family trio 7524 with a history of G4P0. Compound heterozygous missense variants NM_001322247.2:c.401C>G (p.Pro134Arg) and c.992G>A (p.Gly331Asp) of MEI4 were identified from the mother of family trio 7523 with a history of G4P0 (Table S3). A recent study has shown that bi‐allelic missense variants in MEI4 cause preimplantation embryonic arrest and female infertility, and the c.401C>G (p.Pro134Arg) variant has been characterized (Pan et al. 2024).

Discussion

Taking advantage of next generation sequencing, a few studies have explored monogenic causes of RPL using a case–control research design (unrelated patients with RPL versus healthy controls; Gourhant et al. 2021; Lee et al. 2024), a trio‐WES/WGS strategy of couples and miscarriage tissues (Qiao et al. 2016; Srivastava et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023; Workalemahu et al. 2023) or a case‐only study of patients with RPL/products of miscarriage (Najafi et al. 2021; Xiang et al. 2021). The findings from these studies greatly helped us to understand the genetic etiology of RPL. WES on trio is an effective approach to identify potential genetic causes of RPL. However, the numbers of samples in previous studies were relatively small. In the present study, we recruited 55 couples with RPL and miscarriages of their current pregnancies. Therefore, a relatively large sample size is the strength of this study.

Previously, a pilot WGS study in three families provided evidence for single nucleotide variants (SNVs) relevant to RPL, including a wealth of de novo variants that were loss‐of‐function intolerant, as well as inherited missense compound heterozygous SNVs associated with fetal death (Workalemahu et al. 2023). Accordingly, in this study, we have found that nearly 70% (38/55) of the miscarriages from couples with RPL carried de novo protein‐altering variants, with a very large majority (93 out of 117) falling into the intolerant genes. Considering the autosomal or X‐linked recessive model of inheritance, we have prioritized 10 candidate genes with homozygous or compound heterozygous variants for RPL, revealing a greater number of genes associated with RPL than the previous study (Workalemahu et al. 2023). In this study, 65.8% (77/117) of the de novo protein‐altering variants and 77.8% (14/18) of the recessive candidate variants are missense, being in line with a previous report, where 83.6% (51/61) P/LP variants identified from 30 unrelated couples with unexplained recurrent spontaneous abortion by WES were missense (Mou et al. 2022). Another interesting finding of this study is that the majority (8/12) of the miscarriages with inherited variants in candidate genes coincidentally had de novo variants that hit the first quartile of the intolerant genes, implying a possible additive contribution of a set of genetic variants to RPL and a complex genetic etiology of RPL.

Recently, a genomic autopsy study using exome sequencing or genome sequencing in trio‐ or quad‐analysis has provided a definitive or candidate genetic diagnosis for 105 out of 200 (52.5%) families who had experienced pregnancy loss or perinatal death (Byrne et al. 2023). Among the reported disease‐causing genes is the POLG gene, which encodes the catalytic subunit of mitochondrial DNA polymerase. Interestingly, the POLG gene also showed up in the candidate genes for RPL in our study. Compared with a WES positive rate of 52.5% in the study mentioned above, we have only identified de novo or inherited P/LP variants in 9 families, giving a ‘positive’ rate of 16.4% (9/55). This difference might be explained by the inclusion/exclusion criteria in patient selection being largely different between the two independent studies. By reviewing 421 cases related to pregnancy loss including termination, miscarriage, spontaneous miscarriage, stillbirth, and others, the most recently curated database PLoV summarized a total of 535 variants of 293 genes that potentially contribute to the loss of euploid pregnancies and are classified into three groups: high, intermediate, and low confidence (Maksiutenko et al. 2025). Notably, there are three genes overlapped by PLoV and our study, namely ANKRD11, POLG, and FLNA, all of which are of high confidence in PLoV. These overlaps somehow support the reliability of our results. However, overall, our findings and the PLoV database show that there is a lack of common causative variants for RPL among the published studies, reflecting a great heterogeneity of genetic etiology in RPL.

Ciliopathies refer to a clinically and genetically heterogeneous group of human conditions pathologically characterized by defects in primary cilium structure and function (Leigh et al. 2009). Notably, five out of the 10 candidate genes in the study are ciliary genes, including CENPF, ADAMTS9, CEP290, RPGRIP1L, and FLNA (Vasquez et al. 2021). Centromere protein F (CENPF), localizing to mother centriole, cilium basal body and axoneme, has been implicated in human cliophathy and microcephaly phenotypes. The ciliogenesis regulator ADAMTS9 has periciliary localization and takes participation in hedgehog signaling. Meckel–Gruber syndrome (MKS) is a severe ciliopathy that causes intrauterine lethality and death immediately after birth. Ciliopathy genes TMEM67, CEP290, and RPGRIP1L have been known as MKS loci, namely MKS3, MKS4, and MKS5, respectively (Hartill et al. 2017; Pang et al. 2024). CEP290 and RPGRIP1L are components of a ciliary assembly module, which together with transmembrane protein TMEM67 localize to the ciliary transitional zone (TZ) (Hartill et al. 2017). Filamin A (FLNA) is distributed in cilium basal body, transition zone and apical cell surface. FLNA plays a role in ciliogenesis, basal body positioning, and regulating Wnt signaling. The enrichment of ciliopathy genes in our candidate gene list implies a causal relationship between defects in cilia biogenesis or function and RPL. It would be an interesting study to explore whether variants of ciliopathy genes are one of the common causes of RPL based on more clinical and genetic evidence.

In addition to embryonic lethal genetic variants associated with RPL, the maternal effect gene MEI4 could be a candidate gene for RPL, as supported by homozygous or compound heterozygous missense variants of the MEI4 gene from the mothers of two independent family trios. MEI4 protein is crucial for DNA double‐strand break formation during meiosis. Variants of this gene may cause developmental defects during oogenesis, which eventually contribute to preimplantation embryonic arrest and pregnancy loss (Pan et al. 2024).

The strengths of this study include exclusion of chromosome abnormalities and CNVs prior to WES and use of the trio‐WES approach. Though we have recruited a greater number of family trios with RPL than previous studies, the limitations of the study lie in the facts that the sample size was not sufficiently large to detect more recurrent pathogenic variants associated with RPL, and that the vast majority of the identified variants were VUS. Furthermore, paternal genes that lead to embryo arrest and pregnancy loss were not discussed due to the limited knowledge in the field. We expect to further address these issues in future research.

Collectively, trio‐WES analysis reveals that de novo and/or inherited variants impacting gene functions might contribute to RPL, and there is a significant complexity and heterogeneity in exomic variants associated with RPL. Our findings provide useful insights to clinicians for genetic counseling and clinical management of unexplained RPL.
