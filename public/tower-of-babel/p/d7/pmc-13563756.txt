Integrated microbiome-metabolome analysis implicates Acinetobacter guillouiae in arachidonic acid metabolic remodeling and endometrial cancer cell proliferation
Authors: Wenjing Ma, Jie Pi, Huanzhi Wan, Haoyu Wang, Wuyue Han, Min Hu, Jingchun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLesion-associated microbiota have emerged as potential regulators of tumor biology. However, the ecological organization and metabolic relevance of the local endometrial microbiome in endometrial cancer (EC) remain incompletely defined. This study aimed to characterize microbiome-associated metabolic alterations in EC tissues.MethodsWe performed integrated 16S rRNA sequencing and untargeted metabolomic analyses using paired EC and adjacent non-tumor endometrial tissues. Microbial diversity, taxonomic composition, co-occurrence network architecture, and metabolomic pathway alterations were evaluated. Conditioned medium derived from Acinetobacter guillouiae was used to explore its association with arachidonic acid (AA) metabolism and proliferative phenotypes in EC cells, followed by pharmacological pathway interrogation and xenograft validation.ResultsOverall microbial diversity was not significantly different between EC and adjacent tissues, whereas EC tissues exhibited a more connected and centralized microbial co-occurrence network. Taxonomic analyses identified enrichment of A. guillouiae in EC tissues based on 16S rRNA taxonomic assignment. Untargeted metabolomics revealed a group-level metabolic shift in EC tissues, with AA metabolism among the most prominently enriched pathways. In EC cells, A. guillouiae-derived conditioned medium increased intracellular AA levels, enhanced cPLA2 phosphorylation, induced AA-metabolizing enzymes, and activated the TLR4/NF-κB/ALOX5 signaling axis. Pharmacological inhibition of cPLA2, ALOX5, TLR4, or NF-κB partially attenuated the metabolic or proliferative effects associated with A. guillouiae conditioned medium. In a xenograft model, intratumoral exposure to A. guillouiae was associated with accelerated tumor growth.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that A. guillouiae-associated microbial alterations may contribute to AA metabolic remodeling and proliferative phenotypes in EC. This study provides a microbiome-metabolome framework for understanding lipid metabolic heterogeneity in EC and supports further validation of bacteria-associated AA metabolism as a potential therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00877-2.

Introduction

Endometrial cancer (EC) is one of the most common malignancies of the female reproductive system, and its incidence has continued to increase worldwide in recent years [1]. Although most patients diagnosed at an early stage have favorable clinical outcomes, high-risk histological subtypes, recurrent disease, and advanced-stage EC remain associated with unsatisfactory therapeutic responses and poor prognosis [2, 3]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying EC progression is needed to identify additional molecular and microenvironmental factors that may contribute to disease heterogeneity.

Advances in sequencing and metabolomics technologies have expanded our understanding of the microbiome as a potential regulator of cancer biology [4–6]. However, microbiome-related effects in cancer may arise from anatomically and functionally distinct microbial reservoirs. Gut microbiota can influence systemic immunity and host metabolism, whereas vaginal and endometrial microbiota may more directly shape the local reproductive tract microenvironment. In addition, bacteria detectable within tumor tissues or tumor-associated tissues have been increasingly recognized as components of the tumor microenvironment, where they may interact with cancer cells, stromal cells, immune cells, and metabolic pathways [7–9]. In EC, previous studies have mainly focused on vaginal or uterine microbial composition, whereas the ecological organization and metabolic relevance of local tumor-associated microbial communities remain incompletely understood.

Arachidonic acid (AA) metabolism represents a central lipid inflammatory pathway that may be particularly relevant to hormone-associated and inflammation-related malignancies, including EC [10, 11]. AA is released from membrane phospholipids mainly through phospholipase-mediated hydrolysis and is subsequently metabolized by cyclooxygenases, lipoxygenases, and cytochrome P450 enzymes into bioactive lipid mediators. These metabolites can regulate tumor cell proliferation, inflammatory signaling, angiogenesis, immune modulation, and therapeutic response [12–14]. Importantly, microbial products and bacteria-associated inflammatory signals have been reported to influence phospholipase activity and downstream AA metabolic enzymes [15, 16]. Nevertheless, whether local EC-associated microbial alterations are linked to AA metabolic remodeling remains unclear.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the relationship between local tissue-associated microbial features and lipid metabolic remodeling in EC. To this end, we integrated 16S rRNA sequencing and untargeted metabolomic profiling using paired EC and adjacent non-tumor endometrial tissues. We further applied microbial diversity analysis, taxonomic profiling, co-occurrence network analysis, and candidate taxon prioritization to identify bacteria for functional evaluation. Conditioned-medium experiments, pharmacological pathway interrogation, and xenograft validation were then used to explore the potential association between candidate bacteria, AA metabolic remodeling, and EC-related proliferative phenotypes. This study was designed to provide a microbiome-metabolome framework for understanding how local microbial alterations may contribute to lipid metabolic heterogeneity in the EC microenvironment.

Methods

Clinical samples

Paired tumor and adjacent non-tumorous endometrial tissues were obtained from patients undergoing primary surgical resection for EC at Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University (Wuhan, China) between September 2024 and September 2025. None of the enrolled patients had received preoperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy, nor did they have a documented history of major metabolic or cardiovascular disorders. All tumor samples were pathologically confirmed as endometrioid adenocarcinoma. Tissue specimens were collected immediately after surgical excision, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80℃ until further analysis. The collection and use of human endometrial cancer and paired adjacent non-tumor tissue samples were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University (Approval No. WDRY2023-K137). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Cell lines and cell culture

The human EC cell line Ishikawa was purchased from Procell Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). The HEC-1-A cell line was obtained from the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling and routinely tested to confirm the absence of mycoplasma contamination. Ishikawa cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. HEC-1-A cells were maintained in McCoy’s 5 A medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cells were cultured at 37 ℃ in a humidified incubator containing 95% air and 5% CO2.

Reagents

The cytosolic phospholipase A2 (cPLA2) inhibitor CAY10650 (MCE, HY-10801), 5-lipoxygenase inhibitor Zileuton (MCE, HY-14164), Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) inhibitor CAY10614 (MCE, HY-135042), and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) inhibitor JSH-23 (MCE, HY-13982) were purchased from MedChemExpress.

Bacteria and bacterial culture

Acinetobacter guillouiae (A. guillouiae, ATCC 11171) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Bacteria were cultured on nutrient agar plates or in nutrient broth under aerobic conditions at 37 ℃, following the recommended protocols provided by ATCC.

Preparation and standardization of conditioned medium (CM)

A. guillouiae was inoculated into nutrient broth and cultured at 37℃ with shaking at 200 rpm until reaching the logarithmic growth phase. Bacterial cells were then collected by centrifugation and washed three times with sterile phosphate-buffered saline to remove residual culture medium components. The washed bacterial pellet was resuspended in antibiotic-free complete cell culture medium, and the bacterial concentration was adjusted to 1 × 108 CFU/mL. The bacterial suspension was incubated at 37℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 for 24 h. Subsequently, the culture supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 4000×g for 10 min to remove bacterial cells and further sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 μm pore-size filter, yielding sterile CM. A. guillouiae-derived CM. Sterility of the filtered CM was confirmed by plating aliquots onto nutrient agar plates and incubating at 37℃ for 24 h. Control medium was prepared in parallel using the same complete cell culture medium incubated under identical conditions without bacteria. Prior to use, CM was diluted to 5%, 10%, or 20% (v/v) with the corresponding complete culture medium, depending on experimental design. Antibiotics were supplemented according to specific experimental designs to ensure a final concentration of 1% penicillin-streptomycin during cell treatment. All CM preparations were stored at -80℃ before use.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Yeasen, 40203ES60). Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at appropriate densities, and the time point at which cells became adherent was defined as 0 h. Cells were treated with 5%, 10%, or 20% (v/v) A. guillouiae-derived CM or control medium for the indicated time points. At the indicated time points, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. To account for differences in initial cell attachment, absorbance values at each time point were normalized to the corresponding 0 h values.

Colony formation assay

Colony formation assays were performed to assess long-term proliferative capacity. Briefly, 500 cells per well were seeded into culture plates and allowed to attach. Cells were then treated with the indicated concentrations of inhibitors or vehicle control for 24 h. After treatment, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium containing the corresponding CM concentration or control medium as required by the experimental design, and cells were cultured for 14 days. At the experimental endpoint, culture medium was removed, and cells were gently washed with PBS. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min, stained with crystal violet, and rinsed thoroughly to remove excess dye. Representative colony images were captured using a light microscope, and colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Cell lysates were sonicated and centrifuged to collect the supernatants. Protein samples were mixed with loading buffer and denatured at 95 ℃ for 10 min. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 ℃. The following day, membranes were washed and incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection system, and images were captured accordingly. The antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

ELISA

Cell samples were processed according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Cells were harvested, washed with pre-cooled PBS, and lysed to obtain cell supernatants, which were clarified by centrifugation. Intracellular AA levels were measured using an AA ELISA Kit (Elabscience, E-EL-0051), and intracellular leukotriene B4 (LTB4) levels were quantified using an LTB4 ELISA Kit (Elabscience, E-EL-0661).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using a commercial RNA isolation kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cDNA was synthesized from mRNA using Hifair® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yeasen, 11141ES60). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, 11201ES08) on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System. The relative expression levels of target genes were quantified using the comparative Ct method. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

16S rRNA sequencing and microbiome analysis

EC tissues and paired adjacent non-tumor endometrial tissues were collected under sterile conditions and immediately snap-frozen. Microbial genomic DNA extraction, 16S rRNA gene amplification, library construction, sequencing, and primary bioinformatic analysis were performed by Personalbio (Shanghai, China). Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered and processed to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), followed by taxonomic assignment using a reference 16S rRNA database. Microbial composition was analyzed at multiple taxonomic levels. Alpha diversity was assessed using Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, Pielou’s evenness, and Good’s coverage indices. Beta diversity was evaluated using Bray-Curtis distances and visualized by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), with group-level differences assessed by permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). Genus-level profiles were further used for heatmap visualization, random forest feature-ranking analysis, and co-occurrence network analysis. Zi-Pi analysis was performed to evaluate the topological roles of microbial nodes within the co-occurrence networks. Detailed procedures and parameters for sequence processing, taxonomic assignment, heatmap clustering, random forest analysis, network construction, and Zi-Pi analysis are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Untargeted metabolomic profiling of paired EC and adjacent non-tumor endometrial tissues was performed by Personalbio (Shanghai, China), including metabolite extraction, liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) data acquisition, primary data processing, metabolite annotation, and downstream statistical analysis. Briefly, metabolites were extracted from tissue samples and analyzed by LC-MS in both positive and negative ion modes. Raw data were processed for peak detection, retention time alignment, peak integration, normalization, and quality control. Differential metabolites between EC and paired adjacent non-tumor tissues were identified using the criteria of VIP > 1, P < 0.05, and fold change > 1.5 or < 0.67. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. Detailed metabolite identification and annotation information is provided in Supplementary Table 3. Detailed procedures and parameters for LC-MS acquisition, data processing, metabolite annotation, differential metabolite screening, and KEGG enrichment analysis are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Xenograft tumor model establishment and treatment

Three-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at the animal facility of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University and allowed to acclimatize for one week prior to experimentation. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Basel Declaration and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University (Approval No. WDRM-20230410D). Mice were randomly assigned into experimental groups (n = 5 per group) and provided with irradiated standard chow and sterile water ad libitum. At four weeks of age, mice were subcutaneously injected in the right flank with 1 × 107 Ishikawa cells suspended in sterile saline. When tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm3, mice in the experimental group received intratumoral injections of A. guillouiae (1 × 107 CFU in 50 µL saline), whereas control mice were injected with an equal volume of saline. Tumor size was measured every three days, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume = length×width2 × 0.5. At day 21, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised for subsequent analyses.

Immunohistochemistry

Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into serial slices. Immunohistochemical staining and IHC score for Ki-67 was performed according to standard procedures as previously described [17]. Stained sections were examined under an inverted microscope, and images were randomly captured from representative fields.

2.15 Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9) or R software (version 4.0.3). All cell-based experiments were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise indicated. For comparisons between two groups, paired or unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used for normally distributed data, as appropriate, and Mann-Whitney U tests or Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank tests were used for non-normally distributed data, as appropriate. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance followed by appropriate post hoc tests. Correlations were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. Beta diversity differences were evaluated using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) based on Bray-Curtis distance matrices, with R² and P values reported where appropriate. For microbiome and metabolomics analyses involving multiple comparisons, P values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method where applicable. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. In figures, significance levels are indicated as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001; P ≥ 0.05 was considered not significant (ns).

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study cohort

Endometrial carcinoma (EC) tissues and paired adjacent non-tumor endometrial tissues were collected from patients with clinically diagnosed EC at Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University (Wuhan, China) between September 2024 and September 2025. Patients with a history of metabolic disorders, cardiovascular disease, obesity or underweight status, or those who had received antibiotics, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy prior to surgery were excluded. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, paired EC and adjacent non-tumor tissue samples from ten patients were included for subsequent analyses. Paired adjacent non-tumor endometrial tissues were used as the control group. The clinical characteristics of the enrolled patients are summarized in Supplementary Table 4.

Genus-level microbial composition is altered in EC tissues

To characterize the global microbial landscape associated with EC, we profiled the taxonomic composition of paired EC and adjacent non-tumor tissues across multiple hierarchical classification levels. EC tissues generally harbored a higher number of classified microbial features across several taxonomic ranks, particularly at the genus, family, and order levels (Fig. 1A). At the phylum level, Proteobacteria represented the predominant phylum in both EC and adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 1B). Bubble plot analysis integrating multiple taxonomic layers further showed that Gammaproteobacteria represented a dominant bacterial class across samples (Fig. 1C). Consistently, phylogenetic tree-based abundance analysis showed that Proteobacteria was broadly represented in both groups (Fig. 1D). At the genus level, EC tissues showed enrichment of selected genera, including Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas, and Bacillus, whereas Ochrobactrum and Serratia were relatively more abundant in adjacent non-tumor tissues. These findings suggest that EC tissues exhibit genus-level compositional alterations despite shared dominant higher-level taxonomic features.

Preserved microbial diversity with pronounced interindividual heterogeneity

Alpha and beta diversity analyses were performed to evaluate differences in microbial composition in paired EC and adjacent non-tumor tissues. Alpha diversity reflects within-sample microbial richness, diversity, and evenness. Five commonly used alpha diversity indices were calculated, including Chao1, Shannon and Simpson diversity indices, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity (Faith PD), and Pielou’s evenness (Pielou E). No significant differences were observed between EC and adjacent non-tumor tissues across these indices, indicating comparable overall microbial richness, diversity, and evenness between the two groups (Fig. 2A).

Beta diversity analysis was conducted to assess between-sample differences in microbial community structure. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distances showed partial overlap between EC and adjacent non-tumor samples, with the first two principal coordinates explaining 25.3% and 13.0% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 2B). The presence of several separated samples may reflect interindividual heterogeneity in microbial community composition among clinical tissue specimens. Consistently, PERMANOVA analysis did not support a strong global separation between the two groups (R2 = 0.054, P = 0.389). These results indicate that EC-associated microbial alterations are not primarily reflected by uniform shifts in overall community diversity. Beta-diversity component analysis was further performed to characterize the structure of community dissimilarity between EC and adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 2C). Together, these results indicate that EC-associated microbial alterations were not primarily reflected by uniform shifts in overall community diversity.

Hierarchical clustering further revealed substantial sample-to-sample variation in genus-level microbial composition (Fig. 2D). Rather than forming a completely separated EC-specific cluster, the samples displayed heterogeneous compositional patterns, with enrichment of selected genera observed only in subsets of EC samples. To further examine the abundance patterns of representative genera, focused single-genus hierarchical clustering was performed for Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas, and Ochrobactrum (Supplementary Fig. 1). Although these analyses did not produce complete separation between EC and paired adjacent non-tumor tissues, Acinetobacter showed a relatively more apparent EC-biased pattern, with higher abundance observed in multiple EC samples. In contrast, Pseudomonas did not show a consistent group-biased pattern, whereas Ochrobactrum was more prominent in a subset of paired adjacent non-tumor samples.

ASV- and genus-level profiling identifies candidate EC-associated microbial features

To further delineate microbial features associated with EC, we analyzed microbial profiles at both the ASV and genus levels. Venn diagram analysis identified 772 ASVs unique to EC tissues and 980 ASVs unique to paired adjacent non-tumor tissues, with 183 ASVs shared between the two groups (Fig. 3A), indicating differences in fine-scale microbial features. At the genus level, heatmap analysis was performed to visualize the abundance patterns of the top 20 genera across individual samples (Fig. 3B). The heatmap revealed heterogeneous genus-level profiles, with several highly abundant genera, including Acinetobacter, Ochrobactrum, Sphingomonas, and Kaistobacter, showing sample-specific variation between EC and paired adjacent non-tumor tissues. Given the interindividual variability, these heatmap patterns were interpreted descriptively rather than as definitive evidence of differential abundance. PCoA based on selected genus-level features showed a partial group-level shift between EC and paired adjacent non-tumor tissues rather than complete separation (Fig. 3C).

LEfSe analysis further identified multiple genera associated with EC, including Limnohabitans, Bilophila, Haemophilus, and Acinetobacter (Fig. 3D). To complement differential abundance analysis, random forest analysis was applied as an exploratory feature-ranking approach based on genus-level profiles. This analysis ranked Candidatus Arthromitus, Acinetobacter, and Ochrobactrum among the top features distinguishing EC from adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 3E). Collectively, these analyses indicate that EC tissues display heterogeneous but group-biased genus-level taxonomic alterations.

EC tissues exhibit increased microbial network connectivity

To further investigate whether microbial compositional alterations in EC were accompanied by changes in potential microbial interaction patterns, genus-level co-occurrence networks were constructed for EC and adjacent non-tumor tissues. Compared with the relatively sparse connected network observed in adjacent non-tumor tissues, the EC-associated network showed increased connectivity among microbial genera(Fig. 4A-B). Quantitative topological analysis further supported this observation. Relative to adjacent non-tumor tissues, the EC network contained a greater number of nodes and edges, accompanied by a shorter average path length and reduced network diameter, suggesting a more compact network architecture. In addition, increased closeness centrality, degree centralization, average nearest neighbor degree, and betweenness centrality suggested a shift toward a more centralized and hub-enriched topology (Supplementary Table 5).

To assess the ecological roles of individual taxa within these networks, Zi-Pi analysis was performed (Fig. 4C-D). In this analysis, Zi represents within-module connectivity, whereas Pi represents among-module connectivity. Both adjacent non-tumor and EC networks contained genera located in the connector region, indicating the presence of taxa with potential cross-module connectivity in both groups. Compared with the adjacent non-tumor network, the EC network showed a broader and denser distribution of genera in the connector region, suggesting enhanced among-module connectivity in EC-associated microbial networks. Notably, Acinetobacter, Bacillus, and Achromobacter appeared as EC-specific highlighted genera in the Zi-Pi analysis, whereas Pseudomonas and Ochrobactrum were highlighted in both networks. These findings suggest that selected EC-associated genera may contribute to the increased connector-like topology of the EC microbial network.

EC tissues exhibit AA metabolism-associated metabolic alterations

Altered microbial network structures in EC tissues may be accompanied by changes in host metabolic states through potential microbe-metabolite interactions. Therefore, we next performed untargeted metabolomic profiling to examine metabolic differences between EC and paired adjacent non-tumor tissues. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering showed a group-level metabolic shift between EC and adjacent non-tumor tissues, although several samples clustered close to or within the opposite group, indicating interindividual metabolic heterogeneity (Fig. 5A).

Across positive and negative ion modes, a total of 523 differential metabolites were identified, including 477 upregulated and 46 downregulated metabolites in EC tissues compared with paired adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 5B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis ranked AA metabolism as the most significantly enriched pathway, followed by phenylalanine and tryptophan metabolism (Fig. 5C). Within the AA metabolic pathway, AA itself showed a non-significant increasing trend in EC tissues, whereas several representative downstream AA metabolism-related metabolites, including COX-, LOX/ALOX-, and CYP450-related lipid mediators, displayed higher levels in EC tissues compared with paired adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 5D). A. guillouiae-derived CM is associated with cPLA2 activation and intracellular AA accumulation in EC cells

In light of the enrichment of AA metabolism in EC tissues, we next sought to identify candidate EC-associated bacteria that might be linked to this metabolic alteration. Based on previous integrated taxonomic analyses, Acinetobacter showed an EC-biased abundance pattern at the genus level, although it did not reach statistical significance after multiple-testing correction. We further examined the lowest available taxonomic assignments from the 16S rRNA sequencing analysis and visualized the abundance patterns of the top 20 candidate bacteria across individual samples (Fig. 6A). Among these candidates, A. guillouiae showed a cancer-biased abundance pattern and belonged to the genus Acinetobacter, which was also implicated by genus-level profiling, random forest feature ranking, and network analysis. Considering its abundance pattern, biological plausibility, and the availability of a standardized culturable strain, A. guillouiae was selected for downstream CM-based functional evaluation.

To examine whether A. guillouiae-derived CM influences EC cell phenotypes, Ishikawa and HEC-1-A cells were treated with increasing concentrations of CM. CM exposure increased cell viability and colony-forming capacity in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 6B-C). Consistent with the tissue metabolomic findings, intracellular AA levels were increased in EC cells following A. guillouiae CM treatment (Fig. 6D). This increase was accompanied by cPLA2 activation, as indicated by enhanced phosphorylation at Ser505, whereas total cPLA2 protein levels remained largely unchanged (Fig. 6E). These findings suggest that A. guillouiae-derived CM is associated with enhanced proliferative phenotypes, increased intracellular AA accumulation, and activation of the cPLA2-AA axis in EC cells.

A. guillouiae-derived CM is associated with activation of TLR4/NF-κB/ALOX5 axis and supports EC cell proliferation

Given the increase in intracellular AA levels following A. guillouiae CM exposure, we next examined whether downstream AA-metabolizing enzymes were altered in EC cells. A. guillouiae CM increased expression of key AA-metabolizing enzymes, including arachidonate 5-lipoxygenase (ALOX5), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), and cytochrome P450 family 4 subfamily A member 11 (CYP4A11), with ALOX5 showing the most pronounced induction (Fig. 7A). Pharmacological inhibition of cPLA2 with CAY10650 reduced cPLA2 activation but did not fully abolish the CM-induced upregulation of ALOX5, COX-2, or CYP4A11 (Fig. 7B-C). These findings suggest that cPLA2 activation and AA-metabolizing enzyme induction may represent coordinated but partially separable components of the AA metabolic response induced by A. guillouiae CM.

Because ALOX5 showed prominent induction, we further assessed its downstream lipid mediator LTB4. Blockade of ALOX5 using Zileuton reduced production of LTB4 in the presence of A. guillouiae CM, supporting the involvement of the ALOX5-LTB4 branch in this response (Fig. 7D-E). We next examined upstream inflammatory signaling that may contribute to ALOX5 induction. A. guillouiae CM increased TLR4 expression and enhanced phosphorylation of the NF-κB subunit p65 (Fig. 7F). Consistently, inhibition of TLR4 with CAY10614 or blockade of NF-κB signaling using JSH-23 attenuated CM-induced ALOX5 transcription (Fig. 7G-H). Functionally, inhibition of TLR4 or NF-κB reduced the colony-forming advantage associated with A. guillouiae CM treatment (Fig. 7I). Together, these findings support a model in which A. guillouiae-derived CM engages TLR4/NF-κB signaling and is associated with activation of the ALOX5-LTB4 branch of AA metabolism, which may contribute to the proliferative phenotype observed in EC cells.

Intratumoral A. guillouiae enhances EC xenograft growth

To assess the impact of A. guillouiae on EC growth in vivo, a subcutaneous xenograft model was established using Ishikawa cells, followed by intratumoral administration of A. guillouiae or saline as a control (Fig. 8A). Tumor growth was monitored over time. Growth curve analysis demonstrated that tumors in the A. guillouiae-treated group showed a faster growth trend compared with controls, resulting in increased tumor volume during the observation period (Fig. 8B). Consistently, gross examination of excised tumors revealed larger tumor masses in the A. guillouiae group at the experimental endpoint (Fig. 8C). Quantitative analyses further confirmed significantly increased tumor volume and tumor weight in the A. guillouiae-treated mice (Fig. 8D-E). At the cellular level, immunohistochemical staining showed enhanced Ki-67 expression in tumors exposed to A. guillouiae (Fig. 8F), which was corroborated by elevated Ki-67 IHC scores (Fig. 8G), reflecting increased proliferative activity in vivo. Together, these in vivo results indicate that intratumoral exposure to A. guillouiae is associated with enhanced EC xenograft growth and increased tumor cell proliferation.

Discussion

Although lesion-associated microbiota have been increasingly implicated in tumor biology, the ecological organization and metabolic relevance of local microbial communities in EC remain incompletely understood [18–20]. In this study, we integrated 16S rRNA sequencing and untargeted metabolomics using paired EC and adjacent non-tumor endometrial tissues and identified a microbiome-metabolome pattern characterized by altered microbial co-occurrence network architecture and enrichment of AA metabolism. Importantly, the overall microbial diversity was largely preserved, whereas genus-level features and network topology showed heterogeneous but group-biased alterations in EC tissues. Functional experiments further suggested that A. guillouiae-derived CM was associated with activation of the cPLA2-AA axis and TLR4/NF-κB/ALOX5 signaling in EC cells, supporting a potential link between candidate EC-associated bacteria, inflammatory lipid metabolism, and proliferation.

One notable feature of our microbiome data is that EC tissues did not show a uniform shift in overall microbial richness or beta-diversity separation. This finding suggests that EC-associated microbial dysbiosis may not necessarily manifest as a broad loss or gain of microbial diversity. Instead, the alterations observed in this cohort were more evident at the level of selected genera and co-occurrence network organization. Such a pattern is biologically plausible for local tissue-associated microbiota, particularly in low-biomass reproductive tract samples, where microbial signals are often sparse, heterogeneous, and strongly influenced by host microenvironmental conditions. From an ecological perspective, tumor-associated microbial communities may exert biological effects not only through changes in the abundance of individual taxa, but also through changes in community organization, cross-module connectivity, and microbial-metabolic coordination. Therefore, the increased network density and connector-like topology observed in EC tissues may reflect a shift toward a more integrated microbial ecological state, which could facilitate coordinated interactions between bacterial communities and host metabolic pathways.

The enrichment of AA metabolism provides a plausible metabolic link between local microbial alterations and EC biology. EC is closely associated with inflammatory and metabolic risk factors, including obesity, estrogen-related signaling, and chronic inflammatory states, all of which may influence lipid mediator production within the endometrial microenvironment. AA is a central substrate for inflammatory lipid mediator production, and its downstream metabolites can regulate tumor cell proliferation, inflammatory signaling, angiogenesis, immune-cell recruitment, and therapeutic response [12, 13, 21]. Therefore, the enrichment of AA metabolism observed in EC tissues may represent more than a passive metabolic signature; it may reflect an inflammatory lipid metabolic state that is functionally coupled to the tumor-associated microenvironment.

A. guillouiae emerged as a candidate bacterium of interest in this study, but its role should be interpreted cautiously in the context of 16 S-based taxonomic assignment and exploratory feature prioritization. The rationale for selecting A. guillouiae was not based solely on statistically significant differential abundance. Rather, its corresponding genus, Acinetobacter, was implicated across complementary analyses, including genus-level taxonomic profiling, random forest feature ranking, and co-occurrence network analysis. In addition, Acinetobacter showed connector-like topological features in the EC microbial network, suggesting that it may occupy an ecologically relevant position within the altered microbial community. The availability of a standardized A. guillouiae reference strain further allowed functional testing using CM-based assays. Based on the current evidence, A. guillouiae is best interpreted as a candidate functional bacterium associated with EC-related microbial and metabolic alterations, rather than as a fully validated EC-specific driver.

Mechanistically, our data suggest that A. guillouiae-derived CM may influence AA metabolism through two coordinated components. First, CM exposure activated cPLA2, as indicated by increased Ser505 phosphorylation, which is consistent with enhanced intracellular AA availability because cPLA2 is a key enzyme responsible for releasing AA from membrane phospholipids. Second, CM exposure activated TLR4/NF-κB signaling and induced AA-metabolizing enzymes, particularly ALOX5, thereby promoting the ALOX5-LTB4 branch of AA metabolism [22–24]. These two events should not be interpreted as completely independent processes. Rather, cPLA2 activation may increase substrate availability, whereas TLR4/NF-κB-mediated enzyme induction may facilitate downstream utilization of AA into inflammatory lipid mediators. This interpretation is consistent with our observation that cPLA2 inhibition reduced cPLA2 activation but did not fully abolish downstream enzyme induction, suggesting that additional inflammatory signaling inputs may contribute to AA-metabolizing enzyme expression.

The functional consequences of A. guillouiae exposure may be context-dependent [25, 26]. Evidence from other tumor types or biological systems does not necessarily predict its behavior in EC, because bacterial effects are shaped by tissue-specific epithelial barriers, nutrient availability, immune tone, hormonal context, and local metabolic states [27–30]. The endometrial microenvironment is particularly sensitive to inflammatory and lipid metabolic cues, which may provide a permissive context for A. guillouiae-associated activation of AA-related signaling [31]. Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence that A. guillouiae is universally pro-tumorigenic, but rather that it may exert EC-context-dependent effects through interaction with inflammatory lipid metabolism. It should also be emphasized that A. guillouiae-derived CM contains multiple bacterial products and soluble factors. Therefore, the observed cellular responses should be interpreted as a composite response to bacterial exposure rather than the effect of a single defined bacterial metabolite. Accordingly, our experiments were designed to determine whether A. guillouiae CM activates intracellular AA metabolism in EC cells, not to test whether AA is directly supplied by CM or acts as the sole mediator of the proliferative phenotype.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the clinical cohort was relatively small, and the microbiome-metabolome findings require validation in larger independent EC cohorts. Second, species-level resolution based on 16S rRNA sequencing is limited. Therefore, species-specific qPCR, fluorescence in situ hybridization, culture-based validation, or metagenomic sequencing will be needed to confirm the presence and spatial distribution of A. guillouiae in EC tissues. Third, because tissue-associated microbiome studies in low-biomass samples are susceptible to contamination and batch effects, more stringent negative controls and technical validation should be incorporated in future work. Fourth, A. guillouiae-derived CM contains multiple bacterial products and soluble factors, and the specific bacterial component responsible for activating intracellular AA metabolism remains to be identified. Finally, intratumoral administration of A. guillouiae in a subcutaneous xenograft model does not fully mimic natural bacterial colonization or the native endometrial microenvironment. Despite these limitations, the present study provides a microbiome-metabolome framework for understanding how local microbial alterations may be linked to inflammatory lipid metabolic remodeling in EC.

Conclusions

This study reveals altered local microbial network organization and AA metabolism-associated metabolic remodeling in EC tissues. Integrated microbiome-metabolome analyses and functional experiments suggest that A. guillouiae-derived CM can activate the cPLA2-AA axis and TLR4/NF-κB/ALOX5 signaling in EC cells, supporting its role as a candidate bacteria-associated modulator of inflammatory lipid metabolism and proliferative phenotypes. These findings provide a microbiome-metabolome framework for understanding lipid metabolic heterogeneity in EC and support further investigation of bacteria-associated AA metabolism as a potential therapeutic vulnerability.
