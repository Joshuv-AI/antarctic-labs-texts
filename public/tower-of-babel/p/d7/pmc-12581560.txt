A scoping review to identify opportunities and challenges for communities of South Asian origin in accessing mental health services and support in high-income countries
Authors: Gayathri Menon, Haribondhu Sarma, Amy Bestman, Cathy O’Callaghan, Uday Narayan Yadav
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCommunities of South Asian origin in high-income countries experience a significantly high prevalence of mental health disorders relative to other migrant populations. Emerging evidence highlights socio-cultural and systemic challenges to mental health care access for culturally diverse communities in high-income English speaking countries; however, there is no comprehensive qualitative evidence regarding the opportunities and challenges faced by these communities. To address this knowledge gap, this scoping review aimed to explore the lived experiences of South Asian communities in accessing mental health care across four selected high-income nations (the United Kingdom, Australia, the United States, and Canada).MethodsThis scoping review followed reporting guidelines for scoping reviews outlined by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement. A systematic search was conducted to identify qualitative studies and mixed-methods studies published in English between January 2000 - January 2023 in three electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus. The search strategy included keywords related to South Asian communities, mental health and access to health care in the chosen high-income countries. Extracted qualitative data was analysed using a thematic analysis.ResultsAnalysis of the 25 included studies identified interlinked opportunities and challenges informing the ability of South Asian communities to access mental health care. Findings have been structured according to dimensions of accessibility of services: (1) approachability, (2) acceptability, (3) availability and accommodation, (4) affordability, and (5) appropriateness. Our study identified myriad challenges faced by South Asian communities including inflexible accessibility to diverse mental health care, difficult family dynamics, stigma and acculturative issues hindering mental health care usage and poor mental health care experiences informing perceptions of mental health care and re-engagement.ConclusionThe research finds that access to mental health care for those from South Asian backgrounds requires a comprehensive and nuanced framework that addresses population-specific barriers, systemic inequalities and socio-cultural factors. Recommendations include increasing cultural safety, facilitating flexible models of care and service delivery in practice, alongside policy and funding reflective of systemic mental health care access issues, alongside implementation research to assess the effectiveness of such recommendations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24619-7.Keywords: High-income countries, Mental health access, Mental Health Services, Opportunities and challenges, South Asian origin, Stigma

Introduction

Mental health conditions include mental, behavioural, or emotional disorders causing varied impairments through interlinked biological, psychological, social, environmental and economic factors [1]. Global mental health condition-associated disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs), where each DALY indicates the loss of one year of full health equivalent, rose from 80.8 million (1990) to 123.5 million (2019) [2]. There are also increasing proportions of total mental health conditions globally [2]. Studies in Australia have noted that the status of individual mental health is a crucial health marker for culturally and linguistically diverse populations [3–5].

The unique pre and post-migratory experiences of culturally diverse populations alongside socio-historical contexts, significantly shape community and individual well-being and health [4]. Research highlights that one group, those of South Asian origin (Afghani, Bangladeshi, Bhutanese, Indian, Maldivian, Nepali, Pakistani, and Sri Lankan) in high-income nations experience the highest level of mental health morbidity [6, 7]. South Asian communities comprise of heterogeneous demographics - different origin countries, socio-economic status, religion, cultural practices and beliefs, resulting in diverse migration cohorts [8–11]. Across the global literature, South Asian people experience higher levels of mental health distress or disorders relative to non-South Asian people, particularly in high-income English speaking nations such as Canada [12–14], Australia [15], the United Kingdom (UK) [16, 17] and the United States (USA) [6, 12–14, 18–23]. However, despite the increased prevalence of ill mental health, South Asian people are significantly less likely to seek treatment [12–14]. Emerging, evidence from high-income countries highlights specific mental health care access challenges for South Asian populations due to external and internal stigma, culturally derived illness beliefs and limited accessibility to culturally appropriate resources and services [6]. This is underpinned by adaptive and cultural challenges associated with socio-economic hardships, migration contexts (refugee, skilled migrant, migration cohorts), language and cultural differences, and challenges navigating different health systems [6, 24].

Despite efforts to deconstruct these barriers, accessing quality, reliable, easily navigable and culturally safe mental health care remains challenging in high-income nations [24]. This can also be attributed to the multi-faceted nature of accessibility not being reflected in current literature due to the complexities of exploring the nuances of accessibility [25, 26]. Furthermore, many accessibility models present linear and static approaches and concepts [27]. However, to allow for systematic change to increase accessibility, this must be reflected in the literature exploring the opportunities and challenges of mental health care access. The need for this care is critical due to implications for South Asian communities associated with decreased healthcare utilisation and greater relative chronic disease risk (for example, cancer, respiratory problems, cardiovascular disease and diabetes mellitus) [24, 28, 29].

Exploring intersectional and multidimensional South Asian community experiences is essential to bridge mental health care gaps with strengths-based, person-centred and sustainable approaches that mobilise existing positive attributes and resources of the target population [29–31]. The use of qualitative perspectives to highlight South Asian lived experiences is crucial for understanding the gaps in mental health care and how to better support this community. These perspectives will facilitate the development and prioritisation of culturally responsive mental health care.

However, to date, there has been limited primary and secondary evidence examining South Asian mental health care access in high-income nations and restricted focus on certain communities. For example, limited research in Canada and the UK examines South Asian mental health care access, while Australian studies often highlight specific sub-groups (i.e. Indian or Pakistani), refugees or examine general health or healthcare access [4, 6, 13, 32–36], rather than examining the experiences of mental health care for this population. In addition, there is a gap in research that explores the opportunities and challenges for mental health care in South Asian populations living in high-income countries. To address this gap, this review examines the lived experiences of South Asian communities accessing mental health care across four specific high-income nations (defined by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) with considerable South Asian diasporas, Australia, the UK, Canada and the USA through two interlinked research questions:

What are the lived experiences and perceptions of South Asian communities in four high-income nations with large South Asian diasporas regarding mental health care access?

Methods

The use of a scoping review was chosen due to the heterogeneous characteristics and experiences of South Asian communities. This methodology allows for the exploration of the breadth of literature related to the study research questions, identification of literature gaps and capturing diverse experiences of the South Asian diaspora. This method also allows for the nuanced exploration of qualitative studies on the lived experiences of South Asian communities. This scoping review followed scoping review reporting guidelines and criteria set by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement and The Joanna Briggs Institute [37, 38].

Database searches were conducted in English using different keyword combinations (Table 1) in three electronic databases: Medline/PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science between January 2000 and January 2023. This review was guided by the Arksey & O’Malley, 2005 [39] five-stage framework (identification of research question, identification of relevant studies, selection of studies, data charting and summarisation and dissemination of results) [39]. Positionality

Three authors of this paper are South Asian researchers in Australia with particular expertise in health systems, chronic disease and implementation science (GM, HS, and senior author UNY). As South Asian researchers, the authors have lived experiences as a part of culturally diverse communities in high-income countries which have informed their perspectives, understanding and analysis of the themes evident in the literature. Other researchers (AM and CO) have rich experiences working with culturally diverse communities and priorities in Australian settings.

Conceptual framework

In order to examine the opportunities and challenges to mental health access through the lens of South Asian communities in high-income countries, the authors developed a novel framework based on the Conceptual Framework of Access to Healthcare [27] and the migrant trajectory perspective [42] (Fig. 1) to guide data collection, extrapolation, synthesis and analysis. The Framework of Access to Healthcare was chosen as it acknowledges that access to healthcare is impacted by a health service’s provision of care (and the degree to which services are accessible), but also by an individual’s access to care (from the demand side) [27, 43]. This analysis has focused on how service accessibility impacts on individual experiences of care for South Asian communities, using the dimensions of acceptability, availability/accommodation, affordability, appropriateness and approachability [27, 43]. This recognises that these domains impact on the population’s ability to seek, perceive, reach, pay or engage with healthcare [27, 42, 43]. Additionally, this framework has been selected due to its successful use in assessing complexities of healthcare access in a dynamic manner [43]. The migrant trajectory perspective explores dynamic relationships between migrants and health services [27, 37, 39, 42]. Therefore, by combining these theoretical frameworks, our analysis allows for greater recognition of the multidimensional components of mental health care access for South Asian communities.

Search strategy

Search terms (Table 1) pertained to six key domains through combinations of keywords related to South Asian communities, mental health care,lived experiences and perceptions , the five domains of Levesque’s Conceptual Framework of Access to Healthcare [27, 43] (approachability, acceptability, availability and accommodation, affordability, and appropriateness), four high-income nations (UK, Australia, USA and Canada) with large South Asian communities and qualitative studies – utilising Boolean operators including “OR” or “AND”, brackets and asterisks.

Study selection

All search results identified from databases were exported to EndNote X9 (citation manager) and uploaded onto COVIDENCE. Two reviewers conducted title and abstract screening, with the guidance of automated duplicate removal via COVIDENCE. Subsequently, the reviewers selected relevant papers using inclusion and exclusion criteria. One reviewer independently assessed full-text articles that met inclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, to reach a consensus within the study team.

Quality assessment and critical appraisal

All included studies were subject to quality assessment and critical appraisal to assess and comment on the quality of the available research evidence ( Supplementary file 1-Table 1 and 2). The Joanna Briggs Institute’s Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research utilised ten questions (which can be answered as yes, no, unclear or not applicable) to examine the possibility of bias in each study design, conduct or analysis 38]. The quality of the included studies were then examined (Supplementary file 1- Table 2) to ensure that moderate to high quality evidence was utilised in this study.

Data extraction and data synthesis

Data extraction and data synthesis was conducted using a combination of deductive and inductive thematic analysis. The primary investigator conducted data extraction of all included studies (satisfying full-text analysis) from the abstract, introduction, results and discussion, through inductive thematic analysis, allowing charting and coding of findings relevant to the research questions [44]. Data extraction was guided by the parameters of the modified framework developed by the authors (outlined in the conceptual framing).

Findings were charted in a spreadsheet and coded into broad iterative themes [44, 45]. Subsequent sub-group analysis further grouped findings and refined emerging themes [44, 45]. Deductive thematic analysis was utilised to code opportunities (internal or external facilitators) and challenges (internal or external barriers) to mental health care access [44, 45]. Descriptive data (i.e., high-income countries, participant demographics) on each study was collected (Supplementary file) to provide a contextual understanding of the collected themes. Descriptive synthesis techniques, as per the Joanna Briggs Institute Reviewers manual, were utilised to synthesise and analyse collected data, extrapolate relationships and differences between the themes and sub-themes on opportunities and challenges to accessing mental health care [46]. Data was further grouped and synthesised into higher level themes using an iterative approach to identify core themes which emerged across the included studies. These higher-level themes (subclassified by opportunities and challenges) were grouped under the five domains of accessibility in accordance with the definition of each domain outlined by Levesque, Harris & Russell, 2013 [27].

Results

A total of 2068 initial articles were identified from three databases, of which 54 duplicates were removed, and 1789 studies were screened by title and abstract (Fig. 2). A full text review was conducted with 178 papers, with 153 studies excluded during this stage for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Finally, 24 qualitative studies and one mixed-methods study (two from Australia, one from Canada, six from the USA and 16 from the UK) were included in the final review and critically appraised (Supplementary File). Critical appraisal was conducted to assess the quality and validity of the included papers, using the Joanna Briggs Institute’s Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research. Critical appraisal highlighted that four studies were of moderate quality while 21 were of high quality, therefore all studies were included for analysis.

Study general characteristics

Of the included studies, methodologies included semi-structured interview (n = 9), focus groups only (n = 6), focus group and interview (n = 3), in-depth interviews (n = 3), case study (n = 1), qualitative survey (n = 1), community engagement evaluation (n = 1) and mixed methods feasibility study (n = 1). The definitions of South Asian populations varied, with studies including the following groups; Pakistani (n = 16), Indian (n = 12), Bangladeshi (n = 10), Nepali (n = 6), Sri Lankan (n = 6), Bhutanese (n = 3), South Asian (with no or limited separation of definition by country) (n = 3), Maldivian (n = 2), Afghani (n = 2) and Mixed ethnicity - South Asian and British (n = 1). As echoed by existing South Asian health literature, limited studies have emerged exploring the experiences of Bhutanese, Afghani, and Maldivian people in depth; suggesting a significant gap in the literature.

Approachability

The first theme explores the degree that services are approachable, with one component that individuals know that the service exists, and the barriers that prevent individuals from approaching services. Approachability (addressed by 15 included studies) was linked to factors such as health literacy and knowledge of health issues. Key barriers to approachability included (1) individual and service level stigma, (2) cultural shame, (3) limited mental health literacy (for community members and providers) and (4) limited resources in non-English languages. Opportunities identified to address approachability included (1) improving mental health literacy, (2) deconstructing mistrust and stigma, (3) tailored opportunities for psychoeducation, (4) service diversification and (5) multi-agency collaborations with communities. The following section provides specific examples of this theme from the review.

Perceptions of mental health care needs were impeded by individual and service level stigma, compounded by the challenges of ethnically matching care. This was in part due to the application of Western mental health concepts (inapplicable to South Asian populations), experiences of racism and cultural shame which informed community expectations (i.e., upholding honour), limiting the social acceptability of mental health care [19, 47, 49, 57]. One study from the UK found that cultural shame may be used by other community member to reinforce women’s roles in family life and encourage women to remain silent about their problems [49] preventing women from engaging with health services. The research also identified significant gaps in mental health literacy for South Asian communities compounded by language barriers, non-representative health promotion and difficulties comprehending medical explanations, evoking isolation [19, 47–49, 51–54, 58]. Other research highlighted that generational disparities in mental health literacy created challenges within families [51, 53, 56, 59]. Effective strategies included tailored opportunities for psychoeducation, service diversification and feasible multi-agency collaborations with communities [5, 48–50, 55, 58].

Acceptability

Acceptability (addressed by 20 included studies) refers to the cultural and social factors that impact on whether an individual accepts the service and the appropriateness to seek care [27]. Acceptability of services was strengthened by culturally aware and safe services; however, fears of stigmatising experiences were heightened by a lack of culturally appropriate and acceptable models of care. This review found limited appropriate and safe mental health care for South Asian communities alongside sociocultural issues such as gender roles and unmet healthcare needs due to delayed presentation or impeded mental health care re-engagement.

Mental health stigma (including, personal, interpersonal and intergenerational) resulted in shame, internalised discrimination, trivialisation of mental health and denial of mental health conditions [19, 47–53, 57–61, 63–65, 69]. South Asian culture and stigma informed dominant mental health beliefs, negative attitudes about mental health care (questioning efficacy of socially stigmatised health care providers - psychiatrists), treatment acceptability (discordance with rigid care models even when ethnically-matched), poor mental health self-acceptance and limited suitable alternatives (due to religious or cultural constraints) [48, 51, 54, 56, 58, 64, 65, 69]. Fears of stigmatising experiences with healthcare professionals, exacerbated by overly clinical environments, encouraged mental health care avoidance or pressure to distort relationships between culture and mental health, to evade cultural stereotyping [47, 62, 70].

Seeking mental health care with “outside people” was often avoided due to privacy concerns, potential isolation or subsequent mental health care disengagement if information spread via community or non-medical staff [49, 51, 53, 60, 69]. Non-culturally informed mental health care deterred access as most South Asian services-users were assigned to white healthcare staff, creating difficulties in exploring cultural drivers of mental health care [5, 49]. Numerous studies highlighted that South Asian individuals described health care providers as paternalistic “god-like” decision-makers, due to their inaccessibility rooted in cultural unawareness, unresponsiveness or dismissiveness (even if patient-requested referrals were made) [49, 51, 58, 59, 63, 64, 69]. However, these issues were not exclusive to non-South Asian health care providers, with service-users describing providers as untrustworthy, especially when from shared communities [49, 51, 58, 59, 63, 64, 69].

The lack of cultural awareness resulted in dismissed concerns about familial shame, pathologisation of cultural experiences, inflexible and untailored practice, with limited recognition of mental health-associated co-morbidities (i.e. eating disorder), resulting in poor patient acceptance of illnesses [48, 51, 54, 59, 65]. South Asian service users felt expected to become “cultural experts” to assist healthcare staff but were faced with cultural and language barriers [58, 59, 64, 65]. For example, one UK study noted “Eight out of ten participants reported that their therapist or counsellor had very little understanding or awareness of the client’s own cultural context [which] negatively impacted on their experience of therapy” [65, p. 8]]. These experiences, echoed across many included studies, foster mistrust and internalised stigma, compromise patient safety, amplify the burden on support networks and delay mental health care [49, 54, 64, 65, 69].

Individual factors such as the projection of familial values, religious beliefs or manifestations of the collectivist South Asian culture impacted on service acceptability [19, 47, 49, 51, 54]. A particular focus in the studies reviewed was the gendered impact of culture on mental health care acceptability. For example, gendered burdens of shame acted to silence women, reinforce gender roles and obstruct access to mental health [49, 51, 57],. Additional, cultural practices such as male and parents governing medical history, treatment and mental health care re-engagement acted as barriers for women [48, 54, 57, 60, 69]. Despite women facing great concerns about interplays between culture, familial gender roles and identity, they were more likely to discuss mental health compared to men who were confined by masculinity and ingrained fears of negative societal perceptions of weakness [54, 59].

Improvements in South Asian and non-SA mental health care were noted with culturally aware, safe and sensitive practices and patient-provider ethnic matching [59]. There was also a small number of studies that found services fostering privacy and safe disclosure, encouraged open patient-provider discussions [56, 58, 69]. Despite shortages in South Asian healthcare staff, multiple studies noted that ethnocultural and gender matching were important [48, 49, 51, 52, 59]. However, challenges such as confining mental health to stereotypes without recognising individuality and concerns around privacy may result in a preference for non-culturally specific (or white) providers [5, 49, 52, 63].

Availability and accommodation

Availability and accommodation (addressed by 17 included studies), was the ability of services to be physically reached promptly [27]. Accessing local mental health care, which is multidisciplinary, affordable and flexible encourages service attendance. However, difficulties in accessing timely referrals, post-referral disengagement, poor continuity of quality care and limited physical accessibility to services - hinders access.

Multiple studies noted that health care provider referral delays caused delayed mental health presentation until physical manifestations or acute crisis in vulnerable populations (i.e., older South Asian people) [47, 48, 50, 51, 59, 65, 68, 69]. One UK based study described that carers felt they needed to exaggerate patient symptoms to access suitable care, including to non-health sectors such as police [48]. Other challenges included limited quantity, quality, safety and continuity of care, limited transport and family/employment responsibilities [19, 47, 49, 50, 60, 62, 65]. Health comorbidities created unique challenges for mental health care [61, 62, 67]. Additionally, post-referral, some South Asian individuals disengaged due to mental health care’s perceived futility [47, 48, 50, 51, 65, 68]. At the service level, numerous studies outlined that reaching mental health care was supported by flexible, affordable, health insurance-eligible, local and multidisciplinary/coupled healthcare [19, 47, 60, 65, 68].

Affordability

The fourth dimension of accessibility related to affordability, (addressed by 12 included studies) or the ability to spend resources and time to use services [27]. Research highlighted that the more affordable and feasible mental health services are, the greater opportunities for access; however, services need to recognise the cultural and familial barriers which shape the financial access to mental health services.

Healthcare affordability impacted mental health care accessibility, facilitating non-linear mental health support pathways [27]. Financial challenges included decreased female financial independence, prohibitive or restrictive fees and limited health insurance or perceiving mental health care as unproductive investments [19, 47, 62, 69]. One study noted affordability as a trade-off for quality or quantity of mental health care access and that subsidised care was limited by service caps [47]. There was evidence of some free mental health services, telephone helplines and accessible accessory support (i.e., nutritionist in the context of eating disorders) [19, 47, 49, 50, 60, 62, 65].

Appropriateness

The final dimension, appropriateness (addressed by 18 included studies), referred to the fit between services and client needs; this also includes the opportunity to utilise quality services [27]. Mental health care appropriateness involved the initial identification, utilisation and continuation of service usage; however, for South Asian communities’ challenges included service-based racism and discrimination alongside the complexities of migration and acculturation. Using strengths-based culturally appropriate measures to strengthen mental health care alongside systemic changes is necessary to address issues related to discrimination in services and the challenges presented by dynamic acculturation.

The literature shows that mental health care and support must reflect heterogenous South Asian experiences. There was an identified need for youth barriers to be addressed, particularly in youth unable to access support in white focused health systems or lacking specific culturally informed resources [5, 19, 47, 64, 69]. Older generations’ slower acculturation created diverse mental health needs across family units [19, 47, 49, 64].

To overcome healthcare access barriers, papers supported the use of innovative approaches that meet the needs of South Asian people, with a priority on using community members’ preferred languages to validate heterogenous South Asian experiences. Online sources (formal and informal) introducing mental health care-related information were effective, particularly for youth, when resources were tailored [52, 54]. Early interventions and patient-centred narrative therapy over cognitive behavioural therapy in youth was found to divert mental health care goals from symptom management to storytelling – sharing distress, fostering unity and reducing individual burden [54, 65, 69]. This was noted by one study as being particularly effective for South Asian women, where group therapy fostered social support [50]. Other mental health care opportunities included assessments of familial psychological mindedness and discussing sensitive topics (family, drug and sexual history) directly yet respectfully [50, 69].

Individuals and communities also have a key role in supporting appropriate mental health care. Our review found that engaging family [51, 56, 63, 64], faith based sectors [50–52, 54, 59] and peer-led models of care [51, 56] facilitated help-seeking and increased access to mental health care. Differing from general populations in the countries studied, South Asian people prioritised immediate kinship relationships in facilitating initial help-seeking with social networks acting as second-line support systems [48, 53, 64]. However, excessive family involvement in decision-making significantly delayed help-seeking through stigma and poor health system navigation [59].

Discussion

Given the significant mental health needs for South Asian populations living in high income countries, this review aimed to understand the lived experiences and perceptions of this community with respect to access to mental health care. This review is the first to systematically review the opportunities and challenges of access to care experienced by South Asian communities across multiple high income countries. This review identified three key areas for discussion.

The first was that this study took a unique approach by drawing on two conceptual models to create a novel conceptual framework to examine access to mental health care for South Asian communities, the Conceptual Framework of Access to Healthcare [27] and the migrant trajectory perspective [42]. This lens has enabled clear identification of both the system-driven factors and the individual level factors that enable or act as barriers to accessing mental health care. For example, poor service feasibility (for service users and carers), service mistrust and cultures of delayed presentation affects accessibility of mental health services [71]. Addressing these system factors requires going beyond health promotion interventions (that focus on the individual) and focusing on the structural socioeconomic inequalities that act as barriers [35, 72–74]. However, the availability of services alone will not ensure that mental health care needs are met for this target population; the dimensions of access must be consistently and adequately met in response to the dynamic effects of the external cultural environments and patient needs. This requires the removal of deficit-based approaches to mental health care in South Asian communities. For example, measures such as using patient-preferred languages allow greater expression to validate heterogenous South Asian experiences by improving the approachability and appropriateness of mental health care [48, 51, 59].

This leads to our second point, this research has identified the overwhelming lack of culturally safe and appropriate mental health services in the literature from high income countries. This includes the challenges associated with lack of appropriate communication (i.e., miscommunication and non-patient-centred) [75, 76]. While interpreters have a role to play, their input can exacerbate mistrust, due to confidentiality issues, unprofessional behaviour or interference [75, 76]. This highlights the need again for structural changes that create safe and quality mental health care. Policymakers should consider the expansion of holistic health (integrating social, physical and mental care) service delivery (i.e. transcultural mental health centres) to create large-scale mental health care networks, founded in strength-based approaches and increased service approachability (i.e. coupled services and proactive community engagement) [77–79]. Furthermore, flexibly accessible (i.e., financial and physical) services create help-seeking opportunities when supported by positive individual and community characteristics. However, flexible methods of service access such as telehealth should ensure that they overcome significant access gaps for patients from culturally diverse backgrounds [80]. This requires research into identifying the adaptations necessary for telehealth and mental health initiatives to be usable and effective for South Asian communities alongside system wide strengthening health infrastructure by policymakers. At the service level, greater prioritisation of service coverage (including universal health care-based programs such as increasing access to mental health Care Plans in Australia) is necessary to address prominent access gaps [81]. For example, while the Australian based Better Access Initiative has increased mental health care availability, it lacks approachability and affordability as it cannot provide free interpreter services for private psychologist consultations [82]. Based on this, we recommend formal inquiry into culturally supportive mental health care policies and programs which has been identified as necessary to systematically identify specific service gaps [81]. This must be paired with culturally inclusive co-designed national standards (i.e. National Quality & Safety Standards in Healthcare) which prioritise mental health care access for culturally diverse populations using explicitly stipulated targets [78]. Long-term effectiveness of cultural responsiveness in strategic policy requires population-specific longitudinal and implementational research, guided by frameworks such as The Framework for mental health in Multicultural Australia and policy targets [78]. Therefore, allowing for the assessment of the implementation of culturally adapted mental health interventions to examine their efficacy and outcomes in improving accessibility. Moreover, negative experiences of service users and carers accessing culturally safe services demonstrates the need for carer support and culturally diverse liaison officers, underpinned by frameworks (such as the modified framework introduced in this study) to recognise culturally diverse mental health care experiences and their family/community effects [77–79, 83–85]. This requires policymakers to prioritise culturally diverse systemic accessibility issues to facilitate suitable primary and secondary service delivery through policy and funding arrangements deconstructing socioeconomic and structural challenges that are constructed around physical illness [78, 86].

Finally, there was a clear role for professional development of clinicians themselves to provide supportive and culturally appropriate mental health care to South Asian populations. Diverse mental health care and health care provider cultural competency supports South Asian mental health care needs and re-engagement [87, 88]. Despite well-reported discrepancies in South Asian communities’ mental health and mental health care literacy, remedies are often rooted in Eurocentricity and comparative to host populations’ mental health conceptualisations [35]. Recognition of the heterogeneity of South Asian communities in high-income countries and the unique experiences which led to migration and settlement in these environments (i.e. fleeing conflict, seeking occupational growth, family circumstances) is essential to addressing these challenges [89]. Furthermore, there must be recognition of issues of stigma and culturally bound and sensitive health issues for culturally diverse communities. A key example is the parallels of gendered stigma for women accessing health care related to mental health or sexual and reproductive services [90]. Therefore, flexible patient-centred care models must be implemented through mandatory cultural safety and cross-cultural health care provider training. For example, this study highlighted the important roles of family and community for South Asian people; therefore, providing opportunities to include integration programs (family, community or religious-based) alongside formal care [35, 78, 91, 92]. Greater financial investment into co-designed culturally diverse components of medical curriculums is necessary to address the root causes of cultural dissonance and develop robust cultural safety skills [81]. While ethnic-matching addresses patient-provider cultural incongruence, our study emphasises heterogeneous South Asian perspectives vary across contexts [35, 93–95].While culturally-based connections may provide sanctuary from discrimination when discussing culturally stigmatised topics like mental health [35], cultural matching confines individuals ethnically, which may disregard explorations of intersectionality between other identities (i.e., gender, sexuality, faith) [35, 94].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include (i) the generation of comprehensive evidence extrapolating qualitative data on opportunities and challenges faced by South Asian people accessing mental health care in four high-income countries (Australia, Canada, UK, USA) (ii) the application of iterative theme-identification processes addressing South Asian community perspectives (iii) all three investigators holding South Asian heritage, ensuring that findings consider community strengths and views (iv) use of critical appraisal tools to assess the included studies’ quality. Review limitations included the search being restricted to three databases with inclusions of peer-reviewed articles published in English, limitations in keywords and publication bias due to omissions of published program reports, grey literature, and policy guidelines. Such omission may impact contextual understanding of the unique mental health access opportunities and challenges for South Asian communities in the four high-income countries alongside the policy frameworks existing in these settings.

Exclusion of studies written in non-English languages may have resulted in omission of insights about mental health access amongst South Asian communities which may have impacted the breadth of research included in this review. Furthermore, nuances of mental health care (i.e., mood disorders vs. psychiatric disorders) affecting mental health care access could not be explored due to the scope of the review. Moreover, while the study explores the experiences of South Asian community in four high-income English speaking countries, it did not include others such as the Republic of Ireland and New Zealand, leading to gaps in the exploration of experiences in these nations.

Conclusion

This review highlights mental health care access opportunities and challenges constructed by the dynamic nature of accessibility, family and non-family communities, experiences and views of mental health and its care, acculturative processes and the complexities of identity. Across all four high-income countries, limited culturally diverse specific mental health care exists and recognises their needs and mental health explanatory models alongside cultural and familial challenges of stigma, creating limited sustainable access. Numerous opportunities exist for strength-based approaches capitalising on diverse South Asian experiences, holistic models of care for mental health with inclusion of of community and family in the care model. This underscores the need for a implementation research that facilitates coordinated action across multiple levels- including individual. Family, community or social networks, mental health providers, and institutions delivering care - to ensure delivery of holistic, culturally responsive mental health services for South Asian people who are left behind by the existing mental health care system.
