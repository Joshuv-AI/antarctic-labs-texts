Dynamics of neutrophilia at the neurovascular unit arising from repeated pulmonary inflammation
Authors: Wesley Chiang, Herman Li, Linh Le, Jennifer David-Bercholz, Ana I. Caceres, Danial Ahmad, Erica Squire, Kamryn S. Stecyk, Mariah Marrero, Amanda Pereira, Rene Squire, Claire Lim, James L. McGrath, Ania K. Majewska, Niccolò Terrando, Harris A. Gelbard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe role of neutrophils in mediating neurovascular vulnerability has been increasingly implicated in various acute inflammatory models of neuroimmune crosstalk between the periphery and the brain. This study aimed at understanding the early phases of crosstalk following repetitive inflammation to the lung and ensuing neuropathology. Such a model of frequent inflammatory injury to the lung is pertinent to understanding the focal neurologic risk of constant exposure to aerosolized environmental hazards leading to progressive pulmonary disease.MethodsTo model repeated pulmonary inflammation, we applied a three-dose regimen of intranasal (i.n.) lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in C57BL/6J mice and studied the impact on the inflammatory environment of the brain, with a specific focus on neutrophil dynamics at the neurovascular unit (NVU). Tissue and circulatory inflammatory profiles were screened via bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) protein content and cellularity, transcript analysis of brain tissue, and flow cytometry of peripheral blood. Intravital two-photon microscopy (2PM) of the brain vasculature identified neutrophil dynamics at the NVU. Immunofluorescence validated neutrophil dynamics and identified neuroinflammatory hallmarks and peripheral immune factor interactions at the NVU. In vivo findings were corroborated and replicated in murine and human microphysiological systems (MPS) modeling the blood-brain barrier as a proxy demonstration of the translational relevance of our findings.Results2PM of tdTomato-Ly6G+ neutrophils demonstrated increased levels of circulating neutrophils and corresponding engagement with cortical brain vasculature after the three-dose repeated i.n. exposure regimen. Neutrophilia at the NVU was corroborated with increased transcript levels of Ly6G and other pro-inflammatory markers. This coordination between endothelial pathophysiology and neutrophil phenotypes was recapitulated in separate murine and human MPS models. Systemic neutrophilia in the lung and circulation was found to be cotemporaneous to neutrophilia at the NVU based on the cellularity of BAL and peripheral blood samples collected at the same endpoints. Immunohistochemical analysis of brain tissue implicates temporal coordination between vascular surface adhesion molecules with changes in neutrophil dynamics from adhesion, crawling, stalling, and transmigration. Extravasation of neutrophils was accompanied by sustained paravascular deposition of fibrinogen and microgliosis up to 72 h after the final i.n. dosing. Microglia-associated effector functions for synaptic pruning and regulation of neutrophil activity demonstrated distinct temporal profiles in the hippocampus independent from transduction along the primary olfactory cortex.ConclusionsOur results identify systemic levels of neutrophilia accompanied by ingress and extravascular accumulation in brain parenchyma that correlated with sustained microglial activation. Pathology to the brain parenchyma is further complemented by the observation of paravascular fibrinogen deposition that alters synaptic metabolism. Thus, we highlight a key role for neutrophil signaling and ensuing neuroimmune interactions from the lung to the brain as a generalizable model of repeated respiratory exposure to inflammatory agents.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03771-w.

Introduction

Respiration, an involuntary human function, is essential for our survival, yet al.so forces our bodies to be constantly exposed to potential health hazards [1]. These hazards range from the increasing number of respiratory pathogens to the also increasing release of aerosolized toxicants as industrial byproducts [2]. Risk of exposure to these environmental hazards is also drastically increased in densely populated, urbanized areas where individuals are continually exposed to a complex milieu of respiratory pathogens and aerosolized toxicants [3]. The expansive surface area of the alveolar epithelium (25-fold greater than the skin) and direct access to the circulatory system is a unique vulnerability encountered by the respiratory system [3–6]. Thus, under constant exposure to environmental toxicants, the respiratory system is an important site peripheral injury that could lead to systemic immune activation that affects other end organs, such as the brain [7].

While clinical presentations of respiratory symptoms for pulmonary damage, such as in acute lung injury (ALI), are typically apparent and treated in an urgent fashion, the increased risk for subsequent complications to other organs, including the brain, remains understudied. A growing body of research in neuroimmunology has demonstrated considerable coregulatory mechanisms that link inflammation between the brain and peripheral organs [8]. Unfortunately, changes in neurologic function and brain pathology following ALI may not be immediate and are often overshadowed by respiratory distress. As a result, follow-up screening for neurologic outcomes is not part of the standard of care [9, 10], and longitudinal studies have reported neurocognitive impairment impacting nearly half the study population, with no improvement between years 1 and 2 following ALI [11]. As these individuals age, their altered neuropathology due to ALI-associated neuroinflammation primes them for future neurologic complications with worse prognosis, including mortality [12]. A complication not captured in these studies is the continued immunologic challenge to the lungs due to pervasive exposure to environmental air pollutants in the dense, urbanized areas that most of the population resides in. Recent census data indicates that over 80% of the United States, as well as ~ 50% of the world, live in such metropolitan areas [13, 14]. Continual exposure to aerosolized environmental toxicants has been demonstrated to prime vulnerable populations, such as those recovering from recent pulmonary damage, for neurodegenerative disorders [4, 15–18]. This complication thereby raises the following question: “After which exposure did immunologic activation of the lung couple to immunologic activation of the brain?” This was saliently highlighted by the recent COVID-19 pandemic; rampant transmission via infectious aerosolized droplets was most pervasive in dense, metropolitan communities and has led to a post-infectious sequelae affecting neurologic health [19–23]. A 2021 study of 236,379 COVID-19 patients estimating that a 33–62% incidence of neuropsychiatric disease and highlights the clinical urgency to better understand early neuroimmune mechanisms that link lung injury to brain inflammation. It is possible that continued pulmonary inflammation from environmental pollutants may affect when, and to what severity, individuals develop neurologic sequelae.

To start addressing some of these questions, we have developed an in vivo model of repeated respiratory inflammation via multi-hit intranasal (i.n.) delivery of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to model cumulative induction of ALI-like pathology. Particularly, the treatment paradigm we used here serves to model the early stages of sub-chronic exposure that could be relevant to exposure risks in metropolitan areas. This approach allowed us to study the point of induction of lung-brain neuroimmune linkage resulting from repeated inhalation exposure; here, we focused on a neutrophil-centric mechanism. Despite being a bacterial endotoxin, LPS has been broadly validated as a generalized inflammatory agent for various disease models [24, 25], including respiratory models of ALI and rhinitis [26–29]; its canonical recognition by toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) overlaps with many inflammatory signaling cascades encompassing both infectious and sterile injury [30–32]. Unsurprisingly, LPS is commonly found as a component of biological organic matter in the complex composition of aerosolized particulate matter that are environmental toxicants to the respiratory system [33–36].

Here we applied a three-dose sub-chronic LPS regimen to interrogate the role of systemic neutrophil activation in establishing a neuroimmune link between the lung and brain along a vascular route. Following the induction of pulmonary damage in a mouse model, we demonstrated a substantial impact on brain pathology, including neurovascular damage, microgliosis, and synaptic loss that could not be attributed to inflammatory transduction through the primary olfactory. Using microphysiological systems (MPS) featuring both murine and human cells, we evaluated the implications of our findings to human disease. Our results further highlight significant vulnerability of the brain to peripheral factors and suggest a neutrophil-centric mechanism during early phases of lung-brain neuroimmune crosstalk.

Results

Establishing minimally repeated sub-chronic paradigm for i.n. LPS induction of ALI-like pathology with coincident neuroinflammation

While chronic induction of severe lung damage has been extensively modeled in the literature [26, 37, 38], studies focusing on the initiation of the immune crosstalk (i.e., minimally sub-chronic exposure) along the lung-brain axis are limited. Thus, we first determined the minimal number of repeated exposures to subacute doses of i.n. LPS required to induce an ALI-like phenotype with concurrent inflammatory activation in the brain (Fig. 1A). C57BL/6J mice (12-week-old) were administered bilateral i.n. LPS (40 µL, 0.25 µg/µL) every 24 h, with mice randomly assigned to euthanasia and sample collection endpoints after 1, 2, and 3 doses of i.n. LPS (Fig. 1B). Complementary saline instillation controls were conducted, and all cohorts of mice were euthanized 24 h after the final dose of their assigned regimen for sample collection of either bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) or whole brain tissue.

The BAL was first screened for total cellularity and protein composition to evaluate the level of respiratory inflammatory following each i.n. LPS challenge (Fig. 1C). Although all dosing regimens resulted in significant increases in total cellularity compared to matched saline controls (1 i.n. = 10-fold, 2 i.n. = 15-fold, 3 i.n. = 30-fold), only three exposures resulted in a significant increase in total protein content. The lack of significant changes in the proteinaceous content of the BAL after one and two doses of i.n. LPS suggests that the alveolar epithelium remains structurally intact and does not satisfy the criteria for induction of an ALI-like phenotype. Correspondingly, we evaluated the inflammatory profile of the brain by screening for changes in the transcript levels of a select panel of neuroinflammatory markers relevant to studies of chronic induction of ALI (Fig. 1D). Like the profile of BAL, only the three-dose regimen clearly demonstrated an inflammatory profile in the brain; we observed clear upregulation of transcripts for lymphocyte antigen 6 complex 6GD (Ly6g), myeloperoxidase (Mpo), interleukin 1β (Il1β), and tumor necrosis factor α (Tnfα). Of particular interest were the increases in Ly6g and Mpo in the brain given their primary association with neutrophil activation, an immune cell recruited during early phases of inflammatory activation. The lack of significant changes in the levels of Mcp1 (monocyte chemotactic protein 1), typically released by activated microglia and tissue-resident macrophage, further confirms that the 3-dose regimen results in a linked lung-brain inflammatory profile. As such, we determined 3 doses of i.n. LPS was the minimum number of repeated exposures to mimic a sub-chronic model for studying the early phases of lung-brain neuroimmune linkage following pulmonary damage.

Cotemporaneous pulmonary, vascular, and cerebral neutrophilia in early-stage induction of pulmonary damage from repeat exposure model

We then phenotyped the specific cellular profile of the BAL in the LPS 3 i.n. paradigm to assess the inflammatory stage of the lung that is cotemporaneous with early stages of neuroinflammation (Fig. 1E). Using a modified Wright-Giemsa stain (Diff-Quik), we identified neutrophils as the dominant constituent of the BAL (~ 80% of all cells), and the primary cell type involved in driving the increased cellularity based on estimated raw counts (~ 200-fold increase). Both lymphocytes and macrophages exhibited marginal, but not statistically significant, increases in estimated raw counts (~ 12-fold to ~ 7-fold, respectively), indicative of a neutrophil-dominant, early-phase inflammatory activation profile. The combined observation of neutrophil-dominant recruitment and a damaged, leaky lung epithelium (increased protein content in the BAL) after three exposures to i.n. LPS is consistent with ALI-like pulmonary pathology that we did not observe from acute exposure to LPS administered either intranasally or intraperitoneally (Supplemental Figure S1) [39, 40]. Indeed, the synchronous neutrophilia at both the lung and the brain suggest a neutrophil-dependent mechanism regulating the severity of neuroinflammation following lung-related injury.

Analysis of neutrophil content in the peripheral blood was next performed to screen for systemic neutrophilia in circulation and implicate lung-brain inflammatory crosstalk along a vascular route (Fig. 2A); this would explain the cotemporaneous observations of neutrophilia at both endpoints of the neuroimmune axis. Peripheral blood samples from both males and females were collected 24 h after the 3rd i.n. dose and analyzed via flow cytometry in LPS-treated and saline-matched controls (Fig. 2B). We observed that Ly6G+ cells were significantly increased in LPS-treated mice of both sexes. This not only confirmed our hypothesis of a vascular route for neutrophil-dependent lung-brain neuroimmune coupling, but also implicates this as a sex-independent regulatory mechanism [41, 42].

To better study the role of neutrophils, we translated our multi-hit i.n. LPS paradigm to Catchup reporter mice that express the fluorescent tdTomato in one copy of their Ly6g loci to endogenously tag neutrophils [43]. This allowed us to use intravital two-photon (2P) microscopy through somatosensory cortical windows for real-time visualization of neutrophil content and activity in the brain vasculature, which was outlined via intravenous (i.v.) injection of 2,000 kDa FITC-dextran (Fig. 2C). We generated metrics on the frequency of Ly6G+ events in the 2P image sequences using TrackMate in ImageJ [44], and measured an 8-fold enhancement in tdTomato+ neutrophil (red) content compared to that of saline-matched controls (Fig. 2D). This increase in neutrophil numbers was accompanied by a 50% higher frequency of multi-frame trajectories that represent neutrophils stalling and interacting with the endothelium. To account for differences in neutrophil number due to the density of vessels in the selected fields-of-view, we normalized the data against the volume of FITC-dextran measured, as a proxy for vessel volume, and found more pronounced increases (18-fold and 4-fold, respectively) in the count per µm3 of vessel (Supplemental Figures S2A & S2B).

We also immunohistochemically quantified neutrophil content (Ly6G+ DAPI+ objects, Fig. 3B) in the stratum lacunosum moleculare (SLM), the outermost layer of the hippocampal CA1 region, of post-mortem brain sections collected at 6, 24, and 72 h after the 3rd i.n. dose of LPS and saline-matched controls (Fig. 3C). These results not only corroborate the observed neutrophilia from the cortical window 2P image sequences that peaks within the first 24 h after the 3rd dose, but also identify a persistence of increased neutrophil content across different brain areas for at least 72 h. This quantification of elevated neutrophil content in intracerebral vasculature further supports the idea that systemic neutrophilia temporally links similar neutrophil populations present in both the lung and brain during early stages of repeated pulmonary inflammation.

Sub-chronic i.n. LPS induces neutrophil ingress and neurovascular reactivity after three doses

Further analyses from the 2P data allowed us to inquire about the biophysical behavior of neutrophils, including whether changes in frequency of different types of neutrophil engagement with the endothelium could be indicative of distinct neutrophil activation states in response to the multi-hit i.n. LPS paradigm [45, 46]. In a subset of image sequences (Fig. 2C, Supplemental Videos 1–4), we manually annotated the following states of neutrophil engagement at the endothelium – stagnated stalling, constant rolling, pause-and-go crawling, and transmigration through junctions. Clustering analysis was performed on the trajectory outputs from the neutrophils identified using TrackMate and were able to assign the neutrophils into the four categories of dynamic interactions at the endothelium that we visually annotated (Fig. 2C and F, Supplemental Figure S3). From the binned results, a majority (75%) of the identified neutrophil trajectories explored a more confined region. This likely reflects dynamic interactions between neutrophils and the endothelial surface that leads to activation of various pro-inflammatory effector states and even complete extravasation of neutrophils into the parenchyma [45, 46].

Next we immunohistochemically stained for neutrophil-endothelial interactions to validate the clustered TrackMate results that indicated 40% of stalled neutrophils were undergoing transmigration by the 24-hr endpoint (Fig. 3A). The brain vasculature was labeled for CD31 (i.e., platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule 1, PECAM-1) and allowed for further interrogation of spatial distribution of neutrophils at various terminal endpoints (Fig. 3D). From the IHC data, we categorized neutrophil-endothelial interactions into three states: (i) tdTomato-Ly6G+ DAPI+ neutrophil objects in CD31 + vessels, (ii) transmigrating tdTomato-Ly6G+ DAPI+ neutrophil objects touching, but not in, CD31 + vessels, or (iii) extravascular tdTomato-Ly6G+ DAPI+ neutrophil objects not associated with CD31 + vessels (Fig. 3E). This analysis demonstrated that while total neutrophil counts peaked earlier (6 h) after the 3rd i.n. LPS insult, the observation of neutrophil counts at later time points (24 h and 72 h) after the 3rd i.n. dose can be attributed due to infiltrating neutrophils into the brain tissue itself, reflecting their increased half-life [47]. Convincing evidence for this was reflected by the measured accumulation of extravascular neutrophil objects from 6 h to 72 h post 3rd i.n. LPS dose. The accumulation of neutrophils that we measured is in line with the known half-life of neutrophils, which can increase upon infiltration [47].

We also assessed the presence of other indicators of neutrophil transmigratory behavior to explain the increased ingress into the hippocampal parenchyma (Fig. 3D, Supplemental Figures S4-S7). Specifically, we focused on previous reports of neutrophil accumulation abluminal to the BBB associated with (i) increased nuclear lobe segmentation [48], (ii) available CD31 (i.e. PECAM-1) adhesion sites [49], and (iii) VCAM-1 adhesion spots [49]. As a proxy for the number of nuclei associated with each neutrophil, a semi-quantitative parameter for nuclear lobe segmentation was calculated based on the number of DAPI+ objects touching Ly6G+ objects (Supplemental Figure S8). In Fig. 3F, we plotted frequency plots of total lobes per neutrophil aggregated from all replicates in a treatment group to capture the changes in the distribution of number of nuclear lobes at 6, 24, and 72 h after the 3rd i.n. LPS dose. The replicate averaged lobe numbers are shown in the adjacent box plot and used to evaluate a statistically significant increase in nuclear segmentation (i.e. “hypersegmentation”) that aligns with our other observations of increased transmigratory activity at the 24-hr endpoint. We also found concomitant increases in CD31 expression that remained elevated throughout the 72-hr period, but decreased in spatial distribution (Fig. 3G); this may reflect the formation of junctional “hot spots” in the BBB over time to localize inflammation and leukocyte recruitment [49, 50]. In contrast, VCAM-1 expression in CD31 + vessels reached a peak at 6 h after the 3rd i.n. LPS exposure then declined to near baseline levels by the 72-hr endpoint. This may also reflect the concentration of inflammation to junctional “hot spots” [51]. Additionally, this decrease in surface VCAM-1 was not compensated by, nor dependent on, changes in GLUT-1 signaling (Supplemental Figure S9). The complementary results of neutrophil trajectories, from 2P imaging, and neutrophil-endothelial interactions, via IHC, demonstrate widespread engagement with and sporadic migration through vasculature in the somatosensory cortex.

Neutrophil-induced endothelial dysfunction in murine and human microphysiological systems

To determine the translational relevance of our neutrophil-centric mechanism for brain inflammation via endothelial disruption, we constructed MPS models for both the murine and human BBB [52].

In the murine MPS model (Fig. 4A), bEnd.3 monolayers were cultured on optically transparent silicon membranes (µSiMs) and exposed to neutrophils isolated from the peripheral blood of C57BL/6J mice 24 h after our three-dose repeated i.n. exposure paradigm with either vehicle (saline control) or LPS as outlined in Fig. 1B. Neutrophils from saline or i.n. LPS groups reflect a vehicle baseline and stimulated inflammatory profile, respectively. These were applied to monolayer cultures of cerebral endothelial cells (bEnd.3) on a microphysiologic chip in the absence or presence of a mixture of pro-inflammatory cytokines (“CytoMix”) that are elevated in circulation after lung injury. The montage in Fig. 4B depicts typical immunocytochemical results for expression of CD31 (i.e., PECAM-1), VCAM-1 and citrullinated histone H3 (H3-Cit), a pivotal mediator of pro-inflammatory nuclear release of chromatin from neutrophils (the primary constituent of neutrophil extracellular traps, NETs). We assessed the individual roles of the circulating inflammatory profile and neutrophil presence at the BBB in driving inflammatory activation, a predictor of BBB damage, in Fig. 4C. In bEnd.3 models, basal expression of VCAM-1 is low, thus induction of increased expression of VCAM-1 is a standard proxy for inflammatory activation of the endothelial monolayer. VCAM-1 induction was quantified as the net increase of VCAM-1 expression in VCAM-1 + objects as volume-normalized intensity. This demonstrated that inflammatory activation of the endothelium was agnostic towards the response profile of the neutrophils (i.e., isolated from saline-treated vs. LPS-treated mice) without preliminary exposure to an inflammatory milieu, i.e., CytoMix. Furthermore, we investigated whether sex-based differences in neutrophil populations influenced our results from repeated i.n. exposure to saline or LPS. Similar to our in vivo experiments, the sex of the neutrophil donors did not affect the agnostic behavior of the endothelium to the activation profile of the neutrophils (Fig. 4D) without prior activation of the endothelial barrier.

The human MPS models involved primary human neutrophils and hiPSC-derived endothelial cells (EECM-BMEC) grown on the same µSiMs (Fig. 5A). Like the murine experiments, we mimicked a pro-inflammatory cytokine milieu using species matched CytoMix and reproducibly demonstrated differential neutrophil engagement with brain endothelial monolayers dependent on inflammatory induction (Fig. 5B). We also found a significant shift in the frequency of neutrophil transmigration across the µSiMs in activated EECM-BMEC monolayers; this shifted from solely exploring the luminal surface (~ 100%, vehicle-treated) to predominantly transmigrating to the abluminal surface (~ 60%, CytoMix-treated) (Fig. 5C). In aggregate, the alignment of MPS data between the two species provides evidence for primed neutrophils in the vasculature as a mechanism for lung-brain neuroimmune linkage relevant to human disease.

Paravascular fibrinogen accumulation and synaptic targeting accompany systemic neutrophilia during early lung-brain inflammatory coupling

We then returned to our mouse model to evaluate neutrophil-mediated changes more intensively at the NVU during early stages of lung-brain neuroimmune linkage. Pertinently, the co-regulatory feedback between neutrophil activity at the endothelium with changes in fibrinogen metabolism is well documented [46, 53–58]. In concert, these two inflammatory mediators are known to drive initial BBB breakdown, with paravascular fibrinogen eventually binding synapses with complement factors to act as biomolecular “eat me” signals to astrocytes and microglia (Fig. 6A).

Upon staining for fibrinogen at the hippocampus (HPC), we found demonstrable increases in deposition, assessed as the total volume of immunoreactive objects, around the vasculature that is persistent throughout the 72-hr observation window after the third i.n. LPS exposure. This increase of fibrinogen+ objects was complemented by a similarly sustained decrease in spatial overlap with CD31 expression (Fig. 6B). This resembles paravascular deposition in the parenchyma, which is typically correlated with targeting of synapses with complement factors as biomolecular “eat me” signals for microglia and astrocytes to engulf and eliminate (Fig. 6A), and leads to deleterious alterations in cognitive function [55, 59]. Investigation of synaptic structures labeled by post-synaptic density 95 (PSD95) and scaffolding protein Homer-1 in the HPC (Fig. 6C and Supplemental Figure S10) identified consistent downregulation in the number of detected puncta for both markers compared to saline vehicle. Examination of the average expression of Homer-1 per puncta, by normalizing the intensity of each punctum to its volume, demonstrated no significant differences, and represents an “all-or-nothing” loss of post-synaptic features. Linking this to fibrinogen deposition and synaptic elimination [59], we measured significant colocalization of fibrinogen immunoreactive objects with Homer-1 immunoreactive punctae suggesting that the increase in paravascular fibrinogen is associated with loss of postsynaptic elements (Fig. 6D and Supplemental Figure S11).

Persistent neutrophil ingress correlates with dampening of microglial activation profiles

The decline in synaptic densities coinciding with increased paravascular deposition of fibrinogen implies the activation of glial cells, a typical feature of neuroinflammatory activation [46, 55, 56, 60]. Interestingly, when evaluated for IBA1 and CD68 immunoreactivity as proxies for inflammation and phagocytic activity in microglia (Fig. 7A), we found a temporal profile that demonstrated peak microglial activation between 6 and 24 h after the third i.n. LPS dose, followed by a notable decline in both IBA1 + and CD68 + objects at the 72-hour endpoint (Fig. 7B). This temporal profile was captured at both an ensemble aggregate level, as well as population-level distribution of individually segmented IBA1 + microglia cells (Supplemental Figure S12). The timing of this decline in microgliosis and phagocytic activity, however, may explain the sharp loss of synaptic content within 6 h after the third i.n. LPS, that then stagnates through the 72-hr endpoint (Fig. 6C). Given the similar levels of fibrinogen-targeted Homer-1 densities (Fig. 6D), a continued decline in synaptic densities was expected through the 24-hr timepoint; this suggests a potential activity-based diminution of phagocytic potential in affected microglia.

This decline in microglial activation could simply arise from transition to an inflammatory resolving state, such as efferocytosis of apoptotic cells and their associated debris (i.e., neutrophils), after a peak inflammatory stage [61–64]. This may exhaust phagocytic surveillance of microglia away from fibrinogen-targeted synapses. However, the decline in CD68 immunoreactivity does not align with the phagocytic clearing of debris in efferocytosis (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, the kinetics of neutrophilia up to 72 h after the third i.n. LPS exposure suggests continued ingress through the BBB (Fig. 3) that upon interaction with microglia could either drive sustained pro-inflammatory activation in the parenchyma [65, 66] or drive immunosuppression of certain microglial activation states relevant to cognitive impairment [67, 68]. In both scenarios, however, neutrophil engagement with microglia has been shown to downregulate phagocytic function. Thus, we next screened for whether the persistent accumulation of neutrophils in the parenchyma resulted in significant levels of neutrophil-microglia crosstalk. As shown in Fig. 7A and C, cellular objects that contain regions positive for both Ly6G and IBA1 fluorescence increases at the + 6 h endpoint and is persistent through the + 72 h endpoint after the third i.n. LPS exposure. However, the sustained interaction between the two cells over this period did not result in increasing uptake of neutrophil content into CD68 + objects in the microglia (Fig. 7D and E); this also does not align with the possibility for microglia efferocytosis as an upregulated mechanism in our data. Rather, it further implicates a downregulation of phagocytic function in the microglia, potentially due to crosstalk with neutrophils to explain the rapid loss of synapses that plateaus after + 6 h.

Neurovascular vulnerability and microgliosis are not observed in anterior modules of the olfactory cortex in sub-chronic 3-dose i.n. LPS model

The SLM is a key relay between the entorhinal cortex and CA1 of the HPC, where inputs from the entorhinal cortex synapse on CA1 pyramidal cells and regulate memory encoding and retrieval. Thus, a confounding driver for our results is inflammatory propagation through an olfactory route. While the large volume (40 µL) of each dose was selected to ensure exposure along the entire respiratory tract, the i.n. delivery method has been shown to induce microgliosis in the olfactory bulb (OB) in studies of chronic rhinitis [26, 69]. Following innervation along the primary olfactory cortex, this could eventually lead to inflammatory activation of the entorhinal cortex, and thereby the HPC through the CA1 relay. Thus, we screened for microgliosis and vascular vulnerability along the rostral-caudal axis the murine brain sections to assess the viability of this confounding mechanism (Fig. 8).

We first took a global assessment of rostral-caudal inflammatory propagation by staining for IBA1 reactivity (Fig. 8A) and FITC-dextran leakage from vessels (Fig. 8B and Supplemental Figure S13) near the OB and the dorsal HPC. In the rostral sections near the OB we did not find clear indications of widespread inflammatory activation. Separately, in the dorsal HPC we measured clear microgliosis and vascular leakage from larger vessels, in alignment with our neutrophil-centric mechanism. However, we additionally screened for potential differences in inflammatory propagation in the olfactory cortex that may arise from activation of either tufted or mitral cells in the OB [69–71]. As shown in Fig. 8C, we disaggregated our assessment of microgliosis near the OB and dorsal HPC into functional subregions that map to different transduction pathways. For the most rostral section near the OB, we measured IBA1 reactivity in the anterior olfactory nucleus (AON), the tenia tecta (TT), the olfactory tubercle (OT), and the anterior piriform cortex (PIR) near the OB; the DG, CA1, PIR, and mediodorsal nucleus of the thalamus (mdThal) were all measured in the dorsal hippocampus. While we reaffirmed activation of the hippocampus, we also observed heightened IBA1 reactivity from the PIR near both the OB and dorsal HPC. We also observed transient microgliosis in the OT and mdThal 6 h after the 3rd i.n. LPS dose.

We then tracked the potential for inflammatory propagation via mitral cell innervation along the PIR that could then lead to microgliosis in both the mdThal and the dorsal HPC (Fig. 8D and F). Slices at different depths along the rostral-caudal axis were assessed for microglial activation as shown in Fig. 8D. Tracing the anterior to posterior PIR in all five slices found a constant level of elevated IBA1 reactivity throughout the rostral-caudal axis at each treatment endpoint compared to saline controls. Both the mdThal (slices 3–5) and hippocampus (slice 4–5) demonstrated elevated IBA1 reactivity that was most significantly increased at the most caudal section (Fig. 8E and F). Considering the lack of activation of the AON and TT, as well as dampening in the OT after the + 6 h endpoint in Fig. 8C, we did not find robust evidence to suggest inflammatory propagation through the primary olfactory cortex along neither tufted nor mitral cell pathways. Rather, the location of the PIR and OT along the superficial surface of the cortex and the proximity of the mdThal and DG to a ventricle suggest that a vascular-mediated mechanism best explains the neuroinflammatory profile we have observed in the 72-hr period following 3 doses of i.n. LPS (Fig. 8G).

Discussion

In this study, we applied a three-dose i.n. LPS paradigm as a minimally sub-chronic exposure model to study early neuroimmune coupling that is bilateral and seemingly sex-independent (Supplemental Figure S14). Using this exposure model, we implicate cotemporaneous neutrophilia in the lung, systemic circulation, and neurovasculature as key early mediators of lung-brain inflammatory coupling. Particularly, the downregulation in the phagocytic ability of microglia within + 72 h after three i.n. exposures may provide insight into the correlation between increased risk for neurodegenerative diseases (such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s Diseases) and the frequency of exposure to poor air quality containing aerosolized inflammatory factors [3, 8, 33–35]. Such insights may be applicable in understanding the full extent of neurological repercussions in individuals from populations more vulnerable to developing post-infectious sequelae following COVID-19 [19–23]. It is possible that continued inflammatory exposure with ensuing neutrophil recruitment to the parenchyma may lead to terminally immunosuppressed microglia with decreased phagocytic potential, thereby leaving the brain vulnerable to the accumulation of waste factors, such as aggregates of misfolded amyloid-β and α-synuclein, relevant to Alzheimer’s Disease and Parkinson’s Disease, respectively [64, 66, 67]. Our work identifies a potential neutrophil-driven mechanism in early stages of activations along the lung-brain neuroimmune axis relevant to better understanding how chronic respiratory exposure increases risk for pathologic brain disorders. Continued insights from such studies may help identify therapeutic targets to mitigate neuroinflammatory responses and is the subject of our ongoing research.

We first evaluated changes in BAL cellularity and proteinaceous content (Fig. 1) to demonstrate that lung barrier breakdown, indicative of severe lung injury, did not occur until the third i.n. LPS exposure. Further analysis of the BAL cellular composition identified extensive neutrophilia as the predominant driver of increased cellularity. Interestingly, we also measured upregulation of pro-inflammatory and neutrophil-associated transcript levels in brain tissue collected at the same terminal endpoint. The cotemporaneous presentation of inflammatory stress in the lung and brain after 3 doses of i.n. LPS led us to define the 3-dose paradigm as the minimal number of repeat exposures needed to model early-stages of lung-brain neuroimmune coupling.

In our transcript analysis of the brain we could not identify a significant change in monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1) despite its prevalence as a general chemoattractant for immune cells [72]. Further, BAL cellularity also demonstrated a decrease in the percent composition of macrophages and monocytes. This cemented our focus on a neutrophil-driven mechanism along a vascular route to link lung and brain inflammatory states during early stages repeated respiratory exposure. Via a combination of flow cytometry, 2P intravital microscopy, and IHC of thin brain slices, we reproducibly measured and validated increased neutrophil content in both peripheral circulation and the NVU to confirm a profile of systemic neutrophilia (Figs. 2 and 3).

Clustering of TrackMate generated outputs of neutrophil trajectories from the collected 2P image sequences allowed us to characterize the dynamics of neutrophilia during this period of early inflammatory linkage between the lung and brain [48]. We were able to annotate instances of four distinct canonical neutrophil-endothelial cell interactions and identified an increased bias towards endothelial transmigration. Complementary IHC analyses for neutrophilia in the brain were performed on the SLM of the hippocampus, selected for both its involvement in neurodegenerative disease and its functional linkage to the primary olfactory cortex via the entorhinal cortex. To quantify the fractional content of neutrophils that are in vessels, transmigrating, or present in extravascular brain parenchyma, we evaluated the overlap of Ly6G+ DAPI+ objects with CD31 + vessels and binned them as fully, partially, or not all overlapped. This quantified an accumulation of extravascular neutrophils over a 72-hr period post 3rd i.n. dose of LPS, congruent with documented neutrophil behaviors in the brain [47], and highlights the induction of a sustained vascular inflammatory profile within our minimally sub-chronic model. We also determined the degree of nuclear segmentation in the neutrophils based on the number of segmented DAPI+ objects at least partially overlapped with a Ly6G+ cell; this identified a “hypersegmented” population at 24 h after the 3rd i.n. dose. Previous studies have linked “hypersegmenation” in neutrophils with increased transmigratory potential [48], and would corroborate the biophysical interactions captured in both the annotated 2P image sequences and the IHC analysis of Ly6G-CD31 interactions.

In our assessment of neurovascular health, we observed a transient elevation in VCAM-1 expression that did not align with the temporal profile of sustained CD31 upregulation and neutrophil engagement. This diminished VCAM-1 response may be due to negative transcriptional regulation by activating neutrophils or simple cleavage into soluble VCAM-1 that exacerbate endothelial damage [73, 74]. We corroborated this in Supplemental Figure S9 by assessing changes in co-expression of VCAM-1 and glucose transporter 1 (GLUT-1) because of the known regulatory roles for GLUT-1 in the structural integrity of endothelial function [75–77]. We confirmed that GLUT-1 expression remained constant across the three terminal endpoints compared to saline control (Supplemental Figure S9A). As a result, we suggest a GLUT-1-independent mechanisms for changes in barrier permeability – based on the consistency of VCAM-1 and GLUT-1 co-expression (Supplemental Figure S9B), VCAM-1 + CD31+ double immunoreactivity (Fig. 3H), and dextran extravasation (Fig. 8B) – that reflects a reactive endothelial cell inflammatory profile. This may also contribute to fibrinogen deposition because of dynamic flux between competing upregulation and downregulation pathways from neuroinflammation and hypoxia from pulmonary damage, respectively [76–78].

Indeed, the response to neutrophilia can involve the acute phase protein fibrinogen, typically as a protective response to NETosis that occurs with neutrophil cell death [56]. Soluble fibrinogen circulates in plasma, but can deposit in the brain at paravascular spaces, similar to early lesions in multiple sclerosis, where it can attach to axonal nodes and co-localize with microglia [55, 60]. Paravascular fibrinogen can also serve as sites for neutrophil adhesion in Alzheimer’s related models [46]. In line with this, we measured a temporal trend in vascular and paravascular fibrinogen accumulation similar to that of neutrophil engagement and ingress (Fig. 6). Notably, sustained elevated levels of fibrinogen through the 72-hr post-exposure period can be driven by impairment of fibrinolytic mechanisms during high levels of neutrophil engagement at the BBB [57, 79]. However, synaptic targeting by fibrinogen, with increasing loss of synaptic densities did not persist up to the 72-hr endpoint after 3rd i.n. LPS exposure. Rather, the amount of fibrinogen-labeled Homer-1 densities peaked between + 6 and + 24 h, with loss of synaptic contact happening primarily within the 6-hr endpoint after 3rd i.n. dose of LPS.

The stunted depletion of synaptic structures despite continued paravascular fibrinogen deposition is best explained by altered phagocytic activity in glial cells. Thus, we first screened for changes in IBA1 and CD68 expression as proxies for microgliosis and phagocytic activity, respectively (Fig. 7). The trends of both markers followed that of fibrinogen targeting of Homer-1 synaptic densities and peaked between + 6 and + 24 h after the third i.n. LPS exposure. These results further highlight a divergence from the rapid depletion of synapses at + 6 h that plateaus through the + 72 h endpoint. Specifically, the temporal trends of fibrinogen targeting of synapses and increased phagocytic of activated microglia should have resulted in a continued decline in synaptic content. We then evaluated whether persistent neutrophil accumulation in the parenchyma resulted in neutrophil-microglia interactions that inhibited efferocytotic mechanisms in microglia [65–68]. Using detected objects that were Ly6G+ IBA1 + as a proxy for neutrophil-microglia interactions, we identified a significant fraction of extravasated neutrophils were in contact with microglia by the + 6 h endpoint and sustained throughout the + 72 h period after the 3rd i.n. exposure. However, the consistent interaction between the two cell types did not result in increased breakdown and phagocytosis of neutrophils by the microglia (i.e., Ly6G+ CD68 + IBA1+ objects) and further highlights the diminished phagocytic potential in microglia.

These results implicate neutrophil-dependent regulation of microglial activation profiles as the likely mechanism for the downregulation of phagocytic activity that blunted the removal of fibrinogen-targeted synapses. This highlights a dual role for neutrophils to enhance vascular and paravascular deposition of fibrinogen as well as dampen efferocytosis in microglia. We posit that a potential synergism between these two outcomes can be identified through plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 (PAI-1). While its canonical function inhibits the breakdown of fibrinogen by fibrinogen, PAI-1 has also been found to increase following lung injury [80], enhance neutrophil activation [81], and also act as a “don’t eat me” signal on the surface of neutrophils [82], as well as other viable cells. While an assessment of neutrophil-associate PAI-1 was not in the scope of our current study, it demonstrates a potential mechanism to causally intertwine the effector mechanisms of neutrophils on both fibrinogen metabolism and microglia phagocytosis we report in our study.

A confounding mechanism to our proposed neutrophil-centric mechanism along a vascular route is the possibility for inflammatory propagation along an olfactory transduction network from the OB through the primary olfactory cortex and into the entorhinal cortex, that then synapses into the hippocampus in the CA1 [70, 71, 83, 84]. Our assessments of the OB for IBA1 reactivity and high molecular weight FITC-dextran leakage from vessels did not demonstrate prevalent microglial activation nor vascular vulnerability (Fig. 8). Conversely, both metrics for inflammatory activation were significantly elevated in the dorsal hippocampus. Evaluation of functional subregions in the primary olfactory cortex did identify persistent microgliosis in the anterior piriform cortex that could implicate olfactory-mediated activation along a mitral cell pathway; this would diverge from previous evidence for inflammation along a tufted cell route in chronic i.n. LPS studies [26, 69]. However, following the piriform at different depths along the rostral-caudal axis did not indicate a clear directionality of inflammatory propagation, while both the mediodorsal nucleus of the thalamus and hippocampus clearly demonstrated a trend of microgliosis that migrated caudal to rostral. This, complemented by the lack of activation in the other subregions of the anterior olfactory cortex led us to conclude that a vascular-mediated mechanism to be the primary driver of neuroinflammation in our sub-chronic model.

Lastly, we reproduced our findings of neutrophilia and endothelial dysfunction using MPS with murine and human cells to mimic the BBB. These MPS experiments were used as preclinical models to demonstrate the translational relevance of our in vivo model. Using these MPS models, we corroborated our findings of increased potentiation of neutrophils for BBB adhesion and transmigration with hallmarks of neutrophil inflammatory activity. The murine MPS, additionally, allowed us to corroborate the sex-independence observed in our flow cytometry analysis. Given the similarities in results of the murine and human MPS models, we posit that our proposed neutrophil-centric paradigm for lung-to-brain neuroimmune linkage is likely sex-independent in humans as well. Further epidemiologic and neuropathologic studies are needed to verify this observation of sex-independence.

Methods

Sex as a biological variable

In the in vivo mouse experiments and in vitro murine MPS samples, both male and female sexes were accounted for as a variable. An initial screen of BAL and brain transcripts involved on male mice, but all subsequent experiments based on questions from the results of the initial screen validated that similar effects were observed in both male and female mice before aggregating them. The in vitro murine MPS uses neutrophils isolated from both male and female mice. The human MPS system only included the male sex as a biological variable due to the origin of the iPSC donor source and the human volunteer for neutrophils in circulation.

Mice

All mice were housed under a 12-hour light/dark cycle with access to water and regular chow ad libitum in temperature and humidity-controlled environment. Wild-type male and female C57BL/6J were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and housed at Duke University and the University of Rochester Medical Center (URMC). Male and female Catchup mice (C57BL/6-Ly6g(tm2621(Cre-tdTomato)Arte)) were bred and maintained by the Gelbard laboratory at URMC. The Catchup mice were genotyped following previously reported procedures [43]. In brief, ear biopsies were collected around weaning age (3–4 weeks) and digested using a Mouse Direct PCR Kit (Fisher; cat: B40015) following the manufacturer’s protocols. The forward primers for the wild-type (WT) and knock-in (KI) are 5’-GGT-TTT-ATC-TGT-GCA-GCC-C-3’ and 5’-ACG-TCC-AGA-CAC-AGC-ATA-GG-3’, respectively, with a common reverse primer of 5’-GAG-GTC-CAA-GAG-ACT-TTC-TGG-3’. A control primer for separate gene was used consisting of a forward 5’-GAG-ACT-CTG-GCT-ACT-CAT-CC-3’ and reverse 5’-CCT-TCA-GCA-AGA-GCT-GGG-GAC-3’.

Reagents and antibodies

All reagents used in this manuscript are noted in line with manufacturer details where appropriate in the methods. We have also compiled this information in Supplementary Tables S1-S4.

Delivery of LPS

Mice received a total volume of 40 µL LPS (E. coli O111:B4; Sigma; cat: L2630) via bilateral i.n. exposure at a dose of 0.25 µg/µL diluted in 0.9% NaCl saline solution per delivery timepoint at either single timepoint (10 µg total LPS), or across three timepoints spaced 24 h apart across a 48-hour treatment period (0 h, 24 h, 48 h; 30 µg total LPS). Mice that received i.p. delivery of LPS which was performed under isoflurane (Patterson Veterinary, Greeley, CO) anesthesia. All mice recovered from intranasal LPS instillation and were included in the study.

Collection and analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) samples were collected 24 h after the final i.n. exposure in the assigned treatment group. BAL was performed by cannulation of the trachea and gentle instillation/aspiration (3 times) of 1.0 ml of PBS with protease inhibitor cocktail tablets (Roche, Indianapolis, IN). The lavage fluid was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was stored at -80 °C for later protein content assessment. The cell pellet was treated with red-blood-cell lysing buffer (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA), washed, and resuspended in 200 µl of PBS. Total cell counts were determined with a hematology analyzer (Scil Vet ABC, Gurnee, IL), centrifugated onto cytoslides (Cytospin 3, Shandon Inc, Pittsburg, PA) and stained with Diff-Quick (Dade-Behring Inc., Newark, DE). Differential cell counts were obtained by microscopic counting of a minimum of 200 cells/slide, using standard morphological and staining criteria. The total protein in the BALF was measured with a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA).

Real-Time PCR (qPCR)

Brains were harvested from wild-type C57BL/6J mice 24 h after the final i.n. dose of their assigned treatment group. Brain tissue was homogenized in a NextAdvance bullet blender at 4 °C in Eppendorf Safe-Lock tubes, followed by extraction of total RNA from the homogenates using the RNeasy Lipid Tissue Mini Kit from Qiagen (Germantown, MD, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis was performed right after the RNA isolation with the High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) following the manufacturer’s instructions, using 200 ng of RNA per 20 µl cDNA reaction. The resultant cDNA was analyzed using TaqMan® Gene Expression Assays (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) in 10 µl reactions. Thermal cycling was performed with the Real-Time PCR system QuantStudio 5 (AppliedBiosystems). Each sample was performed in triplicates and normalized to the endogenous Act-b gene expression. The CT value of each well was determined using the QuantStudio 5 software and relative quantification was determined by the ΔΔCT method. Specific TaqMan® assays used are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Flow cytometry

Cell pellets were resuspended in FACS buffer (1% BSA + 2.5 mM EDTA in 1x PBS), and Trypan Blue (ThermoFisher; cat: 15250061) stained aliquots were counted on a hemacytometer. After counting, aliquots of 5E6 cells were first blocked with 20 µg of Fc block (ThermoFisher; cat: 14-9161-73) for 30 min on ice. The cells were pelleted at 300 xg for 5 min at 4 °C and resuspended in FACS buffer with fluorophore-conjugated primary antibodies (CD11b PE-TexasRed, ThermoFisher, cat: RM2817; Ly-6G/Ly-6 C, AlexaFluor700, cat: 56-5931-82) validated for flow cytometry. Staining reactions were performed in the dark at 4 °C with buffers and reagents kept on ice until use. The cells were pelleted at 300 xg for 5 min at 4 °C and washed via resuspension in FACS buffer and centrifugation at the same conditions. The cells were then fixed via resuspension in 100 µL of 2% PFA in FACS buffer and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 10 min. The cells were then pelleted and resuspended in 500 µL of FACS buffer. Samples were run on a FACSymphony A1™ (BD Biosciences) controlled by FACS Diva software (BD Biosciences). Results were processed using FCS Express™ (De Novo Software). Details for peripheral blood collection and sample handling can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

Two-photon intravital microscopy

2P intravital microscopy was performed on Catchup mice with somatosensory cortical cranial windows implanted at least 30 days prior to induction of assigned i.n. exposure group. Full details on cranial window surgery can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

An Olympus Fluoview FVMPE-RS Twin-Laser Gantry multiphoton microscope system equipped with two tunable lasers (Spectra-Physics; MaiTai HP DeepSee and InsightX3) was used for intravital imaging. Two-photon excitation was achieved using 100 fsec laser pulses (80 MHz) tuned to 840 nm with a power of ∼50 mW measured at the sample focal plane. For anesthetized imaging sessions, mice were anesthetized via i.p. delivery of a fentanyl cocktail prepared with the same protocol used for cranial window implantation. To visualize the brain vasculature, 100 µL of high molecular weight (2,000 kDa) FITC-Dextran was introduced via tail vein injection at a concentration of 50 mg/mL in 0.9% NaCl saline solution 5 min before intravital imaging. Rodent body temperature was maintained at 37 °C with a heating pad, and the animal’s eyes were protected with lubricant ointment. Intravital imaging was performed using 4x digital zoom at 512 × 512-pixel resolution. Stacks containing 41 slices (1 mm step size) were imaged every 90 s for 15 min to obtain 4D (x, y, z, t) “hyperstack” data used for analysis using ImageJ. Full details available in the Supplementary Materials.

Immunofluorescence

IHC staining of paraformaldehyde (PFA)-fixed brain slices (40 μm thick) was performed as previously reported [85]. Sections were washed to remove cryoprotectant and reduce autofluorescence. Sections were subsequently incubated in blocking buffer (1.5% BSA, 3% normal goat serum, 0.5% Triton-X, and 1.8% NaCl in 1X PBS) containing primary antibodies at room temperature overnight. After washing, sections were then incubated overnight in Alexa Fluor conjugated secondary antibodies (ThermoFisher 1:750) in the same blocking buffer composition. Finally, sections were washed three times with 1X PBS + 1.8% NaCl, mounted on glass slides with Prolong Diamond Antifade Reagent (Invitrogen P36961). For dextran leakage from small and large vessels 40 kDa FITC-Dextran (ThermoFisher; cat: D1845) was injected 5 min prior to perfusion. Full details of the IHC staining protocol can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

Immunocytochemistry of the murine MPS were stained similarly to previous protocols [86], where the cells were briefly rinsed with ice-cold 1x DPBS and then fixed for 12 min at room temperature with 4% PFA in 1x PBS. The cells were washed, blocked with 5% BSA, and stained via an indirect (primary and secondary) immunolabeling protocol before the MPS was disassembled and the membranes mounted onto coverglass for imaging. Full details of the murine MPS staining protocol can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

Both IHC and ICC prepared samples were imaged on the “grid-confocal” structured illumination acquisition system detailed in the Supplementary Materials.

Image analysis

ICC and IHC images were analyzed for semi-quantitative analysis using a combination of Volocity 3DM Quantitation Software (Quorum Technologies) and various packages in ImageJ (NIH). A summarized workflow includes an initial illumination correction and “fine noise filter” over all images. Immunoreactive objects were determined as signal at least n standard deviations above the mean intensity in each channel. Intensity, volume, and position details were summarized in the software and exported to Excel for further workup before importing the data into GraphPad Prism for statistical analysis. Image panels were assembled using Affinity Designer. Full details are available in the Supplementary Materials.

Assembly of Microphysiological Systems

Model murine and human MPSs were assembled following previously published protocols of culturing human-derived cells on µSiM devices in either an open well or flow cell configuration [52, 87–89]. The murine MPS experiments were conducted using an open well configuration. The human MPS experiments were conducted using the flow cell configuration. Full details of µSiM assembly can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

Neutrophil engagement and activation in murine MPS

The murine MPS models were constructed by culturing immortalized murine brain endothelial cell line (bEnd.3) purchased from ATCC (cat: CRL-2299) on µSiM devices. All MPS maintenance and exposure periods to treatments were conducted in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Full details of bEnd.3 cell line maintenance and murine MPS setup can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

The bEnd.3 monolayers in the murine MPS were stimulated with CytoMix, a cytokine cocktail comprised of 150 ng/mL IL-1β (PeproTech; cat: 211-11B) and 150 ng/mL TNFα (PeproTech; cat: 315–01 A) in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (ThermoFisher; cat: 10567022) + 1% fetal bovine serum (Atlas Biologicals; cat: F-0500-D). The selection of IL-1β and TNFα were based on reports of their elevation in serum samples of studies related pulmonary disease [90–92]. Prior to stimulation, the cells were first serum deprived in this 1% serum composition without CytoMix overnight. After 6 h of stimulation, freshly isolated neutrophils from “vehicle” and “ALI” C57BL/6J mice of either sex were added to the MPS and incubated for 3 h before fixation, immunocytochemical staining, and imaging. Approximately 175,000 neutrophils in 75 µL were added to each open well murine MPS. Full details can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

Neutrophil transmigration in human MPS

Human MPS models were constructed using extended endothelial cell culture method-brain microvascular endothelial cell like cells (EECM-BMECs) differentiated from IMR90-4 hiPSCs (WiCell; cat: iPS(IMR90)-4) and cultured in µSIM devices as previously described [52, 87–89]. Full details on the culturing the human MPS models can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

Neutrophil trafficking studies were performed using previously described methods [93, 94]. Briefly, whole blood was drawn from consenting donors and neutrophils were subsequently isolated using a density separation gradient (1-Step Polymorphs, Accurate Chemical & Scientific Co., Westbury, NY). Following isolation, neutrophils were used within three hours to ensure viability. For experimentation, freshly isolated neutrophils were incorporated into hESCR media at a concentration of 3 million cells/mL, deposited into µSiM devices with EECM-BMECs, and placed into an incubation stage (Okolab s.r.l., Pozzuoli, Italy) set to a physiological temperature of 37 °C. The stage was part of a phase-contrast microscopy system (Nikon Ti2E Inverted Microscope, Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and transmigration studies were recorded for 30 min at a framerate of 0.25 Hz with a 40x long working distance lens (NA 0.55). Statistical analysis

One- and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Holm-Sidak’s or Tukey’s post-hoc corrections for multiple comparison tests were used to analyze all data. The specific chosen analysis and post-hoc corrections for the independent data sets are identified, with accompanying rationale, in the appropriate figure legends. All data are presented as mean ± standard error (SEM) with significance at p ≤ 0.05, and all data points representing biological replicates plotted. The sample sizes are identified in the figure legends for each respective dataset.
