Exploring competence in mental health crisis management: a qualitative study of Norwegian prehospital emergency medical personnel
Authors: Hege Skundberg-Kletthagen, Kari Kirbakk Fjær
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to investigate how prehospital emergency medical personnel in Norway perceive their competence and manage encounters with patients who are experiencing mental health crises.DesignA qualitative descriptive design using content analysis was employed, following the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines.SettingThe study was conducted within four emergency medical care services in a Norwegian hospital trust, covering both urban and rural areas.ParticipantsIndividual interviews were conducted with 16 health professionals (9 men and 7 women), aged 24–55 years, with 1–18 years of service experience.ResultsThe results revealed one main theme—‘Balancing security and patient care’—with two subthemes—‘Increasing mental health-related assignments in prehospital care’ and ‘Challenges related to the lack of competence and resources in handling mental health crises’.ConclusionsThis study highlights the increasing complexity and frequency of mental health crises that are faced by prehospital emergency medical personnel in Norway. These personnel often feel unprepared due to a lack of competence and clear procedures, forcing them to rely on clinical experience. Police involvement, particularly in rural areas, underscores the need for better collaboration between law enforcement and healthcare services. To enhance the quality of care, it is essential to implement a standardised curriculum for paramedicine education and provide comprehensive mental health training for all prehospital emergency medical personnel, including emergency medical communication centre operators and other healthcare professionals involved in emergency care.

Introduction

In Norway, the ambulance service is organised as part of the specialist healthcare system. Ambulances are coordinated by the emergency medical communication centre (EMCC), which plays a central role in managing and dispatching prehospital emergency services nationwide. Prehospital emergency medical personnel (EMP) are often the first point of contact for patients experiencing mental health crises. As such, they play a crucial role in improving the accessibility, timeliness and appropriateness of mental healthcare. This marks a notable shift from previous practice, where their primary focus was on acute treatment and transportation for physical health emergencies.1

Although accurate data on the proportion of ambulance calls specifically related to mental health crises in Norway is lacking, it is widely believed that such missions have increased in recent years.14 Concurrently, there has been a significant reduction in the number of hospital beds, shorter inpatient stays and a transfer of responsibilities from specialist mental health services to municipal services, with a shift towards outpatient and ambulatory care.5 6 A key limitation, however, is that most outpatient and ambulatory services are not available 24/7. This may contribute to an increasing number of mental health-related assignments for prehospital emergency services.3 Norway’s geography and settlement patterns also pose challenges, particularly due to long transport times to emergency or specialist healthcare services located in urban centres.

Amendments to the Mental Health Care Act in Norway from 2017 aimed to strengthen patients’ rights to self-determination and legal protection. These changes stipulate that patients who require treatment but do not pose an acute and imminent danger to themselves or others, cannot be forcibly treated if they are deemed competent to give consent.7 For some individuals, this may result in a significant deterioration of their condition before they become eligible for specialist mental healthcare. Since 2017, there has also been a reported increase in cases where police assist healthcare services in managing high-risk mental health situations.8 It is important to note that mental illness alone does not necessarily lead to aggression or violence. However, severe substance abuse often co-occurs with mental illness and is a well-established predictor of violent behaviour.9 10

Previous studies show that EMPs are increasingly dispatched to patients experiencing addiction and mental health challenges. These cases now constitute a substantial portion of their workload, placing considerable pressure on prehospital services.2 3 This trend is mirrored in emergency departments (EDs), where visits related to mental health and substance use have increased markedly, highlighting the need for improved triage systems and alternative care pathways.24 Considering these developments, EMPs’ ability to assess and triage patients appropriately is critical to avoiding unnecessary ED transport and ensuring that patients receive care at the appropriate level. Strengthening EMPs’ competence in recognising and evaluating mental health conditions is therefore essential—not only to safeguard patient outcomes, but also to prevent overburdening prehospital services.1113

Although the number of ambulance calls related to mental health crises has increased, Roggenkamp et al found that only a small proportion of these cases require urgent intervention involving ambulance, police and specialist mental health services.2 The challenge, then, is that EMPs often lack the specialist expertise needed to manage such situations effectively. Despite growing international attention to these challenges, there remains a need for more context-specific knowledge. While several studies have identified gaps in paramedics’ training, role clarity and decision-making processes12 1416 and have pointed to potential consequences such as increased work pressure and negative attitudes towards patients with mental health and substance use disorders,1719 there is still limited insight into how these issues are experienced by Norwegian EMPs. Understanding how they perceive their own competence, how they navigate complex mental health encounters and what kind of support they require to provide safe and appropriate care is therefore of relevance.

This study aims to investigate how prehospital EMP in Norway perceive their competence and manage encounters with patients who are experiencing mental health crises.

Methods

Study design

A qualitative and descriptive design, with individual interviews, was conducted and analysed by content analysis.20

Sample and recruitment

The participants in this study were 16 health professionals who are employed in four emergency medical care services within a Norwegian hospital trust, representing both urban and rural areas. Emergency medical services (EMS) encompass a range of occupational categories, predominantly ambulance professionals (emergency medical technicians) and individuals with advanced training in paramedicine, including those with a bachelor’s degree in paramedicine. Occasionally, EMS personnel include registered nurses.21

To be eligible for inclusion, participants had to be authorised healthcare professionals with at least 1 year of experience working in a prehospital setting in a near full-time position. The sample consisted of nine men and seven women, aged between 24 and 55 years (median age: 35). Their years of service ranged from 1 to 18 years (median: 9).

Participants were recruited through their immediate managers, who provided oral and written information about the study. Those who agreed to participate gave written informed consent,22 after which the managers shared their names and contact details with the research team. We did not collect information about individuals who declined participation; consequently, reasons for non-participation are unknown and are acknowledged as a limitation of the study.

Although the sample included both EMCC operators and ambulance personnel, the aim of the study was not to compare these groups. Rather, the analysis focused on shared experiences and perceptions across the prehospital emergency care setting.

Data collection

Both authors conducted individual interviews during the spring of 2020, and all were completed before the national COVID-19 lockdown in Norway on March 12. As such, the pandemic did not influence the conduct of the interviews in terms of logistics, setting or participant availability. The researchers contacted each participant to agree on a time and place for the interview, which was carried out in the participant’s home or workplace, according to the participant’s wishes. The interviews lasted for between 42 and 64 min (median: 48 min). The interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim to ensure accurate and comprehensive data.

A total of 16 interviews were conducted. The number of interviews was determined based on the study aim, the relevance of the sample and the richness of the data. As the analysis progressed, we observed that the data became increasingly repetitive, and no new themes or insights emerged in the final interviews. This suggested that thematic saturation had been reached, and that additional interviews were unlikely to contribute substantially new information.

The interviews were conducted using an open-ended approach, without a semistructured interview guide. Participants were first introduced to the purpose of the study: to explore how prehospital EMP in Norway perceive their competence and manage encounters with patients experiencing mental health crises. They were then invited to share their experiences of such encounters in their everyday work. The interviewer used exploratory follow-up questions such as ‘Can you elaborate?’, ‘Have I understood you correctly?’, and ‘Can you give an example?’ to encourage participants to expand on their reflections and to ensure the conversation remained aligned with the study’s aim. This approach also enabled us to clarify ambiguities and avoid misunderstandings in real-time.

Analysis

A qualitative inductive content analysis method was employed for the data analysis, drawing inspiration from the steps outlined by Graneheim and Lundman.20 Each interview recording was carefully listened to by the two researchers, and the transcripts were read to acquire an overall impression of the material. Meaningful statements aligned with the study’s purpose were identified and marked as meaning units. Subsequently, the text was condensed and reduced into smaller units. These units were then coded, and the codes were organised based on their similarities and differences in content. The codes were presented in a matrix to facilitate further abstraction into themes and subthemes. An example of the analytical pathway from meaning units to themes (including condensation, coding and categorisation) is provided in online supplemental table 1. The analysis process continued until no new codes emerged. Both researchers have specialist competence in mental health and work in health professional education and research but have no experience from prehospital care. This may have influenced both data collection and analysis, for example, by interpreting situations through a clinical psychiatric lens. To minimise potential bias, the analysis was conducted collaboratively, with continuous dialogue and critical reflection on our own assumptions and positionality.

The analysis revealed one main theme: ‘Balancing security and patient care’, with two subthemes: ‘Increasing mental health-related assignments in prehospital care’ and ‘Challenges related to the lack of competence and resources in handling mental health crisis’.

Findings

Balancing security and patient care

The increase in mental health assignments for EMP has led to higher workloads and challenges in handling patients who fall outside traditional services. EMCC operators have noticed a significant rise in inquiries, especially outside regular hours, for mental health assistance. Patients who do not fit into existing services become frequent callers. The lack of expertise and guidelines to solve this mission creates challenges for ambulance personnel, who often feel powerless due to limited treatment options. Ambulance personnel often perceive mental health assignments as time-consuming. However, this perception may be influenced by a lack of interest and knowledge in handling such cases, which challenges their patience. Workplace attitudes and prejudices play a significant role in shaping how ambulance personnel treat patients with mental health issues. Addressing and mitigating these biases is essential for ensuring effective care delivery.

Collaboration with law enforcement is frequently required, particularly in cases involving violence or suicide risk. Nonetheless, challenges such as accessing police assistance and managing associated costs can complicate this collaboration. When dealing with mental health crises, ambulance personnel face dilemmas related to information needs, security concerns and communication barriers. The effective navigation of these challenges often necessitates specialised training and support.

Increasing mental health-related assignments in prehospital care

EMCC operators serve as the first line of response, receiving all inquiries and assessing the situation. They quickly evaluate whether an ambulance needs to be dispatched to clarify the situation. Many inquiries occur outside regular hours for other mental health services, including general practitioners (GPs), ambulatory services or outpatient follow-ups. EMCC operators deal significantly with repeat callers who often need someone to talk to during non-standard service hours. One operator shared this experience: “Just last night, I spoke with a man about 30 times during a shift, and he has support around him […].”

EMCC personnel’s experience is that many patients fall through the cracks and need to fit neatly into mainstream services, which results in them becoming frequent callers within the system. This can be challenging because EMCC operators must answer all calls within 10 s, often handling these situations under time pressure. One operator described it as follows: “It takes a little time. Moreover, it is […] I feel like […] they become like a shuttlecock […], many of them. Suppose there is no immediate risk of suicide or other serious issues, then it may not warrant an ambulance dispatch.” It is often unsatisfactory for the ambulance workers who respond to these assignments to know that no treatment options are available. Instead, however, it becomes a matter of firefighting and symptom relief. They also describe how patients often say that there is no point in going to the hospital or emergency room because there is no help to be had. In cases where the patient is assessed to be competent to provide consent and is not suicidal, they find that the patient quickly returns to their home address.

Although several operators mention taking their time if it does not affect other inquiries, they also find that patients know which buttons to press in order to get help—especially if they want assistance before other services reopen. For instance, patients may mention suicidal thoughts. Operators also have experience of being held accountable if the caller feels they are not receiving the necessary help. Examples include statements such as, “It is your fault if he/she takes their own life”, or situations where someone, after being advised to contact their regular GP the next day, says, “But I am also considering ending my life […].” In such cases, EMCC personnel feel obligated to dispatch an ambulance.

There has been an increase in assignments where the patient’s issue is primarily or secondarily related to mental health problems. For example, a call might be to save a life in the context of self-harm, but the underlying problem is a mental health issue. As one operator explains, “It is common for us to dispatch an ambulance, but […] they might have self-inflicted wounds and have taken some substances, which leads us to send them on a medical mission.”

Furthermore, mental health assignments are often described as being time-consuming tasks. However, some of the ambulance personnel also reflect that the real reason may be a lack of interest, knowledge or patience, and not necessarily the time factor, as one described it: “Body language. You can get a bit like […] ‘Oh, come on!’ Even though you think you are standing there completely calm, it is undeniable that you are stressed.” Attitudes towards patients with mental health issues are influenced by understanding and knowledge, as well as workplace cultures – how one talks about and discusses this issue. As one of the paramedics expressed it: “If the patient expresses suicidal thoughts, then… they [the ambulance staff] roll their eyes and start looking for […] ways to […] how to solve this in the least bad way. So, I think it is simply a lack of competence.”

In ambulance services, there are always two partners; thus, one also depends on the other’s knowledge and attitudes towards these missions. One experiences it like this: “Yes. I think many are probably prejudiced; I do not know where it comes from, whether it is because one finds things difficult or feels that one is not competent to handle the situation well.”

In some cases, a lack of competence and knowledge can contribute to the perception that these tasks are more time-consuming. The medical index used by EMCC as a guide proves to be of limited help in assignments regarding mental health issues. This limitation stems from the difficulty of standardising measures due to the numerous variables involved. As one other illustrated: “An ECG (electrocardiogram) in connection with a heart attack can appear the same. In contrast, a meeting with a person who is in danger of suicide can be perceived very differently.” Additionally, the time pressure that is present in such situations further complicates matters: “There are symptoms that prompt our reaction. However, perhaps certain mental health conditions tend to be more dramatic.” Another EMCC operator expressed a similar sentiment: “I require more knowledge about it, so I do not have to grapple with this uneasy feeling. More professional certainty regarding what to say or to avoid triggering or making mistakes. I attempt to demonstrate care and understanding over the phone. However, we know they are adept at manipulation, and […].”

Another factor is what several describe as a lack of competence and understanding of mental illness in general, but particularly also a lack of skills beyond personal clinical experience. Meetings with relatives can be challenging, especially when there is a lack of knowledge about the patient’s condition, and they feel pressured to answer questions. It is especially demanding to encounter children in situations where it is not reported that minors are present, and they are not prepared. For example, when a 6-year-old opens the door and they realise that it is the mother who has taken pills, or they discover a child hiding because the father has been violent towards the mother. Meetings with relatives often occur when they are in crisis. Standing alongside relatives can be emotionally challenging, especially in situations where suicide has been committed. These situations that require more than their existing knowledge, emphasising non-technical skills, were also in demand.

Other experiences arise when the patient’s situation is unclear, and the EMCC has limited information about the patient from previous encounters. Substance abuse and psychosis issues make specific assignments more demanding and unpredictable. Additionally, if the patients come from a different linguistic and cultural background, they will require a skill set that is distinct from what they possess.

The participants describe that they must primarily rely on their clinical experience, as their education has not traditionally focused on psychiatry and mental health work as essential competencies for their training. The clinical experience that they possess is what they must rely on, and this varies with the number of years for which they have been in the profession and service. Their education had not placed much emphasis on knowledge about mental health or of the legislation that regulates mental healthcare, with some exceptions for those with a nursing background, who had received some in their primary education.

Challenges related to the lack of competence and resources in handling mental health crisis

The ability to receive assistance from other healthcare services, such as the assertive community treatment (ACT) team, was mentioned as a desire but only functioned to a limited extent in practice. As one operator expressed, “I wish they could be used for quite a lot—for example, a phone call with the patient and making an assessment. They are much better than us, you know, at making assessments and […] and have more time. And then they could return the case to us and say that here we need an ambulance and perhaps the police or […]. Moreover, they could sometimes accompany us.” Some proposed that there should be telephone support, especially from the on-call psychiatrist, for situations when their expertise and experience fell short. The recommendation was to include a psychiatric nurse or other professionals with comparable competence.

The assessment of legislative requirements and collaboration with other agencies is part of everyday life. The challenge here may be that different parties work in accordance with different legislation, which only sometimes correspond well and contribute to solving health missions in the best possible way for the patients. EMCC operators have a vital mapping task before ambulance personnel are involved, and many things are mapped out: “Our task is to assess healthcare needs and how we can help them best. Thus, in the first instance, we assess the need for an ambulance—the degree of suicide risk. Moreover, if they are violent or acting up, we need to involve the police. Because ambulance personnel are not allowed to touch the patients physically, they must be voluntary. So, if they are acting up, we need to involve the police. So, we cooperate a lot with the police. There is a very fluid boundary, what is a health mission and what is […] well, substance abuse and violent missions, then.”

The police are the authority that most often assists the ambulance personnel. This is especially the case when there are concerns about potential violence and aggression. Variations were described in how safe or unsafe certain ambulance personnel felt. The amount of information they receive before a mission also varies. Having little information could make them unprepared to plan how to handle the missions. For some, it was essential to approach the assignment with the attitude that they should manage it without assistance, and it should always be attempted without involving the police. Others might feel unsafe and quickly conclude that they need help from the police. They often have the police as a backup’. As one described it: “[…] because it is often threatening when the police arrive. It is much better to be with our healthcare personnel. And then it usually calms down, and then maybe they can join us, you know. “At least talk to us.” Another typical experience is: “[…] if there is an 89-year-old lady […] who is confused and hallucinating, then we must not involve the police.” There is also a widespread understanding of resource challenges within the police force. In rural areas, the police may not be immediately available when needed because they have other missions or are stationed many miles away. Based on this, the police resources are constrained, and assistance may not always be immediately available to assist in healthcare missions without costing money. One other experience with this was as follows: “And the police […] are very adamant that anything labelled psychiatric is healthcare. So, if they are going to assist us with a healthcare mission, they send a bill. Regardless.” Personal safety is the top priority. Sometimes it was best to withdraw from the situation and wait for assistance. It could feel unsafe to have patients in the vehicle; ambulance personnel feared that they might become aggressive, as the interior layout and the available medical equipment posed a potential risk to both the patient and the ambulance personnel. The police are also used as leverage to persuade the patient to voluntarily accompany them to, for example, the emergency department or for admission to hospital. The patient was given an ultimatum: either they came with us, or the police would be called. One exemplified it as follows: “you give them an ultimatum, then. Either it is us (ambulance) or it is the police. Yes, then they calm down a bit.” The dilemma lies in how much information ambulance personnel need from the EMCC to be able to provide the best possible treatment and safe transportation of the patient. In cases where patients have a known history of violence, this was noted in the patient’s journal and could be shared with ambulance personnel, indicating previous threats of violence, aggression or weapons. At the same time, they reflected that it is helpful to meet the patient without too many preconceptions, as they see that patients may not necessarily pose a danger to themselves and others today, even if they have done so in the past. Meeting these patients requires more knowledge about mental illness, but it is equally important to practice skills in communication and handling violence and aggression. One describes it as, “How are you going to communicate? Some tips on how to, how to guide them to other thoughts. I mean, there and then. In a way, make them feel safe with us. Trust us. I missed that a bit.”

Discussion

This study aims to investigate how prehospital EMP in Norway perceive their competence and manage encounters with patients experiencing mental health crises.

Our findings indicate that the number of assignments related to mental health crises is increasing, which has been demonstrated by Roggenkamp et al2 to challenge the prehospital chain in several ways. The EMCC receives calls when patients, relatives or others report needing help in acute situations. EMCC operators in Norway use a medical index with preformulated questions to help triage situations and determine the need for assistance, dispatching an ambulance for urgent cases or advising transfers to a local ED for less urgent situations.23

Gaining a good understanding of the situation requires that the operator has knowledge of acute mental illness and other behavioural health problems that necessitate intervention from prehospital services. Operators rely on understanding the situation based on the words used by the caller. In some cases, the caller may be a frequent contact who is known to the operators, which enables them to have prior knowledge of the patient’s diagnosis and the care that they already receive. In other instances, the patients may be unfamiliar, and the operators must base their understanding of the situation solely on the information provided during the call. This variability highlights a significant challenge: competence regarding the characteristics of individuals with various mental illnesses is often lacking and is primarily based on clinical expertise.11

Ambulance personnel’s pre-understanding before they arrive at the scene is heavily reliant on the information provided by the EMCC. Studies indicate that insufficient competence and training in handling acute somatic situations can be stressful for ambulance workers.24 25 This stress is likely to be exacerbated when dealing with mental health crises and other behavioural health problems, as the complexity and unpredictability of these situations demand a high level of expertise and preparedness. Furthermore, the absence of a clear procedure for handling the assignment can further complicate the situation. Their knowledge of mental illness and attitudes towards individuals with mental illness and possibly substance abuse vary, with many highlighting clinical experiences as their primary source of reliance when making assessments and initiating measures. Substance abuse and psychosis issues make specific assignments more demanding and unpredictable. The increase in police involvement in mental health-related situations, as reported by the Norwegian Police Directorate,8 underscores the need for integrated approaches to handle such complex cases. This often involves collaboration to ensure safety and appropriate care for individuals in crisis. However, the ambulance personnel in our study prefer to handle the assignments themselves, using the police as a backup if the situation feels unsafe and aggression escalates. The involvement of the police in such situations can inadvertently result in heightened public stigma. Previous studies indicate that police interventions are more likely to culminate in arrests for minor offences among individuals grappling with mental illness.26 However, in rural areas in Norway, the police may also be several hours away from where the ambulance personnel are on assignment, and they must either resolve the situation with the resources they have or withdraw. Previous research has shown that paramedics in rural areas often feel less confident when handling mental health crises because they encounter these situations less frequently.15 Additionally, Xanthopoulou et al27 found that police officers lack the necessary competence to effectively respond to mental health crises, including a need for improved training to reduce stigma and minimise the use of force.

When our participants report that many inquiries occur outside regular hours for other mental health services, it raises the question of whether the organisation and accessibility of healthcare services for individuals with mental health issues and substance abuse problems are adequate. The prehospital service may be constrained by a healthcare system with insufficient offerings for all patients, particularly where revolving-door patients do not fit into the available services. Patients may appreciate the quick assistance provided by prehospital services.11 However, our findings showed that it might be distressing for ambulance personnel responding to these assignments to know that no treatment options are available. Instead, their role becomes one of firefighting and symptom relief. They also describe how patients often expressed that there was no point in going to the hospital or ED because there was no help to be had. Despite this, prehospital services must respond to acute crises and travel to patients even when they know that no healthcare service can provide the necessary help. This also increases the pressure on prehospital services. To strengthen the link between the results and national care structures, it is important to consider the role of outpatient services in preventing emergency calls. In Norway, despite the implementation of models such as ACT and Flexible ACT, the limited availability of mental health services outside regular hours remains a barrier to timely intervention. These teams are designed to improve integration and continuity of care across fragmented service systems, yet they typically operate only during office hours and are not accessible to all individuals in need.28 29 Inclusion criteria are often unclear, leaving many patients with severe mental illness and co-occurring substance use disorders without adequate follow-up. These individuals frequently require simultaneous support from both specialist and community mental health services, but risk falling through the cracks—being considered too well for hospital care and too ill to manage independently in the community.30 Internationally, mobile crisis teams and co-responder models have shown promise in reducing reliance on police and emergency medical services by providing person-centred, timely care in the community.31 32 These models emphasise accessibility and collaboration across sectors, which are also key principles in Norwegian health policy but are not yet fully realised in practice. Professionals in both health and emergency services report challenges in managing complex cases due to fragmented structures and unclear responsibilities.2 Strengthening outpatient and community-based services, including after-hours availability and clearer inclusion criteria, could reduce emergency calls and improve continuity of care. In several countries, healthcare personnel with advanced education in mental healthcare work alongside paramedics,2 33 or there are dedicated psychiatric ambulances with personnel with special competence suited for this type of assignment. This approach has also been implemented in some of Norway’s larger cities. However, justifying such specialised services in rural areas has been challenging economically, where a more generalist competence is often required. As previous studies suggest, the role of those working in prehospital services may have to change to meet this increasing need. In the future, they may play a crucial role in assessing the patient’s need for help, which will require increased competence.2 26 34 In their review, Bull et al35 found that individuals who visit EDs for mental health reasons often report having poor experiences. This highlights the urgent need for better mental health education and training for all ED staff.

Our study finds that ambulance personnel often perceive mental health assignments as time-consuming. Simultaneously, they acknowledge that this perception could be due to a lack of interest and knowledge in handling such cases, which challenges their patience. When prehospital personnel feel unprepared, it can lead to patients being treated non-therapeutically and unprofessionally. Examples from our study include displaying inappropriate body language, such as sighing and rolling their eyes in front of the patient or suspecting that the patient is only seeking attention. Reducing the stigma of mental illness in paramedicine is just as important as in other parts of society and healthcare17, and increased knowledge can have a positive impact.

Additionally, the paramedics report a lack of confidence and knowledge as barriers to meeting this growing demand concerning mental health assignments.1 Norway lacks a national standard for the paramedicine education curriculum, which may be considered a barrier to supporting the future workforce in addressing mental health issues effectively. This lack of standardisation results in significant variations in the mental health training provided across different educational programmes, including ambulance technician and paramedic education. In recent years, there has been an increased focus on in-service mental health education in Norway, which is an important initiative. Based on the participants’ experiences, our findings suggest several core competencies that should be considered in the development of standardised mental health training for EMS personnel. First, participants emphasised the need for improved communication techniques, particularly in de-escalating tense situations and building trust with patients in crisis. This includes learning how to guide patients toward safer thoughts and actions in the moment, and how to make them feel safe and respected. Second, there is a need for better recognition and assessment of mental health symptoms, including suicide risk, psychosis and substance-induced states. Participants also highlighted the importance of understanding legal frameworks and the complexities of inter-agency collaboration, especially when roles and responsibilities between health services and law enforcement are unclear or overlapping. In addition, training should address risk assessment and personal safety strategies, including how to manage aggression and unpredictable behaviour in confined spaces such as ambulances. Several participants expressed a desire for access to psychiatric expertise, such as on-call psychiatrists or psychiatric nurses, and suggested that EMS personnel should be trained in when and how to seek such support. Finally, ethical considerations—such as the use of police as leverage when patients refuse care—should be included to ensure that paramedics are equipped to make informed and compassionate decisions in challenging situations. While the need for a standardised curriculum in paramedicine education is evident, our findings also underscore that competence gaps are not limited to paramedics. EMCC operators and other healthcare professionals working in prehospital emergency care similarly require structured, role-specific training in mental health crisis management. Addressing these needs across all professional groups is essential to ensure consistent, safe and collaborative care for patients in crisis.

These competencies reflect the areas where participants felt least prepared and most vulnerable, and they underscore the need for a structured and comprehensive approach to mental health education in paramedicine.

Methodological considerations

The study was conducted in a Norwegian context, and transferability must be interpreted cautiously. However, we believe this study may contribute to the body of knowledge on prehospital mental healthcare. The sample represents variation regarding background variables such as gender, education, roles and geographical context (urban and rural), which contributed to the richness and breadth of the data.

Although we did not conduct formal member checking, peer debriefing or triangulation, we ensured the credibility of the findings through several other strategies. Both researchers (HS-K and KKF) were involved in all steps of the analysis to ensure trustworthiness36 37, and the process was characterised by continuous dialogue, critical reflection and consensus-building.37 The analysis followed a systematic and transparent approach, and the results are illustrated with representative participant quotes to support transparency and allow readers to assess the interpretations.

We chose not to attribute quotes to specific participants or include demographic identifiers such as gender, professional background or years of experience. This decision was made to protect participant anonymity, particularly given the relatively small and specialised professional environment in which the study was conducted. Furthermore, the study aimed to explore shared perceptions and experiences among prehospital EMP, rather than to compare subgroups. The variation in the sample was intended to ensure information richness, not to support subgroup analysis. This approach allowed us to maintain focus on the thematic content and avoid overinterpretation based on individual characteristics.

As with all qualitative research, potential limitations such as social desirability bias and the dual role of researchers must be acknowledged. In addition, we did not document the number or characteristics of staff who declined participation, and we are therefore unable to report reasons for non-participation.

Conclusions

This study highlights the increasing complexity and frequency of mental health crises faced by prehospital EMP in Norway. These personnel often feel unprepared due to a lack of competence and clear procedures, which forces them to rely on clinical experience. The issue of police involvement, particularly in rural areas, highlights the need for better collaboration between law enforcement and healthcare services. To enhance the quality of care, it is essential to implement a standardised curriculum for paramedicine education and provide comprehensive mental health training for all prehospital EMP, including EMCC operators and other healthcare professionals involved in emergency care.
