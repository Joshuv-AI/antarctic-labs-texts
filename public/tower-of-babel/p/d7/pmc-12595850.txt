Activation of the zona incerta GABAergic circuit for the treatment of pain hypersensitivity in Parkinson’s disease mice model
Authors: Juntao Cui, Junliang Qian, Yaying Shi, Yanhong Liu, Le Qu, Wenting Jia, Junxia Xie, Limin Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPain is a prevalent and frequently undertreated non-motor symptom in Parkinson’s disease (PD), affecting approximately 40% to 85% of patients. The zona incerta (ZI) is primarily an inhibitory subthalamic region involved in nociceptive neurotransmission. Previous studies demonstrated that the ZI is an important gamma-aminobutyric acid-ergic (GABAergic) source to the ventral tegmental area (VTA), but whether the ZI-VTA pathway participates in PD pain processing is still unclear. Given that deep brain stimulation targeting the ZI has demonstrated beneficial effects on pain perception in PD patients, we hypothesized that GABAergic signaling within the ZI may play a critical role in PD pain hypersensitivity.MethodsMale C57BL/6 mice and Vgat-Cre mice were used in the experiments. A unilateral PD mouse model was established through the injection of 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) into the striatum. We performed von Frey test and hot/cold plate tests to assess nociceptive behavior in PD mice. We then employed fiber photometry, chemogenetic, and optogenetic approaches to investigate the roles of ZI GABAergic neurons and ZIGABA-VTA circuit on PD pain hypersensitivity.ResultsActivation of ZI GABAergic neurons alleviated pain hypersensitivity in PD mouse model, whereas inhibition of these neurons was sufficient to induce pain hypersensitivity in control naïve mice. Furthermore, we demonstrated that projections from GABAergic neurons in the ZI to VTA are involved in regulating pain associated with PD. Chemogenetic and optogenetic activation of the ZIGABA-VTA circuit alleviated pain symptoms; conversely, inhibiting activity within this circuit exacerbated both mechanical and thermal pain hypersensitivity.ConclusionsOur results indicate that the ZI GABAergic neurons and their downstream projections to the VTA play crucial roles in modulating pain processing under both physiological conditions and during PD states. This research offers fresh perspectives on the PD pain mechanism. Considering our previous report indicating that stimulation of ZI GABAergic neurons improved motor performance in 6-OHDA-lesioned mice, we propose that the ZI may serve a dual regulatory function concerning both motor behavior and pain response associated with PD.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-025-02198-7.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, Zona incerta, GABA, Ventral tegmental area, Pain hypersensitivity

Background

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the selective loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc), accompanied by a reduction in dopamine levels within the axonal terminals of the striatum and the aggregation of intracellular α-synuclein. With the global increase in the aging population, the incidence of PD is experiencing a significant upward trend, with projections indicating that 25.2 million individuals will be living with PD worldwide by 2050 [1]. Patients with PD primarily exhibit classic motor dysfunction, including resting tremor, muscle rigidity, bradykinesia, postural instability, and abnormal gait. It is worth noting, however, that non-motor symptoms are also prevalent and can be distressing [2].

In addition to the classical dopaminergic deficit, an increasing body of evidence has underscored the substantial alterations in the gamma-aminobutyric acid-ergic (GABAergic) system associated with PD, spanning from premotor phases to advanced stages [3]. Patients diagnosed with PD exhibit reduced plasma levels of GABA [4]. Magnetic resonance imaging reveals markedly lower concentrations of GABA in both the sensorimotor cortex and upper brain stem regions among individuals with PD [5, 6]. The dysregulation of the GABAergic system plays a critical role in both motor and non-motor symptoms associated with PD. It is integral to basal ganglia function, thereby regulating inhibitory tone on structures such as the globus pallidus, SNc, and thalamus, thus preventing excessive stimulation of the cerebral cortex. Postmortem studies have identified disturbances within GABAergic neurons located in the basal ganglia [7], while administration of the GABAA agonist zolpidem has demonstrated significant efficacy in alleviating dyskinesia among patients with PD [8]. The non-motor symptoms, including rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder, olfactory dysfunction, gastrointestinal abnormalities, visual disturbances, cognitive impairments, mood disorders and pain, are also prevalent in PD and represent a considerable source of disease burden. The involvement of the GABAergic system has been reported concerning these non-motor manifestations as well [9].

Pain, recognized as one of the earliest clinical non-motor symptoms of PD, is a prevalent yet frequently under-recognized manifestation of this condition. As early as 1817, James Parkinson described the comorbidity of pain in individuals diagnosed with PD [10]. It afflicts approximately 40 to 85% of PD patients [11]. Given the rising prevalence of PD, it is projected that by 2030 there will be approximately 3.7 million individuals worldwide suffering from untreated PD-related pain; however, around 50% of PD patients experiencing pain do not receive any analgesic treatment [12–14]. Accurate identification of PD pain is crucial for its diagnosis and effective treatment. However, classifying pain associated with PD presents considerable challenges. Generally speaking, PD-related pain can be classified into five categories: musculoskeletal pain, dystonic pain, neuropathic pain, akathitic discomfort and central pain [14–16]. PD patients exhibit lower pain thresholds to thermal, cold, mechanical or electrical stimuli compared to healthy controls, which indicates the presence of a hypersensitivity state [17]. These painful symptoms further restrict patients’ daily activities and motor functions, exacerbate motor disorders, and heighten vulnerability to other non-motor symptoms such as anxiety and depression, thereby perpetuating a vicious cycle [18–20]. Nevertheless, the underlying mechanisms are complex and not yet fully understood. Several brain regions and neurotransmitter systems, including the dopaminergic system within the basal ganglia, the serotonergic system in the spinal cord, and the GABAergic system located in the periaqueductal gray (PAG), are implicated in central pain processing [21]. In this study, we focus on another GABAergic system found in the zona incerta (ZI) that also plays a critical role in endogenous pain modulation.

The ZI serves as an integrative node interconnected with the basal ganglia, thalamus, and brainstem regions that are implicated in PD pathology [22]. In rodent models, the ZI can be roughly divided into four components: rostral ZI, dorsal ZI, ventral ZI, and caudal ZI. The dorsal and ventral regions are central areas collectively referred to as the medial ZI [23]. Distinct components of the ZI exhibit diverse connectivity patterns and fulfill multiple roles [24]. Notably, deep brain stimulation (DBS) of the ZI has emerged as a promising therapeutic intervention for alleviating PD motor symptoms [25]. Our previous study demonstrated that both chemogenetic and optogenetic activation of GABAergic neurons in the ZI attenuated motor symptoms in PD mouse models [26]. Beyond its role in motor functions, morphological evidence reveals that the spinothalamic tract sends dense nociceptive inputs to ZI, suggesting a potential role of ZI in nociceptive neurotransmission [27]. Specifically, suppressed GABAergic signaling within the ZI has been shown to induce neuropathic pain in rat models of thoracic hemisection spinal cord injury and chronic constriction injury of the sciatic nerve [28, 29]. Clinically, DBS targeting the ZI has yielded beneficial effects on PD pain perception. Stimulation of the ZI at physiological frequencies (20 Hz) has been found to reduce experimental heat pain while increasing pain scores in patients with PD [30, 31]. Given the dual role of the ZI in mediating both PD-related motor symptoms and pain processing, we hypothesize that GABAergic neurons within this region may represent a critical neural substrate integrating motor deficits alongside sensory disturbances observed in PD. The VTA, which is a key downstream target structure connected to the ZI [32, 33], is also implicated in affective components related to pain experiences [34–36]. 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) injection into the VTA produced significant decrease in pain threshold [37]. However, it remains unclear whether GABAergic innervation from the ZI to the VTA plays a role in regulating pain associated with PD. Therefore, investigating the ZI-VTA circuit may yield novel insights into the neural mechanisms underlying PD-related pain.

In this study, unilateral injection of 6-OHDA into the striatum was employed to induce PD in mice. 6-OHDA is the most commonly used neurotoxin for eliminating dopaminergic neurons in the SNc and inducing PD pain-like behavior in rodents [38, 39]. We utilized chemogenetic and optogenetic techniques to bidirectionally modulate GABAergic neurons within the ZI and their axonal projections. Our results support the hypothesis that GABAergic neurons within the ZI are implicated in pain hypersensitivity processing in PD mouse model. Activation of the ZI GABAergic neurons and the ZIGABA-VTA pathway reduced pain hypersensitivity in 6-OHDA mice. Given that pain is often associated with negative emotional states such as anxiety and depression—two prominent non-motor symptoms of PD—we also investigated how modulation of ZI GABAergic neurons affects anxiety and depressive-like behaviors in 6-OHDA-treated mice.

Methods

Animals

The care and use of animals, as well as the experimental protocols employed in this study, were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Qingdao University. All experiments involved 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice of SPF grade, with a body weight averaging 22 ± 2 g, obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. Vgat (Vesicular GABA Transporter)-Cre mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories. The use of Vgat-Cre mice and specific viral vectors is a meticulously chosen experimental strategy to enable precise targeting, manipulation, and analysis of GABAergic neurons. Vgat is a membrane protein localized to synaptic vesicles in GABAergic neurons. Its sole function is to pump newly synthesized GABA from the neuronal cytoplasm into synaptic vesicles—an essential step for GABA to be released during synaptic transmission. Nearly all GABAergic neurons that are capable of releasing GABA express Vgat. Therefore, it is a pan-GABAergic marker that covers the entire GABAergic population, including those that are positive for GAD67. The Vgat-Cre mouse line allows Cre recombinase expression under the control of the Vgat promoter, enabling genetic access specifically to GABAergic neurons. This combined approach offers a precise, reproducible method to dissect GABAergic neurons. A maximum of four mice were housed per cage under controlled temperature and humidity conditions on a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with ad libitum food and water provided. Efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and to reduce the number of animals utilized in this research.

6-OHDA (Cat. No. abs44119665) was purchased from Absin. L-ascorbic acid (Cat. No. 95209) and desiparamine (Cat. No. D3900) were purchased from Sigma. Apomorphine (APO) (Cat. No. R828372) was purchased from Shanghai MacLean Biochemical Technology Co., LTD. Clozapine N-oxide dihydrochloride (CNO) (Cat. No. 6329) was purchased from Tocris. Rabbit anti-mouse tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) antibody (Cat. No. AB152) was purchased from Merck. Rabbit anti-mouse GABA antibody (Cat. No. A0310) was purchased from Sigma. Donkey anti-rabbit green fluorescence secondary antibody (Cat. No. A21206) and donkey anti-mouse green fluorescence secondary antibody (Cat. No. A21202) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. pAAV-GAD67-MCS-mCherry-3xFLAG-WPRE, pAAV-GAD67-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry-WPRE, pAAV-GAD67-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry-WPRE, pAAV-EF1a-DIO-EGFP-WPRE, pAAV-EF1a-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-EGFP-WPR, pAAV-hSyn-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry-WPRE, pAAV-hSyn-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry and pAAV-GAD67-EGFP-P2A-NLS-Cre-WPRE were purchased from Obio Technologies. rAAV-rGAD67-EGFP, rAAV-rGAD67-ChR2-EGFP and rAAV-rGAD67-ArChT-EGFP were purchased from BrainCase Co., Ltd. All other biochemical or biological regents were of the highest grade available from local commercial sources.

Experimental protocol and groups

The experiment was divided into four distinct parts.

First, we established a PD mouse model through unilateral striatal injection of 6-OHDA. The animals were categorized into a control group and a 6-OHDA group. Various behavioral tests were conducted to assess changes in movement and pain-like behaviors. Additionally, TH immunofluorescence was performed to elucidate the extent of injury to the nigrostriatal system.

Second, we aimed to investigate the effects of in situ chemogenetic activation or inhibition of GABAergic neurons in the ZI on pain-like behavior in normal naïve mice. For chemogenetic activation experiments, animals were divided into four groups: mCherry + physiological saline (PS), mCherry + CNO, hM3D + PS, and hM3D + CNO. For chemogenetic inhibition experiments, they were classified into mCherry + PS, mCherry + CNO, hM4D + PS, and hM4D + CNO. Virus was injected into the bilateral ZI. 21 days post-viral infection, CNO was administered; 30 min later, a series of behavioral tests were conducted.

Third, we examined the influence of both chemogenetic activation/inhibition and optogenetic activation/inhibition of ZI GABAergic neurons on pain-like behavior as well as anxiety and depression-like behaviors within the 6-OHDA PD mouse model. For chemogenetic experiments, 6-OHDA-lesioned PD mice were assigned to six groups: mCherry + PS, mCherry + CNO, hM3D + PS, hM3D + CNO, hM4D + PS, and hM4D + CNO. 21 days after viral infection, CNO was administered and 30 min later, a series of behavioral tests were conducted. For optogenetic manipulation, 6-OHDA-lesioned PD mice were divided into the EGFP group, the ChR2 group, and the ArChT group. After 21 days of viral expression, corresponding laser stimulation was given in each behavior.

Fourth, we studied the influence of activation or inhibition of the terminals from ZI to VTA on pain-like behavior. The chemogenetic section is divided into: hM3D + PS group, hM3D + CNO group, hM4D + PS group, and hM4D + CNO group. The optogenetic section is divided into the EGFP group and the ChR2 group.

6‑OHDA lesion surgery

The 6-OHDA-induced PD model was established following a series of detailed procedures. Thirty minutes prior to the experiment, desipramine was administered via intraperitoneal injection at a dosage of 25 mg/kg body weight to protect noradrenergic neurons. The mice were subjected to deep anesthesia using isoflurane, with an induction rate of 3% and a maintenance rate of 1%. Throughout the surgical procedure, a heating pad was utilized to maintain the body temperature of the mice, and erythromycin ointment was applied to their eyes to prevent light-induced damage. Using the bregma as a reference point, the coordinates for targeting the striatum were determined as follows: AP: +0.4 mm; ML: ±1.8 mm; DV: -3.5 mm. A cranial drill was employed to create an opening in the skull overlying the target brain region, followed by an injection of 6-OHDA (2.5 µg/µl, 2 µl) or 0.9% PS into the left striatum at a rate of 6 nl/s. The syringe remained in place for an additional 10 min to facilitate diffusion of 6-OHDA and prevent backflow before being withdrawn slowly. Post-injection, mice were allowed two weeks for recovery during which their weights were recorded daily. Two weeks after administration of 6-OHDA, an APO-induced rotation test was conducted to assess whether successful establishment of the PD mouse model had occurred. The number of rotations was counted manually three minutes post-APO injection; only those mice exhibiting more than 40 rotations within ten minutes were selected for subsequent studies.

Virus injection

As mentioned above, the mice were subjected to deep anesthesia using isoflurane, with an induction rate of 3% and a maintenance rate of 1%. Their heads were then fixed to a stereotaxic frame (RWD, 68018). A glass syringe containing virus was inserted into the ZI (AP: -1.9 mm, ML: ±1.4 mm, DV: -4.2 mm) or VTA (AP: -3.4 mm, ML: ±0.5 mm, DV: -4.2 mm). The virus (100 nL) was injected into the ZI or VTA at a rate of 0.3 nL/s, and the syringe was left in place for 10 min after the injection to allow virus diffusion.

For chemogenetic manipulation of the ZI GABAergic neurons, pAAV-GAD67-MCS-mCherry-3xFLAG-WPRE (1.31 × 1013 vg/mL), pAAV-GAD67-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry-WPRE (5.02 × 1012 vg/mL) or pAAV-GAD67-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry-WPRE (5.10 × 1012 vg/mL) were injected into the ZI of C57BL/6 mice. pAAV-EF1a-DIO-EGFP-WPRE (1.97 × 1013 vg/mL), pAAV-EF1a -DIO-hM3D(Gq)-EGFP-WPRE (3.57 × 1012 vg/mL) were injected into the ZI of Vgat-cre mice. For chemogenetic manipulation of the ZIGABA-VTA pathway, pAAV-hSyn-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry-WPRE (1.33 × 1013 vg/mL) or pAAV-hSyn-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry (1.26 × 1013 vg/mL) was injected into the ZI. pAAV-GAD67-EGFP-P2A-NLS-Cre-WPRE (1.15 × 1013 vg/mL) was injected into the VTA.

For optogenetic manipulation of the ZI GABAergic neurons and ZIGABA-VTA pathway, rAAV-rGAD67-EGFP (2.00 × 1012 vg/mL), rAAV-rGAD67-ChR2-EGFP (5.02 × 1012 vg/mL) or rAAV-rGAD67-ArChT-EGFP (5.03 × 1012 vg/mL) was injected into the ZI.

Chemogenetics and optogenetics

Inject 100 nl of the chemogenetic virus into the ZI or VTA. Following a three-week period of chemogenetic virus expression, 1 mg/kg of CNO was administered intraperitoneally. 30 min later, a series of behavioral tests were conducted. To achieve selective manipulation of the ZI GABAergic pathway to the VTA, a chemogenetic virus encoding retrograde Cre recombinase (pAAV-GAD67-EGFP-P2A-NLS-Cre-WPRE) was injected into the VTA. This virus will be transported retrogradely along the axon to the neuronal cell bodies that project onto the VTA. Subsequently, a Cre-dependent chemogenetic virus, pAAV-hSyn-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry-WPRE or pAAV-hSyn-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry, was injected into the ZI. Following administration of CNO, ZI neurons projecting to the VTA can be specifically activated, thereby facilitating relatively pathway-specific manipulation.

For optogenetic stimulation of GABAergic neurons in the ZI, after three weeks of expressing either ChR2, ArChT, or EGFP viruses, optical fibers (200 μm O.D., 0.22 NA; Inper Ltd., Hangzhou, China) were unilaterally implanted at a depth of 200 μm above the viral injection coordinates in the ZI. To facilitate optogenetic manipulation of the ZI-VTA circuit, the virus was injected into the ZI while positioning optical fibers 200 μm above the VTA. All fibers were securely attached to the skull using bone screws and dental cement. Illumination of the VTA will selectively activate only those axon terminals originating from the ZI without affecting either the neuronal cell bodies within the VTA or other fibers projecting onto it. Mice were allowed to recover for five to seven days post-implantation and underwent habituation for fifteen minutes following connection to a laser source before behavioral tests were conducted. Lasers with wavelengths of 473 nm (blue) or 589 nm (yellow) were utilized and controlled via an intelligent optogenetic system (QAXK-LASER-R1, ThinkerTech, Nanjing, China), delivering blue illumination at a power output of 5 mW (473 nm; frequency: 20 Hz; pulse width: 5 ms) and yellow illumination at a continuous mode power output also set at 5 mW (589 nm). After the completion of all behavioral tests, immunochemical methods were utilized to confirm both the sites of virus injection and the placements of cannulas. Animals that were located outside designated areas were excluded from statistical analysis.

Behavioral tests

APO-induced rotational behavior

Two weeks following the administration of 6-OHDA, rotational behavior was induced through an intraperitoneal injection of APO (0.5 mg/kg) to evaluate the efficacy of the PD model preparation in mice. After receiving the intraperitoneal injection of APO, the mice were placed in a cylindrical bucket with a diameter of 20 cm and a height of 30 cm. A camera was positioned above the bucket to record the number of rotations. If the number of rotations exceeded 40 within 10 min, the model was considered successfully established. Mice that exhibited fewer than 40 rotations in this time frame were excluded.

Fiber photometry

Optical fiber-based Ca2+ recordings of GABAergic neurons in the ZI were performed using a fiber photometry instrument (ThinkerTech, China). rAAV-GAD67-GCaMP6m was stereotaxically injected into the ZI, and an optical fiber (OD 200 μm, NA 0.37, length 4.3 mm; Inper) was implanted above ZI. Mice were then allowed to recover for 3 weeks before behavioral testing. On the day of the experiment, mice were acclimated for 30 min in the behavioral test room, and basal fluorescence was recorded for 5 min after acclimation. Next, mice were stimulated with von Frey filament (0.6 g). We calculated deltaF/F = (F-F0) / F0, where F0 is the mean of the Ca2+ signal for 5 s before each stimulation and F is the Ca2+ signal for the entire session. The normalized deltaF/F values and traces were visualized using custom-written MATLAB codes (MathWorks, USA). Mice without the correct viral transduction or the correct fiber-optic site were excluded from the analysis.

Von Frey test for mechanical sensitivity

The mechanical withdraw threshold of mice was measured with von Frey filaments. Prior to testing, each mouse was placed individually on a raised mesh grid covered by a clear plastic box and allowed to acclimate for 1 h. Mechanical allodynia was assessed using a series of calibrated von Frey hair filaments, including 0.008, 0.02, 0.04, 0.07, 0.16, 0.4, 0.6, 1, 1.4, and 2 g, according to previous study [40]. Stimulation begins with a 1 g filament applied perpendicularly to the middle plantar surface of each hind paw until the filament bent into an S shape. A response defined as hind paw withdraw, shaking, or licking was considered as positive. After a positive response the next weaker filament was applied, whereas after a negative response the next stronger filament was used. Testing continued until five stimuli were assessed following the first crossing of the withdrawal threshold, or the upper/lower end of von Frey filament was reached before a positive/negative response was obtained. The paw withdrawal threshold (PWT) was determined as the stimuli that elicited a 50% of the positive response, calculated using the previously described up-down algorithm [40].

Cold and hot plate tests for thermal sensitivity

Thermal nociceptive latency was assessed using a cold and hot plate pain detector (SA705, Jiangsu Saionsi Biotechnology Co., Ltd). For the cold test, the plate temperature was maintained at 4 ± 0.2 °C for more than 30 min before testing. For the heat test, the plate temperature was stabilized at 50 ± 0.2 °C. Each mouse was placed individually onto the surface of the cold or hot plate, and the latency to a nociceptive response, such as paw flinching, shaking, or licking was recorded. The test was terminated by observation of a pain-like responses or after 30 s to prevent tissue damage. Trials without a response recorded as 30 s. Paw withdrawal latency (PWL) reflects the cold and hot pain sensitivity.

Open field test

The locomotor activity and anxiety-like behaviors of mice were assessed using the open field test. The mice were positioned in the center of a 40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm open box, with a camera mounted directly above to capture their movements. These movement trajectories were analyzed utilizing the SMART Video Tracking System V3.0. The total duration of the experiment was 11 min, comprising a 1-minute adaptation period followed by a 10-minute recording phase. After each mouse’s test was completed, the area within the frame was wiped with 75% alcohol to reduce the impact of odors on the behavior of subsequent mice. The total distance and movement speed of the mice reflect changes in their motor ability. The number of times the mice entered the central area and the percentage of time they spent there reflect their anxiety level.

Rotarod test

The rotarod can be used to evaluate the balance, grip and motor coordination of rodents. The mice were trained three per day over two consecutive days separately, at 11 rpm per minute on the first day and 22 rpm per minute on the second day. Each training session lasted 300 s, with an interval of at least one hour to prevent fatigue. On the test day, the rod was programmed to accelerate from 4 rpm to 40 rpm over a duration of 300 s. The time taken and speed at which each mouse fell from the rotating rod were recorded, and an average value derived from three trials was employed for statistical analysis.

Pole test

The pole test can be used as an indicator to measure the motor function of experimental animals. Place the mouse head up on the top of a rod with a vertical rough surface (diameter: 8 mm; height: 50 cm), and record the duration taken for head turning. The mice were trained three times a day for two consecutive days. On the day of experimentation, the average duration from these three trials was utilized for statistical analysis.

Elevated plus maze

The elevated plus maze test is often used to evaluate whether mice exhibit anxiety-like behaviors. The cross-shaped maze, 50 cm above the ground, was designated as the observation area. The frame consisted of two mutually perpendicular closed arms and one open arm. A 10-cm-high fence surrounded the closed arms, while the open arm had no fence. Mice had an unobstructed view. Their movement trajectories were recorded using the behavior analysis SMART Video Tracking System V3.0 for a total of 6 min, including a 2-minute adaptation period and a 4-minute recording period. After the test, the internal environment of the cross frame was cleaned and wiped with 75% alcohol to minimize the impact of odors on subsequent mice. The percentage of entries into the open arm and the percentage of time spent in the open arm were analyzed.

Tail suspension test

The tail suspension test was employed to observe whether mice exhibited depressive-like behaviors. At the outset of the test, the mouse’s tail was suspended 30 cm above the ground. The mouse was positioned inverted, with the tail pointing upwards and the head downwards. A camera was positioned directly in front of the mouse. The percentage of immobile time within a 6-minute period was recorded and calculated using behavioral analysis software (SMART Video Tracking System V3.0). Immobility lasting more than 2 s was documented as immobile time. This immobile state in mice is referred to as a “behavioral despair state,” analogous to depressive symptoms, and is utilized to assess depressive-like symptoms in mice.

Forced swimming test

The forced swimming test was employed to observe whether mice exhibited depressive-like behaviors. At the outset of the test, the mice were placed in a transparent container 30 cm high, filled with water at a temperature of 25 ± 1 °C, and a camera was positioned to directly observe the mice. The percentage of immobile time for the mice over a 6-minute period was recorded and determined using behavior analysis software (SMART Video Tracking System V3.0). Recording of immobile time commenced when the mice remained motionless for more than 2 s. This immobile state in mice is referred to as a “behavioral despair.” The depressive symptoms in the mice were assessed based on the percentage of immobility time within the 6-minute period.

Immunofluorescence

After completing all behavioral tests, the mice were deeply anesthetized and subjected to transcardial perfusion with 0.9% NaCl followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. The brain was meticulously extracted and subsequently placed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for a minimum of six hours. Gradient dehydration was then conducted using 20% and 30% sucrose (dissolved in 0.1 M phosphate buffered saline) until the brain sank. The tissues were sectioned into coronal slices measuring 20 μm thick using a freezing microtome (Leica, CM1900) and collected for subsequent immunofluorescence staining. The section was incubated with PBST (0.3% Triton X-100 in phosphate buffered saline) blocking buffer containing 5% donkey serum (Jackson, Cat No: 017-000-001) for one hour at room temperature. Thus, the section was incubated overnight at 4 °C with appropriate primary antibodies: Rabbit anti-mouse TH antibody (1:2000 dilution) and Rabbit anti-mouse GABA antibody (1:300 dilution). Subsequently, the section was treated with fluorescent secondary antibodies at room temperature for two hours. Imaging was performed using a digital pathological section scanning system (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan; VS120).

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± SEM and were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2. Unpaired t-tests were employed for comparisons between two groups, while two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post hoc analyses were utilized for comparisons among multiple groups. A probability value of P < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Unilateral injection of 6-OHDA induces nigrostriatal degeneration, mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity in mice

To establish a PD mouse model, we performed stereotactic injection of 6-OHDA into the left striatum of mice (Fig. 1A), with the experimental procedure outlined in Fig. 1B. Two weeks post-surgery, rotational behavior was induced by an intraperitoneal injection of APO. The 6-OHDA group exhibited a significant increase in rotational behavior compared to the control group (Fig. 1C, t = 12.10, df = 16, P < 0.0001). To further elucidate the injury to the nigrostriatal system, we examined TH immunopositive terminal fibers in the striatum and TH immunopositive cells in the SNc. As illustrated in Fig. 1D and E, there was a marked reduction in striatal TH optical density in mice treated with 6-OHDA compared to control mice, confirming that the injection had achieved its intended effect (t = 14.90, df = 16, P < 0.0001). Additionally, we observed a loss of approximately 43% of TH-positive cells within the SNc (Fig. 1F and G, t = 4.457, df = 16, P < 0.0001). The open field test was employed to assess motor ability; results indicated that total distance traveled by mice in the 6-OHDA group was significantly less than that of controls (Fig. 1H and I, t = 6.635, df = 16, P < 0.0001). The rotarod test and pole test indicated that mice treated with 6-OHDA demonstrated a significant reduction in the time spent on the rotating rod (Fig. 1J, t = 8.801, df = 16, P < 0.0001) and exhibited prolonged durations for head turning (Fig, 1 K, t = 4.844, df = 16, P = 0.0002). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that unilateral striatal injection of 6-OHDA induces damage to the nigrostriatal system along with motor dysfunction.

We then investigate pain hypersensitivity under our experimental conditions in PD mouse model. Mice subjected to lesions from 6-OHDA displayed pronounced mechanical hypersensitivity in the contralesional (right) hind paws, as evidenced by decreased PWT following mechanical stimulation (Fig. 1L and M, t = 6.854, df = 13, P < 0.0001). Initially, we assessed the whether activating of ZI GABAergic neurons could modulate nociceptive responses to mechanical, hot, and cold stimuli. At least 30 min following intraperitoneal administration of CNO, behavioral data indicated that compared to the mCherry + CNO and hM3D + PS groups, the PWT for mice in the hM3D + CNO group was significantly elevated after stimulation with von Frey filaments (Fig. 2D, F(1, 36) = 13.20, mCherry + CNO vs. hM3D + CNO, P = 0.0003; hM3D + PS vs. hM3D + CNO, P = 0.0006). In the hot plate test, the PWL of the hM3D + CNO group was significantly prolonged when compared to both mCherry + CNO and hM3D + PS groups (Fig. 2E, F(1, 36) = 9.133, mCherry + CNO vs. hM3D + CNO, P = 0.0004, hM3D + PS vs. hM3D + CNO, P = 0.0002). Additionally, another form of thermal nociception evaluated through a cold plate test also demonstrated that activation of ZI GABAergic neurons via the hM3D virus resulted in an extended PWL among naïve mice, indicating reduced thermal nociceptive sensitivity (Fig. 2F, F(1, 36) = 13.92, mCherry + CNO vs. hM3D + CNO, P < 0.0001, hM3D + PS vs. hM3D + CNO, P = 0.0012).

We further investigated nociceptive behaviors under conditions of chemogenetic inhibition. In the von Frey test, PWT for mechanical stimulation on right hind paw was significantly reduced in mice from the hM4D + CNO group relative to those from hM4D + PS groups (Fig. 2G, F(1, 36) = 1.191, hM4D + PS vs. hM4D + CNO, P = 0.0409). Similarly, during hot and cold plate tests, PWL for subjects within the hM4D + CNO group exposed to thermal stimuli were markedly diminished when contrasted with results from both mCherry + CNO and hM4D + PS cohorts (Fig. 2H, F(1, 36) = 14.15, mCherry + CNO vs. hM4D + CNO, P = 0.0121, hM4D + PS vs. hM4D + CNO, P < 0.0001; Fig. 2I, F(1, 36) = 9.498, mCherry + CNO vs. hM4D + CNO, P = 0.0003, hM4D + PS vs. hM4D + CNO, P = 0.0016).

Collectively, these findings indicate that the chemogenetic activation of GABAergic neurons in the ZI suppresses the perception of mechanical and thermal stimuli, thereby producing a significant antinociceptive effect. Conversely, the inhibition of these neurons leads to hypersensitivity to both mechanical and thermal pain.

Chemogenetic or optogenetic activation/inhibition of ZI GABAergic neurons has no significant effect on anxiety-like behaviors in 6-OHDA-induced PD mice

The latest research has demonstrated that activating ZI GABAergic neurons can alleviate anxiety and depressive-like behaviors in mice subjected to chronic restraint stress models [41]. Given that both anxiety and depression are also common non-motor symptoms of PD, forming a vicious cycle with pain symptoms, we subsequently employed various behavioral tests to investigate whether ZI GABAergic neurons influence anxiety and depressive-like behaviors in 6-OHDA-induced PD mice.

Three weeks following the 6-OHDA injection, anxiety-like behaviors were assessed using the open field and elevated plus maze tests, while depressive-like behaviors were evaluated through the tail suspension and forced swimming tests. Compared to the control group mice, both the frequency of entries into and the percentage of time spent in the center area by 6-OHDA-treated mice were significantly reduced (Fig. S1A-C. The VTA is recognized for its regulatory role in pain behavior, stress response, anxiety, and addiction. Electrical stimulation of this nucleus following a nociceptive stimulus has been shown to facilitate analgesia [42]. Previous studies utilizing techniques based on retrograde and anterograde transport of horseradish peroxidase have also highlighted connections between the ZI and VTA [33]. Consequently, we hypothesized that modulation of pain-like behavior sensitivity responses in PD mice could be achieved through activation of the ZIGABA-VTA circuit. The schematic representation of viral injection procedures is depicted in Fig. 6D. Figure 6E and F present representative images of the virus injection site. In the Von Frey test, the PWT of the right hind paws in mice from the hM3D + CNO group was significantly higher compared to that in the hM3D + PS group (Fig. 6G, t = 3.820, df = 18, P = 0.0013). Similarly, in both hot and cold plate tests, the PWL of mice in the hM3D + CNO group exhibited a significant increase following exposure to heat (Fig. 6H, t = 4.835, df = 18, P = 0.0001) and cold stimulation (Fig. 6I, t = 4.031, df = 18, P = 0.0008). These findings indicate that chemogenetic activation of the ZIGABA-VTA circuit alleviates mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity induced by 6-OHDA.

Additionally, we investigated the effects of chemogenetic inhibition of the ZIGABA-VTA circuit. Following CNO administration in 6-OHDA mice expressing hM4D, both PWT and PWL were significantly reduced, suggesting an exacerbation of pain sensitivity responses within this PD model (Fig. 6J-L, J: t = 6.006, df = 13, P < 0.0001; K: t = 2.354, df = 13, P = 0.0350; L: t = 5.541, df = 13, P < 0.0001).

In contrast, the EGFP control group exhibited no significant differences between light-on and light-off states (Fig. 7D, F(1, 50) = 61.08, EGFP light-off vs. EGFP light-on, P = 0.9964). Discussion

Animal models have been instrumental in enhancing our understanding and treatment of nociceptive hypersensitivity responses in PD. The 6-OHDA model has been extensively investigated in relation to pain-like behavior modeling in PD. A range of studies have reported hypersensitivity to thermal, mechanical, chemical, and cold stimuli [39]. The nociceptive changes have been observed as early as 1-week post lesion and persist for at least 12 weeks, suggesting stable alterations [43, 44]. These findings support the face validity of the model. The observed reduction in pain threshold following lesioning is complex. There is substantial evidence indicating that the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons plays a significant role, particularly given the restoration of nociceptive thresholds after L-DOPA treatment [45, 46]. Additionally, non-dopaminergic systems such as serotonergic and GABAergic pathways are also implicated in nociceptive hypersensitivity. For instances, 6-OHDA-lesioned rats exhibiting nociceptive hypersensitivity demonstrated a reduced number of 5-HT neurons in both the rostral ventromedial medulla and the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, suggesting diminished 5-HT transmission within these pain-related regions [47]. Furthermore, levels of GABA were significantly decreased in the PAG of PD rats; both mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia were alleviated following stimulation of GABAA receptors in the PAG, highlighting the role of GABAergic transmission in PD-related pain sensitivity [48]. Our current study focuses on the ZI, which is another critical GABAergic inhibitory region involved in pain transmission and processing.

In this study, we assessed the role of ZI GABAergic neurons in nociception regulation in both naïve mice and 6-OHDA-lesioned PD mouse model. As an integrated subthalamic region involved in pain transmission, ZI GABAergic signaling has been previously implicated in neuropathic pain models. Rats subjected to hemisection spinal cord injury demonstrated reduced hind paw withdrawal thresholds, latencies, and diminished activity of ZI GABAergic neurons compared to sham-operated controls [28]. Similarly, rats with chronic constriction injury of the sciatic nerve exhibited hypersensitivity to pain, as evidenced by decreased hind paw withdrawal thresholds and latencies, alongside reduced levels of GABA and decreased neuronal activity within the ZI when compared to sham-treated rats [29]. Utilizing chemogenetic manipulation, we first investigated the effects of alterations in GABAergic neural activity on endogenous pain perception in naïve mice. Our findings revealed that activation of ZI GABAergic neurons alleviated responses to mechanical and thermal pain stimuli, whereas inhibition of these neurons exacerbated such sensations. These results align with those reported by Farzinpour et al., who found that chemogenetic inhibition of ZI GABAergic neurons induced pain and anxiety-like behaviors in naïve mice [49]. Do ZI GABAergic neurons regulate pain symptoms in the PD model? To validate this hypothesis, we applied chemogenetic and optogenetic technique and provided the first evidence that activation of ZI GABAergic neurons alleviated the pain hypersensitivity. To our knowledge, this is the first basic research study investigating the role of the ZI in pain processing within PD mouse models. Currently, there are only two clinical reports that have focused on establishing correlations between the ZI and pain perception in patients with PD. Lu et al. were the first to evaluate the effects of DBS of the ZI on heat perception (45 °C) in twelve PD patients, finding that stimulation at a physiological frequency of 20 Hz, rather than higher frequencies, significantly reduced heat pain among these individuals [30]. Subsequently, these authors assessed Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale Part I (UPDRS-I) pain scores and UPDRS-III motor scores preoperatively and again six to twelve months after subthalamic nucleus (STN) DBS in 74 patients with idiopathic PD. They discovered that more dorsal stimulation within the STN region, which may be associated with stimulation near the ventral ZI, could improve pain scores independently of age or gender factors [31]. Our study strengthens the connection between clinical observations and fundamental mechanisms by identifying GABAergic neurons in the ZI as a critical substrate for pain processing in PD. While translational gaps remain, these findings provide a robust foundation for refining clinical strategies aimed at targeting this circuit, potentially enhancing pain management for populations affected by PD.

Distinct circuits and neuronal subtypes within the ZI are implicated in the decoding of pain symptoms. Activation of a circuit originating from CaMKIIα-positive neurons in the posterior paraventricular thalamic nucleus (PVP) to GABAergic neurons in the rostral ZI (PVPCaMKIIα-ZIGABA) facilitates the development of pain hypersensitivity behaviors in a mouse model of spared nerve injury [50]. Conversely, activation of glutamatergic neurons located in area 2 of the midcingulate cortex projecting to rostral ZI GABAergic neurons (Midcingulate cortex, MCC Cg2Glu-ZIGABA) alleviates neuropathic pain [51]. Projections from GABAergic neurons in rostral ZI to the thalamic paraventricular nucleus (PVT) have been shown to play a role in nociceptive neurotransmission. Chemogenetic and optogenetic activation of the ZIGABA-PVT pathway mitigates pain responses in spared nerve injury models, while inhibition of this circuit induces mechanical hypersensitivity and partial heat hyperalgesia [52]. Parvalbumin-positive neuronal projections from ZI to the posterior complex of the thalamus (Po) (ZIPV-Po), along with glutamatergic neurons within caudal ZI, are also critical for modulating nociceptive behaviors [53, 54]. In this study, we aimed to explore the loop mechanism underlying PD-related pain regulation by employing neural tracer virus techniques to observe downstream projections from ZI GABAergic neurons. Consistent with findings reported by Yang et al. [55], multiple brain regions, including the VTA, were identified as targets. For the first time, through activation of the ZIGABA-VTA circuit, we elucidated its role in mediating PD-related pain perception. Activation of this circuit resulted in reduced mechanical and thermal pain hypersensitivity observed in 6-OHDA-lesioned mice; conversely, inhibition led to exacerbation of pain symptoms. However, it remains unclear which specific cell types within VTA are directly innervated by ZI GABAergic neurons. Notably, both dopaminergic and GABAergic neuron populations within VTA have been implicated in regulating pain responses [35, 36, 56–58]. For instance, inflammatory pain has been shown to reduce the activity of dopaminergic neurons in the VTA [34]. Conversely, optogenetic activation of these neurons resulted in a significant anti-allodynic effect in mice with nerve injuries [36]. Additionally, both VTA GABAergic neuronal activity and extracellular GABA levels were found to be elevated in models of chronic neuropathic pain induced by spinal cord injury [35]. Consequently, future research could investigate the anatomical and functional innervation of these two types of neurons within the VTA by GABAergic neurons originating from the ZI. Furthermore, it would be valuable to explore how the ZIGABA-VTADA or ZIGABA-VTAGABA circuits influence pain perception under PD conditions.

In the present study, we also observed anxiety and depressive-like behaviors in mice injured by 6-OHDA. Both of these non-motor symptoms are frequently encountered in patients with PD. Clinically, approximately 20% to 30% of PD patients exhibit depressive symptom, while 20% to 52% experience anxiety symptom [59]. Most patients report experiencing both symptoms concurrently. Independent research groups have noted mood alterations in PD patients undergoing DBS targeting the ZI; however, these findings remain inconsistent. The earliest case involved a 36-year-old female patient with drug-resistant PD who also suffered from refractory depression; ZI DBS therapy resulted in acute depressive anxiety [60]. Another case involved a 63-year-old female patient with idiopathic PD (without any prior history of mental illness), where DBS targeting the ZI similarly induced an acute transient depressive state [61]. Conversely, another clinical study reported that eleven PD patients experienced reduced subjective levels of anxiety and depression following DBS treatment in the ZI or adjacent areas [62]. The chemogenetic results obtained from our current investigation indicated that neither chemogenetic nor optogenetic activation or inhibition of GABAergic neurons within the ZI significantly affected anxiety-like behaviors. Interestingly, regarding depressive-like behaviors, we unexpectedly found that optogenetic activation of ZI GABAergic neurons and the ZIGABA-VTA circuit led to a reduction in immobility time during the tail suspension test. Given that the ZI is a highly heterogeneous structure comprising diverse cell types, future research should explore how different subtypes of neurons within this region regulate depressive-like behaviors in animal models of PD.

In the present study, we focused exclusively on the relationship between ZI GABAergic signaling and pain hypersensitivity in PD mouse models. Numerous clinical reports, along with our previous research, have demonstrated the role of ZI in movement therapy for PD [25, 63]. DBS targeted the ZI region alleviates motor symptoms, especially tremor, and improves activities of daily living and quality of life in PD patients [64–66]. Furthermore, we previously reported that both chemogenetic and optogenetic activation of ZI GABAergic neurons improved the motor performance of 6-OHDA-lesioned mice [26]. Therefore, it is highly likely that the ZI plays a dual regulatory role in both motor behavior and pain response associated with PD.

Limitation

The chemogenetic strategies employed in the present study involved DREADDs viruses to activate or inhibit GABAergic neurons in the ZI. While DREADDs have gained widespread acceptance in neuroscience research, their safety profile remains inadequately understood. A previous study conducted by Goossens MG et al. reported that high expression levels of the hM4D(Gi) DREADD led to pronounced neuromodulatory effects; however, it also resulted in neuronal loss and tissue damage within the hippocampus [67]. This significant finding offers valuable insights and serves as a crucial reminder for our future applications involving such viral vectors. In this investigation, we utilized a dosage and titer of the hM4D virus consistent with those documented in previously published, well-validated literature targeting this specific brain region [52, 68, 69]. Both results from these referenced studies and our own experimental data did not indicate any evidence of neuronal damage or toxicity associated with the administered viral dosage within the ZI. In our forthcoming experiments, we will further explore and optimize both the dosage of hM4D virus and other similar tools to minimize potential toxic effects while ensuring reliability and efficacy in our research outcomes.

In the present study, 6-OHDA was administered unilaterally into the striatum to establish a hemi-PD mouse model. While this approach mitigated the high mortality rate associated with bilateral full lesion models, it does not fully replicate human PD pathology. Typically, PD in patients involves bilateral but asymmetric degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the SNc, with motor symptoms often initiating unilaterally and progressively affecting both sides over time. However, unilateral lesion models primarily induce damage on one side, failing to capture the bilateral progression and extensive neuropathology observed in human PD. This limitation restricts the translational relevance of findings derived from such models, particularly concerning non-motor symptoms and cognitive deficits that arise from bilateral or network-level dysfunction. Future research will aim to address this limitation by developing a bilateral 6-OHDA lesion model utilizing a lower dose of 6-OHDA. It is anticipated that this enhanced model will more accurately reflect the bilateral pathological manifestations characteristic of human PD.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates a close relationship between ZI GABAergic neurons and pain-like behavior in the 6-OHDA-lesioned PD mouse model. We found that the activation of these neurons alleviated both mechanical and thermal pain hypersensitivity, while their inhibition exacerbated the pain response. Furthermore, the ZIGABA-VTA projection plays a significant role in regulating PD-associated pain. Therefore, activating the GABAergic signaling circuit may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for addressing pain hypersensitivity in PD.
