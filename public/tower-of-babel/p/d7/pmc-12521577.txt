Removal of ciprofloxacin from water by graphene oxide-like carbon nanosheets derived from onion peel
Authors: Masoumeh Ghahremani, Hossein Danafar, Hamed Bahrami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The widespread presence of ciprofloxacin (CIP) in aquatic environments poses significant risks to public health and ecosystems due to its role in promoting antibiotic resistance. Addressing this challenge, we developed graphene oxide-like carbon nanosheets (CNSs) from onion peel waste as a low-cost and sustainable adsorbent for efficient CIP removal from water, where the cost savings originate from free raw materials and an energy-efficient synthesis, thereby valorizing waste and reducing pollutants as key sustainability indicators. The CNSs, synthesized via pyrolysis and ultrasonic exfoliation, exhibited abundant oxygen-containing functional groups and a negatively charged surface (zeta potential: -20.9 mV), with TEM confirming nanosheet structure. Under optimized conditions (pH 7, 0.4 g L-1 adsorbent dosage), the CNSs achieved 90% CIP removal (qmax= 51.28 mg g-1), with adsorption following pseudo-second-order kinetics and the Freundlich isotherm. Reusability tests showed 50% efficiency retention after five cycles. Thermodynamic analysis confirmed the process was spontaneous and exothermic (ΔG° = -7.19 to -6.38 kJ mol-1, ΔH° = -15.26 kJ mol-1). Density functional theory calculations revealed hydrogen bonding between CIP’s amine groups and CNS hydroxyls as the primary adsorption mechanism, with strong binding energies (-87.0 kJ mol-1). This work highlights the potential of agricultural waste-derived nanomaterials as eco-friendly and high-performance solutions for pharmaceutical wastewater remediation, offering a dual benefit of waste valorization and environmental protection.

Introduction

Antibiotics are extensively used worldwide, and their unchecked release into the environment has emerged as an urgent worldwide issue. Studies estimate that 30-90% of antibiotics administered to humans and livestock are incompletely metabolized, leaving active residues1. These compounds commonly find their way into waterways via sources such as pharmaceutical manufacturing discharge, improper disposal of unused drugs, household sewage, and treated wastewater effluents. Despite occurring at low concentrations, the persistent presence of antibiotics in aquatic environments heightens risks to ecosystem stability and public health, primarily by driving antimicrobial resistance (AMR)2.

Fluoroquinolones rank among the most widely used antibiotic classes globally3,4. Ciprofloxacin (CIP), a leading fluoroquinolone, garnered significant focus owing to its large-scale production and prevalence in clinical applications for bacterial infections5. Environmental monitoring studies have detected CIP concentrations typically ranging from tens of µg L−1 in surface waters6,7 to hundreds of µg L−1 in hospital wastewater8,9. CIP’s environmental persistence fuels the spread of antibiotic-resistant bacteria and disrupts aquatic ecosystems10. Given these dual risks and its widespread prevalence, CIP was selected as a model pharmaceutical pollutant for this study, addressing the urgent need to mitigate its public health and ecological impacts.

Various methods have been investigated for antibiotic removal, including biological treatments11, advanced oxidation processes12, ozonation13, photocatalysis14, and membrane filtration15. However, these approaches often face challenges such as prohibitively high operational costs, significant energy demands, the generation of harmful byproducts, and limited efficacy against recalcitrant compounds16. In contrast, adsorption has gained prominence as a preferred method due to its operational simplicity, cost-efficiency, and versatility across diverse aqueous systems16. Researchers have explored adsorbents such as activated carbon17, synthetic polymers18,19, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)20, and ion-exchange resins21,22. Despite their effectiveness, commercial adsorbents like activated carbon, MOFs, and ion-exchange resins face costly synthesis protocols due to high energy consumption (e.g., activation at >800 °C) extensive chemical inputs (e.g., strong acids or bases for functionalization), and their low stability in water or other aqueous media which limit scalability23–25. In contrast, biomass-derived adsorbents, such as those from agricultural byproducts, utilize low-cost precursors and simpler pyrolysis methods, reducing overall expenses and enhancing sustainability26,27.

Consequently, research efforts have pivoted toward low-cost, sustainable adsorbents derived from agricultural byproducts, such as date palm fiber28, sycamore flocs29, and corn cob-derived graphene oxide30. These materials exhibit promising CIP adsorption capacities, with the sycamore floc-based carbonaceous adsorbent demonstrating a very high adsorption capacity, though others like corn cob-derived graphene oxide show a comparatively much lower value29,30. However, their practical application is often constrained by energy-demanding processing or the need for chemical activation, undermining their environmental and economic sustainability.

In parallel, nanomaterials-particularly carbon-based nanostructures-have garnered attention due to their high surface area, chemical robustness, and tailorable surface chemistry. Two-dimensional (2D) carbon materials Like graphene and graphene oxide demonstrate exceptional efficacy in water purification applications. Despite these advantages, synthesizing 2D carbon materials directly from biomass remains technically and economically challenging, limiting their scalability31. Recently, Thangaraj et al. demonstrated the successful synthesis of reduced graphene oxide (rGO) from onion peel waste via single-stage pyrolysis, highlighting its significant potential as a precursor for creating two-dimensional carbon nanomaterials and its effective antibacterial properties32.

This study introduces an eco-friendly method to synthesize GO-like CNSs from onion sheath waste (Allium cepa). These bio-based nanosheets were applied as efficient adsorbents for removing CIP from contaminated water. We systematically evaluated their adsorption performance under varying conditions, including pH, initial CIP concentration, contact time, and adsorbent dosage. Kinetic and isotherm models were analyzed to characterize the adsorption process, complemented by computational DFT studies to uncover the interaction mechanisms. To our knowledge, this work is the first to demonstrate the use of onion-derived CNS for CIP removal, bridging experimental and computational insights to advance sustainable water treatment solutions.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

The onion peel was collected from an onion farm in East Azerbaijan, Iran. CIP was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Hydrochloric acid (HCl) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were purchased from Merck (Germany). All experimental studies were conducted using distilled water.

Preparation of bio-based CNSs

GO-like CNSs were synthesized through pyrolysis of onion peel waste (Allium cepa), following a modified protocol based on Venkateswarlu et al.33. The as-prepared CNSs were subsequently reduced to nanoscale dimensions via ultrasonic exfoliation, as described in Danafar et al.’s work31. A schematic overview of the complete preparation process is provided in Fig 1. Briefly, collected onion peels were visually inspected to remove coarse debris, thoroughly rinsed with distilled water, and air-dried at room temperature until visually dry. No chemical pre-treatment or activation (such as acid/base washing or solvent extraction) was applied prior to pyrolysis32. The dried peels were directly pyrolyzed at 700 °C for 2 h under a continuous nitrogen (N2) atmosphere to produce bulk CNSs. The resulting material was ground into a fine powder, washed sequentially with ethanol and deionized water to remove impurities, and dried to obtain purified GO-like CNSs.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the synthesis process for GO-like CNSs from onion peel waste, including pyrolysis and ultrasonic exfoliation.

Schematic illustration of the synthesis process for GO-like CNSs from onion peel waste, including pyrolysis and ultrasonic exfoliation.

For ultrasonic exfoliation, a 30 mL aqueous dispersion of GO-like CNSs (6 mg mL−1) was sonicated for 0, 5, 15, and 60 minutes under controlled cooling conditions (20–25 °C). The process utilized a 500 W ultrasonic processor (Sonics & Materials Inc.) equipped with a ½-inch titanium probe operating at 20 kHz and 40% amplitude. This treatment yielded ultrathin, nanoscale CNS flakes with enhanced surface area and colloidal stability, critical for adsorption applications.

Characterization of bio-based CNSs

The morphology and dimensions of the GO-like CNSs were analyzed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, FEI model, 120 kV) and Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, PHILIPS EM208s, 100 kV). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were acquired using a Philips PW 1730 diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å, 40 kV, 30 mA) over a 2θ range of 10°–80°. Surface charge characteristics were determined via zeta potential measurements (ZEN 3600 analyzer, Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Bruker Alpha) was employed to identify surface functional groups, with spectra recorded in the range of 4000–400 cm−1. Optical absorption properties were evaluated using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madison, WI) across wavelengths of 200–800 nm.

Determination of pHPZC of GO-like CNSs

The point of zero charge (pHPZC) of the GO-like CNSs was determined using the pH drift method2. First, a 0.01 M NaCl solution was prepared, and its pH was adjusted to values of 2, 3, 5, 7, 9, and 11 using 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH. Then, 0.1 g of the CNS sample was added to 50 mL of each pH-adjusted solution. The mixtures were agitated at 120 rpm for 24 h at 25 °C to reach equilibrium. After 24 h, the final pH of each solution was measured. The difference between the initial and final pH (ΔpH = pHinitial - pHfinal) was calculated and plotted against the initial pH. The point where the curve intersected the line ΔpH = 0 (i.e., where no change in pH occurred) was identified as the pHPZC of the adsorbent. For the synthesized CNSs, this point was determined to be pH 3.8.

Adsorption studies

A stock CIP solution (100 mg L−1) was prepared by dissolving 0.01 g of CIP in 100 mL of distilled water. Working solutions of varying concentrations were prepared via serial dilution of the stock solution. All experiments were conducted at 25 °C using 10 mL test tubes. The pH of the solutions was adjusted using 0.1 M HCl or NaOH.

To evaluate pH effects on CIP removal, a 10 mg L−1 CIP solution was prepared, and its pH was adjusted to 2–11 using 0.1 M HCl or NaOH. A predetermined mass of CNS adsorbent was added to each pH-adjusted solution, and the mixtures were agitated at 120 rpm for 1 hour using a magnetic stirrer.

The influence of contact time (10, 20, 60, and 120 minutes) was investigated by adding a fixed CNS dose of 0.4 g L−1 to 10 mL of 10 mg L−1 CIP solution. After agitation at constant speed for the designated time, samples were centrifuged. The supernatant was filtered, and residual CIP concentrations were quantified. These data were used to model adsorption kinetics, including pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order mechanisms.

Adsorbent dosages ranging from 0.001 to 0.010 g (0.1–1.0 g L−1 in 10 mL) were tested to determine the optimal amount for CIP removal. All trials were conducted at pH 7.0 with a fixed contact time of 60 minutes to isolate dosage effects.

To assess the impact of initial CIP concentration on adsorption, experiments were conducted at pH 7.0 with CIP concentrations ranging from 10 to 30 mg L−1 and a fixed contact time of 24 h. The resulting data were analyzed using Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Dubinin-Radushkevich isotherm models to characterize equilibrium behavior.

Adsorption-desorption and reusability study

CNSs at a dosage of 0.4 g L−1 were equilibrated with a 10 mg L−1 CIP solution at pH 7 in a temperature-controlled shaker at 25 °C for 60 minutes. After adsorption, the CIP-loaded CNSs were separated by centrifugation (4000 rpm for10 minutes). Desorption was performed using 10 mL of 0.1 M NaOH for 30 minutes at 120 rpm. The regenerated CNSs were washed with deionized water, dried at 60 °C for 12 h, and reused for up to five cycles. The desorption solution was filtered and analyzed via UV-Vis spectrophotometry at 275 nm to quantify desorbed CIP.

Computational methodology

Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were executed using the ORCA33 quantum chemistry package, with molecular visualizations generated in Avogadro34.

To ensure a comprehensive and reliable description of the adsorption interactions between CIP and GO, two distinct DFT approaches were employed, namely B3LYP/6-31G(d) and PBE-D3(BJ)/def2-TZVP. The B3LYP hybrid functional with the 6-31G(d) basis set was chosen for its widespread use in modeling organic and hydrogen-bonded systems, providing a reliable baseline for electronic structure and interaction energies35. However, since non-covalent interactions (such as particularly dispersion forces) play a critical role in adsorption processes, the PBE-D3(BJ) functional36 with the larger def2-TZVP basis set was also utilized. This method explicitly includes Grimme’s D3 dispersion correction with Becke-Johnson damping, which significantly improves the description of van der Waals interactions and binding energies in supramolecular systems36. The comparative use of these two methods allows for a balanced assessment of both electrostatic/hydrogen-bonding contributions and dispersion-driven stabilization, offering a more complete mechanistic insight into the adsorption process. Geometry optimizations utilized tight self-consistent field (SCF) convergence thresholds. All binding energy calculations incorporated counterpoise correction for basis set superposition error (BSSE).

The GO nanosheet was constructed following Jafari et al.’s finite-cluster approach35, featuring a 96-carbon hexagonal lattice with hydrogen-terminated edges. The basal plane contained four hydroxyl (-OH) groups and one epoxy (-O-) group. Initial optimizations of isolated GO and CIP were performed at both theory levels using an implicit aqueous solvent model. Subsequent frequency analyses verified true energy minima (zero imaginary frequencies) for all optimized structures.

Ten distinct CIP-GO complexes were generated by systematically orienting CIP near potential interaction sites (hydroxyl/epoxy groups) on the GO surface. Following full optimization of each complex, the most stable configurations were identified based on interaction energy magnitude and structural integrity for detailed analysis.

After sonication, the nanoscale CNSs retained the primary peak at 2θ ≈ 23°, confirming the preservation of the layered graphitic structure despite size reduction. The persistence of the (002) and (100) peaks indicates that the ultrasonic exfoliation process effectively breaks down the bulk material into nanoflakes without completely destroying the crystalline order31. The well-defined peaks suggest a well-ordered, stacked graphitic arrangement, which is essential for providing adsorption sites and maintaining structural integrity during the adsorption process. The similarity in the XRD patterns before and after sonication confirms that the chemical structure and phase composition remain largely unchanged, while the physical size reduction enhances the surface area and accessibility of active sites for adsorption.

FTIR analysis

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy identified surface functional groups on the GO-like CNS and CIP, while also elucidating key interactions governing adsorption. Fig 4 compares the FTIR spectra of (a) pristine CIP, (b) pure GO-like CNS, and (c) CIP-laden GO-like CNS post-adsorption.Fig. 4FT-IR spectra of (a) CIP, (b) pure GO-like CNS, and (c) CIP-laden GO-like CNS.

FT-IR spectra of (a) CIP, (b) pure GO-like CNS, and (c) CIP-laden GO-like CNS.

The FTIR spectrum of CIP (Fig 4a) clearly reveals the characteristic vibrational bands corresponding to its functional groups. A prominent broad peak observed at 3528.75 cm−1 is attributed to the stretching vibration of O-H groups, indicating the presence of hydroxyl functionalities40. N-H stretching (secondary amine) is observed at ~3300 cm−1 (overlapped with O-H)41. The strong band at 1708.73 cm−1 is attributed to the C=O stretching vibration of the carboxylic acid moiety42. Another distinct peak at 1624.69 cm−1 is assigned to quinolones43. Bands observed at 1494.69 cm−1 and 1383.36 cm−1 are related to C-H bending and aromatic ring vibrations. The distinct peak at 1272.75 cm−1 is characteristic of C-F stretching vibration, and the absorption peaks between 1050 and 1000 cm−1 was also assigned to the C-F stretching vibration40,43. These vibrational assignments collectively confirm the presence of key functional groups such as carboxyl, amine, and quinolone rings in the CIP structure41.

The FTIR spectrum of the as-prepared GO-like CNSs (Fig 4b) shows a broad peak centered at 3436.49 cm−1, attributed to O-H stretching vibrations from hydroxyl groups44. Peaks at 2922.40 cm−1 and 2893.48 cm−1 are assigned to aliphatic C-H stretching vibrations45. A strong peak at 1735.83 cm−1 corresponds to C=O stretching, suggesting carboxylic or carbonyl functionalities46. The band at 1437.88 cm−1 may relate to OH vibrations in carboxylic acids32,44. The peak at 875.86 cm−1 is assigned to stretching vibration of aromatic C-H bond32. These observations collectively confirm the presence of abundant oxygen-containing functional groups on the surface of the two-dimensional carbon material, suggesting its resemblance to oxygen-rich GO rather than pristine graphene.

The spectrum of the CIP-laden GO-like CNS (Fig 4c) displays a combination of features from both the CNSs and CIP, with noticeable shifts in certain bands, indicating intermolecular interactions. The broad O-H stretching band appears at 3419.36 cm−1, slightly shifted and more intense than in the CNS spectrum, implying strong hydrogen bonding between the hydroxyl groups of the adsorbent and CIP47. The C=O stretching vibration appears at 1723.91 cm−1, slightly shifted compared to the free drug, suggesting non-covalent interactions48. Peaks at 1621.88, 1452.56, 1383.94, and 1252.74 cm−1 are consistent with the CIP structure but exhibit minor shifts or intensity changes, confirming the presence of adsorbed CIP. As an overall conclusion, the observed spectral shifts and retention of characteristic peaks without the formation of new bands strongly suggest that the adsorption of CIP onto GO-like CNSs occurs primarily through physical interactions, including hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking, rather than covalent bonding or chemisorption.

Surface charge analysis

Zeta potential measurements provided essential insights into the surface charge and colloidal stability of GO-like CNSs. The nanosheets exhibited a zeta potential of −20.9 mV (Fig 5a), confirming a strongly negative surface charge attributable to deprotonated oxygen-containing groups (e.g., carboxyl and hydroxyl) in aqueous media. This negative charge promotes moderate colloidal stability by electrostatic repulsion, effectively mitigating nanosheet agglomeration.Fig. 5Zeta potential of (a) GO-like CNSs, (b) CIP-laden GO-like CNSs.

Zeta potential of (a) GO-like CNSs, (b) CIP-laden GO-like CNSs.

Upon CIP adsorption at pH 7, the zeta potential shifted to −9.47 mV (Fig 5b), reflecting a significant reduction in surface negativity. This shift aligns with CIP’s zwitterionic state at neutral pH, where its positively charged amine groups interact electrostatically with the negatively charged CNS surface. Concurrent non-electrostatic mechanisms - such as π–π stacking between CIP’s aromatic rings and the CNS carbon lattice, and hydrogen bonding with oxygen moieties - likely contributed to charge neutralization. These changes in surface properties confirm successful CIP adsorption and composite formation, while highlighting the role of multifactorial interactions in the adsorption process.

Effect of pH

The adsorption of CIP onto GO-like CNSs is profoundly influenced by the solution pH, as it dictates the ionization state of the antibiotic and the surface charge density of the adsorbent. Experiments conducted across a pH range of 2–11 under fixed conditions (initial CIP concentration: 10 mg L−1, adsorbent dosage: 0.4 g L−1, contact time: 60 minutes, temperature: 298 K). The removal efficiency demonstrated a strong dependence on pH, increasing from approximately 84% at pH 2 to a maximum of 90% at pH 7, followed by a gradual decline to around 85% at pH 11 (Fig 6a).Fig. 6(a) Effect of pH on CIP adsorption onto GO-like CNSs (inset: plot of ΔpH versus initial pH for the determination of pHPZC). (b) Effect of adsorbent dosage on CIP adsorption.

(a) Effect of pH on CIP adsorption onto GO-like CNSs (inset: plot of ΔpH versus initial pH for the determination of pHPZC). (b) Effect of adsorbent dosage on CIP adsorption.

To elucidate this behavior, the pHPZC of the GO-like CNSs was determined experimentally. As shown in the inset of Fig 6a, the plot of surface charge versus pH yields a pHPZC of 3.8. This value is consistent with the pHPZC range typically reported for graphene oxide-based materials49, confirming the acidic nature of our nanosheets due to the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., carboxyl, hydroxyl). Consequently, the CNS surface is positively charged at pH values below 3.8 and negatively charged at pH values above 3.8.

CIP is an amphoteric molecule with two protonation constants: pKa1 ≈ 6.1 (associated with the carboxylic group) and pKa2 ≈ 8.7 (associated with the piperazinyl amine group)2,50. Therefore, its ionic speciation varies with pH. At pH values below 6.1, CIP exists predominantly as a cation (CIP⁺). In the pH range between 6.1 and 8.7, CIP exists as a zwitterion (CIP±), possessing both a positively charged protonated amine group and a negatively charged deprotonated carboxylic group. At pH values greater than 8.7, CIP exists primarily as an anion (CIP⁻).

The interplay between the surface charge of the CNS and the ionic speciation of CIP provides a clear rationale for the observed adsorption trend. Under strongly acidic conditions (e.g., pH 2–3), the CNS surface is positively charged (pH < pHPZC), while CIP is cationic (CIP⁺). The resultant electrostatic repulsion between Like charges constitutes the primary barrier to adsorption, yielding a lower removal efficiency of 84%. The moderate removal observed in this region is thus primarily attributed to non-electrostatic interactions - such as hydrogen bonding between CIP’s functional groups and CNS oxygen moieties, as well as π-π stacking between aromatic systems - which can partially overcome the electrostatic repulsion.

As the pH approaches neutrality (pH 7), a shift occurs: CIP adopts its zwitterionic form (CIP±), while deprotonation of the CNS surface enhances its negative charge. This creates an ideal scenario for strong initial electrostatic attraction between the negatively charged CNS surface and the positively charged protonated amine group on the CIP± molecule. This favorable electrostatic interaction facilitates the close approach of the CIP molecule to the adsorbent surface. Once in close proximity, the primary adsorption mechanism is dominated by multifaceted non-covalent interactions, as conclusively confirmed by our DFT calculations. These interactions include specific hydrogen bonding - between the hydrogen atoms of the protonated amine group in CIP and the oxygen atoms of hydroxyl groups on the CNS surface (as evidenced in Complex 1, with a bond distance of 1.643 Å), as well as between the carbonyl groups (C=O) of CIP and the hydroxyl groups of the CNS - coupled with π-π stacking between the aromatic rings of CIP and the sp2-hybridized carbon lattice of the CNSs. The initial electrostatic attraction thus acts as a crucial driving force to bring the molecules into proximity, while the hydrogen bonding and π-π interactions, revealed by DFT to have high binding energies (up to −87.0 kJ mol−1), are responsible for the strong and specific adsorption stabilization at the molecular level. This synergistic combination of long-range electrostatic guidance and short-range specific interactions leads to the maximum adsorption efficiency of 90% at pH 7 and is a common feature in the adsorption of zwitterionic pharmaceuticals onto charged carbon nanomaterials49.

Under alkaline conditions (pH > 8.7), both the adsorbent and adsorbate undergo further changes. The CNS surface becomes increasingly negatively charged due to further deprotonation, while CIP transitions to its anionic form (CIP-). The resulting electrostatic repulsion between the like-charged CIP- and the negatively charged surface reduces the adsorption capacity, as evidenced by the decreased removal efficiency of 85% at pH 11. In this pH regime, the repulsive electrostatic force counteracts the contributory non-electrostatic interactions.

Furthermore, the findings of this study are consistent with previous reports in the literature. For instance, Theresa Umeh et al.51 and Babu Vamsi et al.52 carried out similar studies and reported that the maximum removal efficiency of CIP was achieved at pH 7. These variations highlight that the optimum adsorption pH is inherently dependent on the adsorbent type and its specific surface functionalization, particularly its point of zero charge. Achieving maximum adsorption at pH 7 in this study offers a significant practical advantage for wastewater treatment processes. Maintaining a neutral pH operation not only facilitates the effective removal of dissolved antibiotic contaminants but is also critical for protecting the microbial communities essential to subsequent biological treatment stages53.

Effect of adsorbent dose

Adsorbent dosage plays a pivotal role in adsorption efficiency, directly influencing the material’s capacity to sequester antibiotics at specific concentrations. To evaluate this relationship, CIP adsorption onto GO-like CNSs was tested at an initial antibiotic concentration of 10 mg L−1. Doses ranging from 0.1 to 1.0 g L−1 were assessed under optimized conditions (pH 7.0, 25 °C, 60 minutes contact time), with results detailed in Fig 6b.

The removal efficiency of CIP increases sharply as the adsorbent dosage rises from 0.1 to 0.4 g L−1. At the lowest dosage (0.1 g L−1), only about 35% of CIP is removed, but increasing the dosage to 0.4 g L−1 elevates efficiency to approximately 90%, marking the peak performance. This improvement stems from the expanded surface area and greater availability of active sites for CIP adsorption as more adsorbent is introduced.

Beyond 0.4 g L−1, further increasing the dosage to 1.0 g L−1 has minimal impact, with efficiency stabilizing near 90%. This plateau suggests that 0.4 g L−1 of adsorbent effectively captures nearly all available CIP molecules at the tested concentration (10 mg L−1), with the adsorbent surface approaching saturation. While additional adsorbent provides extra active sites, the Limited residual pollutant in the solution prevents significant further removal. Consequently, 0.4 g L−1 was identified as the optimal dosage for subsequent experiments.

Effect of contact time

The adsorption of CIP onto GO-like CNSs was evaluated over a period of 10–120 minutes to assess the role of contact time. Experiments were conducted under fixed conditions: initial CIP concentration = 10 mg L−1, adsorbent dosage = 0.4 g L−1, pH 7.0, and temperature = 25°C. The results, detailed in Fig 7a, reveal rapid adsorption kinetics during the initial phase, with removal efficiency reaching approximately 90% within 60 minutes.Fig. 7Effect of (a) contact time and (b) initial CIP Concentration on the value of CIP adsorbed on GO-like CNSs.

Effect of (a) contact time and (b) initial CIP Concentration on the value of CIP adsorbed on GO-like CNSs.

This sharp increase in efficiency stems from the ample accessible active sites and high mass transfer rates at the process onset. Beyond 60 minutes, the adsorption rate plateaued as equilibrium was attained, indicating near-saturation of active sites on the nanosheet surface. The limited availability of unoccupied sites constrained further CIP uptake. The observed kinetics align closely with the pseudo-second-order model, reflecting contributions from chemisorption mechanisms such as π-π stacking and hydrogen bonding between CIP and the adsorbent’s functional groups. Based on these findings, a contact time of 60 minutes was identified as optimal for maximizing CIP removal.

Effect of initial CIP concentration

The influence of initial CIP concentration on adsorption efficiency was investigated using GO-like CNSs at pH 7, an adsorbent dosage of 0.4 g L−1, a contact time of 24 hours, and at 25°C. Solutions with CIP concentrations ranging from 10 to 30 mg L−1 were tested, with results illustrated in Fig7b.

As shown in Fig 7b, removal efficiency decreased from 90% to 65% as the initial CIP concentration increased from 10 to 30 mg L−1. Thus, 10 mg L−1 was identified as the optimal initial CIP concentration for achieving maximum removal efficiency (90%) at the fixed adsorbent dosage of 0.4 L−1 g. However, for applications targeting higher overall adsorption capacity (e.g., industrial wastewater with elevated CIP levels), higher initial concentrations may be more suitable, though with reduced percentage removal stems from the finite surface area and active sites of the adsorbent, which become insufficient to accommodate higher pollutant loads. At lower concentrations (10 mg L−1), the abundant active sites relative to CIP molecules facilitate efficient adsorption, achieving 90% removal. However, at elevated concentrations, the fixed number of active sites saturates, leaving a significant fraction of CIP unadsorbed and lowering efficiency to 65%. This saturation-limited behavior aligns with the Freundlich isotherm model, suggesting multilayer adsorption dominated by hydrogen bonding and heterogeneous surface interactions.

Conclusion

This study establishes onion peel-derived GO-like CNSs as effective adsorbents for CIP removal from aqueous solutions. Synthesized via pyrolysis and ultrasonic exfoliation, the CNSs achieve a maximum adsorption capacity of 51.28 mg g−1 at pH 7, driven by electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, and π-π stacking, with TEM and XRD confirming the nanosheet structure.

Kinetic and isotherm analyses revealed adsorption follows pseudo-second-order kinetics and Freundlich behavior, indicating multilayer adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces. Thermodynamically, the process was spontaneous and exothermic, with negative ΔG° (−7.19 to −6.38 kJ mol−1) and ΔH° (−15.26 kJ mol−1) values. Reusability tests demonstrate 50% efficiency retention after five cycles. DFT simulations corroborated hydrogen bonding as the dominant mechanism, particularly between CIP’s amine groups and CNS hydroxyls, with PBE-D3 calculations showing strong binding energies (−87.0 kJ mol−1). These findings highlight onion-derived CNSs as eco-friendly adsorbents, providing the dual benefit of valorizing agricultural waste (onion peels) into valuable nanomaterials while protecting the environment through efficient CIP removal. Practically, the method’s scalability is promising due to abundant onion peel feedstock, a simple one-step pyrolysis process at 700 °C, and lower energy requirements compared to conventional Hummers’ method or other commercial graphene oxide production routes. Pilot-scale trials could integrate waste heat recovery to enhance sustainability, though challenges like uniform exfoliation at scale require further optimization.
