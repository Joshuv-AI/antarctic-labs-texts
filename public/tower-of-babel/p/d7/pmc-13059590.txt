Latent profiles of caregiver self-care contributions for patients with COPD: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Bin Ma, Jingjing Wang, Mengyuan Zhang, Yue Hou, Ou Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo evaluate the current status and latent profiles of caregiver self-care contributions for patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and examine the associations between demographic characteristics, health literacy, confidence in self-care contributions, family intimacy, and profile membership.MethodsWe recruited 275 dyads of patients with COPD and their family caregivers from five tertiary hospitals between May and November 2022 using convenience sampling. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was used to identify distinct profiles of caregiver self-care contributions. Univariate analysis and multinomial logistic regression were subsequently conducted to examine associations between participant characteristics and profile membership.ResultsLPA identified four distinct profiles of caregiver self-care contributions: low-contributing, under-monitored, maintenance-prioritized, and high-contributing. Significant differences were observed across these profiles in terms of patients’ symptom severity, exacerbation frequency, number of hospitalizations, caregivers’ education levels, caregiving duration, health literacy, confidence in self-management contributions, and family intimacy using univariate analysis. Multinomial logistic regression analysis revealed that caregivers’ education levels, caregiving duration, confidence in self-management contributions, and health literacy were significant predictors of profile membership.ConclusionCaregiver self-care contributions for patients with COPD can be characterized by four distinct profiles, with caregivers’ educational level, health literacy, and confidence in self-management identified as key factors associated with profile membership.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04503-4.

Background

Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) is a heterogeneous respiratory condition characterized by progressive airflow limitation, often accompanied by chronic respiratory symptoms such as dyspnea, cough, and sputum production, which result from persistent airway and/or alveolar abnormalities [1]. Global projections indicate a 23% increase in COPD cases among individuals aged 25 and older from 2020 to 2050, with an estimated 600 million individuals expected to be affected by the disease by 2050 [2]. In addition to respiratory complications, COPD is linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular events, such as myocardial infarction and stroke [3]. The disease not only significantly reduces patients’ quality of life but also remains a leading cause of mortality, with a 35.4% increase in COPD-related death rates observed between 2009 and 2019 [4]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), COPD is projected to become the third leading cause of death globally by 2030 [5]. The growing burden of COPD imposes substantial direct and indirect costs on healthcare systems and societies, making it a major public health concern.

Due to the limited availability of healthcare resources and the increasing prevalence of COPD, healthcare systems are increasingly advocating for patient self-care as a crucial component of disease management. Self-care is defined as the process of maintaining health through health promotion and disease management, and it includes three key components: self-care maintenance, self-care monitoring, and self-care management [6]. Previous studies have shown that effective self-care behaviors in stable COPD patients can alleviate disease-related clinical symptoms, slow disease progression, improve health-related quality of life, and reduce respiratory-related hospitalizations and mortality rates [7]. However, despite the numerous benefits of self-care, COPD patients often engage in inadequate self-care behaviors. This deficiency can be attributed to factors such as disease symptoms, disease progression, and limited health knowledge.

In China, the combination of limited healthcare resources and traditional cultural attitudes has made home care the predominant model for managing chronic diseases [8]. Family caregivers play a crucial role in facilitating patients’ self-care behaviors. Their active involvement in self-care or in encouraging adherence to care routines is referred to as “caregiver contributions to self-care.” These contributions are multifaceted and include three key domains: self-care maintenance, self-care monitoring, and self-care management [9]. Notably, when patients are elderly or in advanced stages of disease, the responsibilities of family caregivers extend beyond daily living assistance to include disease symptom surveillance, medication management, emotional support, and emergency response—all of which are critical to patient self-care [10]. Recent scholarship has increasingly recognized the indispensable role of family caregivers in patient self-care and has emphasized the significance of their contributions to effective illness management [11]. However, due to the heterogeneity of research methodologies both domestically and internationally, coupled with the complex nature of caregiver self-care contributions, identifying the latent profiles of these contributions is essential. Such identification can improve patient care outcomes, refine support strategies for family caregivers, and provide a strong scientific foundation for nursing practices. Despite this acknowledged importance, limited research has specifically focused on the self-care contributions of family caregivers for patients with COPD, leaving the latent profiles of these contributions unclear. Consequently, this study aims to delineate the latent profiles of caregiver self-care contributions through latent profile analysis (LPA) and to explore the factors that influence these profiles. The findings are expected to provide empirical evidence that supports the effective and appropriate engagement of family caregivers in the self-care of COPD patients, thereby enhancing patients’ quality of life and offering practical guidance for family caregiving practices.

Study design and methods

Participants and setting

This cross-sectional study recruited patients with COPD and their family caregivers from five tertiary hospitals between May and November 2022 using convenience sampling. Participants were identified through cooperating hospitals during the study period. Potentially eligible patients and their primary family caregivers were initially screened by trained research staff according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligibility was further confirmed through medical record review and face-to-face verification to ensure that all participants met the study requirements. Only patients and caregivers who met all inclusion criteria and provided written informed consent were included in the final sample.

A total of 300 questionnaires were distributed, and 275 valid responses were recovered, yielding an effective response rate of 91.7%. All recovered questionnaires contained complete information on the study variables. As no formal sample size calculation formula exists for latent profile analysis, sample adequacy was evaluated based on model fit indices and prior methodological evidence. Previous studies suggest that sample sizes of approximately 200 or more are generally sufficient for stable latent profile solutions [12, 13]. Therefore, the final sample size of 275 was considered adequate for the analyses conducted in this study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria of patients

(1) Age ≥ 40 years; (2) Clinical diagnosis in accordance with the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (Revised 2021); (3) Duration of the disease ≥ 6 months; (4) Clear mind, normal thinking, and able to answer questions.

Exclusion criteria of patients

(1) those who participated in other interventional studies; (2) those whose lung function was graded as grade IV after inhalation of bronchodilators; (3) those who combined with acute infections, sequelae of serious cerebrovascular diseases, malignant tumors and other serious physical diseases or mental illnesses; and (4) those who requested to withdraw in the course of the survey.

Inclusion criteria of caregivers

(1) Caregivers were family members designated by patients to provide primary care and did not receive remuneration; (2) Age ≥ 18 years; (3) Accumulated caregiving duration ≥ 3 months; (4) Clear mental state, normal thinking, and able to answer questions.

Exclusion criteria for caregivers

(1) those who participated in other interventional studies; (2) those who combined with acute heart failure, sequelae of severe cerebrovascular disease, malignant tumors and other serious physical diseases or mental illnesses; (3) those who asked to withdraw in the middle of the survey.

Survey tools

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

A self-designed questionnaire was used to collect both general and disease-specific information from patients, including age, sex, education level, employment status, daily activity capacity, disease duration, number of COPD-related hospitalizations in the past year, and comorbidities. Daily activity capacity was categorized as “in good condition” or “damaged” based on the Barthel Index (BI) [14]. Patients who were fully independent in all activities of daily living were classified as “in good condition,” whereas those who required assistance in one or more activities were classified as “damaged”. For caregivers, the questionnaire gathered general information such as age, gender, education, residence, employment status, household income and expenditures, as well as caregiving-related details including caregiving duration, average daily caregiving hours, co-residence with the patient, and the presence of additional caregiving support (Supplementary File 1).

Caregiver contribution to self-care chronic illness inventory (CC-SCCII)

The Caregiver Contribution to Self-Care Chronic Illness Inventory (CC-SCCII), developed by Vellone et al. in 2018 [15], was used to assess caregiver contributions to patient self-care. This scale consists of three dimensions: self-care maintenance, self-care monitoring, and self-care management, with a total of 19 items. The self-care maintenance and monitoring dimensions are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “never” to “always” (1–5), while the self-care management dimension includes items rated on both 5-point and 7-point Likert scales. Standardized scores for each dimension range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better self-care contributions. A score of ≥ 70 on a dimension indicates adequate contributions. The Cronbach’s alpha for the scale in this study was 0.828. For the analysis, standardized scores (rather than raw scores) were used, ensuring comparability across dimensions, particularly in the latent profile analysis.

Mutuality scale (MS)

The Mutuality Scale (MS), developed by Archbold et al. in 1990, was used to assess the quality of the relationship between caregivers and care recipients. The scale comprises 15 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from “not at all” (0) to “very much” (4). The total score is calculated as the mean of all item scores, with higher scores indicating better relationship quality [16]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.923, demonstrating excellent internal consistency.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease health literacy scale

This scale, developed by Maples et al., assesses the level of knowledge related to COPD [17]. It consists of 13 items covering risk factors and treatments for COPD, with each item having three response options: yes, no, and not sure. Correct answers are scored 1 point, and incorrect or uncertain answers are scored 0 points. The total score ranges from 0 to 13, with higher scores indicating better COPD-related health literacy.

Caregiver confidence in self-management of disease scale

This scale was developed by De Maria, M. based on the self-efficacy scale for self-care in chronic illness, to assess caregiver confidence in their contributions to self-care in chronic illness. The scale comprises 10 items across two dimensions: caregiver confidence in self-care maintenance and monitoring, and caregiver confidence in self-care management. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from “not confident” to “very confident” (1–5). Each dimension is standardized on a scale from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater caregiver confidence in the patients’ self-care contributions [18]. The Cronbach’s alpha for this scale in this study was 0.890.

COPD assessment test

This scale, developed by Jones et al., is recommended by the GOLD guidelines for assessing the severity of symptoms of COPD patients [19]. It includes 8 items assessing cough, sputum, chest tightness, mobility, ability to perform daily activities, confidence in going out, sleep, and energy. Each item is scored on a 0–5 scale, with the total score ranging from 0 to 40, where higher scores indicate a more severe condition. The score ranges are as follows: 1–10 (mild), 11–20 (moderate), 21–30 (severe), and 31–40 (very severe). The Cronbach’s alpha for this scale in this study was 0.813.

Ethical review

This study strictly followed the principles of voluntariness, harmlessness, fairness and confidentiality, and informed consent was obtained from the research subjects. The questionnaire data were used only for scientific research, and the specific information of the survey participants was strictly enforced as confidential. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Nursing and Rehabilitation of Shandong University (2021-R-127) before the investigation.

Statistical analysis

This study used R version 4.4.2 and Mplus version 8.3 for data analysis, with statistical significance determined by a two-sided test (P < 0.05). The analytical approach consisted of the following steps: First, descriptive analysis was performed. Continuous variables were assessed for normality. Normally distributed variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range: Q1, Q3). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (%).

Subsequently, LPA was used to identify distinct subgroups of family caregivers based on their patterns of self-care contributions across the domains of self-care maintenance, monitoring, and management. The goal of this analysis was to capture heterogeneity in caregiver contribution patterns and generate empirically derived profiles that aid in interpretation and support subsequent profile-specific analyses. In the LPA, the number of model profiles was gradually increased, starting with a single-profile model, and the best-fitting model was selected based on comparison of model fit indicators. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (aBIC), with lower values indicating better fit. Classification accuracy was assessed using entropy, which ranges from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 reflecting greater classification precision. Model comparisons were further conducted using the Lo–Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR) and the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), where a P value < 0.05 indicated that the model provided a significantly better fit than the model with one fewer profile. Each identified latent profile was required to include at least 5% of the total sample to avoid overextraction of classes [13, 20]. Univariate analysis was then conducted to examine differences in self-care contributions profiles across patients’ and caregivers’ characteristics. Finally, multinomial logistic regression analysis was used to explore factors influencing the latent profiles of caregiver self-care contributions for COPD patients.

Results

Characteristics of COPD patients and caregivers

A total of 275 patients with COPD and their family caregivers were included in this study. Among the patients, 69.5% (n = 191) were male, 30.5% (n = 84) were female, 86.5% (n = 238) were married, 45.5% (n = 125) had elementary school education or less, and 84.4% (n = 232) were retired or unemployed. The majority of patients (70.5%) had co-morbidities, and more than half (51.6%) had impaired ability to perform daily activities. The caregiver group consisted of 58.2% (n = 160) females, 21.8% (n = 60) with elementary school education or less. The majority of caregivers (72%) lived in towns or cities, 53.8% (n = 148) were still working, 62.5% (n = 172) lived with the patient, and 63.3% (n = 174) received assistance in caregiving, as shown in Table 1.

Potential profiles of caregiver self-care contributions to COPD patients

Models with one to six latent profiles were estimated and compared. As shown in Table 2, the AIC, BIC, and aBIC values decreased progressively as the number of profiles increased. Compared to the three-profile solution, the four-profile model demonstrated significantly improved model fit, as supported by both the Lo–Mendell–Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR, P = 0.0029) and the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT, P < 0.001), with acceptable classification accuracy (entropy = 0.832). Although the five- and six-profile models showed slightly lower AIC and aBIC values, the BLRT results were no longer significant, and both models produced very small class proportions (< 5% of the sample), indicating potential overextraction. Considering model fit indices, statistical tests, profile proportions, and interpretability, the four-profile solution (Model 4) was selected as the optimal model. Based on profile characteristics, the four latent profiles of caregiver self-care contributions were labeled as follows: Profile 1 (low-contributing subtype), Profile 2 (under-monitored subtype), Profile 3 (maintenance-prioritized subtype), and Profile 4 (high-contributing subtype). Caregivers in Profile 1 exhibited consistently low levels of contributions across all three self-care dimensions—maintenance, monitoring, and management. Profile 2 was characterized by pronounced deficits in self-care monitoring, despite relatively better performance in self-care maintenance and management. Profile 3 demonstrated high levels of contributions in self-care maintenance but comparatively lower levels in monitoring. In contrast, Profile 4 exhibited consistently high levels of contributions across all three dimensions (Table 2) (Fig. 1).

Results of univariate analysis of caregiver self-care contributions in COPD patients

Using the four latent profiles identified in Model 4 as the grouping variable, one-way ANOVA and chi-square tests were conducted to compare differences in patients’ and caregivers’ demographic, clinical, and psychosocial characteristics across profiles. Continuous variables were compared using one-way ANOVA, while categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The results of the one-way ANOVA analysis showed significant differences in the patients’ place of residence (P = 0.0039), symptom severity (P < 0.001), number of exacerbations (P < 0.001), duration of illness (P = 0.0167), and number of hospitalizations (P < 0.001) across the four classes. Significant differences were also found across the four profiles in caregiving duration (P < 0.001), caregivers’ place of residence (P = 0.0053), presence of assisting caregivers (P = 0.0450), caregivers’ education levels (P = 0.0166), caregivers’ income and expenditure status (P = 0.0279), caregivers’ health literacy (P < 0.001), family intimacy (P = 0.048), caregivers’ self-care management contribution confidence (P < 0.001), and self-care maintenance and monitoring contribution confidence (P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Multinomial logistic regression analysis of patient and caregiver characteristics associated with latent profile membership in COPD

Using latent profile membership derived from Model 4 as the dependent variable, a multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed, with Profile 1 (low-contributing subtype) as the reference group. The analysis identified caregivers’ education levels, caregiving duration, confidence in self-management contributions, and health literacy as significant predictors of membership in Profile 3 (maintenance-prioritized subtype) and Profile 4 (high-contributing subtype). Specifically, compared with caregivers classified into Profile 1, those classified into Profile 3 were significantly more likely to have at least a high school education (OR = 10.2728, P = 0.0023), report longer caregiving duration (OR = 1.5388, P = 0.0012), demonstrate greater confidence in self-management contributions (OR = 1.3804, P = 0.0021), and exhibit higher health literacy (OR = 1.5805, P = 0.0010). Similarly, membership in Profile 4 was significantly associated with having at least a high school education (OR = 6.7001, P = 0.0122), longer caregiving duration (OR = 1.6701, P < 0.0001), higher confidence in self-management contributions (OR = 1.3223, P = 0.0073), and higher health literacy (OR = 1.6342, P = 0.0005) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study identified distinct latent profiles of caregiver self-care contributions and the factors influencing them through an analysis of 275 patients with COPD and their family caregivers. LPA supported a four-profile model as the best-fitting solution, including Profile 1 (low-contributing subtype), Profile 2 (under-monitored subtype), Profile 3 (maintenance-prioritized subtype), and Profile 4 (high-contributing subtype), highlighting substantial heterogeneity in caregiver self-care contributions. This finding is consistent with existing research and underscores the importance of considering individual differences in caregiving research [21]. Further analysis using univariate and multinomial logistic regression analysis revealed that patients’ and caregivers’ characteristics significantly influence caregiver self-care contributions. Specifically, caregivers’ education levels, health literacy, and self-management confidence emerged as key factors in enhancing caregiver self-care contributions. These findings underscore the necessity of implementing targeted, profile-specific interventions that align with distinct caregiver self-care contribution patterns in COPD care, thereby equipping caregivers to maximize their effectiveness in supporting patients.

Impact of patients’ characteristics on caregiver self-care contributions

The results of the univariate analysis showed that the differences in the symptom severity (P < 0.001), number of exacerbations (P < 0.001) and hospitalizations (P < 0.001), duration of the disease (P = 0.0167), and the patients’ place of residence (P = 0.0039) significantly influenced the caregiver contributions to self-care. However, after performing multinomial logistic regression analysis, no association was found between these variables and caregiver self-care contributions. This result suggests that symptom severity, number of exacerbations, number of hospitalizations, duration of illness, and place of residence may not be independent influences on caregiver self-care contributions, and their effects may be confounded by other confounding factors. Current research on the relationship between symptom severity, number of exacerbations, number of hospitalizations, duration of illness and patiens’ place of residence and caregiver self-care contributions also presents different results, for example, in the study addressing the relationship between the number of hospitalizations and caregiver self-care contributions. Bidwell et al. found that among patients with heart failure, higher hospitalization rates were associated with lower caregiver contributions in self-care maintenance [22], whereas a study by Wilson. et al. showed that the number of patient hospitalizations was not significantly associated with either caregiver self-care maintenance or self-care management contributions [23]. Contributing to this inconsistency may stem from differences in study samples, the degree of control for confounders, and patterns of interaction between patients and caregivers. Therefore, subsequent studies need to further control for confounding factors based on larger sample sizes and analyze in depth the specific mechanisms by which these factors influence caregiver self-care contributions. In addition, the results of this study further suggest that clinical staff should focus on patients who have experienced frequent deterioration of their conditions in the past year, and explore the possible causes and potential influences on the deterioration of their conditions, so as to guide family caregivers to assist patients in self-care in a more effective way.

Impact of caregivers’ characteristics on caregiver self-care contributions

Univariate analysis showed that caregiving duration was significantly associated with caregiver self-care contributions profiles among patients with COPD. In the multinomial logistic regression analysis, using Profile 1 (low-contributing subtype) as the reference group, caregiving duration significantly predicted membership in Profile 3 (maintenance-prioritized subtype) and Profile 4 (high-contributing subtype), whereas it did not significantly predict membership in Profile 2 (under-monitored subtype). This association may be attributed to the increased understanding of the patients’ condition that caregivers develop over time, enhanced communication between caregivers and patients regarding disease management and physical sensations, and improved disease-related knowledge and symptom management skills. Specifically, caregiver contributions to self-care monitoring and management increased with longer caregiving duration. However, existing research on the relationship between caregiving duration and caregiver self-care contributions presents inconsistent findings. Some studies have found no significant effect of cumulative caregiving years on caregiver contributions to self-care maintenance, monitoring, or management in patients with chronic heart failure [24]. These discrepancies may stem from differences in disease type and severity. Future research should further investigate the role of caregiving hours in the self-care contributions of COPD caregiver.

Univariate analysis showed that caregivers’ educational level and health literacy were significantly associated with caregiver self-care contributions profiles among patients with COPD. Multinomial logistic regression analysis further confirmed that higher educational level and greater health literacy significantly predicted membership in Profile 3 (maintenance-prioritized subtype) and Profile 4 (high-contributing subtype), but not in Profile 2 (under-monitored subtype), using Profile 1 (low-contributing subtype) as the reference group. Enhancing caregivers’ education and health literacy is crucial for improving their self-care contributions. Consequently, clinical healthcare professionals should prioritize the health education of COPD caregivers, focusing on disease prevention, treatment, and fundamental healthcare practices. Additionally, caregivers should be made more aware of the importance of medication adherence and vaccination in preventing COPD exacerbations. Specifically, healthcare providers can organize regular educational sessions for caregivers, covering COPD disease knowledge, medication importance, and vaccination necessity, to strengthen their disease management knowledge. Furthermore, online consultation services can be implemented to offer caregivers timely professional guidance, helping them address challenges encountered during the caregiving process. These interventions not only enhance caregiver self-care contributions but also promote better disease management and improved patient outcomes [25, 26].

Univariate analysis showed that confidence in self-care management was significantly associated with caregiver self-care contributions profiles among patients with COPD. After adjustment for potential confounders, multinomial logistic regression analysis further confirmed that greater confidence in self-care management significantly predicted membership in Profile 3 (maintenance-prioritized subtype) and Profile 4 (high-contributing subtype), but not in Profile 2 (under-monitored subtype), using Profile 1 (low-contributing subtype) as the reference group, consistent with previous findings, and studies in elderly trauma patients and Parkinson’s disease patients have also found that when caregivers have higher self-efficacy, they typically feel more capable of managing the needs of their patients, and that increased confidence typically leads to greater involvement in supporting patients’ self-care activities and providing emotional support, providing more effective rehabilitation or psychological support to patients, in reducing caregiving stress, alleviating adverse emotions such as anxiety and depression, and being able to better assist patients in self-care [27, 28]. Therefore, in clinical practice, enhancing the self-efficacy of caregivers of COPD patients can optimize caregiving behaviors and improve patient health outcomes while reducing the disease burden on families and society. This can be achieved through multidisciplinary collaborative care teams comprising physicians, nurses, dietitians, and psychologists. Interventions such as disease education, psychological support during hospitalization, and continuous post-discharge support (e.g., telephone follow-ups and online platforms) can be implemented [29]. Additionally, dichotomous psychological interventions like cognitive-behavioral therapy and tele-motivational interviewing can increase patient and caregiver motivation, thereby enhancing self-efficacy [29, 30].

Univariate analysis indicated that family intimacy was significantly associated with caregiver self-care contributions profiles among patients with COPD (P = 0.0498). However, in the multinomial logistic regression analysis, using Profile 1 (low-contributing subtype) as the reference group, family intimacy did not show a significant association with membership in any of the other profiles, including Profile 2 (under-monitored subtype), Profile 3 (maintenance-prioritized subtype), and Profile 4 (high-contributing subtype). According to dualistic disease management theory, caregivers who share a reciprocal relationship with the patient are more likely to view the patients’ illness as a shared problem and engage in health-promoting activities. Such caregivers may not perceive caregiving tasks as burdensome and are therefore more willing to contribute effectively to patient self-care [31]. Current research on family intimacy and caregiver self-care contributions has primarily focused on chronic heart failure patients, with studies showing that greater family intimacy is associated with higher levels of caregiver self-care contributions [32, 33]. However, research in the COPD population has been limited, with most studies being small-sample cross-sectional studies that have rarely explored the effect of family intimacy on caregiver self-care contributions. Future studies should continue to investigate the role of family intimacy in caregiver self-care contributions among COPD patients through large-scale and longitudinal follow-up studies.

Conclusion

The self-care contributions of family caregivers of COPD patients can be characterized by four profiles, and caregiver self-care contributions may be affected by caregivers’ education levels, health literacy, and caregivers’ self-care management confidence. The findings highlight the need for interventions targeting caregivers’ education, health literacy, and psychological support to optimize caregivers’ self-care contributions, thereby improving the quality of patient care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; therefore, the findings reflect associations between variables rather than definitive causal relationships. Future studies employing longitudinal designs or intervention approaches are warranted to clarify the temporal ordering and causal pathways among key variables. Second, although this study identified meaningful associations related to caregiver self-care contributions among patients with COPD, the underlying mechanisms and interactions among these factors were not fully explored. Further research is needed to investigate these mechanisms in greater depth to inform the development of targeted interventions. Third, while a range of patient and caregiver characteristics were examined, the possibility of unmeasured confounders cannot be excluded, which may have affected the precision of the findings. Future studies incorporating additional patient-level factors, such as cognitive function and psychological status, would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants of caregiver self-care contributions. Finally, although the sample size met commonly recommended thresholds for latent profile analysis and yielded acceptable model fit and classification accuracy, the relatively modest sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies with larger samples and validation across different populations and clinical settings are needed to further confirm the stability, robustness, and broader applicability of the identified latent profiles.
