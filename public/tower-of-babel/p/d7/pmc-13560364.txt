Association between body composition and bone mineral density in young and middle-aged adults
Authors: Qi Yanmeng, Zhou Xiaoyu, Wang Xiao, Shang Xuesong, Chen Anqi, Jiao Xitong, Han Xueyan, Li Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this cross-sectional study, we aimed to explore the correlation between body composition and bone mineral density (BMD) in young and middle-aged people with different gender and different BMI groups. We selected 1,295 young and middle-aged people (20 to 50 years old) who underwent physical examination at the Health Management Center of the International Medical Service (Xidan Branch) of Peking Union Medical College Hospital from January 2020 to June 2024 as the study subjects, and demographic characteristics, laboratory examinations, body composition(assessed by bioelectrical impedance analysis, including measurements of skeletal muscle mass, skeletal muscle index, body fat mass, percent body fat, fat-free mass, and visceral Fat Area), and dual-energy X-ray bone absorptiometry (DXA) results were collected. Based on gender and body mass index (BMI), the study participants were categorized into three groups: normal weight (18.5 kg/m² ≤ BMI < 24 kg/m²), overweight (24 kg/m² ≤ BMI < 28 kg/m²), and obese (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m²). One-way ANOVA was employed to assess differences among the three groups. The relationship between BMI and BMD was analyzed using multiple regression models along with natural cubic spline models. Additionally, multiple regression models were used to examine the association between body composition and BMD. There were 774 males (216 in the normal body composition group, 380 in the overweight group, and 178 in the obese group) and 521 females (386 in the normal body composition group, 99 in the overweight group, and 36 in the obese group), and there was a significant difference in the laboratory results, body composition, and BMD among the three groups of males and females (all P < 0.05). Multiple regression analysis, after adjusting for age, smoking and alcohol consumption history, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus, revealed a significant positive correlation between BMI and BMD at all skeletal sites (all P < 0.05). The obese group exhibited a more pronounced increase in BMD compared to the overweight group (lumbar spine BMD: β = 0.04, 95% CI 0.02–0.07; femoral neck BMD: β = 0.09, 95% CI 0.07–0.11; total hip BMD: β = 0.11, 95% CI 0.09–0.13; all P < 0.05). Further analysis of the nonlinear relationship between BMI and BMD demonstrated that in women, BMD at all sites increased with higher BMI. In contrast, men exhibited an inverted U-shaped nonlinear association, with a decline in BMD observed in the high BMI range (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²). The correlation between body composition and BMD, skeletal muscle mass (SMM), skeletal muscle index (SMI), and fat free mass (FFM) were significantly positively correlated with BMD at all sites in both males and females (P < 0.05), and percent body fat (PBF) was negatively correlated with BMD at all sites in the obese group of males, the overweight groups of males and females (P < 0.05). Among young and middle-aged individuals, men show a decline in BMD when BMI reaches 30 or above, whereas women continue to experience a gradual increase in BMD with rising BMI. Additionally, elevated PBF is negatively correlated with BMD. This suggests that maintaining an appropriate BMI and PBF may be beneficial for bone health in this age group.Keywords: BMI, Bone mineral density, Body composition, Percent body fat

Introduction

Bone mineral density (BMD) is the amount of bone mineral per unit area or volume and is currently the main method for diagnosing osteoporosis (OP) [1]. Decrease in BMD can cause serious consequences such as bone loss and osteoporosis and even fractures. Decrease in bone density can be caused by a variety of factors, such as advanced age, reduced exercise, changes in sex hormone levels, genetic susceptibility, etc [2–4]. Changes in body composition, such as obesity, especially abdominal obesity, and loss of muscle mass, can also affect BMD.

The relationship between obesity and BMD is very complex, with the increase of body mass index (BMI)all parts of the BMD increased significantly, suggesting that BMI has a protective effect on BMD, but when the BMI > 30 kg/m2, obesity is associated with a decrease in BMD [5], the femoral neck BMD was significantly reduced in the severely obese group (BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2) [6], suggesting that excessive obesity may adversely affect BMD, and excessive visceral adipose tissue (VAT) is associated with reduced BMD in women [7, 8]. Obesity and osteoporosis are common disorders, with nearly one-third of the world’s population facing overweight and obesity, and the prevalence of osteoporosis is 2% in people over 50 years of age [9], while the prevalence rises to 25% in people over 80 years of age [10]. In recent years, with the transformation of lifestyle, obesity, especially in the youth population obesity rate increased significantly. According to the “Report on Nutrition and Chronic Disease Status of Chinese Residents (2020)”, the overweight and obesity rate among residents aged 18 and above in China exceeds 50%, while the rate among school-age children and adolescents is 20% [11]. Overweight and obesity have become a serious social issue threatening national health. Peak bone mass is a critical factor influencing bone mass in adulthood, with adolescence being the period of fastest bone mass accumulation, reaching peak bone mass around the age of 30 [12]. Exploring the correlation between obesity and bone density in young and middle-aged adults under 50 years old is conducive to better promoting bone health and preventing osteoporosis at an earlier stage. However, most current clinical studies focus primarily on elderly populations, with limited research on the relationship between body composition and BMD in young and middle-aged adults under 50 years old.

The present study investigates the correlation between body composition and BMD in the young and middle-aged population, to better understand how body composition affects BMD, which will help to identify populations at higher risk of osteoporosis, and provide a new theoretical basis and preventive measures for osteoporosis prevention.

Methods

Study population

This is a retrospective observational study on patients underwent physical examinations at the International Medical Service department of the Peking Union Medical College Hospital (Xidan Campus) from June 2020 to June 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) body mass index (BMI) ≥ 18.5 kg/m2; (2) age between 20 and 50 years old; (3) Subjects with complete clinical documentation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who suffered from diseases that seriously affect bone metabolism, such as rheumatic diseases, malignant tumors, metabolic bone diseases, thyroid or parathyroid diseases, chronic kidney disease, adrenal or gonadal diseases, etc.; (2) patients who had recent administration of medications that affect bone metabolism, such as bisphosphonates, sex hormones, glucocorticoids, diuretics, anti-psychotics, etc.; (3) patients who have a history of long-term bed rest or a history of severe trauma (e.g., falling from a height or being involved in a car accident, etc.) resulting in lumbar spine and hip fractures (Fig. 1).

Variable collection

Demographic information

Trained healthcare professionals administered a structured questionnaire to collect information on participants’ age, current and past medical history. Height, weight, waist circumference, and hip circumference were measured using standardized, fixed instruments. All measurements were performed with participants wearing light clothing and without shoes or hats.

Anthropometric measurements

Waist circumference was measured as the horizontal circumference at the level of the umbilicus. Hip circumference was measured as the circumference around the widest part of the hips (the most prominent point of the buttocks). Laboratory indicators

Blood samples were obtained from the antecubital vein after participants had fasted for more than 8 h overnight. Fasting blood glucose (FPG), total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were measured on the Abbott ARCHITECT c16000 automatic biochemical analyzer with original matched reagents from Abbott Laboratories. Bio-Rad quality control products (batch No.45870) containing three concentration lots (45871, 45872, 45873) were adopted for bracketed internal quality control; one QC measurement was conducted before and after each analytical run, complying with standard IQC requirements.

Body composition analysis

Body composition was assessed using the InBody770 bioelectrical impedance analyzer (Biospace, Korea). The device directly measures multi-frequency (1 kHz – 1 MHz) electrical impedance (resistance and reactance) of the whole body and body segments. Using these impedance values, along with the participant’s height, weight, age, and sex, the following body composition variables were calculated by the instrument’s built-in algorithms: Percent Body Fat (PBF), Body Fat Mass (BFM), Visceral Fat Area (VAT), Fat Free Mass (FFM), Skeletal Muscle Mass (SMM), and Skeletal Muscle Index (SMI).

Bone mineral density (BMD)

In this study, a GE-Lunar iDXA dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) system was used for data acquisition. Two anatomical sites, the lumbar spine and the hip, were scanned. The region of interest (ROI) for the lumbar spine was L1–L4, and for the hip, the femoral neck and total hip.

Lumbar spine scan: The subject lies supine in the center of the scanning table, with the body midline aligned with the table midline. Both upper limbs are extended and placed alongside the torso. A lower limb fixation pad may be used or not as needed. The scan range for the lumbar spine extends from 2 to 3 cm below the umbilicus to 2 cm below the xiphoid process. The displayed image covers from the upper half of the L5 vertebra to the lower half of the T12 vertebra. Principle for labeling lumbar vertebral numbers: The line connecting the bilateral anterior superior iliac spines is at the level of the L4–L5 intervertebral disc, or the vertebra where the line connecting the two pedicles and the spinous process forms a “—” shape is considered L5. Using this as a reference, the lumbar vertebrae are labeled sequentially from bottom to top.

Proximal femur scan: The subject lies supine in the center of the scanning table, with the body midline aligned with the table midline. Both upper limbs may be placed on the chest. The left proximal femur is routinely measured. The left lower limb is slightly abducted and internally rotated by 15–25° so that the femur is parallel to the long axis of the table and the axis of the femoral neck is parallel to the table surface.

Scan range for the femur: From below the greater trochanter (approximately the proximal one-third of the femur) to above the acetabulum. In the scanned image, the upper margin should clearly show the entire femoral head, including part of the acetabular structure, and the lower margin should show part of the proximal femoral shaft below the lesser trochanter. The femoral shaft should be perpendicular to the bottom edge of the image. The lesser trochanter in the proximal femoral shaft should not be visible or only a small portion should be visible [13–21].

Study sample size calculation

The primary outcome variable of this study is BMD, a continuous variable. The sample size of this study was determined with reference to previously published findings. For example, Zhang Wanli et al. published a study titled “Correlation between fat distribution and femoral neck strength composite index in obese postmenopausal women,” which was a cross-sectional study involving 293 patients. The results showed that the femoral neck strength composite index was reduced in obese postmenopausal women, and both subcutaneous and visceral fat were negatively correlated with the femoral neck strength composite index in this population [22]. Catriona Hilton et al. analyzed data from 4,900 healthy individuals aged 30–50 years from the Oxford Biobank and concluded that fat distribution influences the correlation between obesity and bone mineral density [23]. In this retrospective study, the sample size was also determined by data availability, and we ultimately included the clinical data of 1,295 participants who underwent health checkups at the Health Management Center of the International Medical Department (Xidan Campus) between January 2020 and June 2024.

Statistical analysis

Participants were categorized into three BMI groups according to the thresholds proposed by the Working Group on Obesity in China for Chinese adults [24]: normal weight (18.5 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 24.0 kg/m2), overweight (24.0 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 28.0 kg/m2), and obesity (BMI ≥ 28.0 kg/m2). Participants with missing data were excluded.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) depending on normality, and compared across BMI groups using one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test, respectively. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test. Given established sex differences in the associations between obesity/body composition and BMD, all analyses were stratified by sex.

We first evaluated the associations between BMI categories (normal weight as reference) and BMD at the lumbar spine (L1-L4), left femoral neck, and total hip using multivariable linear regression models, adjusting for age, smoking status, alcohol consumption, history of hypertension, and history of diabetes. To explore potential non-linear associations between continuous BMI and the three BMD indicators, unadjusted natural cubic spline models with 3 degrees of freedom were fitted [25, 26]. To assess nonlinearity, a linear model and a natural cubic spline model were fitted separately for each sex and BMD indicator, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the two nested models.

Next, we examined the associations between individual body composition components (BFM, PBF, VAT, FFM, SMM, SMI) and each BMD indicator using multivariable linear regression models, adjusting for age, smoking status, alcohol consumption, history of hypertension, and history of diabetes. These analyses were further stratified by BMI categories, as the relationships between body composition and BMD may be modified by obesity status. We use Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) to assess multicollinearity in the regression.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Results are reported as beta coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and a two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparative analysis of clinical data among the three groups

This study included 1295 study subjects with a mean age of 41.32 ± 5.31 years. There were 214 in the obese group, 479 in the overweight group, and 682 in the normal weight group. Comparisons were made among the three groups. As BMI increased, the percentage of male patients and those with a history of smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and diabetes gradually increased (P < 0.05). Regarding laboratory tests, FPG, HbA1c, and TG showed a gradual increase with increasing BMI, while HDL-C gradually decreased (P < 0.05). However, serum TC and LDL-C levels were highest in the overweight group rather than showing a continuous increase. Analysis of body composition showed that WHR, WHtR, BFM, PBF, VFA, FFM, SMM, and SMI gradually increased (P < 0.05). DXA showed that lumbar spine, femoral neck, and total hip bone density gradually increased, and all differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Table 1).

Correlation between BMI and BMD

Multiple linear regression analysis, adjusted for age, smoking and alcohol history, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus, revealed that lumbar spine, femoral neck, and total hip BMD significantly increased with higher BMI (all P < 0.001). This positive association was particularly pronounced in the obesity group (lumbar spine BMD: β = 0.04, 95% CI 0.02–0.07; femoral neck BMD: β = 0.09, 95% CI 0.07–0.11; total hip BMD: β = 0.11, 95% CI 0.09–0.13).

Subgroup analyses by sex were consistent with the overall findings. In the male obesity subgroup, BMI remained positively correlated with BMD (lumbar spine: β = 0.07, 95% CI 0.04–0.10; femoral neck: β = 0.08, 95% CI 0.06–0.11; total hip: β = 0.11, 95% CI 0.08–0.13). Similarly, in the female obesity subgroup, a positive association was observed (lumbar spine: β = 0.08, 95% CI 0.03–0.12; femoral neck: β = 0.07, 95% CI 0.03–0.11; total hip: β = 0.10, 95% CI 0.07–0.14). These results suggest that, both in the overall population and across sex-specific subgroups, higher BMI was associated with elevated BMD, with the most notable increase observed at the hip (Table 4).

Correlation between body composition and BMD

The association between body composition and BMD was evaluated using multiple linear regression, adjusted for age, smoking and drinking history, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus. All VIFs in the regression are below 10, indicating no multicollinearity was detected.

In males across obesity, overweight, and normal weight groups, SMM, SMI, and FFM were significantly positively correlated with BMD at all measured sites (P < 0.05). In the obesity and overweight groups, PBF was negatively correlated with BMD at all sites (P < 0.05), but not in the normal weight group (P > 0.05). (Table 5).

Discussion

This study revealed a significant positive correlation between BMI and BMD in young and middle-aged adults, with notable gender differences observed. While BMD increased across all measured sites in women as BMI rose, an inverted U-shaped nonlinear relationship was observed in men. Notably, in the high BMI range (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²), suggest that the association between BMI and bone health may vary by gender and weight category in young and middle-aged adults.

In recent years, many studies have found that the relationship between BMI and BMD is not a simple linear correlation, but there is a saturation effect. Ouyang Y et al. grouped adolescents by gender and age, and found that there is a saturation effect in the association between BMI and BMD, and adolescents of different ages have different BMI saturation values in terms of BMD [6].Ma M et al. based on the multivariate linear regression of the U.S. population aged 50 years and older analysis showed that the association between BMI and BMD was not a simple linear relationship, and the saturation effect analysis showed that the BMI saturation effect value for BMD was 26.13 kg/m2 for the hip and 26.82 kg/m2 for the spinal region, and that the best BMD may be obtained by keeping the BMI at a value of mild overweight (about 26 kg/m2) [27]. Zhang Y et al. used the National Health and Nutritional Examination Survey (NHANES) latest data to study the association between BMI and femoral neck BMD by multivariate logistic regression modeling and also found a saturation effect between obesity and BMD [28].

BMI contributes to higher BMD through direct weight-bearing effects and the application of mechanical tension on bone tissue [29]. However, in obese individuals, particularly those with abdominal obesity, various factors detrimental to BMD gradually increase. For instance, centrally obese males tend to exhibit lower testosterone levels [30, 31], while increased visceral fat mass elevates inflammation markers [32]—such as TNF-α, interleukins (IL-1, IL-12, IL-17, IL-18, and IL-33), and interferons—which can directly suppress bone formation or promote bone resorption [33]. In our study, we observed that BMI correlates differently with BMD among young and middle-aged males and females. In females, higher estrogen levels appear to exert a protective effect on BMD, which may explain why BMD maintains a linear relationship with BMI even in obese females. In contrast, among males within the high BMI range, BMD showed a declining trend. This may be because the adverse effects of obesity on BMD could counteract or even outweigh the benefits of mechanical loading. Furthermore, compared to females, males exhibited significantly higher rates of smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, and diabetes—especially among obese males. These unhealthy lifestyle factors may also contribute to reduced BMD.

The global prevalence of overweight and obese in the year 2020 was estimated at 2603 million (38%) and 988 million (14%) people, respectively. The prevalence of obesity in males (14%) was lower than in females (18%). Sex-specific differences are evident in all regions of the world [34]. Data from 3663 population-based studies with 222 million participants from 197 countries covering more than 99% of the world’s population demonstrated that the global age-standardised prevalence of obesity has increased from 8.8% in 1990 to 18.5% in 2022 in women and from 4.8% in 1990 to 14.0% in 2022 in men [35]. This study found that among young and middle-aged participants aged 20 to 50 years, the obesity rate in males was higher than that in females, at 23.0% vs. 6.9%. From 2004 to 2023, among the population aged 18–69 years in Shandong Province by sex and urban-rural residence, the mean BMI, prevalence of overweight, and prevalence of obesity all showed increasing trends, with a faster rate of increase in males than in females [36]. This suggests that the obesity rate among young and middle-aged populations in China differs from the global trend. The reasons may be that men experience higher mental stress and have a higher proportion of eating out, leading to greater calorie intake [37]; moreover, due to social development, the intensity of physical labor undertaken by men has weakened, reducing energy expenditure and resulting in fat accumulation [38].

Skeletal muscle mass, fat-free body weight, and BMD were positively correlated, consistent with previous studies [39–42]. As muscle mass increased, BMD also rose. This relationship can be attributed to the direct mechanical loading and stress that skeletal muscle exerts on bone [43]. Additionally, skeletal muscle and bone interact through endocrine pathways. For instance, bone tissue secretes prostaglandin E2 to promote muscle growth, while muscle tissue releases myokines such as myostatin, which negatively regulates muscle growth and helps maintain BMD [44–46].

This study revealed that PBF in the male obese group, the male overweight group, and the female overweight group was negatively correlated with BMD at all measured sites. Adipose tissue may positively influence BMD through several mechanisms: mechanical loading that stimulates bone formation, conversion of androgens to estrogens within fat tissue [47], secretion of leptin by adipocytes to activate osteoblasts [48], and release of lipocalin to promote osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, thereby increasing BMD [49, 50]. However, obesity and overweight conditions are often associated with insulin resistance, hyperglycemia, and abnormal lipid metabolism, all of which can contribute to reduced bone strength [39, 51]. As PBF increases, these detrimental factors may gradually outweigh the beneficial effects, eventually leading to a decline in BMD.

Increasing BMD in middle-aged adults before the age of 50 helps prevent the onset of osteoporosis and reduces the risk of fractures caused by osteoporosis. The findings of this study suggest that young and middle-aged adults should maintain their BMI and body fat percentage within a moderate range, especially men, as this contributes to protecting bone health in this age group. Lean body mass is positively correlated with BMD, while excessively high body fat percentage is negatively correlated with BMD. This indicates that for young and middle-aged adults, exercise programs that provide mechanical stimulation to the bones and increase muscle mass contribute to improved bone density. A combination of impactful activities multiple times per week (such as running, jumping, or ball sports) and planned resistance training targeting large muscle groups (particularly the lower limbs and core muscles) is beneficial for bone health.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow for causal relationships to be established between BMI, body composition, and BMD. Second, information on physical activity level and menopausal status (in women) was not available. Both factors have been reported to be potentially associated with the outcome variable. Their absence from the models may lead to residual confounding, particularly in subgroup analyses involving female participants. Future studies incorporating these variables are warranted to validate our findings. Third, the small sample size in the female obese group may affect the accuracy of the findings; future studies would benefit from a larger cohort. Finally, body composition was assessed using the InBody Body Composition Analyzer. Employing more precise methods such as DXA or quantitative CT for body composition analysis in future research would yield more reliable conclusions.

Conclusion

In young and middle-aged populations, BMI was positively correlated with BMD. However, significant gender differences were observed: whereas higher BMI was associated with increased BMD in women, in men, BMD gradually declined when BMI reached 30 kg/m² or above.

Body composition analysis revealed negative correlations between fat percentage and BMD at all sites in male obese/overweight groups and female overweight group. These findings highlight the importance of maintaining appropriate BMI and body fat levels for optimal skeletal health, especially among young and middle-aged males.
