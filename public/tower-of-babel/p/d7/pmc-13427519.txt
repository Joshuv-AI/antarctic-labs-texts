A novel AMHR2 gene mutation causing transverse testicular ectopia: A rare case report and literature review
Authors: Tien Dung Mai Ba, Quang Tuan Dang, Van Hao Pham, Vinh Phuoc Nguyen Ho, Minh Dat Vo, Quang Huy Huynh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transverse testicular ectopia (TTE) is a rare anomaly often associated with persistent Müllerian duct syndrome and infertility. We report a 34-year-old man with primary infertility, a vacant right hemiscrotum, and a left scrotal mass. MRI showed both testes in the left hemiscrotum, with a heterogeneous right ectopic testicular tumor. Surgery confirmed TTE without gross Müllerian remnants; bilateral orchiectomy was performed because of malignancy and contralateral atrophy. Histopathology revealed seminoma. Genetic testing identified novel compound heterozygous AMHR2 variants. This case highlights the value of MRI, genetic evaluation, and early management to reduce infertility and malignant transformation risks in TTE patients worldwide.

Introduction

Transverse testicular ectopia (TTE) represents a clinical rarity in developmental urology, defined by the aberrant migration of both gonads into a single hemiscrotum or inguinal region. This condition is frequently comorbid with an ipsilateral inguinal hernia on the side of ectopic displacement. Beyond simple maldescence, TTE is often part of a complex syndromic presentation involving Persistent Müllerian Duct Syndrome (PMDS), hypospadias, disorders of sex development (DSD), or scrotal abnormalities. Although the literature suggests a median diagnostic age of 4 years,1 clinical recognition often occurs incidentally during adult surgical procedures, with preoperative identification remaining a diagnostic challenge.2 While surgical exploration often reveals the pathology, high-resolution preoperative imaging is paramount for surgical mapping and the detection of occult Müllerian derivatives.

In this report, we document an exceptional case of an adult male diagnosed preoperatively via magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with TTE and a synchronous right testicular malignancy. Molecular investigation identified a novel genetic signature, a compound heterozygous mutation in the AMHR2 gene which has not been previously cataloged in global genomic repositories. Recent reports continue to emphasize that adult TTE associated with PMDS is diagnostically challenging and often requires integrated imaging, operative, pathological, and genetic evaluation.3,4 While TTE and PMDS reports in Vietnam remain sparse and largely confined to pediatric morphological studies, this case marks the report of an adult TTE patient presenting with a confirmed seminoma and a distinct AMHR2 genetic variant.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old male presented with a two-week history of persistent left scrotal discomfort and a palpable, firm mass. His medical history was notable for 12 years of primary infertility, including a failed in-vitro fertilization (IVF) attempt in 2014, and a 2019 laparoscopic left inguinal hernia repair where transverse testicular ectopia (TTE) was noted incidentally but remained untreated. Physical examination revealed a vacant right scrotum and a normal urethral meatus. In the left hemiscrotum, two distinct structures were palpated: a dominant, firm, and tender 50 × 50 mm mass (ectopic right testis) and a separate, atrophic 3 cc soft testis (left testis) situated superomedially.

Laboratory investigations showed normal hematological and renal profiles, though biochemical markers revealed a mildly elevated LDH (264 U/L) and a profound hormonal imbalance characterized by hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (FSH: 23.9 mIU/ml; testosterone: 5.43 nmol/l). Semen analysis confirmed absolute azoospermia. Scrotal MRI substantiated the diagnosis of TTE, showing both gonads descended through the left inguinal canal. The right ectopic testis exhibited a 52x36 × 50 mm heterogeneous solid lesion highly suggestive of seminoma, while the left testis appeared severely atrophic (13 × 7 mm) (Fig. 1, red and yellow asterisks). Discussion

Transverse testicular ectopia (TTE) is an exceedingly rare developmental anomaly, with an estimated incidence of approximately 1 in 4 million. Historically, the condition was first documented in 1886 by von Lenhossek through an autopsy finding. Despite its long clinical history, the exact embryological pathogenesis of TTE remains a subject of ongoing debate. Several mechanical and anatomical theories have been proposed, including the development of both gonads from a single genital ridge, abnormal adhesions between the testis and adjacent structures, or early fusion of the Wolffian ducts. The latter suggests that the descent of one testis mechanically drags the contralateral gonad into the same inguinal canal.5

Clinically, the hallmark of TTE is an inguinal hernia associated with a contralateral undescended testis. This classic presentation was evident in our patient, who had a documented history of laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair on the left side alongside a vacant right scrotum. While the median age at diagnosis typically ranges from 4 to 5 years, our report highlights a significant clinical outlier an adult presentation. We hypothesize that this delayed diagnosis in our case may be attributed to a combination of low socioeconomic status and a lack of medical awareness, leading to a decade-long interval between the initial clinical suspicion and definitive surgical intervention.

According to the landmark classification established by Gauderer et al.6 TTE is categorized into three distinct clinical phenotypes based on associated anatomical anomalies. Type 1, the most prevalent variant accounting for 40% to 50% of cases, is classically associated with an inguinal hernia and contralateral cryptorchidism, mirroring the gross clinical presentation of our patient. Type 2, comprising approximately 30% of cases, is defined by its association with PMDS a syndromic constellation first delineated by Jordan in 1895. Characterized by the retention of Müllerian derivatives (including fallopian tubes, the uterus, and the upper vagina) in otherwise phenotypically normal males, PMDS arises from defects in the anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) signaling pathway. While structural mutations in the AMH gene (located on chromosome 19p) underscore certain cases, peripheral receptor insensitivity due to mutations in the AMH type II receptor (AMHR2) gene on chromosome 12 represents another major molecular etiology, both typically demonstrating an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern.4,7

Molecular disruption or resistance at the receptor level compromises AMH-mediated regression, and the persistent, bulky Müllerian structures mechanically impede physiological gonadal descent, driving both testes into a single inguinal canal and culminating in Type 2 TTE.7 Conversely, Type 3 TTE, representing the remaining 20%, involves diverse genitourinary malformations such as scrotal abnormalities, fused vasa deferentia, or seminal vesicle cysts independent of PMDS pathway defects.

In our patient, targeted genomic analysis of the AMHR2 gene uncovered a novel genetic signature comprising two uncataloged, likely pathogenic compound heterozygous variants. The first is a frameshift duplication (chr12:53425860-53425861; c.800dup, p.Pro268SerfsTer30) inducing a premature stop codon, and the second is a missense substitution (chr12:53431250; c.1499G > A, p.Cys500Tyr). Review of the current literature regarding AMHR2 mutations reveals distinct genotypic expressions; for instance, Unal et al.8 (2018) identified a homozygous c.233-1G > A mutation presenting discordantly as bilateral cryptorchidism in one sibling and TTE in another, while Cima et al.9 (2024) documented a homozygous exonic variant (NC_000012.11:g.53823315T > C) in a 13-year-old male with classic PMDS and bilateral cryptorchidism.

Crucially, our case introduces a highly unique clinical paradox: despite harboring a novel, damaging compound heterozygous mutation in AMHR2 molecularly linked to PMDS Type II, the patient exhibited no macroscopically identifiable Müllerian remnants during meticulous intraoperative exploration, presenting purely with TTE and subsequent malignant transformation. This rare constellation underscores the vital clinical necessity of advanced genomic sequencing in atypical TTE cases, providing critical insights that further elucidate the highly variable and incomplete penetrance in genotype-phenotype correlations.

Preoperative diagnosis of TTE remains notoriously difficult, with the vast majority of cases discovered incidentally during surgical interventions for inguinal hernias, hydroceles, or infertility evaluations. Although advanced imaging modalities such as ultrasonography, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have moderately enhanced diagnostic accuracy, rates of misdiagnosis and inappropriate management remain unacceptably high.10,11 Recent case reports and case series further support a multimodal approach in which physical examination, scrotal/inguinal ultrasonography, MRI or CT when indicated, and diagnostic laparoscopy are used selectively to define testicular location, cord anatomy, Müllerian remnants, and malignancy risk.11, 12, 13 In our patient, the ectopic gonad was initially recognized during a laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair in 2019; however, the lack of immediate surgical repositioning allowed a five-year window of unmitigated heat stress and aberrant signaling, ultimately driving malignant transformation in the right ectopic testis. TTE carries an estimated 18% risk of oncological degeneration, a propensity significantly higher than that observed in conventional cryptorchidism. The most prevalent histological subtypes documented include embryonal carcinoma, classical seminoma, yolk sac tumor, and teratoma.3,14,15

In contemporary adult literature, malignant outcomes associated with TTE are exceedingly rare. Adult cases reported by Yang et al. highlighted the added complexity of infertility, prior surgery, comprehensive imaging, and fertility-preserving surgical planning in TTE associated with PMDS.3 In addition, seminoma complicating PMDS-associated TTE has been documented only in isolated case reports.14 Consequently, the identification of a classical seminoma in our patient's right ectopic testis underscores the critical necessity of early diagnostic recognition and definitive surgical intervention to mitigate oncological risks. This case provides pivotal insights that expand the highly restricted global registry of adult TTE complicated by synchronous malignancy.

Furthermore, severe spermatogenic impairment resulting in absolute azoospermia is a hallmark characteristic of long-standing adult TTE.16 The fundamental therapeutic tenets for managing TTE comprise the preservation of reproductive potential, concomitant hernia repair, orchiopexy, meticulous exploration for Müllerian remnants, and stringent long-term oncological surveillance. In this case, the comprehensive involvement of the right testis by the seminomatous mass, combined with end-stage fibrosis and atrophy of the contralateral left gonad, provided a clear pathophysiological explanation for the patient's profound infertility.

Standard surgical techniques for viable ectopia typically include transseptal orchiopexy or transperitoneal transposition, both requiring sufficient mobilization of the spermatic cord structures (the vas deferens and gonadal vessels) to achieve tension-free scrotal placement. However, given the confirmed right-sided neoplasm and the severely atrophic, non-functional state of the left testis which presented an unacceptably high risk for future malignant degeneration bilateral orchiectomy was deemed the safest oncological approach. Concurrently, a microdissection testicular sperm extraction (micro-TESE) was executed in a desperate attempt to salvage viable spermatozoa for assisted reproduction; unfortunately, consistent with the severe bilateral parenchyma destruction, no spermatozoa were retrieved.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that while preoperative diagnosis of TTE remains historically challenging, high-resolution Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) serves as an invaluable diagnostic tool capable of rendering an accurate preoperative mapping of ectopic gonads and occult pelvic structures. Furthermore, our findings underscore the pivotal clinical necessity of comprehensive genetic screening for AMH and AMHR2 gene variants in all suspected TTE cases. Such molecular investigations not only fortify the definitive diagnosis but also provide profound insights into the intricate embryological and genetic mechanisms underlying disorders of sex development. Ultimately, early clinical recognition of TTE is paramount to optimizing the windows for testicular preservation and fertility salvage, while drastically minimizing the risk of long-term malignant transformation in the ectopic parenchyma.
