Traditional Cardiovascular Risk Factors Are Stronger Related to Carotid Intima‐Media Thickness Than to Presence of Carotid Plaques in People Living With HIV
Authors: Marc J T Blaauw, Marvin A H Berrevoets, Wilhelm A J W Vos, Albert L Groenendijk, Louise E van Eekeren, Nadira Vadaq, Gert Weijers, Andre J A M van der Ven, Joost H W Rutten, Niels P Riksen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in people living with HIV, who are at higher risk than the general population. We assessed, in a large cohort of people living with HIV, which cardiovascular, HIV‐specific, and lipoproteomic markers were associated with carotid intima‐media thickness (cIMT) and carotid plaque presence. We also studied guideline adherence on lipid‐lowering medication in individuals with high and very high risk for cardiovascular disease.Methods and ResultsIn 1814 individuals with a median (interquartile range) age of 53 (44–60) years, we found a carotid plaque in 909 (50.1%) and a median (interquartile range) intima‐media thickness of 0.66 (0.57–0.76) mm. Ultrasonography was used for the assessment of cIMT and plaque presence. Univariable and multivariable regression models were used for associations with cIMT and presence of plaques. Age, Black race, body mass index, type 2 diabetes, and smoking (pack years) were all positively associated with higher cIMT. Levels of high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, specifically medium and large high‐density lipoprotein subclasses, were negatively associated with higher cIMT. Only age and prior myocardial infarction were positively related to the presence of a carotid plaque. Lipid‐lowering treatment was prescribed in one‐third of people living with HIV, who are at high and very high risk for cardiovascular disease.ConclusionsTraditional cardiovascular risk factors were significantly associated with higher cIMT but not with carotid plaques, except for age. HIV‐specific factors were not associated with both ultrasound measurements. Future studies are needed to elucidate which factors contribute to plaque formation. Improvement of guideline adherence on prescription of lipid‐lowering treatment in high‐ and very high‐risk patients for cardiovascular disease is recommended.RegistrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT03994835.Keywords: cardiovascular risk management, carotid atherosclerosis, carotid intima‐media thickness, carotid plaques, HIV, lipoproteinsSubject Categories: Atherosclerosis, Cardiovascular Disease

What Are the Clinical Implications?

As traditional cardiovascular risk factors are associated with carotid intima‐media thickness, strict management of these cardiovascular risk factors is important, yet statins are not prescribed in a considerable proportion of people living with HIV with a high risk of developing cardiovascular disease, and better risk management is therefore recommended.

The introduction of combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) resulted in a steep reduction in AIDS‐related morbidity and mortality among people living with HIV (PLHIV). 1 With the increased lifespan, focus has turned to non–AIDS‐related illness, and cardiovascular disease (CVD) is now one of the leading causes of death in people with treatment for HIV infection. 2 Moreover, PLHIV have an increased risk of developing CVD compared with people without HIV. 3 , 4 The exact pathophysiological mechanisms behind the increased risk remain unclear but are presumed to be multifactorial. The cause is most likely a combination of increased traditional cardiovascular risk factors, metabolic adverse effects of cART, and HIV itself. 5

In PLHIV, traditional cardiovascular risk factors are more common than in the general population, and they include cigarette smoking, 6 dyslipidemia, 7 hypertension, 8 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome. 9 HIV and cART are associated with altered lipid metabolism, resulting in a proatherogenic profile that increases the risk of CVD. 10 After initiation of cART, immune dysfunction and inflammation improve by reconstitution of CD4+ T cells. However, restoration is only partial as markers of inflammation, such as soluble CD14, hs‐CRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein), and interleukin 6, and markers of coagulation, such as d‐dimer, remain higher compared with uninfected individuals. 11 , 12 , 13 These biomarkers are also associated with mortality in treated PLHIV. 14 , 15 , 16 Moreover, proinflammatory monocyte subsets are increased even after viral suppression is reached. 17 One of the presumed mechanisms is increased intestinal permeability, which may lead to microbial translocation and chronic immune activation and inflammation. 18 Persistent inflammation can also be sustained by viral coinfections, such as cytomegalovirus, hepatitis B virus, and hepatitis C virus. 19 HIV and persistence of immune activation may lead to immunosenescence and aging of the immune system. Previous studies even showed similarities in immunologic profiles in PLHIV and elderly people without HIV. 20 , 21 A previous study showed that PLHIV had signs of increased arterial inflammation compared with uninfected individuals with similar cardiovascular risk factors, and increased arterial inflammation was associated with circulating CD163 levels, a marker of monocyte and macrophage activation. 22

The presence of atherosclerosis can noninvasively be detected in the carotid artery by ultrasound. The presence of carotid plaques is a strong predictor for myocardial infarction and stroke in uninfected individuals. 23 , 24 , 25 The vascular remodeling that precedes the development of atherosclerotic plaques can be quantified by measuring the intima‐media thickness (IMT) of the carotid arterial wall. This is also associated with future cardiovascular events, although this association is generally weaker than the presence of carotid plaques. 26 , 27 , 28 A previous study showed that PLHIV had a 1.6‐fold greater risk of new plaque formation compared with HIV‐uninfected individuals. 29

The 2000HIV study is a multicenter prospective longitudinal cohort study of PLHIV on cART and aims to discover new biological pathways and biomarkers linked to non‐AIDS comorbidities. 30 In the present study, we analyze carotid IMT (cIMT) and the presence of carotid plaques in PLHIV, and we evaluate which clinical and HIV‐related factors are associated with cIMT and atherosclerotic plaques. Next, we study in detail which lipidomic biomarkers are associated with these ultrasound measurements. Finally, we evaluate statin use according to European guideline recommendations in high‐ and very high‐risk cardiovascular groups. 31

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population and Design

2000HIV Cohort

In this multicenter observational cohort study, PLHIV on suppressive cART were recruited between November 1, 2019, and October 15, 2021, in 4 specialized HIV clinics in the Netherlands. Recruitment took place at Radboud University Medical Center in Nijmegen, Elizabeth TweeSteden Hospital in Tilburg, OLVG in Amsterdam, and Erasmus Medical Center in Rotterdam. This cohort is part of the Human Functional Genomics project (www.humanfunctionalgenomics.org),32 and the study design is extensively described in a methodological article. 30 Inclusion criteria were as follows: HIV‐1 infection, aged ≥18 years, receiving cART for at least 6 months, and a latest HIV‐RNA viral load of <200 copies/mL. Spontaneous HIV controllers were also allowed in this study and were defined as a known HIV infection for >5 years without cART and always an HIV‐RNA viral load of <10 000 copies/mL during which CD4+ T‐cell counts were stable (>500 cells/mm3). Exclusion criteria were signs of acute or opportunistic infections, active hepatitis B/C infection (polymerase chain reaction positive), antibiotic use <1 month before enrollment, or an active malignancy. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethical Review committee region Arnhem‐Nijmegen (NL68056.091.81) and published at https://www.clinicaltrials.gov (ID: NCT03994835). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before inclusion in the study.

Data Collection

Clinical data, including medical history (a diagnosis was made before inclusion in this study) and HIV‐specific information, such as CD4 nadir and duration of cART use, were obtained by extensive clinical record review and from information obtained from the AIDS Therapy Evaluation in the Netherlands National HIV cohort. 33 This national observational HIV cohort includes demographic and clinical data from HIV‐positive individuals who are in treatment in 1 of the 24 Dutch HIV treatment centers and encompasses data from 98% of all PLHIV in care in the Netherlands. Smoking status and number of smoking pack years were assessed by structured questionnaires. Height and weight were measured by standardized protocols during the study visit, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated from these measurements. Blood pressure measurements of the past 2 years before inclusion were collected from the medical record file. Diagnosis of metabolic syndrome was defined as the presence of ≥3 of the following 5 abnormalities 34 : (1) hypertriglyceridemia (triglyceride concentration ≥1.7 mmoL/L or specific treatment for triglycerides), (2) low high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) (for men, HDL ≤1.0 mmoL/L; and for women, HDL ≤1.3 mmoL/L) or use of lipid‐lowering therapy, (3) high blood pressure (systolic blood pressure [SBP] ≥130 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥85 mm Hg) or antihypertensive drug treatment in a participant with a history of hypertension, (4) diagnosis of type 2 diabetes (replacing fasting glucose), and (5) severe obesity (defined as a BMI of >30 kg/m2, which corresponds to a waist circumference ≥102 cm in men and ≥88 cm in women). 35

Immunologic nonresponder was defined as a CD4+ T‐cell count increase <100 cells/mm3 in first year of cART use and a total CD4+ T‐cell count <500 mm3 after at least 2 years of cART. Hepatitis B infection in the past was defined as positive hepatitis B surface antibody combined with positive hepatitis B core antibody. Chronic hepatitis B was defined as positive hepatitis B surface antigen and treatment with tenofovir.

Assessment of Cardiovascular Risk

The Framingham risk score was calculated to estimate the 10‐year risk for a cardiovascular event. This risk prediction model is validated in individuals aged between 30 and 74 years without previous cardiovascular events (myocardial infarction, angina pectoris, ischemic stroke, heart failure, or peripheral artery disease). 36 This sex‐specific model included the following risk factors: age (years), SBP (mm Hg), treated SBP (yes/no), current smoking (yes/no), total cholesterol (mg/dL), HDL (mg/dL), and diabetes (yes/no). Besides the Framingham risk score, we also calculated the recently updated Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation 2 (SCORE2), 37 which is recommended for cardiovascular risk assessment in the European guideline on primary prevention. 38 The model predicts 10‐year risk of cardiovascular disease in European population and is stratified by different risk regions. SCORE2 was calculated in individuals aged between 40 and 69 years without previous cardiovascular events. For individuals aged ≥70 years, the SCORE2–Older Patients was used. 39 Both sex‐specific models included the following risk factors: age (years), current smoking status (yes/no), diabetes (yes/no), SBP (mm Hg), and total and HDL‐C (mmol/L).

For evaluation of guideline adherence on statin prescription and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) treatment goals, the European AIDS Clinical Society (EACS) guideline version 11.1, October 2022, was followed. 31 Very high risk of CVD was defined as clinically proven atherosclerotic CVD, a SCORE2 ≥10%, severe chronic kidney disease with estimated glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min, and diabetes with target organ damage or 3 major risk factors (smoking, SBP >180 mm Hg, total cholesterol >8 mmol/L, LDL‐C >4.9 mmol/L, or early onset of type 1 diabetes >20 years). According to the EACS guideline, unequivocal atherosclerotic CVD on imaging, such as on carotid ultrasound, was also defined as very high risk. In our analysis, this latter criterion was not used because carotid ultrasound measurement is not a routine procedure in daily practice.

High risk of CVD was defined as a SCORE2 ≥5% and <10%, or markedly elevated single risk factor (total cholesterol >8 mmol/L, LDL‐C >4.9 mmol/L, or blood pressure ≥180/110 mm Hg) or moderate chronic kidney disease (estimated glomerular filtration rate >30 and <60 mL/min), or diabetes without target organ damage but with duration ≥10 years and another additional risk factor.

Treatment goal of LDL‐C for very high risk is 1.4 mmol/L and for high risk is 1.8 mmol/L. 31

Carotid Ultrasound

Participants underwent carotid artery 2‐dimensional ultrasound acquired using an L14‐5WE transducer (DC80A; Mindray, Shenzhen, China) processed in the IMT module. IMT was measured in the far wall, 1 cm distal of the carotid bulb in the left and right common carotid artery in triplicate on each side, with IMT measurement software provided by Mindray. This procedure is in agreement with the consensus statement of the American Society of Echocardiography Carotid Intima‐Media Thickness Task Force. 40 The mean and maximum were extracted from a region of interest of 1 cm, and of these values, the average was taken. Only measurements with a Quality Index >0.90 were included.

A plaque was defined as a focal IMT >1.5 mm or thickening of the IMT >50% compared with the mean IMT in the common carotid artery, carotid bulb, or internal carotid artery. 40 All 4 researchers in the different centers were uniformly trained in the Radboud University Medical Center, and procedures were regularly evaluated.

Cytomegalovirus IgG Titer Measurement

Serum cytomegalovirus IgG titers were determined by commercially available ELISA (Genway Biotech, San Diego, CA; catalog number GWB‐892399), according to manufacturer's protocol.

Lipidomic Metabolite Quantification

In all subjects, EDTA plasma samples were taken after at least 4 hours of fasting. These samples were used to quantify a total of 246 lipids and metabolite measurements with high‐throughput nuclear magnetic resonance metabolomics platform (Nightingale's Biomarker Analysis Platform, Helsinki, Finland). This platform provides simultaneous quantification of routine lipids, lipoprotein subclasses (particle concentration, lipid concentration, and composition), fatty acid composition, and various other metabolites, including ketone bodies, glycolysis‐ and gluconeogenesis‐related metabolites, and amino acids. The technological details of the Nightingale nuclear magnetic resonance platform have previously been reviewed. 41 , 42 From the Nightingale panel, we selected lipid biomarkers with absolute quantification units, resulting in 141 remaining biomarkers. Most of the lipid biomarkers were highly intercorrelated. Therefore, we performed unsupervised hierarchical analysis using Ward‐linkage clustering for lipid biomarkers that were highly intercorrelated (Spearman coefficient of correlation [r] >0.75) (n=132 biomarkers), as described previously. 43 This approach led to 12 different clusters of lipid biomarkers (see Table S1 and Figure S1); and for each cluster, a group representative was selected on the basis of the intercorrelation between variables and biological plausibility. For downstream data analysis, we analyzed 12 cluster representatives and the remaining 9 lipid biomarkers (total=21 lipid biomarkers) for more straightforward interpretation. Besides the lipid biomarkers, we also analyzed the nonlipid biomarkers (amino acids, ketone bodies, and glycolysis‐ and gluconeogenesis‐related metabolites) from the Nightingale panel separately. Nightingale data were rank‐based inverse transformed to follow a more normal distribution before analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Numerical variables were described as median and interquartile range. Categorical and ordinal variables were described as frequency (percentage). Comparisons of continuous baseline variables of people with and without carotid plaques were compared using Student t test or Mann‐Whitney U test, depending on data distribution. Noncontinuous baseline characteristics were compared using the Pearson χ2 test or Fisher exact test in case counts were <5. For downstream analyses, cIMT measurements were log transformed to follow a normal distribution. Initially, we used a univariable linear regression model to associate log‐transformed cIMT with patients' characteristics and clinical variables. Next, we performed 2 models for multivariable linear regression analysis to select potential confounders. In the first model, we controlled for sex and age. In the second model, we selected all significantly associated variables from the previous model. Assumptions for linear regression were assessed, and results are shown in Figure S2. One case (number 655) was removed from our model because of high leverage point. To assess the relationship between presence of plaques and patients' characteristics and clinical factors, we performed a logistic regression analysis. We presented the results using odds ratios (ORs) and the corresponding 95% CIs. Again, we used 2 models for multivariable logistic regression. In the first model, we adjusted for age and sex; and in our second model, we selected all statistically significant variables from the previous model. For all regression models, variables with a variance inflation model >2 were removed in the second model to avoid multicollinearity. P<0.05 was considered significant. Assumptions for logistic regression were assessed, and results are shown in Figure S3. Finally, we evaluated the association between carotid ultrasound findings with targeted lipidomic metabolites using a linear and logistic regression model corrected for age, sex, use of lipid‐lowering drugs, and current smoking status. P values were subsequently adjusted using false discovery rate method,44 and adjusted P<0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analysis and visualization were performed in R, version 4.1.2. 45

RESULTS

Study Population

In total, 1895 participants living with HIV were enrolled in this study. Characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. In brief, this cohort consists of 1606 men (84.7%) and 289 women with a median age of 53 years (interquartile range, 16 years) and a median duration of known HIV‐1 infection of 12.4 years (interquartile range, 11.9 years). Of the total study population, 391 participants (20.6%) used lipid‐lowering therapy, and 474 (25%) were diagnosed with hypertension. A total of 75 participants (4%) had a prior myocardial infarction in their medical history, and 61 (3.2%) had a stroke in the past. Almost one‐third (n=559) of this cohort was still smoking at the moment of participation, and about one‐third (n=571) had smoked in the past. Median pack‐years of all participants with known smoking status was 5 years. A total of 514 participants (21.1%) had metabolic syndrome. Of 1895 participants, 1827 (96.4%) underwent carotid artery ultrasound, and 1814 valid measurements were extracted in either left or right carotid artery, of which there were 1776 and 1785 subjects for the left and right carotid artery, respectively (Table 2). In this cohort, 909 of 1814 (50.1%) participants had at least 1 carotid plaque, 624 of 1776 (35.1%) participants had a plaque on the left carotid artery, 689 of 1785 (38.6%) on the right and 404 of 1814 (22.3%) in both carotid arteries. Flowchart of total ultrasound measurements is provided in Figure S4. The median cIMT, calculated as the mean of the measurements on both carotid sides, is 0.66 mm (interquartile range, 0.19 mm) (Table 2). Distribution of mean cIMT measurement and plaque size among all participants is provided in Figure S5. A comparison between participants with and without a carotid plaque (Table 1) showed that participants with a plaque were older (median age, 57 versus 47 years), more often had cardiovascular comorbidities, such as hypertension (34.0% versus 16.7%), prior myocardial infarction (6.6% versus 1.5%), and type 2 diabetes (6.3% versus 3.4%), had a longer known HIV infection (14.2 versus 11.0 years), and were using cART longer (11.7 versus 8.7 years) compared with the participants without a carotid plaque. Most participants were diagnosed with a CVD after they were diagnosed with HIV. Myocardial infarction was diagnosed in 68 participants after diagnosis of HIV and in 7 participants before HIV diagnosis (Table S2).

Presence of Type 2 Diabetes, BMI, Age, Number of Pack‐Years, and Concentration of HDL‐C Is Associated With IMT

For assessment of associations with cIMT, univariable regression model and 2 different multivariable regression models were made (Table 3). In these analyses, 1814 participants with IMT data were included. Almost all demographic variables, cardiovascular comorbidities, lipid laboratory measurements, and HIV‐specific factors, including CD4 nadir and HIV duration, were associated with cIMT in the univariable analysis. For multivariable analyses, model 1 age and sex were included as covariates; and in model 2, all variables with a P<0.05 from the previous model were included. HDL‐C and Large HDL‐C Are Negatively Associated With cIMT

To investigate in more detail the association between lipid and lipoprotein parameters and atherosclerosis in PLHIV, we used lipoproteomic data (n=141) from the Nightingale's Biomarker Analysis platform. Most of the lipid biomarkers were highly intercorrelated (Figure S1). Therefore, we selected 12 cluster representatives among highly intercorrelated lipid biomarkers of at least r>0.75 (n=132/141) based on expert opinion. In addition, 9 remaining lipid biomarkers were analyzed simultaneously (see Methods). HDL‐C cluster (adjusted P=0.026) and phospholipids in medium HDL (adjusted P=0.009) were inversely associated with cIMT in a linear regression model corrected for age, sex, use of lipid‐lowering therapy, and current smoking status (Figure [A] and Table S9). Large HDL‐C cluster (P=0.007; adjusted P=0.052) was negatively associated, and free cholesterol in small LDL (P=0.042; adjusted P=0.176) was positively associated with cIMT without correction for multiple testing. As expected from cluster analysis, other lipid biomarkers in the HDL and large HDL‐C clusters (ie, HDL content of cholesterol, cholesterol esters, and phospholipids, and HDL particle number of medium and large HDL lipoproteins) were also negatively correlated with cIMT (Figure S8).

Guideline Adherence of Statin Prescription in People With High Risk and Very High Risk of Developing CVDs

We evaluated the use of lipid‐lowering treatment in individuals who are at high risk of developing CVD according to the EACS guideline recommendations. 31 This guideline recommends prescription of lipid‐lowering therapy in patients with high and very high risk of developing CVD, with an LDL‐C treatment goal of 1.8 and 1.4 mmol/L, respectively. Very high risk for developing a cardiovascular event is defined as previous clinical CVD, a SCORE2 of ≥10%, severe chronic kidney disease (estimated glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min), or diabetes with organ damage or 3 risk factors (smoking, total cholesterol >8 mmol/L, and SBP >180 mm Hg). In this group with previous clinical CVD, 78.9% of individuals are using lipid‐lowering medication and 35.3% of the group with a SCORE2 ≥10% are on lipid‐lowering therapy. Of all participants with a very high risk of CVD, 47.8% are using lipid‐lowering medication (Table 5). More than one‐third of participants are currently smoking, 35.8% are classified as overweight (BMI ≥25 and ≤30 kg/m2), and 13.7% has obesity (BMI >30 kg/m2). In 10.1% of participants with very high‐risk CVD, treatment goal of LDL‐C 1.4 mmol/L is reached. High risk is defined as a SCORE2 between 5% and 10%, elevated single risk factor (total cholesterol >8 mmol/L, LDL‐C >4.9 mmol/L, or SBP >180 mm Hg), moderate chronic kidney disease (estimated glomerular filtration rate >30 and <60 mL/min), or diabetes without target damage, with a duration >10 years or other additional risk factor. In the group with a SCORE2 between 5% and 10%, 25.8% are using lipid‐lowering medication, and almost 42% are currently smoking. Of all participants who are at high risk of developing a cardiovascular event, 31.6% are on lipid‐lowering therapy, more than one‐third are still smoking, 38.4% are classified as overweight, and 14% are classified as obese. Treatment goal of LDL‐C <1.8 mmol/L in this group is reached in 18.2%. We found carotid plaques in 68.4% in the very high‐risk group, and 37.3% of them are using lipid‐lowering therapy. In 64.9% of the high‐risk group, we found a carotid plaque (Table S13).

DISCUSSION

The main conclusion of this large observational cohort of 1814 PLHIV is that traditional cardiovascular risk factors, and not HIV‐specific factors, are associated with carotid atherosclerosis. Both cIMT as well as carotid plaques predict cardiovascular events, but this is generally stronger for plaque presence than for cIMT. 23 , 25 , 28 , 46 Carotid artery plaque assessment might lead also to reclassification of CVD risk. 47 In this well‐characterized cohort of PLHIV, we found a carotid plaque in 50.1% of all participants. It is difficult to compare this prevalence of carotid plaques with other studies because of differences in baseline characteristics, the method of evaluation, and plaque definitions. Plaque presence in other studies is therefore heterogeneous. A large study from Iceland in >6500 people without HIV revealed plaque presence in 45% of this population. 48 In this study, a carotid plaque was defined as an isolated thickening of at least 2 times the adjacent normal cIMT. In our study, we defined a plaque as a focal IMT >1.5 mm or thickening of the IMT >50% compared with the mean IMT, which is in line with the consensus statement from the American Society of Echocardiography Carotid Intima‐Media Thickness Task Force. 40 Half of the Icelandic study participants were men, compared with 80% in our study, and participants were generally younger (49.7 years) and less often met the criteria of metabolic syndrome (19.8%). In another large study of 6389 participants from an older population with more women (mean age, 69 years; and 61.9% women), carotid plaques were found in 57.8%. 23 A recent study from Italy of 1147 PLHIV with comparable age, classic risk factors, and men‐women distribution, a plaque was found with ultrasound in only 15.8% of all participants. In this study, a plaque was defined as a localized thickening of >1.2 mm. 49 In our study, we found that 41.0% would be categorized as having a plaque by using this same criterion. Another study revealed higher plaque presence (34%) in PLHIV compared with uninfected individuals (25%), as assessed by computed tomography, and participants were matched for age, sex, and cardiovascular risk factors. However, no participants with previous cardiovascular events were included in this study, and participants were also younger (mean age, 45.6 years). 50 In our study, we found more plaques on the right carotid artery than on the left side. This finding is surprising, as in a general population study, there was a higher prevalence of left carotid plaques (67% versus 33%) with also more signs of vulnerability. 51 Also, in a cohort with PLHIV, there were more plaques found in the left carotid artery (12.5%) than on the right side (10.1%). 49 A clear explanation for this finding cannot be given.

We found positive associations between higher cIMT and several traditional cardiovascular risk factors, including age, BMI, smoking pack‐years, and presence of type 2 diabetes, and a negative association with HDL‐C (Table 3). Although PLHIV are at increased risk of developing CVD, we did not find associations with HIV‐specific factors, such as CD4 nadir, total duration of known HIV infection, or total duration of cART exposure, in multivariable analysis. In‐depth analysis of lipidomic biomarkers confirmed the importance of HDL‐C and showed negative associations of cIMT with total HDL and large and medium HDL particles (Figure and Figure S8). Interestingly, the presence of carotid atherosclerotic plaques was not associated with the risk factors that showed an association with cIMT, except older age. In addition, the presence of plaques was also associated with a history of myocardial infarction.

Previous studies in PLHIV found that cIMT increases with age, BMI, and waist circumference. Lower cIMT was found in women, Hispanic individuals, and subjects with higher HDL levels. 52 A recent study found higher prevalence of pathologic findings with carotid ultrasound in PLHIV of older age, male sex, dyslipidemia, CD4 nadir <200 cells/mL, and current raltegravir use. 49 Pathologic findings in this study were defined as presence of carotid plaque, abnormal cIMT measurement >1.0 mm, or both. Specification of current ART use was not included in our association models as recent switch of cART regimen can lead to misinterpretation of associations. Although CD4 nadir was lower in this study, we did not find an association with cIMT or presence of carotid plaque in our models after adjustment for age and sex. In a pooled analysis of 5 cohorts, the association between HIV and cIMT was assessed. 53 In this analysis, higher SBP was associated with higher cIMT in middle‐aged (30–49 years) and older (50–75 years) adult populations. Lower HDL‐C and smoking were strongly associated with cIMT in older HIV‐infected individuals. Cumulative cART use of specific drugs was not included in our model because direct comparison of individuals who were exposed and those who were not exposed to a specific drug led to zero‐inflated distribution caused by the high number of individuals who were never exposed to a specific drug.

Interestingly, we found fewer associations of traditional cardiovascular risk factors with the presence of carotid plaques than with cIMT. Future studies are necessary to investigate what factors drive the progression of IMT thickening into mature atherosclerotic plaques. Even more important from a clinical point of view is the process of plaque rupture or erosion that triggers the occurrence of an acute cardiovascular event. We found a high prevalence of carotid plaques in our study population, and there is probably a substantial heterogeneity in the biological origin of these plaques. Our carotid ultrasound data were not able to differentiate between a vulnerable and a stable plaque. Given the increased cardiovascular risk in PLHIV, it is essential to explore which parameters predispose to plaque destabilization and vascular occlusion. Follow‐up data of the 2000HIV cohort will soon be available, which may give us more information about these factors.

In our study, we investigated associations between targeted lipoproteomic measurements and carotid ultrasound findings in PLHIV. HDL particle concentrations and medium and large HDL particle subclasses were negatively associated with cIMT in our study. This observation is in line with other studies in uninfected individuals in whom these inverse associations were found with IMT. 54 , 55 In contrast to these studies, we did not find positive associations with small VLDLs, intermediate‐density lipoproteins, and all classes of LDLs with cIMT. Particle concentrations of medium and large HDLs were both inversely associated with incident myocardial infarction in a study by Holmes et al 56 and in a Mendelian randomization study. 57 However, the protective role of HDL in CVD is controversial. 58 In observational studies, low HDL‐C is associated with incident CVD, but it is thought that this is mostly caused by the inverse relation of HDL‐C with triglyceride‐rich remnant lipoproteins, such as VLDL. In our study, we did not find a statistically significant positive association between carotid plaque presence and VLDL particles, but we observed a positive trend with very small VLDL cholesterol and intermediate‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (Figure [B] and Figure S10B).

Besides lipoprotein biomarkers, we found positive associations of cIMT with several amino acids, glutamine, leucine, isoleucine, and total concentration of branched‐chain amino acids. Branched‐chain amino acids contain total concentrations of leucine, isoleucine, and valine. Increased levels of branched‐chain amino acids were previously linked to metabolic syndrome, atherosclerosis, and CVD in uninfected individuals. 59 , 60 There are conflicting results of the role of glutamine with CVD. A previous study showed that glutamine inhibits NO release, which impairs endothelial function. 61 Glutamine was associated with incident high cIMT after a 6‐year follow‐up and linked with angiography‐based diagnosis of coronary artery disease. 55 Also, in a small study with PLHIV, glutamine was elevated in coronary artery disease cases compared with controls. 62 In contrast, in another study, lower glutamine concentrations were associated with future cardiovascular events with 15 years of follow‐up, and positive associations were found for leucine and isoleucine. 63

Prevention of CVD is important; therefore, we evaluated the use of lipid‐lowering therapy according to European EACS guideline recommendations in our cohort. Only approximately one‐third of individuals with very high risk of developing fatal and nonfatal CVD were using lipid‐lowering medication. Reduction of LDL‐C with a statin decreased the risk of major vascular events, with 21% per 1.0‐mmol/L LDL‐C lowering in people without HIV. 64 Treatment goals with lipid‐lowering therapy were achieved in a small minority of participants in this cohort. In the previous EACS guideline, 65 the LDL‐C treatment goal for high‐risk CVD was 2.0 instead of 1.8 mmol/L, which may partially explain this low observed percentage. No data were available on possible reasons why not all high‐risk participants were using lipid‐lowering treatment. In our analysis, SCORE2 was calculated on the basis of clinical information from patient medical records and lipid measurements from blood drawn at time of inclusion. Therefore, it might be possible that a fraction of participants recently has become eligible for lipid‐lowering therapy, whereas they were not during their last hospital visit. Also, one of the variables in SCORE2 calculations is SBP. This information is retrieved from medical records from a measurement that was conducted within 2 years before time of inclusion. Because we were not informed about the method of measurement, it is possible that measurements were performed during a regular visit in the consulting room, leading to overestimation attributable to white coat hypertension. In addition to reducing the risk of CVD with lipid‐lowering treatment, active counseling in cessation of smoking is vital as more than one‐third of all individuals in very high‐risk and high‐risk groups were active smokers at the time of participation. Cessation of smoking leads to a hazard ratio (HR) reduction for cardiovascular mortality from 2.07 to 1.37 compared with never smokers and an HR reduction for acute coronary events from 1.98 to 1.18. 66 Also, counseling in weight reduction is recommended 67 as we found that 50% with a very high risk of CVD have a BMI of >25 kg/m2.

The main strength of this study is the extensive number of PLHIV who participated in this study, encompassing 4 centers across the Netherlands, distributed equally as academic and nonacademic institutions, and distributed equally as metropolitan and rural. There are also limitations in this present study. First, only cross‐sectional data were available. This design does not allow us to explore which factors contribute to plaque rupture and CVD occurrence. On the basis of our findings, HIV‐specific factors were not associated with cIMT and carotid plaque presence does not necessarily implicate that HIV is not a risk factor for CVD as we did not compare these associations with uninfected individuals. Second, there were more men examined in our study than women. There is a strong skewed distribution of men and women living with HIV in the Netherlands as the main transmission of HIV is men who have sex with men. 68 Our results should be interpreted with caution for women with HIV. Third, ultrasound measurements were performed by 4 clinicians in different centers. It is possible that minor differences in defining a plaque led to minor overreporting or underreporting. However, all clinicians were uniformly trained, and we conducted regular evaluations to maintain the standardization of the measurements. Fourth, evaluation of the biological nature of carotid plaques was not possible with our ultrasound findings, and therefore differentiation between a high‐risk vulnerable and a stable plaque was not possible. Fifth, cumulative drug exposure was not included in our regression model because of the mentioned zero‐inflated distribution by comparing groups who have been exposed to a specific drug and those who were never exposed.

In conclusion, classic risk factors were more associated with higher cIMT than with carotid plaque presence, and more research is needed to elucidate which factors contribute to plaque formation and destabilization, leading to cardiovascular events in PLHIV. We speculate that better insight into factors driving chronic inflammation may help us to better understand presence of these vulnerable plaques. On the basis of the association between traditional cardiovascular risk factors and cIMT, strict management of these risk factors is important, yet lipid‐lowering drugs are not prescribed in a considerable proportion of patients. Therefore, with the increasing life expectancy attributable to effective suppressive cART, we believe that cardiovascular risk management, including prescription of lipid‐lowering therapy and active counseling in smoking cessation, needs to be improved in these patients.
