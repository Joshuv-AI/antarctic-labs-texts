ALARA Without Modern Equipment: Aging Cardiac Catheterization Laboratory Fluoroscopy Infrastructure in Illinois and California
Authors: Joshua H. Arnold, Arnold H. Seto, Mladen I. Vidovich
Published: 2026

The ALARA paradox: When equipment constrains what operators can achieve

The principle of As Low As Reasonably Achievable (ALARA) is the cornerstone of radiation safety in the cardiac catheterization laboratory. Adopted by the International Commission on Radiological Protection and reflected across regulatory frameworks, ALARA requires that radiation exposure to patients and personnel be minimized to the extent practicable, consistent with procedural objectives.1 In practice, however, ALARA has been operationalized almost exclusively through operator behavior, placing onus on the optimization of fluoroscopy time, frame rates, collimation, and angulation, while the dose-reduction capability of the imaging equipment itself receives little to no attention.2,3 Where interventional cardiologists perform fluoroscopically guided procedures over careers spanning decades, cumulative occupational exposure carries recognized risks of cataract, orthopedic injury related to protective equipment, and potential malignancy.4

Modern catheterization laboratory fluoroscopy systems incorporate dose-reduction technologies not present in platforms manufactured a decade or more ago.5 Published comparisons report 30% to 70% reductions in air kerma and dose-area product with contemporary systems compared with predecessors for matched indications and image quality.6 When an operator is working on a legacy platform, a technology-imposed floor constrains what behavioral modification can achieve. This creates what we term the ALARA paradox: physicians are held to a standard of minimizing radiation exposure, yet the infrastructure provided to them may be incapable of achieving exposures attainable with modern equipment.

A population-level view of catheterization laboratory equipment age

To characterize the scope of this problem, we analyzed mandatory publicly accessible radiation equipment registries from Illinois (Illinois Emergency Management Agency) and California (California Department of Public Health). We identified 654 cardiac catheterization laboratory fluoroscopy systems across 183 hospitals. Equipment generation age was estimated as the interval between each platform's first commercial release year and April 2026; Illinois acquisition dates provided calibration against true in-service age.

Median estimated equipment age was 17.0 years in both states. More than half of all registered units (368 of 651; 56.5%) met a ≥15-year legacy threshold, distributed similarly across states, and over 4 in 5 hospitals (151 of 183; 82.5%) operated at least one legacy system (Table 1). The most prevalent platform, the Siemens Artis Zee, first released in 2009, accounted for nearly 1 in 5 units. Platforms released in 2000 or earlier, including systems with estimated ages exceeding 25 years, accounted for 22% of the inventory; 2 units of the GE Advantx (first release 1995) remained in active registration at major academic centers. The Illinois registry uniquely records acquisition dates, allowing methodological calibration. Under actual acquisition dates, the median true equipment age was 8.3 years, and 13.5% of units exceeded 15 years, which is lower than the model release year estimates, as expected from the lag between commercial introduction and hospital purchase. Even under this more conservative measure, more than 40% of Illinois units had been in service for ≥10 years, and the qualitative conclusion is unchanged: midgeneration platforms from the 2004 to 2013 era predominate, and a meaningful fraction of catheterization laboratories operate systems that predate the routine availability of contemporary dose-reduction technology. At the facility level, a hospital need only have 1 such unit to expose patients and operators in that room to the increased radiation doses of an older-generation platform.

Institutional responsibility and the limits of behavior-focused ALARA

These findings have direct implications for how radiation safety is framed and enforced in interventional cardiology. ALARA is a population-level principle that should reflect what current technology permits. When aging infrastructure imposes a floor on achievable exposure that operator technique cannot overcome, the "reasonably achievable" benchmark shifts, and responsibility for that gap extends beyond the individual physician to the institution providing the equipment.

This matters for both operators and patients. For interventional cardiologists with high-volume practices, even modest per-procedure dose differences accumulate meaningfully over a career.7,8 The orthopedic hazards of prolonged lead apron wear and the lens exposure associated with scatter radiation are well-documented occupational risks.4 For patients, the absence of any universal in-service performance criteria for interventional fluoroscopy systems, which is a gap that stands in contrast to the regulatory scrutiny applied to CT scanners following high-profile overdose events,9 means that patients undergoing procedures in legacy-equipped rooms face exposures that are significantly higher than what modern equipment would deliver.

Contemporary interventional angiography platforms represent multimillion-dollar capital investments per unit, and full fleet modernization is a large-scale institutional project. The absence of any regulatory mandate for age-based equipment replacement creates a structural barrier that operator-level training cannot address. Unlike pharmaceuticals, which carry expiration dates, or implantable devices subject to mandatory postmarket surveillance, radiation-producing equipment in the United States faces no mandatory retirement requirements and may remain in active registration indefinitely, should basic maintenance fees and services be provided.

Implications and call to action

The consistency of findings between 2 structurally different states suggests that aging catheterization laboratory infrastructure reflects a broader national pattern, not a regional anomaly. These findings represent the first registry-based characterization of catheterization laboratory fluoroscopy equipment age at a population level in the United States and provide empirical grounding for several policy considerations.

First, institutional investment in imaging infrastructure should be recognized explicitly as a component of ALARA compliance, not merely a capital budgeting decision. Second, radiation safety curricula and society competency statements should address equipment capability alongside operator technique, acknowledging that the 2 are complementary, not interchangeable, determinants of dose. Third, the feasibility of registry-based equipment age monitoring demonstrated here supports the development of infrastructure-aware benchmarks for ALARA implementation, analogous to the dose reference levels established for CT. Fourth, equity analyses incorporating hospital ownership type and geography are warranted; prior research has documented disparities in access to modern imaging technology by facility type,10 and whether legacy-equipped rooms disproportionately serve higher-risk patient populations deserves examination.

ALARA cannot be fully realized through behavioral modification alone when the equipment provided to operators imposes a dose floor that technique cannot overcome. Recognizing institutional infrastructure as a complementary and necessary lever for radiation safety, and building policy frameworks that reflect this reality, is the logical next step for the field.
