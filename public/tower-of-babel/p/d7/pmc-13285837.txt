Direct oral anticoagulants in portal vein thrombosis in patients with cirrhosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Andrea Boccatonda, Alice Brighenti, Chiara Simion, Angela Napolitano, Carla Serra, Paolo Simioni, Elena Campello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe optimal anticoagulant strategy for portal vein thrombosis (PVT) in patients with cirrhosis remains uncertain, particularly regarding the role of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) compared with vitamin K antagonists (VKAs), low-molecular-weight heparin, or no anticoagulation. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies evaluating DOAC therapy in patients with cirrhosis with PVT. Random-effects models were used to estimate pooled risk ratios (RRs). A total of 21 studies including 21 506 patients with cirrhosis with PVT were analyzed. Among them, 2099 patients were treated with DOACs. Compared with VKAs, DOAC therapy was associated with higher rates of complete PVT recanalization (RR, 3.49; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.06-5.93) and lower risk of PVT progression (RR, 0.19; 95% CI, 0.04-0.78), with no significant difference in major bleeding (RR, 1.09; 95% CI, 0.79-1.51). DOACs were also associated with a lower risk of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH; RR, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.30-0.80). Compared with no anticoagulation, DOAC therapy was associated with lower mortality (RR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.71-0.84) without increased bleeding risk. DOAC benefit on recanalization was more pronounced in studies including only patients with Child-Pugh class A or B, whereas safety outcomes remained consistent across cirrhosis severity strata. In patients with cirrhosis with PVT, DOAC therapy is associated with improved complete recanalization and reduced PVT progression compared with VKAs, without increased bleeding risk and with lower risk of ICH. These findings support the use of DOACs in select patients, although randomized trials are needed to confirm these results.

Introduction

Portal vein thrombosis (PVT) is a frequent thrombotic complication in patients with liver cirrhosis and is associated with adverse clinical outcomes, including portal hypertension progression, hepatic decompensation, and increased mortality.1, 2, 3, 4 Anticoagulation has been shown to promote portal vein recanalization and to reduce thrombus progression in selected patients with cirrhosis with PVT.1,5,6 However, the optimal anticoagulant strategy remains uncertain. Vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) and low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) have historically been used, but their application in cirrhosis is limited by several factors, including unreliable international normalized ratio monitoring, frequent dose adjustments, parenteral administration (for LMWH), and concerns regarding bleeding risk in patients with portal hypertension and varices.1,6,7 Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) offer several theoretical and practical advantages over traditional anticoagulants, including fixed dosing, fewer drug-drug interactions, and no requirement for routine laboratory monitoring.6,7 Evidence supporting the use of DOACs in cirrhotic PVT has emerged primarily from observational studies and small prospective cohorts.3,7, 8, 9, 10 Individual studies have suggested that DOACs may be associated with higher rates of portal vein recanalization and comparable or reduced bleeding risk compared with VKAs or no anticoagulation, but results have been heterogeneous, sample sizes limited, and outcome definitions variable.11, 12, 13, 14 Given the growing off-label use of DOACs in this setting and the absence of adequately powered randomized controlled trials (RCTs), a rigorous synthesis of the available evidence is needed to inform clinical decision-making.11 Therefore, we performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the efficacy and safety of DOACs in patients with cirrhosis with PVT, comparing DOAC therapy with VKAs, no anticoagulation, and LMWH. The primary outcomes were complete portal vein recanalization and major bleeding, whereas secondary outcomes included any recanalization, partial recanalization, PVT progression, any bleeding, gastrointestinal bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), and mortality.

Methods

Study design and reporting standards

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) 2020 guidelines.15 The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database (registration number CRD420261280073).

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed to identify studies evaluating DOACs in patients with cirrhosis and PVT. Electronic databases were searched (including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Scopus) from database inception through 30 November 2025, without lower date restrictions. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings and free-text terms related to “portal vein thrombosis,” “cirrhosis,” “direct oral anticoagulants,” “DOAC,” “rivaroxaban,” “apixaban,” “edoxaban,” “dabigatran,” “warfarin,” and “anticoagulation,” The full search strategies for each database are provided in the supplemental detailed search strategy.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they met the following criteria: adult patients (aged ≥18 years) with liver cirrhosis; patients with radiologically confirmed PVT, or reporting separate data for PVT when mixed indications were present; treatment with ≥1 DOACs; studies including mixed anticoagulation indications (eg, atrial fibrillation or venous thromboembolism) were eligible provided that extractable data for patients with cirrhosis receiving DOACs were available; reporting at least 1 outcome of interest; RCTs, prospective studies, or retrospective cohort studies. Exclusion criteria were case reports or case series; studies without cirrhotic populations or without extractable cirrhosis-specific data; studies exclusively evaluating malignant PVT (eg, tumor thrombus); reviews, editorials, or letters without original data; and conference abstracts.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were complete PVT recanalization and major bleeding. Secondary outcomes included any PVT recanalization, partial PVT recanalization, PVT progression, any bleeding, gastrointestinal bleeding, ICH, and mortality. Major bleeding was preferentially defined according to the criteria of the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis. Gastrointestinal bleeding was classified as major bleeding only when explicitly reported, and additional sensitivity analyses were performed excluding studies in which proxy definitions were applied.

Data extraction

Two investigators independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias of observational studies was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool. Randomized studies were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool. Assessments were performed independently by 2 reviewers.

Statistical analysis

Pooled risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the Mantel-Haenszel random-effects model. Meta-analyses were conducted when at least 2 studies provided comparable outcome data. When only 2 studies were available, pooled estimates were interpreted cautiously, with particular attention to clinical comparability and consistency of effect direction. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. Separate analyses were conducted for:1.DOACs vs VKAs (PVT-specific studies);2.DOACs vs no anticoagulation (PVT-specific studies); and3.DOAC vs LMWH.

When LMWH and VKAs were combined within a single comparator arm and separate effect estimates were not available, such studies were included in qualitative synthesis but not pooled in comparative meta-analyses. Prespecified sensitivity analyses included exclusion of proxy bleeding outcomes, restriction to Child-Pugh A and B populations, and restriction to works including all Child-Pugh stages. Publication bias was not formally assessed due to the limited number of studies per outcome.

Risk of bias assessment

Among the 20 observational studies included, most were judged to have a serious overall risk of bias, mainly driven by confounding and the nonrandomized design, reflecting potential confounding by indication (Figure 2). Moderate risk was frequently observed in domains related to participant selection and deviations from intended interventions, whereas domains related to classification of interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting were generally rated as low risk. Overall, only a limited number of studies were classified as having moderate risk of bias. Study characteristics

Across the 21 included studies, 21 506 patients with cirrhosis were included in the PVT-specific analyses. Indeed, several studies included mixed cirrhotic populations receiving anticoagulation for different indications, within which PVT patients were analyzed as predefined subgroups or were identifiable from study cohorts.8,10,16,30 Among anticoagulated patients, 2099 were treated with DOACs. Follow-up ranged from 3 months in smaller retrospective safety cohorts to ∼38 months in large real-world or prospective studies, with most studies reporting follow-up between 6 and 24 months. Across studies, the mean or median age ranged from ∼47 to 71 years. The proportion of male patients varied between 50% and 97%, reflecting the known male predominance of advanced chronic liver disease in several cohorts. When reported, the distribution of Child-Pugh classes ranged from ∼27% to 81% for class A, 19% to 59% for class B, and 0% to 27% for class C. The Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score, when available, generally ranged between 9 and 15, indicating that most included populations had relatively compensated cirrhosis. Acute decompensation was variably reported across studies. Ascites was reported in ∼30% to 40% of patients in several cohorts. Reported bilirubin levels were typically between 0.9 and 2.1 mg/dL, albumin levels between 3.0 and 3.7 g/dL, and international normalized ratio (INR) values between 1.2 and 1.6. Patient serum creatinine values were usually close to normal (∼0.9-1.1 mg/dL), whereas platelet counts were frequently reduced, reflecting portal hypertension. Overall, most studies enrolled patients with compensated or moderately decompensated cirrhosis, and individuals with severe acute decompensation or acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) were rarely represented.10,13,20,27 A detailed overview of the baseline characteristics of the included studies is provided in supplemental Tables 1-4, whereas the baseline liver biochemical parameters are summarized in supplemental Table 5.

DOAC vs VKA

PRIMARY OUTCOMES

Complete portal vein recanalization

Three studies, including 188 patients (122 receiving DOACs and 66 receiving VKAs), reported data on complete recanalization of PVT. Complete recanalization occurred in 52 patients treated with DOACs and in 12 patients treated with VKAs. In the pooled analysis, DOAC therapy was associated with a significantly higher likelihood of complete recanalization compared with VKAs (RR, 3.49; 95% CI, 2.06-5.93; P < .00001). Major bleeding

Seven studies, including 3373 patients (1714 receiving DOACs and 1659 receiving VKAs), reported data on major bleeding. A total of 235 major bleeding events occurred in the DOAC group and 189 in the VKA group. In the pooled analysis, DOAC therapy was not associated with a statistically significant difference in the risk of major bleeding compared with VKAs (RR, 1.09; 95% CI, 0.92-1.29; P = .34). No evidence of statistical heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 0%; P = .779,14,17,22,23,26,27; Figure 3).

Partial portal vein recanalization

Three studies, including 188 patients (122 receiving DOACs and 66 receiving VKAs), reported data on partial recanalization of PVT. Partial recanalization occurred in 27 patients treated with DOACs, and in 6 patients treated with VKAs. In the pooled random-effects analysis, DOAC therapy showed a numerically higher rate of partial recanalization, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (RR, 2.12; 95% CI, 0.87-5.16; P = .10). No significant heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 0%; P = .9417,22,25; Figure 4).

PVT progression

Three studies, including 170 patients (108 receiving DOACs and 62 receiving VKAs), reported data on PVT progression. A total of 2 events occurred in the DOAC group, and 18 in the VKA group. In the pooled analysis, treatment with DOACs was associated with a significantly lower risk of PVT extension than with VKAs (RR, 0.19; 95% CI, 0.04-0.78; P = .02). No statistical heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 0%; P = .4014,17,25; Figure 4).

Any bleeding

Seven studies comprising 3373 patients (1714 receiving DOACs and 1659 receiving VKAs) reported data on any bleeding. A total of 364 bleeding events occurred in the DOAC group and 347 in the VKA group. Treatment with DOACs was not associated with a significant difference in the risk of any bleeding compared with VKAs (RR, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.85-1.09). There was no evidence of statistical heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 0%; P = .76). Overall, the pooled estimate showed no significant difference between treatment groups (z = 0.58; P = .569,14,17,22,23,27; Figure 4).

Gastrointestinal bleeding

Three studies, including 3209 patients (1641 treated with DOACs and 1568 with VKAs), reported data on gastrointestinal bleeding. A total of 230 events occurred in the DOAC group and 180 in the VKA group. In the pooled analysis, DOAC therapy was not associated with a significant difference in the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding compared with VKAs (RR, 1.09; 95% CI, 0.92-1.30; P = .33). No statistical heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 0%; P = .6823,26,27; Figure 4).

ICH

Two studies, including 3148 patients (1598 receiving DOACs and 1550 receiving VKAs), reported data on ICH. A total of 24 events occurred in the DOAC group and 47 in the VKA group. In the pooled analysis, DOAC therapy was associated with a significantly lower risk of ICH compared with VKAs (RR, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.30-0.80; P = .004). No evidence of heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 0%; P = .4223,26; Figure 4).

Mortality

Three studies, including 3164 patients (1611 receiving DOACs and 1553 receiving VKAs), reported data on all-cause mortality. A total of 398 deaths occurred in the DOAC group and 502 in the VKA group. In the pooled analysis, treatment with DOACs was not associated with a statistically significant difference in mortality compared with VKAs (RR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.13-1.93; P = .32). Moderate heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 69%; P = .0414,23,25; Figure 4).

ANY PORTAL VEIN RECANALIZATION

Studies including all Child-Pugh classes (studies on only A/B classes excluded)

When studies restricted to Child-Pugh A or B patients were excluded,17,22 DOAC therapy remained not associated with a higher risk of any bleeding compared with VKAs (RR, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.85-1.09; I2 = 0%). No significant difference in any portal vein recanalization was observed between DOACs and VKAs (RR, 1.86; 95% CI, 0.20-17.28; I2 = 66%). Moreover, no significant difference was observed in PVT progression between DOACs and VKAs (RR, 0.36; 95% CI, 0.04-2.97). DOAC therapy was not associated with a higher risk of major bleeding compared with VKAs (RR, 1.09; 95% CI, 0.91-1.29; I2 = 0%; supplemental Figures 7-10; Table 1).

Certainty of the evidence

Overall, the certainty of the evidence ranged from very low to moderate across outcomes (Table 2). Most included studies were observational and therefore susceptible to confounding and selection bias, although 1 RCT contributed to the evidence base. For the comparison between DOACs and no anticoagulation, certainty was judged very low for mortality and portal vein recanalization outcomes, and low to very low for bleeding outcomes, depending on precision and consistency. For the comparison between DOACs and VKAs, certainty was very low for mortality, any recanalization, and partial recanalization, whereas certainty was judged low to moderate for complete recanalization and low for PVT extension and bleeding outcomes because effect estimates were more consistent and, in some cases, showed a large magnitude of effect. Narrative synthesis on direct comparison between DOACs

The retrospective study by Zhou et al evaluated the efficacy and safety of 2 DOACs in patients with cirrhosis with PVT, including 94 patients (rivaroxaban, n = 52; dabigatran, n = 42).21 Any PVT recanalization occurred in 75.0% of patients treated with rivaroxaban, and 78.6% with dabigatran, whereas complete recanalization was observed in 46.2% and 40.5%, respectively.21 Mortality was low in both groups (5.8% vs 4.8%).21 Bleeding events occurred in 17.3% of rivaroxaban-treated patients and 14.3% of those receiving dabigatran, with major bleeding reported in 5.8% and 2.4%, respectively.21 Moreover, a recent real-world matched cohort presented at the annual meeting of the American Society of Hematology in 2025 compared apixaban and rivaroxaban in patients with cirrhosis with PVT and reported no significant difference in effectiveness or bleeding outcomes, although CIs were wide and follow-up was limited.31 As a conference abstract, these findings were not included in quantitative synthesis.

Discussion

In our meta-analysis, DOAC therapy in patients with cirrhosis with PVT was associated with a favorable balance between efficacy and safety. DOACs were associated with higher rates of portal vein recanalization, particularly complete recanalization compared with VKAs, no increase in overall bleeding risk, and a lower mortality compared with no anticoagulation. Our findings are broadly consistent with an updated systematic review comparing DOACs with traditional anticoagulation (VKAs/LMWH), which reported improved recanalization with DOACs and no clear increase in bleeding.32 This synthesis adds to that body of evidence by emphasizing clinically actionable comparative end points (mortality, recanalization, and bleeding) across pragmatic comparisons (DOACs vs no anticoagulation; DOACs vs VKAs) and by incorporating newer large-scale real-world evidence.23,26 The observed mortality benefit aligns with the direction of effect reported in large database-based analyses (the TriNetX-based retrospective cohort study), with a particularly favorable signal in patients receiving DOACs.23 Moreover, our pooled estimates indicate substantially higher rates of portal vein recanalization with DOACs compared with VKAs, with the most consistent signal observed for complete recanalization. That result is consistent with earlier meta-analytic signals and with the growing clinical experience summarized in contemporary reviews.32, 33, 34 Our findings are consistent with a recent prospective pilot study evaluating apixaban for acute splanchnic vein thrombosis, in which most patients had cirrhosis and a radiological response rate (complete or partial recanalization) of ∼71% after 3 months of therapy.35 From a thrombosis perspective, these findings suggest that DOACs may offer at least comparable (and potentially superior) antithrombotic efficacy in the splanchnic venous circulation despite the altered pharmacokinetic and hemostatic milieu of cirrhosis. In our analyses, DOACs did not increase overall bleeding compared with no anticoagulation or VKAs. Moreover, DOACs were not associated with an increased risk of major bleeding compared with VKAs. These results align with the emerging view that DOACs can be used safely in selected patients with cirrhosis, especially those with compensated disease, provided that portal hypertension–related bleeding risk is assessed and managed (eg, variceal screening/prophylaxis when indicated).33,34

Overall, the certainty of the evidence ranged from very low to moderate across outcomes, reflecting the predominance of observational studies and the potential for residual confounding. Nevertheless, several key findings, particularly the association between DOAC therapy and complete portal vein recanalization, showed consistent effect estimates across studies. Sensitivity analyses showed results consistent with the primary pooled estimates, supporting the stability of the observed associations. Notably, sensitivity analyses excluding studies in which gastrointestinal bleeding was used as a proxy for major bleeding yielded similar results, suggesting that differences in bleeding definitions across studies did not materially influence the overall safety estimates.

Our sensitivity analyses, stratified by Child-Pugh classes, showed similar safety signals, with no increase in bleeding risk associated with DOAC therapy, although efficacy estimates in these subgroup analyses were limited by smaller sample sizes. Recent clinical guidance documents increasingly endorse anticoagulation consideration in cirrhotic PVT in appropriately selected patients, although they emphasize individualized decision-making and careful bleeding risk assessment.36,37 More recently, expert guidance and practice updates supported anticoagulation in recent or progressive PVT, particularly when thrombus burden is clinically meaningful, and acknowledged the increasing use of DOACs.7 Our findings provide quantitative support for this direction, suggesting that DOACs may offer similar efficacy and practical advantages over VKAs without compromising safety.

An important observation across studies is the considerable heterogeneity in DOAC dosing strategies. Although several cohorts used standard therapeutic regimens, dose reductions were frequently adopted in patients with cirrhosis, reflecting real-world clinical caution in the setting of portal hypertension and bleeding risk. This variability in dosing may partially contribute to the heterogeneity observed across efficacy and safety outcomes.

Limitations of the study

Most included studies were observational, raising the possibility of residual confounding and selection bias (eg, preferential DOAC use in patients with less advanced liver disease). Outcome definitions varied across studies. To maximize inclusivity and clinical interpretability, prespecified proxy end points were incorporated in selected analyses (eg, gastrointestinal bleeding as a proxy for overall bleeding). Although sensitivity analyses excluding these proxy definitions yielded similar results, some degree of outcome misclassification cannot be excluded. The number of studies available for several outcomes was limited, restricting formal assessment of publication bias and contributing to imprecision in some pooled estimates. Finally, several clinically relevant variables, including cirrhosis severity (Child-Pugh class), presence and management of portal hypertension–related complications (such as varices), and DOAC dosing strategies, were inconsistently reported across studies, precluding more granular subgroup analyses.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that DOACs may represent a reasonable first-line anticoagulant option in carefully selected patients with cirrhosis with PVT. In this meta-analysis, DOAC therapy was associated with higher rates of complete PVT recanalization and a lower risk of PVT progression than with VKAs, without an increased risk of major or overall bleeding. Moreover, DOACs were associated with a lower risk of ICH and reduced mortality compared with no anticoagulation. These findings support the growing use of DOACs in patients with compensated cirrhosis when bleeding risk is appropriately assessed and managed. However, because most available evidence derives from observational studies, adequately powered RCTs and prospective registries are needed to confirm these results and to better define the optimal anticoagulant strategy and dosing in cirrhotic PVT.
