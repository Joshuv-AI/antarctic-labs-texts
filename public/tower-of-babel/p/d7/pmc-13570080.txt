Donor Site Morbidity After Talar Mosaicplasty: Arthroscopic Medial Femoral Condyle vs Mini-Open Lateral Femoral Condyle Harvesting
Authors: Amir Sabaghzadeh, Saeed Vosughi, Meisam Jafari Kafiabadi, Saber Barazandeh Rad, Babak Toloue Ghamari, Mohammad Pourmahmoudian, Shobeir Rostami Abousaidi, Pedram Khalighinejad, Mehrdad Sadighi, Adel Ebrahimpour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLTs) are challenging injuries that may require osteochondral autograft transfer for large, cystic, or refractory defects. Although mosaicplasty can restore hyaline cartilage and subchondral bone, donor site morbidity remains a concern. This study compared knee donor site morbidity and ankle functional outcomes after 2 technique–donor site combinations used for talar mosaicplasty: arthroscopic medial femoral condyle harvesting and mini-open lateral femoral condyle harvesting.Methods:In this retrospective comparative cohort study, 40 patients with OLTs who underwent osteochondral autograft transfer were available for final analysis. Nineteen patients underwent arthroscopic harvesting from the medial femoral condyle, and 21 underwent mini-open harvesting from the lateral femoral condyle. The mean follow-up was 28.8 months. Baseline characteristics, lesion morphology, functional outcomes, pain scores, and postoperative complications were compared between groups. Donor site morbidity was assessed using the Lysholm score. Secondary outcomes included the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score, Foot and Ankle Outcome Score, Foot and Ankle Ability Measure, visual analog scale pain score, and complication rates.Results:Baseline demographics, follow-up duration, and measured lesion characteristics were broadly similar between groups; however, the small sample size limits interpretation of baseline comparability. With the available numbers, no significant between-group differences were detected in Lysholm, Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score, Foot and Ankle Outcome Score, Foot and Ankle Ability Measure activities of daily living or sport subscales, or visual analog scale pain scores (all P > .05). Overall, complications occurred in 5 of 40 patients (12.5%): 2 of 19 patients in the arthroscopic medial femoral condyle group and 3 of 21 patients in the mini-open lateral femoral condyle group, with no statistically significant between-group difference in complication rates (Fisher exact test, P > .99).Conclusion:In this retrospective cohort, no statistically significant differences were detected between arthroscopic medial femoral condyle and mini-open lateral femoral condyle harvesting for talar mosaicplasty. However, because the surgical approach and donor condyle differed simultaneously, this study cannot isolate the independent effect of approach alone. The limited sample size and wide CIs preclude conclusions regarding equivalence, superiority, or noninferiority.Level of Evidence:Level III, retrospective comparative cohort study.

Introduction

Osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLTs) are injuries of the articular cartilage and subchondral bone, primarily affecting the talar dome. 1 OLTs are frequently associated with acute ankle injuries or repeated microtrauma in adults.1,2 These lesions provide a considerable therapeutic challenge owing to the limited intrinsic healing capacity of hyaline cartilage. 3 Because of the high congruency and contact stresses of the ankle joint, osteochondral defects significantly alter gait mechanics and weightbearing tolerance, often leading to persistent disability.1,4,5 Consequently, if left untreated, symptomatic OLTs can lead to chronic pain, functional disability, and the progressive development of secondary osteoarthritis.2,5,6

Management strategies are primarily guided by lesion size and cystic changes, with consideration of other factors such as lesion containment and location.7-9 Although marrow stimulation is effective for modest abnormalities, it produces fibrocartilage that frequently degrades with time. 8 Consequently, for large cystic lesions or unsuccessful first treatments, osteochondral autograft transfer (OATS) is the preferred therapeutic choice or a feasible salvage technique.10,11 The transplantation of a viable osteochondral unit using OATS reinstates hyaline cartilage and structural integrity. 9 The treatment, originally executed through open techniques, has transitioned to all-arthroscopic methods aimed at reducing soft tissue morbidity and expediting recovery. 12

The ideal harvesting method remains debated. Arthroscopic OATS offers a minimally invasive option, but its limited visualization and smaller working surface make it technically challenging to achieve a perpendicular trajectory.13,14 Currently, direct clinical comparisons between arthroscopic and mini-open strategies are limited, and the trade-off between invasiveness and precision remains unclear in the literature.

The optimal harvest strategy for talar mosaicplasty remains uncertain. The relevant clinical question is not limited to the surgical exposure itself, but also includes the donor condyle, graft number, graft burden, ability to obtain perpendicular osteochondral plugs, lesion location, cystic morphology, and surgeon experience. In the present cohort, arthroscopic harvesting was performed from the medial femoral condyle, whereas mini-open harvesting was performed from the lateral femoral condyle. Therefore, this study compares 2 real-world technique–donor site combinations rather than isolating the independent effect of surgical approach alone. The primary aim was to compare final knee donor site morbidity, assessed using the Lysholm score, between arthroscopic medial femoral condyle harvesting and mini-open lateral femoral condyle harvesting for talar mosaicplasty. Secondary aims were to compare knee-related outcomes, ankle-related outcomes, pain, and postoperative complications.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Ethical Considerations

This retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients who underwent osteochondral autograft transfer for osteochondral lesions of the talus between March 2018 and March 2024 at a tertiary orthopaedic referral center. Patients were identified through the hospital information system and orthopaedic registry and were screened according to predefined eligibility criteria. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Patient Selection

Patients were retrospectively and consecutively screened through the hospital information system and orthopaedic registry. All patients who underwent osteochondral autograft transfer for osteochondral lesions of the talus during the study period were assessed against the predefined eligibility criteria. Patients who met all inclusion criteria and none of the exclusion criteria were included in the final analysis.

Inclusion criteria were (1) age 16-50 years; (2) full-thickness osteochondral lesions of the talus (International Cartilage Repair Society [ICRS] grade III-IV) confirmed by computed tomographic (CT) scan; (3) failed nonoperative management and decision for autologous osteochondral transplantation (mosaicplasty); and (4) at least 12 months of follow-up.

Exclusion criteria were (1) prior surgery on the same knee or ankle; (2) inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis; (3) severe ankle osteoarthritis (Kellgren-Lawrence grade ≥3); (4) uncorrected lower limb malalignment; and (5) neurogenic conditions such as poliomyelitis or cerebral palsy.

Patients were included if they met all criteria and excluded if any criteria were met. No randomization was performed. Patients were categorized according to the technique and donor condyle used during the index procedure. Therefore, the comparison reflects 2 real-world technique–donor site combinations rather than a randomized comparison of surgical exposure alone.

Preoperative imaging and lesion assessment

Preoperative lesion assessment was performed using standard ankle radiographs and preoperative CT. CT was available for all 40 included patients and was used as the principal modality for operative planning and lesion measurement, including lesion localization using the 9-zone grid, measurement of lesion length and width, assessment of subchondral cystic change, evaluation of lesion containment, and estimation of the number and orientation of the required osteochondral plugs. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was available in 7 of 40 patients and had been obtained at outside centers before referral; therefore, MRI was used only as an adjunct to evaluate cartilage integrity and marrow signal changes and was not used for lesion size comparison. Because CT was available for all patients and MRI may overestimate lesion size, lesion measurements and between-group lesion size comparisons were based on CT rather than MRI. Routine diagnostic ankle arthroscopy was not performed before ankle exposure. Lesion surface area was approximated using the elliptical formula: length × width × 0.79. Preoperative CT images were reviewed independently by 2 fellowship-trained foot and ankle orthopaedic surgeons (A.S. and A.E.), and disagreements regarding lesion location, lesion size, cystic change, or containment were resolved by consensus.

Surgical Technique and Grouping

Patients were categorized into 2 technique–donor site groups according to the harvesting technique and donor condyle used during the index procedure: the arthroscopic medial femoral condyle (MFC) group and the mini-open lateral femoral condyle (LFC) group. The choice of technique was not randomized and reflected lesion location, required graft trajectory, expected graft burden, technical feasibility, and surgeon judgment. Procedures were performed under general or spinal anesthesia with the patient in the supine position and a pneumatic tourniquet. All procedures, including knee graft harvesting, ankle exposure, recipient-site preparation, and osteochondral plug implantation, were performed by a single fellowship-trained foot and ankle orthopaedic surgeon with experience in ankle reconstruction and knee arthroscopy. The lesion bed was prepared by drilling to expose healthy, bleeding subchondral bone. Representative intraoperative images of both knee harvesting techniques are shown in Figure 1.

(B) Arthroscopic harvesting from the nonweightbearing medial femoral condyle using standard arthroscopic visualization.

Routine primary ankle arthroscopy was not performed. Ankle exposure was selected according to the preoperative CT-based lesion location, lesion size, cystic morphology, and the need for perpendicular access for osteochondral plug implantation. For medial talar lesions requiring malleolar osteotomy, a medial curved incision was used. A guide pin was inserted from medial to lateral, exiting approximately 2 mm lateral to the axilla of the tibial plafond at an angle of approximately 30° to the long axis of the tibia. After protecting the posterior tibial tendon and neurovascular structures, medial malleolar osteotomy was performed using an oscillating saw and completed with an osteotome to avoid iatrogenic injury to the talar cartilage. The medial malleolus was then retracted distally to obtain wide access to the talar dome.

For lateral talar lesions requiring lateral exposure, a lateral curved incision was used. After protecting the superficial branch of the peroneal nerve, the anterior distal tibiofibular ligaments were incised, and fibular osteotomy was performed approximately 3 cm proximal to the ankle joint line using an oscillating saw. The lateral malleolus was retracted externally to allow visualization of the lateral talar dome. The same osteochondral plug implantation principles were then applied for lateral lesions.

The lesion was identified and probed after exposure. Stable native cartilage and fibrocartilaginous tissue were not routinely debrided before graft insertion. When a large loose osteochondral fragment was encountered, it was excised. The number of plugs was determined using preoperative CT planning and intraoperative sizing. Recipient tunnels were created using the OATS recipient instruments and were made approximately 2 mm deeper than the harvested osteochondral plugs. The harvested plugs were inserted sequentially and gently impacted with the standard OATS tamp until the cartilage surface was flush with the surrounding native talar cartilage. For larger defects, plugs were placed in a mosaic pattern to maximize coverage. Ankle range of motion was assessed intraoperatively to confirm graft congruity, graft stability, and absence of mechanical impingement (Figure 2).

(D) Postoperative radiograph confirming the restoration of the joint line.

Medial malleolar osteotomies were fixed with two or three 4.0-mm cannulated screws placed perpendicular to the osteotomy line. Lateral malleolar/fibular osteotomies were fixed with plate fixation. The ankle was irrigated, hemostasis was achieved, and the wound was closed in layers. A sterile dressing and short-leg splint were applied.

Procedure-related cost was considered descriptively based on the institutional cost of equipment and operating room setup required for each harvesting technique, rather than patient-level total hospitalization charges. Patient-level hospital bills were not compared because they were influenced by length of stay, medications, room type, and other non–procedure-related factors.

No knee donor site backfilling was performed in either group.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Postoperative admission followed the institutional protocol after talar mosaicplasty with malleolar or fibular osteotomy and was used for pain control, wound monitoring, early neurovascular assessment, and postoperative mobilization planning. A standardized postoperative protocol was used for all patients. Ankles were immobilized in a cast or splint for 2 weeks until suture removal. A controlled ankle motion (CAM) boot was then worn for 6 weeks to aid graft integration. Range of motion exercises started at week 4. Partial weightbearing was allowed from week 6, increasing to full weight at week 8, based on pain and evidence of healing. Impact activities and sports return were delayed until at least 6 months postsurgery.

The ipsilateral knee was managed with a compressive dressing after graft harvest. No knee brace was routinely used. Knee range of motion exercises were allowed as tolerated from the early postoperative period, whereas weightbearing progression was primarily determined by the ankle reconstruction protocol.

Outcome Measures

Data were collected from electronic medical records using standardized forms to document the study demographic characteristics, with data entry performed by an independent orthopaedic reviewer who was not involved in surgical procedures or postoperative management. At final follow-up, knee donor site morbidity was assessed using a combination of patient-reported knee outcome scores, donor knee clinical examination, and recorded donor knee complications. Knee-related patient-reported outcomes included the Lysholm score and the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS). Ankle-related patient-reported outcomes included the Foot and Ankle Outcome Score (FAOS), Foot and Ankle Ability Measure (FAAM) activities of daily living (ADL) and sport subscales, and visual analog scale (VAS) pain score. The Lysholm score was used because it captures knee-specific symptoms relevant to donor site morbidity, including pain, swelling, instability, locking, stair climbing, and squatting. Donor-knee clinical assessment at routine follow-up included inspection for swelling or effusion, assessment of knee range of motion, donor site tenderness, anterior knee pain, crepitus, and extensor mechanism function. Donor-knee complications, including hemarthrosis, stiffness, persistent donor site pain, infection, and need for additional treatment, were recorded separately from ankle-site complications.

Donor site morbidity was primarily assessed using the Lysholm score and by reviewing recorded donor knee symptoms and complications, including donor site pain, swelling, hemarthrosis, stiffness, infection, and need for additional treatment. Pain was assessed using the VAS. Postoperative complications, including infection, nonunion, ankle stiffness, hemarthrosis, and donor site pain, were recorded. Data were collected during routine clinical visits or, when information was incomplete or missing, by telephone to address gaps and request a clinic follow-up assessment; this approach was applied consistently across both groups.

Missing Data

Among the 40 patients included in the final analysis, final follow-up data for the primary outcome were available in all patients. No patient was excluded or lost to follow-up after inclusion in the final cohort. Patients with incomplete outcome information before final assessment were not included in the final analytic cohort.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR) for quantitative variables, depending on the results of the Shapiro-Wilk test of normality, and as frequencies for qualitative variables. The independent t test (or nonparametric equivalent) was used to compare continuous variables, the χ 2 test was used for categorical variables, and Fisher exact test was used when expected cell counts were small. A 2-sided P value of less than .05 was considered statistically significant. Ninety-five percent CIs were reported for mean differences in continuous outcomes and for complication rate estimates. No adjustment for multiplicity was applied across secondary outcome comparisons; therefore, all secondary outcome comparisons were considered exploratory. Sex-stratified analyses were performed post hoc and are presented descriptively. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because the sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients treated during the study period.

Established minimum clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds were considered when interpreting the clinical relevance of between-group differences. However, because preoperative patient-reported outcome scores were unavailable, individual patient-level achievement of MCID could not be calculated. Therefore, the Lysholm score was analyzed as a final follow-up between group outcome rather than as a change from baseline outcome.

Results

Of 48 consecutive patients screened during the study period, 8 were not included in the final analysis. In the arthroscopic MFC group, 5 patients were not included: 3 were excluded because of prior surgery or other eligibility-related problems, and 2 were lost to follow-up before final outcome assessment. In the mini-open LFC group, 3 patients were not included: 2 were excluded because of eligibility-related problems, and 1 was lost to follow-up before final outcome assessment. Therefore, 40 patients were included in the final analysis: 19 in the arthroscopic MFC group and 21 in the mini-open LFC group. No patient was excluded after inclusion in the final cohort, and final follow-up data for the primary outcome were available for all 40 included patients.

Postoperative Complications

Overall, postoperative complications occurred in 5 of 40 patients (12.5%; 95% CI, 5.5%-26.1%). In the arthroscopic MFC group, 2 of 19 patients had complications (10.5%; 95% CI, 2.9%-31.4%), both involving the knee donor site: 1 knee hemarthrosis on postoperative day 3 and 1 knee stiffness at 6 weeks. Both resolved or improved with nonoperative treatment. In the mini-open LFC group, 3 of 21 patients had complications (14.3%; 95% CI, 5.0%-34.6%): 1 ankle operative-site deep infection at 3 weeks, 1 ankle swelling at 6 weeks, and 1 persistent donor knee pain beginning at 3 months. The deep infection resolved after irrigation, debridement, and antibiotic therapy without further sequelae. The patient with persistent donor knee pain continued to report mild symptoms at 12 months and final follow-up, with a final VAS pain score of 2 or 3. The between-group difference in overall complication rates was not statistically significant using Fisher exact test (2/19 vs 3/21; P > .99).

Medial malleolar osteotomy was performed in 30 patients, and lateral osteotomy was performed in 10 patients. All osteotomies achieved union. Seven patients (17.5%) underwent implant removal after 1 year because of symptomatic screw or plate irritation; all implant removals were performed after complete osteotomy union. No ankle osteotomy nonunion occurred. Detailed complication timing, management, and status at 12 months and final follow-up are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Discussion

This retrospective comparative cohort study evaluated final knee donor site morbidity and ankle-related outcomes after 2 technique–donor site combinations for talar mosaicplasty: arthroscopic medial femoral condyle harvesting and mini-open lateral femoral condyle harvesting. With the available numbers, no statistically significant between-group differences were detected in final knee-related outcomes, ankle-related outcomes, pain scores, or complication rates. However, the 95% CIs for several outcome differences were wide and crossed clinically meaningful thresholds; therefore, the absence of statistically significant differences should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence, superiority, or noninferiority. Because preoperative patient-reported outcome scores, including preoperative Lysholm scores, were unavailable, postoperative improvement from baseline and patient-level achievement of the Lysholm MCID could not be assessed.

Our findings should be contextualized within the current literature. Osteochondral autograft transfer is considered a treatment option for selected large, cystic, or refractory osteochondral lesions of the talus, and recent knee-to-ankle autologous osteochondral transfer studies have reported favorable mid- to long-term outcomes.14-16 However, donor site morbidity remains a recognized concern, particularly in knee-to-ankle transfer procedures. 17 A recent medial trochlear harvest study reported low donor site morbidity over time. 17 In the present cohort, the overall postoperative complication rate was 12.5%, and complications involved both the donor knee and the ankle operative site. Because preoperative patient-reported outcome scores were unavailable, the present study cannot assess postoperative improvement from baseline or the proportion of patients achieving the Lysholm MCID; therefore, the current findings should be interpreted as final between-group comparisons between 2 technique–donor site combinations rather than as evidence of postoperative improvement after mosaicplasty.

The harvest technique and donor condyle may influence donor site symptoms. Biomechanical studies have shown distinct mechanical properties between human talar and distal femoral cartilage, supporting the concept of donor-recipient tissue mismatch in knee-to-talus osteochondral transfer.18,19 This supports the possibility that mechanical mismatch between donor and recipient cartilage may contribute to postoperative symptoms or structural changes after osteochondral transplantation.

Arthroscopic harvesting from the MFC is theoretically associated with reduced soft tissue disturbance, blood loss, and postoperative hemarthrosis. However, it may compromise graft perpendicularity and geometric precision, potentially resulting in joint surface incongruence. In a cadaveric comparison of arthroscopic and mini-open osteochondral autograft harvesting, no significant differences were found in graft perpendicularity or overall suitability, although a larger proportion of plugs in the arthroscopic group met the authors’ acceptability criteria (69% vs 56%). 20

Donor site morbidity remains a major limitation of mosaicplasty. Previous studies have reported higher knee donor site morbidity after knee-to-ankle than knee-to-knee mosaicplasty, and recent OLT-focused meta-analyses continue to report donor site symptoms after OATS.21,22 In the present cohort, 1 patient in the mini-open LFC group reported persistent mild donor knee pain at final follow-up, whereas the 2 early donor knee events in the arthroscopic MFC group improved with nonoperative management. These findings support careful consideration of graft burden, donor site area, and the number of harvested plugs when planning knee-to-ankle osteochondral transfer. 23

Although sex-stratified outcomes are provided for transparency, these analyses were post hoc and descriptive. The small subgroup sizes preclude any reliable inference regarding sex-specific treatment effects. Therefore, the observed numerical differences should be considered hypothesis-generating only.

Knee-associated complications, such as hemarthrosis, instability, and pain, are the most common issues, affecting up to 20% of knee-to-ankle mosaicplasties. 22 Complications in the present cohort involved both the donor knee and the ankle operative site. Most events resolved with conservative management or targeted treatment; however, 1 patient in the mini-open LFC group reported persistent mild donor knee pain at final follow-up. These findings support the need to report donor knee and ankle-site complications separately in future studies.

From a clinical standpoint, the present data do not establish superiority of either harvesting strategy. Technique selection in this cohort reflected lesion dimensions, lesion location, the need for perpendicular access, expected graft burden, technical feasibility, and surgeon judgment. Arthroscopic MFC harvesting may reduce soft tissue exposure but requires arthroscopy equipment, fluid management, portal setup, and specific technical familiarity. Mini-open LFC harvesting may provide direct visualization and practical access for multiple plugs, but it involves a different donor condyle and a different exposure. In our institutional tariff-based setting, the arthroscopic harvesting setup was estimated to be approximately 1.5% more costly than the mini-open harvesting setup, whereas postoperative immobilization, rehabilitation, follow-up, and routine postoperative care were similar between groups. Because surgical approach and donor condyle differed simultaneously, the present study cannot determine which component, if any, explains the observed numerical differences.

The treatment of large or cystic ankle osteochondral lesions should also be considered in the broader context of subchondral bone restoration and joint-preserving alternatives. In cystic lesions, management of the osseous defect is clinically important because persistent subchondral cysts may compromise structural support beneath the cartilage repair surface. Retrograde drilling or retrograde bone grafting has been described as a joint-preserving option for selected subchondral cystic lesions when the overlying cartilage is relatively preserved.14,24 In contrast, osteochondral autograft transfer is generally reserved for larger, full-thickness, cystic, or refractory OLTs in which restoration of both the articular cartilage surface and subchondral bone is required. 14 Ertan et al 25 reported clinical improvement after retrograde cancellous bone grafting for cystic osteochondral lesions of the tibial plafond, although radiologic recurrence remained a concern. Although their study addressed the tibial plafond rather than the talus, it remains relevant because it highlights cyst management and subchondral bone restoration in cystic ankle osteochondral pathology.19,26

This study has several limitations. First, it was retrospective, single-center, and nonrandomized. Technique selection reflected lesion location, required graft trajectory, expected graft burden, technical feasibility, and surgeon judgment, which may have introduced selection bias and confounding by indication. Second, the 2 groups differed simultaneously in both harvesting approach and donor condyle; therefore, the study cannot isolate the independent effect of arthroscopic vs mini-open exposure alone. Third, the sample size was small, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, and the wide CIs around several estimates mean that clinically meaningful differences cannot be excluded. The absence of statistically significant differences should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence, superiority, or noninferiority. Fourth, preoperative patient-reported outcome scores, including preoperative Lysholm scores, were not available in the retrospective records; therefore, postoperative improvement from baseline and the proportion of patients achieving the prespecified Lysholm MCID could not be assessed. Consequently, the Lysholm score was analyzed only as a final follow-up between-group outcome, and clinical interpretation based on MCID achievement remains limited. Fifth, some patient-reported outcome data were completed by telephone, which may have introduced recall bias. Sixth, although donor knee symptoms and clinical examination findings were reviewed, objective instrumented quadriceps strength testing, standardized patellofemoral-specific donor site scoring, and routine postoperative MRI or second-look arthroscopy were not performed. Seventh, preoperative activity level and sport participation were not consistently recorded in the retrospective records and therefore could not be compared between groups. Finally, all procedures were performed by a single fellowship-trained foot and ankle surgeon at a tertiary referral center, which may limit generalizability to other surgeons, institutions, and practice settings.

Conclusion

In this small retrospective cohort, no statistically significant differences were detected between arthroscopic medial femoral condyle harvesting and mini-open lateral femoral condyle harvesting for talar mosaicplasty in final knee donor site morbidity, ankle-related outcomes, pain scores, or complication rates. However, because the surgical approach and donor condyle differed simultaneously, the study cannot isolate the independent effect of approach alone. In addition, the limited sample size, nonrandomized design, and wide CIs preclude conclusions regarding equivalence, superiority, or noninferiority. Technique selection should therefore remain individualized according to lesion characteristics, required graft orientation, expected graft burden, technical feasibility, and surgeon experience.
