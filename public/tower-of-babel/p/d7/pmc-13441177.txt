Synergistic regulation of hDPSC-iPSCs by tetrahedral DNA nanostructures for dentin-pulp complex regeneration
Authors: Qiong Rong, Yu Guo, Tingting Wang, Xiaobing Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe regeneration of a functional dentin-pulp complex represents a paramount objective in regenerative endodontics. However, current strategies reliant on scaffolds and growth factors are constrained by imprecise biological signaling and inefficient stem cell guidance. To address these limitations, this study aimed to develop a precise nanomaterial-based strategy. We investigated tetrahedral DNA nanostructures (TDNs) as bioactive nanocarriers to enhance the odontogenic differentiation of human dental pulp stem cell-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (hDPSC-iPSCs) within a gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel system. hDPSCs were isolated from young patients’ teeth, reprogrammed into iPSCs, and characterized. TDNs were synthesized, characterized, and shown to be efficiently internalized by cells. In vitro, TDN treatment significantly upregulated key odontogenic markers (DSPP and DMP-1), promoted proliferation, and enhanced migration of hDPSC-iPSCs. For in vivo assessment, TDN-pretreated hDPSC-iPSCs encapsulated in GelMA were implanted into a tooth root canal regeneration model in nude mice. The TDN group regenerated well-structured, dentin-pulp-like tissues exhibiting strong dentin sialophosphoprotein (DSPP) expression, indicative of odontogenic differentiation. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that TDNs potently direct hDPSC-iPSCs toward an odontogenic lineage and support pulp-like tissue formation within a hydrogel niche. The integrated TDN–cell–GelMA platform presents a novel, precise, and minimally invasive strategy for early-stage pulp-like regeneration, with promising potential for clinical translation in regenerative endodontics.Keywords: induced pluripotent stem cells, DNA nanostructures, cell differentiation, dental pulp, dentinogenesis, regeneration

This study demonstrates that tetrahedral DNA nanostructures (TDNs), as bioactive nanoparticles, can effectively enhance the regenerative capacity of stem cells. Our findings show that in vitro treatment with TDNs significantly promotes the odontogenic differentiation, proliferation, and migration of human dental pulp stem cell-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (hDPSC-iPSCs). Subsequently, delivering these TDN-primed cells within a GelMA hydrogel successfully drives the formation of structured dentin-pulp-like tissue in vivo. This combined TDN–cell–GelMA strategy provides a precise and minimally invasive approach, addressing current limitations in regenerative endodontics and showing promising potential for clinical translation.

Introduction

The regeneration of a functional dentin-pulp complex is a central objective in regenerative endodontics.1 Conventional approaches utilizing scaffolds and growth factors face challenges such as imprecise signal delivery, bioactivity instability, and potential immunogenicity.2 Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) offer a promising cell source for overcoming these limitations.3,4 Specifically, iPSCs derived from dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs-iPSCs) are readily accessible and may possess a stronger epigenetic predisposition toward odontogenic lineages, making them advantageous for pulp regeneration.5,6 Although iPSCs can differentiate into functional odontoblast-like cells,7,8 achieving efficient, lineage-specific differentiation and functional 3D tissue integration remains a significant hurdle.9

Nanobiomaterials provide novel means for precise cellular regulation. Tetrahedral DNA nanostructures (TDNs) are notable for their biocompatibility, structural stability, ease of functionalization, and efficient cellular uptake.10,11 TDNs are internalized via membrane interactions and can modulate stem cell behaviors, including proliferation, differentiation, and survival.12–16

However, critical gaps persist. First, while TDN applications in bone, cartilage, and neural regeneration are established, their role in odontogenic differentiation, particularly of iPSCs, is undefined. Second, research has primarily emphasized TDNs as drug carriers17,18 or their effects on isolated cellular processes; their synergistic impact on multiple iPSC behaviors (proliferation, migration, and differentiation) within a complex odontogenic microenvironment is unclear. Additionally, nanomaterial properties can disrupt stemness maintenance and directed differentiation.19,20 Successful regeneration requires effective cell delivery within a biomimetic niche. Although gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel is a common scaffold,21,22 integrating its properties with the regulatory functions of TDNs into a cohesive “material-cell” system requires further investigation.23,24

We hypothesize that TDNs enhance the odontogenic differentiation of hDPSC-iPSCs and facilitate dentin-pulp tissue regeneration in the GelMA hydrogel microenvironment. This study will characterize the properties of hDPSC-iPSCs and TDNs. First, it will investigate the effects of TDNs on the differentiation (via markers MSX-1 and DSPP),25 proliferation, and migration of hDPSC-iPSCs in vitro. Then, a nude mouse model will be used to assess the regulatory effects of TDNs on hDPSC-iPSCs during pulp regeneration in the GelMA hydrogel microenvironment.

This study aims to elucidate the role of TDNs in inducing odontogenesis of hDPSC-iPSCs. It advances the understanding of the interaction between programmable nanomaterials and stem cells, thereby supporting dentin-pulp regeneration strategies.

Methods

Cell culture

Isolation and culture of hDPSCs

Intact healthy mandibular third molars extracted from patients younger than 20 years old were obtained from the department of oral and maxillofacial surgery of our hospital. The informed written consents from all volunteers were obtained. Dental pulp tissue was isolated and digested with collagenase type I and dispase II (Gibco, USA). The primary culture followed the protocol by Gronthos et al.,26 with α-MEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum, 1% L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were passaged and cultured to passage three for subsequent experiments. The experiment was approved by the ethical committee of the first people’s hospital of Yunnan province (2025-GN008).

Reprogramming of hDPSCs to iPSCs

Human DPSCs were seeded into 6-well plates and treated using the Sendai reprogramming kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The reprogramming process was performed based on our previous report.27 Subsequently, a feeder-free culture system was employed. Briefly, culture plates were coated with 50 μg/mL vitronectin (BD, USA), and cells were cultured and expanded using iPS complete medium and specific dissociation reagent (iCell, China). When reaching 50%-60% confluence, the cells were transferred to ultra-low attachment plates and cultured in suspension in embryoid body (EB) medium (Stemcell, USA) for 7 days, followed by adherent culture.

Characterization of hDPSC-iPSCs

Alkaline phosphatase staining

Cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with the BCIP/NBT working solution (Beyotime, China) in the dark for 30 min. The reaction was stopped with PBS washing, and images were captured under an inverted microscope.

Immunofluorescence

Human DPSC-iPSCs were fixed with pre-cooled 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and washed with PBS. Then cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sangon, China) for 10 min at room temperature and blocked with PBST (PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20) supplemented with 10% goat serum for 30 min. Blocking buffer was then removed, and cells were incubated with primary antibodies, including anti-OCT4 (Proteintech, USA, 1:500), anti-SOX2 (Abcam, USA, 1:500), anti-SSEA4 (Abcam, USA, 1:500), and anti-TRA1-60 (Abcam, USA, 1:500) at 4 °C overnight. After PBS washing, secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 and goat anti-mouse Cy3, 1:1000) were added and incubated for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained using DAPI (Beyotime, China). Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Teratoma formation

For the teratoma assay, three 4-to 6-week-old female NOD/SCID mice (SiPeiFu, China) were acclimatized for 7 days. A suspension of 1 × 106 hDPSC-iPSCs was injected into the muscle of one hind limb under aseptic conditions. The animal was euthanized about 8 weeks postinjection, and the teratoma was harvested. Then the tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for paraffin embedding. Hematoxylin-eosin staining was conducted following standard procedures. The animal experiment was approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Yunnan Wosai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (WS2024002).

Synthesis of TDNs

The four single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) sequences for tetrahedral DNA nanostructures (TDNs) assembly were adopted without modification from a previously validated protocol.28 The sequences are: S1 (5′-ATTTATCACCCGCCATAGTAGACGTATCACCAGGCAGTTGAGACGAACATTCCTAAGTCTGAA-3′), S2 (5′-ACATGCGAGGGTCCAATACCGACGATTACAGCTTGCTACACGATTCAGACTTAGGAATGTTCG-3′), S3 (5′-ACTACTATGGCGGGTGATAAAACGTGTAGCAAGCTGTAATCGACGGGAAGAGCATGCCCATCC-3′), and S4 (5′-ACGGTATTGGACCCTCGCATGACTCAACTGCCTGGTGATACGAGGATGGGCATGCTCTTCCCG-3′), all of which were commercially synthesized and quality-controlled by Generay Biotechnology (China). The lyophilized ssDNA samples were stored at −20 °C until use. Prior to assembly, the ssDNA powders were centrifuged at 10 000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C and then reconstituted in nuclease-free water to a final concentration of 100 μM. The solutions were kept on ice for immediate use. A 10× TM buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM MgCl2·6H2O, pH 8.0) was prepared and filter-sterilized through a 0.22 μm membrane. Equimolar amounts of each ssDNA (1 μL of 100 μM solution) were mixed with 96 μL of TM buffer in a 200 μL microcentrifuge tube. The mixture was subjected to thermal annealing in a PCR instrument (Bioer, China) under the following program: denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by rapid cooling to 4 °C and incubation for 20 min to facilitate self-assembly into TDNs. The assembled products were stored at 4 °C.

Purification of TDNs was carried out using Amicon ultrafiltration centrifugal devices (Millipore, USA). For each 500 μL sample, centrifugation was performed at 12 000 × g for 5 min at room temperature. The retentate was washed three times with TM buffer under the same centrifugal conditions. Finally, the purified TDNs were recovered by inverted centrifugation at 1000 × g for 2 min. The concentration of TDNs was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 260 nm using a microvolume spectrophotometer (TIANGEN, China). The purity of TDN was calculated using the following formula: (lane3 + lane4)/(lane1 + lane2 + lane3 + lane4) × 100%.

Characterization of TDNs

Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis

To confirm the successful assembly and purity of the TDNs, non-denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) was performed. A 5 μL aliquot of the sample was mixed with 1 μL of 6× DNA loading buffer (Solarbio, China) and loaded onto an 8% polyacrylamide gel (Sangon, China), with a DNA molecular weight marker (Solarbio, China) serving as a reference. Electrophoresis was conducted in 1× TAE buffer at a constant voltage of 80 V for 60-80 min. Subsequently, the gel was stained in the dark for 30 min using a solution containing GelRed™ nucleic acid stain (Beyotime, China). DNA bands were visualized and analyzed under UV illumination.

Transmission electron microscopy

The morphology of the TDNs was examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Prior to imaging, the sample was briefly ultrasonicated for 1 min to ensure homogeneity. Then, 5-10 μL of the dispersed sample was applied onto a carbon-coated copper EM grid and allowed to adsorb for 1 min at room temperature. After air-drying, the grid was imaged using a transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan).

Nanoparticle size and distribution analysis

The particle size of the TDNs was measured by using a nanoparticle size and zeta potential analyzer (Malvern, UK). This analysis was conducted by Shanghai Weixi Analysis Technology Co., Ltd.

Cellular uptake of TDNs in hDPSC-iPSCs

Confocal laser scanning microscopy

hDPSC-iPSCs were cultured in vitronectin-coated confocal dishes. Cy5-labeled TDNs (assembled from Cy5-modified strands S1, S2, S3, and S4) were applied at a final concentration of 200 nM. After 12 h of incubation, cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Subsequently, actin filaments were stained using actin-tracker green (Beyotime, China; 1:200) for 1 h in the dark, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min. After final washes with PBS, images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Flow cytometry

Cells in routine culture for 2 days were incubated with complete medium containing 200 nM Cy5-TDNs; untreated cells served as the negative control. After 24 h, cells were harvested, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD, USA) to quantify Cy5 fluorescence. Data were processed using the manufacturer’s software.

Odontogenic differentiation induction

Twenty-four hours after seeding, the culture medium was replaced with odontogenic induction medium, consisting of DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 100 nM dexamethasone, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, and 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid (Sigma, USA). Based on preliminary quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) screening, an optimal TDN concentration of 50 nM was used for the experimental group. The medium was refreshed every 3 days, and differentiation was carried out over 7 days.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), quantified spectrophotometrically (TIANGEN, China), and qualified by gel electrophoresis. cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng of RNA using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (ThermoFisher, USA). qPCR was performed with 10 ng cDNA per reaction using FastStart Essential DNA Probes Master (Roche, Switzerland) on a real-time PCR system (ABI, USA). ACTB served as the reference gene, and relative expression was calculated via the 2-△△CT method. All primers were synthesized by Generay (China) with the following sequences: MSX-1 (forward: 5′-CTCAAGCTGCCAGAAGATGC-3′, reverse: 5′-TTCGTCTTGTGTTTGCGGAG-3′); MEPE (forward: 5′-AACAAAGGTTTCCTAGTAAGGGC-3′, reverse: 5′-CCTCCTGTTGGAATGGGGTTG-3′); DSPP (forward: 5′-TGGCGATGCAGGTCACAAT-3′, reverse: 5′-CCATTCCCACTAGGACTCCCA-3′); DMP-1 (forward: 5′-AGGTCTCGCATCTCAGAG-3′, reverse: 5′-TGGAGTTGCTGTTTTCTGTAGAG-3′); and ACTB (forward: 5′-AGCACAGAGCCTCGCCTTTG-3′, reverse: 5′-CTTCTGACCCATGCCCACCA-3′).

Western blot analysis

Proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (ThermoFisher, USA), and concentrations were determined with a BCA assay kit (BestBio, China). Samples were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk in TBST for 3 h, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-MSX-1 (Proteintech, USA; 1:1000), anti-MEPE (Proteintech, USA; 1:2000), anti-DSPP (Santa Cruz, USA; 1:500), anti-DMP-1 (Santa Cruz, USA; 1:500), and anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, USA; 1:15000). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Proteintech, USA; 1:5000) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Blots were developed with ECL substrate (ThermoFisher, USA) and imaged using a chemiluminescence system (Clinx, China). Band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software.

Assessment of cell proliferation by CCK-8 assay

HDPSC-iPSCs were cultured at a density of 3 × 10³ cells per well in ultra-low attachment 96-well plates for 7 days to allow EB formation. The EBs were subsequently transferred to standard plates for adherent culture. Cells were assigned to three experimental groups: group 1 (complete medium: DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS), group 2 (odontogenic induction medium: DMEM containing 10% FBS, 100 nM dexamethasone, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, and 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid), and group 3 (odontogenic induction medium further supplemented with 50 nM TDNs). Following treatment, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, China) was added to each well. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland).

Evaluation of cell migration by Transwell assay

HDPSC-iPSCs were first cultured in suspension for 7 days. The EBs were then transferred for adherent culture under the same experimental groupings as previously described. Subsequently, cells were plated into Transwell inserts (Corning, USA) at a density of 5 × 104 cells per chamber, with the lower chamber filled with complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Following 24-hour incubation, cells were fixed, stained, and quantified under a microscope.

Animal experiment

Pretreatment of human tooth roots

Mandibular premolars extracted for orthodontic purposes were collected. Root canals were instrumented using ProTaper nickel-titanium files. Mid-root segments approximately 5 mm in length were obtained and subjected to sequential treatments: 17% EDTA for 10 min, 19% citric acid for 1 min, disinfection with povidone iodine for 30 min, and 5.25% sodium hypochlorite for 15 min. Samples were verified for the absence of microbial contamination, then stored for further use.

Subcutaneous dentin-pulp-like tissue formation model in nude mice

HDPSC-iPSCs were cultured in odontogenic differentiation medium containing 50 nM TDNs for 7 days. A total of 6 × 105 cells were resuspended in 0.5 mL of complete DMEM with 5% GelMA (EFL, China). Then, 30 μL of cell suspension (3.6 × 104 cells) was injected into the pretreated root canal space; the control group received cell-free medium (GelMA only). GelMA was photo-crosslinked, and the coronal access was sealed with MTA (LONGLY, China). Constructs were subcutaneously implanted into BALB/c nude mice (SiPeiFu, China) and sutured. Three mice were used per group (n = 3 biologically independent implants per group). After 8 weeks, implants were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde.

Specimens were decalcified in 10-volume EDTA solution (LEAGENE, China), followed by fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin embedding, and sectioning. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed according to standard protocols. For immunohistochemistry, sections were incubated with rabbit anti-DSPP antibody (Abcam, USA; 1:200) at 37 °C for 60 min, followed by HRP-conjugated mouse anti-rabbit IgG at 37 °C for 30 min. The Rabbit IgG polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, USA; 1:100) was used as a negative control. Staining was developed with DAB (MXB, China).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was confirmed via Levene’s test prior to parametric analysis. Intergroup comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) post hoc test only when the overall ANOVA reached statistical significance. A significance threshold of P < .05 was adopted. All statistical analyses were executed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.3).

Results

Culture and characterization of hDPSCs and hDPSC-iPSCs

Primary hDPSCs exhibited a fibroblast-like morphology with spindle-shaped adherent growth, tight cellular arrangement, uniform cytoplasm, and a moderate nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio (Figure 1A). When cultured under low-adhesion conditions, hDPSC-iPSCs formed three-dimensional EBs with regular morphology and well-defined edges after 7 days (Figure 1B). Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining revealed intense dark purple deposits and a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, indicative of a highly undifferentiated state (Figure 1C).

Characterization of human dental pulp stem cell-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (hDPSC-iPSCs). (A) Primary culture of hDPSCs. (B) An embryoid body formed by hDPSC-iPSCs. (C) Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining of embryoid bodies derived from hDPSC-iPSCs. Scale bars represent 50 μm. (D, E) Immunofluorescence staining for OCT4, SSEA4, SOX2, and TRA-1-60 expression in hDPSC-iPSCs determined by immunofluorescence. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Scale bars represent 50 μm. (F) Macroscopic view of teratomas induced in nude mice. (G) Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of teratoma slice. All three germ layers were detected. The scale bar represents 150 μm.

Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed pluripotency, with positive staining of OCT4 and SSEA4 (Figure 1D), demonstrating sustained pluripotency gene expression and an undifferentiated phenotype. Additionally, expression of SOX2 and TRA1-60 (Figure 1E) further supported their self-renewal and pluripotency maintenance capabilities.

Subcutaneous injection of hDPSC-iPSCs into immunodeficient mice resulted in the formation of irregular solid nodules after 8 weeks. Histological staining revealed teratomas containing tissues derived from all three germ layers: ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm (Figure 1F and G). These results confirm the ability of hDPSC-iPSCs to differentiate into trilineage derivatives, consistent with definitive pluripotent stem cell characteristics.

Synthesis and characterization of TDNs

As illustrated in Figure 2A, TDNs were formed through the self-assembly of four single-stranded DNA sequences (S1-S4) via complementary base pairing. PAGE analysis (Figure 2B) demonstrated distinct band shifts corresponding to progressive hybridization. Lane 1 (S1 alone) and Lane 2 (S1 + S2) showed clear migration differences. Lane 3 (S1 + S2 + S3) exhibited a band of higher molecular weight. In Lane 4, two upper bands and a faint lower band were observed. The top band corresponded to TDN aggregates, while the prominent middle band confirmed the successful formation of structurally stable TDNs through one-step annealing in TM buffer, with a molecular weight consistent with the expected tetrahedral configuration. The purity of TDN was (90.37 ± 6.73)%.

Synthesis and characterization of TDNs. (A) Schematic illustration of the TDN synthesis process. (B) PAGE analysis shows successful fabrication of TDNs. Lane 1: S1; Lane 2: S1 + S2; Lane 3: S1 + S2 + S3; Lane 4: aggregated TDNs, intact TDNs, S1 + S2 + S3 (from top to bottom). (C) Representative TEM images of TDNs (scale bar represents 200 nm). Triangular symbols indicate well-formed TDNs, circles highlight aggregated structures. (D) Nanoparticle size distribution profile of TDNs based on DLS measurements.

TEM imaging (Figure 2C) revealed individual TDNs with a characteristic triangular morphology, measuring approximately 10 nm in size. Some particles aggregated into sheet-like assemblies, likely due to surface charge or hydrophobic interactions. Nanoparticle size distribution analysis (Figure 2D) showed a hydrodynamic diameter peak at 125.3 nm, notably larger than the dimensions observed by TEM, which can be attributed to particle aggregation during storage.

Cellular uptake of TDNs by hDPSC-iPSCs

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) revealed distinct punctate red fluorescence signals localized primarily in the cytoplasm (Figure 3A), indicating effective internalization of Cy5-labeled TDNs. Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed efficient cellular uptake: cells treated with Cy5-TDNs exhibited a significant rightward shift in fluorescence intensity, along with markedly increased percentages of Cy5-positive cells (93.6%) and elevated mean fluorescence intensity (Figure 3B). These results demonstrate that hDPSC-iPSCs are capable of efficient internalization of exogenous TDNs.

Cellular uptake of cy5-TDNs in hDPSC-iPSCs. (A) CLSM images showed the uptake of cy5-labeled TDNs in hDPSC-iPSCs. Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and the scale bar represents 20 μm. (B) Flow cytometry analysis showed a higher percentage of Cy5-positive cells (93.6%) and elevated mean fluorescence intensity on the right (TDNs-treated) compared to the left (0.17%, control; P <.05).

TDNs promote odontogenic differentiation of hDPSC-iPSCs in vitro

To establish the optimal induction period, the expression of odontogenic genes was assessed by qRT-PCR at different time points. Expression of MSX-1 and DSPP was significantly higher on day 7 compared to day 3, while DSPP levels were also elevated relative to day 10. DMP-1 expression showed no significant variation across days 3, 7, and 10. MEPE expression was highest on day 10 (Supplementary Figure S1A). Based on these results, day 7 was selected for subsequent experiments.

Preliminary screening of TDN concentrations revealed that 62.5 nM significantly upregulated MSX-1, MEPE, and DMP-1 expression, though no concentration markedly enhanced DSPP (Supplementary Figure S1B). Further assessment using 25.0, 50.0, and 62.5 nM TDNs demonstrated that 50.0 nM most effectively promoted the expression of all four genes, showing superior efficacy to 25.0 nM and comparable results to 62.5 nM (Supplementary Figure S1C). Thus, 50.0 nM was identified as the optimal concentration for TDN-induced odontogenic differentiation.

After 7 days of treatment with 50.0 nM TDNs, the cell morphology was still dominated by polygonal odontoblast-like cells, and some cells were arranged in a columnar manner, similar to the “polarized secretion” characteristics of odontoblasts (Figure 4A). Quantitative RT-PCR analysis revealed significant upregulation of MSX-1, MEPE, DSPP, and DMP-1 mRNA (P < .05), with MSX-1 expression increasing approximately 2-fold (Figure 4B). Western blot analysis confirmed corresponding elevations in protein levels (Figure 4C). These findings indicate that TDNs enhance the odontoblastic differentiation of hDPSC-iPSCs through both transcriptional and translational mechanisms.

The effects of TDNs on the proliferation and migration of hDPSC-iPSCs

CCK-8 assay results (Figure 5A) indicated that cell proliferation increased over time in all groups. Compared with group 1, group 2 showed significant inhibition of proliferation by day 3 (P < .05-.01), which was most pronounced on days 5-7, demonstrating that the odontogenic induction medium suppressed proliferation. Group 3 exhibited significantly higher proliferation than group 2 from day 3 to day 7 (P < .01-.001), indicating that 50 nM TDNs partially reversed this suppression. However, proliferation in group 3 remained lower than in group 1 at all time points (P < .05-.01), confirming that TDNs did not fully restore baseline proliferation.

TDNs synergistically enhance the proliferation and migration of hDPSC-iPSCs. (A) Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (n = 3). (B) Cell migration was evaluated via Transwell assay (n = 3). Scale bars represent 100 μm. *P < .05, **P < .01, ***P < .001, ****P < .0001.

The Transwell assay (Figure 5B) showed that cell migration was significantly higher in group 2 than in group 1 (P < .05). Migration was further enhanced in group 3 compared with both group 2 (P < .01) and group 1 (P < .001). These results demonstrate that 50 nM TDNs cooperatively enhance the migratory capacity of hDPSC-iPSCs under odontogenic induction, independently of proliferation status.

Role of TDNs in promoting early dentin-pulp-like tissue formation in vivo

A subcutaneous regeneration model in nude mice was established to assess dentin-pulp-like tissue formation (Figure 6A). Histological analysis via HE staining (Figure 6B) showed that the experimental group exhibited loose connective tissue filling the root canal lumen, with evenly distributed cells forming a pulp-like structure (triangles). Along the root canal wall, dense aggregates of fusiform/stellate cells were observed (pentagons), suggesting active cellular recruitment and early differentiation.

Dentin-pulp-like tissue formation in nude mice following subcutaneous transplantation. (A) Schematic illustration of the animal experimental procedure. (B) Representative histological images of hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for dentin sialophosphoprotein (DSPP), with IgG isotype control. Triangles indicate newly regenerated tissue within the root canal; Pentagons highlight newly formed cells on the root canal wall.

Immunohistochemical staining for DSPP (Figure 6B) revealed distinct positive signals in the root canal wall and newly formed tissue areas of the experimental group, significantly exceeding the low background signals in controls. These DSPP-positive regions partially colocalized with cell-dense areas identified by HE staining. Together, these findings demonstrate that the TDN-iPSCs-GelMA platform is capable of inducing pulp-like tissue formation and recruiting odontoblast-like cells along the root canal wall, as evidenced by the colocalization of DSPP expression with cellular aggregates.

Discussion

This study demonstrates efficient internalization of TDNs by hDPSC-iPSCs. At 50 nM, TDNs induced odontoblastic differentiation, evidenced by upregulation of key genes and proteins, while partially mitigating induction medium-mediated proliferation inhibition and enhancing migration. These findings confirm that TDNs serve as effective bioactive molecules to modulate iPSC odontogenic behavior. Their stable nanostructure facilitates uptake, leading to upregulation of odontoblast markers at transcriptional and translational levels. The dual regulation of proliferation and migration suggests functionality beyond differentiation induction, potentially creating favorable conditions for cell recruitment and expansion. In vivo, TDNs promoted dentin-pulp complex-like structures with DSPP-positive cells, demonstrating pro-regenerative potential. Thus, TDNs likely orchestrate key processes in stem cell fate determination by mimicking extracellular matrix signals or acting as nucleic acid carriers, providing a novel basis for iPSC-based pulp regeneration strategies.

The synthesized TDNs exhibited characterization (e.g., TEM morphology, PAGE bands) consistent with literature,10,11,28 ensuring material standardization and reliability. Efficient uptake by hDPSC-iPSCs aligned with Chen et al.29 and Liang et al.,30 supporting TDN’s transmembrane capability per cell-nanoparticle interaction principles.31,32 TDNs significantly upregulated the expression of odonto/osteogenic genes and proteins, indicating their differentiation-activating effect. While Notch signaling (Zhou et al.33) was not directly validated, enhanced differentiation corroborates TDNs as effective nanomaterials for stem cell fate modulation. More importantly, TDNs promoted pulp-like tissue formation with DSPP-positive signals, consistent with regenerative endodontics strategies emphasizing cell homing and differentiation34,35 and bioactive material-mediated microenvironment mimicry.36 This strengthens credibility and indicates TDNs as a viable complement to existing stem cell-based regeneration protocols.37,38

This study also revealed findings that differ from prior research due to experimental design, cell models, treatment conditions, and evaluation criteria. A biphasic proliferation regulation by TDNs was observed. Unlike Zhou et al.33 reporting pro-proliferation in basic medium, our odontogenic induction context showed TDNs mitigated the inhibitory effect of the induction medium-mediated proliferation without full restoration. This discrepancy stems from methodological differences; previous studies used basic media,24,39,40 while our model mimics in vivo differentiation. Induction media components like dexamethasone and β-glycerophosphate are known to suppress proliferation.41,42 TDNs’ partial rescue suggests modulation of differentiation responses or metabolic stress, highlighting context-dependent bioactivity. This may relate to tooth development mechanisms where morphogens like BMP4 and FGF8 balance proliferation and apoptosis.43,44 TDNs likely fine-tune signaling networks, reconciling differentiation with proliferative support,45 offering precise regulation for dentin-pulp regeneration.9

Furthermore, TDNs enhanced hDPSC-iPSCs’ migratory capacity independent of proliferation. While migration is crucial for pulp regeneration via homing,46 nanomaterial-mediated regulation is underexplored. We first show in an odontogenic induction microenvironment that TDNs synergistically promote migration beyond basal medium levels, suggesting a distinct mechanism from chemical inducers. This aligns with material topology regulating cell behavior.47–49 Differences may stem from iPSC-derived cells’ plasticity and sensitivity to nanotopographic cues, with the Transwell assay simulating in vivo traversal. A distinct expression pattern was revealed, with pronounced upregulation of MSX-1 versus balanced upregulation in small-molecule inducer studies.50 While MSX-1 and DSPP roles are established,51,52 TDNs may preferentially activate early transcriptional decisions centered on MSX-1. TDNs’ nucleic acid nanostructure offers a different cellular interface than small molecules, potentially altering downstream pathway activation and network interactions, shaping unique gene expression.53,54

In vivo, our cell-TDN suspension approach yielded neotissue with “central-loose/peripheral-dense” biphasic patterning, unlike scaffold-based strategies.23,55,56 This suggests TDNs intrinsically guide spatial cellular organization. While literature focuses on TDN-scaffold composites,57,58 TDNs alone can facilitate organized regeneration by modulating cell-cell interactions or biomechanical properties. The approach using GelMA hydrogel carrier reveals innate tissue-guiding activity, streamlining pulp regeneration strategies.

Our findings corroborate TDN’s core functions: enhanced uptake, differentiation induction, and tissue regeneration promotion. Variations illuminate our system’s uniqueness and TDN’s multifaceted nature, highlighting cell type, microenvironment, and evaluation criteria as critical variables. This provides empirical evidence and a foundation for future mechanistic and applied studies. In summary, TDNs internalized by hDPSC-iPSCs modulate their proliferation, migration, and odontoblastic differentiation, and when carried by GelMA, they achieve pulp-dentin-like tissue formation in vivo. Further mechanism elucidation is needed, but this establishes a foundation for functional regeneration.

Unlike inorganic nanoparticles that rely on passive ion release or organic scaffolds that provide topographical cues,59–61 our TDNs are internalized by hDPSC-iPSCs and directly regulate odontogenic genes at transcriptional and translational levels. Wei et al.62 used miR-126-3p-loaded TDNs to promote DPSC angiogenesis, but the present study extends this paradigm with several novel observations: unmodified TDNs alone drive odontogenic differentiation (revealing intrinsic bioactivity), the first integration of TDNs with iPSC-derived cells for dentin-pulp regeneration, and context-dependent biphasic regulation of proliferation with synergistic migration. Thus, unlike passive strategies, our TDN-iPSC-GelMA platform introduces intracellularly active nanomaterials that directly orchestrate stem cell fate. Challenges including long-term in vivo stability and scale-up costs remain.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The in vitro experiment lacked a TDN-only group (without odontogenic induction medium), leaving it unclear whether observed effects arise intrinsically from TDNs or synergy with inductive conditions. Similarly, the in vivo study omitted the GelMA + TDNs (without cells) control, limiting causal attribution. Future studies will incorporate these missing controls. Our concentration screening identified 50.0 nM as optimal for in vitro odontogenic differentiation under static conditions, yet this is an in vitro optimum rather than a true therapeutic window. Systematic evaluation of dose-dependent effects on proliferation, migration, differentiation, and cytotoxicity in a dynamic in vivo microenvironment is required. Moreover, proliferation and migration were characterized only at 50.0 nM, without exploring concentration dependence.

The subcutaneous nude mouse model is appropriate for proof-of-concept but has clinical limitations. Native human dental pulp operates under low oxygen tension (3-6% O2) and high hydrostatic pressure within rigid dentin walls,63 whereas the subcutaneous site is relatively normoxic (∼21% O2) with minimal mechanical loading. Moderate hypoxia (5% O2) enhances odontogenic differentiation of dental pulp stem cells compared to normoxia,64 suggesting the employed model may not represent the optimal oxygen niche. Additionally, the “tooth root-subcutaneous” construct lacks apical retrograde blood supply and periodontal ligament connections. Revascularization depends solely on host vessel ingrowth from surrounding subcutaneous tissue, a process temporally and anatomically distinct from native pulpal microcirculation.65 Direct comparisons between subcutaneous and orthotopic models have revealed site-dependent outcomes. Ruangsawasdi et al.66 implanted human tooth roots at dorsal versus calvarial subcutaneous sites and found significant differences in tissue ingrowth and DSPP expression, with calvarial sites yielding superior regeneration. Orthotopic large-animal models (e.g., canine) that preserve apical blood supply and periodontal attachment more closely mimic human conditions.67 Thus, our in vivo findings demonstrate proof-of-concept potential rather than predicting clinical performance.

Conclusions

This study establishes TDNs as potent bioactive nanomaterials for directing the odontogenic differentiation of hDPSC-iPSCs within a GelMA hydrogel microenvironment. In vitro, TDNs significantly upregulated the essential odontogenic markers (MSX-1, DSPP, DMP-1, and MEPE) at both gene and protein levels, while concurrently alleviating the inhibition of proliferation and enhancing cell migration. In vivo, subcutaneous implantation led to the regeneration of pulp-like tissue containing DSPP-positive cells, which closely mimics the native architecture of the dentin-pulp complex. The nanoscale design of TDNs enables efficient cellular internalization and precise control over stem cell behavior without compromising pluripotency. Importantly, TDNs exhibit multifunctional regulation, orchestrating proliferation, migration, and differentiation, surpassing conventional growth factor-dependent strategies. Furthermore, this TDN‑mediated approach within a hydrogel carrier unveils an intrinsic tissue-organizing capability, offering a paradigm shift from traditional scaffold-based regeneration.

Collectively, these findings highlight TDNs as a versatile and potent platform for extracellular matrix-mimetic signaling, laying a robust foundation for advanced iPSC-based therapies in dentin-pulp complex regeneration. Future studies should systematically evaluate TDN concentration response curves, transition to orthotopic large-animal models, and consider prevascularized constructs to better define the therapeutic window.
