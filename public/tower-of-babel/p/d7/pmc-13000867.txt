Commemorative Naming, Intergenerational Legacy, and Continuing Bonds: An Interpretive Phenomenological Study of Being a Living Commemoration
Authors: S Shorer, M Mahat‐Shamir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCommemorative naming—naming a child after a deceased family member—is a culturally rooted practice with profound emotional and relational implications. Although previous research has explored naming traditions from sociological and cultural angles, little attention has been given to the psychological experience of those who carry such names. This qualitative study examined the lived experiences of 14 Israeli adults named after deceased relatives, using interpretative phenomenological analysis to uncover personal and intergenerational meanings embedded in this practice. Participants were recruited through purposive sampling and completed in‐depth interviews that revealed two central themes: (a) the continuum between identification and detachment in relation to the commemorative name and (b) how naming operates as both social compliance and an enduring emotional connection with the deceased. The findings illustrate the dynamic tension between honoring family legacy and negotiating personal autonomy. Participants described feelings of pride, duty, emotional burden, and identity conflict. Grounded in contextual family theory and the concept of continuing bonds, the analysis highlights how commemorative naming functions as both a vehicle for familial resilience and a source of psychological strain. These insights offer valuable implications for clinical practice. Therapists and family practitioners should consider naming histories as a meaningful part of family narratives, especially in contexts of grief, legacy, and relational ethics. Raising awareness of the emotional weight of commemorative names may support families in making more intentional, ethically sensitive naming decisions that consider the impact on future generations.Keywords: contextual theory, continuing bonds, grief, names, naming process, relational ethics

Introduction

Naming people is a traditional practice that goes back to the beginning of human history and had been documented in various numerous cultures (Hough and Izdebska 2016). A child's name carries significant emotional and cultural meaning at both private and social levels (Abu Hatab 2021; Felecan and Bugheșiu 2021). The selection of a name is not a neutral act; it has implications that can positively influence a child's future or conversely, impose emotional stress related to family heritage and history (Bondi 2014; Wheeler 2023). Various naming practices have been described in different cultural settings around the globe (Hough and Izdebska 2016), presenting varied motivations and expectations that are expressed through the naming process and its influence over the future life of the name carrier (Mateos et al. 2011). For example, in different European cultures, names used to signify belonging to certain families or collectives; thus, they carried symbolic meanings both for the name carrier and others in their social surroundings (e.g., Sangoi 1999; Smith 1985). However, such traditions have changed with modernity, in what seems to reflect larger social trends, such as shifts in proximity to religion and in keeping with social traditions, accommodations to and acceptance of progression, and so on. In Asia, Shaik (2024) described how the naming practices of the Dudekula people in India have changed according to the political atmosphere in their region, in an attempt to provide a better future to their offspring. Parents in that area might change their children's names even during their childhood's years to accommodate current sociopolitical trends (Shaik 2024).

In this large‐scale domain of naming practices, giving a child the name of an ancestor is a specific tradition, common in both Western and non‐Western communities for various purposes. However, regardless of its purpose, when children are named after family members or other significant figures, even if only via a middle name, it establishes a conscious or unconscious link to family narratives, memories, and associated meanings (Tomanek 2022). Parental investment was found to be biased toward children named after close relatives (Šaffa et al. 2021).

Individuals who are named after deceased relatives often must cope with decisions made by their parents at pivotal moments when the name bearers had no ability to participate or consent, and these decisions deeply affect their lives. Boszormenyi‐Nagy's contextual family theory provides a framework for understanding these dynamics, highlighting four intersubjective dimensions: facts, individual psychology, transactional patterns, and relational ethics (Le Goff 2001). In this framework, the inherently asymmetrical parent–child relationship involves a delicate balance between entitlement and indebtedness. Naming a child after a deceased family member can thus be understood as a parent's expression of grief, a symbolic act intended to commemorate or honor the lost individual. However, this practice inevitably marks the child in a significant and lasting way, creating a symbolic bond between living and deceased that the child did not choose and typically cannot openly negotiate (Leichtentritt and Mahat Shamir 2025).

Naming practices involving deceased relatives also represent forms of continuing bonds, embodying memorialization and intergenerational continuity (Silverman and Klass 1996). The act of naming after a deceased person involves cultural dualities, reflecting both broad societal traditions and intimate, local family dynamics (Abu Hatab 2021; Erdem and Safi 2018). Cultural attitudes toward this practice vary widely. For example, in Polish culture, naming a child after a deceased sibling or living parent is often perceived as psychologically burdensome and indicative of unresolved grief; thus, it is recommended only as a secondary or middle name to minimize identity conflicts (Tomanek 2022). In line with this study, Testoni et al. (2021) presented findings of a qualitative study that explored the experience of being named after a deceased brother. The researchers highlighted the emotional toll the name carriers faced as they struggled to establish personal independent identity in a familial context that marked their being with everlasting reminders of an early loss and the related emotions it automatically evoked.

Seen from another perspective, in Arab cultures naming children after ancestors or revered figures, such as the Prophet Muhammad, strengthens familial and cultural continuity, underscoring the importance of heritage preservation (Abu Hatab 2021). Similarly, the Ashkenazi Jewish tradition views naming people after deceased relatives as essential to maintaining intergenerational continuity, positioning the child as a “living monument” of familial memory (Zerubavel 2007). On the other hand, in some cultures, naming is done only after the baby is born, based on the context and the newborn's temperament and character. Giving unique symbolic names to children is a common practice in these communities (e.g., Agyekum 2006).

Naming practices also reflect broader cultural tensions between tradition and modernization. Parents in both developing and Western societies may use naming to express desires for assimilation into modern cultural contexts (e.g., Mori‐Kolbe 2020; Ogihara et al. 2015). Conversely, parents in traditional societies often choose names rooted in cultural or religious heritage, highlighting their desire to preserve identity in an increasingly globalized world (Al‐Qawasmi and Al‐Haq 2016; Letavajová 2021). Thus, naming decisions exist along a continuum shaped by the ongoing tension between traditional and contemporary forces (Felecan and Bugheșiu 2021; Stahl 1992).

Prior research predominantly explored naming from cultural perspectives or the standpoint of name givers. We aimed to address this paucity of research using a social work perspective and highlight how cultural practices shape lived experience, identity formation, and intergenerational narratives of care, grief, and belonging. We sought to fill this significant gap by focusing on the lived experiences of individuals named after deceased relatives. By illuminating these personal narratives, this research sought to deepen the understanding of relational dynamics, familial obligations, and ethical considerations inherent in naming practices tied to commemoration and intergenerational memory.

Method

This study employed a qualitative, interpretive phenomenological approach (Smith 2019) designed to explore individuals' lived experiences of a particular phenomenon from their perspective. This methodology was well suited to address the study's aim of gaining a deep understanding of the experiences of participants named after deceased ancestors they never met, yet were compelled to relate to due to the persistent reminder of their connection to their namesake.

Participants

After obtaining approval from the second author's university's institutional review board, purposive sampling was used to recruit participants via social media (mainly Facebook). A general call for participation was published as a public post (not in a specific group), inviting individuals who met the criteria of being named after a deceased family member to participate in this study. Fourteen individuals who met the eligibility criteria responded to the invitation, contacted the authors via email, and completed the interview. Recruitment stopped once thematic saturation was achieved (Hennink et al. 2017). All names used in this paper are pseudonyms.

All the participants were Jewish; 10 came from European or Ashkenazi backgrounds, and four came from North African backgrounds. Eight participants were Orthodox Jews and six identified as secular. Participants' average age was 38.2 (SD = 15.5). Twelve participants were married with children, one was divorced, and one was single. Nine participants had a commemorative first name; for five others, it was a second name used less frequently in daily life. Two participants were named after fallen relatives who had been Israel Defense Forces soldiers, five were named after individuals who died prematurely in civilian circumstances (such as an illness or tragic accident), two were named after relatives who were Holocaust victims, and six were named after relatives who passed away naturally in old age.

Recruitment through social media meant that participants self‐selected into the study, indicating that they were literate in Hebrew, active on social media, and motivated to share personal and familial experiences of naming and legacy. This approach may have excluded individuals who were less involved in social media or less comfortable discussing emotionally charged topics. The research team remained reflexively aware of these boundaries of inclusion and the potential power imbalance between researcher and participant, striving to maintain an atmosphere of empathy, respect, and collaboration throughout the interviews.

Interviews

After reviewing information about the study purpose and procedures, those who wished to participate provided signed informed consent. Each participant took part in a single in‐depth interview lasting approximately 3 h (ranging from 1 h and 45 min to 3.5 h), conducted in their home in a private setting where only the interviewer and participant were present. Participants were invited to choose the location of the interview, and all expressed a preference for being interviewed in their home, which they perceived as comfortable, safe, and conducive to open conversation. This setting also aligned with the study's relational, participant‐centered approach and respected cultural norms that value hospitality and familiarity, thereby supporting both emotional safety and authenticity in the dialogue. Because data saturation was achieved, repeat interviews were deemed unnecessary and therefore were not conducted. The interviews were conducted by three research assistants, all of whom are advanced‐level social workers (two women and one man) and none of whom are authors of this manuscript. Interviewers were trained by the authors in qualitative interviewing techniques. No prior relationship existed between the interviewers and participants before the commencement of the study. Participants were informed of the study objectives, including its focus on exploring the experiences of individuals named after a deceased family member.

Regarding potential biases and assumptions, the interviewers reflected on their attitudes toward the research topic, specifically issues related to child naming. None of the interviewers was named after a deceased relative. These reflections were regularly documented and discussed with the authors, ensuring transparency and mitigating potential biases throughout data collection and analysis. The research team comprised Israeli social work academics and practitioners with extensive experience in qualitative and grief‐related research. The researchers have Jewish cultural backgrounds, similar to the participants, yet hold diverse levels of religious observance and social worldviews. This shared cultural familiarity facilitated rapport and understanding, and the team remained mindful of potential assumptions arising from these commonalities. Throughout the research process, the team engaged in reflexive discussions regarding their social locations, professional roles, and emotional proximity to the phenomenon of naming after deceased relatives. These reflections were documented and revisited throughout data collection and analysis, promoting transparency, relational accountability, and awareness of interpretative influence. This reflexive stance, grounded in the ethos of anti‐oppressive social work, guided the team's engagement with participants as co‐constructors of meaning rather than subjects of inquiry.

All interviews were conducted in Hebrew, and quotes were translated word for word into English at the writing stage. Transcripts were returned to participants for feedback and validation; all participants reviewed and confirmed the accuracy of their transcripts.

A semistructured interview guide was designed with open‐ended questions to encourage participants to share their perceptions of their name. The guide was developed based on the literature and reviewed for clarity, though it was not formally pilot tested. It included questions such as: “Please tell me about your name; who are you named after?”; “Please tell me how and why your name was chosen”; “Please share your feelings and thoughts regarding your name”; “Do you feel connected to the relative you are named after? If so, in what way?”; “Did carrying this name affect you throughout the years and if so, in what ways?”; and “How do others react to your name?”

Such questions were intended to facilitate in‐depth exploration of participants' emotional, social, and personal experiences related to their experience of being named after a deceased family member. Importantly, ethical considerations extended beyond procedural approval and informed consent to include relational and reflexive ethics central to qualitative social work research. The research team was attentive to potential emotional risks during interviews, adopting an empathetic, participant‐centered stance and allowing breaks or withdrawal at any time. Power dynamics were mitigated through the use of conversational, nondirective interviewing and by emphasizing participants' expertise in their experiences. Interviews were conducted in a flexible manner, allowing questions to be adapted according to the flow of conversation and participants' responses, which facilitated comfortable, natural dialogue. The researchers, all trained in social work, engaged in ongoing reflexive discussions to ensure emotional safety and ethical sensitivity throughout the study. Moreover, given the sensitive nature of the topic, participants were informed that follow‐up support was available, either from the research team, composed of trained clinical social workers, or through referral to external mental health services. None of the interviewees, however, chose to use this support.

Analysis

Analysis followed interpretive phenomenological analysis procedures outlined by Smith et al. (2021) that uniquely emphasize an idiographic approach, analyzing each case meticulously and exploring response patterns across cases. The analysis involved line‐by‐line transcript examination, focusing on descriptive, linguistic, and conceptual elements. The first stage involved maintaining an open mind and exploratory attitude to facilitate a comprehensive, detailed account of the data (Smith et al. 2021). Initial margin notes were transformed into emergent experiential themes in the opposite margin to capture key elements of participants' experiences succinctly. Themes were clustered, and superordinate themes were generated using abstraction and subsumption techniques within and across cases (Smith et al. 2021). The first author led the initial analytic steps, after which the data were audited by the second author, who read over the transcripts and emergent themes at each stage. This enabled a rigorous search for assumptions that may have influenced the analysis; only minor modifications, mainly regarding semantics, resulted from this auditing.

Later stages of analysis and writing were collaborative, with the authors sharing, challenging, and modifying concepts (Eatough and Smith 2017). Both authors discussed each other's interpretations of the data and contributed to the writing process. Finally, reflection was used for verification (Smith and McGannon 2017); participants were asked to review the transcribed interviews and the researchers' interpretations and indicate if they were representative of their experiences. All participants affirmed the findings and suggested theoretical interpretations, confirming their relevance to their lived experience.

Credibility and Transferability

Several strategies were implemented to enhance the findings' credibility and transferability (Denzin and Lincoln 2000). First, the use of in‐depth, semistructured interviews enabled consistency checking and inconsistency clarification during the interview process. Second, interviews were conducted to capture the realities and perspectives of participants as accurately as possible; therefore, no attempts were made to push participants toward a certain way of thinking during interviews, and many opportunities were provided for them to clarify their thoughts. Additionally, discussions between the interviewers and the two authors were consistently used to identify potential biases and preconceived assumptions.

Third, credibility and transferability were enhanced by the analysis being conducted by two researchers of different academic backgrounds, life stages, and professional and personal experiences with bereaved individuals. Despite these differences, no major disagreements regarding the analyses occurred; moreover, their analysis was later examined by a group of colleagues to ensure adherence to the research process. Finally, a member check was performed, wherein research participants received prepublication versions of the research report (Smith and McGannon 2017) to provide feedback on the accuracy and reasonableness of the data interpretation. Member checking, defined as seeking feedback from participants about data or interpretations (Lincoln and Guba 1985), has gained such pervasive and consistent endorsement as a validity measure that it is frequently regarded as essential for robust qualitative research.

Thus, the data and researchers' interpretations were consistently judged from various perspectives at all stages of analysis (Denzin and Lincoln 2000). This way of working is in keeping with Benner et al. (2009) assertion that using multiple readers to interpret text often leads to additive insights. Furthermore, providing a detailed description of the analysis and using participants' exact quotes enables the ability to assess the process through which the results were obtained and critically evaluate the researchers' interpretation of participants' input.

Findings

All study participants were named after individuals who had died before their birth. For some participants, the name held personal significance, as it did for other members of their family. In contrast, for others, the name was devoid of meaning for both them and their family members and was given mainly as a cultural ritual, intended to appease external forces acting on the nuclear family.

Accordingly, the research findings reveal a range of experiences positioned along two continuums presented here as two themes. The first, “Living in the Light or Living in the Shadow: Connection to or Recoil from the Name,” presents participants' perceptions of their name as a representation of a deep personal connection and sense of belonging or feelings of distance and detachment. The second theme, “Hidden Identity: Name Selection as Social Compliance and Continuing Bonds with the Deceased,” relates to the motivations behind participants' name selection: from social appeasement and external ritual to a genuine connection to the meaning of commemoration and continuing bonds. This continuum was expressed through various aspects of enduring relationships and the intergenerational transmission of connections with the deceased.

The findings reveal that naming a child after a deceased family member inevitably created a link between the commemorated individual and the name bearer, as part of the relational dynamics in the family system. Shira (32, Ashkenazi, orthodox), who was named after her mother's brother, who died in a military accident, articulated this trend well:

When someone decides to name a person after someone else, they form a bond between three parties: the person being commemorated and their family, the parents who make the decision, and the one who carries the name. It's very beautiful—it connects generations and different family circles.

Living in the Light or Living in the Shadow: Connection to or Recoil From the Name

Naming a child can be a complicated task. In many cases, it holds a legacy that the name bearer is compelled to carry or conversely, resists carrying. Participants described this situation as binding, carrying an emotional weight—whether positive or negative—that demanded acknowledgment.

On one end of this continuum, seven participants described the honor they felt carrying the name of a former respected family member. These participants described a special connection they had with their ancestors and their family as a whole, in its abstract, holistic meaning. For example, Ofra (30, Ashkenazi, orthodox), who was named after her maternal grandmother, said: “I have positive feelings towards my name, as I know it means a lot for my mother and that for her, it represents love and connection. Thus, the fact that she gave it to me warms my heart.” In another interview, Abraham (19, North African, secular), who was named after his mother's father, said:

At some point of my adolescence, my mother wanted to officially change my name, as it was old‐fashioned. But I objected and insisted on keeping it, as it is an honor for me to carry his name! It makes me feel as if I continue his life path.

These intergenerational bonds seem like a living manifestation of the family as a moral value, more than a mere structural concept. Parental loyalty to deceased family members is often transmitted intergenerationally through the naming of children. In some cases, such as that of Abraham, this inherited loyalty may be internalized to such an extent that the child's sense of allegiance surpasses that of the parents.

However, other participants described a more complex attitude toward their name and the reasons it was chosen. For example, Benjamin (41, Ashkenazi, orthodox), who was named after his grandfather's brother, who was killed as a soldier in the 1973 Yom Kippur War, described how his mother was 16 when her uncle died. During the shiva (mourning period), she promised her mother (the interviewee's grandmother) that if she had a boy, she would name him after her uncle. In his interview, Benjamin elaborated on what this meant for him:

It's very meaningful to me, and it feels like a responsibility, an obligation to honor his memory. Throughout the years, I received the feeling that he was a very special person and that I must honor his memory. I've attended his memorials for as long as I can remember, visiting the cemetery and taking part in the remembrance. And at every memorial ceremony, I conduct a personal reckoning, evaluating where I stand in relation to where I believe I should be, given that I carry the name of someone who died for the nation.

I mostly think about the terrible tragedy that befell the nation—the loss and bereavement, both collective and personal—by the fact that he died so young.

Beside the intergenerational connection in the family and national context embedded in his personal name, Benjamin illuminated the responsibility placed on him as a name bearer toward the person being commemorated and society on a larger scale. His words reveal a strong link between the personal identity of both people—him and the deceased—alongside a broader public and social identity oriented toward the collective. His developmental path was shaped in light of this sense of duty and loyalty to others and by honoring the name he received. His annual introspection regarding the extent to which he has fulfilled the unspoken obligations to which he feels bound might be both challenging and potentially stressful. Still, he said the man he commemorates gave his life for the greater good, both in life and in death, and it seems that this element of the family's story has continued to resonate through the interviewee's life as an intergenerational link. Benjamin elaborated:

As a child I didn't feel connected to my name, because of its complexity and its heaviness. Over the years, I realized that it is the person who gives meaning to the name, not the name that defines the person. I believe that when someone is named after another person—especially when it's an uncommon and complex name—it requires effort and emotional processing by the one carrying it, and this process unfolds in stages.

His words highlight the fact that alongside the sense of duty and honor in bearing the name of someone deemed worthy of commemoration, it is also burdensome, raising mixed feelings. Other participants described this process, emphasizing the complex feelings they hold toward this fundamental element of their identity. David (42, North African, secular), who was named after his grandfather, elaborated:

Being named after someone is problematic for the name barrier, because they are bound to carry a burden throughout their life. From the day they are born, they are expected to live up to others' expectations. … They basically live in the shadow of the person whose name they carry, and there is always a comparison. … Still, people turn out different from what was expected of them, and when they're named after someone, it can be harmful. … I don't like it, and that's why I didn't name any of my own children after anyone else.

Unlike Benjamin, who spoke of the value‐based connection he feels to his name and the person he commemorates, David emphasized the complexity of name selection and the multidirectional nature of the process, which begins with a single moment of choice and influences the lives of those involved throughout their entire life, not necessarily in a positive way. Accordingly, about half of the participants expressed the other end of the continuum: a desire to change their name, a feeling they had carried since childhood. For example, Bella (84, Ashkenazi, secular), who was named after her paternal grandmother, who had died relatively young, said:

At some point, I wanted to change my name, like some of my friends had done, but I didn't dare. … I wanted to disconnect from the meaning tied to the family I was named after, which was connected to the Holocaust. I didn't want to be the family memento.

Bella clarified how the name she was chosen to carry did not fit her wishes. Commemorating the distant family past overshadowed her development, and she described a sense of alienation and conflict regarding this part of her life. Unlike Benjamin, who seemed to resolve his conflict regarding the imposed connection with members of previous family generations, Bella's decision not to change her name seemed to reflect a double‐edged situation. Because she never shared her feelings with her parents, it remained only a wish. In another interview, Elon (30, Ashkenazi, secular), a man with a second name of a distant, childless aunt, elaborated:

I never asked my grandmother about the choice of my name because there was simply no room for it. There was no consideration of whether the child would want or need the second name—it was just obvious that this is what you do, and that's it. And even now, as I talk about it, I feel like you're interviewing someone else. That it has nothing to do with me.

His words provide a striking example of emotional detachment from a name, even while carrying it throughout life.

Hidden Identity: Name Selection as Social Compliance and Continuing Bonds With the Deceased

Participants' ongoing connections with the figure of the person after whom they were named seemed to reflect their family relations. Some mentioned how others' reactions toward them made them realize they carry a hidden identity. For example, Rona (56, Ashkenazi, orthodox), who was named after her grandfather's mother, described: “Every time we met, his eyes would fill with tears because I reminded him of his mother. He didn't cry. And it's important for me to mention that I really don't like my name.” In another interview, Ezra (40, Ashkenazi, orthodox), who was named after his mother's brother, noted:

My grandfather had a hard time being around me. He couldn't say my name [because it was his dead son's name]. So, whenever he saw me, he would call me “Eve's Ezra.” He needed to literally separate me from the person he saw when I was around.

Ezra's account reflects the emotional complexity that memorial naming can evoke in families, whereby the name simultaneously connects and distances generations. For his grandfather, the name reactivated grief and required linguistic separation to manage the pain of recognition. Other participants described how the emotional responses of their family members made them realize they are seen not only as who they are, but also as representations of the ongoing emotional ties with other members of their family—the people they commemorate. Acknowledging this complex identity evoked mixed emotions regarding their connection to the person they were named after. Some participants described feeling like they were carrying a hidden passenger or shadow identity that had followed them since birth. For example, Rebecca (34, Ashkenazi, secular), who was named after her maternal grandmother, said:

Naming a child after a respected family member can instill honor and reverence, but it may also create pressure. He might fear disappointing others by acting in ways deemed inappropriate, ultimately feeling restricted from living as his authentic self, who he really is.

Her words shed light on the experience of having a dual inner identity and being a continuation of another person's life. The obligation to the deceased they commemorate can be an emotional burden that shapes their motivation and behavior. Ezra (40, Ashkenazi, orthodox), who was named after his uncle, who died in a traumatic event at age 15, described this complex relationship:

As a child, having a strange and unique name mostly felt like a burden to me. It was terribly embarrassing. … The meaning of my name is a value‐based statement, that “We stand with the outsider and the weak,” but that's something I only came to understand as an adult.

Alongside these complicated relations with his name, he elaborated on the feeling of living in the shadow of another person's life and death:

I actually counted down the years until I turned 15, the age at which he died. Then came my 16th birthday, and that fear disappeared [laughs]. … I realized I was carrying some sort of a burden for my grandparents, and that this was about something beyond my personal relationship with them. I was a living memory of something that was important and deeply meaningful to them. … I'm a memorial site [laughs]. Even if it was unspoken and beneath the surface.

Ezra said he does not share the deep meaning others find in his name. The emotional burden he felt since early childhood was reflected in his fear that he might die young, like the person he commemorates—a concept he referred to as the parallel age. Coping with such circumstances might evoke mixed feelings regarding the name and connections to its origin. In a similar context, Elon (30, Ashkenazi, secular), the man who bears the second name of a distant aunt, reflected on the complexity of living in the shadow of a woman's commemorative name:

The name is just the symptom, the surface—it doesn't bother me at all. But its meaning, the narrative behind it, that's what matters. The name they gave me sounds like an American male name. I like American culture, so it sounds cool to me. But because it's after a woman, it carries a completely different tone. It sounds heavy, old, ancient, feminine.

Elon's description sheds light on another facet of the ongoing internal tension of carrying another person's name: rejection of its meaning to minimize its influence over self‐perceptions. Throughout the interview, he complained about the fact that his siblings did not receive a commemorative name, especially not one of the opposite sex. This, he suggested, represents a relatively extreme application of the commemorative naming tradition, as an outward‐facing act of social appeasement, rather than a personal and meaningful connection to the person being commemorated. Sara (33, North African, secular), who was named after her maternal grandmother, described her obligation to a person she never met. Her words exemplify another aspect of the social level of naming:

Sometimes I feel guilty when I think I haven't lived up to the standards of the other, “first” Sara. I always feel like there are eyes watching to see whether I'm fulfilling that or not. Being named after someone isn't only an honor—it's a responsibility. As a child, I tried harder to match those expectations. Today, I'm learning to listen more to myself and to what feels right for me. But she lives within me, in some way, and I feel that I carry her with me.

The conflict between her self‐loyalty and the expectations imposed on her by her family as the “continuation of the first Sara” manifested in moments of criticism she described throughout the interview and at peak moments of her life, during which she chose to engage in an imaginary dialogue with her deceased namesake, drawing strength from that connection. This identity complex can be interpreted in various psychological ways. According to the participants of the current study, it became internalized throughout their development in diverse ways, through symbolic acts and more explicit expressions. Jakob (38, North African, orthodox), named after his paternal grandfather, a Holocaust survivor who died relatively young from an illness, explained his role as a link between family generations:

My older brothers never traveled to visit the concentration camps because they were always told there wasn't money for it, and my father, in general, didn't want them to go there. But when I came of age, I said I wanted to go, and I spoke to my grandmother, who gave her approval for the trip, and we found funding. After I came back, I visited her several times, and she was very happy that I visited there. Upon my return, she told us things we had never known about the war, the family, and about the liberation from the camps. … I was glad it happened, because before that we knew nothing about their early life. They had never spoken about it. My grandfather kept his shirt from Auschwitz until she forced him to throw it away, but they never told us anything about that time. So, I felt like I was sent there, and that trip was very meaningful to her and to the entire family.

In a seemingly unconscious and unplanned way, Jakob served as a central link in the family chain. Through him, the family could begin to speak more openly about his grandfather's past. Moreover, they could engage more fully with painful and difficult events that had left emotional imprints and shaped relational patterns in the family. Again, it appears that carrying a commemorating name brings with it an emotional weight.

Other participants described mixed feelings regarding their commemorative role in their families and expressed emotional detachment when it came to references to the person they were named after. These silences and emotional avoidances framed the familial relationship to loss embodied in the commemorative name as a kind of taboo—something not to be questioned or spoken about. Benjamin (41, Ashkenazi, orthodox), named after his grandfather's brother, who was killed as a soldier in the 1973 Yom Kippur War, described the movement between his emotional connection to the name and disconnection from its complex meaning:

My grandfather sometimes commented on how much I resembled him, even though I didn't see any resemblance. For example, when I enlisted and came home in uniform, at first glance he was stunned and said, “You look so much like him.” But I didn't dwell on it or give it much thought over the years. When you ask about it now, I'm glad to know that, at the very least, it brought my grandfather some comfort.

Suppression of individual needs in favor of the family stands out clearly in these examples, alongside the assumption that this family behavioral and moral code will pass on to future generations. Implicitly, there is also an expectation that the name bearer will accept this arrangement and act in accordance with this norm, which has been instilled in them from birth. However, half of the study participants explicitly noted that the burden of carrying the family's commemorative legacy was an emotionally complex experience that they would not have chosen, had they received the option. Jakob (38, North African, orthodox), who was named after his paternal grandfather, described it well, saying: “It feels like too much for a person to carry.”

Still, some participants identified with the family tradition and its significance. For example, Noa (32, Ashkenazi, orthodox), who was named after her father's childless aunt, described it as follows:

As I matured, I realized the meaning of commemoration, and I felt privileged to be named after someone who was meaningful to our family heritage. I believe it adds meaning and highlights both persons' value and helps preserve the family meanings.

On the other hand, some participants complained about the binding and nonnegotiable nature of the connection, because changing a name in adulthood is perceived as a relatively extreme step in altering a significant identity component. For example, Noach (30, Ashkenazi, secular), named after his grandfather's brother, who was killed while fleeing due to his Jewish identity during World War II, said:

I won't name my child after anyone. I value my family, but I don't believe in this particular tradition, as it marks the person without giving him an option to choose his path in life. I believe people should raise and educate their children in a noncoercive way. My mother didn't understand that at the time.

Discussion

The findings presented in this study resonate deeply with Boszormenyi‐Nagy's theoretical framework of familial loyalty and relational ethics, providing a meaningful lens to interpret participants' varied experiences (Boszormenyi‐Nagy and Spark 1973). This approach considers that loyalty in families operates as an invisible fabric, featuring structured group expectations to which all family members implicitly adhere. This notion illuminates why commemorative naming creates such profound emotional implications, binding the name carrier in an implicit relational contract involving previous and subsequent generations.

Boszormenyi‐Nagy's conceptualization of relational ethics was deeply influenced by Martin Buber's philosophy of dialogue, particularly his distinction between “I–Thou” and “I–It” relationships (Buber 1923/1958). In I–Thou relations, individuals engage with one another as whole, subjective beings, fostering mutual recognition, responsibility, and ethical presence. In contrast, I–It relations treat the other as an object or means to an end, diminishing reciprocity and moral awareness. Drawing on Buber, Boszormenyi‐Nagy extended this ethical orientation to the family system, proposing that human connectedness entails intergenerational obligations of fairness, trust, and care. In this framework, commemorative naming can be understood as an I–Thou gesture that sustains the moral dialogue between the living and the deceased, while also carrying the potential to become an I–It dynamic when the name imposes inherited expectations or unresolved debts on the bearer.

Participants in the current study articulated experiences of inherited loyalty, which aligns with Boszormenyi‐Nagy's concept of family accounts, wherein each generation inherits debts and obligations from preceding generations (Boszormenyi‐Nagy and Spark 1973). As illustrated by Abraham's and Benjamin's narratives, their loyalty to deceased family members became internalized, surpassing even their parents' loyalty, thereby affirming Boszormenyi‐Nagy's view that such intergenerational obligations profoundly shape individual identities and family dynamics (Boszormenyi‐Nagy and Spark 1973). Abraham's insistence on retaining his grandfather's name, despite parental reservations, exemplifies how loyalty operates as a source of individual and familial resilience rather than a mere constraint. These findings elaborate on previous knowledge pertaining to similar conditions among people who carry their late sibling's name (e.g., Testoni et al. 2021).

Conversely, the sense of burden articulated by participants such as David, Bella, and Elon underscores Boszormenyi‐Nagy's assertion that accumulated familial debts across generations may impose emotional weight and psychological strain, potentially leading to relational difficulties. These participants described their commemorative names as burdens or sources of emotional detachment, echoing Boszormenyi‐Nagy's warning that unresolved familial obligations can generate individual and relational dysfunctions. In some cases, these unresolved obligations extended beyond the participant's experience, shaping the reactions of family members to the living name bearer. For example, Ezra recalled that his grandfather could not bring himself to say Ezra's name because it carried the memory of his grandfather's deceased son, highlighting how naming can reawaken grief, ambivalence, or even avoidance among relatives. David explicitly voiced his resistance to imposing a similar legacy on his children, highlighting his critical awareness of the intergenerational transmission of obligations and debts (Boszormenyi‐Nagy 1987).

The complexity of commemorative naming practices further reflects Boszormenyi‐Nagy's dialectical theory of personality and conceptualization of ontic dependence, in which individual identity and autonomy inherently emerge through relational contexts (Boszormenyi‐Nagy 1987). Participants frequently described navigating a delicate balance between personal identity formation (on the entitlement end) and familial expectations (on the indebtedness end), embodying the paradoxical notion of autonomy achieved through relational connection rather than separation. Benjamin's, Ezra's, and Sara's experiences particularly highlight this dialectic: Their evolving identities had been shaped profoundly by ongoing dialogues—real or imagined—with their deceased namesake and family expectations, emphasizing how relational definitions of the self significantly contribute to individual identity formation.

Exoneration is a major process in this context, because it allows the easing of past emotional burdens of culpability from the shoulders of an individual who may previously have carried blame (Le Goff 2001). It may replace blame with a mature appreciation of a person's or situation's choices, thereby constituting an ethical reengagement. Thus, exoneration offers a significant opportunity to process emotions such as shame, blame, ambivalence, rage, grief, and guilt, which often lie between family members, as participants of the current study demonstrated in their depictions.

However, whereas Boszormenyi‐Nagy suggested that intergenerational family relations are usually dialogic and based on I–thou appreciation, the current study examined a case in which one generation appears to take advantage of its entitlement by using members of the next generation to fulfill its emotional needs. Whether the issue concerns grief or social belonging, participants highlighted the possibility of receiving exoneration through giving it; that is, they described having the option to offer their parents understanding and acceptance regarding their naming process. According to Boszormenyi‐Nagy, this process can be a profound source of relief and healing (Boszormenyi‐Nagy and Krasner 1986).

Furthermore, the concept of continuing bonds (Klass et al. 1996) emerges clearly in conjunction with familial loyalty. This framework posits that maintaining psychological connections with the deceased can play an adaptive role in bereavement processes. In our study, commemorative naming served as a tangible expression of these continuing bonds, reinforcing familial loyalty and sustaining connections across generations. Participants frequently described feeling ongoing emotional ties with deceased ancestors, indicating that commemorative names function as meaningful mediums through which the deceased remain symbolically present and influential in their lives. Such bonds intertwine closely with familial loyalty, reinforcing relational obligations and contributing to the individual's sense of identity and continuity. Previous research presented the contribution of a name to an individual's behavior, even in the case of family or last names (Yu et al. 2022).

Participants' testimonies also validated Boszormenyi‐Nagy's (1987) relational ethics theory, asserting that familial loyalty extends beyond immediate generations and includes responsibilities toward future family members. Participants explicitly acknowledged their roles as bridges connecting historical family trauma with future generations, thus engaging with what Boszormenyi‐Nagy termed “transgenerational solidarity,” an ethical imperative to consider the relational consequences for future generations.

Yet as Boszormenyi‐Nagy (1987) emphasized, intergenerational influences are predominantly unidirectional: Current and previous generations affect subsequent ones, but future generations cannot influence past generations. This unilateral influence places considerable ethical responsibility on current family members, because their actions and choices, such as naming, can have irreversible implications for descendants. This concept is vividly illustrated in the cases of Bella and Noach, whose inherited burdens underscore the significant, sometimes negative consequences that naming decisions can impose.

In conclusion, framing participants' narratives with Boszormenyi‐Nagy's theoretical constructs, alongside the continuing bonds perspective, provides deeper insight into the intricate dynamics surrounding commemorative naming. It clarifies how familial loyalty and continuing bonds function both as resources fostering resilience and connection and as potential sources of emotional burden and identity conflict. Ultimately, this approach underscores the ethical complexity and far‐reaching relational implications inherent in commemorative naming practices, advocating greater awareness and intentionality in familial decisions that affect future generations.

Clinical Implications

This study highlights important considerations for clinicians working with individuals named after deceased family members. Professionals should be aware of the dual effects of commemorative naming, which can foster resilience but also create emotional burdens. Therapy may involve exploring clients' relationship with their name, considering this aspect as central to their identity and family dynamics. Clinicians are encouraged to discuss how commemorative names influence clients' self‐perception, assessing whether the name is a source of pride or emotional strain. Therapeutic interventions can assist clients in processing complex emotions around inherited identities and intergenerational expectations, potentially reducing guilt or ambivalence.

Integrating Boszormenyi‐Nagy's relational ethics into therapeutic work may help clients achieve emotional resolution by reflecting on familial naming decisions and negotiating personal autonomy. Family therapy could further facilitate open dialogue about naming decisions, enhancing family cohesion and resolving personal and interpersonal conflicts. Last, psychoeducational interventions for prospective parents about the emotional implications of commemorative naming could mitigate potential psychological stress for future generations.

Study Limitations

Regarding study limitations, participants self‐selected into the study by responding to a public call for participation. This may indicate that their commemorative names hold particular personal or emotional significance or that they were more inclined toward reflection on this topic than others in Israeli society who share similar naming experiences but chose not to participate. Although this self‐selection may limit the transferability of findings, it also provided insight into the experiences of individuals for whom the meaning of their commemorative name is especially salient. Moreover, this study relied on a specific, relatively small cohort of Jewish Israeli citizens. Although Israeli culture is very diverse and the participants had varying levels of religious orthodoxy, their cultural norms and values do not represent the diversity of Israeli or Jewish cultures and may have influenced how they perceived and expressed their experiences. Participants from different cultures and societies, where other cultural codes are observed, might illuminate different aspects of the studied phenomenon. For example, it is possible that people from traditional and collectivist cultures, where the public good is more highly valued than the individual's needs, may interpret intergenerational naming traditions differently. However, the presented commemorative naming dynamics may apply to other cultural contexts, such as traditional or collectivist societies. Further research is recommended to explore how these dynamics manifest across varied cultural, familial, and community settings. Such research might better explain the broader aspects and various nuances of naming and its relation to the intergenerational transmission of grief and familial patterns.
