Deep contrastive learning framework identifies cell‐type‐specific drug targets in Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Yuxin Yang, Jielin Xu, Yuan Hou, Yadi Zhou, Andrew J Saykin, Feixiong Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONIdentifying disease‐modifying drug targets is crucial for developing effective Alzheimer's disease (AD) treatments.METHODSWe present a deep contrastive learning framework for cell type‐specific AD‐associated genes identification (alzCL). alzCL creates cell‐type‐specific representations of genes by integrating human brain single‐nucleus RNA‐sequencing data with the human protein–protein interactome, thereby capturing both genetic signatures and functional features.RESULTSBy integrating human brain snRNA‐seq data, alzCL outperforms the state‐of‐the‐art models by 18% to 24% in area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Via alzCL, we computationally identified 16, 164, and 221 AD‐associated genes across astrocytes, microglia, and inhibitory neurons, respectively. Top prioritized genes (e.g., MAP3K5, P2RX4, and PRKD1) are significantly enriched in multiple AD‐associated inflammatory and other pathobiological pathways. By integrating drug–target interaction data with alzCL‐predicted AD‐associated genes, we identified potential repurposable drugs for AD, including selonsertib and paroxetine.DISCUSSIONAlzCL offers a deep contrastive learning framework for discovery of disease‐associated genes and drug targets in AD.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, artificial intelligence, contrastive learning, deep learning, drug repurposing, protein–protein interaction

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD), the leading cause of dementia, is a neurodegenerative disease with notable symptoms like cognitive decline and memory loss. 1 AD is estimated to affect 40 million people worldwide currently and is projected to affect more than 100 million people in 2050 due to aging populations. 2 Accumulation of amyloid beta (A β) plaque and hyperphosphorylated tau in brain is believed to be correlated with AD development. 3 Existing treatments of AD primarily provide symptomatic relief while doing little to stop disease progression. 4 Most clinical trials only target well‐studied disease pathways, such as A β, yielding less desirable results and even doing more harm than good. 5 Discovering new genes associated with AD can reveal understudied pathways and offer insightful tips for potential drug targets that can aim at the underlying cause of AD, thereby halting disease progression. 6

It is crucial to discover new AD genes in a cell‐type‐expressed manner as the brain comprises numerous cell types, such as astrocytes, neurons, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and endothelial cells, and different cell‐type‐expressed functions distinctively. Identifying cell‐type‐expressed genes that are associated with AD can help develop more targeted treatments, which can regulate specific disease‐associated pathways within cell types that are mostly related to AD instead of modifying disease‐associated pathways within the brain entirely. 7 Additionally, analysis of gene expression at the single‐cell level could lead to a better understanding of the underlying mechanisms of AD and accurate modification of disease‐associated pathways. 8

Human protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks provide insight into how proteins function and interact. Proteins function, interact, and express differently in different cell types. Existing PPI network‐based deep learning approaches, such as NETTAG, have provided critical insights into AD and identified AD‐associated genes. 9 , 10 However, one primary weakness of these models is they rely solely on generic PPI networks. Using a multistage and systems biology method could help in understanding disease mechanisms. 11 Leveraging gene co‐expression network analytic methods to study brain gene expression profiles of AD patients could offer valuable insights in discovering AD‐associated genes. 12 One recent study revealed the importance of leveraging single‐cell‐level PPI networks in providing insights into human diseases. 13 Yet enabling deep learning models to fully harness all available cell‐type‐expressed PPI networks and identify genes related to complex human diseases, especially AD, remains a key issue. Additionally, existing models fail to incorporate multi‐omics layers of genes, which is the key to bridge the gap between genotype and phenotype and increase comprehension about gene, cell, and tissue functions. Therefore, developing a new deep learning tool that thoroughly studies single‐cell‐level PPI networks, integrates multi‐omics information, and identifies AD‐associated genes is required to overcome the limitations in existing works.

In this study, we introduce a deep Contrastive Learning framework for identifying cell‐type‐expressed AD‐associated genes (termed alzCL). Compared with established methods, which exclusively use generic PPI networks and just conduct task‐expressed training of deep learning models, 9 , 10 our framework harnesses cell‐type‐expressed PPI networks and applies a pre‐training—fine‐tuning scheme to integrate information, improve generalization capability, and make predictions. Specifically, alzCL integrates genetic information about proteins and network topological information through contrastive pre‐training, which aggregates genes from the same cell type and separates genes from different cell types, followed by fine‐tuning of the alzCL framework to enable alzCL to identify AD‐associated genes. We leveraged the fine‐tuned alzCL framework to identify 16, 164, and 221 AD‐associated genes, across astrocytes, microglia, and inhibitory neurons, respectively, with complementary multi‐omics evidence. In summary, the alzCL framework provides a practical AI tool to rapidly identify disease‐associated genes for AD in a cell‐type‐expressed manner or AD‐related dementia if broadly applied.

METHODS

A Contrastive learning framework identifies AD‐associated genes

We developed a deep contrastive learning framework aimed at identifying cell‐type‐expressed AD‐related genes. This framework integrates genetic features and cell‐type‐expressed topological features for genes (Figure 1, Supplementary Methods). Genetic features obtained using Mendelian randomization (MR) provide important genetic information about how a gene is relevant to AD, while cell‐type‐expressed topological features constructed using node2vec 14 on each cell‐type‐expressed PPI network encode network topological information around each gene, enabling alzCL to understand how genes interact within a specific cell type. Graph neural networks (GNNs) built on GATv2 graph attention layers 15 efficiently learn representations from PPI networks through a dynamic attention mechanism. This dynamic attention mechanism allows GNNs to dynamically change the importance of neighboring genes within the cell‐type‐expressed PPI networks during the training process. The alzCL framework consists of four major steps:

Schematic illustration of alzCL. (A) Feature construction. Obtaining topological features from PPI network structure using node2vec. (B) Contrastive pre‐training. Topological and genetic features are combined in a graph neural network and projected onto a high‐dimensional space. Positive samples are subgraphs of PPI sharing the same cell‐type‐expressed PPI with the anchor sample, while negative samples are selected from cell‐type‐expressed PPI whose cluster center is closest to the cluster center of the anchor's cell‐type‐expressed PPI. (C) Fine‐tuning and prediction. A predictor composed of a multilayer perceptron (MLP) is appended to the contrastively pre‐trained graph neural network (GNN). The predictor is fine‐tuned on datasets highlighting AD‐associated genes. The predictor takes features of genes output from GNN and outputs the probability of a gene being AD‐associated. (D) Multi‐omics validation. Several multi‐omics evidence sources, including drug‐target, differentially expressed gene (DEG), pathway analysis, transcriptomics, and proteomics, are used to validate the prediction on which genes would be associated with AD.

Feature construction (Figure 1A), where node2vec 14 is used to calculate cell‐type‐expressed topological information for each gene in each cell‐type‐expressed PPI network, and QTL is used to obtain AD‐associated genetic information for each gene.

Contrastive pre‐training (Figure 1B), where a GNN is used to combine both genetic features and topologic vectors of genes before contrastively trained to aggregate genes within the same cell type and separate genes from different cell types. Specifically, both genetic features and topological features for each gene are projected onto the same space before being added together and projected to a lower dimension for contrastive pre‐training purpose. The pre‐trained GNN produces distinct representations for genes from different cell‐type‐expressed PPI networks.

Cell‐type‐expressed fine‐tuning and prediction (Figure 1C), where a predictor composed of a multilayer perceptron (MLP) is appended to the pre‐trained GNN before being fine‐tuned on AD‐associated genes training dataset to leverage learned cell‐type‐expressed representations of genes to predict cell‐type‐expressed relation scores for genes with AD.

Multi‐omics evidence validation (Figure 1D), where six different sources of multi‐omics evidence, including drug‐target, differentially expressed gene (DEG), and differentially expressed protein (DEP) evidence, were used to validate the prediction results.

RESULTS

Performance evaluation

We first comprehensively evaluated the performance of the alzCL framework on two sets of cell‐type‐expressed PPI networks using two large single‐nucleus transcriptomics datasets, Religious Orders Study/Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP) 16 (i) CELL2023 and (ii) ROSMAP NATURE2024 (Table S1). For each fine‐tuning dataset, we used 80% samples for each cell type as the training set and 20% samples for each cell type as the test set. We conducted 10‐fold cross‐validation on the training set for each cell‐type‐expressed PPI network. We assessed the performance of alzCL when using a cell‐type‐expressed PPI network as input and when using a generic PPI network as input. We observed that when using a cell‐type‐expressed PPI network dataset, alzCL fine‐tuned on the Open Target dataset showed an average improvement of 24.14% (0.828 vs. 0.667, p < 0.0001) in area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) compared to when using the generic PPI network (Figure 2A and Figure S1A). Notably, alzCL displayed very high accuracy on Oligodendrocyte from ROSMAP CELL2023 with an AUROC of 0.931 and Astrocyte from ROSMAP NATURE2024 with an AUROC of 0.945 (Figure 2A and Figure S1A). alzCL also showed similar results when fine‐tuned on the dataset based on DISEASES and Agora with an average improvement of 18.09% (0.743 vs 0.630, p < 0.0001) in AUROC by adopting the cell‐type‐expressed PPI network instead of the generic PPI network (Figure S2). Additionally, ablation studies confirmed that the integration of topological and genetic features, the GATv2 backbone, and contrastive pre‐training was essential for optimal performance (Figures S3 and S4 and Supplementary Materials S2.6). alzCL also demonstrated strong robustness to label noise when negative training sets were augmented with random unlabeled data (Figures S5–S8 and Supplementary Materials S2.7).

Identification of AD‐associated genes in a cell‐type‐expressed manner

We next focused on using alzCL to identify cell‐type‐expressed AD‐associated genes. We first used our trained alzCL model to generate AD‐association scores for all genes from all PPI networks (Method). Then we filtered out genes with an AD‐association score lower than 0.6, the threshold of being an AD‐associated gene of our model. In total, we identified 6340 potential AD‐associated genes from the ROSMAP CELL2023 dataset and 8827 potential AD‐associated genes from the ROSMAP NATURE2024 dataset (Table S2). Then we validated these genes using multi‐omics evidence. Specifically, we used six different evidence types, including drug‐target, DEG in disease‐associated astrocyte (DAA), DEG in disease‐associated microglia (DAM), DEG in brain bulk RNA‐seq, DEG in brain microarray, and differentially expressed protein (DEP) in brain proteomics. We retained the top 15 genes from each cell type with the highest predicted AD‐association score, at least one different type of evidence for analysis, and positive brain expression specificity. We identified a total of 82 cell‐type‐expressed genes that were related to AD from the ROSMAP NATURE2024 dataset (Figure 3) and a total of 114 genes from the ROSMAP CELL2023 dataset (Figure S9). We found 20 genes from the ROSMAP NATURE2024 and 16 genes from the ROSMAP CELL2023 datasets, respectively, with at least three different types of evidence. These six types of evidence displayed a low correlation and high dissimilarity (Figure S10 and Supplementary Materials S2.2), indicating each type of evidence captured distinct biological features.

Discovery of AD‐associated genes in glial cells and neurons

We next turned to reveal AD‐associated genes that had not yet been determined by existing works within the cell‐type context of glial cells (astrocytes and microglia) and inhibitory neurons. Astrocytes and microglia play key roles in the central nervous system (CNS) in regulating neural activity, brain homeostasis, and brain health, 17 while neurons are vital for brain activity and cognitive function. 18 Several genes were reported to be associated with AD by affecting the function or pathway of glial cells and neurons. 19 , 20 Here, we leveraged the astrocyte PPI network, microglial PPI network, and neuronal PPI network provided in the ROSMAP dataset 16 to discover AD‐associated genes within these cell types. There are 1005, 707, and 1880 distinct genes from the astrocyte PPI network, microglial PPI network, and neuronal PPI network, respectively. We first used alzCL to predict an AD‐association score for all these genes. We observed that 69, 540, and 915 genes from astrocytes, microglia, and inhibitory neurons, respectively, had a predicted AD‐association score exceeding 0.6. Subsequently, we collected genes that were known to be AD‐associated from Open Targets, 21 TIGA, 22 DISEASES, 23 Agora, 24 and ADSP GVC top hits genes, 25 resulting in 5098 different genes. We then removed genes that had known AD associations and focused on genes with at least one type of multi‐omics evidence from the predicted AD‐associated genes, which led to 16, 164, and 221 astrocyte‐, microglia‐, and inhibitory neuron‐expressed genes, respectively. We selected the top 10 genes from each cell type and their surrounding genes and drugs targeting these selected genes to visualize the PPI network (Figure 4A). We observed three genes (PRKD1, HAP1, and HDAC1) are shared between astrocyte PPI and neuronal PPI networks, while one gene (NFKB2) was shared between the astrocyte PPI and microglial PPI networks.

Discovery of AD‐associated genes in astrocyte

With respect to astrocyte‐expressed genes, three genes (CKB, PC, and FAH) exhibit three types of multi‐omics evidence and four genes (RGR, MAP3K5, PDLIM4, and PRKG1) exhibit two types of multi‐omics evidence (Table S3). We conducted pathway enrichment analysis using the Gene Ontology (GO) biological process (BP) 2023 gene set, GO molecular function (MF) 2023 gene set, Reactome pathways 2024 gene set, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) 2021 human gene set provided by Enrichr. 26 These 16 genes are significantly enriched (adjusted p < 0.05) in 49 GO BP pathways, seven GO MF pathways, and four KEGG pathways (Table S3). We found these 16 genes were significantly enriched in several pathways that are involved in AD pathology. For example, these 16 genes are significantly enriched in 10 interleukin (IL)‐related pathways and six DNA damage‐repair‐related pathways (Table S3). These 16 genes are also significantly enriched in six DNA damage‐repair‐related pathways (Table S3).

Consideration of the drug‐target network revealed six genes (PC, CKB, FAH, MAP3K5, PRKG1, and STAT5A) were drug targets for 20 drugs (such as staurosporine, selonsertib, midostaurin, and ipatasertib). We noticed that MAP3K5 (mitogen‐activated protein kinase kinase kinase 5), also known as ASK1 (apoptosis signal‐regulating kinase 1), had a predicted AD‐association score of 0.688. MAP3K5 is a hub gene in the astrocyte‐expressed PPI network (Figure 4A). MAP3K5 is significantly involved in several pathways regulating c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase (JNK) and mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades. Studies have indicated that both JNK and MAPK cascades are involved in undesirable processes, such as tau phosphorylation, amyloid plaque formation, and apoptosis of neurons in the brains of AD patients. 27 , 28 Additionally, MAP3K5 is a protein that promotes inflammation, apoptosis, and fibrosis. MAP3K5 is a target for several drugs, including selonsertib, staurosproine, and k252a (Figure 4A). Selonsertib is a selective inhibitor of MAP3K5 in clinical trials for pulmonary arterial hypertension disease. 29 We used AlphaFold3 30 to predict the binding model between MAP3K5 and selonsertib. We observed that selonsertib formed two hydrogen bonds with Val91 (Figure 4B), which indicates selonsertib binds strongly to MAP3K5. A predicted aligned error (PAE)‐based confidence metric also showed high confidence of the predicted binding mode (Figure S11). By using our in‐house blood‐brain barrier (BBB) permeability predictor, we showed that selonsertib was a BBB‐permeable drug (predicted BBB permeability score 0.826). We believe selonsertib could also be a therapeutic treatment for AD by targeting MAP3K5 in astrocytes. CKB (creatine kinase B), another top predicted AD‐associated gene with a prediction score of 0.667, is the target of creatine and phosphocreatine (PCr) in our drug‐target network (Figure 4A).

Discovery of AD‐associated genes in microglia

Concerning microglia‐expressed genes, one gene (C1QC) exhibits five different types of multi‐omics evidence, six genes (CTSZ, PYCARD, ALOX5AP, HK2, CD68, and CAPG) exhibit four types of multi‐omics evidence, and 16 genes exhibit three different types of multi‐omics evidence.

P2RX4 (purinergic receptor P2×4), the top predicted gene within these 164 genes with a predicted AD‐association score of 0.937, is a member of the P2X receptor family. 31 P2RX4 is involved in pathways related to inflammation (Table S3). Studies suggest P2RX4 is involved in several pathologies, such as inflammation and rheumatoid arthritis. 32 P2RX4 is a drug target for eslicarbazepine (treating epilepsy) and paroxetine (antidepressant drug) from our drug‐target network (Figure 4A). In previous studies, paroxetine showed promising results on AD mice, 33 and eslicarbazepine acetate could function as a dual therapy for both epilepsy and neurodegenerative diseases. 34 The binding model of P2RX4 and paroxetine predicted by AlphaFold330 suggested that paroxetine formed hydrogen bonds with Trp194, indicating a strong bond to P2RX4 (Figure 4C). SPHK1 (sphingosine kinase 1), with a predicted AD‐association score of 0.932, was found to have reduced levels in the AD brain, which may contribute to the dysfunction of microglia. 35 SPHK1 is enriched in several pathways related to the immune system, inflammatory response, and NK‐κ B. SPHK1 is a drug target for idronoxil (Figure 4A), a signal transduction inhibitor that induces apoptosis in cancer cells. 36 One study proposed idronoxil's potential as therapeutic treatment for amyloid plaques. 37 ST14 (suppressor of tumorigenicity 14), another highly predicted AD‐associated gene with a predicted score of 0.911, is a protease that is associated with cancer invasion and metastasis. 38 From our drug‐target network, eight drugs target ST14. Nafamostat is an anticoagulant targeting ST14 (Figure 4A). 39 Existing studies suggest anticoagulants are associated with a lower risk of AD and dementia. 40 IKBKE (inhibitor of nuclear factor κB kinase, subunit epsilon), with a predicted AD‐association score of 0.910, is a gene that regulates inflammatory responses through the activation of type I interferon (IFN), NF‐κ B, and STAT signaling. 41 IKBKE is enriched inNF‐κ B‐, immune system‐, IFN‐, and inflammation‐related pathways. Studies suggest that the inhibition of IKBKE could reduce inflammation, which could further slow the development of AD. 42

Discovery of AD‐associated genes in inhibitory neurons

As for inhibitory neuron‐expressed genes, one gene (LOX) exhibits four different types of multi‐omics evidence, and nine genes (ARL3, PTP4A3, CALB2, FAH, CA8, SLC7A8, RRAGD, KIAA1217, and SNRNP70) exhibit three different types of multi‐omics evidence.

RPKD1 (protein kinase D1), a predicted AD‐associated gene with a score of 0.870, is a gene that encodes a serine/threonine protein kinase involved in many cellular processes. 43 From our drug‐target network, PRKD1 is a target of 10 drugs. We noted bryostatin‐1, an investigational drug for HIV and AD, targets PRKD1. Existing studies suggest bryostatin‐1 is a promising drug to slow the progression of AD. 44 By applying AlphaFold330 to predict the binding mode of PRKD1 and bryostatin‐1, we observed that bryostatin‐1 formed hydrogen bonds with Lys612 and Asp665, indicating a strong bond with PRKD1 (Figure 4D). SLC47A1, another top 10 predicted AD‐associated gene with a prediction score of 0.868, is another solute carrier family gene. SLC47A1 is a target of 13 drugs. Notably, SLC47A1 is a target of metformin (Figure 4A), a type 2 diabetes drug. Existing studies suggest that metformin is a potential repurposable drug for AD. 45 , 46 These results demonstrate the capability and potential of alzCL for identifying AD‐associated genes and discovering potential uncharacterized AD‐associated genes.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we presented a cell‐type‐expressed AD‐associated gene identification framework based on contrastive learning called alzCL. Our proposed alzCL framework leverages the power of MR with quantitative trait loci (QTL), responsible for providing genetic information, and node2vec, 14 tailored to harness PPI network topological information. Through comprehensive evaluations on diverse datasets, we thoroughly assessed the alzCL framework's performance. In addition to performance evaluation, we discovered AD‐associated genes within glial cells (astrocytes and microglia) and neurons, because of the importance of glial cells and neurons in the brain system and AD progression. We identified 16 AD‐associated genes in astrocytes, 164 AD‐associated genes in microglia, and 221 AD‐associated genes in neurons. We further provided extensive evidence on the relation between these genes and the development, progression, and treatment of AD. Our comprehensive evaluations and detailed analyses firmly established that the fusion of genetic and topological information fueled with contrastive learning improves the predictive performance of deep learning model to identify AD‐associated genes. As a result, alzCL represents a significant advance in the field of genomic study, providing a robust framework for AD and other complex disease associated genes prediction if broadly applied.

However, we admit alzCL still has several potential limitations. First, the alzCL framework considers only interactions between proteins, neglecting the potential to incorporate drug–drug interactions and drug–protein interactions. One possible solution could be to integrate alzCL with information from compound‐protein interactions (CPIs). One recent study highlighted the potential of deep learning models in investigating CPIs. 47 Additionally, our framework only integrates genetic evidence and topological features of proteins, which ignores the three‐dimensional (3D) structure information of proteins, dynamic conformational changes in protein structures, protein functions, and mutation effects of proteins. Incorporating 3D structure information from state‐of‐the‐art AI models, such as AlphaFold3, 30 protein function models, 48 and mutation effects on proteins using AI models like AlphaMissense, 49 could provide additional information to alzCL and overcome a current limitation of alzCL. While alzCL displays a high predictive accuracy, its performance is inherently constrained by the quality and completeness of existing PPI networks, as well as the quality of snRNA‐seq data. Furthermore, alzCL primarily focuses on intracellular protein interactions. Integrating intercellular communication and spatial transcriptomics data would allow alzCL to capture a more thorough picture of AD and, ultimately, predict highly potent and cell‐type‐specific therapeutic targets. Finally, it is important to note that the use of node2vec to construct topological features based on global cell‐type‐expressed PPI networks leads to a transductive learning setting. To mitigate the risk of data leakage, the node2vec algorithm remains entirely label‐free. It has no access to the disease‐association label of any node during the random walk phase. Consequently, although the topological information is shared across data splits, the predictive label remains strictly isolated in the training set. In addition, we argue that a transductive approach is more appropriate for gene prioritization in PPI networks than an inductive learning setting. Removing test or validation genes from the PPI network to create a strictly inductive split would artificially separate the interactome, distort the functional neighborhoods of genes, and introduce embedding instability, where the same gene would receive different embeddings across cross‐validation folds. Since the human protein–protein interactome is relatively static, the focus of this work is not to generalize to unseen nodes but to uncover unknown disease associations and potential therapeutic targets for existing genes within established PPI networks. Experimental validation could further confirm the prediction made by alzCL. Furthermore, a notable limitation of the current study is the lack of orthogonal validation of predicted drug target candidates using high‐resolution spatial or histological data. While alzCL successfully prioritizes cell‐type‐specific candidate genes and drug targets, verifying these candidate genes and drug targets via spatial transcriptomics or immunohistochemistry is essential to confirming the cell‐type specificity of the predicted drug targets.

Even though we only studied the identification of AD‐associated genes using alzCL, we firmly believe that the alzCL framework has broader application in different complex diseases, such as Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, cardiovascular diseases, coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19). A promising future direction lies in integrating different types of networks, such as drug–drug networks and drug–disease networks, and different levels of networks, such as tissue‐level networks and organ‐level networks. Finally, experimental validation of these predicted AD‐associated genes using human and/or patient induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived cellular models is highly warranted. 50
