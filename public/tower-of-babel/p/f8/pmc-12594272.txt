Treatment variations in localised prostate cancer in Australia and New Zealand: a registry study
Authors: Karen Chiam, Albert Bang, Visalini Nair‐Shalliker, Michael Caruana, Manish I Patel, Shomik Sengupta, Ian Vela, Andrew Kneebone, Henry Woo, Michael O'Callaghan, Braden Higgs, Alex Jay, Karen Canfell, David P Smith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe the patterns of care and factors associated with treatment uptake of localised prostate cancer, using the bi‐national Prostate Cancer Outcomes Registry–Australia and New Zealand (PCOR‐ANZ) database.MethodsData for 36 504 patients, diagnosed between 2015 and 2018, from New Zealand and seven Australian jurisdictions were evaluated. Multinomial logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with the likelihood of receiving no active treatment, radiotherapy (RT), androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) and combined ADT + RT, compared to radical prostatectomy (RP).ResultsThe most common disease risk group and treatment were intermediate‐risk disease (49.2%) and RP (40.3%). Men with low‐risk disease were mostly managed by no active treatment (81.5%). Compared to men treated with RP, the likelihood of receiving other treatments was higher in residents from regional areas, those from lower socio‐economic status (SES) areas and those diagnosed at regional or public hospitals. Being in the lowest SES group was associated with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.84, (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.67–2.02) of having no active treatment, an OR of 1.53 (95% CI 1.32–1.78) for RT, an OR of 2.66 (95% CI 2.10–3.36) for ADT and an OR of 2.56 (95% CI 2.23–2.93) for ADT + RT.ConclusionsFactors related to SES and hospital provider were associated with treatment choices in localised prostate cancer patients, independent of their age or disease risk classification.Keywords: prostate cancer, treatments, patterns of care, registry, population health

Introduction

Australia and New Zealand have one of the highest age‐standardised prostate cancer incidence rates in the world [1]. The management of prostate cancer is complex due to heterogeneity of the disease, management choices and treatment‐related side effects. This is especially the case for localised prostate cancer, where the long‐term disease‐specific survival outcomes were similar among men who received radiotherapy (RT), radical prostatectomy (RP) and no active treatment, but they experienced different side effects, even up to 15 years post‐diagnosis [2].

An understanding of the population‐wide patterns of care for prostate cancer allows local clinical practice to be aligned with international guideline recommendations, such as the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and the European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines. It also allows us to identify differences that may be associated with inequity in patient outcomes. This concern is driven by the knowledge that, in several countries, there are disparities in care leading up to prostate cancer diagnosis and in survival outcomes [3]. In Australia, men residing in more affluent areas, men with higher socio‐economic status (SES), men with private health insurance and men living in metropolitan areas are more likely to have participated in PSA testing and experience better survival rates, compared to men with lower SES and those living in rural and remote areas [4, 5]. The extent to which these patterns are related and the role of management in the association are not well understood.

Previous Australian studies looking at the patterns of care for prostate cancer were based on cohorts from individual states, primarily Victoria, South Australia, and New South Wales [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. In this study, we describe the treatment patterns of localised prostate cancer using data from the Prostate Cancer Outcomes Registry–Australia and New Zealand (PCOR‐ANZ). PCOR‐ANZ is a bi‐national population‐based registry that collects the patient, treatment and follow‐up details of men diagnosed with prostate cancer in Australia and New Zealand [12]. The estimated population coverage of PCOR‐ANZ was approximately 72% by 2018 [13]. The study also aimed to determine the clinical and socio‐economic factors associated with variations in care for localised prostate cancer.

Methods

Study Cohort and Registry Data

PCOR‐ANZ collects data from each of the participating jurisdictional cancer registries (Table S1), with annual data quality audits performed. For this study, PCOR‐ANZ data for 40 078 men diagnosed between 2015 and 2018 were obtained. Men with metastatic disease, defined as N1 and/or M1 positive status, were excluded (n = 3493). A further 79 men with treatment dates prior to their date of diagnosis and two men who had opted out of the registry were excluded. A total of 36 504 men were included in the study analysis, with different contributions of cases from each jurisdiction (Table S1).

For cases diagnosed in Australia, postcode of residence at time of diagnosis was used to determine remoteness area (ARIA) and socioeconomic status (SES), based on 2016 data from the Australian Bureau of Statistics. As postcode information was not available for New Zealand, ARIA and SES classifications were coded as missing for New Zealand in the subgroup analysis.

Ethical approval for this study was received from the NSW Population and Health Services Research Ethics Committee (2020/ETH01096).

Disease Risk and Treatment Classifications

The NCCN risk classification system was used in this study as it provides a more detailed disease risk stratification than the EAU system. It also allows comparison with previous studies analysing the PCOR‐ANZ datasets using the same disease risk stratification system [9, 10, 11]. Disease risk was based on a modified version of the NCCN risk classification guidelines (version 1, 2023; Table S2). Classification into the very‐low‐risk group was not possible as PSA density data were not available. For the intermediate‐risk group, men with more aggressive disease – International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) Grade Group (GG) 3 – were classified as a subgroup separate from the remaining men in the intermediate‐risk group (ISUP GG ≤2). Men with missing clinical T stage were classified as having low‐risk disease if they had PSA <10 ng/mL and ISUP GG 1 disease. Men who were not allocated into any of the NCCN risk groups due to insufficient clinical details were grouped as ‘unclassified’.

Primary treatment was defined as the first treatment received by the patient following their diagnosis. Men who did not receive any treatment within the first 6 months of diagnosis were classified as having no active treatment, which included men undergoing active surveillance or watchful waiting. Overall, men were classified into one of the following treatment groups: no active treatment, RP, RT, androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) or combined ADT and RT (ADT + RT). For ADT + RT treatment, the receipt of ADT and RT had to occur within 6 months of each other. A subset of 134 men had treatments other than the above‐mentioned. These men were included in the analysis but not reported due to small numbers in each group and possible risk of reidentification.

Statistical Analysis

Cohort characteristics are presented using descriptive statistics. Differences in treatment distributions stratified by NCCN risk group were assessed using the chi‐squared test. Multinomial logistic regression was conducted to examine the associations among study variables (with the largest subgroup as reference) and the likelihood of undertaking a specific treatment, using RP as the reference outcome category. This was performed as an unadjusted model for age, year of diagnosis and clinical variables. Patients' sociodemographic and hospital characteristics were assessed using a model adjusted for age, NCCN risk group and jurisdiction. Missing data for each study variable were included in the logistic regression as a subgroup. P values < 0.05 were taken to indicate statistical significance. All analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

Characteristics of the study cohort are provided in Table 1. There was an incremental increase in the number of cases from 2015 to 2018 due to increased recruitment into the registry rather than an actual rise in disease incidence. The median age at diagnosis was 68 years. A higher proportion of men were residents in major cities (60.1%) and were diagnosed in metropolitan (76.4%) and private (60.1%) hospitals. There was some variation in the distribution of Australian men across different SES subgroups, with 24.1% in the most advantaged group and 13.4% in the least advantaged group. Almost half of the men (49.6%) were diagnosed via TRUS‐guided prostate biopsy. However, the use of transperineal prostate biopsy has steadily increased between 2015 and 2018 (from 25.4% to 45.3%), overtaking TRUS prostate biopsy as the main method of diagnosis in 2018 (Fig. S1).

The median diagnostic PSA level for the study cohort was 7 ng/mL, with 57.5% of men having a PSA level between 3 and <10 ng/mL. The most common ISUP GG at diagnosis was GG 2 (32.6%), followed by GG 1 (28.2%). There was a high level of missing information for clinical staging (47.0%). Nearly half of the study cohort were classified as having intermediate‐risk disease (49.2%), followed by 21.3% with high‐risk, 19.8% with low‐risk and 3.5% with very‐high‐risk disease. However, the disease profile for men aged ≥80 years was different, with high‐risk being the most common risk group (43.0%; Fig. S2).

Treatment Distribution

Overall, RP (40.3%) was the most common treatment, followed by no active treatment (39.7%), ADT + RT (9.5%), RT (6.9%) and ADT (3.1%; Table 2). When stratified by disease risk, RP remained the most common treatment for men in the high‐risk (44.3%) and intermediate‐risk groups (51.1% for those with ISUP GG ≤2, and 57.4% for ISUP GG 3). In the intermediate‐risk sub‐groups, those with ISUP GG 3 were less likely to undergo no active treatment (−15.7%) and more likely to undergo ADT + RT (+7.1%). Men with low‐risk and unclassified risk disease were mostly managed by no active treatment (81.5% and 84.8%, respectively). For men with very‐high‐risk disease, the uptakes of RP (30.0%) and ADT + RT (30.1%) were comparable. Of note, 28.7% of men in the cohort did not receive any active treatment for the entirety of their follow‐up period (data not shown).

Association Between Treatment and Clinical Factors

Age, year of diagnosis and NCCN risk group were significantly associated with the uptake of specific treatments (unadjusted model, P < 0.0001; Fig. 1). Compared to men aged between 60 and 69 years, younger men (≤59 years) were more likely to receive RP vs other active treatments, while men aged ≥70 years had increased odds of receiving other treatments. Men diagnosed in more recent years (2017 and 2018) had increased odds of receiving treatments other than RP.

Compared to men with intermediate‐risk subgroup with ISUP GG ≤2, those with intermediate‐risk subgroup with ISUP GG 3, high‐risk and high‐ and very‐high‐risk disease had higher odds of receiving ADT‐related treatments. Men with low‐risk disease had increased odds of having no active treatment (odds ratio [OR] 7.95, 95% CI 7.38–8.56). As examples, for men with very‐high‐risk disease, the ORs for receiving ADT and ADT + RT were 38.99 (95% CI 30.15–50.43) and 11.35 (95% CI 9.61–13.39), respectively. Conversely, the high‐risk and very‐high‐risk disease groups were associated with lower odds of receiving RT. The independent associations with PSA, ISUP GG and clinical stage are presented in Fig. S3.

Association Between Treatments and Sociodemographic and Hospital Characteristics

All factors were significantly associated with the uptake of specific treatments (adjusted model; P < 0.0001; Fig. 2). Compared with men living in major cities, those living in the inner and outer regional areas had higher odds of receiving treatments other than RP. Living in outer regional areas was associated with an OR of 1.74 (95% CI 1.53–1.99) for receiving ADT + RT. Similar associations were observed for hospital location, with patients diagnosed in regional hospitals having higher odds of receiving other treatments compared to RP.

Association between patient sociodemographics and hospital characteristics and the likelihood of receiving no active treatment, radiotherapy (RT), androgen deprivation therapy alone (ADT) or combination treatment with radiotherapy (ADT + RT). All treatment outcome comparisons were made using radical prostatectomy as the reference outcome category and represented as adjusted odds ratio (OR; to age, National Comprehensive Cancer Network risk group and jurisdiction) and 95% CI.

The associations with SES were consistent across all treatment comparisons. With decreasing SES, there was an incremental increase in OR of receiving treatments other than RP. Men in the lowest SES group had an OR of 1.84 (95% CI 1.67–2.02) for no active treatment, an OR of 1.53 (95% CI 1.32–1.78) for RT, an OR of 2.66 (95% CI 2.10–3.36) for ADT, and an OR of 2.56 (95% CI 2.23–2.93) for ADT + RT. Being diagnosed in a public hospital, which may also indicate SES or insurance status, was associated with an increased odds of receiving treatments other than RP.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest population‐level study describing the patterns of care of localised prostate cancer in Australia and New Zealand. Our findings suggest alignment between Australian population‐based patterns of care and NCCN risk‐stratified treatment recommendations for localised prostate cancer. However, there were some exceptions. Treatment uptake was influenced by SES, area of residence and hospital provider, suggesting drivers other than disease risk for the patterns of care at the population level. Regardless of age and disease risk, men living in major cities, men diagnosed in a metropolitan or private hospital, and men with a higher SES were more likely to receive RP compared to the other mainstream treatment options for localised prostate cancer.

The study highlights the increased adoption of active surveillance in contemporary practice. First recommended by the NCCN as a preferred management for low‐risk disease in 2010, the initial uptake of active surveillance was slow and varied globally [14]. In Australia, the uptake of active surveillance in men with low‐risk disease ranged from 23% to 44% in smaller study cohorts between 2008 and 2015 [9, 10, 11, 15]. In our study, the uptake was found to have increased since then. Similar trends were reported in Sweden, Canada and the United States, although at a slower rate of increase and greater regional variation in the United States. Based on Sweden National Prostate Cancer Register data (2009–2014), 91% and 74% of men with very‐low and low‐risk disease underwent active surveillance [16]. In 2013, the uptake of active surveillance for low‐risk prostate cancer across seven Canadian provinces was reported to be 69.9% [17]. However, the treatment patterns varied significantly by region, ranging from 53.3% to 80.8%. In 2021, the uptake of active surveillance in a cohort of 20 000 men in the United States was 60% [18, 19].

The dominant disease risk group and treatment observed in our study cohort are consistent with previous studies [6, 9, 10, 11, 20, 21, 22]. However, we report a higher proportion of men with no active treatment and a lower use of RT compared to previous Australian studies. For example, a Victorian study of 14 025 men reported that 23.9% received no active treatment, while 21.3% received RT [10]. Differences between the studies are likely attributable to variations in patient characteristics, patient demographics, and changes in treatment patterns over time and the methods used to classify treatments. Previous studies have defined ‘no active treatment’ based on treatment records within 12 months of diagnosis instead, thereby excluding men who have discontinued surveillance for active treatments within the first year. Inconsistencies also exist in the treatment classifications for RT and ADT. For example, a study based on cases diagnosed in South Australia and Victoria did not assess neoadjuvant or adjuvant ADT and RT as an independent treatment group [11]. These men were accounted for as having either RT or ADT treatments, which contributed to their higher uptakes of RT (22.2%) and ADT (9.5%), whereas a study from the United States (598 640 men) counted these men as undergoing ADT [20]. As a result of these various approaches to treatment classifications, caution is required when comparing studies on patterns of care.

Studies have consistently reported the influence of SES and affluence on the patterns of care for prostate cancer. Men with higher SES are more likely to prefer RP over RT and are more likely to receive active treatment [6, 8, 11, 20]. The association between SES and RP is potentially influenced by the fact that RP is more commonly performed in private hospitals in Australia, with associated out‐of‐pocket costs [7, 23]. In contrast, the evidence on region of residence has been mixed. Overall, men living further away from major cities have lower use of RT due to accessibility considerations (e.g., distance to radiotherapy centres and multiple trips required). This was reported in an Australian study (4003 men), where closer proximity to the nearest RT centre, but not area of residence, was found to be associated with an increased likelihood of undergoing RT vs RP [6]. A study from the United States indicated that older men, men of minority race and those with lower income and no private insurance were more likely to receive RT than RP, and were also more likely to receive no active treatment compared to active treatment (within the low‐risk group) [20]. Area of residence was not specifically assessed, but this and other studies from the United States have reported regional treatment differences that were attributed to differences in local practice and accessibility of treatment facilities [22, 24, 25]. In Canada, significant variations in treatment patterns for men with low‐risk disease diagnosed between 2010 and 2013 were also observed across seven different provinces [17]. For instance, the uptake of RP ranged from 35.9% to 12.0%. However, the study did not assess the factors contributing to these variations. The variations in local practice and access to treatment facilities have not been formally assessed in Australia and New Zealand. However, our study findings suggest that such variations may exist and warrant further investigation regarding the reasons for these patterns.

Although the differences in treatment uptake based on patients’ sociodemographic factors suggest disparities in care, current evidence does not suggest that this will contribute to differences in survival outcomes. The 15‐year ProtecT trial demonstrated no significant difference in prostate cancer mortality and metastases among patients with low‐risk disease who received no active treatment, RT or RP [2]. While these are important findings, it should be noted that the ProtecT trial was based on treatments between 1999 and 2001 and may not reflect contemporary practice and care. The different treatments are also associated with different long‐term quality‐of‐life profiles [2, 26, 27]. Thus, the variations in treatment choice based on socio‐economic factors may lead to differences in quality of life and subsequent supportive care required in the population.

Our study has several limitations and challenges. PCOR‐ANZ data do not distinguish active surveillance from watchful waiting, which has different management approaches and long‐term outcomes [13, 28, 29]. Improvements in the accuracy of data collection for active surveillance and watchful waiting are difficult to achieve but are critically important when comparing patterns of care. Other limitations include the challenge of comparing data between Australia and New Zealand, particularly the inability to assess and compare indices based on socio‐economic factors and regional and rural areas of residence, as well as the absence of comorbidity data, which may also influence treatment choices. The timely collection of data by cancer registries across different states and territories is another challenge. However, although our study outcomes may not necessarily represent the most current treatment patterns in 2024, they remain important for future studies as a benchmark to assess any changes in clinical practice. Additionally, while we have reported significant associations between hospital characteristics and treatment uptake, these factors were based on the hospitals in which patients were diagnosed and not where treatment was performed. Nevertheless, information on the diagnostic hospitals is an important estimate of the proportion of men likely to travel from regional areas to major cities. In this cohort, we estimated that 45% to 58% of men may have to travel from regional and remote areas to receive hospital services for their diagnosis (Fig. S4).

Overall, this study describes the treatment patterns for localised prostate cancer in the largest cohort to date from Australia and New Zealand. The care provided for patients with localised prostate cancer largely aligns with the treatment recommendations in international guidelines, including the high uptake of active surveillance in men with low‐risk disease. However, variations in care were observed among sociodemographic groups. The extent to which this may impact longer‐term survivorship, quality of life, and mortality outcomes is of interest and should be monitored.
