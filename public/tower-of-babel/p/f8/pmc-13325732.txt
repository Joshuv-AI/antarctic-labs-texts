Preventive pharmacotherapy initiation after lipoprotein(a) measurement in low-risk adults
Authors: Om A. Kothari, Kevin Maymi-Quintana, Rohan S. Ganti, Nicholas A. Bosch, Sheilah Bernard
Published: 2026

Introduction

Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is a genetically determined lipoprotein particle and an established cardiovascular risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), including myocardial infarction (MI), ischemic stroke, and peripheral artery disease (PAD) [[1], [2], [3]]. Elevated Lp(a) confers cardiovascular risk independent of traditional lipid parameters such as low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Defined as ≥50 mg/dL (≥125 nmol/L), elevated Lp(a) affects approximately 20–25 % of the global population and represents a prevalent source of cardiovascular risk [2,5,6].

Unlike LDL-C, Lp(a) concentrations are minimally responsive to lipid-lowering therapies [3,6]. Although it is a cardiovascular risk factor and current guidance recommends single lifetime Lp(a) measurement, no pharmacologic intervention is approved [1]. Risk factor optimization is recommended.

Despite therapeutic limitations, interest in Lp(a) has increased, driven by its prognostic value and the emergence of targeted therapies [2,3,7]. With increasing guideline-directed Lp(a) testing, clinician response to elevated concentrations, especially in the absence of guideline-based treatment indications, remains unclear [1].

Accordingly, we examined the initiation of lipid-lowering therapy, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors, and aspirin following Lp(a) measurement among adults without established ASCVD, diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), LDL-C ≥ 190 mg/dL, prior lipid-lowering therapy prescription, or other high-risk conditions. By isolating patients without traditional treatment indications, we sought to characterize clinician response to elevated Lp(a) in a primary prevention population.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a multicenter retrospective observational cohort study evaluating initiation of preventive pharmacotherapy following Lp(a) measurement. We used the TriNetX U.S. Collaborative Network (TriNetX, Inc., Cambridge, MA), a federated electronic health record (EHR) platform including de-identified data from 70 participating U.S. health care organizations [8,9]. Analyses were performed within the TriNetX environment without direct patient contact; institutional review board approval was not required.

Study population

Adults aged 18–75 years with an ambulatory Lp(a) measurement between January 1, 2021, and January 1, 2026 were eligible. The index date was the date of Lp(a) measurement.

To isolate preventive decision-making, we excluded patients with any of the following within three years of the index event: ASCVD, including coronary artery disease (ICD-10 code: I25.x), MI (ICD-10 code: I21.x), ischemic stroke (ICD-10 code: I63.x), or PAD (ICD-10 code: I73.x); diabetes mellitus (ICD-10 codes: E08–E13); CKD (ICD-10 code: N18.x); familial hypercholesterolemia (ICD-10 code: E78.01); family history of ischemic heart disease (ICD-10 code: Z82.4); or active nicotine use (ICD-10 code: F17.x), LDL-C ≥ 190 mg/dL, triglycerides ≥500 mg/dL, or prior use of lipid-lowering therapy (statins, ezetimibe, or bile acid sequestrants), PCSK9 inhibitors (alirocumab, evolocumab, or inclisiran), aspirin, niacin, or bempedoic acid within three years of the index event.

Exposure

The exposure of interest was elevated Lp(a) at the index measurement. Lp(a) was elevated (≥125 nmol/L or ≥50 mg/dL) or non-elevated (≤124 nmol/L or ≤49 mg/dL) [1]. When available, nmol/L values were prioritized.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was initiation of lipid-lowering therapy within 90 days of Lp(a) measurement. Secondary outcomes were PCSK9 inhibitor and aspirin initiation. Niacin, bempedoic acid, and bile acid sequestrants were not analyzed due to low counts. A prespecified sensitivity analysis extended follow-up to 180 days.

Covariates and propensity score matching

Covariates including demographics, diagnoses, and laboratory measurements were ascertained on or three years before the index date to balance adequate capture of recent medication exposure while minimizing misclassification from remote therapy (Supplemental Table 1). Body mass index and LDL-C were categorized using clinical thresholds. A propensity score for elevated versus non-elevated Lp(a) was estimated using a regularized logistic regression including all covariates. Patients were matched 1:1 using greedy nearest-neighbor matching without replacement. Balance was assessed using absolute standardized mean differences (SMDs), with SMD <0.1 indicating adequate balance. Balance was visualized using propensity score density plots.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using means with standard deviations for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables. Medication initiation rates were compared using relative risks (RRs) and absolute risk differences with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs). Time-to-initiation was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier method with log-rank testing and hazard ratios (HR). Analyses were two-sided with α = 0.05; secondary outcomes were considered hypothesis-generating.

Primary outcome

Within 90 days of Lp(a) measurement, lipid-lowering therapy initiation was uncommon but more frequent among patients with elevated Lp(a) compared to those with non-elevated Lp(a) (3210 [21.4 %] vs 1401 [9.4 %]; RR, 2.29 [95 % CI, 2.16–2.43]); absolute risk difference 12.1 % (95 % CI, 11.3 %−12.9 %) (Fig. C). Most initiations were statins (3118 [20.8 %]).

Time-to-event analyses showed earlier and sustained initiation among patients with elevated Lp(a) (Fig. D).

Secondary outcomes

PCSK9 inhibitor initiation was rare but more frequent among patients with elevated Lp(a) (79 [0.53 %] vs 18 [0.12 %]; RR, 4.39 [95 % CI, 2.63–7.32]). Similarly, aspirin initiation was uncommon but more frequent among patients with elevated Lp(a) (312 [2.1 %] vs 174 [1.2 %]; RR, 1.79 [95 % CI, 1.49–2.16]).

Time-to-event analyses demonstrated earlier and sustained initiation of PCSK9 inhibitors and aspirin among patients with elevated Lp(a) (Supplemental Figures 2 and 3).

Sensitivity analysis

In a prespecified sensitivity analysis extending follow-up to 180 days, lipid-lowering therapy was initiated more often among patients with elevated Lp(a) (24.1 % vs 10.8 %; RR, 2.22 [95 % CI, 2.10–2.34]). PCSK9 inhibitor (0.75 % vs 0.17 %; RR, 4.31) and aspirin (2.8 % vs 1.6 %; RR, 1.70) initiation was rare but directionally similar. Time-to-initiation analyses demonstrated similar directional associations for all therapies (all log-rank p < 0.0001).

Discussion

In this contemporary U.S. cohort of lower-risk adults, elevated Lp(a) was associated with earlier and more frequent initiation of preventive pharmacotherapy. However, absolute prescription rates were modest; nearly 80 % of patients with elevated Lp(a) did not initiate lipid-lowering therapy. PCSK9 inhibitor and aspirin initiation was even less common, suggesting selective rather than systematic responses to elevated Lp(a). Together, these findings suggest that elevated Lp(a) appears to occasionally influence prescribing behavior - consistent with recent reports [10].

These findings should not be interpreted that such prescribing is guideline-directed. Current recommendations recognize Lp(a) as a risk-enhancing factor rather than as a treatment target, and no therapy is approved solely for Lp(a) elevation. This analysis describes contemporary practice rather than appropriate management.

A notable limitation is the inability to calculate the pooled cohort equation or PREVENT risk scores. Some patients without ASCVD may nonetheless meet lipid-lowering eligibility thresholds based on estimated 10-year risk. Hence, some observed therapy initiation may reflect appropriate risk-based primary prevention, independent of Lp(a). Residual confounding cannot be excluded.

LDL-C concentrations were comparable after matching, and individuals with LDL-C ≥ 190 mg/dL were excluded. Although stratified matching for LDL-C concentrations reduces the likelihood that prescribing differences were driven by LDL-C, residual LDL-related risk influencing clinical decision-making cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

Among adults without traditional indications for pharmacotherapy, elevated Lp(a) was associated with more frequent, albeit uncommon, initiation of preventive treatment. By restricting the cohort to a lower-risk primary prevention population without guideline-based treatment indications, this study isolates clinician response to elevated Lp(a). These findings underscore the heterogeneity of contemporary management and highlight the need for evidence-based guidance.
