Association of Physical Activity with Abdominal Aortic Calcification in Chronic Kidney Disease: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Yang Kong, Aihua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLimited studies have investigated the impact of physical activity (PA) on vascular calcification (VC) in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD). Therefore, this cross-sectional study aimed to investigate the link between PA and abdominal aortic calcification (AAC) in CKD patients.MethodsThis study involved 571 participants with CKD from the 2013–2014 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). PA was measured by total weekly time (hours) and metabolic equivalent task (MET) levels. Weighted Poisson regression analyses (both univariate and multivariable) were conducted to investigate the associations between PA and AAC. Subgroup analyses were performed to detect interaction effects, and restricted cubic splines were applied to assess potential nonlinear relationships.ResultsLonger weekly PA time and higher MET levels were associated with reduced AAC prevalence and severity. Each 1-h increase in PA was associated with a 1% lower prevalence of AAC (PR = 0.99, 95% CI 0.98–0.99, p = 0.002), while the high MET group showed a 29% lower prevalence compared to the inactive group (PR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.55–0.92, p = 0.01). These associations were independent of traditional and CKD-specific risk factors. Subgroup analyses revealed no significant interaction effects, and restricted cubic spline models did not indicate a nonlinear association.ConclusionOur study demonstrated that PA was independently and inversely associated with AAC burden in patients with CKD. These findings highlight the potential value of PA as a modifiable lifestyle factor in the comprehensive management of CKD.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is identified as a major global public health issue, with an estimated global prevalence of 13.4% [1]. Vascular calcification (VC) is a common complication in all stages of CKD patients, with a prevalence ranging from about 45% to 90% [2]. Age, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension are independent risk factors for VC in patients with CKD. Evidence supports that VC is associated with cardiovascular disease (CVD) events and all-cause mortality, and that it serves as an independent predictor of poor prognosis in CKD patients [3]. The abdominal aorta is one of the large arteries that is particularly prone to early calcification in this population. Previous studies have indicated that abdominal aortic calcification (AAC) is an independent predictor of adverse outcomes and is strongly linked to an increase in mortality in CKD [4, 5]. Besides, the abdominal aortic calcification index (ACI) can be used to predict the occurrence of coronary artery calcification in patients with CKD [6]. Compared with other arteries, such as the coronary and carotid arteries, AAC has a higher prevalence and calcification score, and is also more readily detectable [7]. Due to its high incidence and ease of detection, it is always used to assess VC in CKD patients. Common assessment methods include dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), ultrasound, and computed tomography. The Kauppila score of DXA has been widely used as a common method to assess AAC severity [8, 9].

Physical activity (PA) is considered the cornerstone of lifestyle interventions in managing CKD [10]. Evidence suggests that PA enhances cardiopulmonary fitness, improves body function, optimizes clinical outcomes, and reduces all-cause mortality in CKD patients [11–14]. In addition to these proven benefits, researchers have begun to investigate the potential role of PA in enhancing vascular function. Some randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have demonstrated that PA can significantly reduce arterial stiffness in patients with CKD [15–20]. However, most of these studies have focused on patients with CKD stages 3–5 or those undergoing hemodialysis. They primarily used pulse wave velocity (PWV) or augmentation index to assess arterial stiffness, while relatively few have directly evaluated VC. To date, evidence from large-scale studies examining the association between PA and VC in patients with CKD remains limited. Therefore, this cross-sectional study involved 571 CKD participants from the 2013–2014 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to investigate the relationship between PA and AAC in CKD and to further explore potential moderating effects.

Methods

This study was prepared and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines, and the completed STROBE checklist is provided as online supplementary material (for all online suppl. Material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552553).

Study Population

The NHANES is a cross-sectional, nationally representative study that employs a multistage, stratified probability sampling design to collect data from individuals in the USA. Among the cycles in which PA data were collected, the AAC score data were only available in the 2013–2014 cycle. Therefore, we used NHANES data from this cycle for our analysis.

In the initially enrolled 10,175 participants, individuals were excluded for the following reasons: (1) missing complete DXA examination data (n = 7,035), (2) missing PA data (n = 4), (3) missing covariate data (n = 546), (4) ultimately, participants who did not meet the diagnostic criteria for CKD were excluded (n = 2,019). A total of 571 participants were included in the final analysis (shown in Fig. 1).

Study Variables

AAC Assessment

Lateral DXA scans of the thoracolumbar spine were conducted at the NHANES mobile examination center, where AAC can be accurately assessed. Severity was evaluated using the Kauppila classification (score range: 0–24), a semiquantitative method in which the abdominal aorta is divided into four segments (L1–L4), each scored 1–3 according to the extent of calcification. Segment scores were summed to obtain the total score [9]. In our analysis, AAC was defined as a total Kauppila score ≥1, and absence of AAC was defined as a score of 0. AAC severity was stratified into four groups according to the total Kauppila score: none (0), mild (1–4), moderate (5–15), and severe (16–24) [21].

Physical Activity

PA was assessed using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ), in which participants reported the frequency (number of sessions per week), duration (time spent per session), and intensity (moderate or vigorous) of various physical activities, including leisure-time physical activity (LTPA), occupation-related physical activity (OPA), and transportation-related physical activity (TPA). Total metabolic equivalent task (MET)-minutes per week were calculated by summing the MET-minutes from three domains (OPA, TPA, and LTPA). In accordance with the WHO/GPAQ guidelines, weights of 4.0 and 8.0 METs were assigned to moderate- and vigorous-intensity activities, respectively. MET levels were subsequently categorized into 4 groups based on WHO recommendations: inactive (0 MET-min/week), low (<600 MET-min/week), moderate (600–2,999 MET-min/week), and high (≥3,000 MET-min/week) [22]. PA time was derived by converting the minutes of vigorous-intensity exercise from OPA and LTPA into hours, multiplying the resulting hours by two, then adding the hours of moderate-intensity exercise from LTPA, OPA, and TPA [23].

CKD Diagnostic Criteria

The diagnosis of CKD was based on an estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio ≥30 mg/g. For this purpose, we collected data on participants’ urine albumin and creatinine concentrations, as well as their urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio values. Then estimated glomerular filtration rate was calculated using the CKD-EPI (CKD Epidemiology Collaboration) equation [24].

Covariates

For demographic characteristics, we included gender, age, race/ethnicity, marital status, education level, and poverty-income ratio (PIR). Age was divided into two groups: <60 years and ≥60 years, representing middle-aged and elderly individuals, respectively. Race/ethnicity was classified as non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Mexican American, other Hispanic, and other races. We categorized the education level into three groups: less than high school, high school graduate, and above high school. The PIR was classified as below poverty (PIR <1) and above poverty (PIR ≥1). Laboratory evaluations encompassed mineral homeostasis markers (serum phosphorus, calcium, and vitamin D), lipid metabolism indices (TC and HDL-C), and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) as an indicator of inflammatory status.

Information on common comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, and CVD) and lipid-lowering medication use was collected. The diagnostic criteria for diabetes are as follows: (1) fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, (2) random blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, (3) two-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, (4) HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, (5) ever used antihyperglycemic medication or insulin, or (6) being diagnosed as diabetes by a doctor. Hypertension diagnosis criteria encompassed average systolic/diastolic blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mm Hg, antihypertensive medication usage, or ever being diagnosed with hypertension [25]. CVD includes a history of coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, heart attack, stroke, or angina.

Lifestyle factors included body mass index (BMI), smoking status, and alcohol consumption. Smoking status was categorized into three groups according to the number of cigarettes consumed: former smoker, never smoked, and currently active smoker. Alcohol intake was divided into five categories: former, heavy, mild, moderate, and never. BMI was categorized according to American adult BMI classifications as follows: non-overweight (BMI <25.0 kg/m2), overweight (25.0 ≤ BMI <30.0 kg/m2), and obese (BMI ≥30.0 kg/m2) [26].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses accounted for the complex NHANES survey design, incorporating sampling weights (WTMEC2YR), primary sampling units (SDMVPSU), and stratification (SDMVSTRA). For analyses restricted to the participants with CKD, a subpopulation analysis approach was applied to ensure valid standard errors. Potential selection bias was evaluated by comparing baseline variables between included and excluded participants via t test, and chi-squared tests. Participants were first stratified into two groups according to AAC status and compared at baseline. Categorical variables were characterized using counts and weighted percentages, while continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard errors. To assess whether baseline characteristics varied between the two groups, we applied the weighted chi-square test to analyze categorical variables and a weighted t test for continuous variables. A univariate Poisson regression model was first constructed to evaluate the crude relationship between PA time (hours) and MET levels with AAC, followed by multivariable Poisson regression models with progressive adjustments for potential confounders. To verify robustness, model 4 was performed by further adjusting for CKD-related mineral metabolism markers (serum phosphorus, calcium, and vitamin D), systemic inflammatory indices (NLR), lipid profiles, and lipid-lowering drug use. Participants with missing data for these additional covariates were excluded from the analysis. Restricted cubic spline analysis based on the multiple Poisson regression model was performed to explore the potential nonlinear relationship between weekly PA time and the prevalence of AAC. Additionally, subgroup analysis was used to explore interaction effects, stratified by age, gender, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, CVD, and CKD stage. Then we conducted two additional sensitivity analyses: (1) a multivariate Poisson regression in a subgroup excluding participants with known CVD to minimize potential reverse causality; (2) a weighted ordinal logistic regression of PA or MET level and AAC severity. The analysis was performed in R software 4.4.2. Statistical significance was established at a two-tailed p < 0.05.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Our analysis included 571 participants, of whom 45.1% were male, 54.9% were female (weighted proportion). Among all participants, 43.21% had AAC, 35.61% had diabetes, 75.15% had hypertension, 24.5% had CVD, and CKD stages were distributed as follows: 19.29% Stage 1, 23.51% Stage 2, 53.95% Stage 3, and 3.25% Stages 4–5. Participants with AAC had a greater proportion of CKD stage 3, higher prevalences of hypertension and CVD, lower HDL-C levels, and greater use of lipid-lowering medications. Regarding PA, participants with AAC had a statistically significant lower proportion of OPA engagement and reported shorter total weekly PA time (5.38 h/week vs. 9.61 h/week), compared with an overall population average of 7.78 h/week (Table 1). Comparison between participants included in the analysis and those excluded due to missing AAC data showed that the excluded participants had higher BMI (p < 0.001) and a greater prevalence of CVD (p = 0.005) (online suppl. Table 1).

Correlation between PA and AAC

The univariate Poisson regression analysis showed that age ≥60 years, hypertension, CVD, current smoking, and marital status (widowed/divorced/separated) were significantly associated with a higher prevalence of AAC, while greater PA time and higher MET level were significantly associated with a lower prevalence of AAC. Other factors, including alcohol consumption, BMI, diabetes, and CKD stage, did not show significant associations (Table 2). In multivariate Poisson regression analysis, we found that longer PA time was significantly correlated with lower AAC rates in both the crude and adjusted models (Table 3). After full adjustment, each additional hour of PA was associated with a 1% decrease in the rates of AAC (PR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98–0.99, p = 0.002). Consistent findings were observed when PA was categorized by MET levels; compared with inactive individuals (0 MET-min/week), participants with high MET levels (≥3,000 MET-min/week) had a 29% lower prevalence of AAC (PR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.55–0.92, p = 0.01) (Table 3). The observed associations remained statistically significant after additional adjustment for mineral metabolism markers, NLR, lipid profiles, and lipid-lowering therapies in model 4 (n = 538).

The inverse association between PA time or MET level and AAC remained generally consistent across subgroups (p for interaction > 0.05), with no significant interactions were observed (Tables 4, 5).

Additional Analyses

Sensitivity analyses further confirmed the robustness of our findings. First, after excluding individuals with a history of CVD, the association between PA levels and the prevalence of AAC remained statistically significant (p < 0.05) (online suppl. Table 2). Ordinal logistic regression analysis revealed that weekly PA time was inversely associated with AAC severity (p = 0.001). Compared with the inactive group, participants with high MET levels (≥3,000 MET-min/week) had 44% lower odds of being in a more severe AAC category (OR = 0.56, 95% CI 0.39–0.82, p = 0.003). A similar trend was observed for the moderate MET group, although the association did not reach statistical significance (online suppl. Table 3).

Discussion

Main Findings

In this study of CKD participants from NHANES 2013–2014, we observed that PA was inversely associated with both the presence and severity of AAC, suggesting a reduced overall burden of VC among physically active CKD patients. Each additional hour of weekly PA was associated with a 1% reduction in AAC prevalence, and participants with higher PA levels had lower odds of severe AAC. Although the effect size remains relatively modest as weekly PA hours increase, both the prevalence and severity of AAC decrease significantly once cumulative activity reaches high MET levels (≥3,000 MET-min/week), suggesting a threshold effect rather than a simple linear relationship between PA and AAC.

In CKD, VC is characterized by medial calcification, which often coexists with intimal calcification [27]. Medial arterial calcification involves the progressive deposition of calcium phosphate in the arterial medial layer, leading to increased arterial stiffness, left ventricular hypertrophy, and impaired perfusion of organs. In contrast, intimal calcification is associated with traditional risk factors and ischemic events, including myocardial infarction, ischemic heart disease, and ischemic stroke. This may lead to biological heterogeneity in the response to PA in different vascular beds. However, methods that can distinguish between these two types of VC remain limited. Common techniques include thin-section computed tomography imaging, optical coherence tomography, and histopathological examination, but their application is hindered by high cost or invasiveness [27].

Prior RCTs have demonstrated that PA notably mitigates the arterial stiffness of CKD patients. In the study by Greenwood et al. [20], 18 patients with stage 3–4 CKD underwent 12 months of resistance and aerobic training. Compared with the usual care group, exercise intervention significantly reduced PWV by −2.30 m/s (95% CI, −3.02 to −1.59) [20]. A recent study by Lai et al. [17] demonstrated that long-term aerobic exercise significantly improved carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity and walking speed in patients on chronic hemodialysis. A 2023 meta-analysis, which included 25 studies with a total of 875 CKD patients, found that PA alleviated arterial stiffness, with an average decline in central PWV by 0.61 m/s and a mean reduction in augmentation index by 3.4% [17]. In addition, research has reported that exercise improves bone mineral metabolism in dialysis patients. It is associated with higher serum calcium and lower serum phosphorus, alkaline phosphatase, and parathyroid hormone levels, suggesting that PA may delay medial calcification through this mechanism [28].

These findings support that PA improves vascular health in CKD. Building on this evidence, our study further suggests that higher levels of PA are associated with a lower burden of VC, as assessed by direct measurements of AAC. However, reverse causality may exist, as pre-existing CVD can impair functional status and lead to sedentary behavior. Previous studies have shown that patients with CVD often have sarcopenia and may experience declines in PA even prior to a cardiovascular event, which could contribute to lower observed PA levels [29, 30]. To address this potential bias, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to participants without CVD. The persistent significance of the PA-AAC association in this subgroup suggests that the observed relationship is independent of cardiovascular-related functional limitations. Notably, these results highlight the potential cardiovascular benefits of incorporating PA into the comprehensive management of CKD.

In multivariable Poisson regression models, we observed that advanced age was also significantly associated with a higher prevalence of AAC (online suppl. Table 4 and 5). Interestingly, while univariate analysis initially suggested a lower AAC prevalence among current smokers, this paradoxical association lost statistical significance after adjusting for potential confounders in the multivariable models (online suppl. Table 4 and 5). This shift indicates that the initial counter-intuitive result was likely driven by confounding factors rather than an independent protective effect of smoking.

Potential Mechanisms

Accumulating evidence suggests that PA exerts vasculo-protective effects in CKD by modulating several key pathological processes, including systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction. Specifically, by modulating immune cell function and reducing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, PA helps to reduce systemic inflammation [31–36]. At the same time, aerobic exercise has been proven to reduce oxidative stress by enhancing antioxidant defenses through upregulation of enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and catalase, increasing glutathione levels, and improving overall antioxidant capacity [33, 37]. In addition, PA improves endothelial function in non-dialysis CKD patients [38–40]. Prior studies have also reported that PA may improve abnormal calcium-phosphorus metabolism in CKD patients; however, the inverse association between PA and AAC remained statistically significant after additional adjustment for serum phosphorus, calcium, and vitamin D levels in Model 4, suggesting that this pathway does not fully account for the observed association. Collectively, these findings underscore that PA likely attenuates VC through multiple parallel mechanisms rather than a single pathway.

In recent years, PA has gradually been incorporated into the management of CKD. Guidelines from organizations such as KDIGO and NICE recommend that patients with CKD engage in regular exercise. Some guidelines, such as the European Clinical Consensus Statement, also provide specific recommendations regarding the type, duration, and intensity of exercise. However, the vast majority of CKD patients still engage in significantly insufficient levels of PA [41]. This is due, on the one hand, to limited facilities provided by healthcare institutions, and on the other hand, to a general lack of awareness and motivation among both patients and healthcare providers, resulting in the currently limited application of PA interventions in CKD management [42].

Strengths and Limitations

By focusing on CKD patients, we systematically explored the association between PA and AAC. Our findings provided evidence-based insights for VC management in this population, which holds significant clinical implications for improving patients’ quality of life and cardiovascular outcomes. Second, the study utilized the Kauppila scoring system, an objective semiquantitative method, to assess AAC severity. Moreover, leveraging the multistage stratified probability sampling design of NHANES ensured broad generalizability of the findings, while multivariate Poisson regression models adjusted for key confounders and multiple sensitivity analyses enhanced the statistical robustness of the conclusions.

Yet, some limitations still exist. Firstly, the cross-sectional design restricts causal inference, precluding longitudinal assessment of PAs long-term effects on AAC progression; besides, reliance on self-reported PA data via the GPAQ may introduce recall bias. Second, potential selection bias may exist, as participants with higher BMI and CVD prevalence were more likely to be excluded due to DXA imaging noise and functional limitations. Consequently, the generalizability of our findings may be primarily limited to CKD patients without extreme obesity or severe CVD. Another important limitation is that NHANES did not include systematic assessments of physical function. In CKD populations, PA is closely linked with functional status, and prior studies have shown that exercise interventions can significantly improve physical performance in patients with CKD [43]. Conversely, functional limitations can markedly reduce daily activity levels. Therefore, the association between PA and AAC may be partially confounded by unmeasured physical function. Finally, owing to the limitations of detection methods, we could not differentiate between medial and intimal calcification. Future studies should develop and apply more accessible assessment approaches and incorporate objective functional assessments.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that PA is independently and inversely associated with AAC burden in patients with CKD. These findings expand our understanding of the complex relationship between PA and vascular health in this high-risk population and underscore the potential role of PA-based lifestyle interventions in the comprehensive management of CKD.
