Clinical significance of lncRNA GAS5 in deep vein thrombosis and its mechanism of action on vascular endothelial damage
Authors: Hongyu Yang, Shuping Wang, Fei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe current research aims to clarify the clinical significance and molecular function of long-non-coding RNA growth arrest-specific 5 (GAS5) in deep vein thrombosis (DVT).Methods92 DVT patients and 86 controls were included in this study. Real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed to measure GAS5, microRNA (miR)-134-5p, and forkhead box protein 3 (FOXP3) levels. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves evaluated the diagnostic significance of GAS5 in DVT. Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression analyses assessed the prognostic significance of post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS). An in vitro model using CoCl2-induced HUVECs was established to measure cell proliferation, apoptosis, adhesion factors, and inflammatory factors via Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), flow cytometry, RT-qPCR, and Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Protein expression of FOXP3, HIF-1α, and VEGFA was evaluated via Western blot. Dual luciferase report, RIP, and RNA pull-down assays confirmed the direct binding of miR-134-5p to GAS5 or FOXP3.ResultsGAS5 and FOXP3 were downregulated in DVT patients’ serum and CoCl2-induced HUVECs, while miR-134-5p was upregulated. GAS5 distinguished DVT patients from controls with 89.13% sensitivity and 83.72% specificity. At 18-month follow-up, 32.60% of patients developed PTS, which was more common among those with low GAS5 expression. Mechanistically, GAS5 and FOXP3 bind to miR-134-5p. CoCl2 enhanced the protein levels of HIF-1α and VEGFA. Higher GAS5 levels reduced CoCl2-induced proliferation suppression, apoptosis promotion, and adhesion and inflammatory factors upregulation in HUVECs, but miR-134-5p reversed this effect.ConclusionGAS5 levels are decreased in DVT patients, serving as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker. Elevated GAS5 targets miR-134-5p/FOXP3 axis to alleviate endothelial dysfunction and inflammation, thereby reducing thrombosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06197-7.

Background

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) arises primarily from venous endothelial injury, slow blood flow, and hypercoagulability, causing blood to clot and disrupt venous return, particularly in the lower limbs [1]. Annually, 900,00 new DVT cases and 300,000 deaths are reported in the US [1], while China sees over 12 million new cases [2]. Despite treatments like anticoagulation, thrombolysis, and surgery, 20–50% of patients develop post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS), which may include gangrene, severe bleeding, and venous death [3]. A dislodged thrombus can also cause pulmonary embolism, leading to cardiovascular arrest and death [4]. Furthermore, DVT onset is concealed, progresses rapidly, and may be asymptomatic, causing high miss rates [5]. Venography is limited by invasiveness and allergy risks, while D-dimer’s specificity is low due to its presence in stroke and infections. Urgent need for novel, highly specific markers to accurately diagnose DVT and improve patients’ outcomes.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are crucial in modulating the initiation and development of vascular diseases. Several lncRNAs are implicated in the development and progression of DVT. For instance, SNHG12 exerts its influence by regulating miR-424-5p [6], NEAT1 via modulating miR-218-5p/GAB2 [7], and ASB16-AS1 also participates in this process [8]. Notably, growth arrest-specific 5 (GAS5), also known as NCRNA00030 and SNHG2, is a lncRNA located on human chromosome 1q25.1, comprising 13 exons spanning 2,554 bp. GAS5 expression is reduced in hypoxic pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells, promoting cell proliferation and vascular remodeling [9]. Damage to vascular endothelial cells (VECs) is critical in DVT formation, and GAS5 is implicated in endothelial injury across diseases. For instance, decreased GAS5 levels contribute to homocysteine-induced damage in cardiac microvascular endothelial cells [10]. Conversely, GAS5 overexpression attenuates oxidative stress and inflammation in VECs and maintains the vascular barrier, thereby alleviating sepsis-induced lung injury [11]. In hypertensive patients, downregulated GAS5 exacerbates microvascular dysfunction and stimulates retinal neovascularization [12]. Specifically, GAS5 is vigorously less expressed in chronic pulmonary embolic pulmonary hypertension, where it correlates with arterial wall thickening and regulates pulmonary artery smooth muscle cell viability and migration [13]. Of concern, antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a major cause of thrombosis, with patients exhibiting significant downregulation of GAS5 [14]. However, the precise role of GAS5 in DVT remains unclear.

Previous research has revealed that cytoplasmic lncRNAs can counteract the inhibitory action of microRNAs (miRNAs) on their downstream target genes via a “sponge” effect, whereby they bind to miRNAs. miRNAs, small non-coding single-stranded RNAs, play a role in the development of various diseases [15, 16]. Notably, miR-134-5p levels rise during ox-LDL-induced apoptosis and inflammation in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), leading to impaired endothelial barrier function [17]. Additionally, forkhead box protein 3 (FOXP3), also referred to as AIID, EIETER, IPEX, JM1, and XPID, resides on chromosome Xp11.23 and comprises 12 exons. Prior studies have indicated that FOXP3 expression declines in DVT, and enhancing its levels notably suppresses DVT development in mice [18]. Our bioinformatics analysis revealed that both GAS5 and FOXP3 can target miR-134-5p. However, it remains unclear whether GAS5 can modulate the vascular endothelial barrier in DVT by targeting the miR-134-5p/FOXP3 axis.

Based on these findings, this study hypothesizes that GAS5 may play a role in vascular endothelial dysfunction in DVT via the miR-134-5p/FOXP3 pathway. To investigate this, we conducted a mechanistic study using clinical patient data and an in vitro HUVECs model. Unlike prior research, our study is the first to demonstrate that reduced GAS5 expression serves as a diagnostic biomarker for DVT patients and can predict the onset of post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS). Furthermore, we have newly identified that the GAS5/miR-134-5p/FOXP3 axis helps slow DVT progression by regulating vascular endothelial dysfunction and inflammation. This research enhances our understanding of this intricate vascular condition, addresses the knowledge gap regarding the clinical relevance and underlying mechanisms of GAS5 in DVT, and offers fresh perspectives for managing DVT patients.

Materials and methods

Participants

From June 2021 to December 2023, 92 patients diagnosed with acute DVT in the lower extremities were enrolled at the Affiliated Hospital of PanZhiHua University. Inclusion criteria were (1) the presence of clinical symptoms such as edema and discomfort in the affected limb; (2) the first-time confirmation of DVT was made via the lower extremity venous Duplex ultrasound examination; (3) aged 18 years or older; (4) onset of disease less than 14 days; and (5) the patients exhibiting unilateral disease occurrence. Exclusion criteria for the study encompassed the following: (1) individuals presenting with concurrent active lower limb ulcerations; (2) those who had undergone immunosuppressive or anticoagulant treatment within the preceding four weeks; (3) patients diagnosed with coagulopathies; (4) subjects with primary vascular pathologies, circulatory disturbances, or immunodeficiency conditions; (5) individuals with a history of infarctions, including cerebrovascular and myocardial events; (6) patients afflicted with malignant tumors; and (7) pregnant females. Simultaneously, 86 age- and gender- matched controls from our institution’s healthy checkups were enrolled. The study secured ethical approval from the Affiliated Hospital of PanZhiHua University Hospital(approval number: 2021054) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, with participants providing informed consent. Based on this, we calculated Cohen’s h effect size. As noted in the introduction [3], the incidence rate of PST ranges from 20 to 50% in patients with DVT. Based on this, we calculated Cohen’s h effect size. Using p1 = 0.50 and p2 = 0.20 in the formula, we obtained an effect size h = 0.6435, suggesting a medium-to-large effect. With a significance level (α) of 0.05 (two-tailed) and a power (1-β) of 80%, G*Power software determined that a minimum of 88 cases were required. Factoring in a 20% attrition rate, the minimum adequate sample size for this study should be 110 cases. However, the actual number of participants enrolled was 178, significantly surpassing the minimum requirement. This ensured a robust study design with an ample sample size.

Sample collection and follow-up study

Clinical baseline demographics of the subjects, such as age, gender, and body mass index (BMI), were documented. Blood samples were obtained within 24 h of their visit. An automated coagulation analyzer was utilized to measure prothrombin time (PT), thrombin time (TT), international normalized ratio (INR), activated partial thromboplastin (ATPP), and fibrinogen (FIB) levels. Additionally, aliquots of blood were allowed to sit at room temperature before being centrifuged at 2500 ⋅ g for 15 min. The resultant serum supernatants were collected and stored at -80℃ for future use. All patients with DVT were followed for up to 18 months, and all patients completed the follow-up study. Moreover, the development of PTS was assessed using the Villalta Scale from the International Society on Thrombocytosis (ISTH) guidelines, with a Villalta Scale score greater than or equal to 5 being considered PTS [19, 20].

Cell culture

Human vascular endothelial cell (HUVEC) was obtained from the BeNa Culture Collection (catalog number: BNCC378266, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM (catalog number: 11965092, Gibco, USA) with 10% Fetal bovine serum (FBS, catalog number: 10437028, Gibco, USA) in a humidified incubator at 37℃ and 5% CO2. HUVECs were exposed to various Cobalt chloride (CoCl2) concentrations (0, 50, 100, 150, and 250 µM) based on the study [21]. After 24 h, GAS5 expression was measured using real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR). The optimal regulatory CoCl2 concentration was chosen for cell collection at 0, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h. RT-qPCR measured GAS5 expression. Based on prior research and findings, a 12 h treatment of HUVECs with 250 µM CoCl2 was selected for the in vitro DVT model [22, 23].

Cell transfection

The pcDNA3.1 GAS5 overexpression plasmid (oe-GAS5) and its corresponding empty vector negative control (oe-NC) were purchased from GenePharma. The oligonucleotide miR-134-5p mimic (catalog number: miR10000447-1-5) and its negative control (NC mimic, catalog number: miR1N0000001-1-5), and small interfering (si) RNA oligonucleotides specific to FOXP3 (si-FOXP3, catalog number: siB170327084238-1-5) and a negative control siRNA (si-NC, catalog number: siN0000001-1-5) were purchased from RiBoBio. Once the cells reached the logarithmic growth phase, the above plasmid (100 nM) and/or oligonucleotide (50 nM) were mixed with the transfection reagent Lipofectamine 3000 (catalog number: L300001, Thermo Fisher, USA), and then supplemented to the cells after standing at room temperature for 20 min.

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from serum samples and cells using Trizol LS reagent (catalog number: 10296010, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA), and its concentration and quality were assessed by NanoDrop spectrophotometry, yielding an A260/A280 ratio of 1.8 to 2.2, indicative of high RNA purity. 500 ng RNA were subjected to reverse transcribed to cDNA by Primer-Script One-Step RT-PCR kit (catalog number: RR064A, Takara, Japan) or miRNA First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (catalog number: B532453-0020, Sangon Biotech, China). The PCR reaction mixture, comprising cDNA, primers, ddH2O, and either the SYBR Green Real-Time Master Mix Kit (catalog number: 4385612, Bio-Rad, USA) or MicroRNAs qPCR kit (catalog number: B532461, Sangon Biotech, China), was prepared in a 20 µl volume and amplified using a PRISM7500 system. The CT values of lncRNAs, ICAM-1, or E-selectin and miRNAs were normalized to GAPDH or U6, respectively, and their expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences used for RT - qPCR analysis were listed in Supplemental Table S1.

Cell proliferation assay

The Cell Count Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay from Bimake (catalog number: B334304, Shanghai, China) was employed to assess cell proliferation. Cells (1 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with a CCK-8 solution diluted in DMEM every 24 h. Following a 2-h incubation at 37℃, proliferation was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm using a Synergy H1 microplate reader (Bio-Tek, CA, USA).

Cell apoptosis assay

Cells (1 × 10⁴ cells per sample) were washed with PBS, trypsinized, and resuspended in 100 µl of binding buffer. Apoptosis was then assayed using an Annexin-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) kit (catalog number: K101, BioVision, USA) by incubating the cells with 5 µl of Annexin-FITC for 5 min at room temperature, followed by the addition of PI for another 5 min. After adding 400 µl of binding buffer to resuspend the cells, the apoptotic cell count was quantified on a flow cytometer following filtration through a 200-mesh sieve.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Concentrations of inflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α, catalog number: E-EL-H0109), interleukin-6 (IL-6, catalog number: E-EL-H6156), and IL-1β (catalog number: E-EL-H0149, Elabscience Biotechnology Co.) were analyzed utilizing a commercial ELISA kit. In summary, supernatants and protein standards underwent dilution and were dispensed into ELISA plates pre-coated with specific antibodies. Incubation at 37℃ for 90 min facilitated the removal of unbound antibodies. Subsequently, a biotinylated antibody solution was introduced and incubated for an additional 60 min at 37℃. This was followed by the addition of 100 µl of HRP-conjugated solution and a 30-minute incubation. Afterward, 90 µl of substrate solution was added and incubated for 15 min. Upon adding the stop solution, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using an enzyme spectrophotometer.

Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) leakage assay

The LDH leakage rate in the culture medium was quantified using a commercial LDH-estimation kit (catalog number: Agappe-11407022, Mysore, India). LDH activity was determined by measuring the spectrophotometric reduction of a tetrazolium dye via NAD(H) oxidation as per the manufacturer’s protocol [24], and results were expressed as a percentage leakage.

Western blot assay

Cells were rinsed with PBS and lysed using RIPA buffer (catalog number: P0013B, Beyotime, China) supplemented with a 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (catalog number: P1045, Beyotime, China). Protein concentration was measured in the enzyme laboratory via a BCA protein assay kit (catalog number: P0010S, Beyotime, China). Denaturation was achieved by adding the sample buffer and incubating for 10 min. Subsequently, 30 µg of protein samples underwent separation via SDS-PAGE and were then transferred onto PVDF membranes. The blots were blocked using 5% skim milk dissolved in TBS for 2 h, followed by three washes with TBST. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4℃ with primary antibodies: anti-HIF-1α (1:1000; catalog number: 36169, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-VEGFA (1:1000; catalog number: ab46154, Abcam, USA), anti-FOXP3 (1:1000; catalog number: 4402, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), and anti-β-actin (1:3000, catalog number: sc-7210, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA). After three additional washes with TBS containing Tween-20 (TBST), the membranes were incubated with goat polyclonal IgG secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (1:3000; catalog number: sc-2004, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA). After incubation at room temperature for 2 h, wash three times with TBST. Finally, the chemiluminescent signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence solution, clarity™ Western blotting substrate (catalog number: WBKLS0500, Millipore, USA). The protein band signals on the membranes were visualized using the ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). Full uncropped western blot is shown in Supplemental Figure S1-S4.

Bioinformatics analysis

The LncRNASNP2 online database (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/lncRNASNP/#!/) predicts target miRNAs for GAS5. Both the miRWalk (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/) and miRPathDB databases (https://mpd.bioinf.uni-sb.de/overview.html) predicted target mRNAs for miR-134-5p and identified Forkhead Box protein 3 (FOXP3).

Nuclear/cytoplasmic fractionation assay

The PARIS kit (catalog number: AM1921, Invitrogen, USA) localized GAS5 in HUVECs. Cells were washed with PBS, lysed with 500 µl buffer on ice for 10 min, and then centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min to separate cytoplasm (supernatant) from nuclei (precipitate). Cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions were mixed with an equal 2 ⋅ binding buffer and collected. RT-qPCR analyzed the RNA to confirm GAS5 localization, using GAPDH for the cytoplasm and U6 for the nucleus as controls.

Luciferase reporter assay

The GAS5 or FOXP3 sequence containing the miR-134-5p binding site was subcloned and inserted into a luciferase reporter vector to construct the wild-type (WT)-GAS5 or WT-FOXP3 plasmid. The sequence mutated at the binding site constructed the mutant (MUT)-GAS5 or MUT-FOXP3 plasmid. HUVECs were placed in 24-well plates at 2⋅105 cells/well and cultured overnight. Then, using Lipofectamine 3000, the recombinant plasmids were co-transfected with NC mimic or miR-134-5p mimic. After 48 h, cells were lysed, and luciferase activity was assayed. Relative luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla-luciferase activity for comparison. Fold change in fluorescence was calculated relative to the control group.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays

The Magna RIP kit (catalog number: 17–700, Millipore, USA) was employed for RIP assays. Following cell harvesting, lysates were prepared using an RIP lysis buffer. Next, the lysates were incubated with anti-human Ago2 or control IgG-conjugated magnetic beads at 4 ℃ overnight. After bead washing, proteinase K treatment was performed to digest proteins, and the RNA bound to the beads was purified for subsequent RT-qPCR assay.

RNA pull-down assay

HUVECs were lysed with 1 ml RIPA buffer (containing cocktail and ribonuclease inhibitor) on ice for 30 min. After sonication and centrifugation, the supernatant was incubated with a biotin-GAS5 or miR-134-5p probe for 4 h, followed by streptavidin-magnetic beads for 2 h. The beads were then washed and used for RNA immunoprecipitation, followed by RT-qPCR.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Unpaired t-tests were used to assess differences between two groups, while ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc analysis was performed for multiple groups. Statistics and visualization were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Statistical significance for all data comparisons was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Expression and diagnostic significance of serum GAS5 in patients with DVT

Clinical baseline characteristics of DVT patients and controls were analyzed (Table 1). Demographic features and medical histories were similar between groups. However, DVT patients had persistently lower PT and ATPP time, and evidently higher INR, FIB, and D-dimer levels compared to controls (P < 0.05). Notably, DVT patients exhibited lower serum GAS5 levels compared to controls (P < 0.001, Fig. 1A). Furthermore, GAS5 demonstrated a significant negative correlation with D-dimer (r = -0.698, P < 0.001, Fig. 1B). Further analysis revealed that GAS5 had a sensitivity of 89.13% and specificity of 83.72%, effectively distinguishing DVT patients from the controls with a high diagnostic value (AUC = 0.916, 95%CI: 0.874-0.957, Fig. 1C).

GAS5 levels can predict PTS occurrences in DVT patients

All 92 patients underwent an 18-month follow-up period, during which 32.60% (30 patients) developed PTS. Patients who developed PTS had lower serum GAS5 levels compared to non-PTS patients (P < 0.005, Fig. 2A). Additionally, DVT patients were classified into high and low GAS5 expression groups based on the mean values (0.50 ± 0.19). Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that patients with low GAS5 expression were more likely to develop PTS (log-rank P = 0.010, Fig. 2B). Univariate Cox analysis showed that GAS5, age, BMI, female, hyperlipidemia, and D-dimer were influential factors for PTS and incorporation of these variables into a multifactorial Cox analysis found that lower GAS5 (HR: 0.381, 95%CI: 0.159–0.912, P = 0.030), and D-dimer (HR: 2.226, 95%CI: 1.029–4.817, P = 0.042, Table 2) were potential risk factors for the development of PTS in patients with DVT.

Impact of GAS5 overexpression on HUVECs proliferation, apoptosis, adhesion, and inflammation

To deeply analyze the functional role of GAS5 in DVT, we used CoCl2-treated HUVECs to mimic hypoxic endothelial injury, based on prior research [23]. As shown in Fig. 3A and Supplemental Figure S1, a significant increase in HIF-1α and VEGFA protein levels was observed in HUVECs as CoCl2 concentration rose (P < 0.05). Additionally, extending the induction period with 250 µM CoCl2 resulted in a notable elevation of both HIF-1α and VEGFA protein levels (P < 0.05, Fig. 3B and Supplemental Figure S2). The research findings indicate that we have successfully established a hypoxic condition.

Transfection with the oe-GAS5 plasmid effectively increased GAS5 expression in HUVECs (P < 0.05, Fig. 3E). Notably, overexpression of GAS5 effectively suppressed the inhibitory effect of CoCl2 on GAS5 levels (P < 0.05, Fig. 3F). CoCl2 also increased LDH release, indicating cytotoxicity, which GAS5 overexpression reduced (P < 0.05, Fig. 3G). CCK-8 analysis showed that CoCl2 inhibited HUVEC proliferation, but GAS5 overexpression ameliorated this effect (P < 0.05, Fig. 3H). Furthermore, the elevation of GAS5 suppressed CoCl2-induced HUVEC apoptosis (P < 0.05, Fig. 3I). CoCl2 increased ICAM-1, E-selectin, and inflammatory factors TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, but GAS5 attenuated these effects (P < 0.05, Fig. 3J-K). In conclusion, GAS5 protected against CoCl2-induced endothelial injury and inflammation.

GAS5 directly binds with miR-134-5p

Cytoplasmic lncRNAs interact with miRNAs as ceRNAs to regulate their expression. We used cytoplasmic and nuclear separation experiments to find that GAS5 is predominantly located in the cytoplasm (Fig. 4A). Bioinformatics prediction reveals a target binding site for GAS5 and miR-134-5p (Fig. 4B). The miR-134-5p mimic suppressed the luciferase activity of WT-GAS5 but not MUT-GAS5, compared to the NC mimic (P < 0.05, Fig. 4C). In RNA pull-down assays, the bio-GAS5 probe significantly precipitated miR-194-5p compared to the Bio-NC control (P < 0.05, Fig. 4D). Furthermore, both GAS5 and miR-134-5p were enriched in the anti-Ago2 group (P < 0.05, Fig. 4E). Moreover, miR-134-5p levels were higher in DVT patients than in controls (P < 0.05, Fig. 4F) and inversely correlated with GAS5 expression in patients (r = -0.716, P < 0.05, Fig. 4G). CoCl2 increased the miR-134-5p levels in HUVECs, but this effect was reduced by oe-GAS5 (P < 0.05, Fig. 4H).

To investigate GAS5’s role in regulating DVT progression via miR-134-5p, we conducted rescue expression. The GAS5 overexpression suppressed miR-134-5p expression, but this was reversed by the miR-134-5p mimic (P < 0.05, Fig. 5B). Discussion

DVT pathogenesis is complex, involving venous endothelial damage, coagulation, and inflammation. Early symptoms are subtle, leading to frequent missed diagnoses. Some patients with DVT may develop PTS, severely impacting their quality of life. The study focused on GAS5 expression in DVT patients, assessing its diagnostic and prognostic value. We found lower serum GAS5 in patients, distinguishing them from controls, and indicating a prognostic role for PTS development. Hypoxia-induced endothelial injury and inflammation drive DVT development. Treating HUVECs with CoCl2 revealed that GAS5 reduced endothelial injury, adhesion factors, and inflammation, potentially via the miR-134-5p/FOXP3 axis.

Emerging evidence shows that lncRNA exerts an essential role in the biological process of DVT formation. For instance, reduced expression of RAMP2-AS1 has been implicated in predicting DVT in elderly individuals [25]. CRNDE levels were notably increased in postpartum DVT cases and exhibited an inverse correlation with serum coagulation markers [26]. Additionally, SNHG12 emerged as an independent predictor of DVT occurrence in affected patients [6]. Previous studies show GAS5’s involvement in diabetic nephropathy [27] and osteoarthritis [28]. Endothelial progenitor cells can turn into endothelial cells (ECs), enhancing tissue blood supply by forming new vessels. GAS5 controls the growth and aging of endothelial progenitor cells [29]. In coronary artery disease, GAS5 deletion reduces endothelial progenitor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while increasing apoptosis [30]. Vascular endothelial damage is crucial in triggering DVT. It leads injured ECs to release adhesive tissue factor, initiating coagulation. Previous studies showed that IL-1β-induced retinal vascular endothelial cells had significant GAS5 downregulation [31]. GAS5 suppressed pulmonary vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, inhibiting angiogenesis and arterial wall thickening in pulmonary embolic hypertension [13]. Overexpression of GAS5 reduced oxidative stress and inflammation, and maintained the vascular barrier in endothelial cells, alleviating sepsis-induced lung injury [32]. Additionally, GAS5 was reduced in homocysteine-induced endothelial cell injury in cardiac microvessels, modulating apoptosis [10]. Our main concern is that antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) frequently causes both arterial and venous thrombosis [14]. Studies show that GAS5 is noticeably downregulated in APS patients. Similarly, we observed reduced GAS5 levels in CoCl2-injured HUVECs. Notably, our study uniquely found prominently lower GAS5 levels in the serum of DVT patients.

Imaging tests like ultrasound and CT angiography are crucial for diagnosing DVT, but lack sensitivity for early detection. In contrast, lncRNA expression changes early in DVT, aiding early diagnosis and intervention before symptoms appear. Moreover, while D-dimer is a common DVT screening tool, its specificity is low and can be elevated in tumors, infections, and trauma. By contrast, lncRNA expression is tissue-specific, offering a more accurate reflection of DVT progression. Previous studies have highlighted the diagnostic value of lncRNA NEAT1 and NORAD in DVT. Our study revealed that low GAS5 expression effectively distinguishes DVT patients from controls, with high specificity and sensitivity. PTS is a severe complication of DVT, with an incidence of 20–50% within 2 years despite standard anticoagulation [33]. Our 18-month follow-up study found a similar PTS incidence of 32.60%. Importantly, we discovered that reduced GAS5 levels could serve as a prognostic marker for PTS in these patients.

The initiation of DVT typically stems from endothelial damage in blood vessels, a natural barrier preventing coagulation. Hypoxia exacerbates endothelial cell dysfunction, leading to blood stagnation and heightened thrombosis risk. When endothelial injury occurs, cell permeability rises and adhesion molecules ICAM-1 and E-selectin upregulate, facilitating leukocyte and platelet adhesion. This, coupled with heightened inflammation, exacerbates endothelial damage. Previous studies showed that CoCl2 mimics hypoxia in HUVECs during DVT, inhibiting proliferation, promoting inflammation, apoptosis, and ICAM-1 and E-selectin expression [34]. Under hypoxic conditions, HIF-1α specifically binds to the hypoxia-responsive element within the VEGFA gene promoter. This interaction drives the transcription of VEGFA and subsequently boosts its expression. The upregulated VEGFA then binds to receptors, such as VEGFR-1 and VEGFR-2, on the surface of vascular endothelial cells. This binding activates downstream signaling cascades, initiating the process of angiogenesis. Angiogenesis enhances tissue oxygenation, serving as a crucial adaptive mechanism of the body in response to hypoxia. In diseases associated with hypoxia, like DVT, abnormal changes in VEGFA expression are closely linked to the development and progression of the disease. Notably, consistent with findings reported [35], our experiments demonstrated that as the induction time and concentration of CoCl2 increased, there was a corresponding dose-and time-dependent elevation in the levels of HIF1-α and VEGFA proteins. These results confirm the effective creation of an in vitro hypoxic environment. Our novel findings are that GAS5 overexpression noticeably mitigates these CoCl2-induced endothelial effects.

Dysregulated miRNAs exert an essential role in the biological process of DVT. miR-6132 overexpression worsens DVT formation [18], while exosomes with miR-483-5p reduce DVT-induced inflammation [36]. miR-134-5p, as one of many miRNAs, has been implicated in acute myocardial infarction [37] and early vascular dementia due to chronic ischemia [38]. MiR-134-5p contributes to endothelial dysfunction, being upregulated in 7-ketocholesterol-treated aortic endothelial cells and linked to inflammation and adhesion factor dysregulation [39]. miR-134-5p rises during endothelial progenitor cell senescence, linked to anti-angiogenesis [40]. In diabetes, far infrared light enhances endothelial function and blood flow recovery by inhibiting miR-134-5p in endothelial colony-forming cells [41]. miR-134-5p is also elevated in ox-LDL-induced apoptosis and inflammation in HUVECs, weakening endothelial barrier function [17]. Our findings align with previous studies, showing miR-134-5p elevation in CoCl2-induced endothelial injury. Notably, we also observed a significant increase in miR-134-5p levels in DVT patients for the first time. Intriguingly, recent studies have revealed that specific lncRNA located in the cytoplasm can function as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) to sponge miRNAs. GAS5 targets miR-134-5p, and its protective effects on endothelial injury were reversed by miR-134-5p.

FOXP3, also known as AIID, EIETER, IPEX, JM1, and XPID, resides on chromosome Xp11.23 and comprises 12 exons. It belongs to the forkhead transcription factor family and is crucial for regulatory T cells. Previous studies show that FOXP3 + tumor cells proliferate rapidly and regulate blood vessel formation [42]. In melanoma, FOXOP3 levels drop significantly in endothelial cell damage [43]. In breast cancer, FOXP3 inhibits HUVECs’ function [44]. FOXP3 has anti-inflammatory and cardiomyogenic effects, benefiting myocardial infarction [45]. Recent studies show that FOXP3 levels drop in DVT, and boosting its expression significantly inhibits DVT formation in mice [18]. In this study, we found that reduced FOXP3 levels in DVT patients and CoCl2-induced HUVCEs. Importantly, we discovered a targeting relationship between FOXP3 and miR-134-5p, where FOXP3 negatively regulates miR-134-5p expression. Furthermore, silencing FOXP3 significantly abrogated GAS5’s effects on CoCl2-induced proliferation, apoptosis, LDH release, and vascular adhesion/inflammatory factor expression in HUVECs. Therefore, GAS5 may be involved in DVT progression through the miR-134-5p/FOXP3 axis.

It must be acknowledged that this study has certain limitations. Firstly, the single-center design and short follow-up period may introduce bias and limit the generalizability of our findings. Secondly, the in vitro model we employed, specifically the use of HUVECs, is an oversimplification. HUVECs do not fully capture the accuracy of our results in representing real-world scenarios. Moreover, we did not incorporate animal models in our current research. The inclusion of animal model data, such as the assessment of thrombus volume and the incidence of PTS after overexpression or knockdown of GAS5, could have provided stronger support for our current findings. To address these limitations, we plan to conduct multi-center studies in the future to enhance the reliability and generalizability of our results. Additionally, we will establish relevant animal models to further validate the mechanisms and effects observed in our in vitro experiments. These directions will be the primary focus of our subsequent research. Furthermore, considering the confounding role of hypertension in our present investigation, subsequent studies ought to include non-hypertensive individuals. This will enable a more precise assessment of the specific impact of GAS5 on vascular function in deep vein thrombosis patients, offering more robust evidence for clinical applications. Furthermore, we will do refined stratified analyses on different patient/clinical factors to improve study quality. And we’ll carry out prospective cohort studies and use external validation datasets. Given the complexity of signaling pathways in the GAS5/miR-134-5p/FOXP3 axis related to DVT endothelial injury, we’ll use advanced techniques like single-cell sequencing and phosphoproteomics in follow-up studies for in-depth exploration.

Conclusion

In conclusion, GAS5 levels were markedly lower in DVT patients and served as a biomarker for diagnosing and predicting PTS. Moreover, GAS5 suppresses thrombus formation by targeting the miR-134-5p/FOXP3 axis, affecting endothelial cell function and inflammation.
