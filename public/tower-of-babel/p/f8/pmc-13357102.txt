WAC is associated with malignant phenotypes and Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity in liver cancer cells
Authors: Kejia Yuan, Zexian Ding, Lei Peng, Jinghua Zeng, Han Yang, Qi Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLiver cancer remains a major cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide. WW domain-containing adaptor with coiled-coil (WAC) participates in multiple biological processes, including transcriptional regulation, autophagy, and cell cycle progression, but its role in liver cancer remains insufficiently defined. This study aimed to investigate the expression pattern, clinical relevance, biological function, and potential pathway association of WAC in liver cancer.MethodsPublic datasets, including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), were analyzed to assess WAC expression and its association with clinicopathological features, prognosis, predicted drug sensitivity, and immune-related characteristics. WAC expression was further examined in liver cancer cell lines. Loss-of-function experiments were performed to evaluate the effects of WAC silencing on cell proliferation, migration, invasion, cell cycle distribution, and apoptosis. Transcriptomic analysis and molecular assays were used to explore pathways associated with WAC, and SKL2001 was applied to assess the involvement of Wnt/β-catenin signaling.ResultsWAC was upregulated in liver cancer tissues and cell lines, and higher WAC expression was associated with adverse clinicopathological features and poorer survival outcomes. WAC knockdown suppressed liver cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, induced G2/M phase arrest, and promoted apoptosis. Transcriptomic and molecular analyses indicated that WAC silencing was associated with reduced Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity. Activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling by SKL2001 partially reversed the inhibitory effects caused by WAC knockdown. WAC expression was also associated with predicted sensitivity to several chemotherapeutic agents and immune microenvironment-related features. The proposed WAC-FZD10 association was based on computational prediction and requires experimental validation.ConclusionsWAC is upregulated in liver cancer and is associated with malignant cellular phenotypes and Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity. These findings suggest that WAC may serve as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target candidate in liver cancer.

Highlight box

Key findings

• WW domain-containing adaptor with coiled-coil (WAC) was upregulated in liver cancer tissues and cell lines, and higher WAC expression was associated with adverse clinicopathological features and poorer survival outcomes. Functional assays showed that WAC silencing suppressed liver cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, induced G2/M phase arrest, and promoted apoptosis. Mechanistically, WAC knockdown was associated with reduced Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity, while the predicted WAC-FZD10 association remains to be experimentally validated.

What is known and what is new?

• WAC is known to participate in several biological processes, including transcriptional regulation, autophagy, cell cycle progression, and DNA damage responses. Previous studies have suggested that WAC may exert context-dependent roles in different cancer types, but its function in liver cancer remains poorly defined.

• This study provides evidence that WAC is aberrantly expressed in liver cancer and is associated with malignant cellular phenotypes. In addition, our findings suggest that WAC may be linked to Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity, predicted drug-sensitivity profiles, and immune-related features of the liver cancer microenvironment.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• WAC may represent a potential biomarker and therapeutic target candidate in liver cancer. However, the current findings should be interpreted as preclinical evidence, and further studies using additional liver cancer models, direct pharmacologic validation, and biochemical assays are required to clarify the precise regulatory mechanism and translational significance of WAC.

Introduction

Liver cancer poses a major global health burden, accounting for 4.7% of new cancer cases and 8.3% of cancer-related deaths, ranking sixth in incidence and third in mortality globally, with new cases expected to exceed one million cases by 2025 (1-3). Liver cancer pathogenesis remains incompletely elucidated, with established risk factors encompassing chronic hepatitis B/C Virus (HBV/HCV) infections, aflatoxin exposure, excessive alcohol consumption, obesity, type 2 diabetes, and smoking (4). Liver cancer’s global burden drives evolving therapeutic strategies (1,5,6). Current treatments encompass resection (2,7), transplantation (7), ablation (8), transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) (8,9), and systemic therapy (8,9). The challenges of early detection in liver cancer are underscored by the fact that over 50% of patients are diagnosed at the symptomatic stage, typically presenting with palpable masses or cancer-related pain (2). As a result, advanced-stage management has increasingly relied on systemic therapies, such as targeted agents and immunotherapies, which have demonstrated survival benefits (10-12). However, the expanding use of targeted therapies has also led to an increase in both primary and acquired resistance (13). This development highlights the urgent need for the discovery of diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets to drive breakthroughs in liver cancer diagnosis and treatment.

The WW domain-containing adaptor with coiled-coil (WAC) gene is located on chromosome 10p11.2-p12.1 (14). The WAC protein contains two functional domains: An N-terminal WW domain (129-162aa) and a C-terminal coiled-coil domain (CC, 618-644aa). The WW domain facilitates ubiquitin-dependent proteostasis, signaling cascades, and RNA Pol II-mediated transcriptional/splicing processes (15-17). The CC domain mediates the formation of the RNF20/40 E3 ligase complex, which is essential for H2BK120ub1-dependent transcriptional regulation (15,16). Additionally, the CC domain regulates alternative splicing through the formation of biomolecular condensates (18).

WAC localizes to both the nucleus (15,19-21) and cytoplasm (19,20), critically regulating histone ubiquitination (15), transcriptional control (15), autophagy (17,19,20,22), and cell cycle progression (15,21,23). During mitotic progression, WAC undergoes phase-specific phosphorylation: nuclear-localized during interphase, cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1)-mediated phosphorylation activates the mitotic regulator polo-like kinase 1 (Plk1). In the G2 phase, aurora A kinase (AURKA)-dependent phosphorylation in the G2 phase ensures mitotic entry (21). In the DNA damage response, WAC orchestrates cell cycle checkpoint activation and progression (15,23). WAC has emerged as a context-dependent regulator of oncogenic mechanisms across various cancers. In glioblastoma (GBM), WAC orchestrates a complex interplay between autophagy and apoptosis pathways, with its overexpression leading to suppressed proliferation and concurrent activation of both P62/LC3B-mediated autophagy and apoptotic processes (17). Furthermore, WAC has been implicated in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), in which it harbors recurrent mutations (24). In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), WAC-RET (rearranged during transfection) exhibits distinct structural features that may underpin a unique oncogenic mechanism (16). Bioinformatics analyses have further revealed tumor-specific expression patterns of WAC across multiple cancer types (25-27). Despite these findings, the functional roles and underlying mechanisms of WAC in hepatic pathophysiology remain significantly underexplored.

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is frequently hyperactivated in malignancies (28). This pathway is activated when extracellular Wnt ligands bind to their receptors, leading to the accumulation of intracellular β-catenin, which subsequently translocates to the nucleus. In the nucleus, β-catenin recruits transcriptional co-activators to promote the expression of oncogenic genes (29). The Wnt/β-catenin pathway plays a critical role in promoting proliferation, migration, and invasion across multiple cancers, including lung cancer (30), stomach cancer (31), B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) (32) and other tumors. In liver cancer, the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is a key regulator of tumor progression, it orchestrates tumor cell proliferation, migration, and apoptosis through multifaceted mechanisms (33). Mechanistically, androgen receptor (AR) drives hepatocarcinogenesis by binding the enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2), thereby enhancing H3K27me3-mediated silencing of Wnt inhibitors, which amplifies β-catenin signaling (34). Conversely, SRY-box transcription factor 11 (SOX11) exerts tumor-suppressive effects by phosphorylating transcription factor 4 (TCF4) and upregulating nemo-like kinase (NLK), both of which antagonize Wnt/β-catenin signaling to inhibit proliferation and induce apoptosis (35). The Wnt/β-catenin pathway further promotes liver cancer progression by interacting with the tumor microenvironment (TME). Activation of canonical Wnt signaling enhances the formation of the E-cadherin/β-catenin complex, thereby increasing metastatic potential (36). Clinically, the competitive activator circRNA-SORE has been shown to activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, leading to sorafenib resistance in liver cancer (37). Collectively, these findings highlight the central role of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in the pathogenesis and progression of liver cancer. This underscores the importance of systematically exploring the mechanisms by which this pathway contributes to liver cancer development and progression.

Our study is the first to reveal that WAC is overexpressed in liver cancer and is associated with adverse clinicopathological features and poor survival. Functional analyses indicate that WAC is associated with the regulation of liver cancer cell proliferation, apoptosis, cell cycle progression, and migratory/invasive phenotypes. Mechanistically, our data suggest that WAC is associated with Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity in liver cancer cells. These findings support WAC as both a potential prognostic biomarker and a therapeutic target in liver cancer. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0480/rc).

Methods

WAC expression and datasets analysis

We obtained transcriptome data of patients with cancers through The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database from the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) database (https://xenabrowser.net/). From this dataset, we extracted gene expression data specific to liver cancer, which included 374 liver cancer tissues and 50 normal liver tissues, as well as the corresponding clinical data. Additionally, we accessed the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database official website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) to download the liver cancer-related dataset GSE14520, which included the gene expression data of 225 liver cancer tissues and 220 normal liver tissues. TCGA-plot R package was used to analyze the expression of WAC in pan-cancer (*P<0.05, **P<0.01, ***P<0.001). UALCAN (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) is a comprehensive and user-friendly interactive web resource for analyzing cancer omics data. Using clinical data, we explored the correlation between WAC expression and clinical indicators such as age, tumor grade, gender, tumor stage, and TP53 mutation status. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Human Protein Atlas (HPA) analysis

HPA (https://www.Proteinatlas.org/) was used to analyze the protein expression of WAC in normal and liver cancer tissues.

Association of WAC expression with survival outcomes

Clinical data and WAC expression data for liver cancer patients were downloaded from the TCGA database via the UCSC platform. The Kaplan-Meier plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis) was used to analyze survival differences between the high WAC expression group and the low WAC expression group, and the cutoff for WAC expression was established based on the median expression levels identified through web-based analysis.

Analysis of drug treatment and WAC expression

Utilizing the normalized transcriptome data of TCGA-LIHC together with the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer v2 (GDSC2) database, we predicted the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of multiple compounds for each TCGA-LIHC patient using the oncoPredict R package. Detailed results of the drug-sensitivity correlation analysis are provided in table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tcr-2026-0480-1.xlsx.

Immune infiltration analysis

The relationship between WAC and lymphocyte infiltration, immunomodulators, chemokines, and receptors in cancer was analyzed in the TISIDB (http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/).

Cell culture and transfection

Normal liver cell line (THLE-2) and liver cancer cell lines (HepG2, 97H) were purchased from a cell bank at the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). THLE-2 cells were authenticated by STR profiling and showed an exact match with the following human cell line(s) THLE-2 in the ExPASy STR database (13 core STR loci), serving as a suitable normal liver cell line control for further studies. THLE-2 cells were cultured in 1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin; HepG2 and 97H were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin; and all cells were maintained in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 ℃. In this study, HepG2 and MHCC-97H were used as representative liver cancer cell models; however, HepG2 is not an ideal model for canonical hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) biology and should be interpreted with this limitation in mind.

Cells were transfected twice with siRNA in a 24 h interval. The following siRNA duplexes were ordered from RiboBio (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China): Scrambled siRNA sequence: 5'-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU-3', siWAC-1 (5'-GAAAGAGAACAGAGACAAAdTdT-3'), siWAC-2 (5'-GUAUUCAUCGAAGAGUCACdTdT-3'). siRNA transfections were done with Lipofectamine RNAi MAX (13778150, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

For stable WAC knockdown in HepG2 and 97H cell lines, the target sequence 5'-CGGAGATCTGATAGTCCTGAATTCAAGAGATTCAGGACTATCAGATCTCCG-3' was synthesized and cloned into the pLV3-U6-shRNA-Negative-Control-CopGFP-Puro backbone (MiaoLingBio, Wuhan, China) to generate the shWAC construct. The pLV3-U6-shRNA-Negative-Control-CopGFP-Puro plasmid was used as control of knockdown. HEK293T cells were transfected with the recombinant vector in conjunction with two packaging plasmids, pMD2.G and psPAX2, using lipo8000 (C0533-0.5 mL, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) as transfection reagents. After 48 h transfection, the viruses-containing cell culture supernatants were collected and filtered by 0.2 µm filter membrane (SLGPR33RB, Millipore, Massachusetts, USA). Subsequently, the target cell lines were transfected with the corresponding lentiviral particles in the presence of 10 µg/mL polybrene (SC-134220, Santa Cruz, Shanghai, China) for 6 h. The following selection for positive cells was achieved through the application of 10 µg/mL puromycin (HY-K1057, MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) in HepG2 and 97H cells.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA from cultured cells was extracted with TRIzol (R401-01, Vazyme, Jiangsu, China). cDNA synthesis was performed using the EvoM-MLVRT Premix (AG11706, Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) qPCR was performed by using a SYBR Green Premix Pro TaqHs qPCR kit (AG11701, Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) on the Bio-Rad CFX96 Connect real-time PCR detection system. Data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method with ACTB as the housekeeping gene. The primer sequences of chosen genes were shown in Table S1. For the p38 assay, the extension time was increased to 30 seconds at 72 ℃ to account for the larger product size (589 bp), and the annealing temperature was optimized to 63 ℃ to enhance specificity.

Western blots (WB)

Cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and incubated on ice for 30 minutes. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 15 minutes at 4 ℃. Protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (P0011, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, 30 µg of protein per lane was separated on a 10% gel by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (C631100, Sangon, Shanghai, China) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (ISEQ00010, MilliporeSigma, USA). The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 2 hours at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 ℃ with diluted primary antibodies. The membrane was then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit/mouse IgG; Cat. Nos. 7074s and 7076s; Cell Signaling Technology, Massachusetts, USA) for 1 hour at room temperature. Primary antibodies included proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) (Cat. No. 2586, Cell Signaling Technology, Massachusetts, USA), WAC (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; Cat. No. ab203591), and GAPDH (YM3029, Immunoway, Plano, Texas, USA), with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) used as a loading control. Protein bands were visualized using chemiluminescence (Bio-Rad, GE Healthcare, CA, USA). For proteins whose molecular weight differed by ≥10 kDa from the loading control, the same membrane was cut. When the sizes were too similar to permit sequential detection, two identical gels loaded with the same lysate were run in parallel; one was probed for the target protein and the other for the loading control.

Cell viability and colony formation assays

Liver cancer cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3×103 cells per well. Cell viability was measured at 450 nm using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (K1018, APExBIO, Houston, Texas, USA) at four time points (0, 24, 48, and 72 h). To assess cell proliferation, colony formation assays were performed. Single-cell suspensions were prepared, and 1,000 cells were seeded into each well of a 6-well plate. After 14 days, the cells were fixed with 4% PFA (4% Paraformaldehyde Fix Solution, E672002, Sangon, Shanghai, China) and stained with crystal violet (C0775, Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA). Each plate was thoroughly rinsed three times with distilled water and scanned.

Transwell invasion assay

The Transwell chambers (24-well, 8 µm pore size, Cat. No.3422, Corning, Life Sciences, USA) coated with Matrigel (Cat. No.354230, BD Biosciences, USA) were used for invasion assays. Approximately 1×105 cells were seeded into the upper chambers and incubated overnight. The upper chamber contained serum-free medium, while the lower chamber contained medium supplemented with 10% FBS (10099-141, Gibco, USA). After 48 hours of incubation at 37 ℃, non-invaded cells were gently removed. The invaded cells were fixed with 4% PFA, stained with crystal violet for 15 minutes, and counted under an inverted microscope in five randomly selected fields of view.

Scratch wound healing assay

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates (703001, NEST, USA) and cultured to reach 100% confluence. A sterile P200 pipette tip was used to create a scratch in the cell monolayer. Images of the wound area were captured at 0, 24, 48 and 72 h using a Leica microscope (Nikon, Japan). Cell migration rates were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Flow cytometry

For cell cycle analysis, cells were harvested and fixed in 70% cold ethanol overnight at 4 ℃. After washing with phosphate buffered saline (PBS), the cells were incubated with 100 mg/L ribonuclease A (RNase A) and 50 mg/L propidium iodide (PI) solution (C1352, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at 37 ℃ in the dark for 30 minutes. The distribution of cells in the G1, S, and G2/M phases was analyzed by flow cytometry using a FACSCanto II (BD Biosciences, 488 nm).

Cell apoptosis was assessed using an APC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit with PI staining (Cat. No. 640932, BioLegend, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were washed twice with 4 ℃ PBS and incubated with 5 µL of Annexin V-APC and 10 µL of PI in the dark at room temperature for 15 minutes. The proportion of Annexin V-APC-positive and/or PI-positive cells was determined by flow cytometry using a FACSCanto II (BD Biosciences, 488 nm).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using R software (version 4.3.1) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Differences between two groups were assessed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test was employed.

The correlation between WAC expression and predicted drug sensitivity (IC50) or the expression of other genes was determined using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Survival curves based on TCGA data were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and statistical significance was assessed using the log-rank test.

A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels in the figures are denoted as follows: ***, P<0.001, and ****, P<0.0001.

Results

Elevated WAC expression in liver cancer and its correlation with clinicopathological features

We analyzed the TCGA database and found that compared with normal tissues, the expression of WAC was significantly different in 11 tumor types, including breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), GBM, kidney chromophobe (KICH), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), thyroid carcinoma (THCA), uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), and stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) (Figure 1A). Subsequently, we analyzed the data from the TCGA-LIHC database (T=374, N=50) and observed that WAC was significantly overexpressed in LIHC samples compared with adjacent normal tissues (P<0.001) (Figure 1B). Further paired analysis revealed that WAC was highly expressed in 50 paired liver cancer samples (P<0.001) (Figure 1C). We then used the expression data from GSE14520 (T=225, N=220) for additional validation, confirming that WAC was highly expressed in tumor tissues from GSE14520 datasets (P<0.001) (Figure 1D). WB analysis further validated the elevated expression of WAC in liver cancer cell lines (HepG2 and 97H) compared with normal hepatocytes (THLE-2) (Figure 1E). HPA dataset provided evidence for WAC overexpression at the protein level in liver cancer tissues (Figure 1F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate the upregulation of WAC and suggest its potential oncogenic role in liver cancer.

Analysis of the UALCAN database revealed that WAC expression in liver cancer is associated with gender (Figure 1G) and age (Figure 1H) and increases progressively with tumor stage (Figure 1I) and grade (Figure 1J). In addition, WAC expression was higher in tumor protein 53 (TP53) mutant tumors (Figure 1K) and significantly increased in patients with lymph node metastasis (Figure 1L). These findings suggest that WAC may play a role in tumor progression, malignancy, and metastasis, and may be related to TP53-related pathways.

Survival analysis showed that in the TCGA cohort, liver cancer patients with higher WAC expression levels had significantly worse overall survival (OS), progression-free interval (PFI), disease-specific survival (DSS), and disease-free interval (DFI) (Figure 1M-1P). These results showed that elevated WAC levels were associated with poorer survival outcomes in LIHC patients; however, further multivariable analyses will be needed to determine whether WAC has independent prognostic value.

Predicted drug-sensitivity analysis

To investigate the association between WAC expression and predicted drug-sensitivity profiles, we estimated the drug sensitivity of each patient in the TCGA-LIHC cohort using the oncoPredict framework trained on the GDSC pharmacogenomic database. The correlation between the predicted IC50 of drugs and WAC gene expression levels was analyzed (Figure 2A-2I). A total of 198 drugs were analyzed, with all results provided in table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tcr-2026-0480-1.xlsx. Nine representative results were selectively presented, showing that WAC expression levels were significantly correlated with the predicted IC50 values of the following drugs: Vinblastine (R=−0.517, P<0.001), Cytarabine (R=−0.206, P<0.001), Docetaxel (R=−0.229, P<0.001), Navitoclax (R=−0.373, P<0.001), Vorinostat (R=−0.361, P<0.001), Nilotinib (R=−0.291, P<0.001), Axitinib (R=−0.527, P<0.001), Afatinib (R=−0.176, P<0.001), PD173074 (R=−0.635, P<0.001). These findings suggest that WAC expression is associated with predicted chemotherapeutic sensitivity profiles in liver cancer in silico. This observation indicates that WAC may have potential value as a biomarker for stratifying candidate therapeutic agents, although direct pharmacologic validation is still required.

WAC expression is associated with immune-related features in the TME

It is well established that the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) plays a pivotal role in the initiation, progression, and final prognosis of tumors (38-40). Tumor cells can modulate TIME, thereby influencing key biological characteristics of tumors (41). To further explore the immune-related correlates of WAC expression, we analyzed the associations between WAC expression and lymphocyte infiltration, immunomodulators, chemokines, and receptors using the TISIDB database. For clarity, six representative features from each category are presented in the main figures. Our findings reveal that high expression of WAC is negatively correlated with the infiltration of major immune cells, immunosuppressants, chemokines and receptors (Figure 3A-3F).

WAC silencing suppresses the proliferation, migration, and invasion of liver cancer cells

Systematic WAC perturbation was achieved through dual siRNA-mediated silencing (siWAC-1/siWAC-2) in two liver cancer cell models (HepG2 and 97H). Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by immunoblotting and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) (more than 70% reduction) (Figure 4A,4B). CCK-8 assays showed that WAC knockdown significantly inhibited liver cancer cell proliferation (Figure 4C). In addition, the expression of PCNA, a key marker of cell proliferation, was significantly downregulated in liver cancer cells transfected with siWAC-1 or siWAC-2, confirming proliferation impairment at the molecular level (Figure 4D). To further investigate the role of WAC in liver cancer cell proliferation, we generated stable WAC-knockdown HepG2 and 97H cell lines using short hairpin RNA (shRNA). RT-qPCR and WB analyses confirmed that shWAC markedly downregulated WAC expression levels (Figure 4E,4F). Colony formation assays revealed that the colony-forming ability of WAC-knockdown HepG2 and 97H cells was markedly diminished compared to that of control cells (P<0.05) (Figure 4G,4H). Collectively, these findings suggest that WAC serves as a critical regulator of liver cancer cell proliferation. To explore the potential role of WAC in liver cancer progression and metastasis, we performed wound healing and transwell assays to investigate its effects on liver cancer cell migration and invasion. The wound-healing assay showed that WAC knockdown significantly reduced wound closure in liver cancer cells compared with the control group (Figure 4I,4J). Because WAC knockdown also affected proliferation and cell-cycle progression, the reduced wound closure observed in this assay may partly reflect slower cell growth rather than a pure migration defect. Furthermore, transwell migration and matrigel invasion assays revealed a significant reduction in the number of migrating and invading cells in the stable WAC-knockdown group compared to the control group (Figure 4K,4L), providing additional support for impaired migratory/invasive potential after WAC knockdown. Collectively, these findings suggest that WAC promotes the migration and invasion of liver cancer cells, identifying it as a potential oncogene and a promising target for inhibiting tumor growth and metastasis.

WAC silencing induces G2/M phase arrest and promotes apoptosis in liver cancer cells

To elucidate the mechanistic basis of proliferation arrest, we performed synchronized cell cycle analysis by flow cytometry. After 48 hours’ post-transfection, siWAC-1 and siWAC-2 induced G2 phase arrest in liver cancer cells, this analysis revealed that WAC perturbation induces phase-specific checkpoint activation (Figure 5A,5B). WB analysis further demonstrated that silencing WAC led to the downregulation of cyclin A2 and cyclin D1 in both HepG2 and 97H cells (Figure 5C). Additionally, Annexin V/PI co-staining showed WAC silencing significantly promoted both early and late apoptosis in liver cancer cells (Figure 5D,5E).

WAC is associated with Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity in liver cancer cells

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed to delineate the regulatory role of WAC in hepatocarcinogenesis by comparing WAC-silenced HepG2 cells with their wild-type counterparts. Comparative transcriptomic analysis identified 827 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), comprising 705 downregulated and 122 upregulated transcripts (Figure 6A,6B). To determine the biological relevance of these transcriptional changes, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in core oncogenic pathways (P<0.001), particularly the Wnt signaling pathway, MAPK cascade, and PI3K-AKT axis (Figure 6C). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis demonstrated alterations in cancer-related processes, such as signal transduction, regulation of cell population proliferation, and negative regulation of cell population proliferation (Figure 6D). To explore the specific molecular pathways by which WAC regulates tumorigenesis and development, we selected key regulators from these pathways for validation by qPCR. qPCR analysis showed that WAC knockdown significantly decreased the mRNA levels of β-catenin, PI3K, and p38 in both HepG2 and 97H cells, whereas AKT showed opposite changes in the two cell lines and p27 showed no significant change (Figure 6E). WB analysis showed that silencing WAC downregulated the expression level of β-catenin protein (Figure 6F). To further confirm the regulatory effect of WAC on the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, we treated cells with the Wnt signaling pathway agonist SKL2001 to restore the Wnt signaling. The experimental results showed that SKL2001 notably restored the downregulation of β-catenin, PCNA, cyclinA2 and cyclinD1 protein levels caused by WAC knockdown (Figure 6G). Bioinformatic analysis of the TCGA database revealed a positive correlation between catenin beta 1 (CTNNB1, the gene encoding β-catenin) and WAC expression in LIHC (Figure 6H). Furthermore, qPCR analysis demonstrated WAC knockdown downregulated the mRNA levels of WNT16 and FZD10, which are ligands and receptors of the Wnt classical signaling pathway, respectively (Figure 6I). Further molecular docking analysis predicted a possible binding mode between WAC and FZD10 (Figure 6J), suggesting a hypothetical association that requires experimental validation. Collectively, these findings suggest that WAC is associated with Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity in liver cancer cells, while the proposed link with FZD10 remains based on computational prediction.

Discussion

Our study suggests that WAC is associated with cell cycle progression, migratory/invasive phenotypes, and Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity in liver cancer cells. WAC is significantly upregulated in liver cancer tissues and cells and may function as a pro-tumorigenic factor in liver cancer (Figure 6K). Previous study has reported that WAC may exert tumor-suppressive effects in GBM, where it induces apoptosis and autophagy (17). Furthermore, pan-cancer analyses reveal that WAC exhibits differential expression across various cancers, being upregulated in some and downregulated in others. These findings underscore the context-dependent role of WAC in cancer development. We propose that this phenomenon may be attributed to tissue-specific signaling pathways, with WAC interacting with distinct molecular networks in different cancers. Alternatively, the role of WAC could be influenced by differences in epigenetic and post-translational modifications among various cancers (32,34). Epigenetic alterations, such as DNA methylation or phosphorylation, may affect WAC’s expression, thereby contributing to its diverse roles in different cellular contexts.

Given the influence of signaling pathways and the TME on drug resistance (13,43,44), this study explored the association between WAC expression and predicted drug-sensitivity profiles. Our findings suggest that WAC expression is associated with immune-related features of the liver cancer microenvironment. In contrast, the TMEs in other cancers may exhibit distinct behaviors, potentially supporting tumor suppression (45). Notably, the observed correlation between WAC expression and predicted sensitivity to several chemotherapeutic agents suggests that WAC may be associated with drug-response profiles in silico. This mechanism may involve modifications to the TME, such as stromal reprogramming, or facilitation of immune evasion, particularly in light of the fact that cold immune microenvironments are typically associated with poor immune responses (46). Such immune-related features have been associated with adverse clinical outcomes in previous studies (47), although the present data do not establish a causal role for WAC in these processes. To address this limitation, future research should focus on direct pharmacologic experiments to determine whether WAC modulates therapeutic response in liver cancer. Specifically, our observation that higher WAC expression correlates with predicted sensitivity is noteworthy. We speculate that this association may be related to the role of WAC in promoting cell cycle progression, as suggested by our experimental data. Cancer cells in a more proliferative state may display altered sensitivity to cytotoxic drugs that target cell-cycle-associated processes, such as vinblastine and docetaxel. Direct pharmacologic validation of this hypothesis will be an important objective for future studies.

Primary liver cancer encompasses several types, including HCC, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, hepatoblastoma (HB), and fibrolamellar carcinoma. The HepG2 cell line, originally derived from an HCC tumor in a 15-year-old Caucasian male, exhibits molecular and expression profiles more closely aligned with HB. Nevertheless, HepG2 remains widely used in liver cancer research, although it is not an ideal model for canonical HCC biology and this limitation should be taken into account when interpreting the present findings. In this study, we employed both HepG2 (HB origin) and MHCC-97H (HCC origin) cell lines. WAC knockdown was achieved using both siRNA and shRNA techniques. Transient knockdown via siRNA allowed us to assess the immediate functional effects of WAC loss on liver cancer cells, while stable knockdown using shRNA provided a model to investigate the long-term consequences of WAC depletion and established a foundation for further mechanistic studies. Functional assays demonstrated that siWAC/shWAC consistently suppressed proliferation, invasion, and migration in both lines, induced G2/M phase arrest, and promoted apoptosis. It should be noted that the wound-healing assay was performed over 0–72 h, and because WAC knockdown also reduced proliferation and altered cell-cycle progression, the reduced wound closure may partly reflect slower cell growth rather than a pure migration defect. Therefore, the scratch assay results should be interpreted with caution, while the transwell data provide additional support for reduced migratory/invasive potential after WAC knockdown. Further investigation suggested that WAC may be involved in liver cancer pathogenesis in association with Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity. The concordant results observed in these two liver cancer cell models support the reproducibility of our findings in the tested systems, while the generalizability of these observations to broader liver cancer contexts should be interpreted with caution.

Functional analyses support a pro-tumorigenic role of WAC in liver cancer cells. Mechanistically, WAC drives tumor progression through two interconnected pathways: promoting cell cycle progression to enhance proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis processes to promote survival, which aligns with previous reports on its role in cell cycle regulation and autophagic apoptosis (17,21,22). The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is well-established as a critical role in embryonic development and adult tissue homeostasis, and its dysregulation is inextricably linked to cancer development (28,48). Studies have demonstrated that dysregulated Wnt/β-catenin signaling drives liver cancer pathogenesis by modulating cell cycle regulators (e.g., cyclins) to enhance proliferation and induce drug resistance through pathway crosstalk (29,49-51). Furthermore, activation of this pathway has been linked to TME remodeling (52), while its inhibition has been shown to suppress liver cancer growth by inducing autophagy-mediated proliferation arrest (53,54). Despite our experimental evidence that WAC knockdown decreases the expression of FZD10 and β-catenin in HepG2 and 97H cells, the precise mechanism by which WAC is associated with the Wnt/β-catenin signaling axis remains unclear. Specifically, it is not yet established whether WAC physically interacts with to components of this pathway to regulate β-catenin expression or localization. This represents one of the key limitations of the current study. SKL2001 is an agonist of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway (55,56). Notably, the addition of SKL2001 in the WAC knockdown group significantly restored the protein levels associated with proliferation and β-catenin that were reduced by WAC knockdown. These findings are consistent with the Results section and further support an association between WAC and Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity in liver cancer cells. This observation is likely attributed to the fact that the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is already over-activated in liver cancer cells, a finding consistent with previous reports (57). While molecular docking suggested a possible binding mode between WAC and FZD10, this computational result does not establish a verified physical interaction, and the precise mechanism and regulatory nature remain uncharacterized. Further studies using assays such as co-immunoprecipitation and GST pull-down will be needed to determine whether WAC physically interacts with FZD10 and to clarify the biological relevance of this predicted association.

In addition, although inclusion of additional canonical HCC cell lines would further strengthen the study, this was not feasible in the current work and should be considered a limitation when interpreting the scope of our conclusions.

In conclusion, our study first reports WAC overexpression in liver cancer, which is associated with adverse clinicopathological features and poor prognosis. WAC is identified as a potential pro-tumorigenic factor in liver cancer, and we further characterize its association with cellular processes such as proliferation, migration, invasion, cell cycle progression, and apoptosis. Mechanistically, our data suggest that WAC may be associated with canonical Wnt signaling in liver cancer cells, whereas the proposed link with FZD10 is currently based only on molecular docking and requires further experimental validation. These findings hold significant promise for the identification of predictive biomarkers in liver cancer and suggest that targeting WAC may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for the treatment of this malignancy.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study identifies WAC as a potential pro-tumorigenic factor in liver cancer. WAC is upregulated in liver cancer and is associated with malignant clinicopathological features and poorer survival outcomes. Functional experiments showed that WAC silencing suppresses proliferation, migration, and invasion, induces G2/M phase arrest, and promotes apoptosis in liver cancer cells. Mechanistically, WAC may be associated with Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity, while the predicted WAC-FZD10 association requires further experimental validation. These findings suggest that WAC may serve as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target candidate for liver cancer.
