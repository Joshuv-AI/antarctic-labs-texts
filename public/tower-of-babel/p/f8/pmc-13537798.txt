Policy Silences and the Case for Dedicated Legislation for Street Sweepers in India: Policy Content Analysis using the READ Approach
Authors: Gajalakshmi Selvaraj, Abraham Prince Durai Elangovan, Sudalai Subramani, Mahalakshmy Thulasingam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Street sweepers play a vital role in sanitation through solid waste management. They are exposed to a number of occupational hazards, including dust, infectious agents, ergonomics-related disorders, and injuries. Street sweepers are identified as sanitary workers and also municipal solid waste management workers, resulting in fragmentation of guidelines, manuals and legislations governing street sweepers. This necessitates a comprehensive review of existing policy, legal and implementation guidelines, which might apply to street sweepers in India. Content analysis of both policy and nonpolicy documents following the READ approach was done. Thrust areas related to health and safety measures were identified through thematic extraction of health and safety recommendations from the systematic reviews. The document search resulted in one key occupational health policy, four legal documents and nine implementation documents. The policy document analysis identified three key thematic areas, including Enforcement and Compliance, Standards and Awareness and Research, and Monitoring and Evaluation. It was found that there is no separate legislation governing the occupational health of street sweepers. The standards for equipment used, manuals, and job rotation are still in formative stages. There are no fixed medical criteria for joining and continuing as street sweepers. The thematic area research, monitoring, and evaluation was poorly represented. Street sweepers require separate legislation to govern their occupational health and safety. Appropriate standards for equipment, medical criteria for sweepers and regular monitoring should be set up. Researches should be carried out to explore the health risk of sweepers, especially ergonomic and respiratory-related illness.

INTRODUCTION

Urban sanitation includes safe management of human excreta, safe drinking water, and solid wastes, and it plays an integral part in public health by contributing to disease prevention and mortality reduction.[12] Annually 60 million tons of municipal solid waste is being generated and street sweepers play a vital role in its management.[3] They help to keep the city clean through street sweeping, garbage collection, and side drain cleaning. Street sweepers are part of sanitary workers, where sanitary workers also include waste pickers and septic tank and sewer cleaners.[4] Sewer cleaners job description includes unblocking and cleaning sewers and wastewater drains.[4] Apart from being part of the sanitary service chain, street sweepers play a vital role in municipal solid waste management, and they take care of the garbage collection and transportation to the nearest transfer facility.[56] Other solid waste management workers include treatment plant workers and dumpsite workers. While the occupational hazards are mostly shared between the street sweepers and sewage workers, there is difference in the nature, medium and intensity of exposure. Sewage workers are more prone to toxic gases, infectious diseases like leptospirosis and cholera and carry risk for drowning and falls[7] Street sweepers are mostly exposed to ambient dust, vehicular emissions, infectious agents, ergonomics-related disorders, and injuries.[8910] One of the most common occupation-related illnesses prevalent among street sweepers is respiratory illness.[9] It is on a constant rise and has doubled in the last two decades in India.[81112] Musculoskeletal disorder (MSD) due to work is the second cause of disability in the world.[10] It is estimated that around one-third of the street sweepers suffer from at least one MSD.[10] Street sweepers are vulnerable to various infectious agents while sweeping and drain cleaning, including hepatic viruses. The global seroprevalence of hepatitis among street sweepers is estimated to be 20%.[13]

Street sweeping, traditionally considered a low-level occupation, even now remains oppressed and discriminated.[14] Due to their contract nature of their employment, they lack practical health benefits and usually do not have access to quality health care.[814] The contractual nature of the jobs provides them with low income; in addition to this, their educational status and social recognition increase their vulnerability to morbidity and mortality.[14] Given the intersection of vulnerabilities, street sweepers require special attention to achieve the highest degree of physical, mental, and social well-being.

The increase in occupation-related illness, especially respiratory illness, among street sweepers requires immediate action. In India, solid waste management falls under the purview of the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, and municipal solid waste management falls under the purview of the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs through Swachh Bharat Mission. And street sweepers are identified as sanitary workers and also municipal solid waste management workers, resulting in fragmentation of guidelines, manuals and legislations governing street sweepers.[51516] A comprehensive review of existing policies, and legal and implementation guidelines is required to understand the provision of occupational health protection offered to street sweepers, thereby identifying the overlooked aspects of occupational health and identifying the gaps in the implementation.

Despite the significance of street sweepers in the public health context, primary research within the Indian context remains scarce and geographically fragmented. The scope of the existing studies in India is limited as they fail to address the diverse spectrum of occupational hazards the sweepers face.[812] The scarcity of primary studies in India limits the ability to conduct a targeted review to develop an evidence-based framework to analyze the national occupational health policy, and relevant legal and implementation documents. To address this, a review of systematic reviews was done to build a comprehensive framework.[1718] They provide evidence-based interventions by synthesizing findings from multiple research studies.[1718] In the current study, the framework developed from a global systematic review allowed us to evaluate Indian national policies against international evidence, ensuring national gap analysis is grounded in global evidence. This will aid in bridging the gap between research and implementation and also strengthen the occupational health delivery systems.

METHODOLOGY

Content analysis was carried out to understand the policy landscape of occupational health in India, specifically with reference to street sweepers. The review included both policy and nonpolicy documents to understand the existing strategic approach toward identified health problems and implementation status, respectively. Review protocol

The document analysis was carried out by adopting a systematic framework approach known as the READ approach.[19] The four steps included in the READ approach are i) identification of relevant documents, ii) extraction of data, iii) analysis of data, and iv) distillation of findings.

The document search was conducted in three steps: the first step was a primary search across the identified government portal for policy, legislative, guidelines, and manual documents post-2000, following the first comprehensive manual for municipal solid waste management. The search was restricted to national-level policy documents to ensure standardized evaluation of the occupational health landscape of street sweepers. The documents section, publication section, manuals, and resources section were searched on the above-mentioned websites. The second step was the identification and inclusion of any foundational or guiding document mentioned in the primary documents before 2000. The third step was a database search in PubMed, Scopus and Embase databases using the search query constructed with the keywords “Occupational health,” “Sanitary workers,” “Street sweepers,” and Boolean operators “AND” and “OR.” The search was conducted on the 1st of April 2025; the search was restricted to include only systematic reviews related to street sweepers and other sanitary workers. The results were downloaded to Excel, and duplicate records were deleted. The eligibility criteria for inclusion were as follows: the study design should be a systematic review, the study setting should be global, the study population should be sanitation workers or street sweepers, the exposure should be occupational, and the outcome should be related to occupational health hazards like respiratory issues, musculoskeletal disorders, and occupational injuries. The literature search was done by a single reviewer to identify relevant reviews, the screening process was carried out by two independent researchers, and disagreements were resolved through mutual discussion. The quality of the included studies was appraised following the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical checklist for systematic reviews and research syntheses.[20] The data were extracted using the tool provided by JBI for conducting an umbrella review.[21] The documents identified through the first two steps were categorized under three categories.[19] The national policy on Safety, Health and Environment at the Workplace was considered an official document.[19] The nonpolicy documents, including laws and regulations, such as the Contract Labor Act and solid waste management rules, were categorized as legal documents.[19] The other nonpolicy documents, including training manuals, reports, operational plans, and project proposals such as Swachh Roads and Swachh Bharat, were categorized as implementation documents.[19] The data from the systematic reviews identified through the PubMed search were extracted to Microsoft Excel.

Data extraction and analysis

The data extraction and analysis were conducted through an iterative and multistage process. The thematic areas were identified within the National Policy on Safety, Health and Environment at the Workplace using a qualitative content analysis approach. To ensure comprehensive understanding, an iterative review of the policy content was done. The initial codes were generated through open inductive coding, where specific phrases and directives were labelled based on their relevance to occupational health protection. The codes were categorized based on their similarities, such as provisions, research and monitoring. Simultaneously, the occupational health recommendations extracted from the global systematic reviews were categorized based on their similarities like health education, facilitation and monitoring.

Analysis of legal and implementation documents

The identified legal and implementation documents were analyzed using a deductive content analysis method. The research team conducted a systematic screening of the documents to identify specific provisions for manual street sweepers within the framework developed from policy documents and a review of systematic reviews. Additional codes emerging apart from the framework were included through axial coding. The primary coding was conducted by one reviewer; subsequently, additional reviewers independently verified the coding to ensure reliability and consistency. The final thematic framework was modified to reflect the presence and absence of thematic areas across all documents, providing a clear visualization of the implementation gaps.

The study followed a hybrid methodology including an umbrella review technique and document analysis using the READ approach. The findings were reported using an adapted PRISMA-ScR checklist to compensate for the lack of a specialized reporting checklist is available for content analysis.[22] The adapted checklist is available in the supplementary files.

RESULTS

Overview of documents

The document search resulted in the identification of a key occupational health policy titled “The National Policy on Safety, Health and Environment at Workplace.” The search for related legal and implementation documents in the context of street sweepers resulted in the identification of four legal documents and nine implementation documents. The search for foundational or guidance documents referred to in the identified documents resulted in the identification of the Contract Labor Act (1970).[52324] The documents identified along with their classification are given in Figure 2, which shows that there are no dedicated policies or legislations for the welfare of street sweepers. Street sweepers are considered under both sanitary workers and solid waste management workers, which is evident from the implementation documents of Swachh Bharat, Swachh Roads and Municipal Solid Waste Management manuals.[51525]

Significant developments with respect to the implementation of street sweeper’s occupational health have been identified from the year 2016. The major developments include Contract Labor Act applicability, personal protective equipment (PPE) provision and statutory organization requirements. The NAMASTE (National Action for Mechanized Sanitation Ecosystem) guidelines was launched to eradicate the hazardous cleaning of sewers and septic tanks, but it does not include street sweepers who are assigned with additional responsibility of drain cleaning according to Swachh roads (2016).[425] The timeline of the documents included in the review, along with the significant developments, is provided in Figure 2.

The three major thematic areas are (1) Enforcement and compliance, (2) Standards and Awareness, and (3) Research, monitoring, and evaluation. The thematic areas were used as a reference framework to review whether the thematic areas identified from the policy are implemented for street sweepers.

The qualitative analysis of nonpolicy documents using the thematic framework revealed the implementation gaps. Research, monitoring, and evaluation were poorly represented in the nonpolicy documents with respect to the street sweepers. While delegation of administrative responsibility, institutionalization, skill enhancements, and training sessions have been given enough attention in the legal and implementation documents [Figure 3], whereas the medical criteria, appropriate standards, manuals and framework development of prevention and control are in formative stages with respect to street sweepers [Figure 3].

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to review the occupational health policy landscape relevant to street sweepers. The document search yielded one policy document, four legal documents and nine implementation documents. The policy document analysis resulted in three key thematic identifications, including Enforcement and Compliance, Standards and Awareness, and Research, Monitoring and Evaluation. The relevance of legal documents and the implementation document to street sweepers was assessed. It was found that there is no separate legislation that governs the occupational health of street sweepers. The standards for equipment used, manuals, and job rotation are still in preliminary stages. There are no fixed medical criteria set up for joining as street sweepers and continuing as street sweepers. The thematic area of research, monitoring, and evaluation was poorly represented.

Street sweepers lack separate legislation or a scheme for occupational health. The existing legislation and schemes focus more on sewage workers and treatment plant workers, as they are considered high-risk groups. This has led to the negligence of street sweepers in existing legislation and implementation documents. In India, approximately 40% of the urban sanitation workers are street sweepers, while only 15% of them are sewage workers.[26] The burden of disease will be greater when a large population is not given proper attention, even if they have a lower risk in comparison with a much smaller population. The hazardous exposure involved in street sweeping is different from that of sewage workers, with air pollution being the predominant exposure.[49] The majority of street sweepers belong to a lower socioeconomic status, a socially vulnerable group to respiratory infections.[8] Exposure to air pollution at work increases their susceptibility to respiratory-related infections. From the year 2016, the legal document applicable to the occupational health of street sweepers is the Contract Labor Act (CLA) 1971. Even though the act has undergone time-to-time amendments to address the relevant issues, it doesn’t suit for the street sweepers.[27] The present ambient air pollution indexes indicate the severe health risks; the CLA doesn’t cover the safety measures for air pollution. It is mandatory to establish separate legislation for street sweepers to address the heightened burden of diseases due to intersecting risk factors.

Appropriate standards for ergonomic equipment and job manuals for street sweepers are yet to be fully established. Skill enhancement of the street sweepers in handling equipment has received adequate recognition in the implementation documents. Street sweepers have low to no education, which indicates the importance of increasing their awareness towards occupational diseases.[814] Increasing their awareness would result in a positive change in attitude towards occupational hazards and protect them. While ergonomic training (15.38%) and job rotation (0%) have no proper mention in implementation documents, in comparison with preventive education (69.23%) and skill enhancement workshops (46.15%). The lack of job rotation and ergonomic training has resulted in an increase in the prevalence of musculoskeletal disorders as well as respiratory-related illnesses.[910] There are workflow recommendations to minimize the impact of occupational hazards among street sweepers in developed countries.[2829] Street sweeping involves repeated motion, constant bending and heavy weight lifting, all of which add to the strain on the musculoskeletal system.[1030] MSD’s not only cause chronic pain to the sweepers it also reduces their work efficiency and might well render them unable to work leading to livelihood loss.[31] Job rotation among street sweepers like shifting from street sweeping to garbage collection periodically will reduce the impact of dust exposure on respiratory system.[29] The national policy for street sweepers should explicitly adopt OSHA 29 1910.134 standards and categorize street sweepers as high-risk workers and provide them with air-purifying respirators with maximum efficiency of removing particulate to ensure their safety from respiratory issues.

There are no strict medical criteria set up to start work as a street sweeper and to continue to be one. Health screening for the existing sweepers is also not established properly. For workers exposed to dust considered as hazardous to health in factories and mining have prerequisite to ascertain medical fitness.[32] But the street sweepers are neglected considering their exposure is of lower risk. On the contrary there are studies which indicate sweepers have higher prevalence of respiratory related illness.[89] This mandates to set up medical criteria for joining the work. Regular health screening has benefits of early detection and intervention, lack of it clearly impact the chronic disease prevalence among the street sweepers.[9] The constant exposure to occupational hazard without regular screening leads to increased severity of disease undetected. The cost of treatment at the point of late detection is higher with poor prognosis affecting the livelihood of the individuals. Setting up medical criteria to join and health screening at regular intervals are therefore necessary to ensure their occupational health.

Despite consistent emphasis on strengthening the occupational health delivery there is a lacuna in implemetation of occupational health monitoring across the world.[9113334] The poor representation of monitoring and evaluation reflects ineffective and incomplete implementation, leading to occupation-related diseases among street sweepers.[935] It plays a critical role in evidence-based decision-making, providing the base for future course correction. Mere provision of protective equipment and PPE does not guarantee safety from occupational hazards. Regular monitoring and evaluation of the usage of the provided equipment and PPE will ensure safety.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study provides a comprehensive understanding of the policy landscape with respect to street sweepers supported by the framework developed using global evidence. The study was conducted with the publicly available online documents; internal circulars were not included in the analysis. While the study provides a critical view of national status, regional-level policies were not included, which might have added additional information. The data analysis is carried out by a research team without triangulation with stakeholder involvement leading to a certain degree of subjectivity.

CONCLUSION

To bridge the gap in occupational research, monitoring, and evaluation, studies on potential risks such as infectious diseases (e.g. tuberculosis) and the establishment of occupational disease registries are essential for identifying latent diseases. The impact of PPE use on street sweepers’ health and mitigation measures for ergonomic and respiratory-related illnesses should be examined to establish appropriate equipment standards. Similarly, assessing the feasibility of combining mechanization with manual sweeping might inform the development of improved operational manuals. In summary, street sweepers require separate legislation to govern their occupational health and safety to ensure clear standards for equipment, medical criteria, and regular monitoring.
