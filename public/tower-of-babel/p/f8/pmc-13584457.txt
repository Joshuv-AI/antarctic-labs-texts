Childbirth readiness and its influencing factors in the Yangtze River Delta region of China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ningying Zhou, Shanshan Hu, Fei Sun, Danni Wu, Min Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildbirth readiness is an important strategy in maternal and child health. Current research on the factors influencing childbirth readiness has focused primarily on resource-poor settings to reduce mortality rates. However, in resource-rich areas, emphasis is placed on the experience and quality of childbirth. Based on Meleis’ transition theory, this study examines the factors affecting childbirth readiness in China’s economically active Yangtze River Delta region from multiple perspectives.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted from March to May 2023, using convenience sampling to select 350 pregnant women in late pregnancy at a tertiary level A maternity hospital in Wuxi, China. Outcomes were assessed based on the General Information Questionnaire, along with the Childbirth Readiness Scale (CRS), eHealth Literacy Scale (eHEALS), Fear of Birth Scale (FOBS), and Family Adaptation, Partnership, Growth, Affection, and Resolve Index (Family APGAR Index) scores. An independent samples t-test or ANOVA was used for one-way analysis, and multiple linear regression was used for multifactorial analysis to determine the factors influencing childbirth readiness.ResultsThe statistical results showed that the total mean scores of the CRS, eHEALS, FOBS, and Family APGAR Index were 74.60 ± 8.97, 32.36 ± 6.18, 43.94 ± 24.42, and 8.41 ± 2.10, respectively. According to the multivariate linear regression analysis, abortion (p = 0.033); parity (p < 0.001); attending maternity school (p = 0.048); and eHEALS (p < 0.001), FOBS (p = 0.015), and Family APGAR Index (p = 0.046) scores were the main factors affecting childbirth readiness.ConclusionCurrent research indicates that pregnant women have moderate-to-high levels of childbirth readiness. Pregnant women with a history of abortion and multiparity and those who attended maternity schools have relatively high levels of childbirth readiness. Family care support and e-health literacy are positive factors, whereas fear of birth is a negative factor for childbirth readiness. Therefore, the government and healthcare institutions can provide personalized childbirth readiness services to pregnant women based on the individual characteristics mentioned above.

Background

Pregnancy and childbirth pose tremendous stress and challenges to women’s physical and mental health. According to the latest World Health Organization (WHO) data, inadequate childbirth readiness contributed to nearly one maternal death every 2 min in 2020 [1]. Thus, we are far from achieving the Sustainable Development Goal of reducing the global maternal mortality ratio to less than 70 deaths per 100,000 live births by 2030 [2]. In addition, the increasing number of women of advanced maternal age in both developed and developing countries puts them at an increased risk of high-risk pregnancies, placing additional demands on childbirth readiness. As early as 2005, the WHO identified childbirth readiness as a critical strategy for antenatal care to reduce maternal mortality and provide timely access to health resources and services related to childbirth [3].

Childbirth readiness refers to knowledge, care, availability of resources, and psychological preparation to facilitate a normal birth [4]. Studies have shown that a lack of awareness and knowledge related to childbirth readiness leads to inadequate preparation of pregnant women for delivery. Moreover, inadequate childbirth readiness may increase analgesic use and a preference for cesarean section surgery for delivery [5]. Conversely, adequate childbirth readiness can increase pregnant women’s confidence in giving birth and help them work better with healthcare professionals to reduce perineal injuries, improve their birth experience, increase pregnancy satisfaction, and improve birth outcomes [6]. In recent years, national and international scholars have shifted the focus of research toward childbirth readiness to avoid the adverse effects of inadequate childbirth readiness on breastfeeding, infant health, fertility intentions, and emotional experiences [7, 8].

Numerous studies have shown that demographic and obstetric factors are the main factors influencing childbirth readiness. The demographic factors include age, occupation, marital status, education, economic status, place of residence, and economic level, and the obstetric factors include history of stillbirth, number of parturitions, number of obstetric checkups, and planned pregnancy [9, 10]. Other influencing factors affecting childbirth readiness include knowledge acquisition of childbirth readiness, awareness of obstetric risk signs, attitude towards childbirth readiness, and quality of antenatal services [11]. Limenih et al. [12]reported that women who live in urban areas and have a higher level of education are more likely to be prepared for childbirth than those who live in rural areas and have a lower level of education owing to better access to information on childbirth readiness knowledge and better access to health care services. A study by Letose et al. [13]in Ethiopia showed that women who undergo early checkups are more likely to have higher levels of childbirth readiness than those of women who do not undergo early checkups, suggesting that health checkups enable women to better identify and mitigate risk factors during pregnancy, thereby encouraging them to prepare for childbirth. Childbirth is a complex process involving many aspects; therefore, the factors influencing it should be explored from a multidimensional perspective.

Foreign research on impact factors has focused mainly on resource-poor regions, whereas less research has been conducted in more economically active areas. Some scholars point out that childbirth is a transitional process and pregnant women may face challenges in terms of changes in their physical health, role relationships, expectations, and competencies, making them vulnerable to risks during this fragile time [14]. While fear of birth as a negative psychological emotion has been shown to decrease pregnant women’s confidence in childbirth and increase the risk of cesarean section; therefore, it may be detrimental to childbirth readiness [15]. Meanwhile, studies have demonstrated that the incidence of domestic violence, the husband’s income, and other related family functions can reflect a family’s ability to support pregnant women and have a significant impact on the degree of pregnant women’s preparation for childbirth [16].

Currently, with the increasing popularity of the Internet, eHealth literacy plays a crucial role in helping pregnant women prepare for childbirth. Studies have shown that eHealth literacy significantly enhances the preparation for childbirth by providing pregnant women with the tools and resources they need to make informed decisions and manage their health effectively. By effectively utilizing digital health resources, pregnant women can access valuable information, make informed decisions, and improve their overall birthing experience [17].

In the context of the global fertility decline, healthcare providers need to understand the factors influencing childbirth readiness to reduce mortality, improve maternal and child health, and improve pregnant women’s experience of childbirth. Despite the urgent need for attention to this issue, to the best of our knowledge, most studies have focused primarily on access to health facilities and demographic and obstetric characteristics of pregnant women, with limited research being conducted on other influences on childbirth readiness. Moreover, no studies have yet explored the effects of fear of birth, e-health literacy, or family function on childbirth readiness. Additionally, fewer studies have investigated the influencing factors of childbirth readiness in China. Therefore, in this study, we used developmental transition as the nature of transition; pregnant women’s demographic characteristics, pregnancy healthcare status, fear of birth, e-health literacy, and family function as the conditions of transition; and healthcare providers’ care and guidance as practical activities to explore the effects of these factors on the outcome indicator, childbirth readiness. This study provides a theoretical basis for developing intervention programs for healthcare providers.

Conceptual framework

The conceptual model used in this study was based on Meleis’s Transition Theory [18]. The theory explains that “transition” refers to the transition from one life stage, state, or condition to another, and is the process and result of complex interactions between people and their environment. Individuals in transition may face changes in physical health, role relationships, expectations, and abilities that make them vulnerable to risk during this vulnerable time [19]. Childbirth is a developmental transition like transition. To complete the transition phase, women need to be prepared for the transition, including demographic information, psychological status, health knowledge, and family support, to improve the quality of birth and birth outcomes. Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study included pregnant women attending the outpatient obstetrics clinic of a specialized maternity hospital in Wuxi, a central city of the Yangtze River Delta in eastern China. The resident population of Wuxi is more than 7 million, and the annual number of deliveries at the Affiliated Women’s Hospital of Jiangnan University exceeds 10,000. The hospital provides mothers and babies with comprehensive care and has advanced medical resources. This baby-friendly hospital has launched a series of care policies to promote women’s and children’s health. However, in terms of readiness for childbirth, it has failed to form a perfect care plan owing to the limited research on this aspect in China, thus necessitating exploration of the influencing factors and provision of personalized care services for different groups of people. The study was conducted at the Wuxi Maternal and Child Health Hospital from March 2023 to May 2023.

Participants

Convenience sampling was used to select participants. The inclusion criteria were as follows: ① willingness to provide informed consent and cooperate with the survey, ② age ≥ 18 years, and ③ late stages of pregnancy(28–42 w of gestation). The exclusion criteria were as follows: ① language dysfunction or serious mental illnesses and uncooperativeness with communication, ② serious pregnancy complications, and ③ indications for cesarean section.

Sample size

The sample size for the multiple regression analysis was approximately 5–10 times the number of independent variables [20]. The study included 17 independent variables, including age, monthly household income, literacy level, place of residence, etc. A loss to follow-up rate of 20% was considered (due to the refusal of some patients to fill out the questionnaire and invalid questionnaires). The required sample size was calculated to be between 107 and 213.

Measures

Data collection and quality assurance

The survey team members, who received uniform training, explained the purpose and significance of the survey to pregnant women who met the research conditions at the maternity clinic. After obtaining their consent, the questionnaire was distributed one-on-one to ensure the pregnant women completed it independently without outside interference. The survey team members collected the questionnaires after the pregnant women had finished filling them out. In consideration of the quality of the questionnaire filled and the degree of cooperation of the participants, 360 questionnaires were distributed. After excluding incomplete and illogical questionnaires, 350 valid questionnaires were obtained, with a 97.2% effective recovery rate.

Demographic variables

Based on the existing literature, discussions were held with participants to gather information on age, monthly household income, literacy level, place of residence, history of miscarriage, participation in maternity school, daily exercise time for pregnant women, and number of deliveries.

Childbirth readiness scale (CRS)

The Chinese version of CRS was developed by Yuan Mengmei [4]in 2022 and is mainly used to measure pregnant women’s readiness for childbirth, including four dimensions: self-management, information literacy, birth confidence, and birth plan, with a total of 18 entries. The scale is based on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree,” with scores from 1 to 5 and a total of 18 to 90. A higher score indicates better preparation of the pregnant woman for labor and delivery. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale is 0.94, and the half-reliability is 0.88. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.914.

The eHealth literacy scale(eHEALS)

The eHEALS was developed by Norman et al. [21]in 2006 and translated into Chinese with cultural adaptation [22]. This scale mainly measures an individual’s ability to find and evaluate health-related information online. The scale consists of three dimensions—application ability, judgment ability, and decision-making ability—with eight items. The scale is based on a 5-point Likert scale, with options ranging from “very inconsistent” to “very consistent,” with scores from 8 to 40. A higher score indicates higher e-health literacy of the individual. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of this scale is 0.88, and the scale is short and easy to complete. It has been widely used by researchers from many countries in different populations. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.937.

Fear of birth scale (FOBS)

The FOBS, developed by Haines et al. [23]in 2011, is based on the single-item visual analog scale (VAS) used by Rouhe et al. [24]to measure fear of childbirth. It consists of two 100 mm VASs, one representing “calm” to “worried” and the other representing “no fear” to “intense fear.” Both scales range from 0 to 100, and the fear of childbirth score is the average of the two scores, varying from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating a higher level of fear of childbirth. A FOBS score of ≥ 50 is used to classify a respondent as fearful of childbirth, whereas a score of < 50 indicates a lack of fear of childbirth. The Cronbach’s alpha for this scale is 0.84 and that in this study was 0.889.

Family adaptation/partnership/growth/affection/resolve index (family APGAR index)

The Family APGAR Index was developed by Smilkstein in 1978 to assess respondents’ satisfaction with family functioning [25]. The scale consists of five items, including adaptability, cooperation, adulthood, affection, and intimacy. The options ranged from “often” to “almost rarely” on a scale of 2 to 0, with a total score ranging from 0 to 10. A higher score indicates better family care. A total score of 0 to 3 indicates severe family dysfunction, 4 to 6 indicates moderate family dysfunction and 7 to 10 indicates good family functioning and family support. The reliability correlation coefficient of the scale is 0.80 to 0. 83. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale in this study was 0.855.

Data analysis

The scale data were manipulated using Excel, with entry and verification performed by two examiners. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22.0. In this study, mean ± standard deviation (SD) was used for quantitative data during descriptive statistics, and frequency counts, constituting ratios, were used for qualitative data. In the CRS factor analysis, an independent samples t-test was used for dichotomous variables, and one-way ANOVA was used for multicategory variables. Correlation analysis used Pearson’s correlation coefficient to analyze the relationships among the CRS, eHEALS, FOBS, and Family APGAR Index. Stepwise multiple linear regression analyses were used, with statistically significant factors in one-way ANOVAs(P < 0.05)used as independent variables and CRS scores as dependent variables [26]. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered a statistically significant difference.

Results

Participants’ demographic and obstetric characteristics

The survey finally identified 350 pregnant women to participate in the study; the age distribution ranged from 18 to 41 years, with a mean age of 30.06 ± 3.87 years, and 80% (n = 280) of the pregnant women were aged between 25 and 34 years. Most pregnant women (n = 168, 48%) had a monthly household income of > 12,000 ¥. A larger proportion of pregnant women had attained education levels equivalent to junior colleges or undergraduate degrees (n = 247, 70.60%). Approximately 65.43% (n = 229) of the pregnant women resided in urban areas and approximately 57.40% (n = 201) of the pregnant women had experienced an abortion. A total of 67.40% (n = 236) of the pregnant women attended maternity school. The daily exercise duration was primarily between 30 min and 1 h for 40.00% of the pregnant women. The majority of the pregnant women (67.10%; n = 235) were primigravid. The results of the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) showed that the differences in maternal abortion, participation in maternity school, duration of daily exercise during pregnancy, and number of parities significantly affected the total CRS score (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Factors associated with childbirth readiness

The demographic and obstetric characteristic variables that exhibited significance in the univariate analysis, along with the total eHEALS, Family APGAR Index, and FOBS scores that were significantly associated with the total CRS score in the correlation analysis were used as independent variables, and the total CRS score was used as the dependent variable in the multiple linear regression equation. Assessment for multicollinearity yielded a variance inflation factor of 1.015 to 1.250 and a Durbin–Watson value of 1.889, indicating the absence of multicollinearity. The residuals were verified to conform to a normal distribution. The results showed that abortion; number of parities; attending maternity school; and the eHEALS, FOBS, and Family APGAR Index scores were the main factors influencing the CRS score (adjusted R-square = 0.364, F = 29.509, p < 0.001). Discussion

In the present study, the total CRS scores ranged from moderate to high levels. The total CRS score for 350 pregnant women was 74.60 ± 8.97, with an item mean score of 4.14 ± 0.50, consistent with the results of a multicenter study from seven provinces reported by domestic scholars in 2023 [8]. This similarity may be attributed to the continuous improvements in the Chinese government’s medical and healthcare systems. Community medicine and Internet hospitals have addressed the issues of women’s access to medical services [27]. The localization of compulsory education over 9 years has resulted in better-educated women who are more adept at choosing and utilizing health resources to improve their childbirth readiness, aligning with the findings of other studies [12].

Among the dimensions assessed in this study, the birth confidence dimension had the lowest score, which may be related to the lack of birth experience among primigravid women and unfavorable pregnancy and birth history or unpleasant birth experiences among multigravida women [28, 29]. Therefore, from the perspective of pregnant women, healthcare workers can help in disseminating pregnancy-related knowledge to pregnant women, fostering their understanding that childbirth is a natural physiological process, relieving their nervousness and anxiety, and enhancing their self-efficacy in childbirth to boost their confidence. Externally, healthcare workers can enhance the support for pregnant women by strengthening partner education, conducting focused group antenatal care education, and increasing the confidence of pregnant women through interaction and communication to improve their childbirth readiness.

Our study shows that parity influences maternal childbirth readiness. Multiparous children had higher birth preparedness scores than nulliparous children, a finding consistent with studies by Kenyan scholars [30]. However, this is not consistent with the findings of a Thai study, which may be related to the differences in the characteristics of the study population. In the Thai [31]study, the maximum age of the participants was only 19 years, and the proportion of primiparous women was only 7.5%, which was not conducive to the acquisition of knowledge related to childbirth readiness and constrained the ability to deal with childbirth-related problems due to the limited education and life experience among the pregnant women. With the adjustment of China’s fertility policy, the age at which women give birth has been rising annually owing to a variety of socioeconomic factors. This suggests that healthcare providers should pay more attention to the readiness of nulliparous women and develop health education programs to enable them to understand the process of childbirth and identify pregnancy-related risk factors.

This study showed that pregnant women with a history of abortion were more likely to prepare for childbirth, possibly because of their higher expectations for their newborns and motivation by the Health Belief Model theory [32] to modify their poor lifestyles, avoid risks, acquire healthcare knowledge, and increase their readiness to deliver to maintain the health of their fetuses. In addition, pregnant women who have undergone abortions will have more experience with childbirth, which, to a certain extent, eliminates their fear of pregnancy and childbirth. Based on this, prenatal care providers should guide pregnant women with no experience of pregnancy to adapt to different stages of pregnancy and alleviate their uncertainties about parenting promptly.

Pregnant women who attended maternity schools were more prepared for childbirth, which is consistent with the results of Iranian scholars [33]. Maternity education programs play a significant role in maintaining the health of mothers and babies by relieving psychological stress, regulating the emotions of pregnant women, increasing the rate of spontaneous deliveries, and reducing fear and pain during childbirth [34]. Only one-third of the pregnant women in this survey had participated in maternity schools. Therefore, health policymakers should strengthen the importance of maternity schools in the future. Besides traditional lecture courses, education can be conducted online, adopting a model of combined online and offline education.

This study identified e-health literacy as the strongest modifiable correlate of childbirth readiness in this cohort. The CRS showed a moderate positive correlation with the eHEALS (r = 0.498, p < 0.01), and eHEALS remained the strongest independent predictor in the multivariable model (B = 0.646, β = 0.445, p < 0.001). This finding is particularly noteworthy because the sample was drawn from a tertiary maternity hospital in the Yangtze River Delta, a relatively high-resource setting where internet access and maternal services are widely available [35]. In this context, differences in childbirth readiness appear to depend less on simple access to information and more on women’s ability to search for, appraise, and apply online health information effectively [36]. Accordingly, interventions in economically developed regions should move beyond merely expanding information channels and instead strengthen digital appraisal and decision-making skills so that pregnant women can translate available online resources into practical childbirth preparation.

This study revealed that women with high Family APGAR Index scores were more likely to have higher childbirth readiness. In China, influenced by traditional Confucianism, family values play a significant role throughout people’s lives, from childbirth to old age, and individuals pay close attention to their feelings for their families [37]. Family support affects the mental health, emotional state, and well-being of pregnant women. Partners, as vital members of the family, play an essential role. Several studies have shown that the economic status of the partner, recognition of danger signs during pregnancy, attendance at childbirth classes, and the couple’s relationship are all important factors that influence childbirth readiness [16, 38]. However, in most developing countries, childbirth is considered a woman’s affair, and men are excluded from childbirth classes or programs because of the obstacles they face in participating in childbirth readiness [39]. Additionally, strained relationships between mothers-in-law and daughters-in-law can lead to negative maternal emotions, adversely affecting childbirth readiness. Therefore, it is imperative to educate family members about the importance of family functioning in childbirth readiness. Families should be encouraged to provide both material and emotional support to pregnant women, helping them build resilience to face various challenges.

Moreover, studies have shown that a higher fear of childbirth is associated with a lower level of childbirth readiness. Fear of childbirth is mainly triggered by pregnant women’s concern for the health of the fetus, fear of labor pain, and uncertainty surrounding the pregnancy process [40]. Higher levels of fear of childbirth in pregnant women affect the choice of mode of delivery, experience of labor, health of the fetus, and desire to give birth [41]. Turkish scholars reported that a partner’s fear of childbirth is strongly linked to a pregnant woman’s fear of childbirth [42]. Additionally, studies have found that proper use of Internet resources can help pregnant women effectively reduce their fear [43]. Therefore, healthcare providers should focus on promoting e-health literacy and active childbirth readiness courses for pregnant women and their partners. Timely counseling can increase their confidence in childbirth, reduce their fear of the unknown, and promote childbirth readiness.

Fear of birth also made an independent negative contribution to childbirth readiness. Although the mean FOBS score in this sample did not indicate universally high fear, FOBS was negatively correlated with CRS (r = -0.345, p < 0.01) and remained significant after adjustment in the regression model (B = -0.043, β = -0.117, p = 0.015). This suggests that, even in a high-resource region with comparatively good access to obstetric care, psychological concerns still undermine women’s confidence and practical preparation for childbirth. In the Yangtze River Delta context, where structural barriers to care may be lower than in under-resourced settings, fear of birth may represent a more specific and clinically actionable barrier to readiness. Therefore, targeted antenatal counseling, routine fear screening, and confidence-building education may be especially important for women who appear materially well supported but remain psychologically underprepared for childbirth.

Strengths and limitations

In this cross-sectional study conducted at a tertiary hospital in Wuxi, China, we explored various aspects of the factors influencing childbirth readiness to help fill in the existing knowledge gaps, thus providing a reference point for improving pregnant women’s childbirth readiness. Although the confidentiality of questionnaire information was assured, some pregnant women may have withheld delivery-related information due to its sensitive nature. This study was conducted at only one center, and the results do not fully represent all regions and centers in China. The region in which the study was conducted is one of the more economically active areas in China, with high levels of education, income, healthcare resource allocation, and access to services. Although income and education levels were analyzed in this study to reduce bias, no differences could be identified. In the future, we anticipate expanding the sample size to include multi-center and multi-territory studies.

Conclusions

This study showed that the childbirth readiness of pregnant women ranges from moderate to high levels and requires improvement. A history of abortion, parity, and participation in maternity schools are factors influencing childbirth readiness. Fear of birth can have a negative impact on the childbirth readiness of pregnant women. Family care and e-health literacy can significantly improve childbirth readiness. Therefore, health policymakers and health plan implementers should encourage families to participate in childbirth readiness, monitor the psychological health of pregnant women promptly, and provide personalized childbirth readiness services based on the different characteristics of pregnant women to guarantee maternal and child health. Additionally, health departments should focus on the electronic health literacy of pregnant women and improve their ability to identify high-quality health information. Women can benefit from the establishment of an internet platform aimed at disseminating pregnancy-related information and pregnancy knowledge.
