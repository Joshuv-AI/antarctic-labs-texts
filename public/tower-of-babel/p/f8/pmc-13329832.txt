Identifying Educational and Resource Needs of General Internal Medicine Physicians in Sickle Cell Disease Management
Authors: Anas Samman, Victoria David, Roy Khalife, Dawn Goodyear, Natalia Rydz, Kelsey Uminski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionSickle cell disease involves acute and chronic complications requiring timely recognition and multidisciplinary care. General internal medicine (GIM) physicians often manage affected adults with limited exposure and few supports.MethodsWe conducted a cross‐sectional web‐based survey of GIM physicians assessing comfort and preferred supports.ResultsEighteen of approximately 85 physicians responded (21%). Common encounters included chronic pain (83%), vaso‐occlusive crises (67%), and acute chest syndrome (44%), yet none reported being comfortable managing complications. Discomfort was greatest for rare complications, and peripartum and perioperative care. Limited exposure was the main driver. Preferred supports included protocols, guides, and decision tools.ConclusionTargeted tools and specialist support are urgently needed to improve care and physician comfort.Trial RegistrationThe authors have confirmed clinical trial registration is not needed for this submission.

Individuals with sickle cell disease (SCD) experience multiple complications requiring frequent healthcare interactions, with initial care often provided by generalist physicians who may have limited exposure to this complex condition. Previous studies have identified significant areas of discomfort in SCD care amongst generalists, particularly in pain management, disease‐specific medications, and recognizing complications [1, 2, 3, 4]. These areas of discomfort may contribute to delayed or suboptimal care, increasing the risk of severe complications in patients with SCD [5]. As healthcare systems increasingly rely on generalist physicians to provide early evaluation and management for patients with rare diseases such as SCD, understanding clinician experience is essential for designing effective supports.

In Canada, the population of patients with SCD continues to grow, due to immigration from regions where the disease is endemic, including sub‐Saharan Africa, the Caribbean, the Middle East, and the Indian subcontinent [6, 7]. A Northern Alberta study demonstrated a 3.5‐fold increase in pediatric hemoglobinopathy patients over a 10‐year period, reflecting trends observed across other non‐endemic areas [7]. Consequently, GIM physicians practicing in centers with historically limited exposure to SCD are increasingly required to make early diagnostic and management decisions for patients with SCD, including in high‐risk and time‐sensitive presentations such as perioperative or peripartum care. Despite this evolving clinical landscape, many non‐specialist physicians receive limited formal training in SCD management. These clinical contexts highlight the intersection between generalist practice, rare disease care, and the need for accessible system‐level decision‐support tools to guide management.

We conducted a needs assessment among staff GIM physicians in Calgary, Canada, to better understand areas of discomfort in SCD care, and preferred supports. Findings were intended to inform local intervention development and identify broadly applicable patterns. In Calgary, care for SCD is centralized within the Southern Alberta Rare Blood and Bleeding Disorders Comprehensive Care Program, which provides longitudinal care to approximately 230–250 adults with SCD, and supports 10–15 SCD‐related hospital admissions per month. Despite this centralized care model, inpatient care for patients with SCD is frequently delivered by GIM physicians. There are four adult hospitals in Calgary where patients with SCD complications may be admitted. Patients with SCD presenting to acute care are initially assessed by Emergency Department physicians, with GIM and Hematology consultation obtained as clinically indicated. Eligible participants were staff physicians practicing within a GIM service, rather than a subspecialty service, at one of these four major adult hospitals.

A web‐based survey was developed using QualtricsXM, and refined with input from general internal medicine physicians and hematologists with experience caring for adults with SCD. Survey content was informed by clinical presentations encountered in practice and areas identified by the study team as relevant to inpatient SCD care. The survey included Likert‐scale, multiple‐choice, and open‐ended questions across four domains: Demographics; comfort managing SCD complications; preferences for educational and decision‐support tools; and institutional supports. The survey (Supporting Information 1) was distributed via departmental email and remained open for six weeks (May–June 2025), with a small participation incentive.

Responses were summarized using descriptive statistics. Free‐text responses were analyzed inductively by two reviewers. Codes and themes were developed collaboratively, and differences in coding or theme assignment were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. The project was deemed exempt from formal ethics review by the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board as a quality improvement initiative.

Twenty‐two GIM physicians responded to the survey, from approximately 85 eligible. Four respondents did not provide any responses and were excluded from analysis, resulting in 18 participants in the final dataset (effective response rate 21%). Among included respondents, completion of individual survey items varied, and not all participants answered every question. In addition, some survey items permitted multiple responses. As a result, denominators differ across analyses depending on the number of responses to each item. Half of the participants (n = 9) had been in practice for 11 years or more, 39% (n = 7) for 5–10 years, and 11% (n = 2) for less than five. Most participants practiced at Foothills Medical Centre (55.6%, n = 10), others at South Health Campus (22.2%, n = 4), Rockyview General Hospital (16.7%, n = 3), and Peter Lougheed Centre (16.7%, n = 3). Over one‐third (38.9%, n = 7) reported not providing care for any patients with SCD in the preceding 12 months, while 55.6% (n = 10) reported caring for five or fewer. One participant reported caring for more than 15 patients with SCD.

Participants reported encountering a broad range of SCD‐related complications. Most commonly encountered were exacerbations of chronic pain (83.3%, n = 15), acute pain crises (66.7%, n = 12), and acute chest syndrome (44.4%, n = 8). Less frequently encountered were splenic sequestration (27.8%, n = 5), stroke (16.7%, n = 3), hyper‐hemolysis (n = 2), avascular necrosis (n = 2), and pulmonary hypertension (n = 1).

The distribution of comfort levels by specific SCD complication is shown in Figure 1A. Participants predominantly reported low comfort in managing SCD complications. Only one participant reported being “extremely comfortable” with any complication, which was stroke. Less commonly encountered SCD complications were associated with a higher proportion of “somewhat uncomfortable” and “extremely uncomfortable” responses. Aplastic crisis elicited the greatest proportion of “extremely uncomfortable” responses, while hepatic sequestration had the highest proportion of “somewhat uncomfortable” responses. A similar pattern was observed for the selected clinical presentations (Figure 1B). No participants reported being “extremely comfortable” managing any of the clinical presentations assessed (red blood cell transfusion, perioperative management, or peripartum management), and few reported being “somewhat comfortable” or neutral. Notably, peripartum management was associated with the highest proportion of “extremely uncomfortable” responses (47.1%, n = 8). We did not identify a clear association between reported comfort levels and years in practice, number of patients with SCD seen, or practice location.

Most participants preferred access to clinical guidelines and protocols (64.7%, n = 11) and quick‐reference materials such as pocket cards, PDFs, or infographics (58.8%, n = 10). Medical grand rounds (47.1%, n = 8) and e‐learning modules or online courses (41.2%, n = 7) were also commonly selected, while workshops were selected less frequently (23.5%, n = 4). Free‐text responses reflected variability in preferences, including “real shadowing, in‐depth training,” as well as comments expressing limited enthusiasm for additional formal educational activities. Regarding clinical tools that would increase confidence in SCD care, the most frequently selected was pre‐formulated pain management protocols (70.6%, n = 12). Other commonly selected tools included quick‐reference guidelines, standardized order sets for common SCD presentations, clinical pathways or decision‐support tools, and electronic medical record alerts or embedded care pathways (each selected by 58.8%, n = 10). Despite interest in structured tools, two participants expressed concern that order sets may discourage clinical reasoning when the rationale for recommendations or decision thresholds is not explicit.

Analysis of free‐text responses identified several recurring themes. Limited clinical exposure was frequently cited, with one participant stating that SCD presentations were “not frequent enough to have pathways close at hand.” Clinical uncertainty was also prominent, particularly around distinguishing acute pain crises from chronic pain exacerbations and in “correctly identifying acute pain versus vaso‐occlusive pain versus chest pain syndromes.” Navigating acute pain episodes in the context of chronic baseline pain was also cited. When asked about institutional resources that would increase confidence in SCD care, participants emphasized the need for clear clinical pathways and embedded decision support tools such as “clinical decision pathways based on guidelines” and “protocols or pathways in Connect Care [local electronic medical record].” Hematology support was consistently identified as critical, with participants highlighting the value of automatic hematology consultation, guidance for admitted patients, and, where feasible, direct admission to hematology services. Similarly, when asked how hematology services and the Southern Alberta Rare Blood and Bleeding Disorders Program could support GIM physicians, participants emphasized automatic hematology involvement to minimize delays in treatment, accessible on‐call and inpatient consultation, and guidance to support appropriate initial assessment and management.

This study provides novel Canadian data describing the comfort levels and perceived support needs of GIM physicians caring for adults with SCD. Limited clinical exposure emerged as a key contributor to this discomfort reflecting similar international studies documenting gaps in confidence and preparedness among generalists caring for patients with SCD [1, 4, 8]. Similar deficiencies in knowledge, confidence, and comfort related to SCD care have also been identified earlier in medical training, including among general internal medicine trainees and medical students [9, 10]. Prior studies have demonstrated that trainees frequently report limited exposure to SCD, discomfort managing acute complications, and a desire for additional disease‐specific educational supports and clinical teaching opportunities [9, 10]. These findings suggest that gaps in preparedness may begin early in training and persist into independent clinical practice, particularly in healthcare systems where exposure to SCD is limited. Our data extend prior work by providing a complication‐specific assessment of physician comfort, examining clinical presentations encountered by GIM physicians, and quantifying the degree of discomfort across these presentations. Participants reported low confidence in more common SCD presentations such as vaso‐occlusive pain crises, despite this being the most frequent reason for healthcare utilization among individuals with SCD. This finding suggests that limited exposure alone may not fully explain physician discomfort and highlights the potential value of clear clinical pathways and decision‐support tools to guide management in time‐sensitive presentations.

Despite practicing in a tertiary care environment where patients with SCD are encountered, respondents reported substantial discomfort across multiple clinical presentations. This highlights the need for educational resources, clinical pathways, and decision‐support tools that support clinicians regardless of the frequency with which they encounter patients with SCD. Other factors likely contribute to physician discomfort, including the complexity and heterogeneity of SCD presentations, uncertainty surrounding management of acute versus chronic pain, and limited exposure to SCD during medical training and clinical practice. Uncertainty regarding when to involve hematology specialists may further contribute to discomfort and highlights the importance of clear clinical pathways and encouragement of early collaboration with hematology teams when clinicians encounter unfamiliar or high‐risk presentations. Notably, peripartum and perioperative management were associated with the greatest proportion of “extremely uncomfortable” responses, highlighting a critical gap in high‐risk clinical contexts where GIM physicians may reasonably be expected to provide care but may have limited prior experience managing the disease‐specific considerations and complications that can arise in patients with SCD during these periods.

Several limitations should be considered. This single‐center study reflects the perspectives of GIM physicians affiliated with the University of Calgary and may not be generalizable to other settings. Community‐based physicians were not included and may have different exposure or access to specialist support. Findings are based on self‐reported comfort rather than objective measures, although perceived comfort remains an important determinant of clinical decision‐making in complex conditions. Voluntary participation may introduce selection bias. However, consistency with prior international studies suggests these challenges are likely more broadly applicable.

This survey was undertaken as a needs assessment to inform the development of future educational and quality improvement initiatives aimed at supporting GIM physicians caring for patients with SCD. As SCD becomes increasingly prevalent in regions with historically limited exposure, healthcare systems must consider how to equip generalists to deliver timely, high‐quality care. Recent qualitative and systems‐level literature examining inpatient SCD care has similarly identified challenges related to delayed pain management, limited provider familiarity with SCD, fragmented communication between inpatient and outpatient teams, and inadequate access to individualized care plans and decision‐support tools [11]. These studies have emphasized the importance of provider education, embedded clinical pathways, individualized care plans, and improved communication with SCD specialists to support timely and patient‐centered care [11]. Our findings suggest that clinicians may prefer practical, point‐of‐care supports rather than traditional educational interventions alone. A multifaceted approach is likely required, combining targeted educational resources, clinical decision‐support tools, and strengthened collaboration with hematology services. Embedding clinical pathways, standardized protocols, or decision‐support tools within electronic medical records may represent one strategy to support early management while facilitating timely specialist involvement. These approaches are aligned with broader international efforts to standardize and improve acute SCD care, including the development of evidence‐based guidelines and structured care pathways for vaso‐occlusive crises and other acute SCD complications [12, 13, 14, 15]. Future work should focus on co‐designing and evaluating these interventions with both hematology specialists and front‐line providers to ensure they are feasible, accessible, and responsive to local practice needs. Additional study is also needed to determine whether these interventions improve clinician comfort, reduce delays in care, and enhance patient experience and outcomes.
