Research on dynamic stiffness match form of indirect fastener based on rail high-frequency wear characteristics
Authors: Xian Wang, Kai Wei, Qianhua Pu, Zelong Liu, Guodong Li, Ping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The stiffness combination of the dual-layer pads in indirect fastener systems significantly influences high-frequency wheel-rail wear behavior. However, the actual stiffness exhibits strong nonlinearity and non-uniformity due to the deformation of the steel plate and the frequency-dependent stiffness of the elastic pads, making it challenging to clarify the underlying mechanisms. To address this, this study develops a dynamic model for indirect fasteners by modeling the ironbase using an Euler beam and representing the frequency/load-dependent stiffness of the elastic pads using a Multi-Point Supported Fractional Derivative Poynting-Thomson (MS-FDPT) model. The study investigates the effects of pad stiffness and damping match form on rail wear. The results indicate that, considering the contact filtering effect, rail wear in the 0–1250 Hz range is primarily concentrated in four major resonance bands corresponding to P2 form, second- and third-order rail bending, and pinned-pinned resonance modes. Neither single-layer support models nor fastener stiffness models that only account for the mass participation of the ironbase can accurately reflect the actual in-service behavior of indirect fasteners, with a maximum simulation error of 136 Hz in the dominant rail acceleration frequency. Without altering the nodal stiffness of the fasteners, adopting a “hard-upper, soft-lower” stiffness match form effectively reduces resonance frequencies other than the P2 form and mitigates wear at these resonance frequencies. The damping characteristics of the fastener system are primarily determined by the lower-stiffness pad. The best suppression effect on high-frequency wheel-rail wear is achieved by increasing the damping of both pads simultaneously, whereas increasing the damping of only the high-stiffness pad may exacerbate high-frequency wear.

Introduction

Railway fasteners, critical components in railway infrastructure, significantly influence both track deformation under dynamic train loads and high-frequency wheel-rail vibrations. Deficient stiffness design may induce rail corrugation1–3 and wheel out-of-roundness(OOR)4,5, particularly in ballastless track systems predominant in high-speed railways and urban metro networks. The Chinese metro design code mandates indirect fasteners in ballastless tracks to maintain optimal track elasticity6. Unlike conventional ballasted tracks employing single-layer pad systems, indirect fasteners utilize a dual-layer elastic pad (rail pad and base pad) vertically integrated with the ironbase.

The dual-layer elastic pad (rail pad and base pad) and the ironbase are vertically combined in the indirect fasteners system, and the structural form of separate anchoring of the rail and the ironbase makes it have better elasticity and lower dynamic buckle pressure loss.

whereas, the stiffness design of fasteners incorporating a daul-layer elastic pad configuration introduces specific challenges. Grassie7 highlighted that in fastener systems utilizing daul-layer elastic pads, the interaction between the rail pad, ironbase, and basepad can trigger rail corrugation akin to the “booted sleeper corrugation” effect. Ma8 and Ye9 studied a direct fastener system with a similar dual -layer elastic pad structure. They used a rigid body model to simulate the ironbase’s motion in the WJ-8 fastener system and found that neglecting its vibrational participation led to a prediction error exceeding 60 Hz in higher-order polygonal wear analysis. Tao10 investigated how variations in rail pad and base pad stiffness, along with the ironbase’s mass characteristics, affect the vibration behavior of the wheel-rail system. Their study revealed that these factors influence the system’s dominant vibration frequency and amplitude, thereby altering the polygonal wear order and growth rate of high-speed railway wheels. These findings indicate that the dynamic stiffness match form of the dual-layer elastic pad, along with the ironbase’s vibrational participation, significantly affects high-frequency wheel-rail interactions and wear characteristics under real train loading conditions, potentially inducing rail corrugation and other high-frequency track defects.

However, exploring the impact of the pad stiffness match form in indirect fasteners on high-frequency wheel-rail wear remains a challenge. First, in an indirect fastener system, the spring clip and anchor bolts are installed separately. The ironbase is fixed to the tie by the anchor bolts, and the pre-tightening force of the bolts is applied entirely to the anchor ends on both sides of the ironbase. This causes the ironbase to bend during installation11. When the base pad exhibits elasticity, it leads to uneven compression. This differs from the installation system used in direct fasteners like the WJ-8, where the spring clip and ironbase are jointly anchored, with the ironbase only subjected to the compressive load of the spring clip. Additionally, considering the load/frequency-dependent nonlinear stiffness characteristics of the elastic pad material12,13, the base pad experiences significant stiffness variation at different locations under uneven compression, affecting the overall combined stiffness. This differs from the stiffness combination system used in previous studies with direct fasteners incorporating the ironbase. Although literature14 established a model for calculating the stiffness combination of indirect fasteners considering the bending deformation of the ironbase, the base pad model based on static load-dependent nonlinearity cannot account for the dynamic frequency-dependent nonlinear stiffness behavior of the elastic pad under dynamic loads. However, these characteristics have been widely proven to be key factors influencing the high-frequency vibration behavior of the wheel-rail system15. Failing to consider the frequency-dependent stiffness characteristics of the fastener system will significantly underestimate the high-frequency response of the wheel-rail system16,17. This will impact the formation and development of wheel-rail wear18, high-frequency noise19, and other track-related defects.

In light of this, this paper combines an Euler beam model that considers structural bending deformation20 with a fractional derivative frequency-dependent stiffness model21 to propose a dynamic simulation model for indirect fasteners, incorporating the real deformation characteristics of the ironbase and the frequency-dependent stiffness variations of the elastic pad. It investigates the impact of the stiffness match form of indirect fasteners on the high-frequency interaction characteristics and wear behavior of the wheel-rail system. Using a metro project as a case study, the frequency-dependent stiffness characteristics of the rail pad and base pad in the DZ III type fastener system are first tested. Next, the dynamic model of indirect fasteners proposed in this paper is introduced and integrated into the vehicle-track coupled dynamics model. Finally, based on field-measured track vibrations, the dynamic response of the wheel-rail system and high-frequency wear behavior of the rail are explored under the dynamic stiffness and damping match form of the rail and base pads in the fastener system. The study proposes stiffness design recommendations suitable for the operating conditions of indirect fasteners, aiming to provide theoretical guidance for the dynamic parameter design of indirect fasteners.

Test and characterization of elastic pad

This paper analyzes the characteristics of the load/frequency-dependent stiffness of the DZ III type indirect fastener used in subways. The Fractional Derivative Poynting–Thomson (FDPT) model and its characterization method are introduced first. Then, a testing device developed based on the ISO 10846-2 is used to test the frequency-variable stiffness and damping of the elastic pad.

This study focuses on the DZ III type indirect fastener used in subways, with the rail pad and base pad as test subjects. Equipment and method

Currently, China’s testing standards for elastic pads22,23 primarily focus on static stiffness testing and low-frequency secant stiffness testing within the 3–5 Hz range. These methods are insufficient for characterizing the FDPT model, which requires stiffness testing under varying frequencies and preloads. This study adopts the characterization process of the FDPT model proposed in21. Static testing and dynamic tangent stiffness measurements under different preloads are used to obtain the load-dependent static stiffness and frequency-dependent stiffness characteristics of the elastic pad. Then, a set of polynomials and a FDZ model are employed to characterize the static load-dependent stiffness and frequency-dependent stiffness under varying preloads. Finally, by combining K∞ under different preloads with the static load-dependent stiffness using Eq. (4), the load/frequency-dependent stiffness characteristics of the elastic pad are obtained.

In the model, static stiffness testing is conducted using a universal testing machine, while dynamic stiffness testing is performed using the equipment shown in Fig. 2. This equipment supports preload levels ranging from 0 kN to 80 kN, with a maximum dynamic excitation force of 20 kN and a frequency limit of 200 Hz. The system achieves a sampling rate of 5 kHz for load and displacement measurements, with a displacement precision of 2 μm.

In terms of testing methodology, the static load-displacement curve of the pad follows the Chinese standard for elastic pad testing in rail transit22, with the load range extended to 0 kN–80 kN. The dynamic stiffness test follows the European railway fastener dynamic stiffness testing standards24,25. Tests are conducted under three different preload conditions: 20 kN, 40 kN, and 60 kN. The tangent stiffness is measured at a fixed preload using a loading velocity of 90 dBV, and the obtained dynamic stiffness and loss factor are calculated using Eqs. (5) and (6). Results and analysis

The characterization results of the rail pad and base pad are shown in Fig. 3. This figure presents the static load-displacement curves (from which stiffness-displacement curves can be derived via differentiation) and the loss factor under different preloads. It can be observed that both the rail pad and the base pad exhibit load-dependent stiffness characteristics, with the degree of nonlinearity increasing as the preload increases. Regarding frequency-dependent parameters, the loss factor is primarily influenced by frequency and remains nearly independent of preload. Simulation model and verification

To investigate the influence of the stiffness match form of fastener pads on wheel-rail interaction and high-frequency rail wear, this study employs a free beam model and a multi-point supported FDPT model to characterize the deformation of the ironbase and the load/frequency-dependent stiffness of the elastic pad. Additionally, the model incorporates the clamping support of fastening clips and the spring washer support of anchor bolts to reflect actual installation conditions of indirect fastener. Finally, the proposed fastener model is introduced into a vehicle–track coupled spatial dynamics model by replacing the classical Kelvin–Voigt fastener representation. In this framework, the supporting force of the rail pad is fed back to the rail, while the supporting force of the base pad is transmitted to the underlying track foundation, thereby enabling coupled interaction calculations with the wheel–rail system. On this basis, the model is further validated against measured vibration responses from a metro line.

Model validation

To verify the accuracy of the self-developed program, this section presents a validation study using a metro express line as a case study. The line is equipped with DZ III fasteners and operates Type A metro trains at a service speed of 90 km/h. The curve radius is 600 m with a superelevation of 120 mm, representing a typical under-superelevation condition. Detailed operational conditions and measurement methods are described in Reference30. Given that the proposed model primarily influences the vertical and torsional support behavior of the rail, and the frequency-dependent stiffness of the pad mainly affects high-frequency vibrations, this validation focuses on the rail acceleration’s resonant frequencies and the measured displacement characteristics on site.

The simulation parameters are consistent with the actual operating conditions of the line. Track irregularities are modeled using the American Class 6 spectrum, with a wavelength range of 0.01 m to 42 m31. The mass and dimensions of the DZ III fastener system’s ironbase are taken from design specifications. The ironbase measures 340 mm in length, 170 mm in width, and 18 mm in thickness, with a mass of 7.98 kg. The initial preload is applied according to installation requirements: each clip provides an initial clamping force of 9 kN, and each anchor bolt is pre-tightened to 40 kN.

To demonstrate the necessity of using the proposed model, the simulation results include not only the results from this model but also those from the existing single-layer support model13 and the fastener support model using a rigid body to simulate the ironbase (Rigid Body Model)8. In all models, the fastener pads are simulated using the FDPT model to represent the load/frequency-dependent stiffness characteristics of the elastic pads. Discussion

Based on the established vehicle-track coupled dynamic model describing the combined stiffness characteristics of the indirect fastener system, this section will explore the impact mechanisms of the dynamic stiffness and damping of fastener pads on wheel-rail interaction and high-frequency rail wear.

Influence of the stiffness matching form of the elastic pad

Although the Chinese metro design standard (GB 50157 − 2013) recommends the use of indirect fastener systems for ballastless tracks, it does not specify constraints on the stiffness match form of the daul-layer elastic pads. In practical applications, the indirect fastener system mainly includes three match forms: “soft upper and hard lower,” “hard upper and soft lower,” and “same stiffness.” Here, “soft” and “hard” describe the relative stiffness of the two pads, but the fastener nodal stiffness of each match form must meet the requirement of not exceeding 40 kN/mm as specified in the standard.

To ensure generality, the subsequent analysis sets three stiffness match forms for the fastener pads, as shown in Table 3. To maintain consistent nodal stiffness of the fastener, the parameters in the table are optimized accordingly. The calculation parameters are achieved by adjusting the static stiffness fitting curve parameters in Table 1 to realize different working conditions, while the fractional derivative representation parameters in Table 1 remain unchanged to ensure consistent frequency-dependent effects of the pads.

For different fastener dynamic stiffness match forms, there is a certain shift in the peak frequency of the vertical and lateral forces. Taking the vertical wheel-rail force as an example, the main resonant frequencies of the frequency bands f1 to f4 under different working conditions are shown in Table 4, with the corresponding spectral results shown in Fig. 10. As the combination mode of the fastener stiffness changes, the main vibration frequency of the f1 frequency band remains relatively unchanged, indicating that the P2 force resonance is unaffected by the stiffness match form of fastener pad. However, as the fastener stiffness match form transitions from “soft upper, hard lower” (case 3) to “hard upper, soft lower,” (case 1) the f2 and f4 frequency bands shift from 380 to 440 Hz and 1025–1065 Hz to 326–374 Hz and 964–1002 Hz, respectively. The vibration amplitude decreases as the rail pad stiffness increases. In the f3 frequency band, in addition to the main resonant frequencies of 552–608 Hz (referred to as f3−1) and 606–640 Hz, two new resonant frequencies appear at 740–790 Hz (referred to as f3−2) and 710–770 Hz (f3−2), with their amplitudes being lower than the main resonant frequency bands. This is related to the low stiffness of the base pad causing the ironbase to act as a “dynamic vibration absorber“10.

The characteristics of the wheel-rail lateral force under different working conditions are similar to those of the vertical force, with resonance frequency shifts and amplitude changes caused by differences in the match forms. In case 1, only the f1 and f4 resonance frequency bands exhibit wear peak values, and the amplitude is the lowest. In case 2 and 3, the lateral force exhibits all four resonance frequency bands (f1 to f4), with case 3 having the highest amplitude. In case 2, a bifurcation phenomenon appears in the f3 frequency band.

Figure 10c reflects the rail wear spectrum characteristics under different cases. It can be seen that the rail wear spectrum exhibits the f1 to f4 resonance frequency bands, with minimal wear depth differences in the f1 band between different conditions, which is consistent with the wheel-rail force analysis results. For the f2 to f4 bands, the wear depth is lower than that of the f1 band and is influenced by the match form of pad stiffness. In the f2 band, the wear depth is similar across all cases, with only a shift in the resonance frequency band. In the f3 band, case 1 and 2 exhibit a bifurcation phenomenon in wear depth, with the vibration primarily occurring in the f3−1 band. The wheel-rail force amplitude and wear amplitude in the f3−2 band decrease as the base pad stiffness decreases, and both are lower than the results of case 3. In the f4 band, the wear depth shifts to lower frequencies as the rail pad stiffness increases, and the amplitude also decreases accordingly. Therefore, using the “hard upper, soft lower” elastic pad stiffness match form can effectively reduce the intensity of high-frequency rail wear, thereby slowing the development of phenomena like short-wavelength rail corrugation.

Influence of the damping matching form of the elastic pad

For indirect fasteners, the damping properties of the two elastic pads may vary due to differences in material composition, thereby affecting high-frequency wheel-rail vibrations. Therefore, based on the calculation cases in Sect. 4.1, this section explores the effects of modifying the fractional derivative parameter (γ) and relaxation time (τ) to adjust the frequency-dependent loss factor of the pads. The study considers three stiffness match forms of the indirect fastener system (“hard-upper, soft-lower,” “equal stiffness,” and “soft-upper, hard-lower”). The impact on wheel-rail system response and wear characteristics is examined under three scenarios: modifying the damping of either the rail pad or base pad independently, or adjusting both simultaneously. case 1 serves as the baseline, using the original fractional derivative parameters of the pads from Table 1. In case 2 and case 3, the fractional derivative parameters of the rail pad and base pad are independently increased to evaluate the effect of enhancing the damping of each component separately. case 4 investigates the combined effect of increasing the damping of both the rail pad and base pad simultaneously. The comparison between the proposed frequency-dependent damping curve and the original parameters is shown in Fig. 11. The loss factor values at 60 Hz, 400 Hz, 700 Hz, and 1000 Hz are also indicated. For the high-damping scenario, the fractional order parameters are set to γ = 6.8 × 10⁻⁶ and τ = 0.48.

To evaluate the impact of pad damping on rail wear depth, the one-third octave effective value is used to describe the frequency-domain characteristics of rail wear in the inner curve of the circular section under different working cases, as illustrated in Fig. 12. The positions of resonance bands f1 to f4 are highlighted in the figure. It can be observed that altering the damping combination of fastener pads mainly influences the wheel-rail wear peaks in resonance bands f1 to f4. Under different stiffness match forms, simultaneously increasing the damping of both the rail pad and base pad effectively reduces high-frequency rail wear at resonance bands, as shown in case 4 in Fig. 12. However, when increasing the damping of only one pad, the simulation results exhibit distinctly different patterns. Specifically, increasing the damping of the low-stiffness pad (the “soft” pad), as shown in case 3 of Fig. 12a and case 2 of Fig. 12c, does not entirely reduce the wheel-rail wear in resonance bands f1 to f4 to the level achieved when both pads’ damping is increased. However, a significant reduction is still observed compared to the baseline case. A similar effect is evident in case 2 of Fig. 12b, where the pads have identical stiffness. On the contrary, increasing only the damping of the high-stiffness pad (the “hard” pad), as in case 2 of Fig. 12a and case 3 of Fig. 12c, results in increased rail wear in resonance bands, rather than the expected reduction. For instance, Fig. 12a shows that the wear depths in bands f1, f3, and f4 are higher than those observed in the baseline low-damping condition (case 1). These findings suggest that in designing damping combinations for fastener pads, simply enhancing the damping characteristic of the “hard” pad may unintentionally intensify wheel-rail wear at resonance frequencies.

Conclusions

To investigate the impact of the stiffness match form of the daul-layer elastic pads in the indirect fastener on high-frequency rail wear under actual installation conditions, this study focuses on the DZ III fastener. The frequency-dependent nonlinear stiffness characteristics of the rail pad and base pad are tested and characterized. A dynamic analysis model for the indirect fastener is established using a free beam model to simulate ironbase deformation and a FDPT model to simulate the load/frequency-dependent nonlinear stiffness of the pads. This model is then integrated into a vehicle-track coupled dynamics system to further explore the influence mechanisms of the stiffness match form and damping characteristics of fastener elastic pads on the wheel-rail interaction behavior and rail wear characteristics in curved tracks. The main research findings are as follows: Compared to the existing single-layer fastener support model and the daul-layer pad fastener model with a rigid body model of ironbase, the proposed model, which considers the flexible deformation of the ironbase and load/frequency-dependent stiffness of the pads, better simulates the actual deformation and vibration characteristics of indirect fasteners. The maximum error in the dominant frequency of rail acceleration vibration is 21 Hz, which is significantly more accurate than the existing models (maximum error of 136 Hz). Without altering the fastener nodal stiffness, adopting the “hard upper-soft lower” stiffness match form for the pads effectively reduces the intensity of wheel-rail interaction and rail wear rate within the frequency bands of second-order/third-order bending resonance and Pinned-Pinned resonance. However, it has no significant effect on rail wear behavior at the P2 force resonance frequency.Overall, the damping performance of the fastener system is determined by the low-stiffness elastic pad. Merely increasing the damping of the “hard” pad exacerbates rail wear in the resonance bands. Simultaneously increasing the damping of both the rail pad and base pad achieves the best vibration suppression effect.

Without altering the fastener nodal stiffness, adopting the “hard upper-soft lower” stiffness match form for the pads effectively reduces the intensity of wheel-rail interaction and rail wear rate within the frequency bands of second-order/third-order bending resonance and Pinned-Pinned resonance. However, it has no significant effect on rail wear behavior at the P2 force resonance frequency.

However, it has no significant effect on rail wear behavior at the P2 force resonance frequency.

Overall, the damping performance of the fastener system is determined by the low-stiffness elastic pad. Merely increasing the damping of the “hard” pad exacerbates rail wear in the resonance bands. Simultaneously increasing the damping of both the rail pad and base pad achieves the best vibration suppression effect.

Overall, the damping performance of the fastener system is determined by the low-stiffness elastic pad. Merely increasing the damping of the “hard” pad exacerbates rail wear in the resonance bands. Simultaneously increasing the damping of both the rail pad and base pad achieves the best vibration suppression effect.

Due to limitations in scope and research focus, this paper primarily investigates the impact of stiffness match forms of elastic pads in indirect fasteners on high-frequency rail wear. However, since the ironbase is a critical vibrating component in the fastener system’s dynamic model, it influences the high-frequency wheel-rail interaction behavior and wear characteristics under actual train loads. Therefore, future research will further explore the effects of the ironbase’s vibrating mass on the high-frequency wear of the wheel-rail system.
