Quality of Life Impairment in Patients With Chronic Cough: How Does It Compare to Other Chronic Conditions?
Authors: Sang Min Lee, Young-Koo Jee
Published: 2026

It had become increasingly difficult to define chronic cough (CC) simply as a symptom, because many cases remain refractory or unexplained. The ERS guidelines address the diagnosis and management of CC as an independent clinical entity and propose specific approaches to managing refractory or unexplained CC.1 CC is likely to impose a substantial burden on affected individuals, and several studies have demonstrated reduced quality of life in patients with CC; however, comparative studies to other diseases or conditions remain scarce.2,3

The present study by Kang et al.4 showed that the mental health (MH) dimension of the 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-36) was significantly lower in patients with moderate-to-severe CC than in those with diabetes mellitus (DM) or rheumatoid arthritis (RA), but it showed no significant difference compared to chronic kidney disease (CKD). In particular, the MH and social functioning (SF) domain scores were significantly lower in patients with moderate-to-severe CC than in those with DM. In contrast, physical functioning (PF) and other physical domains remained relatively preserved, which is commonly observed in psychiatric disorders and certain symptom groups without major physical disability (e.g., tinnitus).

Patients with social anxiety disorder exhibit significantly impaired SF-36 quality of life compared to the general population, particularly in the SF domain.5 Individuals at risk of depressive disorder have been reported to show reductions in MH, role-emotional (RE), and SF, along with decreased overall health utility.6 In RA, MH/mental component summary scores of the SF-36 have been shown to be useful for identifying patients at risk of depression and anxiety.7 Within the anxiety disorder spectrum, including generalized anxiety disorder and panic disorder, impairments primarily manifest in MH and SF, as well as RE and vitality (VT), due to avoidance, hyperarousal, and anticipatory anxiety.8 Beyond psychiatric conditions, tinnitus may not substantially limit PF; however, stress, anxiety, depression, impaired concentration, and sleep disturbance associated with tinnitus frequently lead to marked deterioration in SF, RE, MH, and VT.9,10 Likewise, adults with stuttering have been reported to show reduced quality of life compared to controls, with the impact primarily manifest in SF, MH, VT, and RE domains.11 Acne, a dermatologic condition, is also widely cited as imposing a significantly high psychological and social burden with prominent deterioration in MH and SF.12

Multiple factors may contribute to reduced MH and SF in patients with CC. Coughing in public places may attract attention (e.g., suspicion of infection or misunderstanding as poor etiquette), and repeated interruptions during conversations or social gatherings may lead patients to reduce social activities. Interview-based studies of patients with refractory or unexplained CC have reported that stigma and embarrassment can restrict social interactions—a trend exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic—and may result in social isolation.13 Nocturnal cough can cause sleep fragmentation, leading to sleep deprivation that subsequently worsens emotional regulation and SF. The qualitative study cited above also noted that cough-related sleep disturbance is common and may contribute to reduced SF and isolation. When paroxysmal coughing is triggered by trivial stimuli—such as laughter, speaking, or temperature change—patients may develop anxiety about unpredictable recurrence, leading to the avoidance of social outings or interpersonal contact; consequently, emotional distress and psychomorbidity are common in CC.14 Depression may both result from and exacerbate coughing, while anxiety and depression can heighten cough perception and hypersensitivity, thereby perpetuating symptoms. A population-based prospective study demonstrated a bidirectional association between CC and depression, including an increased risk of recurrent depression.15 Cross-sectional studies have also reported a high prevalence rate of depressive symptoms among patients with CC.16 In a large cohort from the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging, CC is associated with reduced social participation (health-limited social participation) and increased burden in activities of daily living.17

In this study, additional SF-36 domains—PF, role-physical (RP), VT, bodily pain (BP), and general health (GH)—were also decreased in patients with CC compared to HC. In a community-based cohort of Korean older adults (Korean Longitudinal Study on Health and Aging), SF-36 scores were compared according to cough phenotype; the chronic persistent cough group showed lower scores across multiple domains than controls.18 Similarly, another study reported that most multi-item scale scores were reduced, with exception of BP; the impairment was particularly pronounced in GH and RP domains.19

A major limitation of this study is that quality of life in the comparison groups (DM, RA, and CKD) was assessed during maintenance therapy, whereas it was evaluated prior to treatment in the CC group, which may have overestimated the burden attributable to CC. Indeed, several studies have demonstrated that both pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments for CC can improve quality of life.20,21 Therefore, to more accurately compare the burden of CC to that of other chronic conditions, future studies should evaluate quality of life after appropriate treatments for CC, using instruments such as the SF-36 that are applicable across disease groups. Furthermore, beyond the individual level, social burden related to CC—including health care resource utilization and personal care needs17—should also be compared to other chronic conditions or symptoms in future research.
