Severe morbidity following pancreatectomy with vascular reconstruction: impact of intraoperative vascular events and grafted venous reconstructions
Authors: Ramiro Fernández-Placencia, Francisco Berrospi, Carlos Luque-Vásquez, Eduarda Bustamante, Néstor Sánchez, Eloy Ruiz, Vanesa Huamán, Eduardo Payet, Juan Celis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVascular resection and reconstruction during pancreatic surgery has become increasingly common in high-volume centers. However, the impact of intraoperative vascular events and complex venous reconstructions on severe morbidity remains understudied.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study including 77 patients who underwent pancreatic resection with vascular reconstruction at a high-volume tertiary care center between January 2010 and December 2024. Perioperative factors were evaluated through univariate and multivariate analyses.ResultsIntraoperative events occurred in 25 % of the patients; severe morbidity (≥ IIIb according to the Clavien–Dindo classification) was observed in 31 %, and the mortality rate was 3.9 %. Severe morbidity was significantly influenced by intraoperative events (OR=4.3, 95 % CI 1.3–14.6, p = 0.016) and type 4 venous reconstruction (OR=12.7, 95 % CI 1.5–280, p = 0.037). Despite the increasing proportion of type 3 and type 4 venous reconstructions performed over the years, the rates of severe morbidity have remained stable. A notable improvement in the R0 resection rate for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma was observed after 2019, with a significant shift toward more complex venous reconstructions.ConclusionIntraoperative events and type 4 venous reconstruction significantly increase the risk of severe morbidity in pancreatic surgery. These findings underscore the importance of surgical planning, expertise in vascular procedures, and multidisciplinary care to improve outcomes.Keywords: Pancreaticoduodenectomy, Distal pancreatectomy, Vascular resection, Venous reconstruction

Introduction

Over the last few decades, the use of pancreatic surgery in high-volume centers has led to significant improvements in both perioperative and survival outcomes [1,2]. With the advent of neoadjuvant treatments (NAT), particularly for borderline resectable and locally advanced pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), there has been a resurgence in complex surgical procedures [3,4], with the advantages of a higher rate of R0 resection [5,6] and improved overall survival (OS) compared with upfront resection or palliative surgery [7]. In addition to pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) with venous [8] and/or arterial reconstruction[9] and distal pancreatectomy with celiac artery resection (DP-CAR) [10], the scope of these intricate procedures has broadened. They are now frequently used in the management of other neoplastic conditions [[11], [12], [13]], illustrating their growing importance in surgical oncology.

Numerous centers have reported positive results from vascular resections in pancreatic surgery [[14], [15], [16]], whereas other studies have reported increased morbidity and mortality associated with these procedures [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. These divergent conclusions may be explained by the influence of intraoperative events on postoperative outcomes, which may not have been sufficiently addressed in previous studies.

The aim of this study was to analyze the factors associated with severe morbidity, including intraoperative vascular events (IVEs), in patients undergoing pancreatectomy with vascular resection/reconstruction (VRRs) and to report our experience at a tertiary care center specializing in the treatment of neoplastic diseases.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using prospectively collected data from the Department of Abdominal Surgery at the Instituto Nacional de Enfermedades Neoplásicas (INEN) in Lima, Peru. Patients who underwent pancreatectomy with VRR, including PD, distal pancreatectomy (DP), or total pancreatectomy (TP), between January 2010 and December 2024 at our institution were selected.

Postoperative outcomes (morbidity and mortality) up to 90 days after surgery (POD 90) were recorded in our database. The last updated outcome was recorded in March 2025. The Institutional Review Board approved the study (protocol N° 24–499) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the requirement for individual informed consent was waived due to its retrospective nature.

Patient selection

When a case-by-case decision was made during multidisciplinary team (MDT) meetings, patients were considered generally unsuitable for surgery when any of the following items were observed: poor performance status (ECOG≥2), disease progression after NAT, severe malnutrition, bilirubin levels ≥250 μmol/L, or severely impaired liver and renal function [22]. Patients diagnosed with borderline or locally advanced disease received preoperative treatment, primarily neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) regimens such as FOLFIRINOX, gemcitabine/capecitabine, or gemcitabine/cisplatin. In recent years, the use of radiotherapy has markedly declined at our institution due to limited perceived benefit and concerns regarding radiation-induced vasculitis, particularly in patients requiring vascular reconstruction for locally advanced disease.

All patients underwent preoperative triphasic contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scans, which were assessed to determine their anatomical characteristics. The extent and level of resection were planned according to the tumor and individual patient factors.

Endpoints and definitions

The objectives of this study were twofold: first, to identify the intraoperative risk factors that contribute to severe morbidity following pancreatic resection with VRR; second, to provide a detailed account of our experience and outcomes in patients subjected to these surgical interventions.

Clinically relevant postoperative pancreatic fistula (CRPOPF), postpancreatectomy hemorrhage (PPH), biliary fistula, delayed gastric emptying (DGE), and type or venous reconstruction (VenR) were defined according to the International Study Group in Pancreatic Surgery (ISGPS)[[23], [24], [25], [26], [27]]. To define the resectability of PDAC, the NCCN definition was used[28]. R0 resection was defined as the absence of neoplastic cells within 1 mm of the resection margins[29,30]. Severe morbidity (≥ IIIb) was graded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification.

IVEs related to VRR are characterized by incidents such as (a) injury (tear, severance, or inadvertent sealing with advanced energy devices) to the superior mesenteric vein (SMV) or portal vein (PV) during dissection along the vessel groove at the head of the pancreas; (b) acute thrombosis of the mesenteric/celiac vessels or their respective branches; and (c) instances where suspected infiltration of the SMV/PV necessitated VenR, followed by observation of bowel congestion or ischemia.

Follow-up

According to our protocol, all patients who underwent VRR at our institution received daily abdominal ultrasound assessments during the first 72 h after surgery, with a focus on liver perfusion, followed by a triphasic computed tomography (CT) scan evaluation on POD7. Patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) were primarily reserved for cases involving hemodynamic instability, significant blood loss, or severe morbidity.

After discharge, patients were scheduled for outpatient visits on POD15, POD30, and POD90 as part of the postoperative care protocol. To ensure adequate oncologic control, patients underwent trimestral evaluations with CT scans during the first year, biannual evaluations during the second and third years, and annual evaluations thereafter.

Vascular resection and reconstruction technique

. Venous resection and reconstruction

Venous resection and reconstruction was performed following the ISGPS classification and tailored to the extent of vascular involvement (types 1–4). All resections were carried out during the final phase of the pancreatic resection, after oncologic clearance was confirmed. Vascular control was always established prior to venous transection, and reconstructions were performed under loupe magnification for arterial resections only.

Type 1 – lateral venorrhaphy (Fig. 1a)

VenR types 1–3 (ISGPS classification) during pancreaticoduodenectomy. (a) Type 1 - primary venorraphy. (b) Type 2 - Peritoneal patch. (c) Segmental resection of the superior mesenteric vein with preservation of the splenoportal junction. (d) Portomesenteric anastomosis with concomitant splenorenal shunt.

For limited venous wall involvement (<25 % of the venous circumference), a longitudinal or oblique defect was closed using interrupted 5–0 polypropylene sutures. Blalock–Thomas clamps were used to secure both ends of the vein, ensuring bloodless exposure while minimizing vein distortion. A single Bulldog clamp was applied to the splenic vein if necessary. The anastomosis lumen were irrigated with heparinized saline before closure. Special attention was given to avoid narrowing of the lumen before primary closure.

Type 2 – patch venoplasty (Fig. 1b)

For defects where primary closure risked stenosis, a patch reconstruction was performed using autologous peritoneum harvested from the falciform ligament or anterior parietal peritoneum using the technique proposed by Dokmak et al. [31]. The patch was tailored to size and sutured with 5–0 polypropylene in a running fashion.

Type 3 – end-to-end anastomosis (Fig. 1, Fig. 1)

After complete division of the vein, proximal and distal vascular control was secured using atraumatic vascular clamps. The posterior wall anastomosis was initiated at the left corner using 5–0 polypropylene in an in–out/out–in configuration. A continuous running suture was used for the posterior wall. A stay suture was placed at the right corner to assist in precise alignment. Once the right corner was reached, the stay suture was tied, and the anterior wall was closed. A running suture was used if the venous caliber was adequate; otherwise, interrupted stitches were placed to minimize risk of narrowing or torsion. Reconstruction or ligation of the splenic vein was performed at the surgeon's discretion.

Type 4 – interposition graft (Fig. 2)

VenR type 4 (ISGPS classification) after (a) distal pancreatectomy with left hepatectomy and total gastrectomy for an 18 cm solid pseudopapillary tumor (PTFE graft). (b) Pancreatoduodenectomy for a solid pseudopapillary tumor (internal jugular vein graft) and reimplantation of the splenic vein with the left gastric vein.

In cases requiring segmental resection >6 cm and when direct anastomosis was not possible due to retraction or tension, an interposition graft was used. In early cases, ringed PTFE grafts (6–8 mm) were used; however, due to increased thrombosis rates and need for reintervention, we transitioned to autologous grafts such as the internal jugular vein, harvested simultaneously by a second team.

The graft was anastomosed using a parachute technique, starting with the proximal (SMV–graft) and then the distal anastomosis (portal–graft). Both ends were sutured using running 5–0 polypropylene, reinforced with guiding stitches at both angles. Patency was assessed visually and with Doppler ultrasound after completion.

Before completing all types of reconstruction, the proximal clamp (SMV) was temporally released to allow a flush of approximately 200 ml, ensuring removal of any potential thrombus that could compromise the venous anastomosis. Growth factors were applied for types 3 and 4 to ensure adequate lumen of the anastomosis. Final patency was confirmed by Doppler assessment after clamp release.

Arterial resection and reconstruction

When infiltration of the common hepatic artery was suspected, our group initially attempted vessel divestment. However, in selected cases, arterial resection with end-to-end anastomosis was deemed necessary to avoid unintended injury.

After proximal and distal vascular control was achieved using Bulldog clamps, the anastomosis was performed under magnification using 7–0 polypropylene sutures. A continuous running suture was used for arteries with a diameter ≥4 mm. For smaller vessels (≤3 mm), interrupted sutures were preferred to reduce the risk of narrowing or kinking.

Theory/Calculation

Continuous variables are reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), whereas categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. For the univariate analysis, the Mann‒Whitney U test was used for continuous variables; categorical variables were compared via the chi‒square test or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate; and variables with a p value<0.1 were considered for multivariable analysis. Multivariate analysis was performed via the binary logistic regression method. To further stratify the risk of ICU and hospital stay, severe morbidity, blood loss volume, operative time, intraoperative blood transfusions, and postoperative mortality, the Kruskal‒Wallis test was employed to compare continuous variables across groups stratified by the number of risk factors, alongside the previously mentioned categorical tests. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and R software was used[32].

In the initial phase of our analysis, we performed univariate analysis to identify potential intraoperative predictors of severe morbidity following venous reconstruction. By using a clinically driven approach, we incorporated the following variables into the multivariable analysis: platelet count ×10⁹/L (p = 0.07), type of VenR (p = 0.042), type of pancreatic resection (p = 0.08), type of pancreaticojejunostomy (p = 0.087) and occurrence of IVEs (p = 0.009). These variables were then subjected to multivariate analysis, where two factors emerged as independent predictors of severe morbidity after pancreatic surgery: the occurrence of IVEs (OR=4.3, 95 % CI 1.3–14.6, p = 0.016) and type 4 venous reconstruction (OR=12.7, 95 % CI 1.5–280, p = 0.037). We excluded estimated blood loss (EBL) and operative time from the final model, as they primarily reflect intraoperative complexity and outcomes, rather than serving as independent predictors of severe morbidity. Instead, variables such as the extent of VRR and intraoperative events were considered more reliable operative predictors during pancreatic resection.

When patients were grouped on the basis of the number of cumulative risk factors (Table 4), there was a significant increase in estimated blood loss, intraoperative blood transfusion volume, severe morbidity, length of ICU stay, and in-hospital stay. However, operative time, CRPOPF, PPH, DGE, postoperative biliary fistula and postoperative deaths were not related to the cumulative risk groups.

Postoperative outcomes

The complications were mostly related to CRPOPF [type B, n = 18 (23 %); type C, n = 3 (3.9 %]), PPH [type A, n = 4 (5.2 %); type B, n = 11 (14.3 %); and type C, n = 5 (6.5 %]), and DGE [n = 5 (7 %]).

Mesenteric/portal vein thrombosis was identified in three patients. The first patient was a 68-year-old female PDAC patient who underwent type 3 VenR without any events and reentered on POD 14. A tumor thrombus was excised, and a patch with the peritoneum was placed in the defect. The patient recovered and was discharged on POD 16. Ultimately, the patient died because of disease progression after 5 months and received one course of gemcitabine. The second patient underwent distal pancreatectomy with left colectomy for a solid pseudopapillary tumor measuring 13.5 cm, which was complicated by marked sinistral portal hypertension. Notably, the tumor exhibited significant adhesion to the portal vein and left colon, with tearing observed during mobilization of the pancreatic head. A venous conduit made of peritoneum was placed. On postoperative day (POD) 8, the patient underwent an end-loop colostomy in response to anastomotic leakage and thrombectomy at the graft site. The patient recovered gradually and was discharged on POD 28, after which she underwent bowel transit restitution. One year after discharge, the patient remained alive and free of disease recurrence. The third patient had a PDAC-mimicking tumor associated with severe sinistral portal hypertension and variceal bleeding. The patient underwent type 4 VenR using a PTFE graft without any events during the procedure. On POD2, the patient underwent graft removal owing to thrombosis and salvage with an internal jugular vein graft. The patient recovered gradually and was discharged 48 days later. Pathology revealed chronic pancreatitis, which was attributed to the presence of cholesterol and bile deposits. The patient continues to be monitored, and sixteen months after surgery, she shows no signs of left-sided portal hypertension.

During the follow-up period, three patients died from complications: two deaths were primarily due to Grade C CR-POPF, and one was attributed to Grade C PPH originating from the pancreatic stump. No deaths associated with vascular resections or reconstructions, including thrombosis, stenosis or hemorrhage, were reported.

Shifts in pancreatic resection outcomes

Since January 2020, our institution has intensified its focus on multimodal treatments (neoadjuvant chemotherapy, radiotherapy in very selected cases, dedicated surgical and anesthesiology teams, etc.) and pancreatic resections with VRR. A comparison of these time periods revealed a decrease in the rate of IVEs (31 % vs. 20 %), although this reduction was not statistically significant (p = 0.39). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in severe morbidity (p = 0.89) or mortality (p = 0.19) between the two periods. Notably, from January 2020 to December 2024, our institution did not record any cases of postoperative death associated with pancreatectomy or VRR. Importantly, 49 % of all cases included in our database were treated during this period.

Additionally, there was a significant shift in the complexity of VenR, with type 3 becoming the most common type, moving away from the previously dominant type 1 during the comparison periods, as shown in Fig. 4. Notably, this shift was accompanied by greater utilization of NACT in the same patient group (18 % vs. 63 %, p = 0.047), which contributed to an improved R0 resection rate for patients with PDAC (36 % vs. 81 %, p = 0.04).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that severe morbidity after pancreatectomy with VRR was significantly influenced by IVEs and type 4 VenR. Conversely, the outcome was not affected by the type of pancreatectomy or pancreaticojejunostomy.

The integration of multimodal approaches and complex vascular reconstruction techniques in PD has improved R0 resection and OS rates[16,33]. Previous studies have demonstrated that venous vascular resection during PD is associated with increased morbidity[34,35]. Concurrently, VenR and vein resection have been associated with increased morbidity and mortality [36]. However, these outcomes may be partly explained after the execution of irreversible surgical steps -such as transection of the pancreas- followed by unexpected findings (e.g. superior mesenteric artery infiltration) that precluded curative resection or, by the occurrence of IVEs, which may not have been fully addressed in previous studies.

The major strength of this study is the identification of how IVEs during pancreatectomy with VRR result in severe morbidity, akin to those observed in laparoscopic liver resections converted to open procedures [37]. Although OS is beyond the scope of the present study, the effects of IVEs during pancreatic surgery can also contribute to its reduction [38], while the preoperative decision to perform VenR can improve it [39].

In our cohort, no mortality was recorded in the last 5 years. This could be explained by the selection criteria for surgery, enhanced vascular surgical techniques, and fewer grafts used in each patient, along with improvements in multidisciplinary management. Although the frequency of IVEs in our cohort decreased over the study period, this reduction was not statistically significant. Nevertheless, the rates of severe morbidity and mortality have remained consistently low. This frequency persists despite the increased number and complexity of VenRs, reflecting not a lack of planning but an increase in procedural difficulty, as demonstrated by the shifts in venous resection types. Notably, up to 22 % of patients who undergo pancreatic resection will require vascular reconstruction despite thorough preoperative planning [40].

At our institution, type 4 VenR has seldom been needed, as we prioritize type 3 VenR. Venous resections exceeding 6 cm were more likely to require grafts. In our experience, this type was associated with increased complications for several reasons: prolonged clamping times, synthetic nature of the graft in most cases, the need for systemic anticoagulation which increases the risk of postoperative bleeding. More importantly, despite these efforts, we experienced graft thrombosis especially in PTFE grafts, and secondly with peritoneal conduits. In the latter, observed in 1 case, we hypothesize that the inherent tendency of the peritoneal conduit to collapse may have contributed to graft thrombosis. To date none of the patients treated with autologous grafts have experienced thrombosis in our series. These findings are consistent with international practices, given the well-known risk of thrombosis associated with synthetic grafts [[41], [42], [43], [44]]. However, careful selection of patients and the choice of reconstruction method [45,46] along with standardized management toward improved outcomes [47] are warranted.

Although the International Study Group of Pancreatic Surgery (ISGPS) has established standardized definitions for venous resection[37], there is no consensus regarding AR. We emphasize that our approach to AR was contingent on the feasibility of divestment. If the vessel was suspected to be infiltrated by a tumor or injured during divestment, we proceeded with AR. Unlike other studies[48,49], we did not identify AR as a predictor of severe morbidity, most likely due to the small number of cases in our cohort, which also highlights a very stringent selection before proceeding to AR[50]. These procedures must be performed in a planned manner to avoid intraoperative events[51]. Moreover, if such events occur, the surgeon must be adequately skilled to manage the situation or have timely access to the support of a vascular or transplant surgeon, in accordance with local institutional practice.

When we analyzed the PDAC group, we observed an improvement in the R0 resection rate over time, which aligns with the findings of a previous study that identified a link between planned VenR and achieving R0 resection, even in the context of larger tumors. In contrast to our results, the same study reported that planned VenR is associated with increased morbidity [52]. This highlights the importance of patient selection following neoadjuvant chemotherapy in patients with PDAC, as those with an adequate response have shown improved R0 rates following these extensive surgical efforts.

We also demonstrated that cumulative risk factors significantly impact postoperative outcomes in patients who underwent pancreatic resection with VRR. Operative time, blood loss, and intraoperative blood transfusion volume increased proportionally with the number of risk factors. The length of ICU and hospital stays also increased significantly with increasing risk factor counts, reflecting the severity of postoperative complications. At our institution, stringent criteria are employed to admit patients to the ICU, avoiding routine admissions [53,54]. Patients with 0 or 1 risk factor had median 0- and 1-day ICU stays, respectively. Prolonged recovery times in high-risk patients align with findings from high-volume centers, where the complexity of VRR has been shown to be correlated with increased morbidity[49], extended hospitalizations and ICU stays[55]. However, it is noteworthy that despite the increased morbidity observed in higher-risk groups, 90-day mortality rates did not differ significantly across groups.

The improvement in outcomes over time likely reflects our institutional experience, marked by increased VRR case volume, enhanced team coordination through MDT meetings, expert surgeons in VRR and management of events [56,57], and refinements in perioperative management protocols, including vigilant monitoring and prompt intervention for complications [58]. In addition, centralization of pancreatic resections within a postoperative surveillance, has allowed for earlier identification and treatment of vascular complications. The integration of vascular surgery techniques and principles derived from liver transplantation has further strengthened our approach to more complex vascular resections and reconstructions. These findings further reinforce the paradigm that, to date, pancreatic surgeons and their institutions must be prepared to manage vascular scenarios, adhering to the current principles of centralization and multidisciplinary care, with the objective of reducing failure-to-rescue rates[59,60].

The present investigation warrants cautious interpretation due to its limitations. This is because it involves retrospective analysis, which may introduce unbalanced confounding variables. Additionally, the patients in this study had a variety of diseases with diverse prognoses and treatments. In the case of type 4 VenR, this was a small subgroup. However, as previously mentioned, larger cohort studies have reported similar outcomes. Nevertheless, this cohort provides authentic real-world data from a single institution that centers on specific approaches for resecting tumors that involve crucial blood vessels during pancreatectomies. The postoperative outcomes indicate that VRR yields favorable results and that surgeons should be aware and ready to manage IVEs.

Conclusion

In patients who undergo pancreatic surgery with VRR, IVEs or VenR type 4 have a greater risk of severe morbidity. This highlights the importance of skilled surgeons who possess not only extensive experience and high case volumes but also expertise in performing vascular reconstructions, even in challenging situations, within specialized centers, where such expertise and multidisciplinary management can be leveraged to improve patient outcomes.
