Machine learning survival model for personalised prevention of catheter-related thrombosis in tumour patients

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCentral venous catheters for drug delivery introduce catheter-related thrombosis (CRT) and influence the survival of cancer patients. The key unmet needs to personalise CRT prevention include identifying high-risk patients and optimising extubation time. In this study, we aimed to develop a survival model to facilitate personalised CRT prevention strategies.MethodsWe prospectively collected tumour patient catheterization data across 4 centres. The SM-CRT survival model, which provides both continuous risk ranking (crank) predictions and the survival distribution (distr) predictions was constructed.ResultsHere we include a total of 30,947 patients. The SM-CRT model exhibits robust performance in identifying high-risk patients, with c-indexes of 0.714 in the prospective test dataset and 0.678 and 0.779 in 2 external test datasets based on crank predictions. Femorally inserted central catheter (FICC), peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC), tumours in the thoracic cavity, and alkylating agents are identified as high-risk factors. Patients are subsequently divided into high-risk, low-risk, and long-term period groups on the basis of their distr predictions. The predicted low-risk and long-term groups present significantly fewer CRT events per day than the high-risk group in both the training dataset (odds ratio [OR] = 0.54, 95% CI [0.38–0.91], adjusted p-value [padj] <0.001; OR = 0.39, 95% CI [0.34–0.44], padj <0.001) and the test dataset (OR = 0.47, 95% CI [0.28–0.87, padj = 0.024; OR = 0.41, 95% CI [0.28–0.61], padj <0.001).ConclusionsThe high c-indexes based on crank predictions demonstrated the ability of the SM-CRT model to identify high-risk patients for thromboprophylaxis. Additionally, the SM-CRT model can guide extubation time by identifying high-risk periods through distr predictions.Subject terms: Haematological diseases, Oncology, Prognostic markers, Disease prevention

Introduction

Central venous catheters are convenient for drug delivery and improve patient comfort, but they also introduce catheter-related complications1. Catheter-related thrombosis (CRT) is among the major complications affecting cancer patients. Previous reports have indicated that the incidence of CRT among cancer patients ranges from 2.4 to 61.5%1–3. Addressing this issue involves the following sequential tasks: (1) identifying high-risk factors and patients for interventions such as antithrombotic drugs; (2) optimising the extubation time, avoiding insertion during the most dangerous period; and (3) applying additional prevention strategies if necessary. However, predictive models that accurately realise these functions have not been established and remain uncharacterised.

To accurately predict CRT risk, theoretically, the model must not only consider general patient information (such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI), blood markers, and comorbidities) that contribute to general venous thromboembolism (VTE)4–7 which have shown limited performance for CRT prediction8, but must also systematically consider distinctive features associated with patients who require catheters, such as catheter, tumour, and systemic therapy characteristics. Catheters are considered high-risk factors for thrombosis9; however, previous CRT models have not included catheter-related information10–13 and lacked comparisons between different types of catheters and other catheter-related features, such as catheter tip position. Additionally, tumour types have not been comprehensively characterised in previous studies8,14. Most studies have included only a single tumour type11,15, and some studies have used only the tumour risk score concluded in the Khorana score16. In addition, multiple types of antitumour systemic therapies have been reported to be associated with thrombosis17. However, the treatment types considered in previous VTE models are limited5, and the role of systemic therapies has not been explored in CRT models8, making such models difficult to effectively apply to real-world scenarios with diverse types of systemic treatments.

In addition, to optimise the extubation time, the CRT predictor must predict the distribution of the CRT probability over time. Classification models for event occurrence do not have this function because they typically do not include time-to-event information8,15,18. Survival models incorporate time-to-event information; however, no survival model has been constructed for thrombosis or CRT. Moreover, existing survival models tend to provide only a general risk prediction score without providing a precise prediction of the event occurrence probability over time, regardless of the type of survival task, such as tumour prognosis19 or organ replacement20,21. With advances in survival model development, distribution predictions can be obtained on the basis of specific distributions and kernels22–24. Moreover, there are significant differences in CRT incidence across different catheterization durations6,13,16, and catheters are operable risk factors that can be removed at any time. Thus, distribution prediction, which can be used to guide the timing of catheter removal, is especially important for CRT prediction because CRT risk can be completely prevented by removing the catheter. Survival models that predict distributions constitute the optimal direction for CRT prediction.

Overall, there is currently a lack of understanding regarding the objectives of CRT modelling, and a well-designed and validated CRT prediction model that enables personalised prevention of CRT is needed. Thus, we collected multicentre large-scale catheterization data from tumour patients and recorded variables (such as general patient information, catheter information, tumour status, systemic treatments, and blood markers) as comprehensively as possible to develop a machine learning model called SM-CRT aimed at predicting both the general risk and the time distribution of risk for CRT. The performance and applications of the SM-CRT model are well validated in both the train and test datasets. The proposed SM-CRT model is expected to both identify high-risk patients and assist in catheter placement timing decisions, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

Methods

Study design and population

A diagram of the study design is displayed in Fig. 1. Patients with tumours who underwent catheter insertion, met the inclusion criteria, and were treated in four centres were recruited. The data were prospectively collected and retrospectively analysed. One training cohort and 3 test cohorts were included in our study. Patients recruited from centres 1 and 2 were combined for the multicentre training and testing. Specifically, patients whose data were collected before June 30, 2023, were included in the training cohort, and patients whose data were collected after July 1, 2023, were included in the prospective test cohort. Patients recruited from centres 3 and 4 were included in separate external test sets. The characteristics of the training and 3 testing datasets are listed (Supplementary Table 1).

The present study was approved by the institutional review board of Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College (24/291-4571), with a waiver of informed consent due to the use of deidentified data and minimal risk to participants.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible patients had pathologically diagnosed malignant tumours, underwent successful catheterization, voluntarily participated and reported their data in this study, and were aged ≥18 years. The key exclusion criteria included incomplete patient information, an unknown location of the primary tumour, and no follow-up (patients who did not attend follow-up appointments) after catheterization. In addition, if patients had multiple times of catheter insertion, each time was treated as different case in our study.

Data collection for each patient was independently performed by two authors, followed by manual verification and adjustment for inconsistencies by the third author.

Multiple feature engineering approaches were used to trim features (Supplementary Fig. 1). Factor collapsing approaches were applied for tumour types and chemotherapy types to reduce the number of sparse features. Factors were grouped based on clinical similarity (Supplementary Table 2). For example, ‘lung’ and ‘pleura’ were combined as ‘lung/pleura’. Collapsed binary variables were coded as 1 if any corresponding component variable equaled 1, and 0 otherwise. One-hot encoding and normalisation were performed for the categorical variables to serve as inputs to the model. We further performed univariable selection and collinearity removal to make the model simpler and more robust. In detail, candidate features were first screened using univariable Cox proportional hazards regression, retaining variables with p < 0.05. Pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients were then calculated, and in cases of high collinearity (|r| > 0.80), the variable with the smaller absolute log-hazard ratio was removed.

Catheterization method

The modified Seldinger technique with ultrasound guidance was used for CVC, FICC, PICC, and PORT placement. All CVCs and PORTs were nontunneled catheters inserted through the subclavian vein or jugular vein on the uninjured side (without surrounding tumours such as breast cancer). All PICCs were inserted into the superior vena cava through the basilic vein on the uninjured side, and all FICCs were CVC-based and inserted into the inferior vena cava through the femoral vein on the uninjured side. FICCs are applied mainly in patients with superior vena cava syndrome (SVCS)25, which can lead to oedema of the head, neck, and upper extremities, contributing to haemodynamic stasis and thrombosis26. The material for the CVCs, FICCs, and PORTs was polyurethane, and the material for the PICCs was silicone rubber. The CVCs, PORTs, and FICCs were approximately 18 centimetres long in the vein, and the PICCs were approximately 40 centimetres long in the vein. The outside diameters (ODs) of the catheters were as follows: 4Fr at centres 1 and 2; 4Fr for the PICC at centre 3; 6Fr for the PORT at centre 3; and 6Fr at centre 4.

After catheterization of the nonfemoral catheters, a chest X-ray examination (including the upper limb and neck on the ipsilateral side of the catheter) was performed to confirm the direction of the catheter and position of the catheter tip. All X-ray films were evaluated by a physician specialising in venous catheterization, and at least one radiologist was responsible for the chest X-ray reports. With respect to nonfemoral catheters, the tip of the catheter was typically located in the lower third of the superior vena cava, the cavoatrial junction, or the upper third of the right atrium. If the vertebrae were used as a reference, thoracic vertebra segments 6–8 (T6–T8) were considered the proper position; otherwise, the position was deemed improper. For the FICC, the catheter tip was placed at the start of the inferior vena cava near the common iliac vein, and no improper position was considered.

CRT assessments

Patients were continuously examined until extubation was performed upon the doctor’s request. The diagnosis of CRT was made via vascular ultrasound with Doppler and colour imaging (GE LOGIQTM E9). Ultrasound was performed every 3 months after catheter insertion and if any clinical symptoms suggesting CRT were noted. CRT occurrence was treated as a targeted event in the survival analysis, and the time from catheter insertion to CRT occurrence was considered the CRT-free survival (CFS) time. No CRT until extubation or the last ultrasound follow-up was considered censored timepoints.

Survival model construction and validation

The model was trained with the training set. To choose a model with suitable hyperparameters and evaluate model performance, a nested tenfold cross-validation strategy was applied (Supplementary Fig. 1). Inner tenfold cross-validation was used for hyperparameter tuning, and outer tenfold cross-validation was used for internal validation. Five machine learning models, namely, the surv.random.forest (random survival forest, ‘surv.ranger’ in mlr3), surv.xgboost.aft (XGBoost with accelerated failure time (AFT) model, ‘surv.xgboost.aft’ in mlr3), surv.xgboost.cox (XGBoost with cox proportional hazards model, ‘surv.xgboost.cox’ in mlr3), surv.coxph (classic cox proportional hazards model, ‘surv.coxph’ in mlr3), and surv.elastic.net (regularised cox model with elastic net, ‘surv.glmnet’ in mlr3) were developed.

The models were trained to predict both the continuous risk ranking (crank) and predicted survival distribution (distr) prediction types. Crank is a model-specific one-dimensional risk score produced by the underlying learner used to evaluate the risk ranking and calculate the c-index. The scale and sign of the crank score are model-dependent. In the surv.coxph, surv.glmnet, and surv.xgboost.cox models, the crank score is the common linear predictor. In the surv.xgboost.aft model, the crank score is the negated predicted log survival time. In the ranger.surv model, the crank score is the summed cumulative hazard. Distr is automatically generated by the R mlr3proba framework and is analytically obtained in the following four main stages: (1) crank prediction from a survival model; (2) calibration of the crank score within a proportional hazards formulation; (3) estimation of the baseline cumulative hazard function; and (4) analytical construction of the individual-specific survival function (distr prediction), CDF, and probability density function (PDF). The CDF and PDF can be used to guide the extubation time.

The performance of different models in the outer tenfold cross-validation was compared using the concordance index (c-index) as an evaluation metric. The C-index ranges from 0.5 to 1; a value of 0.5 indicates no discriminative power, which is equivalent to random chance, whereas a value of 1.0 represents perfect discrimination. The Brier score was used for calibration comparisons. The Brier score ranges from 0 to 1, with lower values indicating better predictive accuracy. The models were then trained with all the training data and tested with the test sets.

Kernel density estimates based on distr predictions

For each patient in our dataset, the PDF distr prediction was derived with the constructed survival model (named SM-CRT). The distribution is expected to be continuous over time (days); however, some scope saltation may occur on some days. To obtain a smoother distribution, we computed kernel density estimates for the distribution with a Gaussian kernel and a bandwidth (bw) of 3 (the bandwidth is set to 3 to obtain an overall smooth distribution without losing too many local details). The highest instantaneous probability before the first six months was calculated, and the days with instantaneous probabilities greater than 50% the highest were deemed high risk period. The first day in the high-risk period (t1) and the first day after the high-risk period (t2) were then identified.

Model interpretation

We adopt the partial dependence plot (PDP), which summarises how a covariate influences model-predicted survival functions while averaging over the joint distribution of the remaining features, for survival model interpretation. This allows direct interpretation on the survival probability scale, which is clinically meaningful. To quantify feature importance based on these survival-PDPs, we used the area between the partial dependence survival curves corresponding to the most favourable and least favourable values of a feature over a prespecified time horizon. This method measures how much overall survival probability mass is gained or lost when a feature changes from its worst to its best value, while averaging over all other covariates. Mathematically, this method is equivalent to the difference in the restricted mean survival time implied by the two partial dependence curves, which makes the measure time-aware and clinically interpretable. If the highest feature value had the best survival function, the area was labelled positive and the feature was considered a risk effect; otherwise, the area was labelled negative and the feature was considered a protective effect. A larger absolute number represents greater importance in predicting CRT. The ‘survex’ package in R27 was used for survival model interpretation. The ‘model_profile’ function, which evaluates the change in the survival function value derived by the model as the feature value changes, was used to perform PDP analysis.

Statistics and reproducibility

A Kaplan‒Meier survival curve was generated to estimate the time‒to‒event distribution. The ‘muhaz’ package and ‘muhaz’ function were used to produce a smooth estimate of the hazard function for right-censored data using the global and local bandwidth selection algorithms and the boundary kernel function, and the other parameters were set to the default values28.

Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was used to evaluate the associations between variables and the CRT-free survival. The significance of each variable in the Cox regression model was assessed using the Wald test. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each variable. An HR value greater than 1 indicates an increased risk of CRT, whereas an HR value less than 1 indicates a protective effect. The log2 HRs of different tumour sites in various cavities were compared via the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Specifically, no CRT events occurred for patients with prostate tumours and the HR could not be calculated; thus, we assigned −2.5 as the log2 HR value for prostate tumours in this analysis. P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons between different cavities via the Benjamini‒Hochberg (BH) procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR).

The c-indexes derived from the tenfold cross-validation with different models were compared via the Wilcoxon rank sum test. The median value of the SM-CRT-predicted crank value for each dataset was selected as the cutoff value for dividing the patients into high or low crank groups so that the groups contained approximately equal numbers. Time-to-event data were analysed with the Kaplan‒Meier method to estimate survival probabilities, and comparisons between groups were performed using the log-rank test. A Cox proportional hazards regression model estimating the HR was employed to quantify the effect of crank-based stratification on CRT outcomes.

Differences between risk period stratification groups were compared using Fisher’s exact test and p values were adjusted via the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) FDR procedure. To address potential confounding caused by the imbalance of variables between groups in the risk period stratification, we employed propensity score matching (PSM). PSM was conducted by estimating propensity scores using the crank prediction of the model. Matching was performed using nearest neighbour matching (1:1 ratio) with a calliper of 0.01, without replacement, to minimise imbalance.

All tests were two-sided. P values < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance unless otherwise specified. All the statistical analyses were performed with R statistical software version 4.3.

This study was based on multicentre clinical data. A total of 30,947 catheterization cases were included after applying predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Model development followed a predefined analytical pipeline. Nested tenfold cross-validation was applied in the training cohort, with inner folds used for hyperparameter tuning and outer folds (n = 10 independent splits) used for internal validation to reduce overfitting and information leakage. Final model performance was evaluated in three independent test cohorts, including one prospective temporal validation cohort and two external validation cohorts from separate centres. No experimental biological replicates were involved because this was a clinical observational modelling study. The sample size was not determined by formal power calculation; instead, all eligible consecutive patients meeting predefined criteria during the study period were included to maximise statistical power and generalisability.

Results

Patient characteristics and univariate analysis

From January 1, 2017, to June 30, 2023, we included 25,899 patients in the training cohort. In total, 1031 patients (4%) with CRT were recorded (Fig. 1). The probability of non-CRT survival decreased from 1 to 0.786 over time according to the Kaplan‒Meier survival curve (Fig. 2a). The survival curve indicated that the hazard rate sharply increased before the 11th day after catheter insertion and then decreased to nearly 0 2 years after catheter insertion (Fig. 2b), highlighting the time-dependent risk distribution of CRT events.

A univariate proportional hazard regression model was used to examine potential risk factors in general patient characteristics, blood markers, catheter-related characteristics, systemic treatment characteristics, tumour stage, and tumour site associated with CRT (Fig. 3). Notably, the mediastinum and oesophagus are closer to the superior vena cava than others and have the highest HR values (Fig. 3), suggesting that the spatial distance of the tumour to the superior vena cava may be associated with CRT. Tumours in the thoracic cavity were associated with higher HR values than were tumours in the abdominal cavity (Wilcoxon W = 21, p = 0.017) and pelvic cavity (Wilcoxon W = 18, p = 0.024) (Supplementary Fig. 2a). The survival model accurately predicts CRT events

We then sought to construct a survival model to predict CRT events (SM-CRT) by integrating the above variables. The surv.random.forest, surv.xgboost.aft and surv.xgboost.cox models had the highest c-index values in the outer nested tenfold cross-validation (Fig. 4a). The data of all patients in the training cohort were subsequently used to train the final model for testing with the 3 testing datasets. The surv.xgboost.aft model had a consistently high c-index among these models with the prospective test dataset; thus, it was selected as the final SM-CRT model, and the c-indexes of this model with the two external test datasets were 0.678 and 0.779, confirming good performance. Overall, SM-CRT showed a high performance for CRT prediction. This makes the selection of catheter type for a given patient possible by comparing the crank predictions of optional types.

To further evaluate the performance of the surv.coxph model over time, we calculated the cumulative c-index over time of each model. This temporal metric reflects the cumulative nature of the metric, whereby early correctly predicted pairs also contribute to the estimate at later time points. The result revealed that the SM-CRT model achieved a generally higher cumulative c-index in the early period of catheter insertion, reaching 0.8 in all three test datasets (Fig. 4b). The cumulative c-index decreased gradually over time until it reached the overall c-index level (Fig. 4b), indicating that the prediction became more difficult over time. To further assess dynamic discriminative performance, we also performed time-dependent AUROC and AUPRC analyses, which are valuable in imbalanced settings where correctly identifying the small number of true positive cases is paramount. The AUPRC value remained greater than 0.6 and mostly greater than 0.7 across all test datasets before 119 days, which is the 95% quantile of catheter time in our datasets, indicating the performance of the model in imbalanced settings (Supplementary Fig. 4). After 119 days, the AUPRC showed more unstable trend because of the largely decreasing number of available negative cases (non-thrombosis patients under follow-up) (Supplementary Fig. 4). This also underscores that the perceived stability of cumulative c-index in the late period is a result of cumulative averaging rather than superior long-term discriminative performance. The Brier score was used for calibration comparisons, which revealed that the SM-CRT model had generally low Brier scores (less than 0.25) in all test datasets before 119 days (Supplementary Fig. 5). The median value of the SM-CRT-predicted crank score for each dataset was then selected as the cutoff value for dividing the patients into high or low crank groups. Patients with higher SM-CRT crank scores had a significantly greater risk for CRT than those with low SM-CRT crank scores in the prospective test set (HR = 2.05, 95% CI [1.3, 3.23], p = 0.0015), external test 1 set (HR = 21.67, 95% CI [3.05, 154.32], p < 0.001), and external test 2 set (HR = 3, 95% CI [1.52, 5.99], p = 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 6). These results indicated that the SM-CRT model accurately distinguishes high-risk CRT patients from low-risk patients.

Distr prediction of CRT enables guidance over extubation time

The crank prediction of the SM-CRT model can be used to predict the general risk of patients and guide the selection of catheter type. However, the crank prediction results cannot be used to determine the proper extubation time of patients, which is another operable factor and effective way to prevent CRT. Here, we performed risk period stratification based on the patient-specific the “distr” prediction, which provides the instantaneous CRT probability on each catheter day, to verify the ability of the distr prediction to guide the extubation time (Fig. 5a). In detail, for a given patient in our dataset, we first computed the distr prediction using the SM-CRT model, and we then calculated kernel density estimates for the distribution to obtain a smoother distribution (Fig. 5a). Next, we identified the highest instantaneous probability from the smoother distribution, and the days with instantaneous probabilities exceeding 50% the highest were deemed high risk period. The first day in the high-risk period (t1) and the first day after the high-risk period (t2) were then identified (Fig. 5a). The days before t1 were deemed the low-risk period, and the days after t2 were deemed the long-term period. If the patient’s actual catheter extubation day occurred before t1, then the patient was assigned to the low-risk group, and if the extubation occurred on or after t1 but before t2, the patient was assigned to the high-risk group. If extubation occurred on or after t2, the patient was assigned to the long-term group (Fig. 5a). Because the grouping was based on the daily specific CRT probability, we considered the number of CRT events per day, where each day was treated as a case, as a metric for comparisons among different groups.

Comparisons within each dataset revealed that both the low-risk group (odds ratio (OR) = 0.54, 95% CI [0.38–0.91], adjusted p-value (padj) <0.001) and the long-term group (OR = 0.39, 95% CI [0.34–0.44], padj <0.001) had a significantly lower number of CRT events per day than the high-risk group based on the training set (Supplementary Fig. 7). Similar results were obtained with the prospective test dataset (OR = 0.34, 95% CI [0.09–0.92], padj = 0.059; OR = 0.55, 95% CI [0.32–0.92], padj = 0.059). However, the cumulative number of days in the low-risk group was extremely low (less than 1000) and no CRT events were recorded in the two external test datasets (Supplementary Fig. 7). Thus, we combined all three test datasets for comparison, which revealed that the low-risk group and long-term group had a significantly lower number of CRT events per day than the high-risk group based on the test dataset (OR = 0.47, 95% CI [0.28–0.87], padj = 0.0254; OR = 0.41, 95% CI [0.28–0.61], padj < 0.001) (Fig. 5b). Owing to the potential bias introduced by the patient characteristics, PSM was performed to balance the patient characteristics based on the crank score predicted by the SM-CRT model. The results also revealed that both the low-risk group and the long-term group had significantly fewer CRT events per day than did the high-risk group did (Supplementary Fig. 8). These results suggested that the SM-CRT model effectively identifies high-risk CRT periods, allowing optimisation of the catheter removal time in clinical practice.

Some patients may have a low crank prediction, in which case the CRT risk is not high even in the high-risk period defined by the distr prediction; thus, we further grouped patients by combining the crank and distr predictions to provide a more precise risk stratification for patients. By selecting the median crank value based on the entire dataset as the cutoff, we divided patients into six groups according to different combinations of crank scores (high versus low crank values) and distr periods (high risk, low risk, and long term). The results confirmed that the number of CRT events per day during the high-risk period for patients with low crank scores was similar to that during the low-risk period for patients with high crank scores in both the training (OR = 0.83, 95% CI [0.63–1.51], p = 0.83) and test (OR = 1.09, 95% CI [0.51–2.57], p = 1) datasets (Fig. 5c). And both were significantly lower than the high-risk period of patients with high crank scores in the training and test datasets (Fig. 5c). Thus, these results indicated that the SM-CRT model provides more detailed risk stratifications by combining crank and distr predictions for complicated clinical practice.

Model interpretation systematically reveals key factors associated with CRT

We then aimed to interpret the SM-CRT model based on all the test datasets. Most of the results were similar to the results of the univariate analysis (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 9). Age, sex, thrombosis history, and hyperlipidaemia remained important general patient characterises for predicting CRT (Fig. 6). With respect to catheter types, improper position and FICC were important risk factors, whereas peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) and implanted venous port (PORT) were important protective factors. For different tumour sites, the order of importance for predicting CRT was as follows: esophagus, mediastinum, lung/pleura, bladder/ureter, ovarian/fallopian tube, uterine/vagina, and breast (Fig. 6). Tumours in the thoracic cavity (esophagus, mediastinum, and lung/pleura) were also validated to have higher risk than tumours in the pelvic cavity (bladder/ureter, ovarian/fallopian tube, and uterine/vagina) (Fig. 6). With respect to chemotherapies, the order of importance was as follows: antineoplastic antibiotics, DNA topoisomerase inhibitors, platinum, antimetabolites, and microtubule inhibitors (Fig. 6). Tumour stages and blood markers were not highly important for CRT prediction (Fig. 6). Overall, these findings indicated that catheter type, tumour site, systemic treatment type, and general patient characteristics are important factors associated with CRT.

Discussion

In this multicentre large-scale study, we aimed to apply survival machine learning models to predict both the continuous risk ranking (crank) and the survival distribution (distr) of CRT, and we successfully constructed the SM-CRT model, which was demonstrated to have stable performance based on three independent external test datasets. Moreover, we confirmed that the SM-CRT model optimises extubation time, enabling the personalised prevention of CRT in patients with tumours.

The most important contribution of our study was the application of the distr prediction of the survival model and the demonstration of its considerable value in guiding clinical practice. In the CRT prediction task, the general distribution of CRT occurrence based on the total dataset can be estimated, which is consistent with previous studies6,13,16,29; however, the overall distribution cannot be accurately applied to every case, and the distribution at the patient level determined by a machine learning survival model is needed. Unlike crank prediction, distr prediction provides a PDF and cumulative distribution function (CDF) over time, making interventions with time-related operable variables possible. First, the PDF can be used to determine the high-risk and low-risk periods of CRT at the patient level. In this context, the PDF guides the selection of the extubation time of the catheter to avoid the most dangerous period. Additionally, the PDF guides the antithrombotic treatment window to make the best use of antithrombotics. Future clinical trials are warranted to validate these findings. In addition, the CDF informs the cumulative risk over time, which is expected to be useful for optimising the ultrasound test period; however, whether this approach facilitates the early identification of CRT, thus facilitating early treatment, was not validated. In this context, a trial designed to determine the optimal timing of Doppler follow-up might be advisable. More generally, similar follow-up strategies may be used for other tasks, such as cancer survival follow-up or organ transplantation follow-up.

We also identified multiple factors related to general patient information, catheter information, tumour information, and systemic therapies that are critical to the prediction of CRT. The mechanisms of these factors are explained on the basis of Virchow’s triad of endothelial damage, stasis and hypercoagulability, which are components involved in thrombus formation30. General patient characteristics, such as thrombosis history, sex, age, and hyperlipidaemia, were associated with CRT, which is in agreement with previous studies4–7. A few recognised high-risk factors for chronic cardiovascular diseases, such as smoking, hypertension, and diabetes, have not been identified as high-risk factors for CRT31. One possible explanation is that, unlike cardiovascular diseases, which are caused mainly by long-term chronic conditions, CRT, which occurs within a relatively short catheterization duration, is closely related to strong short-term factors such as catheter use rather than weak long-term effects. Our findings also revealed that CRT is strongly affected by catheter-related characteristics. As demonstrated by the univariate analysis and model interpretation results, the use of FICCs and PICCs was associated with a greater risk of CRT than the use of central venous catheters (CVCs) and PORTs. The higher CRT risk of FICC can be explained by the reduced exercise habits in FICC patients, which can result in high stasis30. Previous studies have reported that PICCs are associated with a greater risk of CRT than CVCs32,33. The vessel size of the basilic veins is smaller than that of other central veins, such as the subclavian or internal jugular veins. Therefore, a PICC occupies a relatively larger intraluminal space, and should be considered the main predisposing factor for PICC-related thrombosis. Catheters, which are foreign objects, promote thrombosis by triggering fXII activation34; in this regard, the longer body of the PICC compared with that of the CVC may contribute to a greater prothrombotic effect. We also observed that the malposition of the catheter tips increased the risk of CRT, which is consistent with previous reports35,36. Chronic microtrauma caused by catheter tips knocking against the vessel wall at every heartbeat or increased chemotherapy-related injuries around catheter tips can explain this factor35. Considering the above factors, a shorter length and smaller diameter of the catheter, proper catheter tip placement, and regular exercise are suggested for reducing CRT events.

The tumour site is also a major determinant of CRT. Malignant tumours can induce a hypercoagulable state in the blood, increasing the risk of thrombosis37. However, how and at which level the different tumour sites contribute to VTE are not well established4,38–40, and their distinct effects on CRT remain unclear14,16. We increased the sample size to a larger scale and extended the tumour types to a broader range, including tumours in the mediastinum, oesophagus, pleura, peritoneum, limb, trunk wall, and testis, making the risk factor map of CRT more comprehensive. We also revealed for the first time that tumours in the thoracic cavity are associated with the highest risk of CRT, followed by those in the abdominal cavity and pelvic cavity. There are two main mechanisms that can explain these results. First, the thrombosis-promoting factors released by the tumour38,41 are diluted as the distance between the tumour site and catheter increases. Thus, tumours in the thoracic cavity have greater effects than those in the abdominal cavity and pelvic cavity. Second, the obstruction effect on the heart and superior vena cava is greater for thoracic cavity tumours than for other tumours42. The obstruction effect increases the pressure in the heart and lowers the blood flow velocity in the vessels, thus significantly increasing the risk of CRT. Prevention strategies, such as antithrombotic drugs, may be applied to these patients in clinical practice.

We also systematically explored the risk of systemic treatments and the relative risk of CRT. Nutrition was associated with a high risk of CRT, which is in agreement with the findings of previous studies43,44. An interpretation of these findings is that nutritional status is associated with surgery and late-stage disease, as patients in this stage lack exercise and are highly static. The high risk of CRT associated with antiangiogenic therapies also agrees with the findings of previous studies2,17,45,46. Chemotherapy can damage the vascular endothelium and lower the levels of natural anticoagulants35, and it is generally considered a high risk factor for CRT2,5–7,17,45–50. However, the relative ranking of the risk of CRT among various chemotherapy types is not well established. The present results indicated that alkylating agents, including nitrogen mustards and platinum, were high-risk chemotherapies for CRT. Antimetabolites were also associated with a high risk for CRT. DNA topoisomerase inhibitors were associated with a mediate risk for CRT, and microtubule inhibitors were associated with a relatively low risk for CRT. These findings may aid in clinical decision-making concerning systemic treatment regimens for tumours.

Our study had several limitations. First, few catheter diameters were considered during model training; only a 4Fr diameter catheter was used in centres 1 and 2. Moreover, few catheter lengths in patient veins were considered, with length differences observed between only PICCs and other catheter types. Thus, diameter and length variables were not included in the model and should be considered in further studies. Second, this study was observational, and whether SM-CRT functions benefit clinical outcomes warrants the validation of interventional studies. Finally, the cutoff values for crank risk stratification and high-risk period definitions need to be further optimised in real-world scenarios.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study demonstrated that machine learning survival models provide not only continuous risk ranking (crank) predictions for identifying patients with high CRT risk but also survival distribution (distr) predictions for optimising the catheterization period. A machine learning survival model with both crank and distr predictions represents a novel approach for the translational application of artificial intelligence models, providing a platform for guiding clinical practice that may be broadly applicable across survival tasks.
