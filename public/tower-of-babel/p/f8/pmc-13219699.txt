Rapid quantitative profiling of kynurenine metabolites in human plasma using LC-MS/MS
Authors: Lian Zhu, Jin Liu, Kailin Li, Yang Wang, Fengjiao Liu, Liu Xie, Yan Zhang, Wei Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tryptophan is an essential amino acid for the human body, and its metabolic processes are closely related to a variety of physiological and pathological processes, including neurodegenerative diseases, immune modulation, and cardiovascular diseases. Simultaneous detection of tryptophan and its nine metabolites is challenging and previous methods may have shortcomings of poor sensitivity, selectivity, and specificity in complex matrices. The technology of LC-MS/MS, with its characteristics of high sensitivity and selective, provides a powerful tool for the detection of biomarkers. A rapid and effective method for the simultaneous determination of kynurenine metabolites (Xanthurenic acid (XA), DL-Tryptophan (TRP), Kynurenine (KYN), 3-Hydroxyanthranilic acid (3-HAA), Quinolinic acid (QUIA), Kynurenic acid (KYNA), 3-Hydroxy-DL-kynurenine (3HK), 2-Picolinic acid (2PA), 5-Hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), and Anthranilic acid (AA)) was established in human plasma using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) technology. Methanol precipitation was used for sample pretreatment, with [2H5]-L-Tryptophan (TRP-D5) as the internal standard. Mass spectrometric detection was performed in positive electrospray ionization mode using multiple reaction monitoring on a triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer. The method’s selectivity, linearity, quantification limit, detection limit, precision, recovery rate, matrix effect, and stability are evaluated. The results show that the linear ranges of 2PA, QUIA, 3HK, 5HT, KYN, 3HAA, TRP, XA, KYNA and AA are 0.25-50, 0.9–225, 1.2–120, 1.4–700, 2-2000, 0.15–150, 180-180000, 0.1–100, 0.1–100, and 0.2–100 ng/mL, respectively; all analytes have good linear relationships with a determination coefficient R2 greater than 0.99. The precision relative standard deviation (RSD) is less than 15%; the spiked recovery rate is 87.54%-107.43%, with an RSD of 2.76%-12.19%. Additionally, the matrix effects for 10 analytes are summarized. At all three QC levels, the matrix effect in plasma is 88.44% to 107.85%, and the RSD is 2.97%-14.62%. These results indicate that there is no significant matrix effect. Stability was evaluated by comparing the measured concentrations after storage with the initial concentrations and expressed as percentages of the initial values. All analytes remained within 85–115% of the initial concentrations under the tested conditions. This study describes the development and validation of a rapid, sensitive, and robust UHPLC–MS/MS method for the simultaneous quantification of ten kynurenine pathway metabolites—including the precursor tryptophan, key intermediates, and neurotoxic/neuroprotective end products-in human plasma. The method employs a simple protein precipitation procedure, a single deuterated internal standard (TRP-D5), and a 13-minute chromatographic run on a C8 column, providing adequate separation of metabolites with diverse physicochemical properties.

Introduction

Tryptophan (TRP) is one of the nine essential amino acids in humans and is primarily metabolized through the kynurenine pathway (KP), producing several biologically active metabolites. Key metabolites such as kynurenine (KYN), kynurenic acid (KYNA), quinolinic acid (QUIA), and 3-hydroxykynurenine (3HK) have been implicated in neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders. Therefore, simultaneous quantification of KP metabolites in biological matrices is important for understanding disease mechanisms and identifying potential biomarkers1. TRP has two main metabolic pathways in the body, with only 5% along the 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) metabolism, and the rest are metabolized through the KP2. In the kynurenine pathway, tryptophan is converted to kynurenine through the action of the rate-limiting enzymes tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase. Kynurenine can subsequently be metabolized into several downstream metabolites including KYNA, 3-HK, and quinolinic acid through different enzymatic pathways in the central nervous system3–6. To understand the pathophysiology of KPs in various diseases and discover potential diagnostic biomarkers, it is important to detection them simultaneously. The challenges of simultaneous detection of KPs are the wide range of polarities and functional groups, significant differences in endogenous levels in biological specimens, matrix effects, selectivity, and repeatability of detection. Therefore, it is necessary to develop a selective, sensitive, rapid and reliable method for the quantitative determination of all KPs in high sample throughput.

Common analytical approaches for tryptophan and kynurenine metabolites include high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) coupled with UV-Vis detection (including diode-array detectors), fluorescence detection, electrochemical detection, and tandem mass spectrometry7–16. Several methods for quantitative analysis of KPs in biological samples have been developed so far, including HPLC-MS/MS platforms. Several liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) methods have been reported for the quantification of KP metabolites in biological matrices. Previous studies have attempted to quantify metabolites in the KP, most approaches suffer from limited coverage in complex biological matrices. However, these methods may suffer from limited analytical selectivity due to interference from endogenous matrix components, particularly when non-MS detection techniques are used. In contrast, LC-MS/MS provides improved sensitivity and selectivity and has therefore become the preferred analytical technique for the detection of tryptophan metabolites in biological samples. In recent times, tandem mass spectrometry has high sensitivity and excellent selectivity, making it the preferred method for detecting experimental biological matrix samples. Previous investigated reported that the use of LC-MS/MS to study human and rat plasma, with the measured concentrations of tryptophan and kynurenine being 0.018 to 0.282, and 0.017 to 0.276 µmol/L, respectively, with a limited applicable measurement range; the lowest quantification limits for tryptophan and kynurenine are 100 and 25 µg/L, respectively, and residual matrices can affect method sensitivity17,18. However, the wide polarity range and large concentration differences among KP metabolites in biological samples significantly complicate their simultaneous detection. In this study, we developed an LC-MS/MS method for the simultaneous quantification of ten KP metabolites spanning the entire pathway, from the precursor TRP to downstream metabolites.

In this work, we established and validated an efficient LC-MS/MS method for simultaneous determination of most of the related compound metabolism in the kynurenine metabolic pathway in human plasma. The analytical method is suitable to investigate the central and peripheral changes of detectable metabolites in human plasma. Given the biological significance of kynurenine metabolism. This analytical approach provides a useful tool for profiling kynurenine pathway metabolites in human plasma and may facilitate future studies investigating alterations in kynurenine metabolism.

Experimental

Chemicals and materials

The chemical reference standards of Xanthurenic acid (XA), DL-Tryptophan (TRP), Kynurenine (KYN), 3-Hydroxyanthranilic acid (3-HAA), Quinolinic acid (QUIA), Kynurenic acid (KYNA), 3-Hydroxy-DL-kynurenine (3HK), [2H5]-L-Tryptophan (TRP-D5), 2-Picolinic acid (2PA) and 5-Hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) were obtained from Chengdu Alfa Biotechnology Co.,Ltd. (Chengdu, China); Anthranilic acid (AA) was obtained from Glpbio Technology (Shanghai, China). The purities of all the reference standards were above 98% according to HPLC-UV analysis. The MS grade formic acid, methanol and acetonitrile was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd. (USA). The ultra-pure water was obtained from Guangzhou Watsons Food & Beverage Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Other solvents were of an analytical grade supplied by Aladdin Industrial Corporation. Their structures are presented in Fig. 1.

Preparation of standard solutions

The selection of solvents for preparing individual stock solutions was based on systematic solubility. Due to the diverse physicochemical properties of the ten analytes, a single universal solvent was not feasible. 3-Hydroxykynurenine (3HK), xanthurenic acid (XA), kynurenine (KYN), and kynurenic acid (KYNA) are dissolved in a 0.1 M sodium hydroxide solution. Tryptophan (TRP), quinolinic acid (QUIA), and picolinic acid (2PA) are dissolved in a 50% acetonitrile solution. 3-Hydroxyanthranilic acid (3HAA) is dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). 5-Hydroxytryptamine (5HT) is dissolved in an aqueous solution. Anthranilic acid (AA) is dissolved in a methanol solution. The internal standard (IS), specifically TRP-D5, are prepared in a 50% acetonitrile solution at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. For sample analysis, the IS is mixed and diluted with methanol to a final concentration of 200 ng/mL. Appropriate amounts of each stock solution are mixed and diluted with methanol to form a mixed stock solution, which is then further diluted with methanol to obtain a series of working solutions at suitable concentrations.

Quality control samples of low, medium, and high concentrations are prepared to assess precision and accuracy. Plasma is mixed with an appropriate amount of the working solution and vortexed for 30 s to prepare samples for mass spectrometry analysis for recovery and matrix effect tests. They are then extracted as described in the “Sample Preparation” section.

Sample preparation

Blood samples from patients and healthy volunteers are collected by psychiatric nurses, with a total of 10 mL, stored in EDTA anticoagulant tubes at -4 °C, and centrifuged, aliquoted, and frozen on the same day. Blood samples are centrifuged at 4 °C at 3000 revolutions per minute for 15 min, and the supernatant, which is the plasma, is placed in a freezing tube and stored in the psychiatric biological sample bank of Xiangya Second Hospital of Central South University at -80 °C. Plasma samples are transported in dry ice, with a transportation time not exceeding 4 h, and the processing of blood samples is completed at Hunan University of Medicine. Take 100 µL of plasma and dilute it three times with methanol. After adding 10 µL of the IS mixture, vortex for 1 min, and centrifuge at 4 °C at 12,000 rpm (13,800×g, rotor radius 8.5 cm) for 20 min to precipitate the proteins. Take the supernatant and dry it with a nitrogen blower, redissolve it with 100 µL of pure water, and centrifuge again at 4 °C at 12,000 rpm (13,800×g, rotor radius 8.5 cm) for 20 min, then take the supernatant to the injection vial. The supernatant is injected for LC-MS/MS analysis. Reconstitution in 100 µL of pure water, rather than a mobile phase-mimicking solvent, was strategically chosen to leverage the low elution strength of the injection solvent. Under the initial chromatographic conditions (2% organic phase B), the sample solvent (100% water) promotes on-column focusing of the polar analytes, thereby minimizing band broadening and improving peak symmetry.

LC–MS/MS conditions

Sample analysis was performed using a Waters AcQuity high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled with a Xevo TQ-S cronos triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Milford, USA). MassLynx software was utilized for instrument control, data acquisition, and analysis.

An EclipsePlus RRHD C8 column (150 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) with a high-pressure pre-filter column was used for chromatographic separation. Solvent A (0.1% formic acid solution) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid solution in a 2:1 ratio of methanol to acetonitrile) were used as mobile phases with the flow rate of 0.25 mL/min under a gradient elution: 0–1.5 min, 2% B; 1.5–2.5 min, 5% B ; 2.5–3 min, 8% B; 3–4 min, 15% B; 4–5 min, 25% B; 5–6 min, 35% B ; 6–9 min, 55% B; 9–9.5 min, 2% B; 9.5–13 min, 2% B. The column temperature was 30 ℃ and the injected sample volume was 2 µL. The sample vials were maintained at 4 ℃ in a thermostatic autosampler. The quantification of all analytes was performed using positive- multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) method. The optimal MS parameters were as follows: capillary voltage, 3.0 kV; nebulizing gas flow rate, 3.0 L/min; heating gas flow rate, 10.0 L/min; drying gas flow rate, 10.0 L/min; source temperature, 300 ℃; heat block temperature, 400 ℃. Method validation

Selectivity

Selectivity refers to the ability to accurately and specifically determine the analyte in a biological matrix. To ensure the selectivity of the method, all metabolites in pure solution samples and actual samples were analyzed at the quantification limit and their chromatograms were compared. Comparing calibration curves prepared in plasma and neat solvent; the slopes were within 15%, confirming no significant matrix effect on selectivity.

Linearity

Dilute the mixed standard solution containing 10 analytes and IS to a fixed concentration in sequence for linearity assessment. Using the ratio of the analyte peak area to IS, construct a calibration curve for each analyte. The mass concentration of the analyte is taken as the horizontal coordinate x, and the ratio of the drug peak area to the internal standard peak area is taken as the vertical coordinate y. A weighted least squares method is used for linear regression analysis (with a weight coefficient of 1/x2).The acceptable linearity was tested using the determination coefficient (R2).

Limit of detection and limit of quantitation

Follow ICH M10 guidelines, defining LLOQ as the lowest concentration with precision ≤ 20% and accuracy within 80–120%, demonstrated using spiked plasma samples. The limits of quantification (LOQ) and detection (LOD) were assessed at the concentration where the quantifier transition occurred: S/N > 10, S/N > 3. The signal of the analyte in the LOQ and LOD samples should be at least 10 times that of the blank sample signal.

Precision

The precision of the method were established by analyzing three independent analytical runs, each containing 6 low, medium, and high concentration QC level validation working solutions. Precision was represented by the relative standard deviation (RSD). In each run, the measured concentration of the QC samples should be within 15% of the nominal value.

Recovery and matrix effect

The recovery rate is assessed by comparing the areas of the analytes in the low, medium, and high concentration (n = 6) pre-spiked samples (samples spiked with QC solution before extraction) and post-spiked samples (samples spiked with QC solution after extraction). Stability

The stability of medium-concentration drug-containing plasma samples was examined under different conditions: at room temperature for 24 h, at -80 °C for 30 days, and after three freeze-thaw cycles at -80 °C. Each concentration of the sample was tested in parallel six times.

Ethical approval and informed consent

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Clinical Research Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University. All participants provided written informed consent prior to sample collection. All procedures were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All required ethical information while adding comprehensive details including: DSM-5 diagnostic criteria confirmed by two independent psychiatrists, specific inclusion criteria (age 18–65, no comorbid psychiatric disorders, medication-free for 4 weeks prior to sampling), exclusion criteria (inflammatory diseases, autoimmune disorders, pregnancy).

Results and discussion

Mass spectrometric and liquid chromatographic optimization

The triple quadrupole mass spectrometer operating in MRM (Multiple Reaction Monitoring) mode was used for electrospray ionization. All of the compounds were studied in both the positive ion modes. The MassLynx software was employed to automatically optimize operational parameters such as ionization energy and collision energy, resulting in the richest relative abundance of precursor and product ions. Additionally, the selection of specific product ions was helpful for the quantification of each ion. Mass spectrometry parameters were optimized to ensure the best detection response and specificity, as detailed in the “LC-MS/MS Conditions” section.

To enhance the accuracy and reproducibility of quantitative analysis. Compared with other internal standards that are structurally similar to the analytes, isotopically labeled internal standards share the same chemical properties with the analytes of interest, and the interference of the matrix on their ionization is similar, which can minimize issues arising from stability, extraction recovery rates, or ionization efficiency. TRP-D5 serves as an internal standard to some extent to correct for matrix effects; however, the main disadvantage of this method is the high cost of isotopically labeled internal standards and the difficulty in obtaining the most suitable isotopically labeled internal standards. Since tryptophan and its metabolites have certain structural similarities, the use of TRP-D5 as an internal standard (IS) can reduce errors caused by sample pretreatment and ionization processes to a certain degree. In this study, only one isotope-labeled internal standard TRP-D5, was employed for the simultaneous quantification of all ten analytes. We acknowledge that using a single IS for chemically diverse metabolites with varying polarities and retention times is a limitation, as it may not ideally correct for differences in recovery or matrix effects across the board. Ideally, matched stable isotope-labeled internal standards for each analyte (e.g., KYN-d4, 3HK-d3) would provide the highest accuracy. However, due to practical constraints regarding the commercial availability and high cost of multiple labeled standards, particularly for less common metabolites like XA or 2PA, a universal IS strategy was adopted. To scientifically validate this approach, we rigorously evaluated the suitability of TRP-D5. As demonstrated in the recovery rates (87.54%-107.43%) and matrix effects (88.44%-107.85%) for all analytes fell within acceptable ranges across three QC levels. These results indicate that, despite structural differences, TRP-D5 can adequately compensate for variations during sample preparation and ionization for this specific panel under the established conditions. Nevertheless, we recommend that future studies aiming for regulatory-grade quantification consider implementing multiple class-specific or matched isotope-labeled internal standards to further minimize potential bias, especially for early-eluting or highly polar compounds such as QUIA and 2PA.

To optimize the sample pretreatment process, different organic solvents (methanol, acetonitrile, acetonitrile with hydrochloric acid) and solid-phase extraction cartridges for protein precipitation were investigated. The final results showed that using methanol for protein precipitation had a simple procedure, high efficiency, sensitivity that met the analytical requirements, and good reproducibility. To improve the solubility of the samples to be analyzed and to reduce the impact of the reconstitution system on chromatographic separation, the role of the initial mobile phase, different mobile phase ratios, and pure water in the reconstitution process were examined. The final results indicated that reconstituting with pure water resulted in good peak shape and no interference from endogenous impurities, showing good specificity.

To optimize the mobile phase pH for comprehensive chromatographic performance, three volatile buffer systems were systematically evaluated during method development: acidic (0.1% formic acid, apparent pH ~ 2.7), near-neutral (0.1% ammonium formate, pH ~ 6.5), and basic (0.1% ammonium hydroxide, pH ~ 9.0). The acidic conditions (0.1% formic acid) were ultimately selected.

To further improve the chromatographic separation and sensitivity of metabolites, this method was developed after extensive testing of different LC parameters. Initially, five different analytical columns were tested for metabolite separation: ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm), ACQUITY BEH C18 (50 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm), EclipsePlus RRHD C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm), and EclipsePlus RRHD C8 column (150 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm). Results indicated that the T3 and C18 columns could not adequately retain all analytes. Here, considering the retention of polar analytes and peak shape, the C8 was chosen as the analytical column. Although tryptophan and its metabolites have different physicochemical properties (varying hydrophilicity or lipophilicity), what they have in common is that all molecules have relatively hydrophobic structures, including indole rings or benzene rings, and different functional groups (carboxyl, hydroxyl, amino) improve the molecules’ hydrophilicity and aid in their ionization, enhancing the mass spectrometry response19. Therefore, the pH value of the mobile phase is a crucial factor for the retention behavior of the molecules. Based on this, formic acid (0.1%) is added to the mobile phase to maintain the system under acidic conditions, resulting in good peak shape. The addition of formic acid slightly prolongs the retention times of the substances, improves the separation effect, and to some extent, increases ionization.By comparing methanol and acetonitrile as organic eluents, the analytes and IS with 0.1% formic acid solution in a 2:1 ratio of methanol to acetonitrile as the mobile phase had lower background signals, higher separation efficiency, and better peak shape. Further testing and optimization of flow rate and column temperature were performed to ensure good chromatographic behavior for all target compounds. Typically, under optimal mass spectrometry and chromatographic conditions, the MRM chromatograms of 10 endogenous substances and 1 IS at the LOQ in the standard solution were obtained (Fig. 2).

Comparison of chromatograms between blank biological samples (healthy donors) and standard solutions demonstrated that the method is selective and discriminative under chromatographic conditions, with MRM transitions observed at the retention times.

Application of the method to human plasma

The LC-MS/MS method established in this paper was used to determine the concentrations of 2PA, QUIA, 3HK, 5HT, KYN, 3HAA, TRP, XA, KYNA and AA in plasma samples collected from 20 cases The distribution of kynurenine metabolites in plasma is shown in Table 7. This method is simple, sensitive, and fast, meeting the requirements for the analysis of biological samples, and is suitable for the determination of the concentrations ofkynurenine metabolites products in bipolar disorder (BP), generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), major depressive disorder (MDD), and healthy controls (HC) human plasma.The validated LC-MS/MS method was applied to quantify the 10 KP metabolites in plasma samples from patients with BP (n = 5), GAD (n = 5), MDD (n = 5), and healthy controls (HC, n = 5). The raw concentrations were presented in Table 7, and the statistical comparisons are summarized in Table 8. Due to the limited sample size, non-parametric statistical methods were employed. A Kruskal-Wallis test revealed significant differences among the four groups for three metabolites: kynurenine (KYN, p = 0.048), quinolinic acid (QUIA, p = 0.039), and 3-hydroxykynurenine (3HK, p = 0.027). Levels were significantly higher in the MDD group compared to the BP group (p < 0.05). Although KYN levels in both MDD and GAD groups appeared elevated relative to HC, these differences did not reach statistical significance after correction, likely due to the small sample size. The MDD group exhibited significantly higher QUIA concentrations compared to both the BP group (p < 0.05) and the HC group (p < 0.05). This elevation of QUIA, a potent neurotoxic NMDA receptor agonist, in MDD patients aligns with the neuroinflammation hypothesis of depression. The MDD group showed significantly higher 3HK levels compared to the GAD (p < 0.05) and HC (p < 0.05) groups. As 3HK is a metabolite associated with oxidative stress, this finding further supports the presence of heightened inflammatory and oxidative burden in major depressive disorder.

Discussion

The present study developed and validated a LC-MS/MS method for the simultaneous quantification of ten metabolites involved in the kynurenine pathway in human plasma. Because these metabolites differ substantially in polarity and endogenous concentration levels, their simultaneous determination in biological matrices remains analytically challenging.

During method development, chromatographic conditions and sample preparation procedures were optimized to achieve reliable separation and extraction efficiency. The EclipsePlus RRHD C8 column provided improved retention for highly polar metabolites while maintaining adequate separation of less polar compounds. Methanol-based protein precipitation was selected for sample preparation due to its simplicity and satisfactory recovery, consistent with previous LC-MS/MS assays for tryptophan metabolites20,21.

The selection of internal standards is another important consideration in multiplex LC-MS/MS assays. Although isotope-labeled internal standards for each analyte would be ideal, they are not available for several kynurenine metabolites. Previous studies have therefore adopted simplified internal standard strategies when supported by appropriate validation experiments22,23. In the present study, TRP-D5 was selected as a universal internal standard due to its structural similarity to several analytes and its stable chromatographic behavior. Method validation demonstrated acceptable recovery (87.54-107.43%), minimal matrix effects (88.44-101.13%), and satisfactory precision (RSD < 15%), indicating that TRP-D5 provided adequate correction for analytical variability under the optimized conditions.

Compared with previously reported LC-MS/MS assays, many analytical methods primarily focus on a limited subset of metabolites such as tryptophan and kynurenine20,21. In contrast, the present method enables simultaneous quantification of ten metabolites across the kynurenine pathway within a relatively short chromatographic runtime. This analytical configuration provides broader metabolite coverage while maintaining a simple and efficient analytical workflow suitable for metabolite profiling studies. For example, de Jong et al., developed an automated online solid-phase extraction LC-MS/MS method for the quantification of tryptophan, kynurenine, and 3-hydroxykynurenine in human plasma. While the method demonstrated good sensitivity and selectivity, it focused on only three metabolites and required an online SPE setup, which may limit its applicability in routine laboratories24. In addition, some previously reported assays rely on relatively complex sample preparation procedures, such as solid-phase extraction or online SPE systems, which may reduce analytical throughput and limit routine clinical applicability. More recent LC-MS/MS methods have improved analytical performance, but many still focus primarily on tryptophan and kynurenine or a small subset of kynurenine metabolites. In contrast, the method developed in the present study enables the simultaneous quantification of ten kynurenine pathway metabolites in human plasma within a 13-minute chromatographic run, using a simple methanol protein precipitation procedure. This analytical configuration provides a practical balance between metabolite coverage, analytical throughput, and sample preparation simplicity, which is advantageous for exploratory metabolomic studies and clinical research involving large sample cohorts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a rapid LC-MS/MS method was developed and validated for the simultaneous quantification of ten kynurenine pathway metabolites in human plasma. The method demonstrated satisfactory sensitivity, precision, and reproducibility. This analytical workflow may serve as a useful tool for exploratory investigations of kynurenine metabolism in psychiatric disorders.
