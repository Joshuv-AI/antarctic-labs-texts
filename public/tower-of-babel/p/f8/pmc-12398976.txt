The influence of cancer treatments on long-term psychological outcomes and quality of life in breast cancer patients
Authors: Yuan Yang, Yanjie Zhao, Kaimin Yao, Wengao Li, Hengwen Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate changes in depression, anxiety, fear of cancer recurrence (FCR), pain, and quality of life (QOL) over 48 months after discharge from primary treatment among breast cancer patients, and to examine the effects of different cancer treatment modalities (i.e., surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy) on long-term physical and psychological outcomes and QOL.MethodsThis is a longitudinal prospective study. All participants completed a battery of questionnaires (PHQ-9, GAD-7, FCR-7, MPQ-VAS, and WHOQOL-BREF) at baseline, 12, 24, and 48 months after discharge. Long-term psychological outcomes and QOL were analyzed using a mixed model repeated measures (MMRM) approach. Group comparisons were expressed as least square (LS) mean differences with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsA total of 143 women with breast cancer were recruited for the study. The mean age was 42.27 years (SD = 7.75). The mean score of PHQ decreased from 3.50 at baseline (T1) to 1.24 at the 48-month (T4). Anxiety levels dropped from 4.09 at T1 to 2.74 at T4, FCR decreased from 21.72 at T1 to 18.70 at T4, and pain levels decreased from 12.96 at T1 to 11.50 at T4. Participants’ QOL increased from 87.77 at baseline to 93.42 at the 12-month (T2), then remained stable thereafter (T3 and T4). The LS mean difference in GAD-7 from baseline to endpoint between participants with or without mastectomy was -2.0996 (95% CI: -3.4842, -0.7150).ConclusionsBreast cancer patients’ depression, anxiety, FCR, and pain levels showed a decreasing trend over 48 months after discharge, while patients’ QOL improved at the 12-month follow-up and remained stable throughout the study period. Patients with mastectomy experienced higher levels of anxiety than those with partial lumpectomy after 48 months post-surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14667-y.

Background

According to the World Health Organization’s 2024 report, there were 2.3 million women diagnosed with breast cancer and 670,000 deaths globally [1]. The global burden of breast cancer has increased significantly, with incident cases rising from 875,657 in 1990 to 2,121,564 in 2021 [2]. Breast cancer is now the most common cancer and the fifth leading cause of cancer deaths among Chinese women [3]. In 2020, 416,371 Chinese women were diagnosed with breast cancer, accounting for 18% of the total number of new cases worldwide [4]. Improvements in diagnostic methods, screening, and cancer treatment have increased breast cancer survival rates in recent years [5]. However, up to 90% of survivors experience unexpected long-term physical and psychological sequelae as a result of cancer treatment [6].

Long-term physical outcomes can include anatomic changes, chronic pain, phantom breast pain, lymphedema, fatigue, menopausal disorders, cognitive dysfunction, sleep problems, polyneuropathy, and sexual issues [6, 7]. Psychologically, a systematic review found increased risks for anxiety, depression, suicide, and neurocognitive and sexual dysfunction in breast cancer survivors compared to women without a prior cancer diagnosis [8]. Relevant research has found that breast cancer patients are under considerable psychological stress, which was related to health-related quality of life (QOL) in terms of body image, future perspective, side effects of systemic therapy, breast symptoms, arm symptoms, and hair loss [9]. A systematic review also found that anxiety, depression, self-esteem, and psychosis had a significant and negative impact on the lives of breast cancer patients, and these psychological changes alter the daily lives of these patients [10].

Depression and anxiety are the two most common psychological symptoms reported by breast cancer survivors. A prospective study found that the five-year prevalence of anxiety and/or depression was 55.4%, with a peak prevalence for anxiety before treatment at 38.0%, and a one-year prevalence for depression at 13.1% [11]. Breidenbach et al. reported that compared with 40 weeks after cancer diagnosis, about one-third of patients with breast cancer had higher levels of depression and anxiety 5 to 6 years after the diagnosis [12]. Another five-year observational cohort study found that nearly 50% of women with early breast cancer reported depression and anxiety after diagnosis, with 25% experiencing these symptoms in the second, third, and fourth years, and 15% in the fifth year [13]. This suggests that depression and anxiety levels in breast cancer patients decrease over a five-year period. A systematic review also found a higher prevalence of symptoms of depression among breast cancer survivors than among the general female population, persistent over more than 5 years after diagnosis [14]. Furthermore, fear of cancer recurrence (FCR) is highly prevalent among long-term breast cancer survivors [15], and previous studies have confirmed associations between FCR, depression, anxiety, and poorer QOL [15–17]. Similar to anxiety and depression trends, Schapira et al. found that the majority of cancer patients with low or moderate FCR levels showed a trend toward resolution over time [18]. Gold et al. found that almost 50% of the patients with breast cancer reported both anxiety and depressive symptoms, and higher levels of anxiety with or without subsyndromal depressive symptoms were associated with increased FCR, hopelessness, uncertainty, loss of control, and a decrease in life satisfaction [19].

Pain is commonly reported by patients after the treatment of breast cancer. A systematic review found that the pooled prevalence of cancer pain was 29.8% post-surgery, and 27.3% post-radiotherapy. About 21.8% of the breast cancer survivors reported persistent pain after breast cancer treatment [20]. Pain compromises the QOL of patients with breast cancer, particularly those with advanced stages of the disease [21]. A 4-year longitudinal study found a slight increase in pain symptoms during the 4-year observation period, and pain severity was mainly predicted by psychological factors (e.g., depression at baseline, life events in first 12 months), while medical factors (e.g., surgery type) played a smaller role [22]. Previous studies found that breast cancer survivors with persistent pain tend to experience greater anxiety and depressive symptoms [23]. A meta-analysis found that lymphedema, axillary lymph node dissection, chemotherapy, hormone therapy, and radiotherapy were significantly associated with higher odds for the development of chronic pain, with lymphedema being the most significant risk factor [24].

Modern breast cancer treatment includes combinations of surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, and anti-HER2-targeted therapy, tailored to the pathological type of the tumor [25]. Surgery is a crucial component of multidisciplinary breast cancer treatment [26]. Depending on the disease stage and type of the tumor, either lumpectomy (removal of the lump only) or mastectomy (surgical removal of the entire breast) may be performed [27]. A meta-analysis indicated that breast cancer patients tend to have a significant reduction in depression (SMD: −0.14; 95% CI: −0.25 to −0.02) and anxiety (SMD: −0.42; 95% CI: −0.56 to −0.28) following breast cancer surgery [28]. Lidia et al. found that about one-third of young patients (age < 40 years) with breast cancer had FCR that was severe and did not improve or worsen in the 5 years after surgery [29]. Evidence suggests that the outcomes of lumpectomy plus radiation and mastectomy are essentially equivalent in early-stage breast cancer [30]. Some studies indicated that psychological disorders (e.g., depression, anxiety) are more common after lumpectomy than mastectomy [31, 32], while others suggested better long-term psychological outcomes for patients who undergo lumpectomy [33–35]. However, other studies reported minimal and non-significant differences in psychological outcomes between the two surgical options [36, 37]. A meta-analysis indicated that QOL tends to be better after lumpectomy due to improved body image, future perspectives, and fewer systemic side effects [38]. Many studies have supported this conclusion [37, 39, 40].

Radiotherapy and chemotherapy are also essential components of multimodal cancer treatment. Following curative resection, adjuvant radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy can enhance disease-free and overall survival [41, 42]. Numerous studies have reported psychological issues such as anxiety, depression, and distress during radiotherapy or chemotherapy [43–45]. Piroth et al. found that patients’ anxiety peaked immediately before radiation therapy and dropped significantly on the day treatment was completed [46]. Additionally, chemotherapy has been closely associated with a fatigue-depression-anxiety symptom cluster among breast cancer patients, leading to significant declines in QOL [47]. Previous study found that most breast patients experienced a decline in FCR during and after radiotherapy, one possible reason is that radiotherapy is usually the final step in their treatment of the disease, and patients may start to feel confident in their efforts to return to normal life [48].

To our knowledge, no current studies in China examine the effects of different cancer treatment modalities on long-term psychological outcomes, pain and QOL in breast cancer patients. Most previous studies have utilized cross-sectional designs, with few longitudinal studies specifically investigating the relationship between cancer treatment and patients’ physical and psychological well-being. This study aims to: (1) investigate changes in depression, anxiety, FCR, pain, and QOL over 48 months after discharge from primary treatment among breast cancer patients; and (2) explore the long-term psychological outcomes, pain and QOL of breast cancer patients treated with different modalities (i.e., mastectomy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy). By systematically investigating and summarizing current knowledge, this study seeks to provide insights into the influence of cancer treatment on long-term physical and psychological outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This longitudinal study consisted of four measurements at baseline (on the day of discharge after primary treatment, T1), 12 months (T2), 24 months (T3), and 48 months (endpoint, T4) after baseline assessment. The study protocol was approved by the Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital Ethics Committee (ref No: 2018295H(R1)) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. The informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All participants were breast cancer patients recruited from Jan 2020 to Mar 2020 at the cancer center in Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital. Participants were eligible if they: (1) were adult (above 18 years of age); (2) had a diagnosis of breast cancer; and (3) were able to read, write and understand Mandarin or Cantonese. Patients were excluded if they: (1) were underage; (2) had any level of cognitive impairment (e.g., dementia, significant memory/attention deficits); or (3) were not receiving any active anticancer therapies (including but not limited to surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, etc.).

Study procedure

Participants were approached on the day of discharge (baseline, T1) by a trained researcher (an oncologist) who explained the study purpose and asked if they would like to participate. For those interested, a written informed consent form was provided. After signing the consent, participants completed a personal information collection form and a series of standardized measurement questionnaires (PHQ-9, GAD-7, FCR-7, MPQ-VAS, and WHOQOL). Participants were assured they could withdraw at any time. At 12 (T2), 24 (T3), and 48 months (T4) after the baseline assessment, participants were contacted again via telephone or WeChat to assess their depression, anxiety, FCR, pain, and QOL. If a participant did not respond after three attempts, they were marked as ‘loss of follow-up’.

Measurements

Basic demographic and clinical data

Participants’ demographic and clinical data (e.g., gender, age, marital status, employment status, educational level, monthly salary, comorbidities, cancer treatment, family cancer history, and suicidal ideation) were collected using a specially designed case record form. Suicidal ideation was assessed with a standardized Yes/No question [49, 50]: Have you ever thought about killing yourself in the last two weeks? Those answering “Yes” were classified as having current suicidality. For participants who endorsed current suicidal ideation (answering “Yes” to the standardized question), our protocol involved immediate evaluation by the on-site clinical psychologist or psychiatrist to assess suicide risk severity. High-risk cases were provided with crisis intervention, including safety planning and urgent referral to psychiatric services, while moderate-risk cases received additional psychological support and close monitoring.

Depressive symptoms

The Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) is a self-rating scale that measures depression-related symptoms experienced in the last two weeks. It contains 9 items (e.g., item 1: Little interest or pleasure in doing things), each scored on a four-point scale from 0 to 3, with a total score ranging from 0 to 27. A total score of 5 or more indicates depressive symptoms [51]. The Chinese version of PHQ-9 has demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties [52].

Anxiety symptoms

The General Anxiety Disorder Questionnaire (GAD-7) is a self-report scale used to evaluate anxiety symptoms over the last two week. It contains 7 items (e.g., item 1: Feeling nervous, anxious or on edge), each scored on a four-point scale from 0 to 3, with a total score ranging from 0 to 21. A total score of 5 or more indicates anxiety symptoms [53]. The Chinese version of GAD-7 has an internal consistency of 0.91 [54].

Fear of cancer recurrence

The Fear of Cancer Recurrence scale (FCR-7) assesses anxiety and worry associated with the fear of cancer returning. The FCR-7 has an internal consistency of 0.92. It contains 7 items (e.g., item 1: I am afraid that my cancer may recur), with total scores ranging from 7 to 40 [55]. A score of 27 or more suggests high FCR. The Chinese FCR-7 has also shown satisfactory psychometric properties (Cronbach’s alpha 0.87) [56].

Pain

The McGill Pain Questionnaire-Visual Analogue Scale (MPQ-VAS) is widely used for pain measurement and was validated in Chinese in 2013 [57]. The MPQ-VAS specifically assessed the participant’s current pain levels on the day of assessment. VAS scores range from 0 (no pain) to 100 (worst possible pain), with a score of 40 or more indicating the presence of pain [58].

Quality of life

The World Health Organization Quality of Life Questionnaire (WHOQOL-BREF) assesses a person’s perception of physical health (7 items), psychological health (6 items), social relationships (3 items), and environmental health (8 items) [59]. It consists of 26 items, with each scored from 1 to 5. Scores are linearly transformed to a 0 to 100 scale, with higher scores indicating better QOL. The Chinese version has excellent psychometric properties [60].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to obtain frequencies, means, and standard deviations (SD) where appropriate. Longitudinal changes in outcomes (PHQ-9, GAD-7, FCR-7, WHOQOL, and pain scores) were analyzed using a mixed-model repeated-measures (MMRM) approach. The analysis compared participants who underwent mastectomy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy to those who did not, adjusting for age (categorized by 35 years), marital status, employment status, and baseline values of the dependent variables as covariates. Fixed effects included cancer treatment, time, and the treatment-by-time interaction. Group comparisons were expressed as least square (LS) mean differences with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) and SPSS version 21.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 143 women with breast cancer participated in the study (Supplementary Fig. 1). Table 1 displays the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants. The mean age was 42.27 years (SD = 7.75). At baseline, 121 women (84.6%) underwent mastectomy, 124 (86.7%) received chemotherapy, and 128 (89.5%) experienced radiotherapy. Most participants were married (n = 121, 84.6%), had a high school or college diploma (n = 142, 99.3%), and reported physical comorbidities (n = 94, 65.7%). Additionally, 110 women (76.9%) reported no family cancer history and no suicidal ideation.

Changes of depression, anxiety, FCR, pain and QOL

The mean score for depression decreased from 3.50 (SD = 2.83) at baseline (T1) to 2.66 (SD = 2.96) at the 12-month follow-up (T2), 1.86 (SD = 2.57) at the 24-month assessment (T3), and 1.24 (SD = 1.82) at the 48-month follow-up (T4). Anxiety levels dropped from 4.09 (SD = 3.52) at baseline to 3.68 (SD = 3.96) at T2, 3.40 (SD = 3.06) at T3, and 2.74 (SD = 2.81) at T4. Patients’ FCR decreased from 21.72 (SD = 3.95) at baseline to 18.70 (SD = 1.65) at T4. Pain levels also decreased from 12.96 (SD = 15.01) at baseline to 11.50 (SD = 13.49) at T4. Patients’ QOL improved from 87.77 (SD = 13.17) at baseline to 93.42 (SD = 13.09) at T2, remaining stable at T3 and T4 (Table 2, and Supplementary Fig. 2).

Influence of cancer treatment

The LS estimates at T4 and mean differences from baseline to T4 in PHQ-9, GAD-7, FCR-7, WHOQOL, and pain score between participants who underwent mastectomy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy to those who did not were shown in Table 3. The LS mean difference in GAD-7 from baseline to T4 between participants with or without mastectomy was -2.0996 (95% CI: -3.4842, -0.7150), indicating that those who underwent mastectomy experienced higher anxiety levels than those who did not after 48 months (Fig. 1). Chemotherapy and radiotherapy showed no significant impact on the long-term psychological outcomes or QOL of breast cancer patients (supple file).

Discussion

Cancer diagnosis and treatment can be traumatic events leading to long-term physical and psychological disturbances. This longitudinal study explored the impact of different cancer treatments on long-term psychological outcomes (depression, anxiety, FCR), pain and QOL among Chinese breast cancer patients. Our findings indicate a decreasing trend in depression, anxiety, and FCR over 48 months post-discharge. Consistent with Burgess’s study, which reported a decline in anxiety and depression from 48 to 15% from the first to the fifth year post-diagnosis [13], our study similarly reflects this trend. A one-year prospective cohort study in Malaysia found significant reductions in anxiety levels at 6 and 12 months post-baseline, while depression levels remained unchanged [61]. In a recent longitudinal study in China, researchers found that 60.7% and 66.0% of the breast cancer women reported stable low-level anxiety and depression throughout the 18-month period after cancer treatment, while 13.0% and 23.3% showed a decreasing trend [62]. Another similar study in China noted stable low levels of FCR in a majority of participants over 18 months, with nearly 20% showing a decreasing trend [63]. Our findings were partially consistent with previous evidence.

Inconsistent with previous studies that reported cancer pain tended to be persistent over time [22, 23, 64], we found a gradual reduction in pain severity over the first 48 months after discharge from primary treatment. In China, effective personalized pain management has been well-developed over the past decades. Traditional Chinese Medicine (e.g., acupuncture, and massage therapy) also contribute significantly to long-term pain control among cancer patients. However, we need to acknowledge that individual variability exists, and pain trajectories also depend on cancer type, treatment response, and comorbidities. Therefore, continuous reassessment in larger representative samples remains critical.

In terms of QOL, women reported improvements at the 12-month follow-up, with stability observed thereafter. However, similar to depression and pain, the effects of mastectomy, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy on long-term QOL were not significant. Findings on the relationship between cancer treatment and QOL are mixed. For instance, Marinkovic et al. found that patients undergoing conservative surgeries reported better QOL than those undergoing radical mastectomy across all four domains (i.e., physical health, psychological, social relationships, and environment QOL) [65]. Han et al. similarly noted that patients receiving breast-conserving therapy had improved body image, satisfaction and QOL compared to those who underwent mastectomy and reconstruction [66]. This supports evidence suggesting that QOL is often better in lumpectomy patients than in mastectomy patients [39, 40, 67, 68]. Patients with total mastectomy usually suffer from chronic pain, swelling, limited limb movement, fatigue, decreased self-esteem, and other psychological problems which may worsen their QOL [69]. In addition, increased evidence indicates that patients undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy face a higher risk of psychological issues, potentially affecting their QOL [70–72], studies found that patients with radiotherapy and chemotherapy are at higher risk of getting psychological problems, such as anxiety, depression, and psychological distress due to adverse effects, which may contribute to poorer QOL [47, 73–75]. Discrepant findings may arise from variations in study samples, sizes, measurement tools, socioeconomic backgrounds, and clinical statuses.

Over the last two decades, there has been a dramatic improvement in the survival of breast cancer patients. The use of combined modalities of treatment, such as surgery, chemotherapy and radiation treatment has brought great benefits to an expanding patient population. However, the achieved medical success comes at some cost in terms of patients’ functioning level and sense of well-being, both physically and psychologically [76]. Our findings indicate that patients who underwent mastectomy still reported higher anxiety levels compared to those who had partial lumpectomy after 48 months. One possible reason is that patients with mastectomy believe that they have a more serious form of cancer that requires more intensive surgery. Related studies reported that compared with breast cancer patients with lumpectomy, there is a higher risk of mortality in patients with mastectomy [77]. Another possible explanation is that breast cancer patients with mastectomy experience entire breast removal, which lead to a permanent change in their appearance and cause long lasting anxiety [78]. The significance of breast removal can lead to body image concerns and increased anxiety [79].

Other studies reported opposite findings, for example, Levy et al. reported that lumpectomy patients fare worse psychologically than those with mastectomy [80, 81]. The aesthetic outcomes of lumpectomy in the early years vary widely, with most women reporting breast asymmetry [82]. The aesthetic outcomes from lumpectomy may also lead to dissatisfaction due to asymmetry and visible scars. Therefore, we failed to demonstrate conclusive findings of which surgical modality (mastectomy or lumpectomy) is the optimal treatment option for patients with breast cancer. Personalized treatment approaches should consider medical necessity (e.g., oncologic safety, disease stage), individual preferences and psychological factors to optimize both survival and long-term well-being.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. Firstly, the results of the current study should be interpreted with caution as only a small number of female breast cancer women from a specific area in China were included, which limits the generalizability of our findings. Secondly, we failed to include several related factors (e.g., social support, endocrine therapy, and targeted therapy) that may affect the relationship between different cancer treatment modalities on long-term psychological outcomes and QOL. Thirdly, as self-reported measurement tools were used in this study, recall bias may exist. Finally, there is potential bias due to different data collection methods (face-to-face at initial assessment vs. telephone/Wechat at follow-ups).

Clinical implications

Despite significant advancements in the survival of breast cancer patients over the past two decades, these medical successes come at a cost to patients’ physical and psychological well-being. Further evidence-based interventions should be provided to cancer patients and their families. First, psycho-educational support programs should be developed, including pain management strategies, coping mechanisms for fear of cancer recurrence, and stress reduction techniques. Second, clinical teams should implement routine psychological screening during follow-up visits using standardized assessment tools. Third, hospitals should establish multidisciplinary survivorship clinics integrating oncologists, clinical psychologists, and pain specialists to provide comprehensive care.

Conclusions

In conclusion, breast cancer patients exhibited a decreasing trend in depression, anxiety, pain, and FCR levels over 48 months post-discharge from primary treatment. Their QOL improved at the 12-month follow-up and remained stable thereafter. Patients who underwent mastectomy reported higher anxiety levels than those who had partial lumpectomy after 48 months. Personalized cancer treatment approaches are crucial in clinical practice to address individual patient needs.
