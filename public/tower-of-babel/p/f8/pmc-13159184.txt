Prognostic value of the atherogenic index of plasma in patients with multivessel coronary disease undergoing CABG: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yang He, Yue-jiao Yang, Hao Chen, Xin-qun Hu, Zhen-fei Fang, Xiao-bo Liao, Hui Yang, Zhao-wei Zhu, Liang Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP), a composite lipid index based on triglycerides (TG) and HDL-C, has been linked to cardiovascular risk. However, the prognostic relevance of AIP after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) in patients with multivessel coronary disease (MVD) is not well established. Therefore, we examined whether AIP predicts long-term outcomes in our cohort.MethodsConsecutive patients with MVD undergoing CABG (2015–2020) were included. The baseline and 1-year AIP were used to define tertiles. Major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs) were evaluated across AIP groups using Kaplan–Meier methods and Cox regression with multivariable adjustment. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to assess potential non-linear (dose–response) relationships between AIP and MACCEs.ResultsIn total, 1,602 patients were analysed, and 414 MACCEs occurred during a median follow-up of 43 months. Kaplan–Meier curves showed that group 3 had a significantly lower survival rate than those in the other two groups (P = 0.022). In adjusted Cox models, with group 1 as the reference, the hazard ratios were 1.11 (95% CI 0.86–1.43; P = 0.42) for group 2 and 1.38 (95% CI 1.07–1.78; P = 0.012) for group 3. However, when the AIP at 1 year postoperatively was used as the primary indicator, the differences between groups were no longer statistically significant. Additionally, RCS models suggested a linear correlation between the AIP and MACCE. Incorporation of AIP into traditional models showed a small incremental gain.ConclusionPreoperative AIP remained an independent predictor of adverse long-term outcomes after CABG among patients with MVD. Optimising AIP through proactive lipid management may help reduce the residual cardiovascular risk and improve long-term prognoses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-026-02895-5.

Introduction

Patients with multivessel coronary artery disease (MVD) continue to carry a substantial risk of late ischaemic events and heart-failure-related complications even after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) [1, 2]. Although revascularisation is often successful, atherosclerosis may still progress in both native coronary arteries and bypass conduits, reinforcing the need for sustained secondary prevention, including intensive lipid control [3, 4].

In lipid management, the lowering of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels remains central to secondary prevention [5]. The latest European Society of Cardiology and European Atherosclerosis Society guidelines recommend an LDL-C target of ≤ 1.40 mmol/L and ≥ 50% reduction from baseline to reduce cardiovascular risk [6]. Nevertheless, due to interindividual variability and genetic factors, achieving target LDL-C levels alone may not translate into optimal outcomes [7]. In addition, recurrent events can still occur despite guideline-directed therapy, often alongside ongoing atherosclerotic progression in native vessels and bypass grafts that may lead to repeat revascularisation [8].

This persistent risk is understood to involve pathological vascular remodelling, which is recognised as a central mechanism driving disease progression in patients with MVD. This process is regulated by a complex molecular network involving multiple pathways, such as inflammatory responses, immune activation, and epigenetic mechanisms [9]. Given the multifactorial nature of atherosclerosis, reliance on LDL-C or any single biomarker may be insufficient for a comprehensive prognostic assessment in patients with coronary artery disease (CAD), particularly those with MVD undergoing CABG [10]. This underscores the need for more refined risk stratification strategies and tailored management in this patient population.

Recently, composite and novel lipid indices have attracted growing attention. The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), defined as log plasma triglyceride (TG) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TG/HDL-C), summarises the TG and HDL-C information and has been proposed as a marker of atherogenic dyslipidaemia [11, 12]. Compared with single lipid measures, AIP captures both atherogenic and protective lipid features, offering a more holistic summary of the lipid status and cardiovascular risk [13]. Previous studies have linked elevated AIP to a higher risk of major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs), especially in acute coronary syndromes; yet, the data on patients with MVD after CABG remain limited [14–16]. Specifically, whether AIP provides an incremental prognostic value beyond the established clinical and surgical risk factors in this uniquely high-risk cohort remains unknown. Accordingly, this study evaluated the association between AIP and long-term outcomes following CABG and assessed its potential incremental value for risk stratification in this high-risk cohort.

Methods

Study design and population

Patients with MVD who received CABG at our institution from January 2015 to December 2020 were included in this retrospective cohort. The eligible participants had angiographically confirmed MVD and received isolated CABG. MVD was defined as > 70% diameter stenosis in at least two major epicardial vessels across two coronary territories, or a single-vessel lesion accompanied by significant left main disease (> 50% diameter stenosis) [1]. The baseline clinical characteristics and follow-up information were obtained from medical records, including TG and HDL-C measured at 1 year after surgery (1-year TG and 1-year HDL-C). The exclusion criteria were concomitant procedures (aortic or mitral valve replacement, aortic root aneurysm repair, surgery for aortic dissection, or complex congenital heart disease), age ≥ 80 years, death during the index admission or within 1 year post-CABG, and incomplete follow-up. All participants provided written informed consent at admission for the use of their medical records for research. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The patient data were deidentified prior to analysis.

Data collection and definition

The medical records were checked by trained clinical staff and the treating physicians. The 1-year postoperative follow-up data on lipid profiles and medication regimens were collected through scheduled outpatient visits and supplemented by structured telephone interviews for patients who could not return. To standardise the timing of the postoperative lipid measurement, we defined the “1-year postoperative lipid level” as the result obtained from a laboratory test performed within a window of 11 to 13 months after the index CABG surgery. This 2-month window was chosen to accommodate the real-world clinical follow-up variability while ensuring the measurement’s temporal relevance to the 1-year time point. The AIP was calculated as log₁₀ (TG/HDL-C) based on these standardised measurements. All laboratory analyses were conducted by certified laboratory technicians using standardised assays, and all lipid concentrations are reported in mmol/L.

Clinical endpoints

The primary endpoint was MACCE, comprising cardiac-related death, non-fatal myocardial infarction (MI), coronary revascularisation (PCI or CABG with internal mammary, radial artery, or saphenous vein grafts), hospitalisation for angina or heart failure, and non-fatal stroke.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD when approximately normally distributed, and as median (IQR; 25th–75th percentile) otherwise. Categorical variables are described as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons used rank-based non-parametric tests for skewed continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. The patients were classified into preoperative AIP tertiles: group 1 (n = 534, AIP < 0.1351), group 2 (n = 534, 0.1351 ≤ AIP ≤ 0.3346), and group 3 (n = 534, AIP > 0.3346). The 1-year postoperative visit was treated as the landmark timepoint for all time-to-event analyses. The cumulative MACCE incidence was summarised with Kaplan–Meier curves. The associations of AIP tertiles with MACCE and its components were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models and reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine the association between AIP groups and MACCE, with the effect estimates reported as HRs and 95% CIs. The covariates were specified a priori according to the clinical relevance and incorporated through three progressively adjusted models to account for potential confounding. The missing values were replaced using the mean of available observations for the corresponding variable. The covariates were entered sequentially. Model 1 comprised age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, peripheral artery disease, left ventricular ejection fraction, smoking status (previous or current), prior myocardial infarction, prior stroke, and prior percutaneous coronary intervention. Model 2 added the values of 1-year HDL-C, 1-year TGs, serum creatinine, NT-proBNP, haemoglobin, and aspartate aminotransferase, whereas Model 3 further included the surgical characteristics and 1-year post-CABG medications (statins, ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, aspirin, P2Y12 inhibitors, β-blockers, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers). To ensure result robustness, sensitivity analyses were performed using bootstrap resampling and quartile-based grouping. Subgroup and interaction analyses were conducted to validate the study reliability, with the interaction effects assessed via likelihood ratio tests. Incremental discrimination was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC). Analyses were performed in SPSS 26.0 and R 4.2.3; two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

Over a 6-year period, we collected data on 1,602 patients with MVD who underwent CABG at our institution after applying the exclusion criteria. The cohort had a mean age of 61.03 ± 8.17 years and consisted of 1,303 men (83.1%) and 299 women (16.9%). The participants were categorised into tertiles according to the preoperative AIP: group 1 (AIP < 0.1351), group 2 (0.1351–0.3346) (Fig. 1), and group 3 (AIP > 0.3346). The baseline characteristics by AIP tertile are presented in Table 1. Across all groups, male sex and smoking (55.5%) were predominant. Compared with group 1, a higher AIP was accompanied by younger age and higher body mass index (BMI), haemoglobin, total cholesterol (TC), TG, and LDL-C, along with a higher prevalence of diabetes.

AIP at baseline levels is associated with the occurrence of MACCE events

During a median follow-up of 43 months, 414 MACCEs were documented (25.8%). The incidence of MACCE in the Q1 (25.1%) and Q2 (24.3%) subgroups was below the overall average, while Q3 exhibited the highest event rate (28.1%), surpassing the average. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to compare the MACCE and its individual components across baseline AIP tertiles (Fig. 2). The survival curves were comparable across tertiles for cardiac death (P = 0.69), non-fatal myocardial infarction (P = 0.17), non-fatal stroke (P = 0.16), or revascularisation (P = 0.61). However, significant differences were noted for MACCE events (P = 0.022) and cardiac rehospitalisation (P = 0.031)(Fig. 1).

To further validate these findings, the participants were regrouped into three categories according to the 1-year postoperative AIP (Table S1). The baseline characteristics indicated that male sex and smoking were highly prevalent across all groups. With increasing AIP levels, there was a progressive rise in the values of height, BMI, haemoglobin, TC, LDL-C, TG, and non-HDL-C (P for trend < 0.05), along with a significantly higher prevalence of previous PCI (P = 0.019). Additionally, LVEF was slightly elevated in groups with higher AIP (P for trend = 0.036), whereas NT-proBNP levels showed a decreasing trend (P for trend = 0.031). Based on this stratification, the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was repeated (Figure S1). Despite differences in the baseline characteristics, MACCE and its individual components did not differ across groups (all P-values > 0.05).

Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine the relationship between the baseline AIP and MACCE (and its components) with adjustment for potential confounders. As shown in Table 2, after full adjustment (Model 3), the HRs were 1.11 (95% CI, 0.86–1.43) for group 2 and 1.38 (95% CI, 1.07–1.78) for group 3, using group 1 as the reference. Even without an adjustment for covariates, a higher baseline AIP corresponded to a greater cumulative risk of MACCE (P value for trend = 0.008). While the trend P-values for non-fatal MI (0.079), non-fatal stroke (0.174), and revascularisation (0.643) were not significant, the MACCE and cardiac rehospitalisation remained significant (P < 0.05).

HRs of AIP associated with MACCEs and individual outcomes after Cox regression analysis

With the covariates adjusted as in model 3, restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to characterise the association between the baseline AIP and outcomes. The P-values for non-linearity were 0.101, 0.001, 0.02, 0.421, 0.041, and 0.116 for MACCE and its individual components, respectively, with the corresponding overall association P-values of 0.003, 0.004, 0.016, 0.633, 0.01, and 0.174 (Fig. 3). These findings suggest that the AIP levels show a significant linear association only with MACCE events. For non-fatal cardiac mortality, non-fatal myocardial infarction, and revascularisation, the trends suggest a potential U-shaped relationship. To further explore this pattern, an RCS analysis was performed using the AIP levels measured 1 year post-CABG, which demonstrated no significant associations with MACCE or its individual components (Figure S2). In contrast, the baseline AIP as a continuous variable was linearly related to the MACCE risk. As visualised by an RCS analysis, each one-standard deviation increment in the preprocedural AIP was associated with a 90.4% increase in the hazard risk (HR 1.904, 95% CI 1.282–2.828, P = 0.001; Figure S3). This association remained stable and was consistent with the trend observed in the previous analysis using AIP tertiles, thereby confirming the robustness of our findings.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the stability of the results, several sensitivity analyses were performed. First, the missing values were handled using interpolation to minimise the sample loss. Next, the bootstrap method was applied, with the original data resampled 1,000 times to generate multiple simulated datasets. Subsequently, the model was refitted to assess the credibility and stability of the parameter estimates. As shown in Table S2, the HRs and 95% CIs derived from the bootstrap method closely aligned with those of the original model, confirming its robustness. Additionally, to further validate our results, we performed a sensitivity analysis using quartile-based grouping. As shown in Table S3, the trend P-values from the quartile-based analysis were consistent with those from the tertile-based grouping, further supporting the reliability of the model. Collectively, these analyses demonstrate the stability of our findings across different methods and grouping strategies, enhancing the credibility of the conclusions.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses evaluated the AIP–MACCE association across sex, age, smoking status, BMI, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and chronic kidney disease (CKD; Table 3). With the exception of sex, the association was broadly similar across strata, and no interaction reached statistical significance (all P-values and P for interaction > 0.05). In men, group 3 was associated with higher MACCE risk (HR = 1.48, P = 0.005), whereas no significant association was found in women (P = 0.95). Additionally, in individuals aged ≥ 65 years, with a BMI < 24 kg/m², smokers, and those without T2DM or CKD, a higher AIP level was also linked to an increased MACCE risk, whereas no statistically significant association was observed in their respective counterparts. Overall, AIP demonstrated a stable predictive value across most subgroups, with sex potentially playing a key role in modulating its predictive performance.

AIP as an incremental predictor of MACCE in patients with MVD undergoing CABG

The discrimination for MACCE was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The AIP-only model yielded an AUC of 0.524. Incorporating AIP into the conventional clinical model changed the AUC from 0.671 to 0.673 (Figure S4). A reclassification analysis showed a net reclassification improvement of 0.89% and integrated discrimination improvement of 0.17%. Model calibration was acceptable based on the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (P > 0.75; Figures S5 and S6).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of patients with MVD undergoing CABG, the baseline AIP showed an independent association with long-term MACCE. This association persisted after adjustment for established clinical risk factors and lipid measures and was consistent in sensitivity analyses. The RCS analysis supported a linear dose–response relationship between the AIP and MACCE risk. Notably, AIP measured 1 year postoperatively did not retain significant prognostic value, suggesting that the preoperative lipid milieu exerts a more durable impact on the long-term outcomes. To our knowledge, few studies have examined both preoperative and 1-year postoperative AIP in relation to the long-term outcomes after CABG. Taken together, these results support the use of AIP as a simple, accessible marker to refine preoperative risk stratification and to inform secondary prevention in this high-risk population.

Residual risk after CABG and the need for novel lipid markers

The 2021 ACC/AHA/SCAI guideline on coronary revascularisation recommends CABG as the favoured strategy for patients with complex MVD, particularly when left main involvement or anatomically challenging lesions are present [17]. However, despite successful revascularisation, several patients experience recurrent ischaemic events, largely reflecting the ongoing atherosclerotic progression in native coronary arteries and bypass conduits [18]. Long-term graft patency remains a major clinical challenge- approximately 40%–50% of saphenous vein grafts fail within 10 years, with a notable proportion occluding even within the first postoperative year [18, 19]. Consequently, the identification of the residual atherogenic risk and early risk stratification are crucial for optimising secondary prevention in this high-risk population [20].

Current lipid management strategies primarily target LDL-C, which remains the cornerstone of secondary prevention. Nonetheless, accumulating evidence suggests that residual cardiovascular risk persists even in patients who achieve guideline-recommended LDL-C targets [21, 22]. This paradox is exemplified in our cohort, where the mean follow-up LDL-C level (2.19 mmol/L) exceeded that mentioned in contemporary guideline recommendations, highlighting the ongoing challenge of optimal lipid management in real-world practice. It is fully acknowledged that LDL-C reduction is the foundation of secondary prevention in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, with robust evidence supporting its role in reducing events and plaque progression. The present study does not dispute the central role of LDL-C; rather, it suggests that AIP may serve as a useful complementary tool alongside aggressive LDL-C management.

Pathophysiological relevance of AIP

The AIP, calculated as log₁₀(TG/HDL-C), integrates both pro-atherogenic (TGs) and anti-atherogenic (HDL-C) lipid components into a single continuous metric. The current evidence suggests that lowering TG levels offers significant cardiovascular benefits, potentially surpassing the conventional advantages of statin-mediated LDL-C reduction [23]. Meanwhile, HDL-C remains a focus of research as a key cardioprotective agent. Critically, however, the clinical significance of AIP extends beyond merely combining the concentrations of these two lipid components. A higher AIP has been associated with a greater proportion of small, dense LDL-C particles that are more susceptible to oxidation and endothelial uptake, potentially promoting plaque formation and instability [24, 25]. Moreover, AIP reflects TG-rich lipoprotein metabolism and HDL-C functionality–processes closely linked to insulin resistance, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction [26–28]. Previous studies have identified AIP as an independent predictor of occurrence of metabolic syndrome, T2DM, and carotid atherosclerosis, conditions that frequently coexist with CAD, reflecting its role as a surrogate of systemic metabolic derangement [29, 30]. A study on middle-aged and older men also reported a higher risk of CVD-related mortality in the highest AIP quintile than in the lowest quintile [31]. These results align with earlier reports linking higher AIP to adverse cardiovascular outcomes in the general population and individuals with established CAD. Importantly, the present findings extend these observations to the high-risk subset of patients with MVD undergoing CABG, providing new insights into the prognostic implications of preoperative lipid patterns.

Preoperative vs. postoperative AIP: timing matters

Baseline AIP provided better discrimination for future cardiovascular events than postoperative AIP, echoing prior evidence from Masoudkabir et al. [32], which also identified elevated AIP levels as a strong predictor of MACCE, ACS, and revascularisation after CABG. However, the present study not only examined the preoperative AIP, but also conducted a dynamic assessment of AIP levels 1 year after surgery, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of its prognostic significance. These results offer a potential predictive tool for patients undergoing CABG, contributing to optimised postoperative management and individualised risk assessment. The superior prognostic value of preoperative AIP compared with postoperative AIP may be explained by several mechanisms. First, preoperative AIP likely represents a chronic, cumulative lipid milieu that contributes to both coronary and graft atherosclerosis. By contrast, the postoperative AIP levels may be confounded by perioperative stress, lifestyle modification, and pharmacologic interventions—particularly lipid-lowering therapy—which can obscure the underlying atherogenic risk [33]. Second, postoperative AIP variability may be affected by adherence to lipid-lowering therapy. Previous studies have reported that up to 80%–85% of patients with CABG do not reach guideline-recommended lipid targets after surgery, reflecting suboptimal secondary prevention [1]. Finally, preoperative lipid profiles are typically obtained under standardised conditions, whereas postoperative sampling is more heterogeneous, potentially introducing measurement variability that weakens predictive precision. These findings suggest that assessing the AIP before surgery provides a more stable and pathophysiologically relevant measure of the residual atherogenic risk.

Subgroup findings and clinical implications

In this study, subgroup analyses suggested possible sex-related differences in the prognostic relevance of AIP. Specifically, the association between AIP and MACCE was evident in men but not in women. Similar sex-dependent patterns between AIP and cardiovascular outcomes have been reported previously [34, 35], likely mediated by the hormonal regulation of lipid metabolism and distinct HDL-C particle characteristics. Nevertheless, given the smaller proportion of women in this cohort, these observations should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in larger, sex-balanced populations.

In the multivariate Cox regression analyses, after adjustment for T2DM, CKD, and other relevant covariates, the predictive strength of AIP was modestly attenuated. This attenuation may reflect the shared metabolic and inflammatory pathways underlying these comorbidities, where AIP alone may not fully capture the complex interplay between lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction. However, the persistent significance of AIP across most subgroups supports its role as a robust and independent prognostic indicator in patients undergoing CABG.

Incremental predictive value of AIP

An ROC curve analysis indicated that adding AIP into conventional risk models modestly improved the discrimination for MACCE prediction. Although the incremental gain in the area under the curve was numerically small, such improvements are clinically meaningful in the context of multifactorial cardiovascular risk prediction, particularly given AIP’s simplicity, reproducibility, and low cost. The observed modest improvement may be attributed to two key factors: the central concentration of AIP values limiting its discriminative power at the extremes, and the partial overlap of its predictive information with conventional risk factors like diabetes and BMI. Therefore, incorporating AIP into perioperative evaluation may enhance individualised risk stratification and support identification of patients more likely to benefit from more aggressive lipid-lowering or metabolic interventions.

Clinical perspective and future directions

From a clinical research perspective, AIP represents an easily obtainable biomarker that integrates the information on TG and HDL-C levels. Our findings indicate that while AIP alone shows some association with clinical outcomes, its incremental value becomes limited and unstable when incorporated into multivariate models containing established clinical, procedural, and laboratory factors. This suggests that AIP’s predictive contribution may be context-dependent and influenced by interactions with other risk variables. Specifically, AIP could be used to identify a distinct high-risk phenotype characterised by atherogenic dyslipidaemia—a group that may remain undertreated despite conventional LDL-C based management. This directly informs patient care by highlighting a subset of post-CABG patients who might benefit from more targeted therapeutic strategies, which is a step toward reducing the long-term burden of cardiovascular disease in this high-risk population.

Antithrombotic therapy forms the foundation of coronary artery disease management, and its intensity is directly related to the ischaemic risk, especially in patients with multivessel disease [36]. A direct clinical implication of the present study is that preoperative AIP may serve as a complementary tool to existing risk‑stratification methods, helping to identify patients who may harbour higher residual inflammatory and pro‑thrombotic risk. For such patients with CABG with elevated AIP, clinicians should regard this biomarker as a factor that strengthens risk assessment when formulating antithrombotic strategies. After comprehensively evaluating the bleeding risk, a more intensive or prolonged antithrombotic regimen could be reasonably favoured to address the heightened ischaemic risk that might otherwise be under‑recognised by conventional evaluation. This offers an evidence‑based perspective for achieving more refined, individualised antithrombotic management within the framework of the current guidelines.

Finally, the context of lipid management is rapidly evolving beyond statins. Emerging agents, such as bempedoic acid, have demonstrated efficacy in lowering cholesterol and reducing the cardiovascular events, offering new options especially for statin-intolerant patients [37]. This study suggests that for patients with persistently elevated postoperative AIP, the focus of lipid management should extend beyond LDL‑C targets alone. AIP can serve as an immediately applicable clinical indicator to identify those with prominent atherogenic dyslipidaemia who may derive greater benefit from targeted agents such as bempedoic acid. This encourages clinicians to look beyond statin monotherapy and, guided by the patient’s AIP phenotype, consider early combination with or switching to novel agents that more comprehensively improve the lipid profile, thereby advancing more precise secondary prevention.

Strengths and limitations

This study may have several notable strengths. First, it draws on a large cohort of patients with MVD undergoing CABG and evaluates the prognostic relevance of serum AIP measured both before surgery and at 1 year. Second, the extended median follow-up duration provides reliability for assessing long-term cardiovascular outcomes. Third, in addition to comprehensive multivariable adjustment and sensitivity analyses, restricted cubic spline models were used to characterise the shape of the AIP–MACCE association and supported an approximately linear dose–response pattern. Fourth, by focusing on “late” events occurring more than 1 year postoperatively, this study design helps exclude early complications directly related to the surgical procedure or perioperative stress, thereby highlighting that the persistent atherogenic dyslipidaemia and atherosclerotic burden reflected by AIP remain a core driver of long-term cardiovascular risk. Finally, because AIP can be derived directly from routine lipid measurements, these findings support its use as a simple, low-cost marker for long-term risk stratification and ongoing metabolic management in patients following CABG.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the external validity may be constrained because this was a single-centre study, and the cohort was mainly East Asian and male (83%); therefore, confirmation in more diverse populations is needed. Second, excluding in-hospital deaths and deaths within the first postoperative year may have led to an underestimation of the overall cardiovascular risk. Third, a key methodological limitation concerns the handling of time-varying treatment effects. Although a step-wise, guideline-based lipid-lowering strategy was standard in our cohort, our study design did not capture the exact initiation dates of medications. Consequently, we were unable to account for changes in the treatment intensity over time or to apply advanced statistical methods such as time-updated covariates or marginal structural models to adjust for this treatment-related confounding. Fourth, the data on comorbidities and medications were collected at the time of follow-up assessment, and changes in the lifestyle or metabolic control occurring during the follow-up period were not recorded. These unmeasured dynamic factors could have influenced the postoperative AIP levels and subsequent clinical outcomes. Fifth, because some patients resided in rural or remote regions, incomplete follow-up and missing data were unavoidable despite multiple imputation. Lastly, despite adjustment for key confounders, residual confounding due to unmeasured factors remains possible.

Therefore, future multicentre prospective studies incorporating serial lipid monitoring and standardised postoperative management are needed to confirm our findings and to clarify how AIP relates to long-term prognosis after CABG. Notably, emerging evidence associates elevated lipid indexes with high-risk plaque features, which are recognised triggers of early postoperative acute events [38, 39]. Given that our study excluded patients who died within the first postoperative year, subsequent research should prioritise investigating whether AIP can better discriminate high-risk patients across distinct postoperative phases. This focus would not only strengthen the evidence chain for AIP throughout the cardiovascular risk continuum, but also hold crucial implications for guiding stratified postoperative management.

Conclusion

Among patients with MVD undergoing CABG, a higher preoperative AIP independently predicted a greater risk of long-term MACCEs. The preoperative AIP likely reflects chronic atherogenic exposure that continues to influence outcomes after surgical revascularisation. Given its simplicity and strong pathophysiological rationale, AIP may serve as a valuable adjunct to the existing risk stratification models and facilitate the early identification of patients with high residual atherogenic risk who would benefit from intensified lipid management and closer post-CABG surveillance.
