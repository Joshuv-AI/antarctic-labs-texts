CDK7 is a novel therapeutic target in fibrolamellar carcinoma
Authors: Manabu Nukaya, Patrick R. Carney, Crystal Cafferty, Katerina Zahed, Isabelle Yun, David P. Al-Adra, Noor A. Kazim, Alaa R. Farghli, Marina Chan, Austin Stram, Jeremy D. Kratz, Mark E. Berres, Andrew Yen, Taranjit S. Gujral, Praveen Sethupathy, Christopher A. Bradfield, Sean M. Ronnekleiv-Kelly
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryFibrolamellar Carcinoma (FLC) is a rare and deadly cancer that arises in young, otherwise healthy patients. For patients that cannot be treated with surgery, only 30%–45% survive to 5 years with current treatment options. These poor survival odds highlight the need for new therapeutic targets. Using patient samples, we identified that abnormal function of cyclin-dependent kinase 7 (CDK7) is a key component of pathways that are essential for FLC cancer cell identity and survival. Consequently, drug inhibitors of CDK7 suppressed these abnormal pathways and also caused cancer cell death in a dose-dependent manner. This held true in several patient-derived models of FLC. We then found that inhibition of CDK7 can combine with other drug candidates to increase the therapeutic response in FLC cells. Taken together, this suggests CDK7 is a promising target for future treatment in human FLC.

Introduction

Fibrolamellar carcinoma (FLC) is a rare and lethal cancer that afflicts young individuals and accounts for approximately 1% of liver cancers.1,2,3,4 The tumor arises in the background of a normal liver, and patients typically present with advanced cancer at the time of diagnosis;2,5 this is due to the absence of known risk factors or tumor markers that can precipitate screening or prevention.1,6,7,8,9 While surgery offers the best chance for long-term survival, most patients experience cancer recurrence after surgical resection.1,5 Unfortunately, for these patients with advanced or recurrent cancer, no proven systemic therapies exist, and only 30%–45% of patients survive to 5 years1,6 When considering that the peak age of diagnosis is 22 years,2 the substantial impact on life years lost underscores the devastating nature of this cancer. Thus, there remains a critical need to improve outcomes for patients with FLC through systemic treatment.

Undoubtedly, the research landscape for FLC changed after the 2014 pioneering study that revealed a unique gene fusion created by a ∼400 kbp deletion between heat shock protein 40 (DNAJB1) and the catalytic subunit alpha of protein kinase A (PRKACA).6 The DNAJB1-PRKACA gene fusion is the proven driver of mutation in the vast majority of human FLC, and the resultant chimeric protein (DNAJ-PKAc) is only present in tumor cells indicating a somatic event.5,10,11 Since its initial discovery, there has been inconclusive evidence of how DNAJ-PKAc drives cellular changes to promote neoplastic transformation and progression of FLC. Concordantly, there have been concerted efforts to understand how DNAJB1-PRKACA drives tumor development and mechanisms of treatment resistance so that new and effective therapies can be identified.11,12 For instance, the chimeric protein demonstrates enhanced protein kinase A (PKA) activity and aberrant localization compared to native PKA,6,13 but inhibiting PKA has proven challenging in FLC given the critical nature of PKA in normally functioning cells (i.e., no therapeutic window).14 While the precise signaling cascade downstream of DNAJ-PKAc remains unknown, recent work has demonstrated that the fusion oncoprotein causes aberrant remodeling of the epigenetic landscape and emergence of FLC-specific super-enhancers.11

Super-enhancers within cancer cells are responsible for high levels of expression of genes that are essential for cell identity and survival.15,16 These critical genes associated with super-enhancers control cell state and cancer hallmark qualities.15 Notably, the profile of super-enhancer-driven genes in FLC uncovered key molecular targets that are uniquely overexpressed in FLC such as SLC16A14 and LINC00473 among others.11,17 These FLC-specific genes can serve as markers for DNAJ-PKAc oncoprotein activity, but a critical gap exists in understanding how the tumor-driver mutation (DNAJB1-PRKACA) transmits signals to ultimately promote super-enhancer-driven gene expression in FLC.

RNA polymerase II (along with other cofactors and chromatin regulators) is known to be highly enriched at super-enhancers—and can transcribe enhancer RNA that may contribute to enhancer function.15 We evaluated human samples of FLC (and paired adjacent liver) for heightened RNA polymerase II (RNA Pol II) phosphorylation, which is an indicator of increased RNA Pol II activity, and identified increased serine-5 (Ser 5) phosphorylation. Ser 5 is the molecular target of cyclin-dependent kinase 7 (CDK7). To support the hypothesis that CDK7 is a potential therapeutic target in tumors harboring DNAJ-PKAc, we engineered an FLC cell line by introducing the DNAJB1-PRKACA oncogene rearrangement into human hepatocellular cells and found evidence that the DNAJB1-PRKACA-expressing cells possessed heightened CDK7 activity as evidenced by RNA Pol II Ser 5 phosphorylation. Further, CDK7 activation in these cells was associated with transcriptional changes in FLC-specific super-enhancer-driven genes, which are regions enriched in RNA Pol II activity.18,19 Selectively inhibiting CDK7 resulted in suppressed expression of certain FLC-specific super-enhancer-associated genes, and also caused potent induction of cancer cell death in patient-derived models of FLC. CDK7 inhibition can also combine synergistically to markedly suppress RNA Pol II phosphorylation and abolish gene expression of FLC-specific super-enhancer-driven genes. For instance, targeting aberrant RNA Pol II activity in combination with CDK7 and CDK9 inhibition revealed markedly reduced expression of LINC00473 and SLC16A14, along with potent synergistic induction of cell death. Collectively, this work uncovers a novel candidate therapeutic vulnerability in FLC.

Results

Increased CDK7 activity and RNA polymerase II phosphorylation in human samples of FLC

Key insight into the transcriptional changes associated with the DNAJ-PKAc oncoprotein in FLC came from a recent work depicting the super-enhancer landscape in human tumors of FLC.11 This led to the identification of molecular targets that are overexpressed in FLC, one of which (SLC16A14) is highly specific for FLC and consistently upregulated in tumors compared to normal tissue (Figure 1A).11,20 The seminal work that uncovered super-enhancer-driven genes utilized a technique that measures nascent RNA, including enhancer RNA transcribed by RNA Pol II.11 RNA Pol II is known to be highly enriched at super-enhancers,15 and to understand what may be driving the aberrant RNA Pol II function, we isolated protein from stored frozen human samples of FLC along with paired normal liver and performed immunoblot to assess for RNA Pol II phosphorylation. We found increased Ser 5 phosphorylation and to a lesser extent serine-2 (Ser 2) and serine-7 (Ser 7) phosphorylation in the human tumor tissue versus human adjacent liver (phosphorylation change [Mean], pRPB-1 [Ser 2]: 1.59 [p < 0.05], pRPB-1 [Ser 5]: 2.70 [p < 0.05], pRPB-1 [Ser 7]: 1.66 [p < 0.05]) (Figures 1B, 1C, and S1). Quantification was determined by a phosphorylation index for Ser 2, Ser 5, Ser 7, and CDK7 (i.e., pRPB-1/RPB-1, pCDK7/CDK7) and the phosphorylation level calculated by phosphorylation index (tumor)/phosphorylation index (adjacent normal). CDK7 binds cyclin H and MAT1 to form the CDK-activating kinase (CAK) which phosphorylates target proteins at serine and threonine sites.21,22 CDK7 modulates transcription by phosphorylating RNA Pol II at specific serine residues in the C-terminal domain (CTD), including the preferential target Ser 5, and potentially Ser 7 and Ser 2, although Ser 2 phosphorylation may occur indirectly through regulation of CDK9 (Figure 1D).23 While increased pCDK7 was not apparent (pCDK7: 1.22 [p > 0.05]), RNA Pol II Ser 5 was clearly evident, suggesting increased CDK7 activity directed toward RNA Pol II. DNAJB1-PRKACA super-enhancer gene expression is correlated with increased phosphorylation of RNA pol II

To investigate if the DNAJ-PKAc fusion protein expression leads to upregulation of super-enhancer gene expression via the activation of CDK7-RNA Pol II pathway, we engineered the DNAJB1-PRKACA gene fusion in a hepatoblastoma cell line (HepG2),24 using a dual-guide RNA/Cas9 that targets the intronic region between exon 1 and 2 of DNAJB1 and the intronic region between exon 1 and 2 of PRKACA (Figures 2A and 2B). Genomic DNA PCR and sequencing across the breakpoint established presence of the DNAJB1-PRKACA gene fusion in six separate clones (Figures 2C and 2D). We confirmed mRNA expression of DNAJB1-PRKACA and performed sequencing of the mRNA transcripts to ensure development of the expected fusion transcript (Figures 2C and 2D). Protein analysis demonstrated the presence of DNAJ-PKAc and native PKA, which is the expected pattern of protein detection in human FLC (Figure 2E).6 Based on expression levels of native genes (e.g., PRKACA) and FLC-specific markers (e.g., SLC16A14 and LINC00473), we selected clone H33 (HepG2DNAJB1−PRKACA) for subsequent assays (Figure 2D). Another clone, H12 (HepG2DNAJB1−PRKACA), also met the same rigorous validation metrics and was utilized for confirmatory tests. FLC-specific super-enhancer gene expression is suppressed by CDK7 inhibition

To investigate if the FLC-specific super-enhancer gene expression is regulated by CDK7-RNA Pol II pathway in a dose-dependent fashion, we used a potent and specific CDK7 inhibitor that is currently in use in clinical trials (SY-5609).25 We examined whether CDK7 modulated the increased phosphorylation of RNA Pol II in DNAJB1-PRKACA-expressing H33 (HepG2DNAJB1−PRKACA) cells. With SY-5609 treatment (0.1–10 μM), we identified decreased CDK7 phosphorylation and decreased phosphorylation of CDK7 targets including Ser 2, 5, and 7 (CTD) of RNA Pol II, and CDK2 (Figure 3C). We treated DNAJB1-PRKACA-expressing H33 cells with SY-5609 (1–300 nM) and found a dose-dependent downregulation of both SLC16A14 and LINC00473 expression (Figure 3D). We also used a separate potent and selective CDK7 inhibitor that irreversibly and covalently binds CDK7 (YKL-5-124)26 and found an identical dose-dependent suppression of SLC16A14 and LINC00473 expression (Figure 3E). Notably, both CDK7 inhibitors showed dose-dependent suppression of the FLC-specific super-enhancer genes SLC16A14 and LINC00473. In contrast, the DNAJB1-PRKACA fusion gene expression did not show the same dose-dependent reduction by the treatment of CDK7 inhibitor (Figure S3).

To assess whether the observed dose-dependent suppression of SLC16A14 and LINC00473 with CDK7 inhibition was gene-specific or due to a widespread reduction in gene expression, we performed RNA sequencing (RNAseq) and analysis of SY-5609-treated H33 cells, including DMSO, 1 nM, 10 nM, 100 nM, and 300 nM (n = 5 each dose). We observed 11,088 genes that demonstrated upregulation or no change in expression at the highest dose of SY-5609 as compared to control (Figures S4A and S4B). This finding is expected as previous literature suggests that CDK7 inhibition primarily inhibits RNA synthesis but can also lead to the stabilization of existing RNAs.27 And while there was decreased expression in 4,872 genes at the highest dose of SY-5609, there were only 3,384 genes that displayed suppression in a dose-dependent manner, including the FLC-specific genes SLC16A14 and LINC00473 (Figures S4C and S4D, Data S4). Our analysis was designed to identify a subset of genes exhibiting dose-dependent downregulation in response to CDK7 inhibition by SY-5609 treatment. We observed that many genes were unaffected or even upregulated, and a distinct subset showed clear, dose-dependent decreases in expression. While we did not observe a uniform or widespread reduction in gene expression, we note that our experimental design and normalization strategy (trimmed mean of M-values normalization without spike-in controls) are not suited to detect global shifts in mRNA abundance. Therefore, our conclusions are limited to relative expression changes, and we cannot make claims about global transcriptional repression. Collectively, these data strongly suggest that CDK7—which mediates phosphorylation of RNA Pol II in DNAJB1-PRKACA expressing cells—is responsible for regulating expression of SLC16A14 and LINC00473, which are FLC-specific super-enhancer-driven genes. Furthermore, and of potential clinical importance, CDK7 inhibition can be used to target both SLC16A14 and LINC00473 in FLC, which is a key finding given the current inability to inhibit these highly promising targets in FLC (i.e., they are currently not “druggable”).

CDK7 inhibition suppresses cancer cell growth in human cell line models of FLC

SLC16A14 and LINC00473 are highly expressed in human FLC compared to normal liver and are not elevated in other liver cancers (such as cholangiocarcinoma or hepatocelluclar carcinoma).11,20 The function of these genes in FLC and the impact of their marked overexpression (compared to paired normal liver) remain unknown but the products of these genes may serve as unique therapeutic vulnerabilities in FLC and is the focus of current investigations.11,17 RNA Pol II (along with other cofactors and chromatin regulators) is known to be highly enriched at super-enhancers—and can transcribe enhancer RNA that may contribute to enhancer function.15 Given the importance of CDK7 in facilitating RNA Pol II function (via phosphorylation), and the known role of CDK7 in cancer-associated super-enhancers,16,28,29,30 our data support the idea that the increased phosphorylation of the Ser 5 residue of RNA Pol II in DNAJB1-PRKACA-expressing cells can be targeted by CDK7 inhibitor therapy.

Given our evidence of CDK7 controlling expression of promising FLC-specific therapeutic targets (i.e., SLC16A14 and LINC00473),31 and the known role of CDK7 on RNA Pol II transcriptional regulation,18,19 we hypothesized that CDK7 inhibition would result in a therapeutic vulnerability in FLC cells. We tested CDK7 inhibition in H33 cells using SY-5609 and found a robust dose-dependent reduction in cell viability, which was confirmed in a separate clone (H12) (Figure 4A). Both H33 and H12 displayed a more potent reduction in cell viability versus HepG2 (i.e., presence of DNAJB1-PRKACA in cells caused increased susceptibility to SY-5609) (IC50, HepG2: 1.87E-8 [M], H12: 4.90E-9 [M], H33: 3.75E-9 [M]). To ensure that the effect we observed was CDK7 dependent, we assessed cell viability with YKL-5-124. YKL-5-124 treatment resulted in a similar dose-dependent reduction of H33 and H12 cell viability (IC50, HepG2: 4.95E-6 [M], H12: 7.48E-7 [M], H33: 6.29E-7 [M]) (Figure 4A). As an additional confirmation, we used siCDK7 using two separate siRNAs against CDK7 and again demonstrated a strong dose-dependent response (Figure S5). The marked reduction in cancer cell viability due to CDK7 inhibition may be due to cell-cycle arrest, induction of apoptosis, or both. We first measured the effect of CDK7 inhibition on cell cycle. We found that SY-5609 treatment resulted in a modest effect in the HepG2 cells, with a moderately reduced percentage of cells in G2/M (26.4% DMSO vs. 15.5% SY-5609, p = 0.0015) and a reciprocal increase in the percent of cells in G0/G1 (47.6% DMSO vs. 56.6% SY-5609, p < 0.0001). In contrast, CDK7 inhibition in H33 cells resulted in a profound cell cycle shift with most cells in G0/G1 (32.5% DMSO vs. 70.9% SY-5609, p < 0.0001) and a marked reduction in the percent of cells in G2/M (40.4% DMSO vs. 8.7% SY-5609, p < 0.0001) (Figure 4B). We subsequently examined the effect on apoptosis with CDK7 inhibition. SY-5609 treatment of H33 cells demonstrated a significant increase in caspase 3/7 activity compared to HepG2 cells at all treatment doses (SY-5609 100 nM, 1 μM, and 10 μM, all p < 0.0001), which was confirmed with the separate H12 clone, demonstrating high sensitivity of DNAJB1-PRKACA-expressing cells to CDK7 inhibition. We again validated the significant increase in apoptosis through detection of cleaved PARP1, which displayed a clear dose-dependent increase in cleaved protein with SY-5609 treatment (Figures 4C and 4D). Together, these data show that CDK7 is a critical component of cell proliferation/viability in DNAJB1-PRKACA-expressing cells, and likewise, CDK7 inhibition may represent a key therapeutic strategy in FLC.

A necessary component of any potential therapy is the identification of a true therapeutic window. Because toxicity may limit translational potential (i.e., therapy tested must be tolerated), we assessed the effect of CDK7 inhibition in human liver cells. We isolated human hepatocytes immediately from fresh liver (transplant donor specimen) and examined the impact of SY-5609 at two separate doses (100 nM and 1 μM) for 24- and 48-h (Figure 4E). While the DNAJB1-PRKACA expressing H33 cells demonstrated dose- and time-dependent sensitivity to SY-5609 treatment, there was no change in cell viability in the primary human hepatocyte (PHH) liver cells compared to DMSO control, confirming the lack of toxicity to normal liver cells at the doses tested. We substantiated our findings with separate dose-response experiments in the human hepatocytes at 48-, 72-, and 120-h treatment with SY-5609 (Figure 4F), to ensure minimal toxicity even with increased duration of exposure. Notably, with increasing time, the LC50 (IC50) value of SY-5609 decreased in the H33 cells (IC50 shifts to the left: 7.97E-8 M, 8.67E-9 M, and 1.71E-9 M at 48-, 72-, and 120-h, respectively), indicating both a dose- and time-dependent cell death. Meanwhile, there was a sizable therapeutic window/index as compared to the human hepatocytes, even out to 120-h treatment duration. We therefore identified a therapeutic vulnerability in DNAJB1-PRKACA-expressing H33 cells that was not present in the human fresh liver tissue (i.e., no cytotoxicity), indicating strong potential for CDK7 inhibition as a treatment strategy.

To validate that these changes were due to CDK7 inhibition and not simply suppression of RNA Pol II (or global transcriptional suppression) we tested triptolide, an inhibitor of the translocase activity of TFIIH.32 While we did observe a decrease in cell survival, there was no difference between survival in HepG2 and H33 cells indicating no unique sensitivity in DNAJB1-PRKACA-expressing cells (IC50, HepG2: 9.06E-9 [M], H33: 4.95E-9 [M]). (Figure S6A). Further, we assessed the expression of the FLC-specific genes SLC16A14 and LINC00473 in H33 cells treated with triptolide and only observed a significant decrease in expression at the highest dose tested (i.e., “threshold effect”), without a dose-dependent linear reduction as was seen with CDK7 inhibition (Figure S6B). Taken together, this suggests that DNAJB1-PRKACA-expressing H33 cells are uniquely sensitive to CDK7 inhibition, which is not recapitulated with inhibition of global transcription via generalized RNA Pol II suppression.

CDK7 inhibition suppresses cancer cell growth in human patient-derived models of FLC

To better understand the clinical relevance of our results, we tested CDK7 inhibition independently in five separate patient-derived models. Given the extraordinarily rare nature of FLC, patient-derived models are typically limited. Using a patient-derived FLC cell line that has been well characterized (FLC-H),10,11 we found suppression of cell growth after SY-5609 treatment compared to control treated cells, with a greater than 50% reduction in cell viability (SY-5609 1 μM, p < 0.01) (Figure 5A). Using two distinct patient-derived FLC cell lines (FLC1025 derived from primary tumor and FLCMet derived from metastatic disease), we identified SY-5609-induced dose-dependent reduction in cell viability, confirming the findings from our H33 cells and the FLC-H cell line (Figure 5B). We next tested the effect of SY-5609 in tissue slices derived from human FLC (Figure 5C) and from a separate set of tissue slices derived from a patient-derived xenograft of FLC (Figure 5D). Dual targeting of RNA polymerase II with CDK7 and CDK9 inhibitors as a therapeutic strategy

Given the recalcitrance of FLC to systemic therapy, we recognize that single-agent therapy at clinically tolerable doses is unlikely to overcome this treatment resistance; instead, combination therapy will likely be necessary. We therefore considered partners with CDK7 inhibitors. CDK7 inhibition caused suppressed super-enhancer-driven gene expression, likely through its impact on RNA Pol II Ser 5 phosphorylation. Meanwhile, CDK9 is responsible for phosphorylating RNA Pol II Ser 2 and enables RNA Pol II to transition to effective transcriptional elongation after CDK7-mediated release of RNA Pol II from the pre-initiation complex (Figure 1D).33,34 CDK7 has been reported to potentially modulate CDK9 activity, but the extent that suppression of CDK7 activity can abolish kinase activity of CDK9 is unclear.23,28 Therefore, in consideration for synergistic partners, we evaluated CDK7 inhibition in combination with CDK9 inhibition, which should suppress the aberrant RNA Pol II activity seen in DNAJB1-PRKACA-expressing cells (i.e., human tumors and H33 cells).

When treating DNAJB1-PRKACA-expressing H33 cells with CDK7 inhibitors, we identified that the phosphorylation of pRPB-1 proteins (Ser 2, 5, and 7) were suppressed with a high dose of SY5609 (1–10 μM) (Figure 3C). Interestingly, treating the H33 cells with SY-5609 at 100 nM dose combined with VIP-152 (potent and specific CDK9 inhibitor) at the same dose resulted in markedly reduced RNA Pol II Ser 2, and 5 phosphorylation (Figure 6A).35 We identified a dose-dependent reduction in SLC16A14 and LINC00473 expression, with about 50% reduction in expression at 100 nM of SY5609 alone (Figure 3D). However, the combination of SY5609 (100 nM) and VIP-152 (100 nM) yielded abolished expression of FLC-specific genes including SLC16A14 (96% reduction) and LINC00473 (98% reduction) (Figure 6B). The combination effect was still observed at 10 nM of SY5609 and 100 nM of VIP-152 (SLC16A14 reduction, SY5609 [10 nM]: 30%, VIP-152 [100 nM]: 0%, SY5609 [10 nM] + VIP-152 [100 nM]: 88%, LINC00473 reduction, SY5609 [10 nM]: 46%, VIP-152 [100 nM]: 0%, SY5609 [10 nM] + VIP-152 [100 nM]: 90%) (Figure 6B). Meanwhile VIP-152 alone resulted in no change in expression of the FLC-specific genes. Collectively, these data provide strong support for a novel druggable target in CDK7 that when inhibited causes FLC cell death and suppression of FLC-specific gene expression. Moreover, the connection between DNAJB1-PRKACA, CDK7, RNA Pol II, and super-enhancer-driven gene targets (SLC16A14 and LINC00473) in FLC is further strengthened by the synergistic combination therapy of CDK7 inhibition and CDK9 inhibition (Figure 1D). This combination will be evaluated in future work in patient-derived xenograft models in vivo to optimize the tumoricidal effect while maintaining low toxicity.

Discussion

The research landscape in FLC changed after a seminal study that revealed the presence of the DNAJB1-PRKACA gene fusion in human FLC and was later shown to drive FLC in a mouse model.5,10,11 The clinical importance of DNAJB1-PRKACA in human FLC was subsequently confirmed in two separate studies, including one in which the authors utilized shRNA spanning different regions of the DNAJB1-PRKACA breakpoint to demonstrate that the FLC cells were reliant upon DNAJB1-PRKACA for cell survival (oncogenic addiction).14 This indicates that following tumor initiation, FLC requires the presence of DNAJ-PKAc (and downstream signaling) to survive. Separately, a group with expertise in vaccine therapy and immune targeting of cancer exploited the fact that the DNAJB1-PRKACA fusion transcript generates a protein product (DNAJ-PKAc) that is the clonal oncogenic driver in FLC, and consequently, FLC displays tumor-specific neoantigens that can be recognized by the immune system.36 Thus, combined, these two studies highlight the critical importance of DNAJB1-PRKACA in FLC and that this fusion uniquely promotes a pathogenic mechanism within the cancer cells that is specifically reliant upon the presence of the DNAJ-PKAc protein. However, there has been inconclusive evidence of how DNAJ-PKAc drives cellular changes to promote neoplastic transformation and progression of FLC.

Therefore, identifying how the upstream driver of cellular changes, DNAJ-PKAc, modulates downstream targets to drive cell growth and metastasis should reveal a critical molecule or set of molecules that relay the aberrant signal. A recent publication analyzing the largest set of human FLC samples to date with multi-omics and single-cell approaches confirmed a transcriptomic signature unique to FLC, supporting the idea that a critical set of genes drives the phenotype.20 Among the top upregulated genes was SLC16A14 with a mean tumor expression level (RNAseq) of 19,291 vs. mean normal liver expression level of 95 (q = 1.3E-60).20 Further, evidence from a recent comprehensive analysis of human FLC revealed downstream reprogramming of enhancer regions and development of super-enhancers that drive high levels of transcription of FLC-specific genes, including specific downstream targets SLC16A14 and LINC00473.6,11,14,36 These key genes associated with super-enhancers control cell state and cancer hallmark qualities,15 highlighting the critical importance of the seminal work that identified genes associated with enhancer hotspots.11 These targets have subsequently been the focus of investigation in FLC and demonstrate essential contributions to cancer cell survival.17,37 SLC16A14 is a channel protein and member of the solute-carrier superfamily and monocarboxylate transporter family of proteins; based on the function of known family members that transport essential metabolic substrates (e.g., lactate, pyruvate, etc.), SLC16A14 likely contributes to the metabolic function of the cancer cells.38,39,40,41 However, the true function of this solute carrier in DNAJB1-PRKACA-driven carcinoma remains elusive, and similarly, why inhibition of SLC16A14 causes cancer cell death is yet to be determined.37 Meanwhile, LINC00473 is a long noncoding RNA that appears to suppress apoptosis and is also intimately involved in modulating tumor cell metabolism.17 In each of these studies, outside of the epigenetic remodeling, it is unclear the connection between DNAJ-PKAc and high levels of gene expression. Therefore, to better understand the link between DNAJ-PKAc and these super-enhancer-driven genes, we investigated RNA Pol II activity which is known to be highly enriched at super-enhancers. We identified evidence of increased RNA Pol II phosphorylation in human tumor (FLC) vs. normal tissues, which may be a consequence of DNAJ-PKAc causing redirection of CDK7 activity toward the super-enhancers. In support, we observed increased expression of super-enhancer associated genes SLC16A14 and LINC00473 with the introduction of DNAJB1-PRKACA in human-derived cancer cells, and inhibition of CDK7 caused suppressed expression of both SLC16A14 and LINC00473. Of note, this is an exciting result given these two highly promising targets in FLC currently have no treatments available.

CDK7 is a serine/threonine protein kinase that exists as a CAK and has gained significant interest as a target in cancer due to its dual roles in cell cycle regulation and transcriptional regulation.18,42 The role of CDK7 has been studied in other cancers such as breast, prostate, and lung; however, to our knowledge this present study represents the first work looking at CDK7 in FLC. CDK7 targets the heptad sequence (Tyr-Ser-Pro-Thr-Ser-Pro-Ser) of the CTD in RNA Pol II, with preferential phosphorylation of Ser 5 in the sequence.19 This is notable due to the fact that among the serine targets, we identified Ser 5 as the most differentially phosphorylated in human FLC tumor versus normal liver and in H33 (HepG2DNAJB1−PRKACA) versus HepG2 cells. Work by Rimel et al. suggests CDK7 plays a “master regulator” role in transcription. While bound to transcription factor II H (TFIIH), it is restricted to acting on RNA Pol-II. However, release from TFIIH allows it to phosphorylate various other target proteins (e.g., CDK1, CDK2), which was a reproducible finding in a separate study.42,43 Therefore, the distinct mechanisms of CDK7 function allow for high specificity and selectivity of substrates, with CDK2 phosphorylation and RNA Pol II CTD phosphorylation representing divergent outputs of CDK7 activation. This mechanistic insight not only accounts for our phosphoprotein results (i.e., preferential phosphorylation of RNA Pol II CTD Ser 5), but also explains the other phenotypic changes that occurred with CDK7 inhibition (SY-5609) in the engineered cell line model (i.e., G0/G1 cell-cycle arrest and suppressed phosphorylation of CDK2 versus suppressed RNA Pol II phosphorylation and suppression of FLC-specific super-enhancer-driven gene expression).

We utilized selective CDK7 inhibitors to provide insight on the clinical relevance of targeting the aberrantly activated CDK7 pathway. For instance, we used SY-5609 throughout this study because it is a potent and selective CDK7 inhibitor with demonstrated effects on cell cycle, transcription, and apoptosis in vitro and in vivo.25 Notably, SY-5609 is currently in use in clinical trials, with a defined safety profile in humans (NCT04247126 and NCT04929223).25 To confirm that the effects of SY-5609 were CDK7 dependent, we also utilized a separate covalent binding CDK7 inhibitor (YKL-5-124) as well as siRNA to CDK7. We utilized two separate compounds as well as siRNA to CDK7 in our initial studies (H33 cells) to support the involvement of aberrant CDK7 function in FLC and confirmed the efficacy of our selected compound (SY-5609) in patient-derived models of FLC. Further, we demonstrate that treatment with a global transcriptional repressor of RNA Pol II, triptolide, does not produce the same preferential effect on DNAJB1-PRKACA-expressing cells as CDK7-specific inhibition. This suggests that CDK7 does in fact represent a unique target in DNAJB1-PRKACA-driven carcinoma.

In addition to promoting cell death in cells harboring the DNAJB1-PRKACA fusion, CDK7 activity was also shown to function as a regulator of components of the super-enhancer-driven landscape in FLC, similar to prior preclinical data supporting CDK7 as a lead target for super-enhancer-driven cancer types (i.e., myelocytomatosis [MYC]).16,29,30 This idea is supported by a study by Neumayr et al. that shows the enhancer within the SLC16A14 gene activates an origin of replication core promoter dependent on CDK7 cofactors, strengthening our evidence that SLC16A14 expression in FLC is dependent on CDK7 activity.44 Notably, SLC16A14 is remarkably specific for FLC, with minimal expression across normal tissue types, other primary cancers, and other metastatic cancers; therefore, SLC16A14 presents a highly intriguing and promising target. Concordantly, we reasoned that therapeutic strategies to use in FLC should suppress these DNAJB1-PRKACA-driven super-enhancer-associated genes, particularly SLC16A14 which serves as a strong marker of DNAJB1-PRKACA-expressing cells.20,37 We capitalized on this concept throughout the current work to demonstrate that CDK7 inhibition represents a strong candidate therapy, and this concept also allowed us to uncover that CDK7 inhibition synergizes effectively with CDK9 inhibitors VIP-152 and NVP-2. CDK9 is another member of the CDK family and is a known phosphorylation target of CDK7, including the Thr186 residue which is necessary for enzymatic activity of the protein.45 CDK9 inhibition, both in isolation and in combination with CDK7, is being investigated in a wide variety of solid malignancies including prostate, lung, breast, ovarian, pancreatic, and esophageal cancer among others.46 Further, there is already evidence that CDK9 expression plays a role in hepatocellular carcinoma, specifically that it is required for MYC-overexpressing tumors.47 Given the robust response of DNAJB1-PRKACA expressing cells to CDK7 inhibitor monotherapy presented above, we hypothesized that further inhibition of components of the aberrantly activated CDK7 pathway would lead to a synergistic response. Our pre-clinical data suggest that CDK7 and CDK9 dual inhibition is a potential novel target for treatment of DNAJB1-PRKACA-driven malignancies and warrants further investigation.

The data presented here support a role of CDK7 in FLC. Targeting of CDK7 induces cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis, and suppression of key FLC marker genes. Notably, this work also begins to untangle the regulation of super-enhancer-driven genes that are uniquely expressed in FLC as a result of DNAJB1-PRKACA and provide mechanistic insight into the unique transcriptional signature of FLC.11,20 Collectively, we provide the basis for CDK7 inhibition as a strong candidate for therapy and for pursuing additional investigations (including patient-derived xenograft models) to clarify the optimal combination therapy that may be applied in human FLC.

Limitations of the study

Despite the compelling data from this current work, a key question that remains is how DNAJ-PKAc causes aberrant activation of CDK7 to promote transcriptional dysregulation. While our discovery advances our mechanistic understanding of DNAJ-PKAc in FLC, the proximity of CDK7 to DNAJ-PKAc is indeterminate. For instance, we do not see clear evidence of increased CDK7 phosphorylation in DNAJ-PKAc-expressing cells/tumors. Further, it is unknown whether CDK7 activity is directly regulated by the fusion oncoprotein, or whether intermediate proteins transmit the signal from DNAJ-PKAc. Although there is evidence that native PKA and CDK7 may have overlapping targets (e.g., nuclear retinoic acid receptor alpha),48 currently, there is no evidence that native PKA directly regulates CDK7 activity. And while it is known that DNAJ-PKAc induces abnormal cAMP signaling and phosphorylates targets distinct from native PKA,12,49 our findings are the first evidence (to our knowledge) of the DNAJ-PKAc oncoprotein causing altered CDK7 pathway activity. Due to the complexity of CDK7 function (i.e., dual function in regulation of cell cycle and transcriptional regulation via RNA Pol II phosphorylation that is dependent on the phosphorylation status of CDK7 itself),19,42,50 there is likely a multifaceted interplay between DNAJ-PKAc and CDK7. In future work, we will seek to understand how DNAJ-PKAc modulates the CDK7 protein to promote transcriptional remodeling.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethics statement

This study was conducted according to Institutional Review Board protocols 2016-0934 (University of Wisconsin-Madison), 33970/1 (Fibrolamellar Cancer Foundation) and 1765 (Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center). Informed written consent was obtained from all human subjects or from the subject’s parent or legal guardian if the subject is a minor.

Human tissue samples

Normal liver samples from patients were obtained from the Translational Science Biocore (TSB)-BioBank at the University of Wisconsin-Madison. FLC tissue samples (FLC 55, 57, 82, 83, 84, 87, 89, 106,110,109,114) were obtained from the Fibrolamellar Cancer Foundation. Patient tumor derived fibrolamellar carcinoma (FLC4) for generation of the patient tumor derived organoid was obtained from the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center.

Cell line and culture

Hepatoblastoma HepG2 cell line was obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA). HepG2 cells are isolated from a 15 year old white male youth. Cells were maintained in RPMI1640 culture medium (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gemini Bio Products, Sacramento, CA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin-glutamine (Life Technologies) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Immortalized Human Hepatocytes (IHHs) were obtained from Applied Biological Materials (T0063, Richmond, BC). The sex of the donor of IHHs is not disclosed. IHHs were cultured in William’s E medium (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gemini Bio Products, Sacramento, CA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin-glutamine (Life Technologies) on Collagen I (Rat tail) -coated culture dishes (Corning, New York, NY) at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Method details

Generation of DNAJB1-PRKACA expressing cells

To generate HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA cells, mammalian dual-guide RNAs-CRISPR-CAS9-EGFP vector was designed (VectorBuilder, Chicago, IL). The guide RNA sequences were designed for excision of the region between the first introns of DNAJB1 and PRKACA on human Chromosome 19 using CRISPOR tool (gRNA1 (DNAJB1) 5′-CAGGAGCCGACCCCGTTCGT-3′, gRNA2 (PRKACA): 5′-GTAGACGCGGTTGCGCTAAG-3′).51 HepG2 cells were transfected with 10 μg of dual-guide RNAs-CRISPR-CAS9-EGFP vector and Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 2 days. Single GFP positive cells were sorted at 4°C using a BD FACSAria III (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) and deposited in 1% gelatin-coated 96 well plates. The excision of the DNAJB1-PRKACA region was confirmed by PCR. The PCR was performed by using a forward primer (DNAJB1-intron 1-F, 5′-AGCTTCTAGCATGTCTGGGG-3′), and a reverse primer (PRKACA-intron 1-R, 5′-CTGGGAAGGCTCATGAGACCT-3′) for DNAJB1-PRKACA fusion site on the genome. Genomic DNA was extracted from clones using Wizard Genomic DNA purification kit (Promega, Madison, WI) and PCR was conducted at a temperature of 98 °C for 5 minutes (1 cycle), 98 °C for 30 seconds, 58 °C for 45 seconds and 72 °C for 1 minute (32 cycles) and 72 °C for 10 minutes (1 cycle) using GoTaq G2 DNA polymerase (Promega, Madison, WI). Additionally, DNAJB1-PRKACA fusion mRNA expression was confirmed by reverse-transcription (RT)-PCR. Total RNA was extracted from cells using the Qiagen RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Complementary DNA was synthesized using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) as per the manufacturer’s instruction and PCR was carried out at a temperature of 95 °C for 5 minutes (1 cycle), 95 °C for 30 seconds, 56 °C for 30 seconds and 72 °C for 30 seconds (30 cycles) and 72 °C for 5 minutes using GoTaq DNA polymerase (Promega, Madison, WI) and a forward primer (hChimera exon 1-F, 5′-GTTCAAGGAGATCGCTGAGG-3), a reverse primer (hChimera exon 3, 5′-TTCCCGGTCTCCTTGTGTTT-3′). The amplified PCR products were isolated and subcloned into pGEM-T easy vector (Promega, Madison, WI) and sequenced using Bigdye terminator v3.1 (Applied Biosystems). The Sequence was analyzed by SnapGene software (Snapgene, San Diego, CA).

Cell viability assay

To determine the changes in cell proliferation in HepG2 cells and HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA clones, we used CellTiter Glo 2 assay systems (Promega, Madison, WI). Approximately 2.0 × 103 cells/well were seeded in a 96-well plate and incubated in RPMI1640 culture medium over 4 days at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After addition of the CellTiter Glo 2 reagent to cell culture medium in 96 well plate and incubation for 10 minutes, luminescence was measured by CLARIOstar Plus microplate reader (BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany). Additionally, the growth of HepG2 cells and HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA clones were measured using hemocytometer cell counting. Cells (5.0 ×104 cells/well) were seeded in a 6-well plate and incubated in RPMI1640 culture medium for 4 days at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were trypsinized, stained with 0.4% Trypan Blue (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) and counted using hemocytometer (Day 0, 2 and 4).

For drug-response analysis, HepG2 cells and HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA clones (H12, H33) (2.0 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in a 96-well plate and incubated with RPMI1640 cell culture medium for 24 hours. After incubation, cells were treated with SY-5609 (1 pM-10 μM) (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ), YKL-5-124 (100 pM-10 μM) (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ) or vehicle (DMSO) for 48, 72 hours or 120 hours. Cell viability was measured using CellTiter Glo 2 reagent. Percent of survived cells and growth inhibition were calculated relative to vehicle control (as 100% of survival). The dose-response curves and IC50 values were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (San Diego, CA). Differences between conditions were tested with a t-test and a p < 0.05 was taken as statistically significant.

Caspase 3/7 and 8 assay

To measure the apoptosis activity in HepG2 cells and HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA clones, we used Caspase-Glo 3/7 and 8 assay systems (Promega, Madison, WI). Approximately 2.0 × 103 cells/well were seeded in a 96-well plate and (1) incubated in RPMI1640 culture medium over 4 days at 37 °C and 5% CO2 or (2) incubated in RPMI1640 culture medium containing SY-5609 (0.1-10 μM) (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ) or DMSO for 24 hours. After addition of the Caspase-Glo 3/7 or 8 reagent to cell culture medium in 96 well plate and incubation for 30 minutes, luminescence was measured by CLARIOstar Plus microplate reader (BMG Labtech, Cary, NC).

Cell cycle analysis

To synchronize cell cycle in HepG2 cells and HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA clones, cells were treated with serum starvation medium (RPMI1640 medium (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin-glutamine (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA)) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 16 hours. Then serum starvation medium was (1) replaced to RPMI1640 culture medium and cells were collected over 24 hours (every 6 hours) or (2) replaced to RPMI1640 culture medium containing SY-5609 (1 μM) (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ) or DMSO for 24 hours and cells were collected. The collected cells were fixed with 70% ethanol and stained with 50 μg/mL propidium iodide (BioLegend, San Diego, CA) and 100 μg/mL RNAse A (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA). Samples were sorted with an Attune NxT flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA) and the DNA content was analyzed for the percent of cells in G0/G1, S, and G2/M phase with ModFit LT 6.0 software at the University of Wisconsin Carbone Cancer Center Flow Cytometry Laboratory. The average coefficient of variation (CV) of each sample was < 6%. Differences between conditions were tested with a t-test and a p < 0.05 was taken as statistically significant.

RNA isolation, sequencing, differential gene expression analysis

We performed RNA isolation and RNA sequencing as previously described.52 Briefly, to evaluate for transcriptomic differences between HepG2 and HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA clones (H33 and H12 cells), bulk RNA-seq was performed on 6 biological replicates collected from each cell line. RNA Isolation was carried out using the RNeasy protocol (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Quality was tested for an RNA integrity number (RIN) > 7.5 on the Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). A total of 300 ng of mRNA was enriched with poly-A selection and sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq2500 platform by the University of Wisconsin Biotechnology Sequencing Core. FASTq files were processed with Skewer and genes were filtered to remove those with low expression.53 Samples were normalized by the method of trimmed mean of M-values.54 Contrasts were drawn with the edgeR package, with differential expression taken when the FDR q < 0.05.55 Pathway testing was performed with the KEGG database (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) using previously described methods.56 The top 500 significant genes were inputted, ordered by q value, and the top significant pathways ordered by q-value (an adjusted multiple testing p-value found using the false discovery rate approach of Benjamini and Hochberg)57 were plotted for visualization. Pathway dot size is indicative of the number of genes in each pathway. All RNA seq data is available at Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) accession number GSE232374.

For the dose-dependent RNAseq experiment, RNA isolation and bulk RNA-seq was performed on H33 cells treated with DMSO, 1 nM SY-5609, 10 nM SY-5609, 100 nM SY-5609 and 300 nM SY-5609. Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and integrity were assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA), and only samples with an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) > 7.5 were used for library preparation. A total of 300 ng of RNA per sample was subjected to poly(A) enrichment for mRNA selection. Strand-specific, paired-end libraries (Illumina TruSeq) were prepared and sequenced (2 × 150 bp) on the Illumina NovaSeq X+ platform by the University of Wisconsin Biotechnology Center Sequencing Core. Raw sequencing reads were adapter-trimmed using Skewer,53 and read quality was monitored throughout using standard ENCODE RNA-Seq pipeline metrics.58 Trimmed reads were aligned to the human reference genome patch version GRCh38.p10 (accession GCA_000001405.25) using STAR,59 a splice-aware aligner, with corresponding gene annotations from Ensembl release 90. Transcript-level abundance estimates were calculated with RSEM (v1.3.1),60 which uses expectation-maximization to infer gene and isoform expression levels. Expected counts from RSEM were rounded to the nearest integer and used for differential expression analysis. Gene-level expression data were analyzed using the edgeR package (v3.40.1)55 in the R statistical environment (v4.2.2). Libraries were normalized using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM)61 method to account for composition bias across sample libraries. Genes were filtered to retain only those with counts per million (CPM) above a threshold in at least as many libraries as there were replicates per condition. This filtering step, which required a minimum of 10 raw reads, minimized background noise and enhanced the statistical power of subsequent analyses. Differential expression was modeled using a negative binomial generalized linear model. Resulting p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method,61 with a significance threshold set at q < 0.05. Distribution of raw p-values was visually inspected to validate the multiple testing correction.

We processed the mRNA-Seq data in terms of a dose-response (or concentration-response) characterization. Briefly, after import of the raw data (rsem_GENE.txt) the data was normalized (TMM) and transformed (VST) to reduce variation, which was already minimal. Next, the primary aim of the workflow was to select significantly responsive transcripts (step 2) exhibiting dose-response character. This was done by choosing the best-fit model among a predefined family of biphasic models (quadratic was opted for as very few genes exhibited monotonic [linear] behavior) to describe the response of each gene across each of the four doses, using DMSO (0) as the baseline using an AIC (prevents over-fitting, which is common in these analyses) to choose the best model taking into account model parameter numbers. FDR as set to 0.05 and results exported to MS Excel file, sorted by significance. Only those genes exhibiting a FDR <= 0.05 are included (Data S4).

Functional enrichment analysis

To identify pathways significantly enriched among differentially expressed genes, we performed enrichment analysis using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. The top 500 differentially expressed genes (ranked by FDR-adjusted q-values) were submitted for pathway enrichment using the function enrichKEGG in clusterProfiler 4.6.2.62 Significant pathways (q < 0.05) were visualized, with dot plots representing pathway significance (adjusted q-value) and gene count within each pathway. Dot size was scaled by the number of input genes mapping to each KEGG pathway.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)

HepG2 and HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA clones were incubated with RPMI1640 cell culture for 24 hours, treated with SY-5609 (1-300 nM) (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ), YKL-5-124 (1-300 nM) (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ) or vehicle (DMSO) for 24 hours. Total RNA was isolated from cells using the Qiagen RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The isolated RNAs (20 ng) were reverse-transcribed and quantitative PCR (qPCR) were perform using GoTaq Probe 1-Step RT-PCR system (Promega, Madison, WI) in a QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The reverse-transcription was conducted at a temperature of 45 °C for 15 minutes, and 95 °C for 2 minutes during one cycle. The denaturation, annealing and extension were performed at 95 °C for 15 seconds and 60 °C for 1 minutes (45 cycles). The mRNA levels were measured with Taqman Gene Expression System (FAM-MGB) (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA) (Assay ID: DNAJB1: Hs00356730_g1, DNAJB1-PRKACA fusion: Hs05061318_ft, SLC16A14: Hs00541300_m1, LINC00473: Hs00293257_m1, VCAN: Hs00171642_m1, OAT: Hs00236852_m1, GAPDH: Hs02786624_g1, β-Actin: Hs01060665_g1). To detect PRKACA mRNA expression, the qPCR primers (Primer 1: 5′-AGCGGGACTTTCCCATTT-3′, Primer 2: 5′-AAGAAGGGCAGCGAGCA-3′) and FAM-labeled probe (5′-FAM-TGGCTTTGGCTAAGAATTCTTTCACGC-3′) were designed using PrimerQuest Tool (IDT, Newark, NJ). The qPCR primers and probe spanned the boundary of exon 1-2 junction since DNAJB1-PRKACA fusion gene harbors exon 2-10 of PRKACA gene. GAPDH and β-Actin mRNA levels were used as internal control and the data were analyzed using the ΔΔCt method.

Immunoblotting

HepG2 cells and HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA clones were lysed and homogenated in CelLytic M lysis reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) with Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA). IHHs, FLC tumors and FLC organoids were lysed and homogenated in RIPA Lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA) with Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA). For immunoblotting, 20 μg of whole cell extract or 40-80 μg of liver homogenate was loaded on Mini-Protean TGX protein gel (4-20% or 7.5%) (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) and transferred to Immobilon-P membranes by electrophoresis (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA). Membranes were incubated in 10% w/v BSA blocking buffer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA) at room temperature for 1 hour and hybridized with primary antibody (PKA(c): 610981 (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ), CDK2: #2546 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA), phospho-CDK2 (Thr160): #2561 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA), CDK7: #2916 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA), phospho-CDK7 (T170): ab155976 (Abcam, Cambrige, MA), RNA polymerase II RPB-1: RPB-1 NTD; #14958, Phospho-CTD (Ser2); #13499, Phospho-CTD (Ser5); #13523, Phospho-CTD (Ser7); #13780 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA), SCG2; PA5-115018 (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA), PARP; #9542 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA), Cleaved PARP (Asp214); #5625 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA), β-Actin: #4967 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA), MAB8929 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) at 4°C overnight. After hybridization with Alkaline Phosphatase (AP)-conjugated secondary antibody (anti-mouse: #7056 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA), anti-rabbit: 111-055-144 (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA)), proteins were detected using NBT/BCIP solution (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA). The samples were loaded separately and membranes were developed independently. The protein levels were quantified using ImageJ software (Fiji). Original, unedited Western Blot images are available through Figshare: https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.30366184.

siRNA transfection

HepG2 cells and HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA clones were transfected in 96-well plate with 0.25-100 nM Silencer small interfering RNA (siRNA) (Negative Control: #1, CDK7: s2830) (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) and Stealth RNAi (CDK7: CDK7VHS40192) (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA). Four days after the transfection, cell viability was measured using CellTiter-Glo 2 reagent (Promega, Madison, WI). After addition of the reagent to cell culture medium, luminescence was measured by CLARIOstar Plus microplate reader (BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany). For immnoblotting, HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA cells (H33 clone) were transfected with siRNA (negative control or siCDK7, 1-10 nM) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent for 48 hours. Whole cell extracts were prepared using CelLytic M lysis reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) with Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA).

Primary human hepatocyte

Primary human hepatocytes (PHHs) were prepared from fresh human liver samples. Liver samples were perfused using perfusion solution I and liver tissues were dissociated by Digestion-Solution containing collagenase P.63 PHHs were isolated and purified from hepatocyte rich cell suspension using 25% Percoll solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) for density gradient centrifugation. Approximately 2.0 × 104 purified PHH cells/well were seeded in rat tail collagen (collagen type I) (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) coated 96-well plate and incubated in hepatocyte Incubation medium at 37 °C and 5% CO2.63 Twenty-four hours after incubation, PHHs were treated with SY-5609 (100 pM - 10 μM) (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ) or vehicle (DMSO) for 2-5 days. Cell viability was detected using CellTiter Glo 2 reagent (Promega, Madison, WI). After addition of the reagent to cell culture medium, luminescence was measured by CLARIOstar Plus microplate reader (BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany).

Drug combination study

To investigate the synergistic effect between SY-5609 (CDK7 inhibitor) and VIP-152 or NVP-2 (CDK9 inhibitors, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ), HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA (H33) cells (2.0 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in an 8x8 matrix in a 96-well plate and incubated with RPMI1640 cell culture medium for 24 hours at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were treated with SY-5609 (0.1-100 nM), VIP-152 (1-100 nM), NVP-2 (0.1-10 nM) or vehicle (DMSO) for 72 hours. Cell viability was measured using CellTiter Glo 2 reagent. Percent of survived cells and growth inhibition were calculated relative to vehicle control (as 100% of survival). Synergy scores and the dose-response curves were analyzed using SynergyFinder tool. Drug synergy was evaluated using the highest single-agent (HSA) model and Loewe model.64 The combination effect of SY-5609 and VIP-152 on FLC-specific gene expression and CDK7-mediated RNA Pol II activity was examined in HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA (H33) cells. HepG2DNAJB1-PRKACA (H33) cells were treated with SY-5609 (10-100 nM) and VIP-152 (1-100 nM), NVP-2 (1-10 nM) or vehicle (DMSO) for 24 hours. The total RNA and whole cell lysate were isolated for RT-qPCR and immunoblotting (described above).

FLC human cell line

FLC-H (FLX1) cells were generated from a patient-derived xenograft model (Oikawa et al., 2015) and grown in RPMI1640 media containing 300 mg/L l-glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and 2.5 μg/mL human hepatic growth factor (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Cells were cultured in a humid chamber at 37°C and 5% CO2. FLC1025 and FLCmet3 cells were generated directly from a human FLC tumors (Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center) and maintained in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), 0.04 μg/mL dexamethasone (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), 0.1% gentamicin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), 1 μg/mL recombinant human insulin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), 0.55 μg/mL human transferrin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), and 0.5 ng/mL sodium selenite (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The media was supplemented with 10% DMEM from cultured human embryonic kidney cells harboring a human RSPO1 transgene and 20 μM Y-27632 ROCK inhibitor. FLC cells were plated at a density of 0.1 x 106 cells/well in 12-well plates. After overnight incubation, cells were treated with vehicle control or SY-5609. Six days post-treatment cells were harvested for counting by hemocytometer. Cell viability was determined using trypan blue staining. Cell counting was performed in three biological replicates per trial and two trials were performed for each condition.

FLC human organoid

Patient tumor derived fibrolamellar carcinoma (FLC4-PDX) for generation of the patient tumor derived organoid was obtained from the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center.65 Xenograft tumors were then developed from the FLC4-PDX tissue in Rag2/IL2RG (R2G2, Inotiv, Indianapolis, IN) mice at the University of Wisconsin-Madison. 3D organoid models were developed from the xenograft tumors. Organoids were grown and maintained in 50% growth factor reduced Cultrex matrix (R&D Systems, Minenapolis, MN) overlayed with Advanced DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Waltham, MA) media supplemented with 1x Glutamax (Gibco, Waltham, MA), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (VWR, Randor, PA), 10mM HEPES (Gibco, Waltham, MA), 10mM nicotinamide (Sigma, St. Louis, MO), 0.5mM N-acetyl-L-cysteine (Sigma), 0.4x B27 without vitamin A (Gibco, Waltham, MA), 500nM A83-01 (Sigma, St. Louis, MO), 500nM SB202190 (MedChem Express, Monmouth Junction, NJ), 500pM Wnt surrogate fusion peptide (ImmunoPrecise, Fargo, ND), 100ng/mL human recombinant noggin (Acro, Newark, DE), 50ng/mL human recombinant EGF (Gibco, Waltham, MA), 500pM HA tagged R-Spondin 1 (produced in house), and 10nM Y27632 (MedChem Express, Monmouth Junction, NJ). Dose response curves were produced by plating FLC PDX organoids at a density of 5000 cells per well in a 96 well plate (Ibidi, Fitchburg, WI). After 24 hours, cells were treated with SY-5609, VIP-152, combination, or DMSO control. VIP-152 was removed after 24 hours of treatment. All media was replaced after 24 hours and 72 hours. Viability was determined using 3D CellTiterGlo (Promega, Madison, WI) at 144 hours. Human FLC organoid were plated for qPCR samples in 12 well plates (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA) and allowed to reach confluency. Confluent plates were treated with SY-5609, VIP-152, combination, or DMSO control. VIP-152 was removed and all media across the experiment was changed at 24 hours. After 96 hours, organoids were removed from the plates and washed twice with 1x PBS. Pellets were resuspended in 250μL of RNAlater (Sigma, St Louis, MO) and frozen at -80C until RNA extraction.

Tissue slice preparation

FLC tumor slices were prepared as described previously.66,67 Briefly, dissected tumor tissues were cut into 400-μm organotypic tumor slices using the Leica VT1200S vibratome microtome (Nusslock) with HBSS as the cutting medium. The slices were then cut into 400-μm cuboids using a McIlwain tissue chopper (Ted Pella) as described previously.66 Cuboids were immediately placed into 96-well ultralow-attachment plates (Corning, Corning, NY) and incubated with Williams’ medium containing 12 mM nicotinamide, 150 nM ascorbic acid, 2.25 mg/mL sodium bicarbonate, 20 mM HEPES, 50 mg/mL additional glucose, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1% (v/v) ITS, 20 ng/mL EGF, 40 IU/mL penicillin, and 40 μg/mL streptomycin containing RealTime Glo reagent (Promega, Madison, WI) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 24 h, the baseline cell viability of cuboids was measured by RealTime Glo bioluminescence using the Synergy H4 instrument (Biotek, Winooski, VT). Cuboids were exposed to DMSO (control), SY-5609 (0.1 - 1 μM) or Staurosporine (0.5 μM; positive control), and overall tumor tissue viability was measured daily, up to 7 d after treatment.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism software (San Diego, CA) unless otherwise indicated. Samples were not excluded from the analysis unless explicitly stated. Standard comparisons of the mean were performed with a t-test with standard deviation unless explicitly stated.
