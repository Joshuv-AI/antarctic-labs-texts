Modular generation of multispecific antibodies using protein trans-splicing
Authors: Jared A Shadish, John D Bagert, Amber N Lee, Kenneth K Sharp, Chunning Yang, Yijia Li, Rui Wang, Seungwook Shin, Alina A Malik, Jia Lin, Fengying Huang, Wenhai Liu, Yariv Mazor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMultispecific antibodies are exhibiting rapid growth in drug development due to their ability to engage multiple therapeutic targets and deliver unique functional properties inaccessible to conventional monoclonal antibodies. The development of these complex modalities, however, is limited by the large design space created by the numerous possible combinations associated with multiple binding moieties and molecular formats. Here, we present a high-throughput platform for the efficient production and screening of complex combinatorial multispecific antibody libraries using intein-mediated protein splicing. To validate the fidelity of this screening approach, we demonstrate that spliced bispecific molecules exhibit indistinguishable biochemical and functional properties compared to bispecific antibodies generated through conventional production processes. We further demonstrate that intein splicing can be combined with the DuetMab platform to create trispecific antibodies and perform dual-splicing reactions to generate bivalent bispecific and trispecific formats. This work establishes intein-based protein splicing as a powerful strategy for the efficient generation and screening of multispecific antibodies libraries, enabling the rapid identification of optimal binding configurations, combinations, and molecular formats that elicit the desired mechanism of action.KEYWORDS: Multispecific, bispecific, intein, combinatorial, high-throughput, DuetMab

Introduction

Multispecific antibodies have emerged as a leading class of biologics with broad therapeutic potential.1 Unlike conventional monoclonal antibodies which bind a single target, multispecific antibodies can simultaneously engage more than one epitope, which has been leveraged to enhanced target specificity, broaden disease coverage, and provide access to novel mechanisms of action such as immune cell redirection, targeted protein degradation, and half-life extension.2–4 Since the first bispecific antibody approval in 2014, the development of these modalities has surged, with over 560 multispecific antibodies currently in clinical trials and over a thousand in pre-clinical development.5 While early molecules were primarily monovalent or bivalent bispecific antibodies, the field has progressed to include more complex formats, including trispecific and tetraspecific designs.

A key driver of progress in the multispecific antibody field has been the maturation of protein engineering technologies that facilitate the production of these complex modalities. While a straightforward strategy involves fusing single-chain binding domains in tandem into a single polypeptide chain, therapeutic development has primarily focused on IgG-like formats. These constructs retain the crystallizable fragment (Fc) domain, allowing them to exploit neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn)-mediated recycling to extend serum half-life, harness antibody effector function as a mechanism of action, and take advantage of established monoclonal antibody production and development infrastructure.6,7 Several technologies and platforms to produce IgG-like bispecific antibodies have been established, and hundreds of these modalities are currently in clinical development.8

A critical challenge in the development of multispecific antibody therapeutics is the large design space commonly encountered during the screening of these complex molecules. In particular, the vast number of possible binder combinations for multiple targets poses significant manufacturing challenges, which can be further compounded by the inclusion of multiple molecular geometries. To tackle this combinatorial challenge, creating libraries of multispecifics from a smaller pool of modular building blocks can greatly simplify and expedite the production process. Several strategies for efficiently screening multispecifics have been developed, typically employing either molecular rearrangement or chemoenzymatic methods. Controlled Fab arm exchange utilizes mutations in the Fc that facilitate heavy chain decomposition and exchange under mild reducing conditions, allowing bispecifics to be reconstituted after subsequent oxidation.9 Multispecifics have also been produced by chemical ligation. For example, disulfide rebridging has been used to add click chemistry handles to the interchain disulfides of two different Fabs, allowing their subsequent conjugation into a bispecific molecule.10 The enzymes Spytag and Sortase have also found utility in generating bispecific antibodies through enzymatic ligation, in which genetically encoded recognition motifs allow for the site-specific functionalization and conjugation of separate domains.11,12

Split inteins provide a particularly attractive solution for the modular generation of multispecific antibodies due to their specificity, mild reaction conditions, and ability to ligate flanking polypeptides together with minimal alteration to native protein sequences.13 Production of a monovalent bispecific IgG, for example, entails the combination of a Fab fused to an N-intein fragment with an IgG heterodimer in which one arm contains a Fab and the other is fused to a C-intein fragment (Figure 1A). This process has been shown for the production of monovalent bispecific IgGs and has been demonstrated to be compatible with automation technologies that enable subsequent high-throughput screening.14,15 The technology was further expanded to monovalent bispecific antibodies with a near-scarless splice junction as well as bivalent bispecific antibodies.16,17

Here, we present methods for the rapid generation of multispecific antibodies with fully customizable format, valency, and geometry using split intein technology. We validate this screening approach by demonstrating that multispecific antibodies generated with split inteins faithfully reconstitute the biochemical, biophysical, and functional properties of antibodies generated through conventional processes. We further demonstrate that this technology can be integrated with the DuetMab platform to generate complex multispecific modalities, including monovalent trispecific, bivalent bispecific, and bivalent trispecific modalities. Overall, this approach provides a versatile and efficient platform for high-throughput production and screening of multispecific antibody therapeutics, accelerating the discovery of optimal binder combinations and molecular formats that drive desired therapeutic functions.

Results

Molecule design and nomenclature

In this study, we demonstrate the power and versatility of intein splicing to generate a broad array of multispecific antibodies with diverse binders, valencies, and formats (Figure 1B). To enable clear discussion of the diverse and complex antibody formats described in this study, we provide a schematic of all antibody building blocks used, along with a consistent naming convention. We utilize the CfaGEP split intein, which has been engineered for enhanced protein trans-splicing activity, expression, and robustness to extein-dependence. When used with our selected extein residues, AEY for the N-extein and CFN for the C-extein, the CfaGEP intein has a reported reaction yield of 90%.18,19 We first created a set of four distinct Fabs with their C-termini fused to the Cfa N-intein (Figures 2A, B, and Supplementary Figure S1A). These Fab-IntN building blocks are represented by letters a–d and bind distinct epitopes on target antigens HER2, EGFR, and cMET. A cognate library of 10 IgG “scaffolds,” represented by numbers 1–10, were created containing the Fc of human IgG1, one or more Fabs, and the Cfa C-intein appended to the N-terminus of one or both heavy chains (Figure 2C, and Supplementary Figure S1B).

Building blocks include (B) Cfa N-intein Fab fusions (Fab-IntNs, lowercase letters a – d) and (C) human IgG1scaffolds fused with the C-intein domain of Cfa (IntC-scaffolds, numbers 1–10). (D) When combined the intein domains ligate the adjoining protein domains, reconstituting a multispecific antibody (corresponding number + lowercase letter) with the splice site marked by a red asterisk. Throughout this manuscript, we identify C-intein scaffolds as numbers, N-intein Fabs as letters, and the multispecific antibody generated via protein splicing of cognate intein components as the corresponding number-letter combination. For example, IntC-scaffold 1 and Fab-IntN a produce the spliced molecule 1a. From the presented set of 4 Fab-IntN and 10 IntC-scaffold building blocks, it is possible to generate 40 distinct combinations of multispecific antibodies. Here, we produce a representative subset of 16 multispecific antibodies that span a variety of formats, including monovalent bispecific (1 + 1), monovalent trispecific (1 + 1 + 1), bi/mono-valent bispecific (2 + 1), bivalent bispecific (2 + 2), and bi/mono-valent trispecific (2 + 1 + 1) (Figure 2D, Supplementary Figure S1C). We also generate a set of monovalent bispecific antibodies using the DuetMab platform to serve as benchmarks for validating the fidelity of molecules generated via protein splicing (Figure 2E).20 These benchmarks are identified with a number and uppercase letter that corresponds to that of the spliced molecule such that, for example, 1A is the benchmark molecule for the spliced molecule 1a. Finally, all antibody-intein fusion constructs were produced with titers greater than 200 mg/L, demonstrating consistent production capabilities of engineered antibody-intein fusions (Supplementary Figure S2).

Monovalent bispecific antibodies produced by protein splicing have similar physicochemical and functional properties to their DuetMab counterparts

For protein splicing technology to be a viable tool for screening multispecific antibodies, the resulting molecules must accurately replicate the properties of multispecific therapeutics generated through conventional approaches. As an initial test, we reacted an IntC-scaffold containing an αCD3 Fab (1) with an αEGFR Fab-IntN (a) to produce a monovalent bispecific IgG1 antibody (1a) (Figure 3A). For comparison we produced the analogous bispecific antibody 1A using the DuetMab platform as described previously.21 Analysis of 1a by reduced SDS-PAGE shows the expected molecular weights of a reconstituted antibody, the removal of intein-containing starting materials, and similar band distribution to the 1A benchmark (Figure 3B). In analytical SEC, 1a exhibited a shorter retention time than its reactants, consistent with increased size expected due to protein splicing, and showed an identical retention time to that of the DuetMab analog (Figure 3C). The expected spliced product was further confirmed by mass spectrometry analysis (Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Table S2). Assessments of antigen binding by biolayer interferometry (BLI) demonstrate that 1a and 1A exhibit similar binding to soluble EGFR and CD3 (Figure 3D). Subsequent kinetic analysis showed that 1a had KD values within 25% of the 1A benchmark for CD3 binding and within 5% for EGFR binding (Supplementary Figure S3A, S3B).

To evaluate the reproducibility of these findings, we generated an additional five monovalent bispecific IgGs binding unique combinations of EGFR, HER2, CD3, and cMET epitopes (1b, 1c, 1d, 2a, and 2c) from their constituent Fab-IntN and IntC-scaffolds (Figure 4A). Comparisons of the spliced molecules to their corresponding DuetMab benchmarks by SDS-PAGE, SEC, and BLI confirmed that the physicochemical characteristics of the antibodies were faithfully recapitulated in each case (Supplementary Figure S4). Subsequent kinetic analysis confirmed that each binder, either on the IntN or IntC starting material, contributes independently to the valency and binding characteristics of the final molecule with measured antigen binding KD’s within 2-fold of the corresponding control molecules, well within the generally accepted margin of error for BLI assessments (Supplementary Figure S3).

Encouraged by these results, we further evaluated the ability of spliced bispecific antibodies to recapitulate the functional properties of conventional antibodies in cell-based assays. To assess tumor-associated antigen binding of the spliced antibodies to EGFR, HER2, and cMET, we performed flow cytometry on the non-small cell lung carcinoma line NCI-H358 (Figure 4B). All spliced molecules showed near-identical binding compared to that of their corresponding DuetMab benchmarks. To assess CD3 engagement of the four bispecific T-cell engagers (TCE), binding to HPB-ALL cells was performed and spliced molecules showed similar binding to the benchmarks (Figure 4C). All measured NCI-H358 cell binding EC50’s for spliced bispecifics were within 1.5-fold of their DuetMab analogs (Supplementary Table 3).

Next, we sought to compare the activity of each molecule in cellular cytotoxicity assays. As EGFR and HER2 inhibition have been shown to reduce cell proliferation and cause cell death,22 we assessed the ability of the αHER2-αEGFR bispecific 2a and the biparatopic αHER2 2c antibodies to directly kill NCI-H358 cells (Figure 4D). The spliced molecules showed equivalent cellular cytotoxicity to their benchmarks, with the αHER2-αEGFR bispecific displaying greater activity than the αHER2 biparatopic. Antibodies 2a and 2c were further evaluated by an antibody-drug conjugate assay in which αFc Fabs conjugated to an auristatin payload were complexed with test articles to induce internalization-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (Figure 4E). Here, near complete cytolysis was observed which provided a more sensitive assessment that confirmed similar activity of spliced molecules and their corresponding DuetMab controls: antibodies 2a and 2A had EC50s of 3.4 and 2.2 nM, respectively, and antibodies 2c and 2C had EC50 values of 8.3 and 10.3 nM, respectively (Supplementary Table S4). Because molecules 1a–d all contain both a tumor-associated antigen and CD3 binding moieties, we assessed their activity in a TCE cytotoxicity assay (Figure 4F). Spliced TCEs showed EC50 values within 1.5-fold of their DuetMab benchmarks (Supplementary Table S5). Quantification of CD25+ and CD69+ positive CD4+ and CD8+ T cells showed equivalent activation between spliced bispecifics and DuetMab benchmarks (Supplementary Figure S5). Collectively, these results provide evidence that spliced bispecific antibodies accurately replicate the physicochemical and functional properties of conventionally produced DuetMab antibodies.

Generation of monovalent trispecific antibodies by protein splicing

Encouraged that the biophysical and functional properties of spliced monovalent bispecific IgGs faithfully recapitulate those of conventionally produced molecules, we sought to further explore the engineering potential of split inteins by combining this technology with the clinically validated DuetMab platform to generate multispecific antibodies of greater complexity. We first created DuetMab IntC-scaffolds that could be reacted with Fab-IntNs to generate monovalent trispecific antibodies. IntC-scaffolds 4 and 5 are αEGFR-αCD3 bispecific antibodies with the C-intein domain fused to the heavy chain N-terminus of the αCD3 or αEGFR Fabs, respectively (Figure 5A). Reaction of these IntC-scaffolds with the αHER2 Fab-IntN b produces 4b and 5b, two monovalent trispecific TCE antibodies with distinct geometric orientations.

Reduced SDS-PAGE analysis of these reactions identify bands with a molecular weight larger than that of either precursor and with a size consistent with the expected spliced products of 75 kDa (Figure 5B). Analytical SEC of 4b and 5b show a shift to an earlier retention time compared to that of the bispecific 1A, indicating an increase in the intact size of the spliced trispecific antibodies (Figure 5C). Assessments of antigen binding by BLI demonstrate that the spliced trispecific antibodies 4b and 5b and corresponding control bispecific DuetMab exhibit similar binding to soluble EGFR, HER2, and CD3 (Figure 5D). Finally, we sought to determine whether the distinct geometric orientations of two spliced trispecific antibodies would cause functional differences in cellular binding or cytotoxicity in a TCE assay.

Whereas binding to EGFR+/HER2+ NCI-H358 cells remains similar for both orientations (EC50s: 4b, 0.36 ± 0.02 nM and 5b, 0.61 ± 0.02 nM), 4b exhibited weaker binding to CD3+ HPB-ALL compared to 5b (Figure 5E) despite similar binding to soluble CD3 antigen as measured by BLI. This difference is consistent with steric hindrance of the αCD3 Fab when directly fused to another Fab when engaging the CD3 antigen as displayed on the cell surface, a phenomenon observed for other TCE modalities.23 The diminished cellular CD3 binding of 4b correlates with its lower potency in a TCE cytotoxicity assay relative to 5b (EC50s: 4b, 0.21 ± 0.06 nM and 5b, 0.06 ± 0.01 nM) (Figure 5F). Both 4b and 5b trispecifics show enhanced EC50 values compared to their monovalent counterparts 1a and 1b, consistent with their bivalent target cell engagement. Altogether, these results demonstrate that intein technology can be seamlessly combined with the DuetMab platform to generate trispecific antibodies.

Construction of bivalent multispecific antibodies via dual-splicing

Up to this point we have described the generation of multispecific antibodies using a single protein splicing event on the IntC-scaffold. We envisioned that performing two splicing reactions on a scaffold containing two IntC fusions could enable the assembly of more complex multispecific antibody formats. To explore this possibility, we generated three distinct dual-IntC scaffolds: a homodimeric mAb-like scaffold for the production of 2 + 2 bispecific antibodies, a monovalent scaffold for the production of 2 + 1 bispecifics, and a bispecific scaffold for the production of 2 + 1 + 1 trispecific antibodies (Figure 6A).

IntC-scaffolds 8 and 9 were combined with αHER2 Fab-IntN b to produce a 2 + 2 αHER2-αEGFR (8b) and a 2 + 1 + 1 αHER2-αEGFR-αCD3 (9b) trispecific antibody, while IntC-scaffold 3 was combined with αEGFR Fab-IntN a to produce a 2 + 1 αHER2-αEGFR (3a) bispecific antibody. Both reduced SDS-PAGE and analytical SEC confirmed the presence of higher molecular weight species consistent with the expected spliced products (Figure 6B, C). Mass spectrometry confirmed the presence of the expected spliced heavy chain (Supplemental Table S1 and S2). These analyses also identified the presence of residual unspliced heavy chain in formats made by dual-splicing, which we attributed to hydrolyzed C-intein. Because unspliced molecules would result in lower avidity, we assessed antigen binding by BLI. Spliced molecules with monovalent antigen binding, CD3 for 3a and 9b and EGFR for 9b, showed similar binding kinetics to their corresponding DuetMab benchmarks (Figure 6D). In contrast, spliced molecules with bivalent antigen binding (EGFR for 8b and 3a and HER2 for 8b and 9b) exhibited slower antigen dissociation compared to the monovalent DuetMab benchmarks, consistent with increased avidity due to dual-splicing reactions. In scenarios where residual amounts of unspliced molecule may significantly alter functional readouts, these impurities could be removed through additional polishing steps (i.e. ion exchange chromatography).

To further explore the potential of dual-splicing to produce complex multispecific antibodies, we generated additional bispecific 2 + 1 (3b and 3c), trispecific 2 + 1 + 1 (10a), and bispecific 2 + 2 (6c and 7b) antibodies (Figure 7A, Supplementary Figure S6). All spliced multispecifics bound to NCI-H358 cells, with those targeting EGFR exhibiting stronger binding (Figure 7B). Given that the measured affinities (Supplementary Figure S3) and reported receptor densities for this cell line are comparable,24 we attribute the observed difference to the selected EGFR binder exhibiting enhanced target engagement within a cellular context. The αHER2 biparatopic 2 + 2 molecules (6c and 7b) and the αHER2–αEGFR 2 + 2 molecule (8a) were evaluated in both direct killing and ADC assays (Figure 7C,D). Among them, 8a demonstrated greater potency, consistent with the NCI-H358 cell binding results. The TCEs in this cohort, molecules 3a – c, 9b, and 10a, all showed attenuated binding to HPB-ALL cells (Figure 7E), consistent with what was observed previously for a sterically hindered CD3-binding Fab (Figure 5E). In a TCE assay 3a exhibited >6-fold increased activity compared to 3b and 3c, consistent with the enhanced target cell engagement observed (Figure 7F). Interestingly, 10a exhibited an 11-fold increase in activity compared to 9b, despite showing comparable cellular binding to both target cells and CD3+ HPB-ALL cells (Figure 7E, Supplemental Table S3). This may indicate that the geometry of target engagement for 10a is more favorable for immune synapse formation. Similar patterns were observed for T-cell activation in both CD4+ and CD8+ cells (Supplementary Figure S7). Collectively, these results demonstrate that dual-splicing can be used to generate complex bivalent bispecific and trispecific antibodies.

Discussion

Bispecific antibody therapeutics have matured significantly in recent years, with multiple molecules now approved and demonstrating meaningful clinical impact across oncology and other disease areas. With growing confidence in these modalities and continued advances in antibody engineering to improve manufacturability, the field has progressed beyond bispecifics to explore increasingly complex formats, including trispecific and even tetraspecific antibodies. As these modalities continue to evolve and mature, there is an increasing need for tools that facilitate the rapid generation and triage of bispecific and multispecific antibodies with the desired biological activity. To remain relevant and effective, such tools must be inherently flexible and capable of accommodating the expanding diversity and structural complexity of next-generation multispecific antibody therapeutics.

In this study, we demonstrate a modular platform for the generation of multispecific antibodies using intein-mediated protein trans-splicing. By combining a small set of Fab-IntN and IntC-scaffold precursors, we successfully generated a diverse panel of multispecific antibodies with a broad range of valencies and specificities (Figure 1B). With purified intein building blocks in hand, we successfully applied this technology to generate multispecifics through a one-hour splicing reaction, followed by approximately three days of additional processing, including reoxidation and buffer exchange. This work expands the utility of split inteins from simple Fab-IgG fusion constructs to a generalizable strategy for programmable multispecific antibody assembly with precise control over binding geometry and complexity. Importantly, we demonstrated that spliced monovalent bispecific IgG molecules faithfully recapitulate the biochemical, biophysical, and functional properties of bispecific antibodies generated through conventional methodologies. Across six distinct spliced bispecific designs, spliced antibodies matched their DuetMab counterparts in SDS-PAGE (Figure 3B), SEC (Figure 3C), antigen binding (Figure 3D), cellular binding (Figure 4B, C), and cellular cytotoxicity (Figure 4D-F).

We then extended this approach to produce monovalent trispecific antibodies by integrating intein domains into existing bispecific DuetMab scaffolds (Figure 5A). The resulting trispecific molecules displayed comparable target-binding properties to corresponding control molecules (Figure 5D). Interestingly, the trispecifics differed in CD3 binding and T-cell engager potency (Figure 5E, F), highlighting the impact of molecular geometry on functional activity, specifically, the influence of steric hindrance at the CD3-binding Fab when placed adjacent to another Fab. Such effects have been observed in other TCE formats and underscore the importance of spatial configuration in multispecific design.

To further demonstrate the versatility of the intein platform, we performed dual-protein splicing reactions on single antibody scaffolds to generate bivalent multispecific antibodies (Figure 6A). This enabled the creation of more complex formats, such as 2 + 2, 2 + 1, and 2 + 1 + 1 architectures, which are otherwise challenging to produce at high homogeneity using traditional high-throughput expression approaches. Antibodies with bivalent-binding domains showed enhanced target engagement and slower antigen dissociation kinetics (Figures 6D and 7B), consistent with avidity effects. Notably, the geometry and valency of antigen engagement directly influenced cytotoxic potency (Figure 7F), suggesting that the spatial arrangement of antigen-binding domains may impact immune synapse formation and downstream signaling efficiency.

Collectively, these findings establish intein-mediated splicing as a powerful tool for the combinatorial generation and functional screening of multispecific antibodies, including monovalent bispecific (1 + 1), monovalent trispecific (1 + 1 + 1), bi/mono-valent bispecific (2 + 1), bivalent bispecific (2 + 2), and bi/mono-valent trispecific (2 + 1 + 1) formats. The ability to decouple binder production from final molecule assembly allows for rapid iteration across different geometric formats, an increasingly valuable capability as multispecific antibody designs trend toward greater complexity and advanced therapeutic mechanisms of action. Intein splicing lends itself well to high-throughput screening applications, where rapid prototyping of multispecific architectures could accelerate lead optimization in discovery-stage programs. Indeed, this approach enables the generation of large combinatorial libraries whose diversity expands exponentially relative to the number of antibody building blocks, making it especially powerful for screening an expansive multispecific design space. For example, a panel of over 1000 multispecific antibodies spanning diverse binders, valencies, and formats could be generated from a set of only 64 intein building blocks. While in this work we focused on the IgG format and Fab-based binders, this technology could in principle be used for the incorporation of other therapeutic moieties such as single-domain antibodies, cytokines, and paratope masking domains. Overall, the intein-based multispecific antibody assembly platform presented here provides a scalable, modular, and robust approach for screening next-generation antibody therapeutics.

Materials and methods

Materials

Antigens were purchased from Acro Biosystems: Biotinylated Human EGFR (cat# EGR-H82E3), Biotinylated Human HER2 (cat# HE2-H822R), Biotinylated Human cMET with His tag (cat# MET-H82E1), Biotinylated Human CD3 epsilon&CD3 delta Heterodimer Protein (cat# CDD-H82W6). Octet biosensors were purchased from Sartorius: Octet® Streptavidin (SA) Biosensor (cat #18–5019), Octet® Protein A (ProA) Biosensors (cat #18–5010), Octet® HIS1K Biosensors (cat #18–5120). Preparatory scale chromatography columns were purchased from Cytiva: HisTrap Excel (cat# 17,371,206), HiTrap MabSelect SuRe (cat# 11,003,494), HiTrap LambdaFabSelect (cat# 17,548,212), HiTrap KappaSelect (cat# 17,545,812). HiLoad 26/600 Superdex 200 pg (cat# 28,989,336). The analytical SEC column was purchased from Waters: ACQUITY Premier Protein SEC Column, 250 Å, 1.7 µm, 4.6 × 150 mm (cat # 186,009,963). All other materials were purchased from Thermo Fisher unless specified.

Cloning, expression, and purification

Cloning, protein expression, and protein purification were performed as described previously.20 Briefly, cloned constructs were transiently transfected into CHO cells. Cell culture supernatants were collected 13 days after transfection and filtered through a .22 µM filter before purification. Fab IntN molecules were purified via IMAC using a His-Trap Excel (Cytiva) column, and IntC scaffolds were purified by protein A affinity chromatography using MabSelect SuRe resin (Cytivia), followed by IMAC, then LC affinity chromatography using either Lambda select or Kappa select (Cytivia) columns as necessary. All molecules were assessed for aggregates via analytical SEC (Waters) and purified via preparatory scale SEC (Cytiva) if necessary. Protein titers were measured via Octet using ProA tips for IntC scaffolds and DuetMabs, or AntiHis1K tips for Fab-IntNs. Final yields were determined by protein A280.

BLI

Binding kinetics were measured on an Octet384 (ForteBio). To capture the effects of avidity brought about by bivalent intein splicing, BLI measurements were performed by first loading biosensor tips with antigen. Antigen at 1 ug/mL for EGFR, HER2, and CD3 and 2 ug/mL for cMET were captured on streptavidin sensors (Sartorious) at for 180s in assay buffer (0.3% BSA, 0.05% Tween20, PBS 7.4). The sensors were then washed in assay buffer before association with test articles in buffer. Dissociation was performed for the given time in assay buffer. For binding, the concentration of the antibody was set at 100 nM for the given duration. For kinetics, a 3-point binding curve at 100 nM, 33 nM, and 11 nM was used. Kinetic parameters were calculated from a non-linear fit using Octet384 software.

Analytical SEC

Protein samples were applied to a Waters column and eluted with 0.1 M sodium phosphate, 0.1 M sodium sulfate, and 0.05% sodium azide at pH 6.8 at a flow rate of 1 mL/min over 5 min using an Agilent 1200 HPLC. Protein concentration was detected using UV absorbance at 280 nm.

LC-MS

Protein deglycosylation was carried out by overnight treatment with PNGase F (NEB cat# P0704S) at 37°C, followed by 1 hour treatment with Endoglycosidase F2 (NEB cat # P0772S) and 10 mM TCEP at pH 4.5 for 1 hour at 37°C. Samples were filtered through a .22 µM filter before injection. Mass spectrometry analysis was performed using an Agilent 6545XT Q-TOF mass spectrometer equipped with a protein macro-trap column (Optimize technologies cat # 10–04818, 3 mm × 8 mm). High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) parameters were as follows: flow rate, 0.4 ml/min; mobile phase A was 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in HPLC-grade H2O, and mobile phase B was 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in acetonitrile. The column was equilibrated in 90%A/10%B, which was also used to desalt the mAb samples, followed by elution in 10%A/90%B. Mass spec data were collected for 100–5000 m/z, positive polarity, a gas temperature of 350°C, a nebulizer pressure of 60 lb/in,2 and a capillary voltage of 5,500 V. Data were analyzed using vendor-supplied Bioconfirm 12.1 software and peak intensities from deconvoluted spectra were used to derive the relative proportion of species in each sample as previously described.

Cell binding

Cell binding was performed by washing suspended cells with cold FACS buffer (1x PBS, 2% FBS, .01% Sodium Azide, 2 mM EDTA) before plating in a 96 well plate at 1x106 cells/mL. Cells were then incubated with serial dilutions of antibody at the given concentrations for 30 min at 4°C. After staining, cells were washed with cold FACS buffer before addition of a 1:100 dilution of 650 labeled anti-human secondary (Thermo Fisher cat# SA5-10137) for 30 minutes. Cells were washed again in cold FACS buffer before measurement using a BD FACSymphony A3 (BD) flow cytometer. FlowJo software was used to calculated mean fluorescence intensity per sample for single cells, and the values were plotted in Prism. EC50 values were calculated using a nonlinear fit in GraphPad Prism.

SDS-PAGE

Protein solutions were boiled for 5 minutes at 95°C in the presence of NuPage LDS sample buffer (Thermo Fisher cat # NP0007) and NuPage sample reducing agent before (Thermo Fisher cat # NP0004) being separated on a 4–12% Bis-Tris gel (Thermo Fisher cat # NP0329BOX) for 30 min at a constant 200 V using a Powerpack Universal Power Supply (BioRad cat # 1,645,070). Gels were subsequently stained with SimplyBlue Safe Stain (Thermo Fisher cat # LC6060), washed with water, and imaged on a Gel Doc EZ Imager (BioRad). Seeblue plus 2 prestained ladder (Thermo Fisher cat # LC5925) was used as a protein standard.

ADC cytotoxicity

Cytotoxicity was measured in NCI-H358 cells, which were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, 11,875–093) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, A56698-01) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15,140–122) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. One thousand cells were plated per well in a 384-well plate. Antibodies were incubated with a proprietary PNU-conjugated anti-Fc Fab giving a final effective drug-antibody-ratio of 4. A serial dilution was performed and the antibody/Fab-PNU conjugate was added to the cells and incubated for 120 hours. Cell viability was measured using CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. The data was fit to a four-parameter dose-response curve using GraphPad Prism, and the reported IC50 is the antibody concentration necessary for achieving half-maximal growth inhibition. Experiments were performed in duplicate.

Direct inhibition cytotoxicity

Cytotoxicity was measured in NCI-H358 cells, which were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, 11,875–093) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, A56698-01) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15,140–122) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. One thousand cells were plated per well in a 384-well plate. A serial dilution of antibody test article was added to the cells and incubated for 120 hours. Cell viability was measured using CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. The data was fit to a four-parameter dose–response curve using GraphPad Prism, and the reported IC50 is the antibody concentration necessary for achieving half-maximal growth inhibition. Experiments were performed in quadruplicate.

Intein splicing reactions

Intein splicing was performed in splicing buffer (PBS, .5 mM Tris-(2-Carboxyethyl)phosphine, Hydrochloride (Thermo Fisher cat # T2556). 4.5 µM IntC scaffold was combined with 6.75 µM Fab IntN and allowed to react for 1 hour at 37°C on a rotary shaker. Reactions were subsequently treated with Ni-NTA agarose beads for 1 hour at room temperature before overnight reoxidation with 10 mM (L)-Dehydroascorbic Acid (Sigma Aldrich cat # 261,556-1 G). The buffer was exchanged using a HiLoad 26/600 Superdex 200 pg column into PBS pH 7.4, and the spliced reactions were concentrated using a 10 kDa MWCO Amicon spin column (Sigma Aldrich cat # UFC801008) before analysis. Concentrations were measured using Octet with a ProA Biosensor calibrated using a DuetMab standard.

Cells and media

The EGFR+, HER2+, and cMET+ cell line NCI H358 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (cat# CRL-5807), and CD3+ cell line HPB-ALL was obtained from DSMZ (cat # ACC 483). Cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 media supplied with 10% heat-inactivated FBS and cultured in a 37°C water jacketed incubator (VWR 10,810–744) supplied with 80% humidity and 5% CO2.

T cell-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (TDCC)

TDCC was measured by CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 Cell Viability Assay kit (Promega). NCI H358 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in RPMI-1640 and supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS. Effector cells, PBMCs from a healthy donor, were mixed with target cells at E:T ratio of 10:1. Antibodies at various concentrations were added to duplicate samples and were incubated with cells for 72 h at 37°C in 5% CO2 atmosphere. After treatment, the remaining target cell monolayer was exposed to CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 reagent for 10 min and OD at 409 nm was measured using an EnVision 2105 Multimode plate reader (PerkinElmer). Cell cytotoxicity was normalized to the minimum cytotoxicity value without antibody treatment and plotted using GraphPad Prism. Experiments were performed in duplicate.

T cell activation

T cell activation from cytotoxicity assay was assessed by flow cytometry. NCI H358 cells were seeded in a 96 well plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in RPMI-1640 and supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS and 50 µM 2-mercaptoethanol. Effector cells, PBMCs from a healthy donor, were mixed with target cells at E:T ratio of 10:1. Antibodies at various concentrations were added to duplicate samples, and cells were incubated for 48. T cells were harvested, stained and analyzed by a BD FACSymphonyTM A3 (BD) flow cytometer. Anti-CD2 and anti-CD4 antibodies were used to identify CD4+ (CD2+ and CD4+) and CD8+ (CD2+ and CD4−) T cells. T cell activation was determined by CD69+ and CD25+. Data was analyzed using FlowJo v10.8.0. and plotted using GraphPad Prism. Experiments were performed in duplicate.

Statistics

IC50 values were calculated using a four-parameter dose–response curve using GraphPad Prism with a symmetrical 95% confidence interval to give a standard error for the calculated parameters. Kinetic parameters with standard error were calculated from a non-linear fit using Octet(R) Analysis Studio 12.2.2.26 using a 1:1 binding model on association and dissociation steps, with global group fitting across matched samples. Number of replicates varied based on the experiment performed and is reported in the figure legends.
