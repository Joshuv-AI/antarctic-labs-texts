Effect of modified anterior approach for ultrasound-guided superior laryngeal nerve block on sore throat after tonsillectomy in adults: study protocol for a double-blind randomised controlled trial
Authors: Hong Li, Ruihao Zhou, Yusi Hua, Jianqiao Zheng, Guo Chen, Tao Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPostoperative sore throat (POST), a common complication following tonsillectomy, significantly impairs patient recovery through persistent pain and dysphagia. The internal branch of superior laryngeal nerve block (iSLNB) can effectively alleviate POST. Specifically, the ultrasound (US)-guided modified anterior approach for iSLNB offers anatomical precision and reduced invasiveness compared with traditional approaches. This study aims to determine whether the US-guided modified anterior approach for iSLNB can relieve POST in adults after tonsillectomy.Methods and analysisA total of 40 patients scheduled for elective tonsillectomy under general anaesthesia will be randomly allocated to either the nerve block group (US-guided modified anterior iSLNB group) or the blank control group (only US scanning). Following the induction of general anaesthesia, the corresponding interventions will be performed, followed by postoperative pain assessment. The main outcome will be the POST intensity during swallowing at 24 hours after surgery. Secondary outcomes will consist of (a) the level of POST at rest at the same 24-hour postoperative time point; pain intensity measurements at rest and while swallowing recorded at 1 hour, 6 hours, 48 hours and on postoperative day 7; (b) the incidence of total pain at 24 hours postoperatively; (c) the time until the first administration of rescue analgesia and the cumulative dosage of rescue analgesic drugs consumed; (d) pain satisfaction; (e) perioperative haemodynamic parameters and (f) the incidence of perioperative complications. This article will outline the study methodology of the randomised controlled trial.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol has received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Audit No. 757 of 2025), and has been formally registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (www.chictr.org.cn). Findings will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed academic journal.Trial registration numberChiCTR2500102999.Keywords: ANAESTHETICS, Anaesthesia in otolaryngology, Adult otolaryngology, Otolaryngology

Introduction

Tonsillectomy ranks among the most commonly performed surgical procedures globally, with over 500 000 tonsil-related surgeries conducted annually worldwide.1 Postoperative sore throat (POST) is one of the most common complications. Its incidence is approximately 25%–50%.2 The pain may persist for around 2 weeks, delaying the return to a normal diet and normal activities. In severe situations, it may result in delayed hospital discharge, emergency department consultations or rehospitalisation.3 4

Key contributing factors to POST after tonsillectomy primarily may involve the following mechanisms: Trauma to the peritonsillar mucosa, along with injury to the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerve fibres, which subsequently triggers inflammation and spasm of pharyngeal muscles.5 Ultimately, such muscle spasms cause tissue ischaemia, further intensifying pain.5 Postoperatively, the exposed surgical site is subjected to pharyngeal movement during swallowing, exacerbating wound pain and impairing healing.6 Additionally, pharyngeal mucosal injury may arise from endotracheal intubation during general anaesthesia.7 Lastly, various surgical techniques are employed in tonsillectomy, each associated with differing levels of postoperative throat pain.8

Currently, the PROcedure-SPECific Postoperative Pain ManagemenT (PROSPECT) guidelines for tonsillectomy recommend the combined use of paracetamol and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) preoperatively or intraoperatively, and the continuation of this regimen postoperatively (excluding patients with contraindications).9 Despite numerous studies comparing combination analgesics in an attempt to identify the most effective postoperative pain management strategy, there remains no consensus on the optimal approach. In tonsillectomy, neither pharmacological nor non-pharmacological interventions for preventing POST associated with general anaesthesia tracheal intubation have demonstrated significant efficacy.10 Furthermore, although the glossopharyngeal nerve innervates the general sensory functions (such as pain, tactile sensation and thermosensation) of the mucosa of the posterior one-third of the tongue, pharynx, soft palate, tonsils and palatine arches, relevant research reports have indicated that glossopharyngeal nerve block has no significant impact on the management of postoperative pain following tonsillectomy.11 In previous studies, the internal branch of the superior laryngeal nerve block (iSLNB) has been shown to alleviate POST resulting from thoracoscopic partial lung resection, suspension laryngoscopy and thyroidectomy.12,14 Our preliminary investigations have demonstrated that ultrasound (US)-guided iSLNB can effectively anaesthetise the larynx for airway manoeuvres (such as awake tracheal intubation) and pain therapy and also exhibits excellent analgesic effects on POST.15 16 Furthermore, the US-guided anterior iSLNB offers simplified operation, reduced procedural time, a single needle insertion site, minimised trauma and enhanced overall safety.15

We postulate that US-guided modified anterior iSLNB can alleviate POST in adult patients following tonsillectomy. In this research, our objective is to explore the effectiveness and safety of US-guided modified anterior iSLNB in managing postoperative pain among adult patients who have undergone tonsillectomy.

Methods and analysis

Overall study design and timeline

This study will be a single-centre, double-blind, superiority, randomised controlled trial. It will aim to explore the efficacy and safety of US-guided modified anterior iSLNB in pain management after tonsillectomy in adults. The protocol is formulated and recorded in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines.17 The schedule outlining participant enrolment, interventions and evaluations is presented in the SPIRIT figure (figures1 2). Participant enrolment and screening began in September 2025, and the study will be expected to be completed in December 2026.

Consent

Anaesthetist A will conduct the introduction to the study, as well as provide education and obtain consent from all participants. Information in Chinese will be used to present the research to participants. Participants are encouraged to ask questions if any aspect is unclear. Written informed consent will be secured following a discussion and confirmation that participants fully understand the study. A model participant consent form is available as an online supplemental additional file1. Participants have the right to withdraw from the study at any time.

Participants: inclusion/exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Patients enrolled in this study will be individuals scheduled for elective tonsillectomy under general anaesthesia at West China Hospital of Sichuan University. Eligible participants will be those classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III, aged 18–65 years and who will provide written informed consent before study enrolment.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria will include the following: patient refusal to participate; contraindications for SLNB such as coagulation abnormalities, local or systemic infection, hypersensitivity to local anaesthetics, severe cardiac or pulmonary dysfunction and inability to cooperate; a history of allergic reactions to anaesthetic agents; ongoing use of chronic analgesics or a past history of substance abuse; the presence of bleeding disorders; significant hepatic or renal impairment; preoperative symptoms such as voice changes or difficulty swallowing and concurrent participation in other clinical studies.

Interventions

Nerve block group

US-guided localisation: The patient will be positioned supine with the head gently extended. The US probe will be positioned in the longitudinal plane and placed perpendicularly to the midline, located between the hyoid bone and the thyroid cartilage, enabling transverse cross-sectional imaging. The thyrohyoid membrane (THM), appearing as a slightly hyperechoic structure, will be clearly identified using US imaging.

Anaesthetic administration: The needle will be introduced in-plane relative to the probe’s long axis. Once the needle tip reaches the THM, aspiration will be performed to confirm the absence of vascular entry; then, a total of 6 mL of 0.25% ropivacaine combined with 3 mg of dexamethasone will be administered.18 Real-time US imaging will be used to confirm the spread of ropivacaine bilaterally along the THM.

Blank control group

Only US imaging will be used; no invasive methods will be employed.

All participants will be instructed to fast for 8 hours before surgery. On entering the operating room, intravenous access will be obtained via a vein in the upper limb. A multiparameter monitoring system will be used to continuously record heart rate (HR), ECG, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), mean arterial pressure and oxygen saturation (SpO2). The anaesthesiologist conducting the nerve block will open the sealed envelope containing the randomisation details to assign the patient to a specific group. Once general anaesthesia is induced, the appropriate nerve block procedures will be executed.

All participants will receive 3 min of pre-oxygenation with a 100% oxygen face mask. Subsequently, anaesthesia will be induced with intravenous administration of midazolam at 0.02 mg/kg, sufentanil at 0.2–0.3 µg/kg, ciprofol at 0.3–0.4 mg/kg and cisatracurium at 0.2 mg/kg. A basal systemic dose of dexamethasone will not be administered to any of the participants. After achieving sufficient muscular relaxation, tracheal intubation will be carried out with a reinforced endotracheal tube (internal diameter of 7.5 mm for male patients and 7.0 mm for female patients). The tube will be secured after confirming proper placement via EtCO2 detection and bilateral breath sound auscultation. Following general anaesthesia, patients in the experimental group will be administered US-guided anterior iSLNB, whereas those in the control group will receive US imaging. Following completion of the procedure, sterile dressings will be applied to the corresponding procedural site.

Anaesthesia will be sustained using desflurane in combination with remifentanil administered at a rate of 0.1–0.3 mcg/kg/min throughout the surgical procedure. Blood pressure and HR will be kept within 20% of the pre-admission baseline levels. Ten minutes prior to the conclusion of surgery, the patient will receive an intravenous dose of 5 mcg of sufentanil. Postoperatively, to reverse the effects of neuromuscular blockade, 2 mL of neostigmine methylsulfate (1 mg) will be administered intramuscularly along with 1 mL of atropine sulfate (0.5 mg) intravenously. In the event of a post-extubation Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) pain score of 4 or higher in the recovery area or hospital ward, 50 mg of flurbiprofen axetil will be provided as a rescue analgesic.

Following surgery, patients who have had a tonsillectomy will typically be transferred to the Post-Anaesthesia Care Unit for extubation and monitoring prior to being moved back to the general ward. Once on the ward, parameters such as the level of postoperative throat pain (assessed using the Numeric Rating Scale from 0 to 10, where higher scores will reflect more severe discomfort); the time to first administration of rescue analgesia; the total quantity of rescue analgesics consumed within 24 hours post-surgery; as well as occurrences of dysphagia, hoarseness, respiratory difficulties and coughing will be monitored, assessed and documented. Additionally, any perioperative adverse events associated with nerve block or tracheal intubation will be recorded. These will include complications such as local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST), grade 3 laryngeal pain, grade 3 hoarseness, aspiration due to reflux, laryngospasm and tracheospasm.

Sample size calculations

The sample size for this study will be estimated using PASS 15 software under a one-sided superiority test framework. Key assumptions will be derived from existing evidence: the control group (no intervention) will be projected to have a mean NRS pain score of 7.23±1.15 on POD 1, based on previously published data,19 while the intervention group will be expected to achieve a mean score of 4.4±2.2, as supported by results from a multicentre prospective cohort study.20 A reduction of ≥1 point on the NRS scale is widely accepted as representing a clinically meaningful improvement21; therefore, the superiority margin (Δ) will be prespecified as 1. The statistical parameters will include a one-tailed significance level (α) of 0.025 and a target power (1−β) of 80%. To accommodate an anticipated loss to follow-up rate of 20%, the required sample size per group will be inflated accordingly. Thus, 20 participants will be deemed necessary in each group, which will yield a total planned enrolment of 40 individuals.

Data collection, management and analysis

Data collection and management

All measurement data will initially be documented on the Case Report Form before being input into the system by independent researchers. Once data entry is complete, the database will be locked to prevent further modifications. Any missing information resulting from participants being lost to follow-up will be consistently coded as ‘incomplete’. Physical data records will be assigned study-specific identification numbers to ensure participant anonymity and will be kept in secure, locked storage units. Access to the anonymised dataset will be restricted exclusively to the study authors.

Data analyses

The effectiveness of US-guided anterior iSLNB will be assessed by comparing patients’ NRS pain scores at rest and during swallowing at multiple time points: 1 hour, 6 hours, 24 hours, 48 hours and POD 7. Additionally, the safety of US-guided anterior iSLNB will be evaluated by analysing the incidence of perioperative complications.

For statistical analysis, the full analysis set will be used. Continuous variables will be summarised using measures such as mean±SD, median (IQR) or proportions. Categorical variables will be described using frequency tables, displayed as counts or ranked values. To assess the normality of the data distribution, the Shapiro-Wilk test will be applied. For comparisons between groups, the chi-square test will be used for categorical data. In situations where expected cell counts are low, Fisher’s exact test will be considered as an alternative. For continuous variables, a t-test assuming normal distribution will be conducted to evaluate mean differences between the experimental and control groups. When the data fail to meet parametric assumptions, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test will be applied as a non-parametric alternative. Statistical significance will be defined as a p-value less than 0.05.

Randomisation and blinding

Participants will be randomly divided into two groups at a 1:1 ratio: the blank control group (only US scanning) or the nerve block group (US-guided anterior iSLNB). The randomisation sequence will be created by the research team prior to the start of participant recruitment. A separate assistant, who will not be affiliated with the study, will open the sealed randomisation envelope 1 hour before the surgical procedure and notify the researchers of the assigned intervention. The anaesthesiologists performing the nerve block will cover the puncture site with sterile dressings following either the nerve block administration or the US-only scan. Because of the inherent characteristics of the study design, the anaesthesiologist administering the nerve block will be aware of the group allocation. Nevertheless, group allocations will be concealed from participants, data collectors and analysts throughout the study.

Monitoring

Adverse events will be gathered and recorded from the moment participants undergo the assigned treatment or intervention, and this monitoring will persist throughout the entire follow-up period. As defined by the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use in their Good Clinical Practice guidelines, an adverse event is any undesired medical occurrence experienced by a study participant during the trial, irrespective of its potential connection to the treatment or intervention provided. Throughout the course of the study, all serious adverse events occurring from the point of participant enrolment up to the conclusion of follow-up will be communicated to the ethics committee and the Quality, Safety and Performance Evaluation Committee within 1 day.

Prevention and treatment for adverse events

SLN injury

Injury to the superior laryngeal nerve (SLN) can cause a reduction in the sensory function of swallowing, giving rise to difficulties such as dysphagia, asphyxia, aspiration and swallowing impairments.22 Clinical evaluation typically encompasses laryngoscopy, stroboscopic videolaryngoscopy and laryngeal electromyography.23 To prevent injury to the SLN, efforts will be made to identify the acoustic shadow of the SLN under US. An appropriate puncture access route will be selected to avoid traversing the area of the SLN. Following an injury to the SLN, treatment strategies will involve voice therapy, steroid therapy and a variety of surgical interventions.23

Laryngospasm

Laryngospasm refers to the protective reflexive closure of the glottis triggered by abnormal stimuli. Clinically, it presents with features such as inspiratory or expiratory stridor. When the spasm persists, it may give rise to hypoxia and hypercapnia. In severe cases, it can even result in life-threatening complications, including cardiac arrest, arrhythmia, pulmonary oedema, bronchospasm or aspiration. The prevention of laryngospasm hinges on the accurate identification of risk factors and the implementation of appropriate preventive measures.24 Once laryngospasm occurs, it is imperative to promptly identify and remove the offending stimuli. This is often accompanied by manoeuvres such as the application of the mandibular thrust, the insertion of an oral or nasal airway and the provision of positive-pressure ventilation with 100% oxygen. If the laryngospasm fails to resolve following these initial interventions, the subsequent step should involve seeking additional assistance and escalating the depth of anaesthesia, either through intravenous administration or inhalation of anaesthetic agents.25

Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity

LAST is defined as a systemic toxic reaction that occurs when the amount of local anaesthetic entering the bloodstream within a given unit of time surpasses the body’s tolerance threshold. In this study, the preventive measure for LAST involves aspiration prior to the injection of ropivacaine. Injection should only be administered when no fluid is aspirated. Once LAST is recognised, treatment will be implemented in accordance with the 2020 edition of the American Society of Regional Anaesthesia (ASRA) and Pain Medicine LAST checklist.26

Aspiration

When patients, for various reasons, have abnormal substances like gastric contents, secretions or blood entering the respiratory tract and lung tissue below the glottis, it can give rise to severe related complications.27 Once aspiration occurs in a patient, the patient should be immediately positioned in a lateral decubitus position or a Trendelenburg position. This is to prevent the aspirated substances from further penetrating into the deeper parts of the respiratory tract. A negative-pressure suction device or a sputum suction tube should be used to promptly aspirate the secretions and food particles from the patient’s oral cavity, nasal cavity and airways.28 Closely observe the patient’s vital signs, including respiration, HR and blood pressure, as well as changes in consciousness and facial complexion. According to the patient’s condition, administer medications such as antibiotics, antitussive and expectorant drugs and bronchodilators. These medications are used to prevent or control infections and improve respiratory function.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the application of US-guided anterior iSLNB in ameliorating POST following tonsillectomy. The findings of this study may present a novel approach for relieving POST after tonsillectomy. This not only aligns with the concept of multimodal analgesia in the field of anaesthesia but also provides patients with a more comfortable experience in diagnosis and treatment.29

Adult tonsillectomy is associated with a high incidence of POST, likely driven by two key factors: surgical trauma causing mucosal injury and damage to vagus nerve fibres (leading to pain)5 along with the impact of tracheal intubation during general anaesthesia.7 At present, the PROSPECT guidelines for tonsillectomy advocate the concurrent use of paracetamol and NSAIDs either preoperatively or intraoperatively, with continuation into the postoperative period (excluding patients with contraindications). Additionally, a single intraoperative intravenous injection of dexamethasone is recommended.9 Nevertheless, research has indicated that the efficacy of the combination of paracetamol and NSAIDs is relatively limited, accompanied by a potential bleeding risk.30 Dexamethasone has demonstrated notable analgesic effects when administered either alone or in conjunction with other analgesics following tonsillectomy. However, associated side effects, including elevated blood glucose levels, increased nocturnal alertness and restlessness, remain unresolved. 31Given that the operative duration of tonsillectomy is relatively short, the preventive measures implemented for POST induced by tracheal intubation have proved ineffective. These measures include applying lidocaine gel or beclomethasone to the tracheal cuff, replacing it with a tracheal tube of a smaller calibre and decreasing the cuff pressure.10

The SLN originates from the vagus nerve at the level of the second cervical vertebra and extends medially towards the THM. The SLN divides into the external and internal branches deep to the internal carotid artery. The internal branch of the SLN descends to a position below the greater horn of the thyroid cartilage and runs in close proximity to the THM, accompanying the superior laryngeal artery (SLA). Both the internal branch of the SLN and the SLA penetrate the lateral surface of the THM. The internal branch of the SLN serves not only as the primary sensory nerve for the major part of the larynx but also provides sensory innervation to the mucosa of the region above the vocal cord level.32 The sensory innervation of the tonsils and the pharyngeal region is highly intricate, primarily derived from the branches of the glossopharyngeal nerve and the vagus nerve.33 The SLN, a branch of the vagus nerve, supplies the principal sensory innervation to the inferior part of the pharynx.34 Although the iSLN does not directly innervate the tonsils, the area it innervates is in close proximity to the tonsillar region. During surgical procedures, traction, oedema and inflammation can have a widespread impact on these regions. Blocking the sensory innervation of the laryngopharyngeal regions supplied by the internal branch of the SLN encompasses a broad area, including the inferior border of the tonsillar bed, the lateral aspect of the root of the tongue and the extensive regions of the hypopharynx. Postoperatively, the signals of swelling and pain from these regions cannot be relayed. In the context of acute postoperative pain, which is characterised by a widespread and ill-defined pain area, SLNB can significantly reduce the overall afferent pain signals. In our clinical practice, we have observed that iSLNB not only effectively anaesthetises the larynx for airway-related procedures but also markedly alleviates postoperative pharyngeal pain in patients. Furthermore, although the iSLNB can relieve POST through its anaesthetic effect on the larynx, it may increase the risk of gastro-oesophageal reflux and aspiration by inhibiting the protective reflexes of the pharynx and larynx.34 However, in prior studies and the preliminary investigations of this study, no serious adverse events, such as reflux or aspiration, were witnessed in patients.12,14

In clinical practice, the traditional posterior approach for US-guided iSLNB (bilateral injection) is impeded by the challenges in clearly visualising the SLN and the anatomical variations of the SLA.35 36 As a result, several research teams have proposed alternative anterior techniques, with their viability confirmed in cadaveric investigations.37 38 These anterior approaches are, in theory, more straightforward with regard to localisation techniques (landmark-guided) and the number of injections (a single injection only). A study on awake tracheal intubation indicated that the efficacy of the US-guided anterior iSLNB is no less effective than that of the traditional posterior approach. Moreover, it offers enhanced safety and causes less physical trauma.15 Therefore, we selected the anterior approach, which is safer and more efficient, for iSLNB.

Given that this technology has not yet been validated in adult subjects, this study prioritises the adult population as the initial research cohort due to dual considerations of research rigour and participant safety. Second, adult participants exhibit superior cognitive comprehension, verbal expression and symptom perception accuracy, enabling them to more reliably complete postoperative pain assessments (eg, NRS) and identify/report adverse reactions associated with the novel technology—thereby enhancing the scientific validity and credibility of efficacy and safety evaluations. Following sufficient validation of the technology’s effectiveness and safety in adults, subsequent studies will be progressively extended to the paediatric population.

Our study has several limitations. First, multiple surgical techniques are available for tonsillectomy, and each technique may result in varying degrees of postoperative pain intensity. Relying solely on a single scoring criterion may introduce inaccuracies.5 Therefore, the surgical technique will be standardised across all participants, with low-temperature plasma tonsillectomy adopted as the uniform surgical method, to mitigate unintended bias. Second, we did not intervene in the local infiltration anaesthesia administered by the surgeon at the conclusion of the surgical procedure. Given that this intervention was carried out in both groups concurrently, its influence on the study was deemed minimal. Third, we employed dexamethasone as an adjuvant to extend the duration of action of local anaesthetics.39 However, a control group was not established to eliminate potential confounding effects associated with dexamethasone. It remains unclear whether iSLNB using ropivacaine alone would yield equivalent effects on the primary outcome. Therefore, we adopt a cautious approach when interpreting our findings and conclude tentatively that modified anterior iSLNB with local anaesthetics supplemented by corticosteroids may reduce postoperative throat pain following tonsillectomy. Fourth, the limited sample size of this study resulted in insufficient statistical power, which impedes the reliable detection of inter-group differences in the incidence of serious yet low-prevalence adverse events such as aspiration and laryngospasm.
