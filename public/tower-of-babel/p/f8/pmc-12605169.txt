Introduction

Cauda equina is a bundle of nerves and nerve roots distal to the conus medullaris, typically levels L1-S5, and contains axons of nerves that provide both motor and sensory innervation to the legs, bladder, anus, and perineum [1]. Cauda equina syndrome (CES) is a rare spinal emergency caused by the compression of cauda equina nerve roots due to lumbar disc herniation, lumbar spinal stenosis, epidural hematoma, spinal neoplasms, or non-compressive causes like spinal arachnoiditis, spinal infections, etc. [2]

Cauda Equina Syndrome presents with symptoms and signs like bladder and/or bowel dysfunction, saddle anesthesia, and sexual dysfunction with or without a neurological deficit in the lower limb. While the diagnosis is primarily clinical, it is often supported by imaging, typically through an MRI scan of the lumbosacral spine or CT Myelography. It is necessary to make a diagnosis as early as possible and offer treatment in the form of decompression surgery, which can be open or minimally invasive like Percutaneous Endoscopic Lumbar Discectomy (PELD) or Unilateral Biportal Endoscopy (UBE) ideally within 48 h of presentation [3].

We present a case report and literature review of a 38-year-old male who underwent a unilateral biportal endoscopic discectomy of the L5-S1 spine for complete CES due to lumbar disc herniation within 48 h of present.

Case Report.

A 38-year-old male, serving army personnel with no known comorbidity, no relevant family, allergy or drug history, presented with an acute onset low back pain for 2 months, urinary incontinence for 10 days, and a tingling sensation in his left lower limb for 1 week associated with constipation for 5 days. There was no history of trauma or fever. Upon examination, palpation of the spine revealed tenderness along the L4-L5 region. His motor and sensory examination of the L2-S1 nerves were intact. However, the perianal sensation was impaired with lax anal tone. The bulbocavernosus reflex was absent, while the plantar reflex was downgoing bilaterally. The patient's laboratory parameters were within normal limits.

X-ray showed loss of lumbar lordosis (Fig. 1), while the MRI revealed a posterior annular tear with disc extrusion and caudal migration at the L5-S1 level, causing impingement on the bilateral traversing S1 nerve roots. There was severe spinal canal stenosis with impingement on the cauda equina nerve roots. Additionally, there was a diffuse disc bulging with a posterior annular tear at the L4-L5 level (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). When correlated clinically, these features supported diagnosis of cauda equina syndrome.

The patient underwent an emergency endoscopic discectomy of the L5-S1 spine with a biportal entry port under general anesthesia. The patient was kept in a prone position over a radiolucent table. Two skin incisions were made from a left-sided approach under fluoroscopic guidance: at the junction of the medial border of the pedicles and the intervertebral disc space, with a space of 1 cm in between. The docking point was the inferior margin of the cranial lamina. Paraspinal skin incisions for the left-sided approach involve the scope portal, instrumental portal, and isthmus docking point made at the junction of the lateral margin of the transverse process (TP) and points 1 cm above and below the target disc space.

The initial docking point of the endoscope and serial dilators were located between the pathologic level of the Spino-laminar junction and the inferior margin of the caudal lamina. The paraspinal muscle was dissected around the docking point using a radiofrequency probe and muscle shaver. The outer layer of Ligamentum flavum and soft tissue were removed using a Kerrison punch to confirm the discectomy landmark.

A retractor was used to protect the nerve root during discectomy throughout the procedure. After annulotomy, the disc was removed with pituitary forceps. (Fig. 4) The disc space was exposed by carefully retracting the dura on both ipsilateral and contralateral sides. The epidural bleeding was controlled, and annuloplasty was performed using a radiofrequency probe. The drain was placed under endoscopic guidance, muscles were approximated and skin incision was closed with a non-absorbable suture.

Intra operative picture of extruded disc being removed with forceps.

Post-operatively, his motor and sensory examination remained intact with significant improvement in perianal sensation and anal tone. The rehabilitation program incorporated physiotherapy, encompassing back-strengthening exercises, bladder training, and functional training. Patient consent was obtained for the purpose of the study with due care to maintain his/her privacy and the report follows the SCARE guidelines [4].

One-month post-operative MRI showed lumbar disc degenerative disease with secondary canal stenosis at the L5-S1 level without significant neural compression (Fig. 5, Fig. 6).

Discussion

Cauda equina syndrome can be broadly classified into incomplete and complete CES [2]. In incomplete CES, a lesion partially damages the cauda equina, leading to the need to strain during urination. In contrast, complete CES involves total damage to the cauda equina, resulting in urinary retention and loss of perianal sensation [5].

Incomplete CES has a better prognosis and good surgical outcome, with immediate spinal decompression within 24 h of presentation [6] while only 70 % of the patients with complete CES may regain bladder function after treatment. Significant motor and sensory recovery are observed in both complete and incomplete CES when surgery is performed within 48 h of symptom onset according to some articles [7] while some guidelines state that there is no safe time window and recommend emergency surgery as soon as practically possible [8].

Cauda equina syndrome can be managed with open surgical decompression (laminectomy, hemilaminectomy, discectomy) or newer minimally invasive techniques like percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (PELD) or unilateral biportal endoscopic discectomy (UBE). Minimally invasive techniques are becoming increasingly popular in well-equipped settings due to their lower morbidity, smaller incisions, shorter operating time, and quicker recovery [9].

UBE involves two small incisions, offering a wide, clear endoscopic view with minimal soft tissue damage than the uniportal approach and allows free manipulation of instruments, which is typically not possible in open spine surgery [10]. Biportal endoscopic discectomy tends to cause less muscle damage with lesser surgical site pain than microscopic discectomy but it also carries a risk of complications such as dural tear (5 %) and epidural hematoma (1 %) [11].

In a retrospective of 32 patients, Park et al. have shown UBE to be an effective option for management of CES with minimal tissue damage and complication rate [12]. Additionally, along with better visualization during surgery, UBE has also shown to facilitate early ambulation post-surgery and improved rehabilitation outcomes with overall quality of life for patients in not just CES but in cases of stenosis and discectomy too [[13], [14], [15]].

Conclusion

Immediate surgical decompression using minimally invasive techniques, such as biportal endoscopic discectomy for cauda equina syndrome, provides improved surgical outcomes, faster patient recovery, and less procedural morbidity.
