A structural model of the impact of client’s mental health components on their motivation to participate in vocational training courses
Authors: Zahra Khoshnodifar, Pouria Ataei, Hamid Karimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study presents a structural model of clients' mental health and their motivation to participate in prison vocational training courses. The research population included all male and female clients in Arak Prison, located in Markazi Province, Iran. Among them, 83 individuals were selected as the research sample using a stratified sampling technique with proportional assignment. The data collection instruments were the Goldberg General Health Standard Questionnaire and the Harter Academic Motivation Questionnaire. The validity of these instruments was confirmed by a panel of experts, and their reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and the composite reliability index. Data were analyzed using the partial least squares statistical method in SmartPLS software. Based on the total mental health score (sum of the four subscales: physical health, anxiety, social action, and depression), 41% of clients were found to be at risk. Moreover, more than 60% of the clients demonstrated an average level of educational motivation. Physical health, depression, anxiety and sleep disorders, and social action explained 44.2% of the variance in clients' educational motivation to participate in vocational training courses, with physical health, anxiety and sleep disorders, social action, and depression contributing the most, respectively. In addition, the three constructs of physical health, depression, and anxiety and sleep disorders accounted for a total of 64.6% of the variance in clients’ social action.Keywords: Academic motivation, Mental health, Prisoners, Social action, Vocational training courses

Introduction

An essential dimension of health is mental health, which is broadly defined as the full capacity to fulfill social, mental, and physical roles; establish balanced and coordinated relationships with others; adapt to and reshape the personal and social environment; and resolve conflicts and internal tensions in a just, rational, and appropriate manner [3, 36, 39]. Mental health is a broad and multifaceted concept, influenced, directly or indirectly, by a range of positive and negative factors. From a public standpoint, mental health is of paramount importance in reducing both material and spiritual costs, thereby aiding the achievement of national goals and societal aspirations [19, 31, 37]. In this context, mental disorders constitute a critical component of the overall disease burden, and the prevalence of mental and neurological disorders is projected to rise within this burden in the future [15, 34].

Today, people’s perspectives on crime and imprisonment have undergone a fundamental shift compared to past centuries, with a growing preference to approach these issues in the context of illness, treatment, and rehabilitation rather than solely through the lens of criminality and punishment [17]. Crimes occur due to a range of hereditary, psychological, sociocultural, and specific environmental factors [6, 23, 28]. Almost all criminological studies, particularly those examining the causes of criminal behavior, indicate that mental disorders are a central and significant factor contributing to the commission of crimes [41, 42]. Therefore, prisons should be regarded as environments where individuals with mental illnesses are concentrated, making it predictable that the prevalence of mental disorders is higher in prisons than in the general population [44].

Accordingly, it is crucial to examine the mental health of prisoners as a cornerstone of their overall well-being, since empirical evidence has shown that the unique conditions of prisons, such as psychological pressure, abandonment, overcrowding, long-term incarceration, separation from family, and other mental and social challenges, negatively impact their mental health [22, 28, 50]. This, in turn, influences prisoners' interpersonal interactions and their engagement in vocational training programs, which constitute a major component of prison rehabilitation efforts. Researchers emphasize that establishing employment workshops within prisons can boost work morale and nurture prisoners' skills and talents [7]. Beyond fostering motivation and hope for a better life, such opportunities also cultivate a sense of self-worth and confidence, as prisoners can use their earnings not only to fulfill personal needs but also to provide a basic livelihood for their families, thereby reducing personal stress [27].

Lack of mental health in an individual not only diminishes their personal and social adaptability, but also threatens the security and mental well-being of their family and broader social groups [10, 32]. In some cases, this issue intensifies to the point that it leads to harassment and violation of the rights of others, resulting in the individual's involvement with legal and criminal authorities. Various studies indicate that 10–15% of prisoners suffer from a lack of mental health [44]. Comparative research shows a direct relationship between unemployment or unstable employment status and the incidence of delinquency and crime. Among economic indicators such as inflation and recession, unemployment is considered significantly more harmful [20]. There exists a notable difference in mental health between unemployed and employed populations. Supporting this perspective, the Theory of Action Limitation explains how unemployment affects mental health. According to this theory, job loss restricts an individual's resources and, consequently, their experiences, hindering their ability to envision a meaningful future, ultimately harming their mental health [52]. In fact, when individuals are denied access to a proper job, which serves as a key indicator of one’s success in society, unemployment, whether overt or subtle, impacts all explicit and implicit dimensions of their lives. This objective and psychological strain gradually undermines their mental health in a predictable manner [43].

A fundamental issue regarding the reintegration (resocialization) of prison clients is the proposition that developing talents and learning jobs and skills suited to individuals' abilities contributes to the correction and treatment of criminals [4]. This concept, which is based on assumptions such as the capacity of convicts to learn and change, and the belief that convicts possess significant abilities in thinking, reasoning, and action, represents one of the most important techniques and approaches for correcting and treating convicts in prisons [35]. Without concentrating on components such as identifying and controlling unconscious motivations or making changes and transformations in the personality structure of convicts, this approach aims to empower them to embrace work and activity within prison and integrate them into a way of life similar to that of the outside world [33].

Research has shown that the low participation of prisoners in formal and vocational training courses stems from insufficient motivation for learning [14, 49]. It has been stated that a lack of awareness regarding educational design patterns, especially motivational patterns, may result in failure, poor performance, and a decline in motivation and interest in the learning process [24]. Ansari et al. [1] argue that the skills training of prisoners is shaped by macro-structural factors in society, intermediate organizational factors, and environmental and physical conditions that underlie the motivation of both prisoners and instructors, ultimately influencing the effectiveness and efficiency of prison training courses.

It appears that one way to encourage prisoners to participate in vocational training is by using instructional design models with a motivational approach. Motivational design is a systematic, goal-oriented, and problem-solving process that identifies the conditions under which learners are motivated to engage with training and actively participate in the learning process [26]. As proposed by Keller, motivational educational design is a structured method for recognizing, applying, and implementing effective motivational elements within a learning environment [16]. These models offer strategies intended to stimulate and sustain learners’ purposeful actions toward achieving desired learning outcomes [47]. In his framework of motivational educational design, Keller integrates motivational theories and strategies with educational design principles, thus offering a practical method for encouraging learners to make greater efforts toward attaining educational goals [5, 11, 26, 30].

Despite the growing body of research on both prisoner mental health and vocational training outcomes, the existing literature reveals several significant gaps that the present study seeks to address. First, although individual mental health dimensions—such as depression or anxiety—have been studied in relation to prisoner behaviour, few studies have simultaneously examined multiple mental health components within a unified structural model to assess their relative contributions to educational motivation. This fragmentation limits the ability of correctional administrators to prioritise mental health interventions that most effectively enhance training participation. Second, the literature on prison vocational education has largely focused on post-release outcomes such as recidivism and employment [8, 38, 51], while the proximal question of what motivates prisoners to engage in training in the first place remains undertheorised. Third, and most critically, there exists a clear disconnect between motivational design theories and empirical research on prison education. Keller's [26] ARCS model—comprising Attention, Relevance, Confidence, and Satisfaction as essential conditions for learner motivation—offers a robust framework for understanding how psychological states translate into educational engagement. However, to date, no study has systematically linked the ARCS components to empirically measured mental health indicators in a prison context.

This theoretical disconnect represents a missed opportunity. Each ARCS component can be conceptually anchored to specific mental health dimensions examined in this study. Attention, the capacity to focus on and sustain interest in learning stimuli, is directly compromised by anxiety and sleep disorders, which disrupt cognitive resources and increase distractibility [25]. Relevance, the perception that learning content is personally meaningful and connected to one's goals, is undermined by depression, which diminishes future orientation and the ability to envisage a purposeful life beyond incarceration [12]. Confidence, the belief in one's ability to succeed in a learning task, is contingent on both physical health—which provides the energy and bodily integrity necessary for sustained effort—and social functioning, which validates one's sense of efficacy through interpersonal feedback and role performance. Satisfaction, the intrinsic and extrinsic rewards derived from learning, is eroded by the anhedonia characteristic of depression and reinforced by the positive affect associated with physical well-being and social connectedness.

Contradictory findings in the existing literature further underscore the need for an integrative model. Some studies report that depression is the strongest predictor of non-participation in prison programs [44], while others emphasise the primacy of anxiety-related avoidance behaviours [28]. Similarly, the role of social action remains ambiguous: some evidence suggests it mediates the relationship between mental health and engagement [31], whereas other findings point to a direct effect on motivation. These inconsistencies likely arise from the lack of studies that test these relationships simultaneously within a single structural model, which is precisely the contribution of the present research.

By explicitly mapping the ARCS motivational components onto the four mental health dimensions and testing their relative predictive power, the current study bridges the gap between motivational theory and the empirical realities of prison education. This integration transforms the ARCS model from a general instructional design heuristic into a testable framework for understanding how specific psychological deficits inhibit or facilitate motivation to learn in correctional settings.

Research has shown that prisoners who participate in vocational training programs demonstrate a significant reduction in recidivism (including reoffending, re-arrest, reconviction, re-incarceration, and technical parole violations) compared to learners who do not participate [51]. Similarly, Aos et al. [2] reported that, on average, vocational training programs led to a 12.6% decrease in the recidivism rate among participants compared to non-participants. Davis et al. [8] found that adult participation in vocational training programs reduced the likelihood of recidivism by 36% compared to those who did not engage in such programs. In their study on the effect of vocational training on prisoners, Valentine and Redcross [48] showed that vocational training programs, along with in-prison employment, contributed to a reduction in recidivism among released inmates.

Cale et al. [7] studied prison skills training and the re-entry of former prisoners, finding that participation in vocational training during imprisonment increased the likelihood of remaining free for 2 to 5 years after release in both men and women. Newton et al. [38] conducted an empirical study on the effect of vocational and skills training programs on recidivism. An important finding was that, although there was a strong link between crime and unemployment, relatively little was known about the impact of vocational and skills training programs on recidivism. Another finding was the limited support associated with participation in such programs and the challenges in achieving subsequent employment success.

Theoretical framework and hypothesis development

The present study is grounded in Self-Determination Theory (SDT; [9]), a macro-theory of human motivation that has received extensive empirical support across diverse educational, clinical, and organisational contexts. SDT posits that intrinsic motivation—the inherent drive to engage in activities for their own sake—flourishes when three basic psychological needs are satisfied: autonomy (the experience of volition and self-endorsement of one's actions), competence (the feeling of effectiveness and mastery in one's interactions with the environment), and relatedness (the sense of belonging and meaningful connection with others). Critically, SDT is not merely a theory of optimal functioning but is increasingly recognised as a framework for understanding mental health itself. As Piltch [40] and Thomas et al. [45] have demonstrated, self-determination is integral to mental health recovery, with the restoration of autonomy, competence, and relatedness serving as both a process and an outcome of psychological healing.

The prison environment presents a near-total deprivation of the conditions necessary for basic psychological need satisfaction. Incarceration inherently restricts autonomy through regimented schedules and limited personal choice; it undermines competence by severing individuals from meaningful work and skill development; and it disrupts relatedness through separation from family, friends, and community. This systematic need frustration provides a powerful theoretical lens through which to understand the elevated prevalence of mental health disorders in prison populations and, critically for the present study, their dampening effect on educational motivation. Specifically, the four mental health dimensions examined in this research—physical health, anxiety and sleep disorders, social action, and depression—can be conceptualised as manifestations of thwarted basic psychological needs. Physical health complaints and anxiety-related symptoms reflect compromised competence, as the energy, concentration, and emotional stability required for learning are depleted. Depression, particularly its core features of anhedonia and hopelessness, represents a profound erosion of autonomy, wherein the individual loses the capacity to envision and pursue self-endorsed goals—including engagement in vocational education. Deficits in social action, encompassing reduced interpersonal functioning and diminished satisfaction in daily activities, signify impaired relatedness, which undermines the social reinforcement and belonging that sustain motivation over time.

To translate these psychological mechanisms into the practical domain of instructional design, the present framework incorporates Keller's [26] ARCS model as a complementary, applied layer. The ARCS model identifies four conditions necessary for learner motivation: Attention (capturing and sustaining interest), Relevance (connecting content to personal goals), Confidence (building learners' belief in their ability to succeed), and Satisfaction (providing intrinsic and extrinsic rewards for learning). When mapped onto SDT, each ARCS component can be linked to specific mental health dimensions. Anxiety and sleep disorders impair Attention by consuming cognitive resources,depression diminishes Relevance and Satisfaction by blunting goal-directed thinking and the capacity for positive affect; and physical health and social functioning jointly underpin Confidence by providing the somatic and interpersonal resources that enable sustained learning effort. This dual-framework integration—SDT as the deep psychological foundation and ARCS as the instructional design bridge—provides a comprehensive theoretical basis for modelling mental health as a predictor of educational motivation in prison settings.

A further theoretical refinement is drawn from recent work by Trigueros and García-Mas [46], who introduced the concept of novelty as a motivational facilitator within SDT-grounded educational research. Novelty—defined as the perception of new, varied, and stimulating experiences—has been shown to enhance intrinsic motivation by satisfying an inherent human propensity for exploration and cognitive engagement. In the prison context, where environmental monotony and sensory deprivation are endemic, the perception of vocational training as a novel opportunity may act as a powerful motivational stimulus. Critically, the capacity to perceive and respond to novelty is not independent of mental health status. Individuals experiencing depression may exhibit diminished sensitivity to novel stimuli due to anhedonia and cognitive constriction,those with elevated anxiety may perceive novelty as threatening rather than inviting; and those with impaired social functioning may struggle to engage with the interpersonal novelty that group-based training provides. Thus, mental health dimensions influence educational motivation not solely as direct constraints—a linear, deficit-based view—but as psychological filters that shape how prisoners perceive, interpret, and respond to the motivational stimuli embedded in vocational training environments. This reconceptualisation transforms the proposed structural model from a reductionist input–output framework into a more dynamic, interactive system wherein mental health and motivation are linked through perceptual and need-based mechanisms.

Based on this integrative theoretical framework, the present study proposes a structural model in which the four mental health dimensions—physical health, anxiety and sleep disorders, social action, and depression—function as antecedent conditions that influence prisoners' motivation to participate in vocational training courses, both directly and through their effects on social action as an intervening variable.

Based on this framework, the following directional hypotheses are proposed:

H1: Physical health is positively associated with educational motivation.

H2: Anxiety and sleep disorders are negatively associated with educational motivation.

H3: Social action is positively associated with educational motivation.

H4: Depression is negatively associated with educational motivation.

H5: Physical health is positively associated with anxiety and sleep disorders.

H6: Physical health is positively associated with depression.

H7: Physical health is positively associated with social action.

H8: Anxiety and sleep disorders are positively associated with depression.

H9: Depression is negatively associated with social action.

Therefore, it can be stated that the use of a motivational educational design model may influence prisoners' participation in the learning and vocational training process. Given the high cost of vocational training in prisons—and to prevent the waste of capital, time, and energy—it is especially important to consider the mental health of incarcerated individuals and their motivation to engage in vocational training courses. Accordingly, this study was conducted to present a structural model of the effect of clients’ mental health components on their motivation to attend vocational training courses.

Methods

This research was an applied study in terms of its purpose, a field study in terms of the extent of monitoring and the degree of variable control, and a descriptive (non-experimental) study in terms of the data collection method. The statistical population consisted of all male and female clients at Arak Prison in Markazi Province, Iran. A conservative value of P = 0.20 was used to maximise the required sample size, yielding an initial target of 104 participants.

A total of 104 questionnaires were distributed. Following data collection, all returned questionnaires were screened for completeness and response quality. Twenty-one questionnaires were excluded based on the following pre-established criteria: (a) incomplete responses exceeding 15% of items on any of the primary scales (n = 14); and (b) evidence of patterned or inattentive responding, such as straight-lining or alternating response patterns (n = 7). The final analytical sample comprised 83 valid cases, representing an 80% response rate. To assess potential attrition bias, the demographic characteristics (age, gender, education, and imprisonment type) of the excluded cases were compared with those of the retained sample using independent-samples t-tests and chi-square tests. No statistically significant differences were observed (p > 0.05 for all comparisons), suggesting that case exclusion was unlikely to have introduced systematic selection bias.

The clients’ mental health was assessed using the Goldberg General Health Questionnaire, which includes scales for physical symptoms, anxiety and sleep disorder symptoms, social action, and depressive symptoms. According to this scale, a score of 1–9 indicates the individual’s physical health in relation to psychosomatic illnesses, while a score of 11–15 suggests average physical health. Individuals in this range more or less experience physical symptoms due to adverse psychological conditions. Furthermore, a score of 16–21 reflects severe symptoms of psychosomatic illnesses, and individuals with such scores should be referred to a specialist for physical evaluation. The symptoms of anxiety and sleep disorders were assessed by examining clinical signs such as severe anxiety, insomnia, feeling under pressure, and irritability. On this scale, a score of 1–9 indicates a favorable condition for maintaining calm and managing anxiety; a score of 11–15 suggests occasional anxiety issues; and a score of 16–21 points to severe anxiety in various situations. Therefore, anxiety control and relaxation training methods are recommended for these individuals.

The social action scale evaluates an individual's ability to perform daily tasks, their sense of satisfaction in completing tasks, feelings of usefulness, learning capacity, and enjoyment of everyday activities. A score of 1–9 indicates full health, while a score of 11–15 reflects relative health in interpersonal relationships and workplace communication. Additionally, a score of 16–21 suggests that the individual experiences difficulties in interpersonal relationships and frequently encounters problems in work-related situations. Therefore, it is recommended that such individuals receive training in interpersonal skills.

On the depression scale, a score of 1–9 reflects an individual's vitality and cheerfulness in daily life, while a score of 11–15 indicates that the person occasionally experiences symptoms of depression. A change in lifestyle can bring about a complete transformation in these conditions. Additionally, a score of 16–21 suggests that the person is likely suffering from depression. Therefore, referral to a specialist is recommended. The Harter Standard Educational Motivation Questionnaire was employed to assess the clients’ educational motivation. This questionnaire contains 33 items and aims to evaluate the level of internal motivation among clients to participate in in-prison vocational training courses. An educational motivation score of 33–66 indicates a weak level of motivation, whereas a score of 66–99 reflects a moderate level. A score exceeding 99 represents a good level of educational motivation.

Another section of the questionnaire focused on the clients’ personal and professional characteristics, including gender, age, educational level, marital status, and the duration and reason for imprisonment. The content validity of the questionnaire was established through a systematic expert review process. An expert panel comprising eight specialists was convened to evaluate the instrument. The panel included: (a) two faculty members in agricultural extension and education with expertise in questionnaire design and psychometrics; (b) two correctional psychologists with clinical experience in prison mental health assessment; (c) two senior prison education officers with direct experience in delivering and evaluating vocational training programs in correctional settings; and (d) two researchers in socio-economic and agricultural extension with methodological expertise in structural equation modelling. The inclusion of experts from both academic and practitioner backgrounds was intended to ensure that the instrument was assessed for both scientific rigor and practical relevance to the prison context.

Each expert independently evaluated the questionnaire across four dimensions using structured evaluation forms:

Relevance: The extent to which each item is essential and appropriate for measuring the intended construct within the prison population.

Clarity: The extent to which each item is worded unambiguously and is comprehensible to respondents with varying educational backgrounds.

Simplicity: The extent to which the language, phrasing, and sentence structure of each item are straightforward and free from technical jargon.

Comprehensiveness: The extent to which the set of items collectively covers the full domain of each construct as defined in the theoretical framework.

In addition, Cronbach's alpha coefficient and the composite reliability index were used to assess its accuracy and reliability. The results indicated that Cronbach’s alpha exceeded 0.7 for all constructs. Furthermore, the composite reliability—used to assess the internal consistency of the measurement model—was above 0.7, suggesting that all constructs demonstrated acceptable composite reliability. Accordingly, the questionnaire content was approved and distributed to the clients. The data were analyzed using the partial least squares statistical method and SmartPLS software.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The distribution of clients by gender indicated that 75% were male and 25% were female. Their average age was approximately 39 years, with the youngest being 26 and the oldest 59 years old. The majority of clients (52.6%) were within the age range of 36–40 years. Regarding education, 41.2% of clients held a diploma, making it the most common educational level.

The frequency distribution of clients in terms of imprisonment history, duration, and frequency revealed that more than half (58.8%) had no history of imprisonment. The most common duration of imprisonment was between 3 and 5 years (28.2%), while over 16% were in a state of uncertainty about the exact duration of their imprisonment. The majority of clients had been incarcerated for offenses such as theft, financial crimes, drug-related charges, fraud, and fighting, listed in order of frequency.

As evident, most respondents had not experienced feelings of worthlessness, hopelessness, feeling bad, or the wish to die in the past month and were in the normal range for the symptom of satisfaction with doing things. Regarding suicidal thoughts and suicide attempts, it can be stated that more than 40% had not thought about or attempted suicide, representing the most frequent response.

Mental health score of clients

The total mental health score for each individual was calculated by summing the scores of the four subscales (physical health, anxiety, social action, and depression). The lower the score, the better the state of health. Scores ranging from 14 to 21 in each subscale indicate the severity of the individual's condition in the respective factor. A total score of 21 or higher indicates poor general health, whereas a score below 21 reflects mental well-being. A total score between 1 and 21 suggests that the individual is in a favorable state of mental health, while a score between 22 and 42 indicates that the individual's mental health is at risk in certain areas. Additionally, a score ranging from 43 to 63 signifies that the individual's mental health has been compromised in multiple aspects, and attention should be paid to improving their living conditions and psychological well-being. Finally, a score between 64 and 84 indicates a serious health condition, and a referral to a relevant specialist is strongly advised. According to the results shown in Table 3, 41% of the clients—the highest frequency—were at risk of harm in some areas of mental health.

Overall model fit

The SRMR was calculated for both the saturated model (which assumes all constructs are freely correlated) and the estimated model (which imposes the hypothesised structural relationships). The SRMR for the estimated model was 0.074, which falls below the conservative threshold of 0.08 recommended for PLS-SEM [21]. This indicates that the discrepancy between the model-implied and observed correlation matrices is within acceptable limits, supporting acceptable overall model fit. The saturated model yielded an SRMR of 0.068, also below the threshold.

Predictive relevance (Q2)

The Stone-Geisser Q2 values were calculated using a blindfolding procedure with an omission distance of 7. The Q2 value for social action was 0.389, substantially exceeding the threshold of zero and indicating large predictive relevance for this endogenous construct [18]: Q2 > 0 = small, > 0.25 = medium, > 0.50 = large). The Q2 value for educational motivation was 0.305, exceeding the medium predictive relevance threshold. These results confirm that the model possesses satisfactory predictive relevance for both criterion variables. In other words, the mental health predictors do not merely explain variance retrospectively but are capable of predicting the endogenous constructs with an acceptable degree of accuracy.

Out-of-sample prediction (PLSpredict)

To further substantiate the model's predictive capability, the PLSpredict procedure with 10 folds and 10 repetitions was conducted. The prediction errors (Root Mean Square Error; RMSE) from the PLS model were compared against those from a naïve linear regression model (LM) benchmark. For the majority of indicators of both social action (5 out of 7 indicators) and educational motivation (14 out of 21 indicators), the PLS model produced lower RMSE values than the LM benchmark, indicating medium predictive power. This provides additional confidence that the model is not merely explanatory but possesses genuine out-of-sample predictive utility.

Multicollinearity assessment

Collinearity among the predictor constructs was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). All VIF values were below the conservative threshold of 3.0 (Table 11), and substantially below the more lenient threshold of 5.0 recommended by Hair et al. [18]. Specifically, VIF values for predictors of educational motivation ranged from 1.427 (social action) to 2.210 (anxiety and sleep disorders), while VIF values for predictors of social action ranged from 1.196 (depression) to 1.751 (physical health). These values confirm that collinearity was not a concern and did not inflate the standard errors of the path coefficients.

Discussion

The present study aimed to develop and test a structural model of the impact of mental health components on prisoners' motivation to participate in vocational training courses. By grounding this model in an integrative theoretical framework combining Self-Determination Theory (SDT; [9]), Keller's [26] ARCS motivational design model, and the concept of novelty as a motivational facilitator [46], the study sought to move beyond descriptive accounts of mental health in prisons toward an explanatory understanding of how specific psychological dimensions facilitate or inhibit educational engagement.

Mental health status in the prison sample. The descriptive findings revealed that 41% of participants were classified as at risk in some areas of mental health, while only 31.3% fell within the optimum range. This finding is consistent with the broader literature documenting elevated rates of mental health difficulties in prison populations compared to the general community [12, 28, 44]. The clustering of symptoms across physical, anxiety, social, and depressive domains underscores the multidimensional nature of psychological distress in correctional settings and supports the need for comprehensive mental health screening and intervention rather than single-disorder approaches.

Physical health as the primary predictor of educational motivation. A central finding of this study was that physical health emerged as the only mental health dimension with a statistically significant direct effect on educational motivation (β = − 0.629, p < 0.001, f2 = 0.143). The negative direction of this coefficient—consistent with the GHQ coding scheme where higher scores denote greater symptomatology—indicates that prisoners experiencing somatic complaints such as fatigue, weakness, headaches, and feelings of illness were substantially less motivated to engage in vocational training. This finding aligns with the "action limitation" perspective articulated by Yarmohamedian and Aridhi [52], which posits that depleted physical resources constrain an individual's capacity to envision and pursue future-oriented goals. Within the SDT framework adopted in this study, physical health impairments may most directly undermine the basic psychological need for competence—the felt sense of being able to effectively interact with one's environment and master new challenges. Vocational training, by its nature, demands physical energy, sustained attention, and bodily well-being,when these are compromised, the learner's perceived competence is diminished, and motivation declines accordingly.

The primacy of physical health also has important practical implications. It suggests that prison-based interventions targeting physical well-being—including adequate nutrition, regular physical exercise, access to medical care, and management of chronic health conditions—may yield motivational benefits that extend beyond health improvement to encompass educational engagement. This is particularly relevant in resource-constrained correctional settings where mental health services may be limited, as physical health interventions may represent a more feasible entry point for enhancing prisoners' readiness to learn.

Non-significant direct effects of anxiety, depression, and social action. Contrary to the study's initial hypotheses, the direct paths from anxiety and sleep disorders (β = − 0.552, p = 0.116), social action (β = − 0.547, p = 0.387), and depression (β = − 0.353, p = 0.846) to educational motivation were not statistically significant, with effect sizes in the negligible range (f2 < 0.03). These null findings require careful interpretation and should not be taken as evidence that these mental health dimensions are irrelevant to educational motivation. Rather, they suggest that the relationships are more complex than a simple direct-effects model can capture.

Several explanations merit consideration. First, from the perspective of the novelty concept introduced by Trigueros and García-Mas [46], mental health dimensions such as anxiety and depression may influence motivation not as direct constraints but as perceptual filters that shape how prisoners interpret the training environment. A prisoner with elevated anxiety, for example, may perceive the social demands of a group-based training course as threatening rather than stimulating, thereby reducing engagement. Similarly, depression may blunt the capacity to experience the novelty of a training opportunity as rewarding. These indirect, perceptually mediated pathways would not be captured by the direct-effects specification tested in the current model and represent an important direction for future research using moderated mediation designs.

Second, the non-significant findings for anxiety and depression may reflect a statistical suppression effect, wherein the shared variance among the mental health predictors—which are moderately intercorrelated (see Table 7)—is primarily channeled through physical health as the dominant predictor. In PLS-SEM, when correlated predictors compete for explained variance, the variable with the strongest zero-order relationship may absorb predictive power from the others. Future studies with larger samples and alternative model specifications (e.g., formative measurement models or hierarchical regression approaches) could help disentangle these shared effects.

Third, the non-significant effect of social action on motivation is particularly instructive. The structural model revealed that physical health, anxiety and sleep disorders, and depression jointly explained 64.6% of the variance in social action (R2 = 0.646), confirming that mental health status is a robust determinant of social functioning in prison. However, social action did not, in turn, predict educational motivation. This suggests that improvements in social functioning—while valuable in their own right for prisoners' overall well-being and reintegration prospects [31]—may not automatically translate into increased motivation to participate in vocational training. Complementary instructional design strategies, such as those derived from the ARCS model [26] that directly target learners' Attention, Relevance, Confidence, and Satisfaction, may be necessary to bridge the gap between social functioning and educational engagement.

Conclusion

The present study aimed to develop a structural model of the impact of mental health components on the motivation of clients to participate in vocational training courses in prisons. The findings indicated that mental health plays a significant role in clients’ educational motivation. Specifically, the components of physical health, anxiety and sleep disorders, social action, and depression had the greatest impact on their motivation, respectively. This underscores the importance of prioritizing the improvement of mental conditions within prison correctional and educational programs.

On the other hand, Keller's motivational design model was identified as an effective framework for enhancing client participation in vocational training courses. By emphasizing motivational factors such as attention, connection, self-confidence, and satisfaction, this model can contribute to designing appealing and effective training programs. Additionally, the findings showed that vocational training programs not only equip clients with technical skills but also provide an opportunity to strengthen social relationships and build their self-confidence.

The results of this study have important practical implications for policymakers and prison administrators. First, considering the significant impact of physical health on clients’ motivation, it is essential to develop a comprehensive plan to improve health and nutritional conditions in prisons. This may include the provision of regular medical services, structured workout programs, and an environment that meets appropriate health standards. In addition, placing special emphasis on sleep disorders—identified in this study as one of the factors that reduce motivation—can yield positive results through the teaching of relaxation techniques and the creation of a suitable environment for rest. Moreover, the findings related to the negative impact of depression on educational motivation underscore the need to expand psychotherapy services in prisons. Establishing specialized counseling clinics with experienced psychologists, holding group therapy sessions, and offering training in coping strategies can help clients better manage their mental health—especially since many, due to the specific conditions of incarceration and separation from family, are vulnerable to negative emotions and suicidal thoughts, which demand targeted interventions.

Another important point is the key role of social interaction in clients’ mental health and motivation. This finding indicates that designing spaces for collective activities in prison can help improve interpersonal relationships and enhance clients’ sense of belonging. Creating group workshops, encouraging collaboration on joint projects, and offering controlled opportunities for interaction with family and the outside community are among the practical solutions in this regard. These measures not only strengthen social skills but also foster a sense of hope and motivation for change among clients.

In the field of vocational training, our results highlight the need to apply motivational design principles in developing training programs. This requires that training courses be tailored to the actual needs and interests of clients, that participatory and experience-based teaching methods be employed, and that positive and encouraging feedback be provided consistently. Moreover, connecting the acquired skills to employment opportunities after release can significantly enhance clients’ motivation to learn.

On a broader scale, the findings of this study emphasize the necessity of adopting a systematic approach to prisoner rehabilitation. A comprehensive resocialization program should address the psychological, educational, occupational, and social dimensions of clients concurrently. Inter-agency collaboration among judicial, educational, health, and support institutions can foster a virtuous cycle of improved mental health, increased motivation to learn, and ultimately, successful reintegration into society. This integrated approach benefits not only the clients themselves but also offers substantial economic and social advantages through reduced recidivism rates.

Theoretical contributions

This study makes several contributions to the theoretical literature on motivation in correctional education. First, it provides the first empirical test of an SDT-grounded structural model linking multidimensional mental health to educational motivation in a prison sample. The finding that physical health was the dominant direct predictor—while anxiety and depression were not—refines SDT's applicability to this population by suggesting that the competence pathway may be more proximally sensitive to somatic than to affective disruptions. Second, the study demonstrates the value of integrating the ARCS model as an instructional design bridge between psychological theory and correctional practice. The non-significant paths from social action and depression to motivation underscore the ARCS model's premise that motivation is not an automatic consequence of improved well-being but requires deliberate design of the learning environment to stimulate attention, demonstrate relevance, build confidence, and provide satisfaction. Third, by introducing the novelty concept [46] into the correctional education literature, the study opens new avenues for research on how the prison environment—characterised by routine, monotony, and sensory deprivation—may either suppress or, when intentionally designed, activate motivational responses to educational opportunities.

Practical implications

The findings carry several implications for correctional policy and practice. The primacy of physical health suggests that prison healthcare services should be viewed not merely as a humanitarian obligation but as a strategic investment in prisoners' capacity to benefit from educational and vocational programming. Integrated health and education pathways—where physical health screening and treatment are systematically linked to educational enrolment and support—may yield synergistic effects. Furthermore, the null findings for direct effects of depression, anxiety, and social action on motivation suggest that classroom-level instructional design is critical. Applying ARCS principles—for example, using hands-on, project-based tasks to capture attention, explicitly linking skills to post-release employment to enhance relevance, providing incremental success experiences to build confidence, and offering meaningful credentials or certificates to foster satisfaction—may help motivate even those prisoners whose mental health status is not optimal. Finally, the substantial explanatory power of mental health for social action (R2 = 0.646) suggests that mental health interventions may have their strongest immediate impact on prisoners' daily interpersonal functioning, which, while not directly motivational, is an essential foundation for successful reintegration.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although the structural model tested in this study is grounded in a well-articulated theoretical framework integrating Self-Determination Theory and the ARCS model, the observed relationships should be interpreted as associational rather than causal. Longitudinal designs that track prisoners' mental health and educational motivation over time are needed to establish temporal precedence and to examine reciprocal effects, such as whether participation in vocational training subsequently improves mental health.

Second, the sample size, although adequate for detecting medium-to-large structural effects as confirmed by the inverse square root power analysis, lacked sufficient power to detect small path coefficients (|ρ|< 0.20). Future studies with larger samples—ideally exceeding 155 participants—would allow for the detection of subtler relationships and enable more complex model specifications, such as multigroup analyses by gender, offence type, or incarceration duration. Multi-site studies drawing from several correctional facilities would also enhance the generalisability of findings beyond the single-institution context of the present research.

Third, all measures were based on self-report questionnaires, which are susceptible to social desirability bias, recall inaccuracies, and common method variance. Although we took steps to ensure anonymity and confidentiality, prisoners may have been motivated to underreport psychological symptoms or overstate their educational motivation due to perceived expectations from prison authorities. Future research should consider incorporating objective indicators such as behavioural measures of training attendance, instructor ratings of engagement, and clinical assessments of mental health to triangulate self-report data.

Fourth, the study was conducted in a single prison in Iran, which may limit the cross-cultural and cross-jurisdictional applicability of the findings. Prison conditions, mental health services, and vocational training provisions vary considerably across countries and even across institutions within the same country. Replication studies in diverse correctional settings are necessary to assess the robustness and boundary conditions of the proposed model.

Finally, the integration of the novelty concept from Trigueros and García-Mas [46], although theoretically enriching, was not empirically operationalised in the current study. Future research should develop and validate measures of perceived novelty in prison educational contexts and test whether novelty moderates or mediates the relationship between mental health and motivation, as proposed in our theoretical framework.
