Exploring the influence of human-animal bonds on suicide characteristics: cross-sectional study of human-animal bond associated suicide in the USA using the National Violent Death Reporting System
Authors: Andreina Giron, Rebecca John, Alice Martino, Laura F Goodman, Peter Yu, Maryam Gholizadeh, Marizen R Ramirez, John Schomberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe human-animal bond has been proposed as a factor in the psychological well-being of individuals. This study examines the distribution and characteristics of suicide precipitated by pet loss or concerns about their welfare. We hypothesise that decedents of suicide precipitated by the loss or welfare concern for animal companions will have characteristics that are significantly different from the general population of suicide decedents.MethodsWe analysed National Violent Death Reporting System suicide data 2003–2021. We categorised them into animal-bond-associated (ABA) and non-ABA (NABA). We compared the two groups across demographics and incident-related characteristics, including age, sex, race, ethnicity, sexual orientation, cause of death, precipitating factors and location. χ2 and t-tests were conducted to assess differences between groups.Results347 731 suicides were analysed, 346 976 were NABA and 755 were ABA. The ABA group was older (mean age 50.6 vs 46.3 years, p<0.0001) and more likely to be white (96.2% vs 87%, p<0.0001). This group also had higher proportions of suicides by poisoning (22.9% vs 14%, p<0.0001), presence of physical health problems (17.8% vs 8%, p<0.0001) and financial problems (17.8% vs 8%, p<0.0001). Suicides in the ABA group were more likely to occur at home (84.1% vs 73.1%, p<0.0001), leave a suicide note (43.7% vs 28.7%, p<0.0001) and disclose intent (25.1% vs 22%, p<0.04).ConclusionABA suicides were more likely to disclose intent and employ less violent methods. Further research is needed to explore the mechanisms through which animal bonds may impact the nature and characteristics of suicide incidents. This can help inform tailored prevention strategies.Level of evidenceLevel III.Keywords: Mental Health, Preventive Psychiatry, Epidemiologic Research Design, Violence

Background

The human-animal bond is a relationship characterised by mutual affection and companionship, often likened to family dynamics.1 2 This bond plays a role in the psychological well-being of individuals, influencing their emotional, mental and physical health.3 Studies have shown that pet ownership can enhance social interactions, reduce anxiety and improve physiological markers of stress, such as cortisol and blood pressure levels.4 However, the emotional impact of pet loss, or concern for possible pet loss, is often underestimated, despite evidence suggesting that it can lead to profound grief and emotional distress.5 This is particularly true for individuals whose pets serve as critical sources of emotional support.6 Recent attention has also turned to the potential influence of the human-animal bond on suicidal ideation and behaviour.7,9 While the bond may offer protective factors, such as a sense of purpose and reduced isolation, the loss of a pet or concerns over its welfare may act as a precipitant for suicide, highlighting the need for further exploration of this association.5 10

Existing literature highlights the complexity of the human-animal bond and its dual role in enhancing mental health and contributing to psychological vulnerability during times of loss.11 For instance, studies have examined the grief responses following pet euthanasia, revealing that such events often constitute disenfranchised grief—an unacknowledged or unsupported form of mourning.12 Additionally, research has identified cases where pet loss or concerns about pet welfare have preceded suicidal acts, coining terms like ‘extended suicide’ to describe these incidents.13,15 Despite this growing body of work, significant gaps remain in understanding the specific characteristics of suicides associated with the human-animal bond. Few studies have quantitatively analysed how the presence or absence of this bond is associated with suicide characteristics, such as the method used, disclosure of intent or demographic trends. This study addresses this gap by leveraging data from the National Violent Death Reporting System (NVDRS) to systematically compare human-animal bond associated (ABA) suicides to those non-humananimal bond associated (NABA).

We hypothesise that ABA suicides are characterised by distinct patterns that are significantly different from the general population of suicide decedents. Additionally, we propose that these incidents are associated with unique precipitating factors, such as physical health concerns or financial stress. The main objective of this study is to examine the demographic, psychosocial and incident-related characteristics of ABA suicides compared with NABA. By filling a critical gap in the literature, this research aims to inform tailored suicide prevention strategies that consider the psychological implications of the human-animal bond.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

This study used data from NVDRS, a comprehensive, population-based dataset that compiles detailed information on violent deaths, including suicides, across the USA. Data from individuals whose deaths were classified as suicide during 2003–2021 were analysed, encompassing narratives from law enforcement and medical examiners, as well as demographic and incident-related variables. Each record was coded with precipitating factors and circumstances associated with the death, providing the foundation for categorising cases into ABA and NABA suicides.

Natural language processing techniques were used to extract key words from narratives related to circumstances of injury and distinguished between ABA and NABA. Suicide cases were included as ABA if detailed narratives and precipitating factor codes explicitly referenced the human-animal bond, such as pet loss, concerns over pet welfare or interactions involving animal ownership. ABA cases were defined as those where the loss of a companion animal or related issues were identified as a precipitating factor. NABA cases were those without any mention of animal-related precipitating factors. Cases with incomplete data on demographics, precipitating factors or method of death were excluded from the analysis. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for cohort studies.16

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. This study analysed deidentified data from NVDRS. As such, it was not feasible to involve patients or the public at any stage of the research process.

Variables

Sociodemographic covariates

Demographic variables included age, sex, race, ethnicity, sexual orientation, marital status, military service and education level. Race was categorised as white, African American, Native American, Asian American or Pacific Islander, two races, other or unknown. Ethnicity was coded as Hispanic or Non-Hispanic and sexual orientation as LGBQ or straight. Marital status categories included single or never married, married or in a domestic partnership, divorced, separated or widowed. Educational attainment was recorded as less than high school, high school graduate, some college or college graduate and above. Military service was recorded as a dichotomous variable indicating whether the decedent had ever served in any branch of the US military, regardless of whether they were actively serving at the time of death.

Primary exposure: ABA

The primary exposure was categorised as ABA or NABA, based on whether animal-related factors were identified as precipitating circumstances in the death narratives. Cases were defined using the R regular expression function ‘grepl’ which organises a corpus of text into a list object and iterates over the contents to identify a match that fits the parameters of a character string or list of character strings. ABA cases were defined using reports from law enforcement and the coroner collected by NVDRS. The list of terms used to define a case was: (“dog put down”, “cat put down”, “cat to sleep”, “dog to sleep”, euthanized dog”, “euthanized cat “,“lost dog”, “lost pet dog”, “lost cat”, “lost her pet cat”, “dog died”, “cat died”, “dog hit by car”, “cat hit by car”, “cat killed”, “dog killed”, “dog ran away”, “cat ran away”, “cat missing”, “dog missing”, “euthanized”, “put down”, “put to sleep”, “dog was sick”, “cat was sick”, “taken to veterinarian”, “taken to vet “,“veterinarian”, “put to sleep”, “s dog”, “s cat”, pet/cat/dog relinquished/given (away/up) /abandoned)).

To improve sensitivity and capture indirect or unexpected phrasing (eg, “hurt”), we also included possessive constructions (eg, “’s dog,” “’s cat”) that broadly referenced pet ownership. All cases identified by this search strategy were then manually reviewed to determine whether the animal-related content was a precipitating factor in the suicide (eg, grief over a pet’s death or distress over relinquishment) versus merely descriptive or incidental (eg, a pet discovering the body). This two-step process allowed for greater specificity in classification and ensured that ambiguous terms like “hurt” were evaluated in the full narrative context.

Mechanism used for death by suicide

Method of suicide was categorised into four groups: poisoning, firearm, hanging and other methods, with classification based on standard NVDRS coding. While these categories are sometimes conceptualised as varying in lethality or violence, we note that per NVDRS definitions, all suicide mechanisms are classified as violent deaths, including poisoning and drug overdose. This broad classification reflects the NVDRS’s inclusion of all self-inflicted injuries, regardless of mechanism, under the umbrella of violent death surveillance.

Mental health and substance use

Mental health and substance use variables included any documentation of mental health diagnoses, history of mental health treatment, presence of depressed mood, known alcohol or substance use problems and prior suicidal ideation or suicide attempts.

Life stressors and interpersonal circumstances

Life stressors and interpersonal problems were abstracted from NVDRS precipitating factor codes. These included physical health problems, financial stress, job-related concerns, housing insecurity or recent eviction, the death or suicide of a friend or family member, recent criminal legal problems, intimate partner conflicts, family relationship problems and interpersonal arguments. The presence of multiple simultaneous crises was also documented as a composite variable.

Incident characteristics

Incident-related variables included whether the decedent had disclosed suicidal intent before death, whether a suicide note was found and whether the death occurred at home. These characteristics were selected to evaluate the communicative and environmental context of suicide events.

Data analysis

Missingness analysis

Missingness patterns were examined across key variables, including age, sex, race and precipitating factors. Little’s Missing Completely at Random test was used to assess whether missing data were random. Cases with excessive missingness (≥10% of key variables) were excluded. All missingness analyses were performed using R V.4.3.1.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographic, psychosocial and incident-related variables, stratified by ABA and NABA groups. Continuous variables were reported as means with SD, and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Differences between groups were assessed using t-tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. Adjusted ORs (AORs) were calculated for each level of a categorical variable after adjusting for age, race, ethnicity and sex. The AOR reports the increased odds of a level of a categorical variable given the decedent was reported to have circumstance related to loss of an animal bond. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p value of <0.05. All analyses were conducted using R (V.4.3.1).

Results

Cohort description

Of a total of 347 731 suicide decedents identified, 755 cases (0.22%) were classified as ABA. Since NVDRS is a state-based surveillance system with staggered adoption over time, the completeness of reporting varies by year and state. Accordingly, this analysis does not aim to draw inferences based on geographic location, as data capture is known to be uneven across regions. The annual number of ABA suicides increased gradually over time with a peak observed in 2016 (figure 1).

Proportion of suicides associated with animal bond by year.

Demographic characteristics

ABA decedents were older (mean years 50.6 vs 46.3, p<0.0001). A higher proportion of ABA decedents were female (30.8% vs 21.6%, p<0.0001) and were of white race (96.2% vs 87%, p<0.0001). In adjusted models, ABA decedents were more likely to be widowed (AOR 1.04, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.06, p<0.0001), divorced (AOR 1.05, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.08, p=0.0001) and less likely to be married (AOR 0.87, 95% CI 0.84 to 0.89, p<0.0001). ABA decedents were also less likely to have less than a high school education (AOR 0.97, 95% CI 0.95 to 0.99, p=0.03) or high school diploma (AOR 0.95, 95% CI 0.92 to 0.98, p=0.01) (tables1 2).

Cause of death

ABA decedents were more likely to die by poisoning (22.9% vs 14%, AOR 1.06, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.08, p<0.0001) and less likely to die by hanging (21.1% vs 27.0%, AOR 0.97, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.00, p=0.05) or other methods (10.5% vs 13.4%, AOR 0.90, 95% CI 0.94 to 0.99, p=0.005). The use of firearms was similar between groups (51.5% vs 49.4%, AOR 1.00, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.04, p=0.72) (tables1 2).

Precipitating factors

ABA suicides were associated with a higher prevalence of physical health problems (17.8% vs 8%, AOR 1.05, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.08, p<0.0001), financial problems (17.8% vs 8%, AOR 1.10, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.12, p<0.0001) and job-related stress (15.8% vs 9.2%, AOR 1.07, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.09, p<0.0001) (see tables1 2). Housing insecurity was also more common among ABA cases (5.8% vs 2.7%, AOR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.04, p<0.0001). ABA decedents were more likely to have recently experienced the death of a friend or family member (19.6% vs 5.6%, AOR 1.14, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.16, p<0.0001) and to have experienced multiple concurrent crises (38.5% vs 26.3%, AOR 1.02, 95% CI 1.008 to 1.03, p=0.0002) (figure 2).

Distribution of animal bond associated suicide precipitants.

Mental health problems were more frequently documented among ABA decedents (53.7% vs 42.1%, AOR 1.09, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.13, p<0.0001) (table 2). Similarly, depressed mood was significantly more prevalent in the ABA group (56.4% vs 32.0%, AOR 1.26, 95% CI 1.22 to 1.30, p<0.0001). Alcohol use problems were also more common among ABA decedents (19.7% vs 16.0%, AOR 1.03, 95% CI 1.008 to 1.06, p=0.0008), no difference was observed for substance use problems (13.3% vs 14.7%, AOR 0.99, 95% CI 0.96 to 1.01, p=0.58) (table 2).

Suicide characteristics

ABA decedents were more likely to have disclosed suicide intent prior to death (25.1% vs 22%, AOR 1.03, 95% CI 1.0004 to 1.06, p=0.04), to have left a suicide note (43.7% vs 28.7%, AOR 1.13, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.17, p<0.0001) and to have died at home (84.1% vs 73.1%, AOR 1.13, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.17, p<0.0001) (tables1 2).

Animal bond loss description

Among ABA decedents, the human-animal bond was most commonly characterised by the death (14.5%) or loss (16.8%) of a pet, followed by euthanasia (5.2%), the pet being killed (5.8%) or sick (5.6%) (figure 3). A total of 229 ABA decedents (30.3%) had documented visits to a veterinarian or animal hospital in the days preceding their suicide.

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to explore the role of the human-animal bond and its association with decedent characteristics by comparing ABA to NABA. We hypothesised that ABA suicides would exhibit distinct patterns, different from the general population of suicide decedents. The results confirmed this hypothesis, demonstrating that ABA cases were associated with older age, higher proportion of female decedents and elevated prevalence of mental health conditions. Over half of ABA decedents had a documented mental health problem (53.7%), and more than half were noted to have experienced depressed mood (56.4%). ABA cases were also significantly more likely to involve financial hardship (17.8%), job-related stress (15.8%) and housing insecurity (5.8%). These stressors, which have been independently linked to increased suicide risk in prior literature, can also increase the likelihood of losing or relinquishing a pet due to inability to afford care, secure pet-friendly housing or manage ongoing responsibilities associated with animal caregiving. The human-animal bond may offer emotional stability and connection for individuals facing chronic stress or depression, and its disruption may amplify feelings of hopelessness or despair. Thus, suicide prevention efforts might benefit from identifying individuals at risk of losing a companion animal due to financial or health-related circumstances and connecting them to targeted support services that could help preserve the bond and mitigate crisis escalation. Notably, ABA suicides were more likely to involve intent disclosure and suicide notes, emphasising the importance of recognising the emotional impact of the human-animal bond in suicide prevention strategies.13 Previous studies have primarily focused on the psychological benefits of pet ownership or the grief associated with pet loss, but few have systematically examined the intersection of these factors with suicide outcomes.3,68 10 This study addresses this key gap by leveraging detailed, national-level data to identify the distinct patterns and precipitating factors of ABA suicides.

Older, white and female individuals were over-represented among ABA cases, suggesting possible sociocultural factors influencing the role of companion animals in these groups. Women, particularly those over 65 years, are significantly more likely to live alone due to longer life expectancy and lower remarriage rates compared with men.19 According to Centers for Disease Control and Prevention data, the average life expectancy for women in the USA exceeds that of men by approximately 5 years.20 21 This increased likelihood of outliving partners may heighten emotional reliance on companion animals for connection and routine. Furthermore, older adults—especially women—are more likely to face chronic health issues, economic instability and social isolation,19 all of which may amplify the emotional impact of pet loss. These intersecting vulnerabilities may help explain the disproportionate representation of this demographic in ABA suicides.

In a prior study, Clements et al8 identified the significant physical and mental health consequences of pet loss, particularly the life-altering nature of this event for bereaved individuals.8 Similarly, our study found that pet loss or concerns about animal welfare were prominent precipitating factors in ABA suicides, suggesting that this bond plays a critical role in emotional distress preceding suicide. However, while Clements et al focused on the broader implications of pet loss or concerns about animal welfare, our study is the first to systematically analyse its role in suicide circumstances using a national-level dataset.

Our findings also extend those of Hunt et al,22 who demonstrated that pet loss during disasters like Hurricane Katrina significantly amplified psychological distress, including post-traumatic stress disorder and depression.22 The heightened prevalence of multiple crises in ABA suicides observed in our study supports Hunt’s assertion that pet loss often compounds existing stressors. However, while Hunt’s study linked pet loss to general psychopathology, our study uniquely connects it to specific suicidal behaviours, such as the higher likelihood of intent disclosure and suicide note presence in ABA cases.

In contrast to a study by Friedmann and Son23, which highlighted the mental health benefits of companion animals, our findings suggest a more complex narrative.23 Our study shows that many ABA decedents were already facing significant adversity—being less likely to be in a relationship, more likely to experience chronic physical or mental health conditions, economic instability and other life crises. Within this challenging context, the human-animal bond may serve as one of the few sources of emotional support and stability. The loss of that bond, whether through pet death, euthanasia, illness or separation, may represent the tipping point that disrupts an individual’s remaining coping mechanisms.

Veterinarians may represent a critical yet under-recognised point of intervention for suicide prevention among individuals experiencing loss of an animal bond. In our study, nearly one-third of ABA decedents had visited a veterinarian prior to their death. These visits often coincided with emotionally distressing events, such as a pet’s serious illness, relinquishment or euthanasia. For pet owners facing financial hardship, the cost of veterinary care may add another layer of acute stress during a crisis. Individuals at highest suicide risk—those facing co-occurring economic, physical health and mental health challenges—may be particularly vulnerable during these encounters. While the primary focus of veterinary professionals is the animal, training programmes should also address the emotional impact of companion animal loss on owners. Veterinary settings could consider implementing protocols for offering mental health resource referrals, posting crisis hotline information in waiting areas and training staff to recognise signs of severe distress. In cases where a pet owner explicitly expresses suicidal ideation, ethical and legal frameworks should be explored to allow for appropriate reporting or referral, similar to procedures in human healthcare. These steps could provide an important safety net for individuals in crisis, particularly when traditional healthcare or mental health systems are not engaged.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the classification of ABA suicides relied on narrative text provided by law enforcement and coroner/medical examiner reports within the NVDRS. As a result, cases without detailed documentation or variation in terminology may have been missed, leading to likely underestimation of the true number of suicides associated with the loss of a pet or concern for animal welfare. Second, the retrospective design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between animal bond-related stressors and suicide outcomes. Third, concerns over data variability are notable. Notably, there is a spike in ABA cases observed in 2016 (figure 1); this may reflect surveillance artefacts rather than a true change in incidence. Specifically, the transition from International Classification of Disease (ICD)-9 to ICD-10 diagnostic coding during this period may have introduced variability in how suicide circumstances were recorded or classified. Fourth, the dataset lacks nuanced information on the duration, intensity or emotional significance of the human-animal bond. Finally, unmeasured confounding remains a concern; in particular, the dataset does not account for access to mental healthcare.

This study highlights the significant influence of the human-animal bond on suicide characteristics and precipitating factors. ABA suicides were associated with unique demographic and psychosocial features, such as an older age, intent disclosure and the presence of suicide notes. By systematically analysing the relationship between the human-animal bond and suicide using national-level data, this study contributes insights to the growing body of literature on the psychological significance of human-animal relationships.

Future studies should aim to build on these findings by incorporating longitudinal designs to explore causal pathways between the human-animal bond and suicide risk. Research should also focus on integrating qualitative research approaches, such as interviews with bereaved family members or survivors of suicide attempts, to yield a deeper understanding of the emotional and relational dynamics underlying ABA suicides. Finally, interdisciplinary collaboration between mental health professionals, veterinarians and public health researchers is crucial to developing interventions that address the unique needs of individuals whose emotional well-being is closely tied to the human-animal bond.
