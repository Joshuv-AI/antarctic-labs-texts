Impact of glucocorticoid dose threshold in definition of lupus low disease activity state: a multinational observational cohort study
Authors: Rangi Kandane-Rathnayake, Alberta Hoi, Worawit Louthrenoo, Vera Golder, Yi-Hsing Chen, Jiacai Cho, Aisha Lateef, Laniyati Hamijoyo, Shue-Fen Luo, Yeong-Jian Jan Wu, Sandra Navarra, Leonid Zamora, Zhanguo Li, Haihong Yao, Sargunan Sockalingam, Yasuhiro Katsumata, Yanjie Hao, Zhuoli Zhang, B M D B Basnayake, Madelynn Chan, Jun Kikuchi, Yuko Kaneko, Tsutomu Takeuchi, Shereen Oon, Sang-Cheol Bae, Sean O’Neill, Geraldine Hassett, Fiona Goldblatt, Kristine Pek Ling Ng, Yih Jia Poh, Nicola Tugnet, Mark Sapsford, Shirley Chan, Cherica Tee, Michael Lucas Tee, Naoaki Ohkubo, Yoshiya Tanaka, Chak Sing Lau, Mandana Nikpour, Eric Morand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study examined if lowering the glucocorticoid (GC) ceiling in the definition of lupus low disease activity state (LLDAS) from 7.5 mg/day to 5 mg/day (LLDAS-5) was associated with better outcomes in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).MethodsData from a 13-country longitudinal SLE cohort (American College of Rheumatology/Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics criteria), collected prospectively between 2013 and 2020, were analysed. Survival analyses were used to examine the longitudinal associations of LLDAS definitions with flare, organ damage accrual (frailty models) and mortality (Cox regression models).Results3801 patients with ≥2 visits were studied, with a median of 2.8 years (IQR: 1.0–5.4) of follow-up data (total visits: 40 949). 2141 (56.3%) patients experienced mild–moderate/severe flares; 717 (20.8%) accrued organ damage, and 80 (2.1%) died. 3072 (80%) patients attained LLDAS in 19 293 (47%) visits, while 2858 (75%) patients attained LLDAS-5 in 17 403 (42%) visits. Most patients in LLDAS were also in LLDAS-5; 214 patients (5.6%) attained LLDAS on at least one occasion, but never attained LLDAS-5. The magnitude of protection provided by LLDAS attainment against flare, irreversible organ damage accrual and mortality was similar with both GC thresholds. HRs (95% CIs) of damage accrual subsequent to spending 12 months in sustained LLDAS and LLDAS-5 were 0.42 (0.33 to 0.54, p<0.0001) and 0.43 (0.34 to 0.55, p<0.001), respectively. Likewise, HRs of flare and mortality corresponding to 12 months in LLDAS and LLDAS-5 were similar.ConclusionsNo evidence was found to support revising the GC dose threshold of the LLDAS definition. Regardless, minimising GC exposure remains a key goal of SLE management.Trial registration numberNCT03138941.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

We sought to determine if lowering the GC ceiling in the definition of LLDAS to 5 mg/day (LLDAS-5) was associated with measurable improvement in protection from flare, irreversible organ damage accrual and mortality in patients with SLE, compared with the original definition. Most patients in LLDAS were in LLDAS-5, and lowering the GC threshold in the LLDAS was not associated with increased protection against organ damage accrual, flare or death.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Lowering GC exposure while maintaining control of disease activity is core to the goals of management of SLE. INTRODUCTION

Glucocorticoids (GC) have been used for the treatment of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) since soon after the discovery of their rapid, broad anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects in the late 1940s. Although two biologicals have been approved for SLE since 2011,1 most patients continue to be treated chronically with GC in combination with antimalarial and immunosuppressive therapies, with large multicentre cohort studies reporting between 69% and 86% of patients, and up to 96% of patients with severe disease, receiving GC.24 GC use in the management of SLE continues despite their well-documented dose-dependent metabolic adverse effects and their contribution to the risk of irreversible organ damage3 5 6 and death.7

Recognition of the harmful contribution of GC use to long-term outcomes in SLE has led to the development and validation of treat-to-target goals for SLE, which incorporate both target states of disease activity and target doses of GC. The lupus low disease activity state (LLDAS) was the first such target state to be developed,8 and it has been prospectively validated as associated with improvements in flare rate, damage accrual, mortality and health-related quality of life.24 912 Alongside goals for low and stable disease activity, the consensus methodology which yielded the definition of LLDAS included a GC dose ceiling of 7.5 mg (prednisolone equivalent) per day; importantly this is a ceiling, rather than a target dose per se. An intentionally more stringent treat-to-target state was defined by the Definition of Remission in SLE (DORIS) group,13 and this has been similarly validated as highly protective11 12 14; DORIS remission requires the absence of clinical disease activity and a daily GC dose ceiling of 5 mg.

Data such as the dose-dependence of GC-related harm, even in patients without active disease,5 and the markedly greater protection from death of GC-free remission compared with DORIS remission or LLDAS,2 have understandably resulted in the pursuit of increasingly stringent goals for GC dosing in SLE treatment guidelines. The 2023 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) treatment guidelines recommend a daily dose ceiling of 5 mg for chronic therapy,15 raising the question of whether the definition of LLDAS should be revised. Here, we have undertaken analysis of a large longitudinal cohort of patients with SLE comparing the utility of LLDAS as originally defined and validated with a modified definition with a 5 mg/day GC dose ceiling, to address this question.

METHODS

Study population

Data from the Asia Pacific Lupus Collaboration (APLC) patient cohort,16 which included patients recruited from 25 sites across 13 countries between 2013 and 2020, were analysed. Analysis was restricted to patients with ≥2 study visits with known LLDAS status. All patients were consenting adults who met either the 1997 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)-modified classification criteria for SLE17 or the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC) 2012 classification criteria.18

Patient and public involvement

Neither patients nor the public were involved in the design or conduct of this study.

Data collection

Data were collected prospectively during routine patient follow-up using standardised data collection paper or electronic forms. Demographic information such as gender, self-reported ethnicity and years of birth, SLE onset and diagnosis were collected at enrolment. SLE disease activity (SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI)−2K),19 Safety of Estrogens in Lupus Erythematosus National Assessment-SLEDAI flare index (SFI)20 and Physician Global Assessment (PGA21), medication types and doses, and laboratory test results were collected at each 3 to 6 monthly routine visits. Organ damage was assessed at enrolment and annually using the SLICC/ACR damage index (SDI).22 A change of one unit in SDI has been demonstrated to be clinically significant23 and was chosen to define damage accrual.

Definitions of LLDAS

LLDAS was defined as published in Golder et al9. In brief, patients were required to fulfil all of the following criteria: SLEDAI-2K ≤4 with no major organ involvement; no new SLEDAI-2K activity since the previous visit; PGA≤1; prednisolone or equivalent ≤7·5 mg/day. The use of antimalarials and standard maintenance doses of immunosuppressive drugs was permitted.

LLDAS-5 was defined following the same criteria, with the exception of the maximum permitted GC dose, which was set at ≤5.0 mg/day. ‘LLDAS>5’ was defined as patients who were in LLDAS but not in LLDAS-5; that is, meeting the disease activity criteria of LLDAS with a daily GC dose >5 mg but <7.5 mg/day.

Percent-time in LLDAS was calculated as the sum of all-time intervals in LLDAS divided by the total length of follow-up, multiplied by 100. Time intervals in LLDAS equalled the length of time between visits if a patient was in LLDAS at two consecutive visits, or half the length of time between visits if a patient was in LLDAS at one visit but not the other. Although proportions of cumulative time in LLDAS as low as 20% have been shown to be protective from adverse outcomes,9 a threshold of >50% cumulative time in LLDAS (LLDAS-50) was used in line with reporting practices in other studies of these endpoints.24 Additionally, we derived sustained LLDAS as described,4 with three cut-offs: 3-month, 6-month and 12-month sustained LLDAS if patients were in these states continuously for ≥91, ≥182 or ≥365 consecutive days respectively.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were flare (mild–moderate or severe as determined using SFI), damage accrual (ΔSDI) and mortality.

Data sharing statement

Access to the APLC dataset is subject to guidelines outlined in the APLC Data Access Policy (available on request). The APLC welcomes requests for aggregate (summary) data or to perform analyses of new research questions, and such requests can be submitted to the APLC Steering Committee via the APLC Project Manager.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are described as mean and SD as well as median with IQR, while categorical variables are described as frequency and percentage (%). Continuous variables were compared using Kruskal-Wallis tests whereas categorical variables were compared using χ2 tests.

Multifailure parametric survival models incorporating Weibull distribution and robust standard errors were performed to examine the longitudinal associations between LLDAS definitions and flare and organ damage accrual. Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine the associations with mortality. These models met the assumption of proportional hazards across different covariates. All models were restricted to complete cases, meaning patient visits with missing data were excluded from regression models.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves were drawn and log-rank test was performed to test the equality of survivor functions of LLDAS definitions over time.

Time-invariant baseline characteristics (eg, gender, ethnicity, gross domestic product (GDP)) and time-dependent variables measured until the visit prior to outcomes (eg, age) were considered as covariates. If univariable associations of covariates with outcomes produced associations with p<0.1, they were considered for inclusion in at least the first multivariable models. Retention of confounders in the models depended on their statistical significance and the impact on magnitude of the hazard ratios (HRs), which are reported with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Diagnostic tests for all survival models were carried out, including goodness-of-fit assessments using likelihood ratio tests, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) to evaluate how well the models fit the data. These diagnostic test results are summarised in supplementary tables.

Two-sided p values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant. No correction for multiple comparisons was made. All statistical analyses were carried out using Stata V.18.

RESULTS

Study cohort

The 2013–2020 APLC cohort includes 4106 patients. For the current study, analysis was restricted to patients with ≥2 study visits with known LLDAS status, excluding 295 patients with baseline visits only and 10 patients without LLDAS assessments. A total of 40 949 study visits in 3801 patients, captured over a median (IQR) 2.8 (1.0–5.4) years follow-up, were analysed. The total analysis time under observation was 12 460 patient-years.

Approximately 92% of the study cohort was female with a median (IQR) age of 29 (21, 39) years at SLE diagnosis and 39 (30, 50) years at enrolment. About 89% of the study participants were Asians; 8% of them had a family history of SLE and about 5% were smokers at enrolment. 53% of patients had tertiary education and 50% were from high-income countries with GDP per purchasing power parity per capita (GDP (PPP)) ≥Int$50 000 (table 1).

Medication use, clinical indicators and outcomes

At enrolment, 80% of patients were on GC with a median (IQR) daily dose of 5.0 (2.5–10); 72% were on antimalarial medications (AM) and 58% were on immunosuppressants (IS) (table 1). During the study observation period, 86% of patients used GC, 79% used AM and 72% used IS at least once. With regard to disease activity, patients’ median (IQR) SLEDAI-2K and PGA at enrolment were 4.0 (2.0–6.0) and 0.5 (0.2–1.0), respectively. The median (IQR) time-adjusted mean (TAM) SLEDAI-2K during the study observation period (also referred to as AMS25) was 2.9 (1.3–4.7), while the median (IQR) TAM PGA was 0.4 (0.2–0.7) (table 1).

About 12% of patients fulfilled the definition of flare at study enrolment and 56% of the study cohort experienced flares at least once during the study observation period. Irreversible organ damage (SDI>0) was present in 38% of patients at enrolment, and 21% of patients accrued more organ damage (ΔSDI>0) during the study observation period. Eighty patients (2%) died during the study period (table 1).

LLDAS attainment

A total of 3072 (81%) patients attained LLDAS at least once, in a total of 19 293 (47%) visits in LLDAS. Approximately 19% (n=729) never attained LLDAS (figure 1a). Protection against flare, organ damage accrual and mortality subsequent to LLDAS attainment regardless of GC threshold was similar (figure 2). For example, the HRs with corresponding 95% CI of the longitudinal association between LLDAS and LLDAS-5 attainment with subsequent flare were 0.62 (0.58, 0.66) and 0.60 (0.56, 0.64), respectively. The risks of organ damage accrual and mortality subsequent to being in LLDAS or LLDAS-5 were also significantly reduced with similar magnitude of protection (table 2). Furthermore, the reductions in risk of flare, organ damage accrual and mortality in patients in each of these states for >50% of observed time (LLDAS-50) were similar between the two GC thresholds. Flare was significantly reduced by 44% for LLDAS (HR 0.56; 95% CI 0.51 to 0.60) and 45% for LLDAS-5 (HR 0.55; 95% CI 0.51 to 0.60). Similarly, organ damage risk was reduced by 37% (HR 0.63; (95% CI 0.54 to 0.72) and 39% (HR 0.61; 95% CI 0.53 to 0.70) for LLDAS-7.5 and LLDAS-5, respectively, and mortality reduced by 59% (HR 0.41 (95% CI 0.23 to 0.75) and 63% (HR 0.37; 95% CI 0.20 to 0.69), respectively. Observations with sustained attainment of the two LLDAS definitions were also similar (table 2), but this study also confirms previous findings that the protections associated with sustained LLDAS for >3, ≥6 and ≥12 months are dose-dependent, and that these are greater than the protection associated with >50% cumulative time in LLDAS.4

Finally, we analysed the subset of patients in LLDAS who had a GC dose >5 mg/day (but <7.5 mg/day). Overall, 214 (5.6%) patients attained LLDAS >5, in 1890 visits (4.6%) during the study observation period. Compared to patients in LLDAS-5, these patients were younger and had a significantly shorter study duration with more patients recruited from low-income countries (GDP (PPP) Int.$<50 000) (table 3). Compared with patients who attained LLDAS-5, LLDAS>5 patients had lower rates of AM use, but significantly higher GC and IS exposure and higher disease activity as measured by time-adjusted mean SLEDAI-2K and PGA scores. Overall outcomes, including flare, damage accrual and mortality were similar between the LLDAS-5 and LLDAS>5 groups, and both subsets of LLDAS exhibited protection against adverse outcomes compared with patients who never attained LLDAS at any GC ceiling (online supplemental table 1).

DISCUSSION

The adoption of treat-to-target approaches in the management of SLE has been promulgated for more than 10 years, following an international consensus process and a review of approaches to how to define such goals in this disease,26 27 accompanied by independent processes to define treatment target metrics. The first to be developed was a low disease activity target, prioritised on the basis that remission attainment in SLE is infrequent and a less stringent but more attainable state could have high utility. The definition of LLDAS arose from a formal consensus methodology including an international group of experts, who were tasked with developing an empirical definition of a low disease activity state associated with improved outcomes8; as treatment-related harm contributes to morbidity and mortality in SLE the process included attention to treatment state thresholds. The results reflected contemporary thinking at the time (2013–2015), including the use of the SLEDAI-2K and PGA to define disease activity goals and a GC threshold of no more than 7.5 mg/day. Importantly, this dose is a ceiling, meaning simply that doses greater than 7.5 mg/day were not considered acceptable, rather than representing a target dose. LLDAS has been shown in multiple independent studies, including a prospective multinational validation study and cohort studies in diverse geographies, to be protective from harmful outcomes, including effects on flare, damage accrual, quality of life and mortality.24 912 LLDAS has now been widely adopted as a clinical trial outcome measure,28 29 following demonstration of useful discrimination in post hoc analyses.30 31 Following the validation of LLDAS, the DORIS definition of remission was developed,13 and similarly validated11 12 14; attainment of DORIS remission in clinical trials has been shown to be more difficult than LLDAS,30 32 33 aligned with the concentric nature of these progressively more stringent treatment goals.

Both LLDAS and DORIS were developed using formal consensus methodology, but with the exception of the disease activity cut-off in DORIS remission which is zero, other thresholds including GC dose are necessarily somewhat arbitrary and were not data-driven. In parallel with the evolution of treat-to-target goals, data on the dose-dependent association of negative outcomes with GC exposure in patients with SLE have continued to accrue, including effects on damage accrual, mortality and quality of life.5 7 Driven in part by these data, treatment guidelines for SLE have evolved towards more stringent ceilings for GC use, with EULAR guidelines in 2019 recommending a target of no more than 7.5 mg,34 in line with the LLDAS ceiling, but a target of no more than 5 mg being set in the 2023 update to these guidelines.15

The authors share the goal of minimising GC exposure in patients with SLE in order to optimise long-term outcomes. However, what does the ambitious goal of 5 mg/day mean for the utility of LLDAS and the interpretation of past and future studies that use this definition? We consider the desirability of a lower GC exposure overall and the validity of a treat-to-target endpoint to be independent concepts. However, here we have undertaken a detailed analysis of GC thresholds in the LLDAS definition in order to better understand this.

First, we found that the majority of patients in LLDAS are in LLDAS-5; in 90% of visits in LLDAS, the GC dose was <5 mg/day. This supports the concept that setting a ceiling dose of GC in a treat-to-target metric does not constrain clinicians from striving for lower GC doses where possible. Second, we found that LLDAS attainment was protective from adverse outcomes of flare, damage accrual and mortality regardless of the GC dose ceiling; this was the case whether analysed on a last-visit basis, in relation to a majority proportion of overall observed time (LLDAS-50) or when looking at multiple thresholds of sustained LLDAS. In fact, the 2019 prospective validation study of LLDAS included sensitivity analysis for the GC dose as well as disease activity thresholds and found no difference in protective effect between cut-offs of 7.5 and 5 mg/day9; the current more thorough analysis confirms this. Overall, we observed the associations of LLDAS attainment with key outcomes, including flare, damage accrual and mortality, which were consistent regardless of whether the GC threshold was set at 7.5 mg or 5 mg. The effect sizes were nearly identical and the CIs overlapped, indicating no material difference in protective associations between the two definitions. Importantly, even under conservative assumptions that take the extremes of the CIs into account, any potential effect of lowering the threshold appears minimal and unlikely to be of clinical significance.

The findings of this study diverge from those of a previous study focused on mortality, in which it was shown that for patients in DORIS remission, lowering the GC dose ceiling from ≤5 mg to <5 mg resulted in a greater protective effect, and a further increase in protective effect was observed when the GC dose was set to zero.7 This suggests that when there is no disease activity, GC dose has an independent effect on outcomes, a finding also demonstrated in a previous study.5 There are several explanations for the lack of difference between outcomes using the two GC dose cut-offs in LLDAS. First, as noted, the number of patients in LLDAS >5 (ie, attained LLDAS but never attained LLDAS-5) was small. In addition, however, LLDAS is a construct including multiple interacting thresholds for both disease activity and treatment, which must all be met for LLDAS to be attained; this implies that in the setting of less than complete control of disease activity, a small difference in GC dose is not enough to independently disrupt the overall protective effects of being in LLDAS. Together, these findings do not support revision of the definition of LLDAS or the need to reanalyse previous cohort studies or trials where LLDAS has been used as an endpoint.

Importantly, analysis of the small group of patients who attained LLDAS>5 reveals some interesting observations. Although they did not have worse outcomes as captured by damage accrual, flare or mortality, this group of patients had higher overall disease activity metrics, including higher baseline and TAM SLEDAI, and higher exposure to immunosuppressives; they were also younger and more likely to reside in lower income countries. This suggests that more severe patients have greater difficulty tapering daily GC dose to 5 mg, a finding indirectly supported by observations of higher rates of relapse in patients with higher levels of circulating type I interferon who taper GC.35

There are some limitations to this study. It was undertaken in a predominantly Asian population and included patients residing in countries with low GDP and underdeveloped health systems; this might mean extrapolation to other populations requires validation. The outcomes analysed in this dataset do not include infections and cardiovascular events that could also represent harmful effects of GC exposure; such data collection is currently ongoing and will be reported in the future. Although using prospectively collected data, the analysis is retrospective and is not as rigorous as study that compared two treatment goal GC ceilings prospectively; the practicalities of such a study mean it is unlikely to be completed.

In conclusion, treat-to-target approaches to SLE have been adopted in international guidelines on the basis of overwhelming high-quality evidence that attaining LLDAS and remission are protective from harmful long-term outcomes. While the authors strongly support the principle of lowering GC dose to the lowest level that controls disease activity, we did not find evidence that the GC dose threshold in the definition of LLDAS should be changed. Analysis of LLDAS-5 as an alternative parallel endpoint is encouraged.
