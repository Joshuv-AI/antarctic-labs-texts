Sarcoidosis: A Clinical Trials Perspective
Authors: Juan J Zapata-Huizi, Manuela Herrera-Tamayo, Josmar J Ríos-Poveda, Eli N Pérez-Rodas, Fernanda Hernández-González, Jacobo Sellares
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSarcoidosis is a systemic granulomatous disorder of unclear origin, most commonly impacting the lungs and mediastinal lymph nodes. Respiratory manifestations occur in most patients and are the main contributors to disease-related morbidity and mortality. Despite decades of clinical experience, the evidence base for treatment has historically been limited, with corticosteroids serving as first-line therapy largely on the basis of observational data and expert consensus rather than robust randomized controlled trials. This comprehensive review examines the current landscape of clinical trials for pulmonary sarcoidosis, with particular emphasis on landmark studies that have shaped contemporary treatment paradigms. Finally, we propose a vision for the future of pulmonary sarcoidosis treatment, emphasizing the need for personalized medicine approaches, validated biomarkers, and international collaborative trials. As our understanding of sarcoidosis pathophysiology deepens and new therapeutic targets emerge, the prospect of disease-modifying treatments that go beyond symptom management becomes increasingly attainable.Keywords: Sarcoidosis, Pulmonary sarcoidosis, Clinical trials, Corticosteroids, Methotrexate, Infliximab, PREDMETH, SARCORT, Immunosuppression, Steroid-sparing agents

Key Summary Points

Recent randomized trials have reshaped first-line treatment of pulmonary sarcoidosis, demonstrating that lower-dose corticosteroid regimens are sufficient and that methotrexate can be a noninferior alternative to prednisone in selected treatment-naïve patients.Evidence supporting steroid-sparing immunosuppressive and biologic therapies remains heterogeneous, with methotrexate having the strongest data, while anti-tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) agents and other immunomodulators show variable efficacy and carry important safety considerations.Emerging targeted and antifibrotic therapies offer mechanistic promise but limited clinical proof, highlighting persistent challenges in trial design, patient heterogeneity, and outcome selection, and underscoring the need for sarcoidosis-specific, adequately powered randomized studies.Advancing sarcoidosis care toward precision medicine requires validated biomarkers, clinical phenotyping, and international collaborative trials to overcome disease heterogeneity and the lack of consensus on optimal endpoints.Recurring methodological limitations—including enrolling patients already stable on corticosteroids and using steroid reduction as the primary endpoint—explain many negative trial results in sarcoidosis; we propose features of an optimal trial design to guide future research.

Introduction

Sarcoidosis is a multisystemic inflammatory disorder that affects the lungs and lymphatic system in over 90% of patients [1, 2]. One-third of patients experience spontaneous remission without need for treatment; nevertheless, approximately half of patients will require therapy, and up to 30% will progress to a chronic, non-remitting form of the disease characterized by severe pulmonary fibrosis, resulting in substantial morbidity and even mortality [3, 4].

Treatment of pulmonary sarcoidosis has historically been largely empirical, with systemic glucocorticoids used as first-line therapy given the disease’s inflammatory nature [5]. However, adverse effects associated with prolonged corticosteroid use, including osteoporosis, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and Cushing’s syndrome, have motivated the search for more specific and better-tolerated therapeutic alternatives [6].

The disease is driven by an exaggerated CD4+ Th1-skewed immune response to unidentified antigens, mediated by activated macrophages and dendritic cells. Granuloma formation is sustained by a pro-inflammatory cytokine network involving tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), interferon gamma (IFNγ), interleukin (IL)-12, IL-18, and IL-23, with additional contributions from Th17/Th17.1 pathways [7]. This improved molecular understanding has opened the door for targeted biologic and small-molecule therapies (Fig. 1) [4, 5, 7, 8].

This review aims to critically analyze clinical trials published in the past decade that evaluate pharmacologic treatments for pulmonary sarcoidosis (Fig. 2) [9–18]. Studies were eligible for inclusion if they reported original clinical data on pharmacologic interventions for sarcoidosis in human subjects, were published in peer-reviewed journals, and reported at least one quantifiable clinical, functional, or patient-reported outcome. Both randomized controlled trials and observational studies (prospective and retrospective cohorts and case series) were included; single-patient case reports were excluded. No minimum sample size was imposed, given that for several agents the entirety of available evidence consists of small case series; however, all such studies are explicitly identified as preliminary or hypothesis-generating throughout the text, and for agents with larger controlled trial data, emphasis is placed on randomized evidence. Studies were not restricted by language, but the focus was on English-language publications from the past decade. We synthesize the evidence for current clinical practice treatment with corticosteroids, conventional steroid-sparing immunosuppressants, and biologic agents (Table 1) [9, 16–22], and summarize emerging therapeutic alternatives reported in recent years (Table 2) [12, 14, 17, 23–28]. We also catalog ongoing and recently completed trials, highlight key evidence gaps, and—drawing on the patterns of failure observed across the trials reviewed—propose the features of an optimal trial design for future sarcoidosis research. Finally, we outline priorities for future research in the pharmacologic approach to this complex disease.

In accordance with Hospital Clínic de Barcelona policies, this activity does not constitute human-subjects research, and institutional review board/ethics committee approval was not required.

Current Pharmacological Approach for Pulmonary Sarcoidosis

Corticosteroids

For over seven decades, oral corticosteroids have been the cornerstone therapy of sarcoidosis [29, 30]. Corticosteroids are widely used, inexpensive, rapidly effective, easily adjustable, have been extensively evaluated in observational studies and clinical trials, and represent both the past and current foundational comparator in clinical trials of novel agents [29, 31].

The optimal dose and treatment duration with corticosteroids remain subjects of debate. First guidelines, such as the American Thoracic Society (ATS), the European Respiratory Society (ERS) and the World Association of Sarcoidosis and Other Granulomatous Disorders (WASOG) 1999 joint statement, the British Thoracic Society (BTS) 2021 statement, and a 2020 Delphi consensus of experts recommend initiating oral prednisone 20–40 mg daily [3, 32, 33]. In contrast, the 2021 ERS clinical practice guidelines on sarcoidosis treatment advise starting at 20 mg daily for most non-life-threatening cases, highlighting the need for research on optimal initial dosing [5].

One of the major randomized trials presenting evidence on corticosteroid dosing in pulmonary sarcoidosis was the SARCORT trial. It compared a higher starting dose of prednisone (40 mg/day) with a lower dose (20 mg/day) among 86 patients followed for 18 months. The incidence of relapse or treatment failure was similar between the high- and low-dose groups (46.5% vs 44.2%, p = 0.75), with no significant differences in time to relapse, lung function improvement (FVC), or patient-reported outcomes, including health-related quality of life and fatigue [16].

Although adverse events were frequent in both groups, patients receiving the higher initial dose (40 mg/day) had significantly greater cumulative corticosteroid exposure without a corresponding reduction in relapse risk or functional benefit. No subgroup demonstrated a clear advantage with the high-dose regimen, and a substantial proportion of patients required re-treatment during follow-up, highlighting the relapsing nature of sarcoidosis and the limitations of prolonged corticosteroid therapy [16, 34].

While the open-label, single-center design and the exclusion of patients with severe organ involvement limit generalizability, SARCORT provides important prospective evidence supporting lower initial corticosteroid doses in non-life-threatening pulmonary sarcoidosis, in line with current ERS recommendations, and underscores the need for steroid-sparing strategies to reduce long-term treatment-related toxicity [16, 34]. This pattern is consistent with historical cardiac sarcoidosis cohorts, in which moderate-dose regimens achieved clinical and structural improvement without evidence of benefit from higher initial doses, further supporting the concept that dose escalation does not translate into superior disease control [35, 36].

Lower steroid dosing remains a recurring theme, as systemic corticosteroid toxicity is dose-dependent yet can remain clinically relevant even at “low” maintenance doses. Analyses from two cohorts of patients with multiple immune-mediated inflammatory diseases demonstrate increasing frequencies of steroid-associated adverse effects with escalating dose and identify complications that may occur at very low exposure (including signals below 5 mg/day), supporting the concept that harm is not restricted to moderate-to-high regimens [6, 37]. In addition, incident cardiovascular risk has been observed even during periods of < 5 mg/day prednisolone-equivalent use compared with non-use, which challenges the notion that low-dose maintenance is unequivocally safe [37].

These complications have driven the pursuit of the lowest effective dose and timely corticosteroid tapering, alongside efforts to develop therapies that aim to replace corticosteroids altogether, allowing sustained disease control while minimizing steroid exposure and thereby reducing the risk of long-term adverse effects [29, 37–41].

Methotrexate

As a steroid-sparing agent in sarcoidosis, methotrexate (MTX) can be used as a first-line option in selected patients. At low doses, it reduces inflammation by increasing adenosine production through inhibition of the folate pathway, and adenosine subsequently downregulates multiple inflammatory and immune processes [5, 29, 42, 43].

The long-standing paradigm of corticosteroids as mandatory first-line therapy was challenged by the results of the PREDMETH trial, a multicenter, open-label, randomized noninferiority study that involved 138 treatment-naïve patients assigned to receive either prednisone or MTX for 24 weeks. Prednisone was initiated at 40 mg once daily and tapered every 4 weeks to 10 mg/day by week 16; by week 24, more than 80% of patients remained on ≥ 10 mg/day. On the other hand, MTX began at 15 mg once weekly and was escalated by 5 mg every 4 weeks to a maximum of 25 mg/week if tolerated; and only about 30% of patients ultimately tolerated 25 mg/week by week 24 [18].

Results found MTX to be noninferior to prednisone for change in percent-predicted FVC at week 24 (mean change + 6.11 vs + 6.75 percentage points, with an adjusted difference − 1.17 [95% CI − 4.27 to 1.93]); these results have proven the first high-quality evidence that MTX can achieve comparable short-term pulmonary efficacy to steroids when used as initial therapy [18]. Although PREDMETH showed a faster onset of improvement in the steroid arm within the first 4 weeks of treatment, drug-specific toxicities were found: prednisone produced notable metabolic effects, including approximately 5 kg weight gain and insomnia by week 24, whereas MTX caused more gastrointestinal symptoms and liver enzyme elevations (9–25%) which were managed with dose or route adjustments [18]. Notably, a small proportion of patients were unable to tolerate MTX because of severe leukopenia, hepatotoxicity, or reduced clearance of its active metabolites in the context of acute or chronic kidney disease [44].

Altogether, the evidence so far suggests that MTX is a reasonable initial option when rapid symptom control is not imperative, while a brief prednisone bridge may be considered when prompt relief is needed [45]. Given the historically low-quality evidence supporting traditionally steroid-first treatment and the SARCORT finding that 20 mg/day performs similarly to 40 mg/day without a clear safety advantage, a final editorial concludes that MTX can reasonably be started as initial therapy, reserving a brief prednisone bridge when rapid symptom relief is required within shared decision-making [45].

Azathioprine, Leflunomide, and Mycophenolate Mofetil

Azathioprine (AZA) was compared with MTX in a case–control study of 200 patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis treated at two centers, a Belgian clinic using azathioprine and a Dutch clinic using MTX. Both drugs produced similar improvements in pulmonary function and allowed corticosteroid tapering, but adverse effects were more frequent in the AZA-treated group who had a higher infection rate [19].

Leflunomide (LEF) was first developed as a less toxic alternative to MTX for treating patients with rheumatoid arthritis [46]. In a retrospective review of 76 patients with pulmonary and extrapulmonary sarcoidosis it was found that LEF, mostly commonly at 20 mg/day, was associated with improved pulmonary function increasing mean FVC from – 0.1 L to + 0.09 L over 6 months (p = 0.001). It also exerted a strong steroid-sparing effect, with the median prednisone dose decreasing from 10 to 0 mg at 6 months (p < 0.001). Across extrapulmonary targets, 83% achieved complete or partial responses. The findings support LEF as monotherapy or complementary add-on therapy in sarcoidosis; however, close monitoring for adverse events (like gastrointestinal, hepatic, neuropathic or infections) is needed since they occurred in 34% of patients, leading to discontinuation in 17% [20].

Furthermore, a retrospective study evaluated mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) in 37 patients who either had failed or were intolerant to immunosuppressive therapy and found no statistically significant changes in pulmonary function tests before and after MMF therapy. Nevertheless, MMF demonstrated significant steroid-sparing efficacy, enabling a reduction in mean corticosteroid dose (p = 0.004) and allowing 38.1% of patients to discontinue prednisone by 12 months. With a manageable adverse event profile (21.1%) and a low discontinuation rate (8%), MMF appears to be a useful alternative for patients unable to tolerate first-line agents such as methotrexate [21]. However, like AZA, MMF is associated with a higher risk of serious infections [47].

As shown above, some retrospective studies have supported the use of AZA, LEF, and MMF as potential steroid-sparing options. These agents may offer efficacy comparable to MTX; however, definitive comparisons of their relative benefits and risks are limited by the lack of prospective data. Moreover, according to the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework, the quality of evidence for these therapies remains low, and in some cases very low [48].

Anti-TNFα

Infliximab and adalimumab are recommended for patients not responsive to prednisone, for those who have an adverse reaction to prednisone, or to facilitate use of lower prednisone doses [1, 5].

In a key clinical trial, infliximab was associated with a statistically significant improvement in percent-predicted FVC at 24 weeks, although no benefit was observed in patient-centered outcomes such as quality of life, exercise capacity, or dyspnea in the overall population, leaving the clinical relevance of the FVC gain uncertain. Subgroup analysis revealed that patients with more severe pulmonary disease (FVC < 70% predicted) experienced greater benefits [9, 49].

In the case of adalimumab, in an 11-patient study, subjects demonstrated high treatment success rates of 82% at week 24 and 80% at week 52 (n = 10). Respiratory function was well preserved, as FVC stabilized or improved in all patients, alongside significant improvements in physician and patient global assessments. Reductions in dyspnea and improvements in walking distance were also frequently observed. The safety profile was favorable, with no severe adverse events reported [22, 50]. Furthermore, another study showed that adalimumab can reduce sarcoidosis disease activity, as assessed by fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG PET) with decreased uptake in nine of ten patients [50].

Despite demonstrating efficacy, anti-TNFα therapies carry important risks requiring careful consideration. One of the main risks is increased susceptibility to infections, particularly tuberculosis and other opportunistic infections [51]. Screening for latent tuberculosis before initiating anti-TNFα therapy and prophylaxis with isoniazid in patients with positive tests is recommended [52]. In addition, TNF inhibitors are paradoxically associated with sarcoidosis-like reactions, which are uncommon (0.04% of cases) and most commonly reported with etanercept (59%); thoracic disease often manifests as lymphadenopathy or parenchymal involvement, presenting from 1 to 84 months after anti-TNFα initiation and improving after stopping the treatment, with or without corticosteroids [53, 54].

It should also be highlighted that other studies found no benefit. In a randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluating both golimumab (anti-TNFα) and the IL-12/23 inhibitor ustekinumab, neither biologic agent achieved a statistically significant improvement in pulmonary function at 16–28 weeks. Golimumab was associated with minor, non-significant gains in cutaneous disease scores, extrapulmonary disease, and corticosteroid dose reduction at weeks 20, 24, and 28 [11]. Nevertheless, the interpretation of these negative results requires careful consideration of the trial’s design limitations. As highlighted in an accompanying editorial, enrolling patients already stable on maintenance corticosteroids precluded detecting any add-on benefit, since granulomatous inflammation was already effectively suppressed [55]. This is further supported by a retrospective analysis of the original infliximab trial demonstrating that patients receiving ≥ 15–20 mg/day of prednisone showed no significant FVC improvement with infliximab, suggesting that corticosteroids at these doses may suppress TNFα sufficiently to negate any additional anti-TNF effect [56]. A steroid-tapering design from the outset of the intervention would better reflect clinical practice and improve sensitivity to detect steroid-sparing efficacy [55].

Rituximab

In the only prospective trial evaluating rituximab for pulmonary sarcoidosis, Sweiss et al. [10] conducted an open-label, phase I/II study of 10 patients with refractory disease who received 1 g intravenous rituximab at baseline and week 2. At 24 weeks, 5 of 10 patients (50%) achieved greater than 5% absolute improvement in percent-predicted FVC, and 7 of 10 (70%) were overall responders based on improvement in either FVC or 6-min walk distance. However, efficacy diminished substantially over time, with only 2 of 10 patients (20%) maintaining FVC improvement at 52 weeks. Notably, two patients died from respiratory failure attributed to disease progression during the study period [10].

Additional evidence from case series suggests adequate tolerance but inconsistent efficacy with different dosing regimens. Cinetto et al. reported on three patients with refractory sarcoidosis treated with rituximab at a single Italian center; one patient with primary pulmonary disease achieved disease stabilization and complete steroid withdrawal using a lymphoma-style protocol (375 mg/m2 every 2 weeks for 6 doses) with sustained quiescence at 18-month follow-up; another patient achieved complete corticosteroid withdrawal but experienced relapse requiring re-treatment, and a third patient with severe ocular disease showed no meaningful response [57].

Beyond pulmonary disease, case series in neurosarcoidosis suggest a potential role for rituximab as salvage or fourth-line therapy. A multicenter retrospective study of 19 patients with central nervous system sarcoidosis found rituximab failed in most cases (14/19, 73.7%), though a subset with multiple cranial neuropathies responded favorably; early use is discouraged given the high failure rate in first- and second-line settings [58].

Given the limited and heterogeneous evidence, rituximab remains a third- or fourth-line therapy for corticosteroid- and TNFα inhibitor-refractory disease, and RCTs are needed to determine optimal dosing and to identify predictors of response [5].

Emerging Therapies for Pulmonary Sarcoidosis

Efzofitimod

Efzofitimod is a novel immunomodulatory agent that selectively targets neuropilin-2, a receptor upregulated on immune cells during pulmonary inflammation, and has shown relevant immunomodulatory effects in sarcoidosis by suppressing key pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic cytokines in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients and by inhibiting granuloma formation in both murine models and in vitro systems [59–61].

In a randomized, placebo-controlled phase 1b/2a trial, monthly intravenous efzofitimod (1, 3, or 5 mg/kg) was evaluated in 37 patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis receiving a protocolized prednisone taper up to 5 mg/day by week 8. Efzofitimod was generally well tolerated at all doses, and treatment discontinuation due to adverse events was uncommon. At 24 weeks, efzofitimod was associated with a dose-dependent steroid-sparing effect, most pronounced with the 5 mg/kg dose, along with dose-dependent nonsignificant improvements in percent-predicted FVC and percent-predicted DLCO (diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide). Significant improvements at week 24 in patient-reported outcomes, such as Sarcoidosis Assessment Tool (SAT; p = 0.018), King’s Sarcoidosis Questionnaire (KSQ; p = 0.022), general health (GH; p = 0.008) and Fatigue Assessment Scale (FAS; p = 0.010) in the 5 mg/kg group were also reported [14].

The topline results of the EFZO-FIT trial (NCT05415137), a phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study evaluating the efficacy and safety of efzofitimod, missed the primary endpoint: a statistically significant reduction in the mean daily oral corticosteroid at week 48. Investigators attributed this result in part to a higher-than-expected rate of steroid reduction within the placebo arm [15]. The failure of EFZO-FIT to meet its primary endpoint—driven in part by an unexpectedly high rate of steroid reduction in the placebo arm—provides further evidence of the methodological challenges discussed above, where steroid tapering as a primary endpoint risks obscuring true treatment effects [55, 56].

Despite missing the primary objective, the trial demonstrated signals of clinical activity at the 5 mg/kg dose. Significant improvements were reported in patient-reported outcomes, specifically the King’s Sarcoidosis Questionnaire (KSQ)-Lung score (p = 0.0479), and a higher proportion of patients achieved complete steroid withdrawal with concomitant clinical improvement compared to placebo (29.5% vs 14.4%; p = 0.0199) [15].

Namilumab

Namilumab is a human monoclonal antibody that binds to the granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) neutralizing its function. GM-CSF has been suggested as a therapeutic target since it stimulates an exaggerated inflammatory response in alveolar macrophages in individuals with sarcoidosis, producing markedly higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, specifically TNFα and IL-1β [62–65].

A large multinational, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial (RESOLVE-Lung, NCT05314517) assessed the efficacy and safety of namilumab in subjects with chronic active pulmonary sarcoidosis; its primary endpoint was the proportion of patients with one or more clinically significant worsening event requiring rescue therapy during the 26-week double-blind period [17]. The trial was stopped in December 2024 because it failed to meet the primary endpoint and missed key secondary endpoints (including change in percent-predicted FVC, success in corticosteroid tapering, and change in the patient-reported KSQ score) [66].

These findings should nonetheless be interpreted with caution, as they derive from publicly available topline communications rather than from peer-reviewed publication of the complete dataset. Furthermore, additional well-designed clinical trials will be needed to fully elucidate the role of GM-CSF inhibition in pulmonary sarcoidosis, and a definitive assessment will only be possible once these results are published in full.

Interleukin Inhibitors

IL-6 is elevated in the blood and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) of patients with sarcoidosis [67]. It plays a role in sarcoidosis pathogenesis and granuloma formation that is still being described and is partially understood [68–71]. This multifunctional inflammatory cytokine is found at high levels in sarcoidosis tissues, making IL-6 blockade an attractive potential treatment for the disease [67, 71, 72].

A case series of four patients with chronic, refractory sarcoidosis treated with tocilizumab, an IL-6 receptor inhibitor, demonstrated positive outcomes after multiple steroid-sparing therapies, including anti-TNF agents, had failed. All four patients showed significant symptom improvement and corticosteroid dose reductions ranging from 33% to 66%. While the results are promising, particularly for patients with pulmonary, cutaneous, and systemic manifestations, these preliminary observations are hypothesis-generating at best; with only four patients and no control arm, no conclusions regarding efficacy can be drawn [23].

By contrast, a phase II, double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized withdrawal trial evaluated sarilumab, another IL-6 receptor inhibitor, in 15 subjects with glucocorticoid-dependent sarcoidosis. During the 16-week open-label period, 4 of 15 subjects (26.7%) discontinued treatment because of worsening sarcoidosis, and chest computed tomography (CT) findings worsened in 5 subjects (35.7%), with 80% of patients with symptomatic pulmonary disease (4 out of 5) experiencing deterioration. The randomized withdrawal phase was limited by small numbers and group imbalance, precluding meaningful conclusions [24]. This evidence so far suggests there is a need for more studies regarding the true efficacy of IL-6 receptor inhibitors (tocilizumab and sarilumab) to fully support its use [23, 24].

Canakinumab, a fully human nonchimeric IgG monoclonal antibody directed against IL-1β, showed negative results in pulmonary sarcoidosis (unpublished data, available on ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT02888080) [73]. CMK389, another fully human IgG1 monoclonal antibody but in this case directed against IL-18, also showed negative results in pulmonary sarcoidosis (unpublished data, available on ClinicalTrials.gov; NCT04064242) [74].

Janus Kinase Inhibitors

A wide range of cytokines involved in the pathogenesis of sarcoidosis, specifically those associated with type 1 (IFNγ, IL-2), type 2 (IL-4, IL-13), and type 3 (IL-23) immune responses, exert their effects through the Janus kinase–signal transducer and activator of transcription pathway (JAK-STAT) [68, 75, 76].

Based on this rationale, an open-label trial of tofacitinib, a JAK1/3 inhibitor, in 10 patients with long-standing sarcoidosis (mean duration 13.2 years) demonstrated promising results for pulmonary involvement. Of eight patients with active pulmonary disease assessed by FDG-PET, five showed a reduction in total lesion glycolysis (TLG) of 50% or greater, with three achieving complete or near-complete resolution (> 98% reduction). Improvement was observed in both lymphadenopathy and parenchymal lung involvement. Molecular analyses identified IFNγ produced by CD4+ T cells as the central driver of macrophage activation and granuloma formation, with additional contributions from IL-6, IL-12, IL-15, and GM-CSF. Clinical improvement correlated with suppression of IFNγ activity, and patients with incomplete responses showed persistent IFNγ, suggesting that higher doses may be required in some cases. These findings support JAK inhibition as a promising therapeutic approach for sarcoidosis through simultaneous inhibition of multiple type 1 cytokines, contrasting with the limited efficacy seen with isolated targeting of TNF or IL-12/IL-23 in previous trials [77].

In another prospective, open-label, proof-of-concept study of tofacitinib as a steroid-sparing therapy in corticosteroid-dependent pulmonary sarcoidosis, three of five patients met the primary endpoint, and all three successfully discontinued prednisone completely during the 1-year extension period. One case was withdrawn because of a neurologic sarcoidosis flare, although pulmonary disease remained well controlled in this patient [12].

A few cases published have reported the efficacy of JAK inhibitors in pulmonary sarcoidosis [13, 25, 78].

Taken together, the evidence for IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-18 inhibition in sarcoidosis remains insufficient to support clinical use: available data are limited to very small, uncontrolled series or single trials with methodological constraints, and negative results from phase II studies of sarilumab, canakinumab, and CMK389 further underscore that these targets require rigorous prospective evaluation before any clinical recommendation can be made [23, 24, 73, 74].

mTOR Inhibitors

Evidence for mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors in pulmonary sarcoidosis remains limited to case reports and small prospective studies. In a case report, a corticosteroid-dependent patient unable to taper below prednisone 15 mg/day achieved complete resolution of pulmonary nodules on chest CT after 10 months of sirolimus 2 mg daily [79].

In a separate case of severe multi-refractory polyvisceral sarcoidosis, sirolimus 2–4 mg daily produced a 10% absolute gain in FVC, 330-m improvement in 6-min walk distance, and successful weaning from supplemental oxygen after 7 months [80].

In the only prospective trial to date, Redl et al. [26] conducted a single-center randomized trial of 16 patients with glucocorticoid-refractory cutaneous sarcoidosis and demonstrated a 70% response rate with systemic sirolimus 2 mg daily, with effects sustained more than 2 years after drug withdrawal.

Antifibrotic Agents

The clinical evidence for nintedanib in fibrotic pulmonary sarcoidosis stems almost entirely from the INBUILD trial, a phase 3 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study of 663 patients with progressive fibrosing interstitial lung diseases. While the overall trial demonstrated nintedanib 150 mg twice daily significantly reduced the annual rate of FVC decline compared to placebo (− 80.8 mL/year vs − 187.8 mL/year; difference 107.0 mL/year, 95% CI 65.4–148.5, p < 0.001), only 12 patients with sarcoidosis were enrolled within the “other ILDs” category [27].

The diagnosis-specific subgroup analysis revealed that patients with sarcoidosis showed a mean FVC decline difference of only − 20.5 mL/year (95% CI − 337.1 to 296.1), which was not statistically significant [81, 82]. The extremely wide confidence interval reflects the severely underpowered nature of this subgroup.

Beyond the INBUILD trial, evidence remains anecdotal. A dedicated randomized trial of nintedanib in fibrotic sarcoidosis (NINSARC, NCT06479603) is currently recruiting in India [83].

On the other hand, the only prospective clinical trial evaluating pirfenidone specifically in pulmonary sarcoidosis was the PIRFS study (Pirfenidone for Progressive Fibrotic Sarcoidosis), a multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled feasibility trial that enrolled only 16 patients (11 pirfenidone, 5 placebo) before early termination due to the COVID-19 pandemic and regulatory challenges [28].

The primary endpoint was time to clinical worsening event (CWE), defined as death, lung transplant, or > 10% absolute decline in FVC. Four patients experienced CWEs—critically, all were in the pirfenidone group (one death, one lung transplant, two with > 10% FVC decline), though no significant difference in time to CWE was detected (log-rank chi-square = 1.469, p > 0.05). The study’s most important finding was prognostic rather than therapeutic: baseline DLCO < 30% predicted was associated with a 67% CWE rate (4/6 patients) compared to 0% (0/9) among those with DLCO > 30% (p < 0.02). No serious adverse events were recorded in either group, and no significant differences emerged in health-related quality of life measures or FVC changes between arms. The originally planned enrollment of 60 patients would have provided adequate power; with only 16 enrolled, no efficacy conclusions could be drawn [28].

Lessons from Failed Trials and the Path Toward Optimal Study Design

A critical reflection on the sarcoidosis trial landscape reveals several recurring methodological pitfalls that likely explain many negative or inconclusive results, independent of the true biological activity of the agents tested. First, as discussed throughout this review, enrolling patients already stable on maintenance corticosteroids as an inclusion criterion—and using steroid reduction as the primary endpoint—creates an inherently insensitive design: inflammation is already suppressed, the placebo arm has no biological reason to deteriorate, and any steroid-sparing signal is systematically diluted [55, 56]. Second, percent-predicted FVC as a single spirometric endpoint fails to capture the full phenotypic heterogeneity of pulmonary sarcoidosis, where obstructive impairment and disproportionate DLCO reduction are common, reducing statistical power in trials that do not account for these subgroups [55, 84]. Third, most non-corticosteroid trials have been underpowered as a result of small sample sizes and short follow-up periods insufficient to detect the slow, relapsing–remitting progression of granulomatous disease [85]. Fourth, the lack of validated biomarkers and accepted surrogate endpoints forces trials to rely on functional measures that are relatively insensitive to early or modest treatment effects, a gap that FDG-PET and emerging molecular signatures may eventually help to address [86, 87]. Fifth, sarcoidosis phenotypic heterogeneity—ranging from self-limiting Löfgren syndrome to chronic multiorgan fibrotic disease—means that unselected trial populations are likely to include patients with divergent prognoses and treatment needs, diluting the signal for any given intervention [85, 88, 89].

An optimal sarcoidosis trial should therefore (1) enroll patients with active, inadequately controlled disease rather than those stable on maintenance therapy; (2) incorporate a mandated corticosteroid taper from the outset of the intervention, with follow-up long enough (≥ 6 months post-taper) to allow placebo-arm deterioration to become evident; (3) use composite or multi-domain endpoints that capture lung function, patient-reported outcomes, and steroid exposure simultaneously, as recently proposed by international Delphi consensus [87]; (4) enrich trial populations by phenotype using clinical, imaging, and molecular criteria; and (5) incorporate biomarkers of disease activity—such as FDG-PET SUVmax, soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R), or transcriptomic signatures—as exploratory endpoints to enable hypothesis generation for future precision medicine approaches.

New Challenges and a Vision of the Future

There are currently ongoing clinical trials in pulmonary sarcoidosis with no published results to date; among the most relevant are (1) a phase 2, placebo-controlled trial of OATD-01 (KITE; NCT06205121) [90], an oral chitinase-1 (CHIT1) inhibitor intended to reduce granulomatous inflammation and thereby modify active pulmonary sarcoidosis evaluated by PET/CT metabolic response and lung function; (2) a phase 1b/2 trial of a chimeric anti-TNFα monoclonal antibody, XTMAB-16 (NCT05890729) aimed at steroid-sparing control and steroid tapering; (3) hydroxychloroquine plus low-dose prednisone vs medium-dose prednisone (QUIDOSE; NCT05247554) [91], a phase 3 noninferiority trial supporting hydroxychloroquine’s immunomodulatory effects to achieve comparable FVC improvement with fewer steroid-related toxicities. In spite of these advances and the promise of therapeutic benefit, significant challenges remain. The heterogeneous nature of sarcoidosis complicates clinical trial design and interpretation, and there continues to be a lack of consensus on optimal outcome measures, treatment duration, and criteria for treatment success or failure.

Despite years of continued efforts to strengthen the evidence, many important clinical questions remain unanswered, including the comparative effectiveness of different treatment strategies, the role of early aggressive intervention, and the optimal management of fibrotic disease. Addressing these knowledge gaps will require sustained commitment to clinical research, international collaboration, and investment in the infrastructure needed to conduct high-quality trials in this relatively rare disease.

While sarcoidosis treatment has improved over time, the lack of a comprehensive understanding of the disease constrains precise therapeutics. This is largely due to its etiological heterogeneity, as the precise causes and underlying pathogenic mechanisms remain unclear [68, 92–94]. Serologic markers, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme and sIL-2R, as well as imaging modalities including FDG-PET, are employed in clinical practice to assess disease activity; however, none have been formally validated biomarkers or accepted as regulatory endpoints in clinical trials [86].

Recent research has identified distinct clinical phenotypes based on patterns of organ involvement, disease trajectory, and molecular signatures, with important implications for treatment selection and prognosis [88, 89, 95]. For example, the “acute” phenotype characterized by the Löfgren syndrome (bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, erythema nodosum, and arthralgias) has an excellent prognosis with high rates of spontaneous resolution and rarely requires treatment beyond nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory agents [96]. In contrast, the “chronic” multiorgan phenotype with cardiac or neurological involvement display and altered regulatory T cell phenotype characterized by apoptosis-related markers and impaired suppressive function which typically requires aggressive and prolonged immunosuppressive therapy [97–99]. On the same page, chest imaging findings may provide insight into underlying immunological and molecular disease patterns. Patients with sarcoidosis and thoracic lymphadenopathy exhibit a distinct immunological profile characterized by increased circulating T regulatory (Treg) cells with paradoxically reduced suppressive function. Additionally, lymph node enlargement was associated with elevated levels of several inflammatory markers, including TNFα, sIL-2R, TNF receptors I and II, and the proliferation marker Ki67 [100]. Thus, leveraging individual genetic backgrounds alongside clinical and radiological features with omics data may facilitate more precise, patient-centered therapeutic strategies [101].

Conclusions

The landscape of clinical trials for pulmonary sarcoidosis has undergone remarkable transformation in recent years, evolving from an evidence-sparse field reliant on expert opinion to an increasingly rigorous discipline with a growing body of RCT data. Landmark studies including SARCORT and PREDMETH have provided critical evidence informing optimal corticosteroid dosing and the role of MTX as a steroid-sparing agent, fundamentally altering treatment guidelines and clinical practice. Simultaneously, investigation of novel therapeutic approaches such as TNFα inhibitors, JAK inhibitors, antifibrotic agents, and emerging biologics has expanded treatment options for patients with refractory disease and holds promise for addressing unmet needs in sarcoidosis.

Looking to the future, the convergence of precision medicine, validated biomarkers, and an expanding therapeutic arsenal offers unprecedented opportunities to transform sarcoidosis care from symptomatic management to disease modification and truly personalized treatment. This will require continued research, international collaboration, and sustained commitment to patient-centered outcomes that integrate both quality of life and clinical metrics. Together, current advances and ongoing research offer hope for improved treatment options for the millions of individuals living with sarcoidosis worldwide.
