Treatment of a case of bilateral long-standing temporomandibular joint dislocation indirectly associated with COVID-19 using eminectomy and mandibular traction
Authors: Tomofumi Naruse, Hiromasa Fukushima, Keisuke Omori, Taro Miyoshi, Ayaka Takemura, Tomohiro Yamada
Published: 2026

Introduction

Following the coronavirus disease-2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, recommendations to wear face masks have proven beneficial in limiting the spread to both patients and medical staff[1,2]. However, detecting oral diseases can be challenging when the lower part of the face is covered with a mask.

Temporomandibular joint (TMJ) dislocation, which results in a locked position of the condyle anterior to the articular eminence, is prevalent in individuals ranging in age from adolescents to the elderly. Acute or habitual TMJ dislocation is relatively easy to reduce manually, whereas long-term TMJ dislocation is relatively rare and is defined as more than 2–3 weeks of TMJ dislocation. Manual reduction is challenging in long-term TMJ dislocation, often warranting surgery[3]. Lennox-Gastaut syndrome is a form of severe childhood epilepsy in which children develop cognitive impairment, leading to intellectual disabilities[5].

Here, we report a case of long-standing TMJ dislocation in a patient with Lennox-Gastaut syndrome and intellectual disability. Due to the patient’s impaired communication abilities and the use of a face mask, the difficulty in closing his mouth went unnoticed. This case report has been reported in line with SCARE checklist[6].

Presentation of case

A 41-year-old man with a chief complaint of difficulty closing his mouth was referred to our hospital in September 2021 His medical history included Lennox-Gastaut syndrome (nonconvulsive status epilepticus and intellectual disability), pseudo-bowel obstruction, and habitual TMJ dislocation. He had undergone corpus callosotomy and colostomy at our hospital 8 months previously. At that time, the patient visited our department for oral management, and panoramic radiography revealed no evidence of TMJ dislocation. He was transferred from our hospital to another hospital for rehabilitation 6 months previously. When he was discharged from another hospital, his family noticed that he had difficulty in closing his mouth. While he was in another hospital, neither his family nor the medical staff noticed his difficulty in closing his mouth due to the absence of visits and the requirement to wear a mask due to the COVID-19 pandemic. The exact period of TMJ dislocation was unknown, but the total energy intake decreased owing to the TMJ dislocation, warranting the use of a nasogastric tube.

A general examination revealed the following: performance status, 1; height, 165 cm; and body weight, 41 kg. Although the patient responded to our questions, he faced challenges communicating with us. Facial examination revealed concavity in the bilateral TMJ regions upon palpation of the mandibular head in the anterior region. Oral examination revealed poor oral hygiene and early contact of the last molar with an anterior open bite (Fig. 1). Panoramic radiography revealed bilateral dislocations of the mandibular condyles (Fig. 2). Three-dimensional contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed that the bilateral condyles locked anterior to eminence (Fig. 3A,B). T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging revealed that the bilateral condyles exceeded the articular disc, and a high-intensity area was observed in the mandibular fossa (Fig. 4A,B). Based on the clinical and imaging findings, we arrived at an initial diagnosis of bilateral long-standing TMJ dislocation. As manual reduction at the outpatient clinic was unsuccessful, the patient underwent bilateral eminectomy under general anesthesia. Manual reduction following general anesthesia including muscle relaxants was attempted, but this approach was unsuccessful. An Al-Kayat-Bramley incision, bilateral eminectomy was performed; joint lavage was performed for draining the accumulated synovial fluid in the glenoid cavity. Mouth closure and bilateral condylar reduction were satisfactory. Condylectomy was not performed. Postoperatively, intermaxillary fixation (IMF) or traction was performed using IMF screws and elastic bands to maintain the normal occlusion and prevent the condyle from moving backward. On postoperative 3 days, oral food intake and rehabilitation for mouth opening and closing were initiated. Four weeks postoperatively, the IMF screw and elastics were removed. Good mandibular function was achieved, and the patient was discharged. No evidence of TMJ dislocation recurrence was observed, and oral function was maintained for 3 years after surgery. Three-dimensional CT revealed repositioning of the bilateral condyles in the mandibular fossa (Fig. 5A,B). Finally, individual normal occlusion was constructed by partial denture. Discussion

The case report describes the successful surgical treatment and mandibular traction for long-standing TMJ dislocation in a patient with intellectual disability, along with a literature review of surgical treatment.

Treatment of long-standing TMJ dislocations has been classified as either nonsurgical or surgical. Nonsurgical options include manual reduction with or without anesthesia or mandibular traction[7,8]. Typically, manual reduction is first attempted in outpatient settings at any hospital. However, surgery is often considered if manual reduction is difficult owing to factors such as fibrosis and scarring in the mandibular fossa. Although mandibular traction is recommended prior to performing surgical intervention[9], surgery is often selected because early reduction is required for long-standing TMJ dislocation. Among the surgical options, Akinbami et al reported temporalis myotomy or high condylectomy to reposition the condyle in the fossa, low condylectomy, gap arthroplasty to correct fusion and restore the joint, horizontal subsigmoid osteotomy, vertical/oblique osteotomy, inverted L-shaped osteotomy, bilateral sagittal split osteotomy; and midline mandibulotomy for maintaining the new joint relation and correct occlusion[7]. Recently, condylectomy via the intraoral approach has been reported[10]. The advantages of this approach are the absence of a facial scar and the reduced possibility of nerve and vascular injury. However, there is a risk of damage to the maxillary artery and the pterygoid plexus. Additionally, there is a risk of persisting anterior open bite[11]. Other studies have shown that eminectomy is effective in treating both habitual and long-standing TMJ dislocation[12,13]. It is a relatively less invasive surgical option, and local anesthesia with intravenous sedation is possible. However, the incidence of facial nerve palsy has been reported to account for 22% of postoperative complications[13]. Furthermore, both techniques have been used for reduction previously[14]. Other studies reported the effectiveness of meniscectomy and lateral pterygoid muscle myotomy[15]. It is currently unclear as to which surgical procedure is the most effective in this situation. Segami et al analyzed the combination of condylectomy, discectomy, and eminectomy and suggested a flowchart for the treatment of long-standing TMJ dislocation. Fibrotic scarring occurs after eminectomy, warranting mandibular fossa curettage. An additional condylectomy, discectomy, or both may be performed based on the situation. The final striping of the pterygoid muscle was blind[16]. Exceptionally, when the mandibular condyle is displaced anteriorly and inferiorly due to trauma and sufficient mobility cannot be achieved even after removal of the scar tissue surrounding the mandibular condyle, intraoral vertical ramus osteotomy is an appropriate treatment option[17]. In our case, eminectomy was initially performed, and condylectomy was considered if reduction was challenging. Eminectomy and postoperative mandibular traction were performed because condylectomy is expected to result in an anterior open bite that could require subsequent IMF for an extended period after surgery. Additionally, the patient had difficulty communicating with us owing to intellectual disability; therefore, appropriate postoperative management was challenging. Fortunately, eminectomy and postoperative mandibular traction resulted in good oral function.

Conclusion

Here, we reported a case of bilateral long-standing TMJ dislocation that went undetected due to the use of a mask to limit the COVID-19 spread. The pandemic has resulted not only in a reduction in the number of medical examinations owing to concerns of spread of infection but also in a delay in the recognition of oral diseases when the lower face was covered by a mask. In addition, a previous report demonstrated that patients with cognitive impairment do not recognize TMJ dislocations[4]. By summer 2025, the spread of the COVID-19 had resumed, wearing a mask is still mandatory in some hospitals and care must be taken to avoid overlooking oral diseases. No evidence of TMJ dislocation recurrence was observed 3 years after surgery.
