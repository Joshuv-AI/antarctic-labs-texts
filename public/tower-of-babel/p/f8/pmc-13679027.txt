A 10-year follow-up survey study: burnout in U.S. pharmacy practice faculty, where do we stand?
Authors: Shareen Y El-Ibiary, Kelly C Lee, Tram B Cat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn 2014, the authors of this study reported burnout rates among pharmacy practice faculty. Ten years later, following the COVID-19 pandemic along with changes to pharmacy student enrollment and demographics, this study aimed to compare burnout rates among pharmacy practice faculty reported in 2014 with 2025. Demographics, participation in workplace well-being programs, and intention to leave the profession were also reported in this study.MethodsAn electronic cross-sectional survey assessed burnout and demographics among pharmacy practice faculty from United States colleges/schools of pharmacy. Burnout was measured using the Maslach Burnout Inventory - Educators Survey (MBI-ES) which has been deemed the gold standard for measuring burnout. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and Pearson/Spearman correlations were used to identify predictive characteristics of burnout.ResultsOf the 2,505 pharmacy practice faculty invited to participate in the study, 327 responded (13%). The MBI-ES scores for emotional exhaustion, depersonalization and personal accomplishment in 2025 compared with 2014 were significantly worse. Faculty aged 31–40 years had the highest emotional exhaustion and depersonalization scores. Lack of hobby was predictive of low personal accomplishment. Of the respondents, 73% reported that they have access to a well-being program at their university. Those who reported that well-being resources were not helpful had worse scores in all three burnout domains. Over 16% of respondents reported that they would likely leave academic pharmacy within the next 3 years.ConclusionsBurnout is higher among pharmacy practice faculty compared to the 2014 report. Access to well-being programs is available for most faculty; their reported usefulness, however, was mixed. Burnout could be contributing to faculty departing academic pharmacy within the next 3 years. Pharmacy academic institutions must continue to work towards mediating burnout among pharmacy practice faculty to enhance well-being and retention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09849-6.Keywords: Burnout, Pharmacy, Faculty

Background

In 2014, we conducted a burnout assessment of pharmacy practice faculty in pharmacy colleges in the United States [1]. Results from our 2014 study showed that 41% of pharmacy practice faculty were experiencing emotional exhaustion [1]. Those most at risk for burnout included women, assistant professor rank, faculty with young children, and those without a mentor or hobby. A little over 10 years later, with more awareness of burnout and after facing a pandemic, pharmacy student enrollment concerns and faculty retention issues, we aim to measure the current status of burnout among pharmacy practice faculty.

Burnout is described as syndrome that results in emotional exhaustion, cynicism, and low perception of personal accomplishment in response to unrelenting occupational stress [2]. It may affect mental and physical health and impair functioning at work. Maslach et al. developed the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) that assesses the three dimensions of burnout (emotional exhaustion, cynicism, low personal accomplishment) and is considered the standard for measuring burnout. Several versions of the MBI exist including one for educators (MBI-ES; MBI-Educator Survey) [3]. The MBI-ES contains nine items to measure emotional exhaustion, five items to measure depersonalization, and eight items to measure personal accomplishment. A high sub-score in emotional exhaustion or depersonalization is considered indicative of clinically significant burnout [3].

Studies have shown that academic pharmacy is facing a manpower crisis [4, 5]. With decreasing student enrollment and workforce turnover and reduction, workloads for pharmacy practice faculty may be increasing. It is likely that burnout or the dimensions of burnout can lead to decreased faculty satisfaction and decreased retention. Job satisfaction and retention are critical to maintaining and enhancing work productivity, improving professional recognition, and promoting pharmacy education [4, 6]. Development, recruitment, and retention of highly qualified and diverse faculty have been identified as priorities, goals and objectives by the American Association of Colleges of Pharmacy (AACP) [7]. In a recent published study by Eiland et al., 22% of faculty reported intending to leave their current position within the next year citing reasons such as unmanageable workload and inadequate work life balance [5]. There have been urgent calls to improve and sustain well-being among faculty and students to help promote retention. This has been also been highlighted in the ACPE Standards 2025 calling for the need to provide access to faculty well-being programs [8].

Since 2014, studies regarding assessment of burnout in pharmacy faculty along with those evaluating pharmacy faculty retention and satisfaction have been published [1, 9–11]. The 2023 American Society of Health-System Pharmacists (ASHP) Section of Pharmacy Educators published a five-year outlook citing predictions of pharmacy workforce and pharmacy academia, known as the Crystal Ball Project [5]. The report forecasted that at least 20% of pharmacy faculty were likely or very likely to leave pharmacy academia in the next five years [5]. An earlier study by Carter et al.10 surveyed Basic Science (including Medicinal Chemistry, Pharmaceutics, Pharmacology/Toxicology) and Pharmacy Practice faculty across the country designed to assess turnover percentages in colleges of pharmacy during a 5-year period. Faculty representing over 80 colleges of pharmacy responded to the survey. Turnover was higher in Pharmacy Practice departments compared to Basic Science departments (10.6% vs. 6%, p < .001) and among female faculty regardless of department (13.2% vs. 8.7%, p < .001). The authors theorized that lack of job satisfaction, job stress from high workload, promotion and/or tenure expectations, pressure to obtain funding for research, lack of sufficient mentoring, and/or conflicting commitments with home and work as contributing factors for high turnover rates. In 2021, Attarabeen et al. found that faculty in pharmacy practice departments had more burnout and stress versus other departments [11].

Since limited evidence exists for the longitudinal assessment of burnout among pharmacy practice faculty using the MBI, we aimed to replicate the study and compare findings from 2014. In this study, we compared the prevalence of burnout among pharmacy faculty members to that measured in our 2014 study. We also evaluated pharmacy practice faculty intentions to remain in academia and in the pharmacy profession within one and three years. Generating this information may lead to strategies that can help reduce or prevent burnout within this population which ultimately may influence faculty retention and satisfaction.

Methods

In order to compare data from our 2014 study of burnout among Assistant, Associate, and Full professors from colleges/schools of pharmacy throughout the United States, we used a cross-sectional survey similar in design with updated demographic questions and items related to workforce retention and well-being programs at institutions. Similar to the 2014 study, burnout was measured using the MBI-ES, which was purchased for distribution from Mindgarden Inc [3]. The MBI-ES instrument remains the gold standard for measuring burnout; while the scoring rubric of subscores remains proprietary to MindGarden, the scale has high validity and reliability among populations studied, including educators [3]. Participants were recruited using the current AACP roster for pharmacy practice faculty. To increase our response rate, incentives for study participation were offered in the form of a drawing for $100 gift cards (six were awarded among respondents). Subjects were emailed information regarding the scope of the research and a link to the survey (see Supplementary File - Survey 1). The survey included the MBI-ES as well as demographic and study-related questions, including assessment of intention to leave academia or the pharmacy profession within the next 1 to 3 years and access to and usefulness of well-being programs. The study was approved by the Midwestern University Institutional Review Board. The investigators received a waiver of written consent from participants for this study. All participants were provided a written information sheet describing the study. By selecting to “I consent” within the coversheet of the survey, the participants were able to participate in the study. Between March 2025 and May 2025, faculty received three follow-up email reminders to complete the survey (separated by 2 weeks each). Investigators administered the survey using the QualtricsXM platform. No personal identifiers were linked to survey results in order to ensure confidentiality. Respondents had the opportunity to provide their email addresses upon completion of the survey to be entered into the gift card drawing. The email addresses were not linked to the survey responses. Data were password-protected and maintained by the investigators. Subjects had the option of withdrawing from participation at any time during the duration of research. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics (percent, mean, standard deviation, median, interquartile range) along with comparative statistics (t-test for comparison of MBI scores between 2014 and 2025, between those who participated in well-being activities at their institution and those with intention to leave academia/profession), and Pearson/Spearman correlations for predictive characteristics of burnout.

Results

Of the 2,505 pharmacy practice faculty invited to participate in the study, 349 (14%) responded to the survey. Upon removal of those who did not fully complete the survey (n = 22), we report the results from 327 respondents (13%).

Most respondents were female (72%), Caucasian/White (81%), and married (78%) (Table 1). There was a fairly even distribution of age groups for those older than 30 years and those who reported having children younger than 18 years old versus those who did not. More respondents did not have an administrative title (63%), worked at public institutions (63%), were in non-tenured, non-tenure track positions (70%), and had a clinical practice (65%).

When asked about the likelihood of leaving academic pharmacy or pharmacy within the next year or next 3 years, there was a positive correlation for all items with emotional exhaustion and depersonalization (Supplemental Table 1). There was a negative correlation with low personal accomplishment for all items. These results suggest that those who are more likely to have symptoms of burnout will leave academic pharmacy or pharmacy in the near future. When the Likert responses were converted into Likely (“Likely” and “Very Likely”) or Unlikely (“Very Unlikely”, “Unlikely”, and “Neutral”), there was a greater number of respondents that stated they were likely to leave academic pharmacy (but stay within the pharmacy profession) within the next 3 years compared to those who stated they were likely to leave academic pharmacy (but stay within the pharmacy profession) within the next year (16.8% vs. 7.0%, respectively) (Supplemental Table 1).

Of the respondents, 17%, 41%, and 73% reported that they have access to a well-being program in their department, college/school of pharmacy, and university, respectively; 11% reported that they do not have access to a well-being program (see Fig. 2). Despite access for majority of respondents, 52% reported that they have not participated in or accessed these resources at the department/college/school or university levels. Of those who responded affirmatively to having participated in or accessed the well-being resources, 76% reported that the resources were helpful (see Fig. 3). Of those who stated that the resources were not helpful, the reasons cited were (1) workload/lack of time to participate, (2) root causes are not addressed or programs lack specificity, and (3) limited availability of resources. When compared across the MBI domains, those who reported that well-being resources were not helpful had higher mean scores [SD] on emotional exhaustion (24.0 [10.8] vs. 35.2 [9.78], p<.001) and depersonalization (6.55 [5.39] vs. 9.59 [5.05], p=.008) and lower mean scores [SD] on personal accomplishment (35.9 [6.82] vs. 32.6 [7.27], p=.025) (see Fig. 3).

Discussion

Faculty demographics

When comparing the 2025 and 2014 survey results, there was a greater percentage of faculty respondents at the associate and professor levels than the assistant level (16% vs. 48.2% at assistant level, 46% vs. 33.9% at associate level, 36.5% vs. 15.7% at professor level), respectively. Unlike the 2024–2025 AACP Profile of Pharmacy Faculty report showing 37% of faculty at the assistant rank level, our 2025 study had a smaller percentage of faculty respondents at the assistant level; a possible explanation for the smaller pool of early-career faculty respondents could either be due to feeling too burned out or lack of interest in completing the survey [12]. Assuming that faculty at the professor level are closer to retirement age and with the shrinking pipeline of early-career faculty to replace those retiring, this could have significant implications on our capacity to fill key academic roles in the future. This highlights the need for proactive succession planning to ensure continuity and workforce stability. Examining potential causes for the small pool of early-career faculty respondents is important to understand how to best address issues related to long-term faculty recruitment and retention [13]. Other potential causes of the shrinking pool of early-career faculty could be a result of decreased hiring of faculty positions due to declining numbers of student pharmacist enrollment as well as the need to generate part of one’s own salary [13, 14]. Unless the underlying root causes are properly addressed, faculty attrition will continue to rise, further intensifying burnout among those who remain.

MBI score comparison

Compared to the 2014 study of pharmacy practice faculty, worsening MBI scores across all three domains were expected and confirmed, especially considering the impact of the pandemic [8]. Exacerbation of the MBI scores may likely have been caused by the continued workload demands of faculty responsibilities (e.g. teaching, service, clinical practice, and research), which have not been clearly and consistently quantified within pharmacy practice faculty.15 In a qualitative study that examined the perceptions of service workload, Lee et al. revealed that a lack of agreement in the definition of service equity between pharmacy administrators and faculty lends itself to unique challenges in quantifying service workload [15]. Unfortunately, when inequitable distribution of service assignments occurs and is not appropriately addressed, faculty satisfaction and ultimately burnout may develop over time [15, 16]. Therefore, transparency, coupled with clear communication and well-defined expectations, play critical roles in promoting faculty satisfaction with equitable distribution of workload [6, 17, 18].

Declining student enrollment

In addition to managing the challenges of faculty workload, external pressures such as declining student enrollment have further compounded the emotional toll. Observed declining student enrollments in pharmacy education could possibly be explained by economic forces of change [19]. During pre-pandemic years, student debt in higher education had been skyrocketing. Perceived value of advanced degrees has been called into question when pharmacy salaries are plateauing and the job market has been shrinking [19]. With the pandemic, a shift in online learning from traditional environments further challenged Doctor of Pharmacy (PharmD) applicants’ return-on-investment for pharmacy education [19, 20]. The perceived value of pharmacy education may further be complicated by the growing expectation for post-graduate training, which extends the duration and cost of a degree marketed as “practice ready.” Consequently, decreasing student enrollment has translated to less tuition revenue, which often covers instructional costs including funding of faculty salaries. Furthermore, with 63% of surveyed faculty working at public institutions and 78% of them being fully funded by their college/school, faculty stress levels would be understandably high due to the reliance on student tuition and the unpredictability of government funding for obtaining grants.

Curriculum transformation effects

Curriculum transformation is another factor that may be influencing pharmacy practice faculty burnout. Over the past several years, many colleges/schools of pharmacy have either undergone or are in the process of undergoing a curriculum transformation [19]. With such an undertaking, time, effort, and resources are required from administrators, faculty, and staff to launch a new curriculum. The additional workload has further intensified faculty’s emotional exhaustion as a new curriculum can often translate into added or new content [19, 21]. As practice evolves due to the growing body of knowledge and skills required of pharmacists, Doctor of Pharmacy accreditation standards are updated to address these contemporary issues. Rather than considering the removal or replacement of certain curricular content, faculty may hold strong emotional attachments to both the material and methods of teaching. Consequently, the reluctance to depart from traditional teaching practices can contribute to teaching overload over time sustaining high workload, which can lead to burnout.

Increased learner needs

In addition, the number of complex learner needs have increased in recent years, thereby adding to the stresses and challenges in teaching and precepting students with higher numbers of those with learning disabilities (LDs) and mental health challenges [22]. The percentage of professional health sciences doctorate students with a disability have increased over the years [23]. Although there has been increased awareness regarding students with LD, training on how to provide reasonable accommodations has been lacking, especially in the experiential setting [24, 25]. Given that 65% of our surveyed faculty have a clinical practice, they are likely encountering challenges in supporting students with LDs in and out of the classroom. It would not be surprising that students with challenges could be an added factor in contributing to signs and symptoms of burnout among pharmacy practice faculty.

Personal and professional life integration

The pandemic served as a wake-up call and prompted individuals to re-evaluate their priorities and focus on what truly matters in their lives [26–29]. Rather than striving to achieve a “work-life balance,” there was a shift in mindset where the goal was to accept and embrace more work-life integration and flexibility became the cornerstone for how personal and professional boundaries are defined [30]. In our 2025 survey, faculty seemed to have scaled back on their workweek with 19% less faculty reported working greater than 50 h per week compared to faculty in the 2014 survey.

In the 2025 survey results, having children (younger than 18 years) had no impact on faculty’s EE compared with the 2014 study which found that EE and DP were worse for faculty who had children specifically aged 1–12. One possible explanation could be that faculty had greater flexibility when working from home, alleviating the stress of balancing a work commute and carpool responsibilities for their children. This is consistent with findings from the Eurofound and the International Labour Office where workers reported positive effects of telework such as reductions in their commute time as well as having greater time autonomy and flexibility, better overall work-life balance, and higher productivity [31]. Interestingly, in a study by Parker et al., parents who teleworked reported higher parental stress compared to parents who worked on-site [32]. Possibly, those with younger children may feel higher stress with telework due to the need to multi-task and fulfill parenting responsibilities. Another possible explanation could be that the stress level is higher for majority of faculty, and there are no differences between demographic groups. Future research should explore whether specific age groups of children serves as a key determinant in influencing parents’ EE.

Access and utility of institutional well-being programs

With increasing burnout awareness, employee well-being programs have become more widely available. When faculty were asked if they had access to well-being programs, individuals that utilized those programs found them to be helpful and had lower scores in emotional exhaustion, depersonalization and higher scores for personal accomplishment (Fig. 3). It is unknown whether burnout symptoms were mitigated as a result of accessing those well-being program. It was reassuring to see that 73% of university programs are offering some type of well-being program, with 41% offered at the College/School level and 57% at the Department level. The attendance and perceived usefulness, however, remains lower than desired. Due to the ACPE Standard 2025 emphasis on faculty and staff well-being, data on availability and utility of institutional well-being programs may become available in the future [8].

Faculty workforce retention

Despite worsening MBI scores compared with 2014, our study did not demonstrate increased intention among pharmacy practice faculty to leave academic pharmacy. Emotional exhaustion, however, was moderately correlated with faculty’s consideration of leaving academic pharmacy (See Supplemental Table 1). This is consistent with the recent National Pharmacist Workforce Survey (NPWS), in which more than 90% of actively practicing pharmacists reported being likely or very likely to be working as a pharmacist in the next year [33]. It should be noted that the NPWS represents only 1.3% of pharmacists working in academia although respondents could have selected hospital/health-system (21.7%) or ambulatory care (5.1%) for their clinical site. Of the NPWS respondents, 26% reported that they were likely or very likely to leave their current job in the next year, which is higher than that reported amongst our respondents. When asked about whether they would leave the pharmacy profession in the next three years, 12.8% reported being likely or very likely with another 11.9% reporting that they would leave the profession to take time off for their health.

Limitations

Our study has limitations. The response rate of our study was lower than the study we conducted in 2014. Given that there has been an increased focus on the topic of burnout across the pharmacy education since our initial publication, faculty may have had less interest in completing another survey on burnout. A reason for a lower response rate could have been that burnout was higher across all faculty groups, affecting their ability to complete the survey. Additionally, our survey participants were limited to those on the AACP listserv. Compared to the AACP 2024-25 Profile of Pharmacy Faculty, we had a higher proportion of full professors and lower proportion of assistant professors, which may reflect differential response rates by career stage. For example, while faculty across all ranks have substantial responsibilities, assistant professors often experience competing demands associated with meeting promotion expectations (e.g., scholarly dissemination, teaching portfolio development), thereby, potentially limiting their availability for survey participation. Response bias may have also been a factor with those who were interested in the issue completing the survey as well as those who did not have burnout and did not complete the survey due to lack of relatability.

Future implications

The results of the study indicate that burnout scores among pharmacy practice faculty have worsened since our last assessment in 2014. Even more concerning is that burnout is now prevalent among all groups, regardless of gender, having children or having a hobby. The COVID-19 pandemic, admissions challenges, student aptitude, faculty attrition, and increased workload may be contributing to these trends. Beneficial well-being programs are offered by many institutions and those who found them helpful had improved MBI scores compared to those who did not. Colleges/schools of Pharmacy should continue to provide well-being programs and further investigate ways to help alleviate burnout by examining promotion criteria, clinical service requirements, and workload. More studies are needed to assess interventions for improving burnout among pharmacy practice faculty.

Conclusions

Burnout continues to exist among pharmacy practice faculty compared with 2014 and has worsened in all three burnout domains. Despite the prevalence of burnout across pharmacy practice academia, it is encouraging to see that most faculty have a well-being program at their institution. Future research is needed to investigate effectiveness of well-being programs and interventions that may help to alleviate burnout among all faculty. Emotional exhaustion was moderately correlated with faculty departures; therefore, effective strategies addressing burnout may help to improve faculty retention.
