Matrix cleanup assisted extraction of phenolic pollutants from diverse wastewaters using a magnetic core shell adsorbent
Authors: Sina Mohammad Mehri, Mir Ali Farajzadeh, Mohammad Reza Afshar Mogaddam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study introduces a novel and environmentally relevant sample preparation strategy for the trace-level determination of hazardous phenolic pollutants in complex wastewater matrices. Unlike conventional extraction methods that merely tolerate matrix interferences, this approach eliminates them before analyte extraction, improving selectivity and reliability. A magnetic core–shell metal–organic framework was employed to selectively adsorb interfering substances from real wastewater samples collected from pharmaceutical, municipal, and petrochemical sources. This material was characterized using scanning electron microscopy, transmission electron microscopy, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, vibrating sample magnetometry, and Brunauer–Emmett–Teller surface analysis. Its magnetic responsiveness allowed for simple and rapid phase separation without the need for centrifugation. Following matrix cleanup, phenolic compounds were derivatized using acetic anhydride in the presence of sodium carbonate and extracted using a vortex-assisted liquid–liquid microextraction technique. The optimized method required minimal amounts of sorbent and organic solvents, improving its environmental sustainability and reducing laboratory waste. Coupled with gas chromatography and flame ionization detection, the method demonstrated excellent analytical performance, with relative standard deviations between 1.0 and 8.3% (for 10 µg L⁻1), recoveries ranging from 62 to 83%, correlation coefficients above 0.9962, and low limits of detection and quantification. Introduction

Phenolic compounds are a major family of pollutants produced from some industrial processes such as pharmaceuticals, petrochemicals, and municipal waste discharges. Their toxic, persistent, and bioaccumulating nature in the aquatic environment has significant implications on human health and the environment overall. Human exposure to phenolic contaminants has shown associations with endocrine disruption, carcinogenicity, and mutagenicity1–5. Therefore, analysis of phenolic contaminants is a key aspect of environmental studies. They can be detected and isolated with a variety of separation and characterization methods, including gas chromatography (GC)6–9, high-performance liquid chromatography10–12, capillary electrochromatography13–17, and capillary electrophoresis18,19. GC is an useful method for phenolic compounds analysis because of their low molecular weight and volatility20. However, broad peaks and poor chromatographic behavior can arise from hydrogen bonding of the hydroxyl (–OH) group of phenolic compounds with the stationary phases of GC column21. Furthermore, phenolic compounds extraction from an aqueous solution is more complicated by its hydrophilic nature22. To address these limitations, phenolic compounds can be derivatized by using a variety of reagents and procedures (alkylation, acylation, silylation, etc.) to prepare their derivatives with low polarity that increase extractability, chromatographic behavior and/or detection sensitivity23–26. Acylation, specifically with acetic anhydride, is the most widely used method. This reaction is usually conducted in the presence of bases such as sodium or potassium carbonate, sodium hydroxide, or phosphate salts, which improve analytes stability and separation efficiency21,27–32. The accuracy of quantifying phenolic compounds in real samples is typically affected by matrix effect, which interfere with the analytical measurements. One possible definition of matrix effect is the influence of coeluting species in complex sample matrices that hinder the analysis because they suppress or enhance the signal from analytes33. Classical extraction methods, such as liquid–liquid extraction and solid phase extraction, are typically used for extracting phenols. However, there are many requirements associated with these methods, including the excessive use of solvents, lengthy time frames, and inefficient removal of the matrix effect26,34–36. Dispersive micro solid-phase extraction (DµSPE) method can be utilized to address these issues. In DµSPE, an adsorbent is dispersed directly into sample solution, allowing it to interact with both the analytes and the interfering matrix components37,38. By modifying certain chemical conditions such as pH, ionic strength, and adsorbent dosage, the adsorbent can adsorb the matrix components or target compounds. This feature of DµSPE makes it a powerful tool for mitigating matrix effect while keeping analytes in solution39. The adsorbent type is one of the significant factors for the success of the DµSPE methods. The main goal of this step is to assist the reduction of matrix effect while keeping the compounds of interest in solution. As a result, a custom adsorbent was needed, which would achieve these goals under optimized conditions. Magnetic core–shell adsorbents have many useful features. The magnetic core would allow easy separation without the need for centrifugation, which remains a barrier with conventional adsorbents40,41. In addition, the outer shell is an adsorbent such as metal–organic framework (MOF). The MOF shell has its advantages, including large surface area, adsorption capacity and porosity, fast adsorption kinetics, reusable, easy disposal, and cost-effectiveness42. One significant benefit of MOF is its adjustable adsorption behavior. By modifying the pH of the sample solution, the interactions between the adsorbent and the sample components can be manipulated39. For example, the ligands such as terephthalic acid on the shell of the MOF can be adjusted to promote the selective adsorption of matrix interferences, while the target compounds remain in solution. The tunable adsorption properties of the MOF are critical for the research goal of minimizing matrix effect, but also maximizing recovery of the analytes. After the DµSPE step, a microextraction method, for example, vortex-assisted liquid–liquid microextraction (VA-LLME) can be used for analytes extraction. In VA-LLME, an extraction solvent is dispersed within the aqueous phase, with the aid of vortex agitation, promoting effective distribution of the analyte in both phases during extraction43–46. Given that VA-LLME offers rapid extraction without complicating the process with a disperser solvent, preservation of volatile analytes, and complete dispersion of the extracting solvent, it is the preferred method over conventional extraction methods47.

In this study, a comprehensive approach was applied in the extraction and quantification of phenolic compounds after eliminating matrix effects in wastewater samples, including pharmaceutical, petrochemical, and municipal wastewaters. First, the DµSPE method using the prepared adsorbent synthesized from an MOF (Co-terphthalic acid that was functionalized on Fe3O4 nanoparticles) was employed to effectively reduce matrix effect while remaining the phenolic compounds in solution. Then, the VA-LLME technique was applied for the extraction of phenols (sodium carbonate was used to alkalize the environment, and acetic anhydride as the derivatizing agent to improve extraction efficiency). Finally, the extracted analyte derivatives were analyzed using a GC-flame ionization detector (FID) to accurately determine their concentrations.

Samples: Real samples were collected from the effluents of municipal wastewater treatment plant, petrochemical wastewater treatment unit, and pharmaceutical wastewater treatment unit (Tabriz, East Azerbaijan Province, Iran). All samples were analyzed without any dilution using the developed method. Prior to extraction, the samples were centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 5 min to remove solid particles.

For optimal contact between analytes, adsorbents, and extraction solvent, a Labinco L46 vortex mixer (Breda, the Netherlands) was utilized.

Microscopy and structural analysis: Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded using a Bruker FTIR spectrometer (Billerica, MA, USA). X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed using a Siemens D500 diffractometer (Karlsruhe, Germany). Morphological and structural characterizations were conducted using a CM120 transmission electron microscope (TEM) (Philips, Leiden, the Netherlands) and a Mira 3 scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Tescan, Brno, Czech Republic).

Surface area and magnetic property analysis: Specific surface area and pore size distribution were determined using Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method with a BELSORP-mini II (Osaka, Japan). The magnetic properties of Fe3O4 nanoparticles and adsorbents were analyzed using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM) (MDKB, Kashan, Iran).

Co-TFA@MAA@Fe3O4 synthesis

The synthesis of the adsorbent was performed in three steps. The first step involved the synthesis of magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles, and the second step included the functionalization of these nanoparticles with MAA. Both steps were carried out according to our previous work39.

Step 1: Synthesis of magnetic Fe₃O₄ core. Magnetic Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles were synthesized via the common co-precipitation method. Briefly, a mixture of FeCl₂·4H₂O and FeCl₃·6H₂O (in a molar ratio of 1:2) was dissolved in deoxygenated water under a nitrogen atmosphere. The precipitation of magnetite was achieved by adding 1 mol L-1 of ammonia solution under vigorous stirring at 50 °C. The resulting black precipitate was isolated by magnetic decantation, washed repeatedly with ethanol and water, and dried under vacuum.

Step 2: Functionalization with MAA. To enhance the stability of the nanoparticles and provide functional groups for the subsequent growth of the MOF shell, the surface of the Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles was modified with MAA. In this step, 0.5 g of the synthesized Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles were dispersed into 100 mL of ethanol, and 2.9 mmol of MAA was added. The mixture was stirred for 24 h at room temperature. During this process, the S atom of MAA form strong coordinative bonds with the Fe atoms on the surface of the magnetite nanoparticles, creating a stable monolayer. The final product, denoted as MAA@Fe₃O₄, was collected using a magnet, washed thoroughly with an ethanol–water (1:1,v/v) mixture to remove any unbound MAA, and dried at 50 °C for 2 h.

Step 3: Synthesis of Co-based MOF shell. The final adsorbent was synthesized by growing a cobalt-terephthalate MOF layer onto the functionalized magnetic cores. First, 2.3 g of Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O was dissolved in 100 mL of ethanol (mixture A). Then, 0.2 g of MAA@Fe₃O₄ was dispersed in mixture A via sonication for 30 min. In a separate container, 1.3 g of terephthalic acid (H₂BDC) was dissolved in 2 mL of concentrated ammonia and then gradually added to 100 mL of ethanol (solution B). The ammonia deprotonates the carboxylic acid groups of H₂BDC, forming terephthalate anions (BDC2⁻), which are essential for coordination with metal ions. Solution B was added dropwisely (1 mL min⁻1) to mixture A under continuous stirring at 40 °C. The Co2⁺ ions coordinatively bind to the oxygen atoms in the carboxylate groups of both the surface-bound MAA and the free BDC2⁻ linkers in solution. This leads to the formation of a crystalline core–shell structure where the MOF shell is firmly anchored to the magnetic core via these coordinative bonds. The reaction proceeded for 2 h after complete addition. The final product was magnetically separated, washed with ethanol–water (1:1 v/v), and dried at 50 °C for 2 h.

The stability of the fabricated core–shell adsorbent is ensured by the nature of the chemical bonds formed during its synthesis. The bond between the Fe₃O₄ core and the MAA modifier is a strong coordinative covalent bond between surface iron atoms and the thiol group, which is stable in a wide range of pH conditions. The subsequent growth of the MOF shell involves the formation of robust coordination bonds between Co2⁺ ions and the carboxylate oxygen atoms from both the pre-attached MAA and the free BDC linkers, creating a stable extended network. This internal coordination framework grants the shell high chemical and mechanical stability, preventing the leaching of the core material during extraction and washing procedures. The magnetic core itself is protected from oxidation and dissolution by the inert MOF shell, further enhancing the overall durability and reusability of the adsorbent.

Experimental procedures

This study consisted of two main stages: matrix effect clean-up and extraction.

DµSPE procedure (matrix effect clean-up)

In a glass test tube, 5 mL of deionized water and 200 mg of sodium carbonate were added. Next, 10 µL of 100 mg L-1 phenolic compounds standard solution, 10 mg of Na2EDTA, and 10 mg of Co-TFA@MAA@Fe3O4 adsorbent were introduced. The vortexing for 3.0 min was applied to ensure adequate contact between the matrix components and the adsorbent. The adsorbent particles were separated using an external magnetic field. Finally, the supernatant was transferred into a 10 mL conical glass test tube for further analysis.

VA-LLME procedure (extraction)

Since the pH was adjusted in the previous step by adding sodium carbonate, no further pH adjustment was necessary. To regulate the ionic strength, 0.05g (1%, w/v) of sodium chloride was added. A volume of 22 µL of 1,2-DBE was mixed with 35 µL acetic anhydride and then introduced at the bottom of the test tube. The mixture was vortexed for 5 min to facilitate derivatization and extraction processes. Afterward, it was centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 5 min to allow phase separation. Finally, 1 µL of the sedimented organic phase was injected into the GC-FID for analysis. Figure 1 illustrates the clean-up and extraction processes.

Results and discussion

Characterization of Co-TFA@MAA@Fe3O4

FTIR is a method used to identify the chemical bonds and components of a material by measuring how it absorbs infrared light. FTIR analysis shows clear changes, proving that the modified Fe3O4 nanoparticles were successfully synthesized first with MAA and then by growing a cobalt-based MOF on them (Fig. 2a). The MAA@Fe3O4 spectrum shows expected peaks at 3400 cm⁻1 (O–H bonds), 2850–2950 cm⁻1 (C-H bonds), and 1600 cm⁻1 (COO⁻ bonds). Importantly, there is no peak relating to S–H bonds, meaning the sulfur in MAA is attached to the iron. After adding the MOF, new peaks appear between 1550 and 1650 cm⁻1, which are from the aromatic C = C bonds in the terephthalic acid of the MOF. The carboxylate (COO⁻) peaks also change (1350–1450 cm⁻1), indicating they are now connected to the cobalt. Also, it can be seen new peaks at 700–900 cm⁻1 from the aromatic rings are appeared. These changes, along with alterations to the Fe–O peak (550–600 cm⁻1), strongly suggest successfully coating the Co-TFA MOF on the modified Fe3O4 nanoparticles.

FTIR spectra (a), XRD pattern (b), SEM images/EDX pattern (c) TEM images (d), VSM curve (e), and BET curve (f) of Co-TFA@MAA@Fe3O4 and or MAA@Fe3O4 nanoparticles.

XRD is a widely used and powerful technique for identifying crystalline structure, determining crystal phases, and examining the structural purity of crystalline solids. This technique is based on X-ray diffraction from the atomically ordered planes of the material and can provide precise information about the crystal arrangement and structural changes of the material due to chemical modifications. In this study, XRD patterns of two magnetic adsorbents were investigated: the first consisted of Fe3O4 nanoparticles modified with mercaptoacetic acid (MAA@Fe3O4) and the second was the same adsorbent after their modification with the MOF (Co-TFA@MAA@Fe3O4) (Fig. 2b). In the XRD pattern of MAA@Fe3O4 adsorbent, distinct peaks are observed at 2θ angles of about 30.2, 35.5, 43.3, 53.6, 57.2 and 62.8°, which are consistent with the inverse spinel structure of Fe3O4 and the JCPDS standard card number 19–062948. These results indicate the preservation of the magnetic crystalline structure of Fe3O4 after surface modification with MAA. It should be noted that due to the amorphous nature and the relatively small amount of MAA attached to the surface of the nanoparticles, its specific peaks are not appeared in the XRD pattern or have very low intensity as a broad region at low 2θ region. However, the presence of this organic agent was confirmed using complementary analyses such as FTIR. In the XRD pattern of the final Co-TFA@MAA@Fe3O4 adsorbent, the Fe3O4 crystalline peaks are still observed, indicating the stability of the magnetic core structure during the step modification process. In addition, new peaks appeared at the 2θ regions of ~ 9.8°, ~ 16.7°, and ~ 27.1°, which are related to the crystalline structure of the Co-TFA MOF. The presence of these peaks indicates the successful growth of the MOF layer on the adsorbent surface and the formation of core–shell structure. In this structure, MAA plays the role of a bridge between the Fe3O4 core and the MOF layer. Overall, the XRD results confirm that the different synthesis steps were performed correctly and the final adsorbent with the desired structure was synthesized.

SEM was combined with energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) to investigate the synthesized adsorbent’s surface morphology and elemental composition. SEM images provide information about the particles’ shape, size, and distribution, while EDX analysis was used to confirm the presence of key elements in the adsorbent structure. As can be seen in Fig. 2c, the synthesized adsorbent has a spherical and relatively uniform surface structure. SEM images were taken at two different magnifications (200 nm and 1 μm). At higher magnification, the particle surface appears rough and uneven, which is due to the growth of the Co-TFA crystalline structure on the MAA@Fe3O4 surface. This MOF coating has caused a crystalline and irregular appearance on the spherical surface of the particles. Therefore, the overall shape of the particles is spherical, and the surface roughness is related to the crystalline structure of the outer layer. Also, in the image with a scale of 1 μm, a slight clustering particles is observed, which is probably due to the magnetic properties of Fe3O4. In the recorded EDX spectrum (Fig. 2c), the presence of Fe, S, Co, C, and O elements is visible. The Fe element is related to the magnetic core of Fe3O4. The presence of S element confirms the surface modification of Fe3O4 by MAA, whose thiol groups are attached to the Fe3O4 surface. The Co, C, and O elements also belong to the Co-TFA structure and indicate the success of the coating process and the formation of the final layer. These results will confirm the correctness of the design of the three-layer structure of the adsorbent.

TEM is a powerful tool for imaging at the nanoscale, allowing the observation of the internal structure, morphology, and atomic arrangement of nanoparticles by passing an electron beam through very thin samples. This technique offers a very high resolution (up to a few angstroms), making it particularly suitable for studying nanoscale structures, such as core–shell absorbers. Figure 2d (scaled at 50 nm) shows the core–shell structure of nanoparticles. The darker areas correspond to the Fe3O4 core, which has a higher electron density and therefore appears darker. The structures with medium contrast and irregular planar morphology around the magnetic nanoparticles are probably related to the Co-TFA MOF shell layer. It should be noted that due to the thin thickness and low electron density of MAA, this layer cannot be directly detected.

The particle size distribution histogram shows that the diameter of the nanoparticles ranges from 2 to 20 nm, with the highest abundance occurring around 7–8 nm. This relatively uniform distribution indicates a controlled and successful synthesis of the nanoparticles. An average size of around 8–9 nm is desirable for magnetic applications, since at this size, magnetite nanoparticles exhibit superparamagnetic behavior.

VSM measures the magnetic properties of materials by detecting their response to a magnetic field. To investigate the magnetic properties of the synthesized materials, VSM measurements were performed at room temperature. Figure 2e shows the hysteresis loops for MAA@Fe3O4 and Co-TFA@MAA@Fe3O4. The MAA@Fe3O4 exhibits a clear hysteresis loop, indicating its ferromagnetic (or superparamagnetic) nature. The saturation magnetization (Ms) value for MAA@Fe3O4 is approximately 8 emu g-1, confirming its significant magnetic behavior due to the presence of Fe3O4 nanoparticles. The relatively low coercivity (approximately 1.7 emu g-1) suggests that the material possesses soft magnetic characteristics. In contrast, the Co-TFA@MAA@Fe3O4 sample shows a significantly narrower hysteresis loop and a reduced saturation magnetization of approximately 3 emu g-1. This decrease in Ms is attributed to the successful growth of the non-magnetic Co-TFA MOF on the surface of the MAA@Fe3O4 nanoparticles. The MOF layer effectively dilutes the overall magnetic moment of the composite material. This change indicates that the surface of the magnetic nanoparticles has been successfully modified with the MOF, reducing the overall magnetic response of the material. The successful growth of the MOF layer is further supported by the change in the shape of the hysteresis loop and the reduction in Ms.. This confirms the successful coating of the Co-TFA MOF on the MAA@Fe3O4 substrate and indicates that the MOF layer is effectively passivating the magnetic properties of the Fe3O4 nanoparticles.

BET is a common method for determining specific surface area of ​​solids, based on the adsorption of nitrogen gas at 77 K. Using this technique, parameters such as specific surface area, volume of pores, and average pore diameter can be calculated. The data obtained from the BET provides detailed insight into the porous structure of adsorbents. According to the results of the BET analysis (Fig. 2f), the specific surface area of the studied sample was 64.275 m2 g-1, indicating a suitable active surface for adsorption processes. Also, the volume of the adsorbed gas monolayer was calculated to be 14.768 cm3(STP) g-1 and the BET constant C was estimated to be 42.883, indicating strong physical adsorption between nitrogen molecules and the adsorbent surface. The total pore volume in the structure was determined to be 0.3527 cm3 g-1, and the average pore diameter was calculated to be 21.949 nm, indicating a mesoporous structure of the material. These properties make the adsorbent very suitable for applications such as DμSPE, water purification, and adsorption of organic or pharmaceutical compounds.

Optimization of VA-LLME parameters

Base type and amount in derivatization

Phenols exhibit poor chromatographic behavior due to their high polarity and strong interactions with the stationary phase by formation of trailing peaks. To address these issues, in this study, derivatization with acetic anhydride was employed, which enhanced volatility, improved resolution, and increased the analytical signal. To determine the most suitable base for the derivatization process, 100 mg of sodium hydroxide and sodium carbonate were tested. As shown in Fig. 3 a, sodium carbonate demonstrates superior performance for all analytes. Therefore, sodium carbonate was identified as the optimal base. The key role of sodium carbonate is to adjust the pH to a mildly alkaline range (pH ~ 10–11), which is critical for the derivatization mechanism. At this pH, phenolic compounds (Ar-OH) are deprotonated to form phenolate anions (Ar–O⁻). These anions are significantly more nucleophilic than their neutral counterparts, enabling them to react efficiently with acetic anhydride in a nucleophilic acyl substitution reaction to form the desired phenyl acetate derivatives. This enhancement in reaction kinetics and completeness directly leads to the observed increase in analytical signals.

VA-LLME conditions: aqueous phase was the supernatant separated from the previous step. 100 mg sodium carbonate was added. 45 µL chloroform as extraction solvent was mixed with 20 µL acetic anhydride as derivatization agent and then added to it. Sodium carbonate regulates pH and facilitates ionization of phenols, enhancing their derivatization. To optimize the amount of sodium carbonate, 0–300 mg were tested. As illustrated in Fig. 3 b, increasing the sodium carbonate amount up to 200 mg improves phenols ionization, resulting in enhanced derivatization efficiency with acetic anhydride and corresponding increase in the analytical signals. However, at higher than 200 mg, it reacts with acetic anhydride, which reduces derivatization and extraction efficiency. Based on these findings, 200 mg was determined to be the optimal amount for sodium carbonate, and it was selected for further optimization.

Derivatizing agent

The utilization of derivatizing agent in the extraction of phenols is one of the most important parameters. Acetic anhydride was chosen due to its high reactivity, rapid derivatization, stable derivatives, and cost-effectiveness. Compared to other derivatizing agents, such as trimethylsilyl chloride, which is moisture-sensitive and requires strictly anhydrous conditions, or diazomethane, which despite its strong derivatization capability it is highly toxic and explosive, acetic anhydride offers a safer, more economical, and environmentally friendly alternative while ensuring the production of stable derivatives suitable for chromatographic analysis. To optimize the volume of acetic anhydride, various volumes (0–80 µL) were evaluated (Fig. 4). Increasing its volume up to 35 µL enhanced the derivatization efficiency; however, at higher volumes, the excessive production of acetic acid as a byproduct overwhelms the pH-adjusting capacity of sodium carbonate. This leads to a consumption of the base and a consequent drop in pH. The lower pH reduces the concentration of the reactive phenolate anions (Ar–O⁻), and the high concentration of acetate ions can shift the derivatization equilibrium backwards, according to Le Chatelier’s principle. Ultimately, this disrupts the conversion efficiency of phenols to their acetate derivatives. Consequently, analytes extraction into the organic phase was compromised, leading to a weaker analytical signals. Based on these findings, 35 µL of acetic anhydride was identified as the optimal volume for the further optimization steps.

Ionic strength

Modifying the ionic strength of an aqueous solution by introducing salts can affect the extraction process through two opposite mechanisms. "Salting-out" effect reduces the solubility of analytes in the aqueous phase, thereby facilitating their transferring into the organic phase and enhancing the analytical signals. Conversely, the "salting-in" effect increases the solution’s viscosity, hindering analytes migration into the extraction solvent and leading to a lower analytical responses. In this study, the impact of ionic strength was assessed using four conditions: saltless, in the presence of NaCl, KCl, or Na2SO4 at the concentration of 1 M. The results indicated low variation among these conditions, though the saltless and NaCl-containing solutions exhibited slightly higher analytical signals (Fig. S1a). NaCl demonstrated a marginal advantage over the saltless condition, likely due to its moderate ionic strength, which promoted effective phase separation without excessively increasing viscosity. In contrast, the salt with stronger ionic effect, such as Na2SO4, may disrupt the derivatization equilibrium, counteracting the potential benefits of salting-out.

Since the 1 M NaCl solutions and the saltless condition yielded nearly identical analytical signals, it was hypothesized that at this concentration, the opposite effects of salting-out and salting-in neutralize each other. To further investigate this, varying salt concentrations (0–10.0%, w/v) were evaluated (Fig. S1b). The results demonstrate that NaCl (1%, w/v) produces a stronger analytical signal than the saltless condition and higher concentrations. This suggests that at low salt concentrations, the salting-out effect dominates, enhancing analytes transferring into the organic phase. However, as the salt concentration increases, the salting-in effect becomes more pronounced, increasing solution viscosity and hindering mass transfer, thereby reducing extraction efficiency. These findings confirm that while ionic strength can influence the extraction process, an optimal salt concentration exists where the beneficial salting-out effect is maximized without the adverse impact of increased viscosity. Therefore, NaCl 1% (w/v) was used as an ionic strength regulator for the subsequent optimization steps.

Selection of extractant

The choice of a proper extractant is vital for effective analytes extraction. This solvent must have a density exceeding water density, have a strong affinity for the target compounds, be chemically inert to the analytes, and be water-immiscible. This investigation evaluated four solvents of chloroform, 1,1,2-TCE, 1,1,1-TCE, and 1,2-DBE Using different initial volumes for each solvent (45, 23, 25, and 22 µL, respectively) was necessary to account for their differing physicochemical properties and to ensure a consistent final volume of the sedimented organic phase (10 ± 1 µL) for all tests. As depicted in Fig. 5a, 1,2-DBE demonstrates the highest analytical signals due to its superior solubility for the analytes examined.

Selection of extraction solvent type (a) and volume (b) in VA-LLME step. Experimental conditions correspond to those shown in Fig. 4, except that 35 µL acetic anhydride and NaCl (1%, w/v) were used.

In the next step, the extraction solvent volume should to be optimized. Therefore, 22–34 µL of 1,2-DBE were evaluated, and the volume of the precipitated organic phase was measured to be 10, 13, 15, and 17 µL, respectively. As reported (Fig. 5b), with increasing 1,2-DBE volume, the analytes became more diluted and their analytical signals decrease. As a result, 22 µL of 1,2-DBE was selected as the optimal extraction solvent for the next optimization steps.

Vortexing time

Vortexing in VA-LLME method is essential for facilitating analytes extraction. Insufficient vortexing time impedes complete extraction, while excessive vortexing causes long analysis time and may disrupt the extraction equilibrium, promoting back-extraction. To determine the optimal duration, vortexing times of 3–9 min were compared (Fig. S2). The results indicate that 5 min provides the best balance, enabling suitable extraction while minimizing potential disruption.

Optimization of DµSPE effective parameters

Aqueous solution pH

The pH of the aqueous sample is a critical parameter in the DµSPE process, as it governs the surface charge of both the adsorbent and the chemical speciation of the phenolic analytes and matrix components, thereby influencing electrostatic interactions and adsorption behavior. In this study, the impact of pH was evaluated across a broad range (pH 2–13). The UP of the target phenols was used as the key performance metric. It is crucial to emphasize that the objective of this DµSPE step is not to adsorb the target analytes, but rather to eliminate matrix interferences while maximizing the retention of the phenols in the aqueous solution for their subsequent derivatization and microextraction. The results revealed two distinct operational regimes:

Acidic to neutral pHs (pH 2–6): Under these conditions, severe turbidity and the formation of pink and blue precipitates were observed following the addition of sodium carbonate in the subsequent VA-LLME stage. This phenomenon is attributed to the dissolution and degradation of the cobalt-based MOF shell, leading to the formation of cobalt hydroxide and carbonate. This adsorbent degradation not only hinders its capacity for matrix cleanup but also compromises the entire analytical process. Consequently, this pH range is entirely unsuitable for the method.

Alkaline pHs (pH 8–13): Within this range, the adsorbent remains stable and no precipitation is observed. Figure 6 demonstrates that the UPs remain consistently high and shows no significant variation across this entire broad range. This observation can be explained by the interplay of speciation and electrostatic forces: On the one hand, the surface of the adsorbent becomes negatively charged due to the deprotonation of its carboxylic acid groups (from terephthalate linker). On the other hand, the phenolic compounds (pKa ~ 9–10) undergo deprotonation to form phenolate anions, with the degree of ionization increasing from pH 8 to 13. This results in a strong electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged adsorbent and the anionic analytes, which effectively suppresses the adsorption of the target phenols across the entire alkaline range. The minimal variation in UP values indicates that the repulsive force is sufficient to prevent analyte adsorption even at the lower end (pH 8), where some phenols may not be fully ionized. Therefore, this broad pH range (8–13) defines the optimal window for the method’s strategy: it ensures the analytes remain in solution while allowing the adsorbent to sequester interfering matrix components through potentially other mechanisms (e.g., hydrophobic interactions, coordination with other metals in the matrix and so on). To maximize operational consistency and avoid introducing different ions that could affect the derivatization equilibrium in the VA-LLME step, 200 mg of sodium carbonate was chosen as the pH adjusting agent for both steps. Dissolving this amount in the sample solution yields a pH of approximately 10.5, which lies well within the identified optimal range. This approach guarantees chemical compatibility between the two stages and streamlines the overall procedure.

Adsorbent amount

The adsorbent mass is a critical parameter in the clean-up process. A low adsorbent mass prevents phenols adsorption; however, it may not effectively reduce or eliminate matrix effect. Conversely, an excessive adsorbent mass can adequately remove matrix interferences but may also lead to the adsorption and loss of phenols. Therefore, optimization of this parameter must be conducted with precision to achieve a balance between minimizing matrix effect and preventing analytes adsorption. In this stage, adsorbent masses of 5–25 mg were evaluated. The results (Fig. S3) indicate that up to 10 mg, no significant adsorption of phenols occurs. However, increasing the adsorbent mass to 15 mg and beyond results in considerable phenols adsorption. Consequently, 10 mg of the adsorbent was selected as the optimal amount for the subsequent optimizations.

Ionic strength

The effect of salt type on the inhibition of phenol adsorption by the adsorbent was investigated in this step. Solutions containing 1 M NaCl, Na2SO4 or KCl, and the saltless conditions were tested. The results showed that none of these salts had no significant effect on phenols adsorption, as the UP values remained constant across all conditions (Fig. S4). The addition of salts resulted in a significant increase in solution viscosity, hindering the movement of analytes toward the adsorbent. Thus, the salting-in effect and the increase in viscosity caused by the added salts led to a decrease in adsorption efficiency. Moreover, the salting-out effect is typically effective when the target organic compounds tend to move out of the aqueous phase onto the adsorbent surface. However, in this study, phenols exist as anionic phenates at the optimal pH (11–12), and the adsorbent surface charge is negative. In such conditions, strong electrostatic repulsion between the adsorbent and phenates prevents adsorption. Therefore, even if salting-out partially reduces the solubility of phenols in water, this effect is negligible compared to the electrostatic repulsion between the adsorbent and phenates, and it ultimately does not affect their adsorption. Therefore, the subsequent optimization steps were performed without adjusting the ionic strength.

Vortexing time

The vortexing operation completes the process of adsorption of matrix components onto the adsorbent. The vortexing time is a key parameter influencing the quantity of compounds adsorbed. Vortexing times of 0–9 min were evaluated based on the UP values. (Fig. S5). The results indicate that vortexing time has little effect on phenols adsorption onto the adsorbent. Strong electrostatic repulsion between the adsorbent and analytes prevents phenol adsorption at short or long vortexing times. It should be noted that in this optimization stage, the goal is not to adsorb phenols, and due to the repulsion between the adsorbent and phenol, the amount of their interaction is independent of the vortex time. However, vortexing must be performed in real sample cleaning stage to eliminate matrix effect. Therefore, a vortex time of 3 min was selected for this section.

Reusability of adsorbent

The reusability of adsorbent is a critical factor for its economic viability. In this study, the Co-TFA@MAA@Fe3O4 adsorbent was evaluated for its ability to undergo multiple adsorption desorption cycles. After the initial adsorption to eliminate matrix effect, the adsorbent was regenerated using 1 mL of iso-propanol: deionized water (1:3, v/v) mixture. Following this desorption process, the regenerated adsorbent was exposed to the analytes solution again. This process was repeated up to five cycles, demonstrating consistent performance of the adsorbent with relative standard deviations (RSDs) ≤ 10%, indicating its suitable reusability.

Method validation

The method showed excellent sensitivity, with LODs between 0.85 and 1.02 µg L-1 and LOQs between 2.8 and 3.36 µg L-1. These values were calculated based on the signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) criteria, defining LOD and LOQ as the concentrations yielding S/N of 3 and 10, respectively. These low limits indicate that the method is able to reliably detect and quantify trace amounts of the target phenolic compounds among other compounds in complex matrices. The linear range of the method was evaluated over a wide concentration range (10–10,000 µg L-1) and was found to demonstrate good linearity with r2 greater than or equal to 0.9990 for all analytes. The calibration curve was constructed using seven concentration levels (LOQ, 10, 50, 250, 1000, 2000, 5000, and 10,000 µg L⁻1), with each level prepared and analyzed in triplicate (n = 3). The precision of the method was evaluated by assessing RSD at different levels (10, 100 and 250 µg L-1) for both intra- and inter-day analyses. RSDs were below or equal to 8.3% for all analytes indicating that the method is repeatable. The efficacy of the method in preconcentrating the target analytes was assessed with the EF parameter. The EF ranged from 310 to 415 indicating the ability of this method to substantially preconcentrate phenolic compounds. The ER ranged from 62 to 83% indicating the efficacy of the method to extract the target analytes from the aqueous matrix. The UP values were in the range of 88–99%, showing that the DµSPE step is done effectively to negate matrix interference, but not adsorbing the target analytes on the adsorbent. Matrix effect study in real samples

Table 2 presents RRs of the target analytes in three different wastewater matrices (pharmaceutical, municipal, and petrochemical) spiked at the concentrations of 50, 250, and 1000 µg L-1 (each analyte). The RRs were assessed both without and with the application of the DµSPE matrix removal step to evaluate the effectiveness of the developed method in mitigating matrix interferences. In the absence of the DµSPE step, significant matrix effect was observed across all wastewaters, as evidenced by RRs falling outside the acceptable range of 80–120%. Specifically, in pharmaceutical wastewater, the RRs for all analytes were very less than 80%, indicating significant negative effect due to matrix components. Municipal and petrochemical wastewaters also showed RR values that were frequently outside of the acceptable range, although less severely than in pharmaceutical wastewater. The variations in RR among different wastewaters highlight the complexity and variability of matrix interferences. Following the implementation of the DµSPE step using the Co-TFA@MAA@Fe3O4 adsorbent, a marked improvement in the RRs was observed. The RR values for all analytes in all wastewaters fell within the acceptable range of 80–120%. This indicates that the DµSPE method effectively removed the matrix components responsible for obtaining low RRs, allowing for more accurate quantification of the target analytes. It is noted that procedural blanks (deionized water taken through the entire sample preparation and analytical process) were analyzed alongside each batch of real samples to monitor for potential contamination from solvents, reagents, glassware, or the adsorbent. The chromatograms of these procedural blanks showed no detectable peaks at the retention times corresponding to the target phenolic compounds. This confirms that the signals measured in the unspiked real samples (Creal) were genuinely originating from the native sample matrix and not from procedural contamination.

Comparison with other methods

The DµSPE-VA-LLME-GC-FID approach was thoroughly compared with the other methods used to evaluate phenolic compounds among diverse sample types (Table 3). The method yields good sensitivity, generating excellent LODs for all analytes of concern, which is needed for determining trace-level concentrations of the phenolic contaminants often found in complex environmental samples. Specifically, the LODs using the method are comparable to or better than those of most other approaches and demonstrate capability for ultra-trace analysis. The method possesses the additional benefit of a wide linear range, providing reliable quantification in a wide range of concentrations typically encountered in environmental samples. Intra- and inter-day precisions demonstrate the method has good repeatability and reliability for routine analysis. A unique dual-step procedure (DµSPE for selective matrix removal, VA-LLME for efficient extraction and pre concentration) produces extremely high EFs for the extraction technique at low-level phenols when compared with the best approaches available. This means the method is suitable for pre concentrating the target analytes while considering the complexity of samples from a variety of matrices. Moreover, the DµSPE step provides very efficient removal of matrix interferences from pharmaceutical, municipal, and petrochemical wastewaters, ensuring reliable analytical performance in complex matrices. In summary, the combination of high sensitivity, broad linear range, remarkable precision, and efficient matrix removal makes this method as a good choice for environmental monitoring.

Greenness assessment of the method

Environmental compatibility plays a pivotal role in minimizing environmental harm, enhancing safety, reducing costs, and making chemical processes more sustainable. To comprehensively evaluate the alignment of the proposed method with the principles of green chemistry, the Complementary Green Analytical Procedure Index (ComplexGAPI) was employed. The resulting pictogram is color-coded (green, yellow, and red), representing high, medium, and low levels of environmental compatibility, safety, and economic feasibility, respectively. Figure S6 provides a visual summary of this assessment for the developed method. Key advantages of the method include: simple sample preparation, minimal solvent consumption, no emission of harmful vapors, and robust performance in quantitative and qualitative analyses. adherence to environmental sustainability principles, reduced safety and health hazards. Additionally, the Environmental Factor (E-Factor) was calculated, which is defined as the ratio of the total waste mass (g) to the mass of the product (g). The calculated E-Factor for this method is approximately 44. This value justifies the environmental feasibility of the procedure, as it reflects efficient resource utilization and waste minimization compared to conventional sample preparation techniques. The E-Factor metric underscores the method’s alignment with green chemistry objectives by quantitatively demonstrating its reduced environmental footprint.

Conclusions

This research introduce, for the first time, an innovative DµSPE-VA-LLME-GC-FID methodology designed for the clean-up, extraction, preconcentration, and measurement of phenolic compounds within complex environmental samples. A magnetic core–shell adsorbent was utilized to overcome the issues of sample clean-up with a new emphasis on matrix removal. Previous studies focused on analytes adsorption, whereas this project focused on matrix removal before analysis. In conclusion, this DµSPE made sample preparation more sustainable, quick, and selective. Also, this innovative procedure adhered to green chemistry principles by employing only a few microliter volumes of organic solvent, which is in line with global initiatives for greener analytical practices. The method showed excellent analytical qualities, such as capturing a wide linear quantification range, great precision associated with low RSDs, good matrix effect elimination, and excellent EFs to compete with. In conclusion, through the use of the techniques mentioned, this DµSPE-VA-LLME-GC-FID method provided a reliable, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly method for quantifying phenolic compounds in numerous real-life samples. The emerging method presented for a strong and sensitive approach for the environmental monitoring of phenolic compounds.
