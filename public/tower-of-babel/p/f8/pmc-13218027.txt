Mechanical-biochemical signaling accelerates scleral remodeling in myopia via the LaminA/C-Emerin-MKL1 axis
Authors: Yuying Liu, Xin Liu, Mingming Liu, Xuetong Wang, Wenzhe Qian, Ting Zhao, Min Li, Bilian Ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Scleral remodeling is the pathological basis of axial elongation during the progression of myopia. However, the mechanism underlying the onset and progression of scleral remodeling remains unclear. Here, based on reanalysis of single-cell RNA sequencing data, we showed that aberrant biomechanics in the sclera serve as signals that promote extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling during the development of myopia via LaminA/C-Emerin-MKL1 axis, and followed by a decrease in collagen synthesis in scleral fibroblasts. Mice with lens-induced myopia (LIM) and guinea pigs with form-deprivation myopia (FDM) exhibited nuclear deformation and dysregulation of nuclear envelope (NE) proteins in sclera. We established a mechanical model to investigate the role of matrix stiffness in regulating collagen synthesis in human primary scleral fibroblasts. In contrast to stiff substrates, soft matrix attenuated the sequestration of LaminA/C and Emerin in the NE and impaired MKL1 nuclear accumulation and COL1A1 transcription. Interventions for MKL1 induced or inhibited the progression of myopia in vivo. We illustrate the therapeutic potential of a novel mechanomodulation strategy that blocks scleral remodeling and myopia progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02809-z.

Introduction

Myopia is the most prevalent refractive error worldwide. Increased axial length during the progression of myopia is associated with a greater risk of irreversible visual impairment and even blindness [1, 2]. Therefore, it is very important to elucidate the underlying pathological mechanisms and to identify safe and effective strategies for the prevention and treatment of myopia. Axial elongation is associated with scleral remodeling [3, 4]. Fibroblasts, which account for the majority of the cell population of the sclera, are responsible for the synthesis of all scleral extracellular matrix (ECM) components [5, 6]. However, the mechanism underlying the onset and progression of scleral remodeling remains unclear.

To further investigate the mechanisms of scleral remodeling in myopia, we re-analyzed a publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset of sclera from a PNAS study on experimental myopia [7]. Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes revealed a significant enrichment of nuclear envelope (NE)-associated pathways. Given that the NE not only maintains nuclear structure but also serves as a physical interface between cytoskeletal forces and nuclear responses, we speculated that NE components might play a role in transducing mechanical stimuli.

The sclera is the main load-bearing connective tissue of the eye, and it is required to maintain optical stability under highly dynamic loading conditions due to external and internal factors [4, 8, 9]. Scleral mechanotransduction has attracted increased interest because the aberrant biomechanics of scleral remodeling may be one of the important pathological changes that occurs during the development of myopia. Siegwart et al. [10] performed uniaxial mechanical tests on the sclera of tree shrews and found an increased creep rate in myopic eyes. Hoang et al. [11] reported that decreased scleral stiffness is proportional to the degree of myopia in guinea pigs with form deprivation myopia (FDM). Accumulating evidence has shown that mechanical stimuli from the surrounding microenvironment can alter long-term cell behavior, transcription patterns, and cell fate [12–14]. Our previous research also revealed that scleral fibroblasts are sensitive to mechanical stimuli, which can regulate the integrin-F-actin-YAP axis and affect the expression of ECM-related genes [15]. The nucleus is the largest and stiffest organelle in eukaryotic cells, and it determines the mechanosensitivity of shuttling proteins and responses to external stimuli [16, 17]. Whether nuclear skeletal elements, such as NE proteins, are mechanosensitive and affect scleral fibroblast behaviors during the progression of myopia is poorly understood.

A-type lamins (Lamin A and C) are the main components of the nucleoskeleton that maintain the mechanical properties of the nucleus [18, 19]. The expression of A-type lamins and nuclear lamina-associated proteins has recently been reported to scale with tissue stiffness [20]. Mechanical stress is transmitted to the nucleus via the linker of nucleoskeleton and cytoskeleton (LINC) complex at the NE [21], and this complex consists of the inner nuclear membrane proteins SUN1 and SUN2, the LAP2–Emerin–MAN1 (LEM) domain protein Emerin and the outer nuclear membrane Nesprin family proteins Nesprin and KASH [22, 23]. Lamin-binding proteins often serve as “adaptors” via which the lamina organizes chromatin, influences gene expression and epigenetic regulation, and modulates signaling pathways [24]. For instance, MAN1 and probably LEM2 must interact with LaminA to localize to the NE and directly bind to DNA [25]. Lamin A/C interacts with emerin to regulate nuclear translocation of MKL1, a mechanosensitive transcription factor [26–28]. Based on these studies, we hypothesized that changes in mechanical properties of the sclera alter NE proteins and downstream signaling, which in turn regulates ECM remodeling in the sclera during the development of myopia.

Thus, in this study, we established experimental animal models of myopia and a mechanical model of scleral fibroblasts to determine the role of the LaminA/C-Emerin-MKL1 axis in ECM remodeling and investigate its effect on the progression of myopia in vivo. Here, we show that scleral mechanotransduction via the LaminA/C-Emerin-MKL1 axis is essential for the development of myopia and that targeting this axis is a promising strategy for the prevention and treatment of myopia.

Materials and methods

Animals

The treatment and care of the animals were conducted according to the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) statement for the use of animals in ophthalmic and vision research. The experimental protocols were approved by the ethics committee of Shanghai General Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (2023AWS113). Male C57BL/6J mice and guinea pigs were housed in standard transparent cages with abundant food and water. The animals were maintained under a 12-h light-dark cycle (lights on at 7:00 AM and off at 7:00 PM) and under ambient illumination of approximately 200 lux during the day.

Lens-induced myopia (LIM) model

The LIM model was established in three-week-old male C57BL/6J mice that wore a -25.00 diopter (D) lens attached to a plastic Elizabethan collar for 4 weeks. One eye was randomly selected to be the model eye, and the other eye served as the control. The refraction and axial length of both eyes were determined before and every 2 weeks after LIM establishment. An infrared photorefractor (Steinbeis Transfer Center, Germany, Fig. S1a) was used to measure the refractive state at baseline, and only mice with less than a 1.00 D difference in refraction between the two eyes were used. A custom-made, ultralong depth, SD-OCT system was used to measure axial length as previously described [29]. In brief, mice were anesthetized with a mixture of 0.6% pentobarbital and 0.2% xylazine (10 µl/g body weight), positioned on a stage and scanned along the optical axis of the eye with an X-Y cross-scanning system (Fig. S1b). To minimize nonspecific effects, any mice with an initial axial length difference > 0.01 mm were removed from this study. The mice were checked every day to ensure attachment of the lens.

FDM model

Pigmented male guinea pigs (2 weeks old) without eye disease or congenital myopia were used to establish a FDM model. A translucent diffuser, which was made from white lattices, was glued onto one randomly selected eye of each guinea pig continuously for 4 weeks, while the other eye without any treatment served as the self-control (SC) eye. The refractive state was measured by an automated infrared photorefractor adjusted to customized for the guinea pig eyes after mydriasis by compound tropicamide eye drops. Axial length was measured using A-scan ultrasonography (Strong 6000 A; Wuhan Strong Electronics Ltd., Wuhan, China) while the animals were under topical anesthesia (0.4% oxybuprocaine hydrochloride). The measurements were repeated at least eight times for each eye.

Transmission electron microscopy

For observation by TEM, mouse eyeballs were fixed in ice-cold 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS for 1 h and cut along the sagittal plane. The cornea and lens were removed, and the remaining tissue was further fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde/60 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.4) overnight. The specimens were washed in 60 mM HEPES buffer, incubated with 1% OsO4 in 0.1 M PB (pH 7.4) for 2 h at room temperature, dehydrated through a series of ethanol solutions, replaced, and embedded in Epon 812 (EM Japan, Tokyo, Japan). After embedding, the blocks were thinly sectioned at 70 nm and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The sections were visualized with an HT7800/HT7700 system (hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Scleral thickness measurement

Eyeballs of mice were carefully dissected and post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for 1 h. For cryosectioning, the tissues were sequentially immersed in 10% and 20% sucrose solutions for 30 min each, followed by immersion in 30% sucrose overnight at 4 °C for cryoprotection. The eyes were then embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and sectioned using a cryostat at a thickness of 20 μm. Sections passing through the optic nerve head were selected to ensure consistency in anatomical location. Brightfield images of the posterior sclera were acquired using a light microscope (Zeiss, Germany), with contrast-enhancing settings applied uniformly across all samples. For each eye, two micrographs were captured from the corresponding posterior scleral region, and in each micrograph, three linear measurements of scleral thickness were taken perpendicular to the outer surface using ImageJ software. Measurements were performed manually by one observer (LYY), and the average of the three measurements was used as the final scleral thickness for each image.

Cell culture and treatment

To investigate the effects of mechanical stimuli on scleral fibroblasts, human scleral fibroblast (HmSF) cultures were established as previously described [6, 7]. All procedures followed the tenets of Declaration of Helsinki. Primary fibroblasts were isolated from human scleral explants and cultured. The explanted tissues were cut into 1 × 1-mm pieces, placed in tissue culture dishes containing high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and incubated in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 95% air at 37 °C. After 7 to 10 days, cells grew out of the explants, and then were passaged after digestion with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco Laboratories, Gaithersburg, MD, USA). Primary scleral fibroblasts derived from human sclera were identified by immunohistochemical staining for vimentin (Boster Biological Technology, Pleasanton, CA, USA; Fig. S1c). Fibroblasts from the third to seventh passages were used for this experiment.

Scleral fibroblasts were seeded on plastic, 50-kappa (stiff) or 8-kappa (soft) commercial polyacrylamide hydrogels coated with collagen type I in 6-well plates or 24-well plates (Softwell; Matrigen, USA). The plates were placed in an incubator and cultured at 37 °C for 12 h.

SiRNA and plasmid transfection, Western blotting and qRT‒PCR and ELISA

Details of SiRNA and plasmid transfection, Western blotting, qRT‒PCR and ELISA are provided in Supplementary Materials, Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy analysis of fibroblast

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min and blocked with blocking buffer (5% BSA and 0.3% Triton in PBS) 1 h at 37 °C, then subsequently incubated with anti-LaminA/C (CST 1:200), anti-Emerin (CST 1:400) and anti-MKL1 (Absin 1:200) antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS three times, cells were stained with secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa 488 (Thermo A11029, 1:1000) or Alexa 594 (Thermo A11012, 1:1000) at room temperature for 1 h. After washing in PBS and labelling and counterstaining with DAPI, the slides were mounted in Fluoromount-G (catalog no. 0100-01, Southern Biotechnology, USA), and observed under a confocal fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany). Nuclei per condition were randomly selected from at least 3 non-overlapping fields of view per experiment. Image processing was conducted using ImageJ.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extraction

Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were extracted using a commercial nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extraction kit (Beyotime, China, Cat. No. P0027) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were washed once with ice-cold PBS and collected by gentle scraping or EDTA treatment to avoid proteolytic degradation. Cell pellets were resuspended in cytoplasmic protein extraction buffer A supplemented with PMSF and vigorously vortexed to ensure complete dispersion, followed by incubation on ice. Cytoplasmic protein extraction buffer B was then added, and samples were vortexed and centrifuged at 12,000–16,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected as the cytoplasmic fraction.

The remaining pellets were further extracted with nuclear protein extraction buffer containing PMSF by intermittent vigorous vortexing on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000–16,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected as the nuclear fraction. All protein extracts were either used immediately or stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

Nuclear morphology analysis

Nuclear morphology was assessed using nuclear area and nuclear roundness ratio [30]. The calculating formula of form factor is the ratio of 4π times the nuclear area (A) to the nuclear perimeter (P) squared (4πA/P2). All measurements were performed using ImageJ, and the pixel-to-micron conversion was calibrated using Analyze > Set Scale based on the microscope scale bar. Then the Area and Perimeter option was enabled in Set Measurements. For electron microscopy images, nuclear boundaries were manually traced with the polygon selection tool by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups, followed by area and perimeter measurement via Analyze > Measure. For immunofluorescent images, nuclear boundaries were segmented based on LaminA/C staining. Fluorescent images were acquired using the confocal microscope equipped with a 63×oil immersion objective at the nuclear mid-plane under identical settings. The image was converted to an 8-bit grayscale format firstly, and then nuclear was semi-automatically segmented using the Magic Wand tool.

RNA sequencing of fibroblast

After transfection with siRNA targeting LaminA/C, Emerin, total RNA was extracted from human scleral fibroblasts using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s procedure. RNA quality and integrity were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and samples with an RNA integrity number > 7.0 were used to construct sequencing library. The RNA was subjected to a series of steps: enrich mRNA by Oligo(dT), mRNA fragment, random hexamer primed cDNA synthesis, size selection and PCR amplification. Raw sequencing reads were processed using Fastp to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2 software. After the final transcriptome was generated, StringTie was used to perform expression level for mRNAs by calculating FPKM. Genes differential expression analysis was performed by DESeq2 software between different groups (siNC vs. siLMNA vs. siEMD). The genes with the parameter of false discovery rate below 0.05 and absolute fold change ≥ 2 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using the Gene Ontology database to identify significantly enriched biological processes. The RNA-seq data have been deposited in the GEO database under accession number GSE316874.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) analysis

The ChIP assay was performed according to the detailed protocol. Briefly, approximately 2–5 × 107 cells cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, followed by neutralization with glycine. To prepare the antibody-bead complexes, the Protein A/G magnetic beads were pre-blocked and incubated with anti-MKL1 antibody or immunoglobulin G (IgG) for 6 h to overnight at 4 °C. The cross-linked cells were lysed and the chromatin was sheared via sonication to an average length of 200–600 bp. For immunoprecipitation, the sheared chromatin lysates were incubated with the pre-prepared antibody-bead complexes for 2–4 h at 4 °C. After washing with Wash Buffer and TE Wash Buffer, the immune complexes were eluted and reverse cross-linked at 65 °C. The purified DNA was then extracted and the enrichment of MKL1 on the COL1A1 promoter was analyzed by PCR using specific primers (Table S1).

Dual-luciferase assay

To investigate the effect of MKL1 on the promoter activity of COL1A1, we cloned the putative promoter of COL1A1 gene upstream of the pGL3-luciferase vector and compared transcription between cells that were cotransfected with and without MKL1 plasmid. The HEK293 cell line was used for the dual-luciferase reporter assay. Transient transfections were performed with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). Luciferase activities were analyzed using a luciferase reporter assay system (Promega). All experiments were repeated at least three times.

Drug and adenovirus infection in vivo

Drug and adenovirus injections were performed under a Zeiss (Gottingen, Germany) operating microscope. Mice were narcotized by intraperitoneal injection. Posterior Tenon’s capsule was inserted along the eyeball from the temporal subconjunctiva, and 4 µL of drug or virus was delivered. The MKL1 inhibitor CCG-1423 was purchased from Absin. Each mouse received unilateral subconjunctival injections of the drug every two days for 4 weeks to maximize effectiveness.

To overexpress MKL1 in vivo, we transduced mice with an adenovirus (ADV) encoding a green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter together with either a plasmid targeting MKL1 (Adv-pcMKL1) or an empty vector (Adv-vector). The plasmids targeting the mouse MKL1 gene were constructed and inserted into the ADV by GeneChem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Twelve three-week-old C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into two groups: the Adv-vector group and the Adv-pcMKL1 group. In both groups, the experimental eyes were treated with subconjunctival injections of either Adv-vector or Adv-pcMKL1 and then underwent the LIM procedure of 4 weeks, while the contralateral eyes remained as internal controls to evaluate the refractive shift and axial elongation specifically attributed to MKL1 overexpression. Mice were sacrificed after the refractive parameters were measured, and sclera was collected and stored for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

Student’s paired t test was used to analyze the differences between myopic eyes and control eyes in mice and guinea pigs. For comparisons between 2 groups in cells, a 2-tailed t test was performed. For comparisons among ≥ 3 groups, 1-way ANOVA or 2-way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey analyses were performed using SPSS package. P values less than 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance (*). All the in vitro experiments were repeated ≥ 3 times, and 3 replicates were estimated to provide 80% power.

Results

LaminA/C and Emerin are downregulated in the sclera of experimental myopia

After 4 weeks of LIM establishment, the affected eyes exhibited axial elongation and myopic refraction shifts during eye growth and a decreased scleral thickness compared to control eyes (Fig. 1a-c), which are typical features of myopia in humans. Western blotting analysis revealed downregulation of COL1A1 in sclera of LIM mice. Uncropped western blots are provided in supplementary material Western blots. Based on previously published single-cell transcriptomic data of the myopic sclera [7], we conducted a reanalysis and found that nuclear envelope proteins were significantly altered in the myopic eyes. (Fig. 1d). Interestingly, an aberrant nuclear morphology, as reflected by the decreased nuclear circularity and reduced nuclear area were observed in scleral fibroblasts by of myopic eyes TEM (Fig. 1e). Expression of LaminA/C, which is the key structural determinant of nuclear shape and stiffness, was then analyzed by western blotting. Results showed that LIM significantly decreased LaminA/C expression in the sclera compared with the contralateral eyes (Fig. 1f). We also assessed the expression of LaminA/C in the FDM model of guinea pigs, which revealed decreased expression of LaminA/C in the sclera of FDM eyes (Fig. 1g, S2a-b).

To investigate differentially expressed genes that encode NE proteins anchoring to LaminA/C in the sclera of myopic eyes, mRNA was extracted from the sclera and analyzed by RT‒PCR. In this study, a total of 9 NE proteins were examined. Overall, we found that the mRNA expression levels of Emerin were markedly lower in the sclera of myopic eyes than contralateral eyes. Furthermore, changes in the expression of Emerin were more pronounced than those of other nuclear proteins (Fig. 1h). Western blotting analysis confirmed that Emerin protein was downregulated in sclera of myopic eyes in both LIM mice and FDM guinea pigs (Fig. 1i-j).

These results demonstrate that the levels of nuclear skeleton protein LaminA/C and NE protein Emerin are reduced in the sclera during the development of LIM mice and FDM guinea pigs.

LaminA/C and Emerin expression in scleral fibroblasts is regulated by ECM stiffness

To investigate mechanotransduction in scleral fibroblasts, we utilized an established in vitro model of substrate mechanics by culturing scleral fibroblasts on collagen I–coated hydrogels with varying degrees of stiffness. Specifically, we grew cells on substrates with an elastic modulus of either 8 kPa or 50 kPa. HmSFs cultured on soft matrix exhibited a smaller nuclear area and downregulated LaminA/C expression (Fig. 2b), which were consistent with previous observations in LIM mice. Compared with cells grown on the stiff matrix, cells grown on the soft matrix exhibited marked decreases in LaminA/C and Emerin expression, accompanied by a significant reduction in COL1A1 expression (Fig. 2c). Since COL1A1 is an important ECM protein, we performed ELISAs to evaluate the effects of substrate stiffness on extracellular secreted COL1A1, COL1A1 levels were significantly lower in the supernatants of cells that were cultured on soft substrate (Fig. 2d). Moreover, soft substrate reduced COL1A1 mRNA expression (Fig. 2e), suggesting that the reduction in COL1A1 protein was, in part, due to decreased COL1A1 synthesis.

Immunofluorescence staining also revealed a reduction in the levels of LaminA/C and Emerin. Because LaminA/C and Emerin are NE proteins, we examined the effect of substrate stiffness on the distribution of LaminA/C and Emerin. Importantly, immunofluorescence costaining revealed the colocalization of LaminA/C and Emerin in cells grown on stiff substrate. However, the Emerin was more dynamic and mislocalized from the NE under conditions of soft substrate (Fig. 2f).

Taken together, our results demonstrate that mechanical microenvironment of soft ECM is sufficient to decrease the levels of collagen, downregulate the expression of LaminA/C and Emerin, and disrupt the distribution of Emerin.

Reduction of LaminA/C attenuates the NE accumulation and overall abundance of Emerin

We next investigated whether mechanotransduction in scleral fibroblasts is influenced by Lamin A/C attenuation. To address this question, we first transfected small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting LaminA/C into cells grown on a stiff surface to determine whether LaminA/C knockdown could mimic the effects of a soft matrix. The siRNA resulted in good knockdown efficiency. A decrease in the nuclear area, as shown by immunofluorescence staining, demonstrated the responsiveness of LaminA/C to a soft substrate (Fig. 3a). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) results demonstrated that Emerin physically associates with LaminA/C (Fig. S2). We further found that LaminA/C knockdown resulted in downregulation of the Emerin protein (Fig. 3b) and mislocalization from the NE (Fig. 3f); these changes were similar to the changes observed in scleral fibroblasts that were grown on a soft matrix. Reduction of COL1A1 levels (Fig. 3b-c) was also observed. Moreover, knockdown of Emerin by siRNA also downregulated COL1A1 expression (Fig. 3d-e). We performed a whole-genome transcriptomic analysis of siRNA-transfected HmSFs. ClueGO analysis of the differentially expressed genes (si-NC vs. si-LMNA vs. si-EMD) revealed deregulation of collagen-containing ECM pathways (Fig. S3a-b).

Attenuation of LaminA/C and Emerin inhibits MKL1 expression and nuclear translocation

We sought to determine how collagen synthesis is influenced by decreased LaminA/C and Emerin. Pathway analysis revealed that the top differentially expressed genes were clearly enriched in pathways related to cancer, focal adhesion pathways and actin skeleton regulation pathways (Fig. 4a). Moreover, LaminA/C and Emerin are known to regulate the subcellular localization of MKL1, a key transcriptional coactivator of many actin cytoskeletal genes [26]. Our previous study also showed that expression of MKL1 in scleral fibroblasts is altered in response to mechanical changes in ECM [31]. Thus, we focused on MKL1 as the downstream regulator. Indeed, soft ECM attenuated the nuclear accumulation of MKL1 in fibroblasts, as determined by immunofluorescence staining and nuclear–cytoplasmic fractionation (Fig. 4b-c). Furthermore, expression of MKL1 was significantly downregulated in cells grown on a soft matrix compared with stiff matrix (Fig. 4d). The downregulation of MKL1 expression was verified in both guinea pigs and mice with experimental myopia (Fig. S4a-b).

Furthermore, MKL1 expression (Fig. 4e) and nuclear accumulation (Fig. 4g-h) were substantially lower in siLMNA-transfected cells than in siNC-transfected cells. These results indicate that downregulation of LaminA/C attenuates the localization of Emerin to the nuclear membrane in cells grown on a soft matrix and substantially mediates the MKL1 signaling pathway. We hypothesize that decreased matrix stiffness primarily attenuates LaminA/C expression, which affects the distribution of Emerin on the NE and controls nuclear export and transcriptional activity of MKL1.

MKL1 is required for modulation of collagen expression in scleral fibroblasts

Furthermore, we explored the functional role of MKL1 in regulation of collagen synthesis. Overexpression of MKL1 partially reversed the loss of COL1A1 protein expression induced by inhibition of LaminA/C (Fig. 5a) or Emerin (Fig. 5b). MKL1 overexpression significantly increased COL1A1 expression without affecting LaminA/C and Emerin expression (Figs. 5a-b, S5). In contrast, knockdown of MKL1 by siRNA had the opposite effect, and the expression of COL1A1 significantly decreased (Figs. 5c-d).

MKL1 activity is partly regulated by its nuclear translocation. We next evaluated the effect of the MKL1 inhibitor CCG14233 on scleral fibroblasts. The nuclear translocation of MKL1 was abrogated in scleral fibroblasts treated with CCG1423 (Fig. 5g). Pharmaceutical inhibition of MKL1 with CCG-1423 dramatically reduced COL1A1 levels, as determined by immunoblotting (Fig. 5e) and ELISA (Fig. 5f). We next determined whether MKL1 could directly activate COL1A1 transcription. We first performed ChIP assays to verify that MKL1 can directly associate with the COL1A1 promoter (Fig. 5h). Then we transfected a COL1A1 promoter-luciferase construct with or without MKL1 construct into HEK-293 cells. MKL1 overexpression significantly increased COL1A1 promoter activity, which suggested that MKL1 might directly activate COL1A1 transcription (Fig. 5i).

Intervention of scleral MKL1 can control myopia progression in mice

To determine whether MKL1 is associated with axial elongation and development of myopia, we first determined whether suppression of MKL1 in sclera induces myopia at baseline. The MKL1 inhibitor CCG14233 was subconjunctivally injected into healthy mice every other day for 4 consecutive weeks, and myopic changes were compared between CCG1423-treated mice and DMSO-treated mice. Interestingly, the CCG1423-injected eyes exhibited axial elongation and myopic shifts in refraction (Fig. 6a-b). Moreover, the sclera was thinner after CCG1423 injection than after control injection (Fig. 6c). The scleral levels of MKL1 and COL1A1 were determined by Western blotting (Fig. 6d). These data suggest that MKL1 loss might promote the development of myopia (Fig. 7).

To further explore the in vivo relevance of our findings, we transduced mice with an ADV encoding a green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter together with either a plasmid encoding MKL1 (pcMKL1) or an empty vector (pcNC) by subconjunctival injection before the establishment of LIM. Western blotting was used to confirm the transfection efficiency of the mice in a preliminary experiment (Fig. S6). Compared with those in Ad-vector-treated mice, myopic shifts in refraction and axial elongation were diminished in MKL1-expressing mice at both 2 weeks and 4 weeks after the establishment of LIM (Fig. 6e-f). Compared with Adv-vector, Adv-pcMKL1 increased scleral thickness after the induction of LIM (Fig. 6g). Concomitant with scleral thickening, expression of MKL1 reversed the downregulation of COL1A1 expression in eyes with LIM (Fig. 6h). These results indicate that expression of MKL1 significantly alleviated the development of myopia in LIM mice.

Taken together, these data suggest that MKL1 could play an essential role in progression of myopia in vivo.

Discussion and conclusion

Scleral ECM remodeling plays a critical role in the development of myopia [5, 7, 32, 33]. However, the signaling pathways governing this process remain poorly understood. In this study, we reanalyzed publicly available scRNA-seq data from a myopia model and identified significant enrichment of NE-associated pathways. Recognizing the established structural and signaling functions of NE components [34, 35], together with the emerging recognition of mechanotransduction involvement in myopia progression [36–38], we further explored NE-mediated nuclear mechanotransduction in response to decreased ECM stiffness. We initially demonstrated that NE dysregulation is an important cause of reduced collagen synthesis during myopic scleral remodeling. In scleral fibroblasts grown on soft matrix, a reduction of LaminA/C expression induces the mislocalization of Emerin from NE, substantially abrogating MKL1 activity. MKL1 functions as a major regulator of collagen synthesis in vitro and in vivo and controls the refractive state. In summary, our data suggest a mechanism by which changes in ECM stiffness regulate collagen synthesis through biomechanics. The strengths of this study are that these findings were shown both in vitro and in vivo and that the findings show the involvement of these mechanisms across species.

In this study, we observed dysmorphic nuclei in scleral fibroblasts of eyes with LIM. Swift et al. [20] reported that the NE is highly wrinkled and folded in cells grown on soft matrix, while stiff matrix and high tension result in flattening of the nucleus. Combined with previous reports on changes in the mechanical properties of myopic sclera [3, 4, 10], we hypothesize that decreased scleral ECM stiffness deforms nuclei during the progression of myopia. Nuclear stability is conferred by A-type lamins [18, 39, 40], and decreased LaminA/C expression in the sclera of myopic eyes may occur because of changes in the scleral mechanical microenvironment. Moreover, in addition to regulating mechanosensitivity, LaminA/C can anchor nuclear membrane proteins, regulate cellular transcription [22, 41], and mediate mechanical signaling to regulate gene expression [21, 42]. Since Emerin is the most downregulated NE protein anchoring to LaminA/C [43, 44], we suspect that Emerin is the protein that predominantly contributes to mechanotransduction from the nucleoskeleton during the progression of myopia. Both LaminA/C and Emerin function as important mechanosensors that transmit biomechanical signals from the cytoplasm to the nucleus [18, 45, 46].

Here, primary scleral fibroblasts were seeded on 50 kPa and 8 kPa substrates to simulate the mechanical environment of the scleral matrix in normal and myopic eyes, respectively. The results showed that soft substrate led to downregulation of LaminA/C, Emerin and collagen expression in scleral fibroblasts, which was basically consistent with the changes in the sclera of experimental myopia models. The ECM components are major factors determining tissue stiffness [47–49]. The interplay between scleral fibroblasts and the microenvironment results in the aberrant mechanotransduction. We performed immunofluorescence costaining and confirmed the interaction of LaminA/C with Emerin in scleral fibroblasts, especially on a stiff substrate. LaminA/C and Emerin jointly regulate the subcellular localization and transcriptional activity of the mechanosensitive transcription factor MKL1 [26], which is essential for the expression of downstream genes, such as actin, collagen, and vinculin [50, 51]. For this reason, we further conducted in vitro experiments to explore the roles of these mechanosensitive molecules in the progression of myopia as well as their potential interactions.

In this context, the downregulation of LaminA/C led to a redistribution of Emerin from the nuclear membrane to the cytoplasm in scleral fibroblasts, which was consistent with previous studies that reported that deletion or mutation of LaminA/C leads to increased Emerin mobility in cells [44, 52, 53]. We also found that decreased LaminA/C expression downregulated Emerin expression, while Emerin inhibition did not affect LaminA/C expression. The results indicated that lamina first acts as a mechanosensor to sense external stimuli and then as a mechanotransducer that converts this information into cellular responses. Furthermore, the results showed that LaminA/C and Emerin suppression resulted in decreased MKL1 expression and nuclear translocation, along with a reduction in COL1A1 levels in cell lysates and culture supernatants. Emerin stabilizes the end of F-actin and enhances actin aggregation [54], and the aggregation state of actin is an important factor that regulates the nuclear translocation of MKL1 [55, 56]. It has been suggested that Emerin can modulate MKL1 nuclear translocation and downstream transcription by regulating the polymerization of intranuclear actin [26]. Therefore, we hypothesized that mechanotransduction through LaminA/C, Emerin and MKL1 might play a critically important role in myopia-induced scleral remodeling.

The results of our experiments with MKL1 siRNA and MKL1 inhibitor CCG1423 revealed the suppression of COL1A1 transcription in HmSFs. In contrast, overexpression of MKL1 stimulated collagen synthesis and secretion. Gain- and loss-of-function experiments provided strong evidence that MKL1 plays an essential role in modulation of collagen expression during the development of myopia. Interestingly, MKL1 has previously been identified as a driver of fibrosis in several organs [50, 57, 58]. For example, MKL1 binds directly to the CTGF promoter to regulate CTGF transcription during renal fibrosis [59]. Our data show that MKL1 binds to the COL1A1 promoter to activate its transcription. It can be assumed that type I collagen in fibroblasts decreases due to downregulation of MKL1.

Notably, administration of CCG1423 to mice triggered the onset and progression of myopia, suggesting that MKL1 is required for ECM remodeling. We hypothesize that a decrease in MKL1 followed by substantial COL1A1 downregulation weakens the scleral framework, thereby facilitating increases in ocular elongation. In contrast, the expression of MKL1 suppressed the progression of myopia in LIM mice. Thus, expression of MKL1 in sclera may serve as a novel approach for preventing the progression of myopia. LaminA/C and Emerin are the major components of the nuclear lamina [53, 60, 61], hampering the direct intervention of LaminA/C or Emerin signaling in mice. Indeed, mutation and deregulation of LaminA/C and Emerin were found to affect the nuclear response, altering several downstream cellular processes, such as mitosis, chromatin organization, DNA replication-transcription, and nuclear structural integrity [62–64].

In summary, our current findings provide critical insights into the biomechanical molecular mechanisms underlying the progression of myopia. Scleral remodeling is a complex process that is regulated by chemical and mechanical cues from the surrounding ECM. Scleral fibroblasts respond to signals modulating the expression levels of collagen. The LaminA/C-Emerin-MKL1 axis identified here plays an essential role in the regulatory mechanisms between collagen synthesis and ECM remodeling. Future studies may further extend these findings by incorporating analyses of clinical samples. This study provides ideas and will hopefully lead to a rational decision to target this axis in the development of therapeutic strategies to prevent the progression of myopia.
