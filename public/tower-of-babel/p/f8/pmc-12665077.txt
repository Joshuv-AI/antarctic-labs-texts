Computationally Guided Design of a Soluble, Abundant and Functional AnfH Variant for the Expression of Fe‐Only Nitrogenase in Plant Mitochondria
Authors: Christina M Gregg, Barnabas Gall, Xueqin Wang, Shoko Okada, Trevor D Rapson, Robert S Allen, Ingrid Venables, Anu Mathew, Craig C Wood
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEngineering nitrogenase directly into crops is a long‐held aspiration in plant biotechnology. Of the three types, the Fe‐only nitrogenase is a promising engineering target, as it has a simpler maturation pathway than the MoFe‐ and VFe‐nitrogenases and does not require any heterometals for its cofactor. However, previously, we have reported that the obligate electron donor of the Fe‐only nitrogenase from A. vinelandii , AnfH, is mostly insoluble when expressed in plant mitochondria. Here, we employed computational methods, the Protein Repair One‐Stop Shop (PROSS) protein optimisation algorithm and Rosetta energy calculations, to design eight variants of AnfH with improved soluble expression. The amino acid substitutions chosen were predicted to lower the free energy of the native state of the protein. All eight AnfH variants, containing between 1 and 11 amino acid substitutions, were more soluble than wild‐type AnfH in plant mitochondria. Of these, three variants were isolated from N. benthamiana leaf, of which AnfH variant 6 (AnfH V6, T200A T228V E241H) had the best features, being approx. 90‐fold more abundant in the soluble fraction. Importantly, AnfH V6 was also functional in 15N2 reduction with A. vinelandii AnfDKG after [Fe4S4] cluster reconstitution. These results show that the computational design strategy used here is a powerful approach for engineering nitrogenase into plants and more broadly to plant synthetic biology and recombinant protein production.Keywords: AnfH, computational design, heterologous protein production, iron–sulfur, maturation, mitochondria, nitrogen fixation, nitrogenase, plant, solubility

Introduction

While synthetic nitrogen fertiliser is indispensable to our existing agricultural systems, it is also a significant contributor to climate change and pollution (Bodirsky et al. 2014; Menegat et al. 2022). Fertiliser production, which currently still relies on hydrogen generated from fossil fuels, is a major source of anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions. The transport of fertiliser to farms and its distribution further contribute to its carbon footprint. Additionally, excess reactive nitrogen species in the soil can be transformed to the greenhouse gas nitrous oxide by nitrifying and denitrifying bacteria. Another negative consequence of fertiliser use is nitrogen runoff into aquatic ecosystems and groundwater, which can lead to algal blooms, loss of biodiversity, and nitrate contamination (Bijay and Craswell 2021; Smith and Schindler 2009; Xia et al. 2020). Thus, there is a strong incentive to reduce global usage of nitrogen fertiliser. One potential solution is to engineer nitrogen‐fixing plants that can use gaseous N2 from the atmosphere rather than relying on reactive nitrogen species in the soil (Beatty and Good 2011). This could be achieved by expressing the microbial enzyme nitrogenase in plant cells (Merrick and Dixon 1984).

Nitrogenase is the only enzyme capable of catalysing the conversion of N2 gas into ammonia. The two‐component enzyme consists of the catalytic unit, dinitrogenase and the obligate electron donor, dinitrogenase reductase, also referred to as the Fe protein. There are three types of nitrogenase, each named after the metals present in the active site cofactor of its catalytic unit: MoFe nitrogenase (NifDK), which contains a [MoFe7S9C]‐homocitrate cluster (FeMo‐co) (Lancaster et al. 2011; Spatzal et al. 2011); VFe nitrogenase (VnfDKG), which contains a [VFe7S8C(CO3)2−]‐homocitrate cluster (VeFe‐co) (Sippel and Einsle 2017); and Fe‐only nitrogenase (AnfDKG), which contains a [Fe8S9C]‐homocitrate cluster (FeFe‐co) (Trncik et al. 2023). In addition to the active site cofactor, each dinitrogenase contains a [Fe8S7] cluster, named P‐cluster, which mediates electron transfer from the electron donor to the cofactor (Danyal et al. 2011; Kirn and Rees 1992). The Fe protein, also referred to as NifH, VnfH or AnfH, respective to their dinitrogenase partner, contains a [Fe4S4] cluster at its homodimer interface from which it transfers an electron to the P‐cluster during a transient interaction with dinitrogenase in an ATP‐dependent process (Georgiadis et al. 1992; Hageman and Burris 1978; Peters et al. 1995).

When engineering nitrogenase into plants, a major challenge is the assembly and stability of the nitrogenase iron–sulfur (FeS) clusters. These clusters are oxygen‐sensitive, and their assembly requires a complex biosynthetic machinery consisting of multiple proteins (Burén et al. 2020). To address this problem, nitrogenase proteins have been targeted to mitochondria or chloroplasts (Allen et al. 2020, 2017; Baysal et al. 2022; Burén et al. 2017; Eseverri et al. 2020; He et al. 2022; Ivleva et al. 2016; Jiang et al. 2022, 2021; Johnston et al. 2023) as the concentration of free O2 is lower in respiring mitochondria (Rozhin et al. 2018), or potentially in non‐photosynthesising chloroplasts or plastids (Aznar‐Moreno et al. 2021; Burén and Rubio 2017; Dixon et al. 1997). Both organelles also contain endogenous machineries for FeS cluster assembly that could aid in FeS cluster biosynthesis on nitrogenase proteins (Vicente and Dean 2017).

Of the three nitrogenase types, previous plant engineering efforts have focussed on either the MoFe‐ or the Fe‐only nitrogenase. The MoFe nitrogenase has the highest specific activity in nitrogen reduction compared to the VFe or Fe‐only nitrogenase (Trncik et al. 2022), and its biosynthetic requirements have been studied most extensively (Burén et al. 2020). An advantage of the Fe‐only nitrogenase, however, is that it does not require a hetero‐metal for its cofactor, as it is currently unknown whether plant organelles contain sufficient levels of molybdenum or vanadium to meet the requirements of a MoFe‐ or VFe‐nitrogenase, respectively. The Fe‐only nitrogenase also needs relatively few accessory proteins for cluster assembly and may thus be an easier target for engineering nitrogen‐fixing plants (Yang et al. 2014). Previously, we reported that the structural components of the Fe‐only nitrogenase from A. vinelandii , AnfDKG and AnfH, can be expressed in plant mitochondria and showed that the subunits of the catalytic unit form a complex (Johnston et al. 2023).

Despite being successfully expressed in the plant mitochondrial matrix, AnfH was only partially soluble (Johnston et al. 2023). This problem had also been previously identified for other nitrogenase proteins when expressed in eukaryotes (Arragain et al. 2017; Burén et al. 2017; Dobrzyńska et al. 2024; Okada et al. 2020). For example, A. vinelandii NifH was extremely insoluble when targeted to the mitochondrial matrix of Nicotiana benthamiana (Jiang et al. 2021). In the case of NifH, this was overcome by screening NifH proteins from a variety of diazotrophic organisms to find a soluble variant, which was then used for functional studies. The same approach was also used to address solubility issues for the maturation proteins A. vinelandii NifEN and A. vinelandii NifB when expressed in S. cerevisiae and N. benthamiana, respectively (Burén et al. 2019; Dobrzyńska et al. 2024; He et al. 2022).

This report takes a different approach, employing a computational and rational protein design to improve a known variant rather than relying on large‐scale variant screening. Specifically, we addressed the solubility issues of AnfH (Johnston et al. 2023) and introduced minimal amino acid changes, thereby substantially enhancing its soluble expression in plant mitochondria. One engineered variant, containing only three amino acid substitutions, was found to be active when isolated from plant mitochondria after reconstitution with [Fe4S4] clusters. This targeted strategy could be applied to other problematic nitrogenase proteins, representing a critical step toward engineering nitrogen fixation into plants.

Results

Design of AnfH Variants That Are More Soluble in Plant Mitochondria

Given the poor solubility of AnfH when expressed in plant mitochondria, we sought to use a computational approach to design variants with improved soluble expression. The Protein Repair One‐Stop Shop (PROSS) algorithm (Goldenzweig et al. 2016; Weinstein et al. 2021) is designed to generate variants that have a lower free energy in their native state compared to the wild‐type protein and are thus predicted to be more stable and soluble when expressed heterologously. The selection of amino acid substitutions is further based on a multiple sequence alignment (MSA), and the algorithm will avoid substitutions that are not naturally occurring within the protein sequence space. Here, we employed this algorithm to generate variants of A. vinelandii AnfH better suited for expression in plant mitochondria (Figure 1A). The crystal structure of A. vinelandii AnfH (PDB 7QQA) (Trncik et al. 2022) was used as an input model, and residues for the P‐loop, switch I, switch II, and cysteines involved in [Fe4S4] cluster binding were excluded from the list of residues allowed for substitution. Furthermore, 112 residues were manually selected and excluded from substitution (Table S1).

Of the nine variants suggested by PROSS (Table S2, MSA quality shown in Figure S1), five were tested for solubility when targeted to plant mitochondria. These contained 11, 10, 9, 6 and 5 amino acid substitutions, respectively (AnfH V1–V5, Table 1, Figure 1B). We chose the designs that contained the smallest number of amino acid substitutions as we assumed that a smaller number of substitutions would less likely interfere with the function of AnfH. All of the AnfH variants contained the pFAγ51 mitochondrial targeting peptide (Okada et al. 2020) and a HA‐tag at the N‐terminus (pFAγ51::HA::AnfH, SN641‐ SN645, Table S3). The AnfH variants were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaf and the solubility of the AnfH proteins was analysed by western blot. As previously observed (Johnston et al. 2023), wild‐type AnfH (AnfH wt, SN651, Table S3) was only partially soluble when targeted to plant mitochondria. In contrast, all computationally generated AnfH variants were highly soluble (Figure 1C, Table S4).

Given these encouraging results, we investigated whether the number of amino acid substitutions could be further reduced while retaining an improved soluble expression in plant mitochondria. To select which substitutions should be retained or removed, respectively, a more detailed analysis of the predicted free energy changes, ΔΔG, relating to each amino acid substitution was carried out using the energy function of the Rosetta molecular modelling suite (Alford et al. 2017; Leaver‐Fay et al. 2011; Lyskov et al. 2013). This analysis revealed that the N112L substitution was only marginally stabilising, i.e., ΔΔG = −0.2 kcal·mol−1 compared to AnfH wt. For these reasons, the N112L amino acid substitution was omitted when designing further variants. T200A, T228V, E234H and G241R were more stabilising, i.e., ΔΔG = −4.4 kcal·mol−1, ΔΔG = −5.6 kcal·mol−1, ΔΔG = −2.8 kcal·mol−1 and ΔΔG = −3.9 kcal·mol−1, respectively. Given that E234H and G241R were more drastic changes regarding amino acid charge and size, these variations were tested separately. Therefore, two more AnfH variants were generated, AnfHV6 and AnfH V7 (SN798 and SN711, Table S3), containing 3 amino acid substitutions each (Table 1).

The solubility of the two variants was tested when targeted to plant mitochondria using the same construct design (pFAγ51::HA::AnfH V6, V7) as described for the other variants (Figure 1D). Both AnfH V6 and AnfH V7 were more soluble when targeted to plant mitochondria than AnfH wt. Overall, the computational design strategy was successful and surprisingly only three amino acid substitutions were sufficient to substantially enhance the soluble expression of AnfH.

To determine whether these AnfH variants were also naturally occurring, a protein BLAST search (Altschul et al. 1990, 1997) was carried out using the amino acid sequence of AnfH V6 as input. No natural variants with the sequence of AnfH V6 were found (Figure S3). We also investigated the variation of the amino acids at positions 200, 228, 234 and 241, both within the smaller set of sequences from the BLAST result (100 sequences, lowest sequence identity = 86%) and within the InterPro family ‘nitrogenase Fe protein’ (IPR005977, 4513 sequences), which contains NifH, VnfH and AnfH sequences. As shown in the sequence logo (Figure S4), there was no variation for Thr200 within the closely related sequences of the smaller BLAST set, but alanine was the most common residue in the larger set of the ‘nitrogenase Fe protein’ family. The other three positions were more variable; both the wildtype and the variant amino acids were present at position 228, 234 and 241 within the BLAST set.

Purification of AnfH Variants From Plant Mitochondria

Currently there is no method that allows an in vivo characterisation of AnfH activity in plants. For this reason, the functional characterisation of AnfH expressed in plants necessitates a purification step. Of the variants that were tested for solubility, we chose AnfH V6 and AnfH V7 for purification and further analysis, as these had the fewest amino acid substitutions.

AnfH wt, AnfH V6 and AnfH V7 were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaf, containing a cytochrome oxidase IV (CoxIV) mitochondrial targeting peptide from S. cerevisiae (Burén et al. 2017; Köhler et al. 1997) and a TwinStrep‐tag for purification, which were translationally fused at the N‐terminus of each AnfH (CoxIV::TwinStrep::AnfH, SN771 for AnfH wt, SN709 for AnfH V6; SN710 for AnfH V7, Table S3). All AnfH constructs were co‐expressed with A. vinelandii NifS and NifU, which are thought to be involved in [Fe4S4] cluster maturation of NifH and may play an equivalent role for AnfH maturation (Dos Santos et al. 2004; Jacobson et al. 1989; Kennedy and Dean 1992). NifM was not included since, in contrast to NifH, AnfH does not require NifM for its maturation (Yang et al. 2014). From here on, purified proteins from N. benthamiana will be referred to as NbAnfH wt, NbAnfH V6 etc., to indicate their expression host.

When we attempted to purify NbAnfH wt expressed in N. benthamiana leaf mitochondria, yields were low (approx. 0.4 mg·kg−1 leaf) and the purity was only approx. 22% (Figure 2, densitometric analysis in Figure S5). In contrast, NbAnfH V6 and NbAnfH V7 were successfully purified from N. benthamiana leaf mitochondria with yields of 8–13 mg·kg−1 leaf and 4–6 mg·kg−1 leaf, respectively. The purity of the eluates was also higher, 61% and 49%, respectively (Figure 2, densitometric analysis in Figure S5). Overall, this corresponds to an approximately 90‐fold improvement in AnfH V6 abundance.

Based on these promising results, we wanted to investigate whether the number of amino acid substitutions could be further reduced. Another AnfH variant, AnfH V8, was generated containing only one amino acid substitution, T228V, and a construct was designed for purification (CoxIV::TwinStrep::AnfH‐V8, SN778, Table S3). While the soluble expression and abundance of this variant were improved compared to NbAnfH wt, NbAnfH V8 was less abundant compared to NbAnfH V6 and NbAnfH V7, with a yield of approx. 4.7 mg·kg1 leaf and 28% purity (Figure 2, densitometric analysis in Figure S5). On the basis of these results, we concluded that NbAnfH V6 and NbAnfH V7 were the best candidates for functional testing, as they were more abundant than NbAnfH V8.

Reconstituted NbAnfH V6 Is Active in Acetylene Reduction with NifDK

To determine whether NbAnfH V6 and NbAnfH V7 were active after purification from plant mitochondria an acetylene reduction assay (ARA) was performed. In this assay, we used NifDK purified from A. vinelandii , AvNifDK, as the catalytic unit, as it has comparatively robust properties and has previously been proven to be functional in ARAs with ScAnfH isolated from S. cerevisiae (López‐Torrejón et al. 2021). However, we were not able to detect any activity for NbAnfH V6 and NbAnfH V7 ‘as isolated’ from plant mitochondria. Since the concentrated eluates from the purification were mostly colourless instead of having the typical brown colour associated with FeS cluster proteins (Figure S6), we speculated that one possible explanation for the lack of activity of the NbAnfH variants could be the absence of the [Fe4S4] cluster in NbAnfH.

To assess whether NbAnfH activity could be restored upon insertion of the [Fe4S4] cluster, a NifU reconstitution procedure was carried out. While the mechanism of the [Fe4S4] cluster donation to Nif and Anf proteins in diazotrophs is not completely understood, it has been shown that apoNifH can be activated by incubation with NifU in vitro (Dos Santos et al. 2004). We carried out a NifU reconstitution procedure to test whether NbAnfH V6 and NbAnfH V7 could be activated. The NifU used for this procedure was A. vinelandii NifU purified from E. coli, EcNifU, (Figure S7) which was chemically reconstituted with Fe2+ and S2− to increase cluster loading. Prior to the AnfH reconstitution with EcNifU, dithionite (DTH) in the AnfH samples was removed as DTH has been shown to destabilise the [Fe4S4] clusters of NifU (Smith et al. 2005). Encouragingly, the [Fe4S4] cluster reconstitution by EcNifU was successful in activating NbAnfH V6 (Figure 3A). This was observed in several experiments; however, some variation regarding the activity levels was observed (Figure S8). In contrast, reconstituted NbAnfH V7 was not active or had extremely low activity (1% of the positive control). In summary, NbAnfH V6 was isolated from plant mitochondria as apo‐AnfH, as it was capable of being reconstituted with [Fe4S4] clusters from EcNifU.

Reconstituted AnfH V6 Is Active in 15N2 Reduction With AnfDKG

Finally, it was important to confirm that the NbAnfH variants would also catalyse nitrogen fixation with A. vinelandii AnfDKG, AvAnfDKG, its native dinitrogenase partner protein. We performed a 15N2 reduction assay of NbAnfH V6 and NbAnfH V7 after EcNifU reconstitution, with AvAnfDKG. Crucially, the activity of reconstituted NbAnfH V6 was comparable to that of AvAnfH (Figure 3B). The variant AnfH V7 was also active for nitrogen fixation with AnfDKG, where an activity of approximately 39% of AvAnfH was measured. We also performed a 15N2 reduction assay with NbAnfH wt that was isolated from N. benthamiana. However, due to the low yield of NbAnfH wt from plant purifications, we were only able to perform one experiment, and NbAnfH wt was not active after reconstitution with EcNifU. For this experiment, NbAnfH wt from several purifications was combined. We observed protein precipitation during the assay setup for NbAnfH wt, which could either originate from the contaminating proteins or from NbAnfH wt itself. The latter would explain why NbAnfH wt could not be reconstituted, and no activity was observed. In summary, the 15N2 reduction assay confirmed that variant NbAnfH V6, containing three amino acid substitutions (Figure 3C–F), was able to donate electrons to AvAnfDKG to reduce N2 to ammonia. The reconstitution experiments showed that variant NbAnfH V6 was isolated from plants in the apo‐state, namely correctly folded and able to accept [Fe4S4] clusters from EcNifU.

Discussion

Here, we present a new strategy to improve bacterial nitrogenase proteins for successful expression in plant mitochondria. Specifically, we used a computationally guided design approach to generate a novel AnfH variant, containing only three amino acid changes, that is more soluble and abundant in plant mitochondria and functional in nitrogen reduction with AnfDKG. This approach leverages well‐studied enzyme systems, such as those from A. vinelandii , as the basis for engineering nitrogenase into plants, rather than searching for naturally occurring variants from non‐model diazotrophs that are not as well characterised (Arragain et al. 2017; Burén et al. 2017, 2019; Dobrzyńska et al. 2024; He et al. 2022; Jiang et al. 2021). This approach could be particularly well suited for cases where there is scarce phylogenetic diversity. The Fe‐only nitrogenase is less common and only found in approximately 5%–10% of genomes that contain a MoFe nitrogenase (Boyd and Peters 2013; Harwood 2020; Koirala and Brözel 2021; Pi et al. 2022). This limits the number of potential candidates when testing naturally occurring variants for expression in plants. Moreover, screens of natural variants for their performance in plants have shown that proteins from thermophilic organisms seem to be better suited for soluble expression in plants and, to our knowledge, the Fe‐only nitrogenase is not present in thermophilic diazotrophs. The design of more stable and soluble proteins by lowering their free energy may mimic the higher stability characteristics of thermophilic proteins, when natural equivalents are potentially unavailable. Using targeted amino acid changes is thus a valuable addition to the nitrogenase engineering toolkit, particularly for the less diverse Fe‐only nitrogenase.

The solubility of heterologously expressed proteins is dependent on many factors, such as the expression host, the expression temperature, the requirement for specific chaperons or maturation proteins, and the intrinsic properties of the protein itself. In this study, we optimised AnfH for heterologous expression by improving its predicted stability. How exactly the amino acid substitutions contribute to the improved stability of AnfH, and how lowering the free energy of the native state may affect the folding/unfolding pathways, is beyond the scope of the current study. However, in practical terms, the improved abundance in the soluble fraction may be due to reducing the accumulation of misfolded protein in the insoluble fraction and by lowering the accessibility of the protein to proteolytic degradation upon unfolding. Remarkably, only one amino acid substitution, T228V, led to a substantial improvement in the soluble expression of AnfH. Overall, however, we found that the optimal balance between minimising amino acid substitutions and improved soluble expression and abundance was provided by three amino acid substitutions, i.e., AnfH V6 was functional while being approx. 90‐fold more abundant than AnfH wt.

Here, we have used PROSS and Rosetta to improve the soluble expression of AnfH. Nonetheless, it should be noted that other software tools for enhancing protein solubility and stability, such as CamSol (Oeller et al. 2023), Aggrescan (Kuriata et al. 2019) or FireProt (Musil et al. 2023), are available and their use might have resulted in similar enhancements to the soluble expression of AnfH. Recently, also deep learning‐based techniques have emerged, capable of predicting sequences for a desired protein fold (ProteinMPNN), (Dauparas et al. 2022), which could be applied to future attempts of improving the soluble expression of proteins (Schmidt et al. 2024). Regarding the choice of amino acid substitutions, PROSS suggested changes to Lys69 and Asn112. In retrospect, however, we should have likely excluded these residues from the possible substitutions as these have been implicated in the interaction between AnfH and AnfG (Schmidt et al. 2024; Trncik et al. 2022).

Of the two variants that were tested for function after in vitro reconstitution with EcNifU, NbAnfH V6 was more active than NbAnfH V7. This was remarkable as they only differed by one amino acid substitution, i.e., E234H and G241R, respectively, both of which are distal to the AnfDKG interaction interface. While substitutions in distal sites can affect function in unexpected ways (Wilding et al. 2019), another possible explanation for the low activity of NbAnfH V7 could be that the [Fe4S4]‐cluster reconstitution procedure with EcNifU was not as successful for NbAnfH V7. NbAnfH wt isolated from plant mitochondria was not reconstitutable with NifU. Possible reasons for the unsuccessful reconstitution of AnfH wt with NifU could be an incorrect protein fold, a low stability of AnfH under the buffer conditions tested and/or the low purity of the sample. In summary, these experiments showed that the amino acid substitutions of AnfH V6, T200A, T228V and E234H, do not interfere with in vitro function and AnfH V6 has superior properties related to soluble expression and stability compared to both AnfH wt and AnfH V7.

NbAnfH V6 was not active as‐isolated when purified from plants, and this lack of activity is likely due to the lack of the [Fe4S4] cluster on the enzyme. These results differ from those observed in S. cerevisiae , from which AnfH wt was isolated in an active form containing [Fe4S4] clusters (López‐Torrejón et al. 2021). This is in line with other studies, where FeS cluster assembly on NifB or NifH was successful in S. cerevisiae, but the same protein accumulated with either no or only a small fraction of FeS clusters in plant mitochondria (Baysal et al. 2022; Burén et al. 2019; Jiang et al. 2022, 2021).

When comparing activity levels of proteins expressed in plant mitochondria, however, it is noteworthy that a NifH protein, i.e. NifH from H. thermophilus, was previously isolated with 2%–4% activity, containing a small amount of [Fe4S4] clusters (Baysal et al. 2022; Jiang et al. 2021) while NbAnfH V6 was not active as‐isolated. There are several possible explanations for the differences observed. Firstly, due to the higher oxygen sensitivity of AnfH compared to NifH (Chisnell et al. 1988), the [Fe4S4] cluster of AnfH could be damaged inside plant mitochondria or it could be degraded during the purification procedure. Secondly, AnfH V6 might never have accumulated [Fe4S4] clusters inside plant mitochondria, despite coexpression of NifS and NifU. It should be noted that it is currently unknown how cluster assembly proceeded for H. thermophilus NifH. It is unlikely that NifS and NifU were actively involved in this process, as no protein accumulation of NifSU was detected in rice callus, despite the presence of mRNA (Baysal et al. 2022). Thus, the endogenous iron‐sulfur cluster machinery of plant mitochondria was likely involved in the assembly of the [Fe4S4] cluster on H. thermophilus NifH. Likewise, in mitochondria of S. cerevisiae, NifS and NifU were not required for the expression of a functional AnfH or NifH (López‐Torrejón et al. 2016, 2021) and in E. coli IscS and IscU are possibly sufficient to assemble the [Fe4S4] cluster on NifH (Dos Santos et al. 2007; Howard et al. 1986; Solomon et al. 2023; Zheng et al. 1998), demonstrating that maturation proteins other than NifSU can donate [Fe4S4] clusters to a NifH or AnfH protein. It is unclear then why neither NifSU nor the endogenous FeS cluster assembly machinery was able to donate a [Fe4S4] cluster to AnfH V6 in plant mitochondria. Thirdly, the [Fe4S4] cluster maturation on AnfH may require additional proteins. For example, AnfU has been proposed to be involved in the maturation of the Fe‐only nitrogenase (Setubal et al. 2009). Lastly, there is a chance that the amino acid substitutions of AnfH V6 affected the maturation of the [Fe4S4] cluster in vivo. While we were able to donate a [Fe4S4] cluster from NifU to AnfH in vitro, we cannot rule out that the amino acid substitutions of AnfH V6 could affect the [Fe4S4] cluster binding affinity and/or the interaction of AnfH V6 with other assembly proteins. Further studies will need to investigate the [Fe4S4] maturation requirements of AnfH and, more broadly, focus on improving FeS cluster loading on Nif and Anf proteins, which remains a key challenge to the expression of an active nitrogenase in plants.

This study has shown that a computationally guided design approach can suggest relatively few changes to a critical nitrogenase protein, resulting in improved soluble expression and abundance in plants, while retaining function. This approach can be applied more broadly to problematic proteins of nitrogenase pathways from well‐studied enzymes so that they can be successfully expressed in plants. This study, like others, has also shown that the loading of FeS clusters onto nitrogenase proteins remains a key challenge for the successful expression of an active enzyme in plants.

Materials and Methods

Bioinformatic Methods

PROSS (Goldenzweig et al. 2016; Weinstein et al. 2021) was used to generate the initial protein designs. The crystal structure of A. vinelandii AnfH (PDB 7QQA) (Trncik et al. 2022) was used as input model and 137 residues were excluded from amino acid substitutions (Table S1). PROSS was run with the standard settings for the MSA. The ROSIE webserver (Lyskov et al. 2013) was used to calculate the Rosetta energy values. Sequence logos were generated with Seq2Logo (Thomsen and Nielsen 2012) without clustering and pseudo count correction. The sequence alignment of the InterPro family ‘nitrogenase Fe protein’ (IPR005977) was generated using MAFFT (Katoh et al. 2002, 2017). AlphaFold 3 (Abramson et al. 2024) was used to generate a structural model of AnfH V6 containing ADP and Mg2+.

Molecular Cloning and Construct Design

Vectors for plant expression were assembled using the GoldenGate cloning method (Weber et al. 2011) and parts were based on Engler et al. (Engler et al. 2014). Sequences for open reading frames and other non‐standard parts were plant codon‐optimised (N. benthamiana or Arabidopsis thaliana ) and ordered from Twist Bioscience or Geneart. Generally, a Cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) enhanced 35S promoter and 35S terminator were used. For AnfH wt, an additional construct was prepared using a combination of three different termination signals based on Diamos et al. (Diamos and Mason 2018). These were the intronless tobacco extensin terminator (EU), the 35S terminator and a shortened Rb7 matrix attachment region (MAR) (Δ1‐730) (modMARS). Mitochondrial targeting peptides and tags used for AnfH constructs are described in the main text. NifS contained a chaperonin HSP60 (CPN60) mitochondrial targeting peptide from A. thaliana (Prasad and Stewart 1992), CPN60::NifS, and NifU contained the F0‐ATPase subunit 9 (Su9) from Neurospora crassa mitochondrial targeting peptide, Su9::NifU (Burén et al. 2017; Jiang et al. 2022). NifS and NifU were expressed from a multigene vector (SL133, Tables S3 and S5). A sequence list of all parts and expression vectors can be found in Dataset S1.

Growth and Transient Transformation of N. Benthamiana

N. benthamiana plants were grown in a growth chamber (Conviron) at 24°C under a 16:8 h light: dark cycle with light provided by a combination of cool white fluorescent lamps and incandescent bulbs. Plants were grown in vermiculite (grade 3, Ausperl) and watered with 10 mL · L−1 HyperGrow GP nutrient solution. The plants were agroinfiltrated when they were 5 weeks old.

Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 containing SN/SL vectors or the p19 vector were grown at 28°C in Luria‐Bertani (LB) broth supplemented with 50 mg·L−1 rifampicin, 50 mg·L−1 gentamycin and the corresponding antibiotic according to the selectable marker on the vector (50 mg·mL−1 carbenicillin or 50 mg·L−1 kanamycin). After 20 h, acetosyringone was added to the culture to a final concentration of 100 μM and the culture was incubated for another 2 h at 28°C and 200 rpm. The culture was then centrifuged at 5000 × g for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in a solution containing 10 mM MES pH 5.7, 5 mM MgCl2 and 100 μM acetosyringone (infiltration buffer), after which the optical density at a wavelength of 600 nm, OD600, was measured. A volume of each culture, including the culture containing the viral‐suppressor construct p19, required to reach a final concentration of OD600 = 0.10, was added to a fresh tube. A. tumefaciens were infiltrated into the leaves of N. benthamiana plants using a 1 mL plastic syringe. Leaves or leaf spots were harvested 4 days after infiltration. Large scale infiltrations were harvested in the dark, i.e., after a prolonged dark period of 16 h, under dim green light.

Solubility Analysis of AnfH Variants Expressed in the Mitochondria of N. Benthamiana

The solubility analysis was carried out as previously described (Okada et al. 2020) with minor changes. Frozen leaf disks were ground in 300 μL solubility buffer (5% (v/v) glycerol, 150 mM NaCl, 100 mM Tris pH 8.0, 1% (w/v) 1‐ethenylpyrrolidin‐2‐one (PVP), 0.2 mM phenylmethanesulfonylfluoride (PMSF), 10 μM leupeptin). From this suspension, 40 μL was taken as the ‘total’ sample, and 60 μL was used for further processing. The latter was then centrifuged for 10 min at 16 000 × g at 10°C. 40 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a new microcentrifuge tube, referred to as the ‘soluble’ fraction. Subsequent sodium dodecyl sulfate—polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and western blotting were carried out as previously described (Allen et al. 2020).

Purification of NbAnfH Variants From N. Benthamiana

Up to 150 g of frozen leaf tissue expressing NbAnfH wt, NbAnfH V6, NbAnfH V7 or NbAnfH V8 was ground in lysis buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.6, 200 mM NaCl, 10% v/v glycerol, 2 mM sodium dithionite (DTH), 0.2 mM PMSF, 10 μM leupeptin) with a NutriBullet blender, filtered through miracloth (Merck Millipore) and centrifuged at 142 400 × g for 1 h at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was loaded onto a 5 mL Strep‐TactinXT 4Flow column (IBA Life Sciences) at 2.5 mL·min−1. After loading of the supernatant, the column was washed with 10 column volumes (CVs) of wash buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.6, 200 mM NaCl, 10% v/v glycerol, 2 mM DTH), after which the bound protein was eluted with wash buffer containing 50 mM biotin. The protein solution was concentrated using an AmiconUltra centrifugal filter (30 kDa MWCO) and stored in liquid nitrogen until use.

Growth of A. vinelandii

A. vinelandii strains DJ2102 and DJ2241 were kindly donated by Dr. Dennis Dean (Virginia Tech, Blacksburg). From DMSO stocks, cells were streaked out onto plates containing Burke's medium (Strandberg and Wilson 1968) supplemented with 13 mM ammonium acetate as nitrogen source (BN medium). The cells were transferred to liquid BN medium (100 mL in 250 mL shake flask) and grown at 30°C and 250 rpm overnight. The next morning, a new culture (250 mL in 1 L shake flask) was set up with an initial optical density at 600 nm, OD600 of 0.1. In the evening, 10 L medium were inoculated with 250 m of the pre‐culture. This culture was grown in a bioreactor (Infors, Labfors‐5) with the following parameters. The dissolved oxygen level, pO2, was controlled by a cascade with variation in stirring speed and air flow rate. The setpoints were as follows: pO2 = 25% (min: 15%, max: 100%), stirring speed = 125 rpm (min: 125 rpm, max: 400 rpm), flow rate = 10 L·min−1 (min: 1 L·min−1, max: 10 L·min−1).

DJ2102 was grown in BN medium with a buffer exchange to Burke's medium with no nitrogen (B medium) similarly to previously described. For the 10 L culture, additional Fe citrate was added to a final concentration of 25 mM (Jimenez‐Vicente et al. 2018). Approximately 16 h after starting the culture, the culture was centrifuged at 7187 × g for 20 min and then resuspended in 10 L B medium in the bioreactor. Cells were harvested 3–4 h after the cells had been resuspended in B medium by centrifugation at 7187 × g for 20 min. The cells were frozen in liquid N2 and stored at −80°C until further use.

DJ2241 was grown in B medium supplemented with 1 mM urea, as previously described (Perez‐Gonzalez et al. 2021). Cells were derepressed for nitrogen fixation by depletion of urea. Cells were harvested approx. 16 h after inoculation and harvested, frozen and stored as described for DJ2102.

Purification of AvAnfDKG, AvAnfH and AvNifDK From A. vinelandii

AvAnfDKG and AvAnfH were purified from DJ2241. AvAnfDKG contained a streptag for purification at the C‐terminus of AnfD. The purification was carried out in a COY anaerobic chamber in a 97%–98% N2/2%–3% H2 atmosphere. Cells were resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 10 μM leupeptin, 0.2 mM PMSF, 5 mM DTH) and sonicated for 3 × 3 min (Hielscher sonicator UP200Ht) at 50% cycle and 50% amplitude. The suspension was centrifuged at 142 400 × g in a polycarbonate bottle with cap assembly (Beckman Coulter). Using an ÄKTA pure instrument, the supernatant was loaded onto a Capto Q column (Cytiva) equilibrated in 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 2 mM DTH. The column was washed with 250 mL of the same buffer. The proteins were eluted with a linear NaCl gradient, consisting of two steps: a steep gradient increase to 150 mM NaCl with 35 mL buffer and then a shallower gradient increase to 400 mM NaCl with 250 mL buffer. The fractions were analysed by SDS‐PAGE. AvAnfH eluted at approx. 250 mM NaCl and AvAnfDKG eluted at approx. 350 mM NaCl.

The fractions containing AvAnfDKG were then loaded onto a Strep‐Tactin XT column (IBA Lifesciences) equilibrated in 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTH using a peristaltic pump. The column was washed with 10 column volumes (CVs) and AvAnfDKG was eluted with 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTH, 50 mM biotin. AvAnfDKG was concentrated using a centrifugal filter (Amicon Ultra, 50 kDa MWCO), and then loaded onto a PD‐10 desalting column equilibrated in 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 2 mM DTH to remove biotin. If required, AvAnfDKG was further concentrated and then frozen in liquid N2.

Fractions containing AvAnfH were chosen based on a Coomassie gel, concentrated, and loaded onto a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 pg column equilibrated in 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 2 mM DTH. AvAnfH was eluted at approx. 80 mL. AnfH was then concentrated using a centrifugal filter (Amicon Ultra, 30 kDa MWCO) and frozen in liquid N2.

AvNifDK was purified from DJ2102 as previously described (Bilyj et al. 2024). Cells were lysed and AvNifDK was purified using the same procedure as described for DJ2241 except that the gradient for the Capto Q column was different. For the purification of Nif proteins, the gradient consisted of a steep gradient increase to 200 mM NaCl with 50 mL buffer, and then a shallower gradient increase to 500 mM NaCl with 300 mL buffer. The fractions were analysed by SDS‐PAGE. AvNifH eluted at approx. 380 mM NaCl and AvNifDK eluted at approx. 250 mM NaCl. AvNifDK was then purified using a Strep‐TactinXT column as described above for AvAnfDKG.

Molecular Cloning of A. vinelandii NifS and NifU and Heterologous Expression in E. coli

Vectors for expression in E. coli were assembled using the EcoFlex cloning system based on the GoldenGate method (Moore et al. 2016). Sequences for the open reading frames of A. vinelandii NifU and A. vinelandii NifS were codon optimised for E. coli and ordered from GeneArt and Twist, respectively. NifU contained a Twin‐Strep‐tag at the N‐terminus for purification. Both transcriptional units were constructed with a T7 promoter, TL5 ribosome binding site and L2U5H08 terminator. The vector backbones used were pTU1‐A‐RFP for NifS and pTU1‐B‐lacZ for NifU. Then, a multigene construct was created, containing both transcriptional units in a pTU2S‐a vector with a p15a origin of replication. The construct was named EFL2‐30.

For expression, EFL2‐30 was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3). From a single colony, an overnight culture was grown in 100 mL LB in a shaking flask at 37°C. The next morning, 50 mL of the overnight culture was transferred to 2 L modified terrific broth medium (mTB) in 2 L Schott bottles with 35 μg·mL−1 chloramphenicol. The culture was stirred with a magnetic stirrer at room temperature. When the optical density, OD600, reached 0.6 ± 0.1, expression was induced with 0.1 mM isopropyl β‐d‐thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and the culture was supplemented with 2 mM (NH4)2Fe(SO4)2 and 0.5 mM cysteine. After induction, the lids of the bottles were closed to transition to anaerobic growth. After 16 h, the culture was quickly harvested by centrifugation at 15 000 × g for 10 min. The pellets were frozen in liquid N2 and stored at −80°C until further use.

Purification of EcNifU From E. coli

The purification of EcNifU was performed in a COY anaerobic chamber in a 97%–98% N2/2%–3% H2 atmosphere with an ÄKTA pure instrument. The cell pellet was resuspended in 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 200 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 2 mM TCEP (buffer A) with 10 μM leupeptin, 0.2 mM PMSF and 25 mg/L lysozyme. The suspension was sonicated for 3 × 3 min (Hielscher sonicator UP200Ht) at 50% cycle and 50% amplitude, and then centrifuged at 142 400 × g in a polycarbonate bottle with cap assembly (Beckman Coulter). The supernatant was loaded onto a Strep‐TactinXT (IBA) column equilibrated in buffer A, washed with 10 CVs buffer A and then eluted in buffer A with 50 mM biotin. The eluate was concentrated using an Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter (30 MWCO) and frozen in liquid nitrogen until further use.

Chemical Reconstitution of EcNifU

EcNifU was chemically reconstituted using a similar procedure as previously described (López‐Torrejón et al. 2016; Marengo et al. 2021). EcNifU was diluted in 25 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) to a concentration of 1–5 mg·mL−1. Then, a solution of 40 mM (NH4)2Fe(SO4)2 was added to a final concentration of 2.5 mM. Then 40 mM Na2S was added to a final concentration of 0.5 mM with gentle constant mixing. A colour change was observed within a few minutes of addition of Fe2+ and S2−. The reaction was incubated overnight in the anaerobic chamber at approximately 28°C. The next day, the buffer was exchanged to Mg‐ATP buffer using a MiniTrap Sephadex G25 column to remove excess Fe2+ and S2−. Mg‐ATP buffer was prepared by dissolving 10 mM MgCl2 and 2 mM ATP in 25 mM Tris pH 7.4, which was followed by a pH adjustment by addition of 1/10 volume 1 M NaOH, resulting in pH 7.8. The protein solution was concentrated using an AmiconUltra centrifugal filter (30 kDa MWCO).

Reconstitution of AnfH Proteins With EcNifU

Buffers for the reconstitution were prepared as follows: Prior to use, all buffers and solutions used were tested for O2 contamination using electrochemically reduced methyl viologen. Mg‐ATP buffer was prepared by dissolving 10 mM MgCl2 and 5 mM ATP in 25 mM MOPS pH 7.0, which was followed by a pH adjustment by addition of 1/50 volume 1 M NaOH, resulting in pH 7.7.

To remove interfering compounds such as DTH and biotin, the elution buffer of AnfH was exchanged to Mg‐ATP buffer using a centrifugal filter (Amicon Ultra, 10 kDa or 30 kDa MWCO). This procedure was carried out twice and the minimum dilution achieved for DTH and biotin was 200‐fold. Chemically reconstituted EcNifU was added to the different AnfH proteins at a ratio of 2:1 and incubated overnight at approximately 28°C.

Acetylene Reduction Assay

The acetylene reduction assay (ARA) was carried out using 4 μM AnfH and 0.2 μM AvNifDK, a molar ratio of 20:1 AnfH:AvNifDK. If EcNifU was present, it was added as part of the AnfH‐NifU reconstitution mixture at a final concentration of 8 μM, a ratio of 40:20:1 EcNifU:AnfH:AvNifDK. Protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Smith et al. 1985). The concentration of AnfH purified from N. benthamiana was adjusted based on a densitometric analysis of the Coomassie gel as it contained contaminating plant proteins (Figure S5).

The assays were set up in a reaction volume of 370 μL in 3.7 mL exetainer vials (Labco). The final concentrations of reducing agent and ATP regeneration mixture were 6 mM DTH, 10 mM MgCl2, 5 mM ATP, 16 mM phosphocreatine, and 0.1 mg·mL−1 phosphocreatine kinase from rabbit muscle. The reaction was started by addition of 1 mL acetylene gas (27% v/v) to the headspace. The acetylene gas was instrument grade (Coregas) and stored in a serum septa bottle containing buffer with 10 mM DTH to minimise potential O2 contamination from the acetylene. After injection of acetylene, the samples were incubated at 30°C and 200 rpm for 30 min. The reaction was stopped by addition of 100 μL 8 M NaOH.

Ethylene production was measured by gas chromatography with flame ionisation detection (GC‐FID) using an Agilent 6890 N GC instrument. A volume of 0.37 cm3 of the headspace was removed using a gas‐tight syringe (VICI Precision Sampling) and manually injected. The instrument settings were as described previously (Allen et al. 2020). The amount of ethylene was calculated based on a standard curve that was prepared using six levels of ethylene as described previously (Bilyj et al. 2024).

Nitrogen Reduction Assay

The reactions were set up as described for the acetylene reduction assay except that 3 mL exetainers (Labco) were used and the final volume was 200 μL. The 15N2 gas was 98% + pure (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Lot #: I‐26354) and potential oxygen contamination was removed by bubbling the gas through 10 mM DTH with methyl viologen as indicator using a syringe and a turret (Figure S10) (Wood and Kennedy 2001). 1 mL of 15N2 gas was added to each of the samples and the samples were incubated at 30°C and 200 rpm for 1 h. The reactions were stopped by adding 50 μL of 1 M HCl. 100 μL of the reaction mix was dried in a tin capsule with approx. 8 mg inert chromosorb (Sercon) for 48 h in a desiccator at room temperature. The samples were then weighed and analysed using a Europa 20–20 Stable Isotope Ratio Mass‐spectrometer (IRMS) electronics rebuilt by Sercon with an Automated Nitrogen Carbon Analysis for Solids and Liquids (ANCA‐SL) preparation system. The system consists of a preparation unit (ANCA‐SL) comprising a combustion tube operated at 1000°C and a reduction tube at 600°C. δ15N values were calculated as per the method of Montoya et al. (Montoya et al. 1996).
