Advanced Glycation End‐Product Carboxymethyl‐Lysine and Incident Heart Failure and Atrial Fibrillation in Older Adults
Authors: Yakubu Bene‐Alhasan, Traci M Bartz, Luc Djoussé, Joachim Ix, David Siscovick, John S Gottdiener, Russell P Tracy, Calvin H Hirsch, Anne B Newman, Sanjiv J Shah, Susan R Heckbert, Kenneth J Mukamal, Jorge R Kizer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdvanced glycation end‐products result from chemical modification of proteins under conditions of hyperglycemia or oxidative stress common with advancing age. Advanced glycation end‐product (AGE) formation alters vascular and cardiac structure and function, yet the prospective associations of circulating AGEs with heart failure (HF) and atrial fibrillation (AF) have not been studied.MethodsWe evaluated the associations of serum Nε‐carboxymethyl‐lysine (CML), a major AGE in tissue proteins, and incident HF and AF in the CHS (Cardiovascular Health Study), a population‐based cohort of older adults. HF subtypes were examined secondarily, as were prevalent echocardiographic phenotypes. CML was measured by immunoassay.ResultsAmong 2685 eligible participants (age 77±5; 63% women; 17% with diabetes), 832 HF and 1016 AF events occurred over a median follow‐up of 9 years. After adjustment for potential confounders, serum CML was associated with a higher risk of incident HF and AF (hazard ratio per SD, 1.10 and 1.09 [95% CI, 1.02–1.17 and 1.02–1.16], respectively). The association with AF was attenuated and nonsignificant after adjusting for estimated glomerular filtration rate and urine albumin–creatinine ratio. The CML–HF relation was similarly attenuated after adjusting for time‐updated myocardial infarction. Both associations were nonsignificant after adjusting for natriuretic peptides or excluding those with elevated levels at baseline. Secondary analyses of incident HF subtypes or baseline cardiac mechanics showed no significant associations.ConclusionsIn older adults, serum CML was prospectively associated with higher risk of HF and AF independent of potential confounders, with evidence of attenuation by certain putative mediators. AGEs and AGE‐countering therapies merit additional evaluation in this high‐risk population.Keywords: atrial fibrillation, cardiac mechanics, cardiovascular disease, cardiovascular health study, geriatric cardiology, heart failure, preventive cardiologySubject Categories: Aging, Heart Failure, Arrhythmias

Advanced glycation end‐products (AGEs) are a class of molecules formed by nonenzymatic reactions between reducing sugars and proteins, lipids, or nucleic acids. 1 , 2 Formation of AGEs on protein residues can denature and cross‐link proteins, profoundly disrupting their structure and function. 3 , 4

AGEs form intracellularly or extracellularly in the setting of hyperglycemia or high oxidative stress, such as occur with advanced age, 5 diabetes, 6 or chronic kidney disease. 7 Although AGEs can also be intestinally absorbed, dietary contributions to circulating AGE levels are minor. 8 Regardless of source, AGEs act to foster or amplify the state of heightened oxidative stress. They do so by increasing expression of the receptor for AGEs and its ligands, activating pro‐oxidative and proinflammatory pathways. 6 AGEs also modify a range of proteins, notably those of the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation chain, increasing generation of reactive oxygen species. 6 Because AGE adducts can persist in long‐lived extracellular proteins, and have been found to accumulate in the context of increased oxidative stress associated with aging, they have drawn intense interest as drivers of aging‐related disorders, and particularly cardiovascular disease (CVD). 9

Nε‐Carboxymethyl‐lysine (CML) is a dominant AGE in human tissues that results from modification of lysine residues by auto‐oxidized sugars, lipid peroxidation, or the α‐dicarbonyl glyoxal. 2 , 10 , 11 As such, it is considered an advanced glycoxidation and lipoxidation end product. 2 , 11 Preclinical and clinical studies have documented deposition of CML and other AGEs in large and small arteries, 12 , 13 , 14 including in rupture‐prone carotid atherosclerotic plaques, 15 as well as the myocardium, 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 implicating these compounds in diabetes‐ or aging‐related cardiovascular complications. In fact, accumulation of CML and AGE adducts in the myocardial and vascular extracellular matrix in rodents or humans with diabetes or of older age has been cited as an important contributor to the pathophysiology of cardiac impairment, heart failure (HF), and atrial fibrillation (AF) in these settings. 20 , 21 , 22

Given CML's status as a dominant AGE in human tissues, its formation through glyco‐oxidation and lipo‐oxidation processes prevalent with advancing age, and the availability of a well‐validated immunoassay for accurate measurement, we and others have undertaken evaluation of circulating levels of CML in relation to CVD in prospective cohorts of older adults. These studies have documented independent associations with arterial stiffness, 23 incident atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), 24 and death. 25 However, despite the proposed role of CML and other AGEs in myocardial disease and its 2 foremost manifestations in older adults, HF and AF, available clinical studies of these conditions have been cross‐sectional or focused on complications of prevalent disease. 26 , 27 , 28 To address the existing lack of longitudinal data on AGEs and the development of HF and AF in older adults, we investigated the potentially causal association of serum CML with incidence of these major aging‐related CVD outcomes in a population‐based study. In addition, we secondarily examined the cross‐sectional association of circulating CML with echocardiographic measures of cardiac mechanics.

METHODS

Data Access

All data reported in this article are available upon reasonable request from the CHS (Cardiovascular Health Study) Coordinating Center at http://www.chs‐nhlbi.org [chs‐nhlbi.org]. Data from the CHS are also available from the Biologic Specimen and Data Repository Information Coordinating Center at https://biolincc.nhlbi.nih.gov/studies/chs/.

Study Population

The CHS is a longitudinal study of community‐dwelling older adults in 4 US communities that began in 1989 to 1990. 29 Detailed information on CHS recruitment, design, and rationale have been published. 29 Briefly, participants were recruited from Medicare eligibility lists via a 2‐stage random sampling method using age and sex strata representative of older adults in the United States. A cohort of 687 predominantly Black individuals was added in 1992 to 1993 to the initial cohort of 5201 individuals enrolled in 1989 to 1990 to form a total cohort of 5888 participants. Of these participants, 4842 completed the examination in 1994 to 1995, and 4413 completed the examination in 1996 to 1997. Participants underwent standardized assessments at each visit, including social, lifestyle, and medical history; physical examination; biospecimen collection and laboratory measurement; and specialized diagnostic testing. 29 , 30 The institutional review board at each participating center approved the study, and each participant gave informed consent.

For the current study, baseline for incident HF and AF analyses was 1996 to 1997, the visit from which specimens were used for serum CML determination. Secondary analyses were quasi–cross‐sectional, since echocardiographic parameters were obtained at the 1994 to 1995 visit, 2 years before CML measurement. After exclusions detailed in Figure 1, a study sample of 2685 participants was available for the primary analysis of HF and AF, and a subset of 2277 participants with CML, covariates, and at least 1 echocardiographic phenotypic measure, following exclusion of extreme CML outliers (>5 SD above the mean), was available for the secondary analysis of echocardiographic phenotypes.

Carboxymethyl‐Lysine

Serum collected during the 1996 to 1997 visit was kept frozen at −70 °C in the CHS biorepository. In 2011, an immunoassay was used to measure CML levels (AGE‐CML Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay, Microcoat, Penzberg, Germany). This immunoassay detects free, protein‐bound, and peptide‐bound CML to a similar degree 31 and has a minimal detectable level of 3.9 ng/mL. The stability of CML during long‐term storage has been demonstrated in prior analyses comparing fresh‐drawn specimens with specimens frozen for >10 years. 24

Incident Heart Failure and Atrial Fibrillation

Incident HF and AF were defined as their first respective detection. Participants attended annual examinations alternating semiannually with telephone contacts through 1998 to 1999, after which semiannual telephone contacts continued. 32 , 33 For HF surveillance, participant surveys were administered during these contacts asking about a new diagnosis of HF by their physician. An Events Committee reviewed inpatient and outpatient records to adjudicate HF on the basis of physician diagnosis and confirmatory signs and symptoms, prescribed medications, and imaging findings. 32 , 33 HF subtypes were evaluated as secondary outcomes, defined by imaging using a left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction ≥50% for HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and <50% for HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). 33

For the AF outcome, CHS participants underwent annual ECGs from 1989 through 1999, which were read centrally. 34 AF was identified by presence of atrial fibrillation or flutter on a study ECG, or an International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) code for either condition documented in medical records or Medicare claims data, excluding AF related to a hospitalization for coronary bypass graft surgery or valve replacement surgery. 34 Holter monitoring in a subset of participants previously showed that only 0.1% of participants with persistent or intermittent AF were not identified by these diagnostic criteria. 34

Event ascertainment for HF was through June 2014 and for AF extended through June 2015.

Echocardiography

Comprehensive 2‐dimensional, M‐mode, and Doppler echocardiograms were obtained in 1994 to 1995 and recorded onto videotapes, as previously described. 35 As detailed elsewhere, echocardiograms were later digitized and speckle‐tracking echocardiography performed in 2016 to 2018 for strain analysis using TomTec Cardiac Performance Analysis version 4.5 software. 36 Briefly, ECG gating of the R‐R interval was used to define the cardiac cycle. LV and left atrial endocardial borders were traced manually in the apical 4‐chamber view by trained readers. Studies were graded for image quality. For secondary analyses, we evaluated 5 cardiac phenotypes: LV longitudinal strain, LV early diastolic strain rate (LVEDSR), left atrial reservoir strain, transmitral E wave/(average of medial and lateral e′ waves), and transmitral E wave/LVEDSR.

Covariate Assessment

We used covariate data from the 1996 to 1997 examination unless otherwise noted. Age, sex, race, educational attainment, medication use, physical activity, smoking status, and alcohol intake were assessed by questionnaire. Standardized measurements of height, weight, and blood pressure were performed during the visit. Body mass index was computed as the ratio of weight (in kilograms) to the squared height (in meters). Fasting laboratory measurements of serum cystatin‐C, hsCRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein), albumin, and NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) were obtained using standard immunoassays. High‐density lipoprotein, low‐density lipoprotein, and triglycerides were carried forward from the 1992 to 1993 examination. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was computed from serum cystatin C. Urine albumin–creatinine ratio (UACR) was computed from a single‐voided urine sample. Diabetes was defined as fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL, nonfasting glucose ≥200 mg/dL, or use of hypoglycemic medications. Homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR) was calculated using a standard approach. Prevalent ASCVD, including coronary heart disease (comprising myocardial infarction [MI] and coronary revascularization), stroke or transient ischemic attack, and peripheral artery disease, were each determined by adjudication of self‐reported disease at enrollment or subsequent events until the 1996 to 1997 examination using standardized definitions. 32 , 37

Statistical Analysis

We described baseline characteristics in the study sample for the primary longitudinal analyses both overall and by quartiles of CML using mean±SD for continuous variables and number (percentage) for categorical ones. The prospective associations between circulating CML levels and HF and AF were evaluated using Cox regression models. These analyses applied cause‐specific survival methods appropriate to our research question in the presence of the competing risk of death, rather than subdistribution survival methods appropriate for risk prediction. Time to event was defined as the time from baseline (1996–1997) to incident HF (for HF outcome) or incident AF (for AF outcome), death, or administrative censoring. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed by examining Schoenfeld residuals, which revealed no meaningful violations. Cross‐sectional associations of CML levels with echocardiographic outcomes were evaluated using multivariable linear regression models. We assessed departures from linearity for Cox and linear regression models based on a visual inspection of penalized cubic spline plots. 38 Plots with 3 and 4 degrees of freedom were examined to accommodate nonlinearity without overfitting. For the main Cox analyses, we secondarily computed the Gain statistic, or difference in normalized deviance between the spline model and the model with the linear predictor.

For multivariable modeling, we constructed a directed acyclic graph based on known biology and prior associations to guide covariate selection (Figure S1). Sequential models were fitted to account for potential confounders (and, as detailed further below, putative mediators). Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, and race for all outcomes, as well as clinic site, reader, and chamber‐specific quality for echo phenotypes. Model 2 additionally adjusted for body mass index, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, systolic blood pressure, antihypertensive medications, diabetes, and serum albumin (for all outcomes). Model 3 (main model) additionally adjusted for prevalent coronary heart disease, prevalent stroke/transient ischemic attack, and prevalent peripheral artery disease.

In all analyses, we assessed the impact of factors that could serve not only as confounders but also as mediators by adding corresponding variables to those in model 3, including log‐hsCRP (model 4), and eGFR and UACR (model 5). Sensitivity analyses were done to explore the impact of additional adjustment for lipids (model 6), HOMA‐IR (in those not on diabetes medications; model 7), or incident MI as a time‐varying covariate (model 8) for AF and HF outcomes. We also examined the impact of adjusting for log(NT‐proBNP) at baseline (model 9) or excluding participants with NT‐proBNP ≥300 pg/mL (model 10). 39 To assess the potential influence of unrecognized clinical HF or AF at baseline, we examined the associations by moving forward the start of follow‐up to 2 years after CML measurement and excluding incident HF or AF cases during this period (model 11). First‐order interactions were explored between CML and age, sex, race, diabetes, eGFR, log(UACR) or log(NT‐proBNP) in relation to the primary outcomes by including appropriate cross‐product terms. In an additional exploratory analysis, we assessed the joint contributions of mediation and interaction by log(NT‐proBNP), eGFR, and log(UACR) to the overall associations of CML with HF and AF. This analysis applied a 4‐way decomposition approach 40 predicated on the assumption that the covariates of interest are entirely downstream of the exposure (mediators), and not upstream (confounders), in its relationship with the primary outcomes. The approach separates the total exposure “effect” into 4 distinct components: “controlled direct effect” (due to neither mediation nor interaction), “reference interaction” (due to interaction only), “mediated interaction” (interaction occurring when the exposure has an effect on the mediator), and “pure indirect effect” (due to mediation only). 40 We did not conduct formal correction for multiple testing on account of strong prior hypotheses for our primary outcomes, for which statistical significance was defined as 2‐tailed α<0.05. Exploratory analyses of interaction or mediation were considered significant for α<0.01. All analyses were carried out with Stata 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) and R (R Development Core Team; www.r‐project.org).

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

Of the 3474 individuals who participated in the 1996 to 1997 examination, 789 were missing CML or covariate measures. These individuals participated by telephone or through limited in‐person visits that did not include physical examination or laboratory measurements. Excluded participants lacking CML or covariate measures had more prevalent ASCVD and higher incidence rates of HF and AF than their included counterparts in the primary analysis who were not missing such measures (Table S1). For the 2685 participants in the primary analysis, the distribution of CML was right‐skewed with a median of 578.4 (interquartile range, 495.1–688.2) ng/mL. The baseline characteristics of this sample, and their levels across quartiles of CML, are presented in Table 1. Levels of age, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, serum albumin, and NT‐proBNP were higher with increasing quartiles of CML in the descriptive analyses, while those of body mass index, HOMA‐IR, triglycerides, and eGFR were lower.

CML, Covariates, and Primary Outcomes

Penalized cubic spline plots with 3 degrees of freedom were consistent with linear associations between CML and incidence of HF or AF after adjustment for covariates in the main model (Figure 2), as confirmed by corresponding Gain statistics (P=0.379 and P=0.238, respectively). CML and HF

During a median follow‐up period of 9.4 (interquartile range, 5.1–14.9) years, there were 832 incident HF events, 282 of which could be classified as HFpEF and 231 as HFrEF. As shown in Table 2, there was a nonsignificant positive association between serum CML and incident HF in the minimally adjusted model (model 1), which strengthened and became statistically significant with additional adjustment for lifestyle and clinical risk factors. After full adjustments in our main model, there was a 10% higher risk of HF per SD increment in CML level. As also presented in Table 2, this association was maintained after adjusting for partial intermediates, namely, the inflammatory biomarker log(hsCRP) and measures of kidney function, eGFR and log(UACR). Secondary assessment showed no association between CML and incident HFrEF or HFpEF in any of the models (Table 3). In sensitivity analyses shown in Table 4, serum CML remained positively associated with incident HF after additional adjustment in our main model for lipids (high‐density lipoprotein, low‐density lipoprotein, and log[triglycerides]) or, with lower risk estimates, insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR) or interim MI. Assessment of the impact of successive adjustments for covariates with substantial (>5%) missingness was repeated by comparing associations in the corresponding participant subsets with available data before adjustment, which confirmed attenuation by kidney measures, but did not show attenuation by lipids or HOMA‐IR (Table S2). The risk estimate decreased substantially and was no longer statistically significant after adjustment for log(NT‐proBNP) or when participants with NT‐proBNP ≥300 pg/mL were excluded (Table 4). The latter sensitivity analyses excluded 57 participants with missing NT‐proBNP values. Of the 608 excluded participants with elevated NT‐proBNP, 35 had levels exceeding the threshold to support an HF diagnosis (NT‐proBNP ≥900 pg/mL for those aged ≤75 years, NT‐proBNP ≥1800 pg/mL for those aged >75 years). 39 Delaying the start of follow‐up by 2 years resulted in a similar risk estimate that remained statistically significant (Table 4). There was no evidence of interaction of CML with age (P=0.28), sex (P=0.77), race (P=0.45), diabetes (P=0.51), eGFR (P=0.01), log(UACR) (P=0.19) or log(NT‐proBNP) (P=0.10). In our 4‐way decomposition analysis (Table S3), possible mediation by NT‐proBNP accounted for 80% (ratio of regression coefficients for pure indirect effect and total excess relative risk, 0.12/0.15) of the association between CML and HF. In turn, possible mediation and mediated interaction by eGFR accounted for the entirety of the association between CML and HF, while possible mediation by UACR accounted for 27% of the CML–HF association.

CML and AF

There were 1016 incident AF events during a median follow‐up of 8.8 (interquartile range, 4.7–14.3) years. As presented in Table 2, there was a significant positive association between serum CML and incidence of AF in the minimally adjusted model, which became stronger with additional adjustment. In the main model (model 3), each SD increment in serum CML was associated with a 9% higher risk of AF. This association persisted with additional adjustment for log(hsCRP) but became attenuated and no longer statistically significant upon inclusion of measures of kidney function (eGFR and log[UACR]) (Table 2). Sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for lipids or interim MI showed persistence of associations, but the relationship decreased in magnitude and became nonsignificant after HOMA‐IR adjustment (Table 4). As for HF, attenuation of risk estimates was a result of adjustment by kidney function measures but was instead related to the smaller sample for HOMA‐IR (Table S2). The risk estimate likewise decreased and was no longer statistically significant after adjustment for log(NT‐proBNP) or exclusion of participants with NT‐proBNP ≥300 pg/mL (Table 4). Delaying the start of follow‐up by 2 years led to a similar risk estimate, although the association became marginally nonsignificant in the setting of fewer incident events. There was no evidence of interaction of CML with age (P=0.08), sex (P=0.60), Black race (P=0.37), diabetes (P=0.99), eGFR (P=0.28), log(UACR) (P=0.68), or log(NT‐proBNP) (P=0.47). In 4‐way decomposition analysis (Table S3), possible mediation by NT‐proBNP accounted for 69% of the association between CML and AF. There was no evidence of mediation by eGFR or UACR.

DISCUSSION

Main Findings

In this cohort of community‐dwelling older adults, we found significant associations between circulating CML levels and higher incidence of HF and AF after adjustment for potential confounders. No significant associations were detected between serum CML and incidence of HF subtypes. Likewise, secondary, quasi–cross‐sectional analyses revealed no significant associations of serum CML with echocardiographic measures of cardiac mechanics or LV filling.

Previous Literature

Based on extensive preclinical evidence linking AGEs to CVD, multiple clinical studies have evaluated the association of systemic AGE levels, whether through assays in circulation or fluorescence readings in skin, to cardiovascular outcomes in the setting of diabetes and advancing age. 23 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 Circulating CML and other AGEs have indeed been linked to incident ASCVD and death in prospective cohorts of older adults, 24 , 25 as they have in the context of diabetes. 43 , 46 Such associations documented in these older, general population cohorts have not been observed in younger, healthier populations largely free of diabetes, suggesting that it is only in the setting of heightened oxidative stress or inflammation that CML may contribute to these outcomes. 41 As relates to cardiac dysfunction, HF and AF, clinical studies of AGEs have likewise supported an association, although these have been cross‐sectional, or included selected populations, with variable results. A small study of patients with type 1 diabetes showed a relationship between circulating AGEs, but not CML, and echocardiographic LV diastolic dysfunction, 42 whereas a population‐based study linked circulating CML and other AGEs with LV systolic and diastolic dysfunction in individuals without, but not with, type 2 diabetes. 47 A larger population‐based study, however, did document an association between skin autofluorescence and LV systolic dysfunction, which was more pronounced among participants with type 2 diabetes. 48 The latter study also found an association of skin autofluorescence with prevalent HF, although this did not vary by diabetes status. Three other studies related circulating CML 26 or pentosidine 27 levels to cardiovascular outcomes or death in patients with HF, irrespective of diabetes. 26 , 27 , 49 Data for AF are more sparse, coming from 1 small study that compared patients with and without this dysrhythmia and related circulating levels of fluorescent AGEs to AF status independent of other risk factors. 28 Different findings in the existing literature as relate to AGEs, and CML in particular, likely reflect differences in study design, populations, and sample sizes, but are consistent with the premise that, unlike many AGEs, CML is predominantly a glycoxidation/lipoxidation product less relevant in diabetes than older age. 2 , 24 , 41 , 50

To our knowledge, this is the first longitudinal evaluation of circulating levels of any AGE in relation to the individual incidence of HF or AF. As such, the associations documented between serum CML and risk of new‐onset HF and AF in older adults represent an important advance in understanding the role of AGEs in these major aging‐related conditions.

Interpretation of Findings

Our analyses revealed that serum CML was associated with incidence of both HF and AF after adjustment for demographic, behavioral, and clinical risk factors, without evidence of a particular association with a specific HF subtype. The associations with HF and AF persisted after adjusting for hsCRP, supporting that CML's contributions are independent of underlying or resulting inflammation. In the case of HF, the association remained after adjustment for eGFR and UACR, while for AF, such adjustment led to moderate attenuation in the risk estimate, which became marginally nonsignificant. Because serum CML reflects a combination of protein/peptide adducts and the free AGE, 51 of which the low‐molecular‐weight species are filtered at the glomerulus, these findings suggest that the observed associations are not primarily explained by CML accumulation from reduced filtration. Although risk estimates for CML's associations with both primary outcomes were weaker after adjustment for HOMA‐IR, this appeared to be a function of the smaller subsample and not attenuation. It is possible that the inverse association between body mass index and serum CML, which results from CML sequestration in adipose tissues, may have offset any attenuation from oxidative stress pathways shared between CML and insulin resistance. 50 That the CML–HF association, but not the CML–AF association, was attenuated after adjustment for intercurrent MI reflects the documented role of CML in atherothrombotic disease, 24 , 41 , 52 but this association retained statistical significance.

Notably, adjustment for NT‐proBNP or exclusion of participants with elevated NT‐proBNP led to attenuation and loss of significant associations for HF and AF. Only a minority of participants with elevated NT‐proBNP had levels above the threshold required to support a clinical diagnosis of HF, which would also require unrecognized symptoms. 39 , 53 In addition, risk estimates for both HF and AF were similar after excluding the first 2 years of follow‐up, which argues against unrecognized prevalent HF as an explanation for the observed associations. Thus, elevated baseline NT‐proBNP levels primarily reflected preclinical heart disease, or pre‐HF. The attenuation observed after accounting for NT‐proBNP could therefore be consistent with the downstream influence of CML on preclinical HF. Pre‐HF also results from CVD risk factors and associated increases in inflammation and oxidative stress, 22 , 54 however, which can promote CML formation. Accordingly, reverse causation from preclinical HF cannot be excluded as a possible explanation for the findings.

Our exploratory 4‐way decomposition analyses illustrate the importance of NT‐proBNP to our primary associations. Under the assumption that NT‐proBNP is solely in the causal pathway from CML to HF, and not at all a confounder, most of the “effect” of CML on HF or AF was putatively driven by NT‐proBNP. In this analysis, NT‐proBNP is technically not a mediator, but instead a marker of an intermediate phenotype 53 on the pathway to HF or AF. In the same exploratory analysis, eGFR putatively mediated the entirety of CML's “effect” on HF, with UACR having a more modest contribution. Yet this exploratory analysis must be interpreted with caution, because concurrent measurement of CML and the putative mediators/effect modifiers lacks the necessary temporality to determine the direction of the associations. Both confounding and mediation of CML's associations by these candidate covariates are possible, yet our analyses are unable to determine the relative importance of each. Moreover, the interrelationships of CML, kidney disease measures, and NT‐proBNP are complex. These cannot be disentangled by our 4‐way decomposition approach, which is able to consider 1 putative mediator at a time, and by the available 1‐time measures of each of these factors at baseline. Further assessment of their roles will require future study with repeated measurements of these molecules over time.

As relates to our failure to detect an association between serum CML and echocardiographic phenotypes, several factors could be accountable. These include the quasi–cross‐sectional nature of the analyses, with echocardiographic measures preceding CML measurement by 2 years; the fact that cardiac mechanics determinations were based on digitization of analog images acquired with ultrasound technology from >20 years prior; and reliance on the apical‐4‐chamber view only, all of which may have contributed to misclassification, biasing our findings toward the null.

Potential Mechanisms and Clinical Implications

The longitudinal associations documented here with HF and AF support the role of CML as a marker and potential mediator of myocardial disease and dysfunction. As a product of oxidative and carbonyl stress, CML both reflects and contributes to processes that drive small‐ and large‐artery disease, as well as myocardial pathology. Such processes involve other AGEs, with which CML is closely correlated. 41 CML and other AGEs have been detected in the intima and media of large arteries, and CML itself has been clinically linked to aortic stiffness in elders, 23 which is a major determinant of cardiac stiffness, HF, and AF. 55 CML is also present in rupture‐prone large‐artery atherosclerotic plaques, 52 linking them to myocardial ischemia and infarction, which our analyses show partly accounted for the CML–HF association. Likewise, deposition of CML has been documented in small myocardial arteries, 13 , 14 implicating these compounds in microvascular endothelial dysfunction, which is believed to be central to the pathophysiology of HFpEF. 54 Apart from vascular effects, AGEs, including CML, have been detected in myocardial tissue in preclinical models of HF, 16 , 17 as well as clinically in HFrEF. 18 The actions of CML or other AGEs, whether through disruption of protein function, including the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation chain to further heighten oxidative stress, the upregulation of receptor for AGE and its ligands to promote inflammation, or the cross‐linking of collagen to increase large‐artery or myocardial stiffness, together offer multiple mechanisms that can foster the development of HF and AF. 9 , 21 , 56 Our analyses revealed no predilection of CML for HFpEF or HFrEF, suggesting that these multiple vascular and myocardial actions have a comparable impact on both disorders.

Although our analyses cannot determine whether CML is a marker or mediator for these disorders, the prospective association, in conjunction with the large body of available preclinical work attesting to AGEs' adverse biological actions, 9 supports a mediating role. Various therapeutic agents with AGE scavenging, metal chelation, antioxidant, and potentially AGE cross‐link–breaking properties have been tested in relation to arterial stiffness and cardiac dysfunction end‐points, but development halted owing to mixed to null results, side effect concerns, or financing obstacles. 57 One potential strategy involves enhancing detoxification of α‐dicarbonyl AGE precursors, including glyoxal and methylglyoxal, through upregulation of the enzyme glyoxalase 1. 8 Another is the use of aptamers, single‐stranded DNA or RNA molecules, for binding AGEs, blocking their interaction with the receptor for AGE, and promoting their removal. 58 Our findings provide increased impetus for the development and testing of anti‐AGE therapies for reducing HF and AF, in addition to ASCVD and other outcomes, in older adults.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations. CML levels were measured at 1 point, and inferences are based on 1‐time readings. It is unclear how variation in CML level over time would influence the associations we found. We did not include other circulating AGEs and cannot evaluate the extent to which the higher risk observed for CML is shared by other AGE species. As noted, however, we have previously documented strong correlations between CML and other key AGEs in this cohort. 41 Despite our adjustment for multiple covariates, there remains a possibility of residual confounding inherent in observational studies. Nor can reverse causation be ruled out. As such, the present study does not allow determination of the causal basis of the observed associations. Measurements were made late in life and study participants needed to survive or attend the 1996 to 1997 examination to have CML and covariates measured, such that our findings apply to healthier older adults. We were unable to explore pathways through which CML could contribute to HF and AF, such as receptor for AGE activation or oxidative stress, because these data were not available. Finally, our HF subtype analyses were limited by incomplete classification and low numbers of events. As indicated by the broader confidence intervals, these secondary analyses lacked power, although the point estimates for each subtype were consistent with the same positive associations observed for overall HF.

CONCLUSIONS

In this cohort of older adults, we document prospective associations of serum CML with HF and AF independent of various risk factors. There was also evidence of attenuation by kidney disease measures and NT‐proBNP, which prior investigations support as lying at least partly downstream of CML in the pathway to these outcomes. The role of intervening kidney disease and preclinical heart disease as mediators of these relationships will require further study. Our findings extend the known associations of this major AGE in human proteins to the 2 most common adverse cardiovascular outcomes in this population, outcomes in need of more effective preventive strategies. These results should motivate renewed efforts at studying the role of CML and other AGEs in these aging‐related cardiovascular disorders, which could support development and testing of novel AGE‐reducing therapies for CVD prevention in this high‐risk group.
