Carriage and susceptibility of Enterobacteria isolated from the local chickens at markets and peri-urban areas of Bobo-Dioulasso, Burkina Faso
Authors: Drissa Zon, Victorien Yaméogo, Roland Nâg-Tiero Meda, Soufiane Sanou, Benjamin Kouliga Koama, Yerbanga R. Serge, Sami Eric Kam, Eliasse Zongo, Kobo Gnada, Georges Anicet Ouédraogo, Mohamed Fawzy Ramadan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSalmonella and Escherichia coli are among the main causes of foodborne illness and negatively impact poultry industry. The aim of this study is to determine the carriage rate and sensitivity of Enterobacteria isolated from the cloacae of chickens. Samples were taken only from healthy local chickens of markets (55 samples) and outlying areas (45 samples) using the swabbing technique. The swabs were pre-enriched and inoculated onto Hektoen agar at 37 °C for 24 h. Bacteria and resistance genes were identified through molecular biology. Antimicrobial susceptibility was tested by Kirby Bauer method.ResultsThe overall prevalence was 65% for Escherichia coli and 27% for Salmonella sp. after biochemical tests and these carrying rates of these strains were 10% for Salmonella and 53% for Escherichia coli after molecular confirmation tests. E. coli isolates (n = 53), 91.43% (32/35) originated from farms, while 75% (21/28) were collected from markets. The detection of the 16S rRNA gene was higher in market samples (25%) compared to farm samples (8.57%). Antibiotic susceptibility testing showed extremely high resistance rates to amoxicillin (> 93%) and tetracycline (> 80%) in both bacterial species. In contrast, high susceptibility rates were recorded to ceftriaxone (> 90%) and norfloxacin (> 89%). Moderate resistance levels were observed to cotrimoxazole and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid Augmentin (> 41%). Comparative statistical analysis between bacterial species showed no statistically significant difference for most antibiotics (p > 0.05). Among Escherichia coli isolates, 58.49% (31/53) exhibited a penicillinase phenotype, compared with 60% (6/10) of Salmonella spp. and ESBL phenotypes were detected in 35.80% (19/53) of E. coli isolates and 40% (4/10) of Salmonella spp. isolates. No statistically significant difference was observed between the species. The blaTEM gene was detected in 41.5% (22/53) of E. coli isolates and in 60% (6/10) of Salmonella spp. isolates. Similarly, the blaCTX-M-9 gene was identified in 35.58% (19/53) of E. coli isolates and 30% (3/10) of Salmonella spp. isolates although this difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05).ConclusionThis study highlighted the presence of strains Enterobacteria resistant to many antimicrobials of therapeutic importance in healthy chickens. For the well-being of consumers, it is necessary to strengthen surveillance and prevention systems.

Background

Poultry farming is a crucial sector of activity for the well-being of populations. Poultry is an economy and insurance against falling production and income [1]. In recent years, the consumption of poultry products (eggs and meat) has increased considerably in all regions of the world. In sub-Saharan Africa and many other regions, poultry represents the first source of animal protein for populations [2]. In Burkina Faso, livestock farming is one of the sectors of activity encouraged to fight malnutrition and unemployment [1]. In Burkina Faso, breeding is dominated by poultry, contributes 18% to the Gross Domestic Product [3]. However, despite the efforts to boost the poultry production, the sector is still confronted to huge difficulties, the most dangerous of which is the weak zoo-sanitary situation [4]. Infectious diseases of parasitic origin (Coccidiosis, helminthiasis, etc.), viral (Newcastle disease, fowl pox, Gomboro, etc.) and bacterial (Salmonellosis, Cholera, Colibacillosis, etc.) are the major obstacles to poultry production [5]. This situation is exacerbated by little or absence of hygiene, supplementary feed and biosecurity measures in the smallholder poultry [6, 7]. Thus, the lack of hygiene and food safety regulations considerably exposes consumers to infectious diseases such as Colibacillosis and Salmonellosis in many developing countries [7]. Escherichia coli and Salmonella, are among the most bacteria implicated in cases of bacterial origin infections [8]. They are the most indexed in gastroenteritis or foodborne diseases and collective food poisoning, very harmful in weakened people (Young-Old-Pregnant-Immunodepressed) in both developed and developing countries [9]. Foodborne diseases kill 3 million children each year and bacterial infectious diseases are the 3rd cause of mortality after malaria and malnutrition [10], by the ingestion of contaminated food and unhygienic food preparations [11]. To fight this heavy economic and boost zoo-technical performances of chickens, the breeders uncontrolled use, inadequate and misuse of many products to resolve animal health like anabolizers, tranquilizers and mainly antibiotics use in prophylaxis, metaphylaxis, therapeutics and as growth promoters [6]. But the irrational and irresponsible exploitation of antimicrobials led to the emergence of microorganisms resistant such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) [12] and Enterobacteriaceae producing of extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (E-ESBLs) [13, 14]. The detection of resistant organisms in chickens and its derivatives products constitute a threat to public health [7]. Indeed numerous studies on poultry farms in Quebec [15], France [16], Mali [17], and the Philippines [18] have highlighted the presence of multi-resistant bacteria. In Burkina Faso, the previous studies have indicated the presence Escherichia coli and Salmonella in various types of samples across the territory [19–22]. Despite the threat posed by antimicrobial resistance (AMR), the WHO and OIE describe significant gaps in surveillance, standard methodology, and data sharing. For this reason, in 2017 Burkina Faso adopted a national action plan to fight antimicrobial resistance (PAN-RAM) based on the one health approach. The RAM surveillance system requires continuous updating of epidemiological data on emerging pathogens. Following this line of inquiry, the goal of this study is to determine the carriage rate and susceptibility of bacteria responsible for Salmonellosis and colibacillosis in the markets and farms of Bobo-Dioulasso.

Materials and methods

Study area

Bobo-Dioulasso is located in the region of Guiriko of western of Burkina Faso, between 11°11’N latitudes and 4°17’W longitudes. The distance of this district to the capital Ouagadougou is 360 km. It has a population of 984 603 spread over an area of 1 805 km2. The city of Bobo Dioulasso is the urban commune of Houet Province, located in the Guiriko region. Due to its geographical location, it is a commercial hub and also the economic and cultural capital of the country. It therefore occupies a strategic position for the development of Burkina Faso [23]. The region is distinguished by its significant biodiversity and has 16 classified forests. The vegetation is of the South Sudanese type, consisting of wooded savannahs, savannahs with trees, and shrublands. There are four classified forests: Kou, Kuinima, Dienderesso, and Kua, as well as multiple forest galleries along the waterways. In view of the entire country, the climate is tropical with two seasons, but the Guiriko region is distinguished by a long rainy season and a short dry season. It is one of the wettest regions with heat wave below 30 °C [24] (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Location of the study area [23]

Study design

This study was conducted in urban (markets) and peri-urban (farms) areas of Bobo-Dioulasso. The study area included eleven poultry markets and nine farms. Samples were collected from major poultry markets within the city. Peripheral sampling sites were selected proportionally across the three Zone d’Appui Technique en Elevage (ZATE) of the municipality. Given the organisation of the livestock farm into three zones, the stratified probabilistic sampling method was implemented for sampling at the periphery [25]. The study area was divided into strata corresponding to each ZATE, and 15 samples were collected per zone. Within each ZATE, three improved traditional chicken farms were randomly selected.

Sampling procedure

Sampling was conducted from February to June 2024. Cloacal swab samples were collected from apparently healthy local chickens using sterile swabs (Copan Venturi Transystem®) [26]. At each site, five chickens were randomly selected regardless of sex. In markets, the survey team first contacted the market leader, who facilitated access to poultry vendors. On farms, a list of producers was obtained in advance from ZATE representatives, and farms were selected based on the availability of farm managers following prior consent. Swabs were moistened in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth and gently inserted into the cloaca [18]. After rotation, each swab was placed into a sterile tube containing 10 mL of BHI broth [13]. Samples from each site were labelled accordingly (M for markets and F for farms). All samples were transported in a cold chain to the bacteriology laboratory of Centre MURAZ and stored at 4 °C. Microbiological analyses were initiated within 6 h of collection [17, 26].

Bacteriological analyses

Upon arrival at the laboratory, samples were incubated at 37 °C for 12 h for pre-enrichment. Following incubation, aliquots of the broth were inoculated onto Hektoen enteric agar (Liofilchem, Via Scozia, 64026 Rose to degli Abruzzi (TE) Italy) for the isolation of enteric bacteria [13].

Pre-identification of Salmonella spp.

After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, translucent colonies with black centers (indicative of H₂S production) or greenish colonies were considered presumptive Salmonella. These colonies were subcultured onto Mueller–Hinton agar (Via Scozia, Roseto degli Abruzzi (TE), Italy) for purification and biochemical characterization. The following biochemical tests were performed: oxidase, catalase, Gram staining, and urease. Gram-negative, oxidase-negative, catalase-positive, and urease-negative bacilli were considered presumptive Salmonella spp [9]. Isolates were stored at −80 °C in Brain Heart Infusion broth supplemented with 30% (v/v) glycerol until further analysis [20]. Molecular confirmation was performed by PCR amplification of the 16S rRNA gene (496 bp).

Pre-identification of Escherichia coli

After incubation, pink to red colonies (lactose-fermenting) were selected and streaked onto Eosin Methylene Blue (EMB) agar (Liofilchem, Zona Ind.le-Roseto degli Abruzzi (TE) Italy) [18]. Following 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, colonies exhibiting a metallic green sheen were subcultured onto Mueller–Hinton agar for biochemical testing. Lactose-positive, Gram-negative bacilli that were catalase-positive, oxidase-negative, and urease-negative were considered presumptive Escherichia coli. Isolates were preserved in BHI broth containing 30% glycerol at −80 °C [20]. Molecular confirmation was achieved by PCR amplification of the uidA gene (1487 bp).

DNA extraction

Reference strains and clinical isolates were cultured on Mueller–Hinton agar. Two to three colonies were suspended in 200 µL of Chelex/proteinase K solution. Genomic DNA was extracted using the boiling method [27]. Samples were incubated at 56 °C for 1 h using a heating block (ProbeTecTMET, Shannon, county clare, Ireland), followed by vortexing. Subsequently, samples were heated at 96 °C for 10 min, vortexed again, and centrifuged (Sigma 1–15, D 37520 Osterade am Harz, Germany, 2005) at 13,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant containing genomic DNA was carefully transferred into sterile Eppendorf tubes and stored at −20 °C until use [26, 27].

Detection of Escherichia coli

PCR amplification of the uidA gene was performed under similar conditions using a T100 thermal cycler (T100 Thermal cycler, Singapore, 2021) described by [28]. PCR products were visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis. E. coli ATCC 25922 was used as a positive control.

Detection of serovars and virulence genes

Salmonella serovars were identified by PCR detection of the specific genes spy (401 bp) and sdfI (304 bp), corresponding to Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella Enteritidis, respectively [7]. The PCR mixture (20 µL) contained 4 µL of Master Mix, 12 µL of PCR-grade water, 0.5 µL of each primer (10 µM), and 2 µL of DNA template. Virulence genes (stxA1 and stxA2) as described by [28], were investigated in E. coli isolates using PCR under similar conditions. The amplification programs are recorded in Table 1.

Agarose gel electrophoresis

PCR products were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis (1–1.5%) in 1 × TAE buffer (Cleaver Scientific, 50X Tris–acetate-EDTA, UK) at 120 V for 1 h. A 10 µL molecular weight marker (50 bp ladder) was loaded into the first and last wells. Gels were prepared by dissolving 2 g or 3 g of agarose in 200 mL of TAE buffer for 1% and 1.5% gels, respectively. DNA bands were visualized using a Gel Doc imaging system (Geldoc GO, Bio-Rad, USA, 2023). The presence of bands at the expected size was considered positive.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method [1]. Bacterial suspensions were prepared in sterile saline and adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standards. Mueller–Hinton agar plates were inoculated using sterile swabs [10]. The following antibiotics were tested: amoxicillin (AML, 25 µg), amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (AUG, 30 µg), ceftriaxone (CRO, 30 µg), gentamicin (CN, 10 µg), norfloxacin (NOR, 10 µg), tetracycline (TE, 30 µg) and cotrimoxazole (SXT, 25 µg). After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, inhibition zone diameters were measured using calipers. Results were interpreted as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant according to CASFM/EUCAST (2019) guidelines. Phenotypic profiles were defined as follows: (i) Wild-type: susceptible to all tested antibiotics; (ii) Penicillinase phenotype: resistance to amoxicillin but susceptibility to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid; (iii) ESBL phenotype: synergy between ceftriaxone and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid; (iv) cephalosporinase phenotype: resistance to ceftriaxone and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid [30]. Quality control was performed using E. coli ATCC 25922.

Detection of antibiotic resistance genes

Antibiotic resistance genes were detected by multiplex PCR. The targeted genes included blaTEM (800 bp), blaSHV (713 bp) and blaOxa-1-like (564 bp) blaCTX-M-1 (688 bp) and blaCTX-M-9 (561 bp) [28, 29]. PCR reactions were performed in a total volume of 20 µL containing 2 µL of DNA template and 18 µL of reaction mixture. Cycling conditions were applied as previously described [29]. PCR products were analysed by agarose gel electrophoresis as described above. Reference strains carrying known resistance genes were used as positive controls, including E. coli ATCC 35218. All amplifications were effected according three steps of PCR, which are primary denaturation, cycle amplification (denaturation, annealing, and extension), and final extension.

Data analysis

Data were entered and analysed using Microsoft Excel. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.3). Qualitative data were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Given the relatively small sample size and the low expected frequencies observed in certain categories, particularly for Salmonella isolates, Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate the association between bacterial species, sampling sites, and both phenotypic and genotypic resistances profiles. This test was preferred over Pearson’s chi-square test because it provides more reliable estimates when expected cell counts are small. Statistical significance was defined at a threshold of p < 0.05. In addition, odds ratios with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95%IC) were calculated to estimate the strength and direction of the observed associations.

Ethical considerations

The experimental procedures were conducted following approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of Nazi BONI University in Bobo-Dioulasso. This approval (CEEA-UJKZ/2021-07) was granted after verification of the research protocol to ensure animal welfare. This study was carried out with the permission of the provincial directorate of agriculture and fisheries resources of Houet through obtaining a collection authorization signed by Provincial Director (N°2024-00000005/MARAH/SG/DRARAH-HBS/DPARAH-H). Access to peri-urban sites was granted by zone managers. Consent and permission from each owner was also obtained before any sampling was carried out. No mortality or adverse effects were observed following the sampling.

Results

Enterobacteria carriage rate and detection of specific genes

This study, carried out on apparently healthy local chickens from farms and major poultry markets in Bobo-Dioulasso, revealed the presence of enteric bacteria associated with responsible for Colibacillosis and Salmonellosis in cloacal samples. Based on biochemical identification, the overall prevalence was 65% for Escherichia coli and 27% for Salmonella spp., with a clear predominance of E. coli cross across all sampling sites. A total of 63 bacterial isolates were confirmed by molecular analysis, including 35 isolates obtained from farms and 28 from markets. Escherichia coli was the predominant bacterial species identified at both sampling sites. In farms, E. coli accounted for 91.43% (32/35) of the isolates, whereas in market it represented 75% (21/28). Phenotypic and genotypic resistance profiles

Phenotypic analysis revealed that penicillinase-producing isolates were the most frequently detected phenotype in both bacteria species. Among Escherichia coli isolates, 58.49% (31/53) exhibited a penicillinase phenotype, compared with 60% (6/10) of Salmonella spp. isolates. Statistical analysis revealed no significant association between species and the occurrence of the penicillinase phenotype (OR = 0.94; 95% CI: 0.17–4.54; p = 1.00).

Similarly, the prevalence of extended-spectrum ß-lactamase (ESBL)-producing isolates was comparable between the two bacterial groups. ESBL phenotypes were detected in 35.80% (19/53) of E. coli isolates and 40% (4/10) of Salmonella spp. isolates. No statistically significant difference was observed between the species regarding ESBL production (OR = 0.84; 95% CI: 0.17–4.57; p = 1).

Molecular analysis revealed that the penicillinase and ESBL phenotypes were associated with the blaTEM (Fig. 8) and blaCTX-M-9 (Fig. 9) genes respectively. The blaTEM gene detected in 41.5% (22/53) of E. coli isolates and in 60% (6/10) of Salmonella spp. isolates. Discussion

The present study provides important insights into the circulation of antimicrobial-resistant Enterobacteria in local chickens of farms and markets of Bobo-Dioulasso. The cloaca represents a critical anatomical site where the digestive, urinary, and reproductive systems converge, making it a key location for detecting microorganisms with the potential to contaminate poultry products. The presence of Salmonella spp. and Escherichia coli in both farms and markets highlights a significant public health concern, particularly in contexts where hygiene practices and biosecurity measures are insufficient. Poultry products (meat and eggs) are well-establish reservoirs for Salmonella, a major cause of foodborne gastroenteritis [31].

The observed Salmonella prevalence (10%) is consistent with report from Ethiopia (7.4%) [32],in Japan (8%) [33] and in Burkina Faso (10.28%) [22], but lower than values reported in other studies from Burkina Faso [19] (54.86%) and [1] (24.84%) and Pakistan (21.34%) [27]. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in hygiene practices, production systems, and sampling strategies. In the human and animal infections, the Salmonella species most implicated are Salmonella Enteritidis and Salmonella Typhimurium [21]. The exclusive detection of Salmonella Enteritidis and absence of Salmonella Typhimurium align with recent findings [7]. According to origin, markets were the most exposed (25%). This prevalence is much lower than the results obtained by Abba et al., 2017 [34] (65.85%) in Tchad. In addition, these results are similar to those obtained in the markets (13.08%) of Bobo-Dioulasso by Bouda et al., 2018 [1]. Our results were the opposite of those obtained in Ethiopia by Gómez et al., 2025 [7]. Variations in prevalence between farms and markets further emphasize the influence of environmental and management factors. These observations are supported by the results of Hussain et al., 2020 [27] who isolated Salmonella in samples collected from butcher shops (21.34%) and found nothing in supermarket samples. According to Alio et al., 2017 [35], these results are below the threshold for Salmonella contamination in poultry (22.34%). Escherichia coli, a commensal bacterium of the intestinal microbiota, were highly prevalent (53%) particularly in farms. This prevalence is higher than that obtained by Boroomand et al., 2018 [33] (44%) in Japan. The identification rate of Escherichia coli in was more prevalent in farms than in markets. This likely reflects environmental contamination associated with poor waste management and accumulation of fecal material [34]. Importantly, no shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC) were detected, consistent with earlier studies [20, 36] in Burkina Faso reporting very low prevalence; previous findings. The predominance of E. coli in farm and of Salmonella in markets supports hypothesis that farms act as reservoirs of commensal bacteria, whereas markets serve as amplification points for pathogen dissemination through handling, transport, and cross-contamination [37].

The high levels of resistance to amoxicillin and tetracycline reflect intense and likely uncontrolled use of these antibiotics in poultry production [27]. Similar findings have been widely reported [7, 31] and are consistent with the frequent use oxytetracycline, belong to the tetracycline family, for therapeutic and prophylactic purposes [13, 37]. In contrast, the preserved efficacy of ceftriaxone and norfloxacin suggests limited exposed to these higher-generation antibiotics, although their prudent use is essential to prevent the emergence of resistance.

The susceptibility of the strains of Salmonella spp. and Escherichia coli revealed the presence of resistant phenotypes encoded by specific resistance genes. Penicillinase-producing isolates were significantly more frequent in farms compared to markets and the prevalence of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) phenotype in farms and markets was not significant difference. Analysis highlighting the carriage of blaTEM and blaCTX-M-9 type ESBL. The presence of blaTEM and blaCTX-M-9 genes confirms the genetic basis of observed resistance phenotypes TEM-type β-lactamases are among the most widespread resistance enzymes in Gram-negative bacteria and are primarily associated with resistance to penicillins and first-generation cephalosporins [31]. Their high prevalence is therefore consistent with the elevated resistance levels observed against amoxicillin. CTX-M enzymes are currently recognized as the predominant ESBL determinants worldwide [38] Resistance to β-lactam antibiotics is primarily mediated by β-lactamases, which hydrolyse the β-lactam ring, thereby inactivating the antibiotic. These enzymes are often encoded on mobile genetic elements such as plasmids and transposons [39], facilitating their dissemination across bacterial populations and environments [38]. The high rate of blaTEM in farm isolates indicates strong selective pressure at the production level, whereas the presence of blaCTX-M-9 in markets suggests horizontal gene transfer during commercialization. The co-occurrence of resistance to multiple antibiotic classes suggests the involvement of co-selection mechanisms and highlights the risk of multidrug resistance spread along the poultry production chain.

Although this study provides important data, several limitations should be considered. The relatively small samples size (n = 100) may limit the generalizability of the results. In addition, sampling was restricted to apparently healthy chickens, which may underestimate the true prevalence of pathogenic bacteria. Furthermore, only selected resistance genes were investigated, whereas other important molecular determinates may also circulate within poultry associated bacterial population. Finally, reliance on cloacal swabs alone may have limited the detection of certain bacterial populations. Further studies involving larger samples sizes, diverse production systems, whole-genome sequencing and multiple sampling sites are needed to better characterize bacterial ecology and antimicrobial resistance dynamics.

Despite these limitations, the results of this study clearly demonstrate that local chickens from farms and markets in Bobo-Dioulasso constitute important reservoirs of Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp antimicrobial-resistant. Strengthening regulations on the use of veterinary antibiotic, raising awareness among farmers, enhancing hygiene practices, and implementing integrated biosecurity measures according to the One health approach appear essential to limit the dissemination of resistant bacteria and preserve the effectiveness of antimicrobials in both animal and human health.

Conclusion

This study show that the cloaca swabs in markets and farms city from Bobo-Dioulasso are heavily contaminated by Escherichia coli and Salmonella species. Investigations have revealed the resistance of these to therapeutically important antibiotics. Molecular analysis revealed that strains were carriage of extended spectrum beta-lactamase encoding genes blaTEM and blaCTX-M-9 which are responsible to co-resistance between β-lactams and other antibiotics. The detection of these strains in faecal samples would implicate chickens in environmental pollution and food poisoning. It is therefore necessary to strengthen control measures and efforts in farms and market. Furthermore, awareness campaigns and training for livestock farmers on the risks of zoonosis and the transmission of antimicrobial-resistant strains must be intensified. Strict adherence to vaccination protocols and biosecurity measures reduce seriously losses in the poultry production. Endogenous health knowledge cans alternatives to fight diseases and boost poultry performance.
