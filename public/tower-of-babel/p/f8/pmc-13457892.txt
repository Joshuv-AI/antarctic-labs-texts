Feasibility of predicting breast cancer Ki-67 expression using histogram features derived from time-dependent diffusion magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Ju Sun, Zihan Zheng, Liumei Zhang, Liqiang Zhou, Yishuang Wang, Longlin Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThrough the quantification of multiple microstructural parameters, time-dependent diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (Td-dMRI) offers a novel approach for establishing urgently needed imaging biomarkers of tumor heterogeneity. This study employed Td-dMRI-derived histogram parameters to predict Ki-67 expression in breast cancer.MethodsA total of 86 female patients with breast cancer were enrolled in this prospective study. Microstructural parameters, including mean cell diameter (d), intracellular volume fraction (fin), intracellular diffusion coefficient (Din), extracellular apparent diffusion coefficient (Dex), and intracellular water pre-exchange lifetime (Tauin), were estimated using the JOINT model based on Td-dMRI data acquired with pulsed (PGSE) and oscillating (OGSE) gradient spin-echo sequences. Two additional parameters, intracellular water exchange rate (Kin) and cellularity, were subsequently derived through calculation. The apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) was calculated from the conventional diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), PGSE (32 and 52 ms), and OGSE (17 and 33 Hz) sequences. Histogram features were extracted based on parameters derived from Td-dMRI. The independent t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare differences between Ki-67 groups. The dataset was divided into a training set and test set at a ratio of 7:3 for internal validation. Clinical and conventional imaging characteristics identified by univariate regression analysis (P<0.1), together with histogram features selected by least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, were used to further establish a logistic regression model in the training set. Model performance was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves in the training and test sets. The area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were calculated.ResultsNine histogram parameters differed significantly between the Ki-67 expression groups; however, no significant difference in ADCDWI was observed (P=0.859). Axillary lymph node metastasis (ALNM) was identified as an independent predictor of high Ki-67 expression (odds ratio (OR) =2.991; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.964–9.278, P=0.058). Among the eight histogram features ultimately selected by LASSO regression, Cellularity_P10 and ADC33Hz_Skewness contributed the most to the model. The combined model achieved an AUC of 0.890 (95% CI: 0.809–0.971), with a sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of 89%, 65%, and 80%, respectively, in the training set, and an AUC of 0.870 (95% CI: 0.712–1), with a sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of 80%, 50%, and 73%, respectively in the test set.ConclusionsTd-dMRI-derived histogram features show promise for predicting Ki-67 expression in breast cancer and may complement conventional imaging.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the second most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide, accounting for 11.6% of all new cancer cases (1). Ki-67 serves as a marker of cellular proliferation in tumors. Elevated Ki-67 expression is associated with active tumor proliferation and an increased risk of cancer recurrence, reflecting underlying tumor heterogeneity (2,3). While biopsy remains the diagnostic standard for assessing Ki-67 expression, its limited sampling often fails to capture the spatial heterogeneity of lesions. Advances in precision medicine have further increased the demand for individualized patient management through precise diagnosis and recurrence risk assessment (4). Therefore, more specific imaging markers for characterizing tumor microstructures urgently need to be identified.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a crucial tool for breast cancer diagnosis, staging, and treatment response monitoring. Tumors with different Ki-67 expression levels exhibit variations in cellularity, cell size, and aggressiveness. These differences may be reflected on MRI through variations in necrosis, apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values, and contrast enhancement. However, these conventional imaging features cannot directly measure changes in tumor tissue microstructure. Additionally, the diagnostic value of conventional diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI)-derived ADC for assessing prognostic factors in breast cancer remains controversial (5-8). Moreover, ADC lacks intracellular microstructural specificity, as it represents a composite measure of water molecule diffusion in a voxel. Given the close association between cell size or cellular density, and tumor proliferation, invasiveness, and metabolism (9-11), cellular-level imaging could provide a more specific characterization of tumor heterogeneity.

The oscillating gradient spin-echo (OGSE) sequence uses rapid oscillations to achieve shorter diffusion times. Time-dependent diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (Td-dMRI) uses both OGSE and pulsed gradient spin-echo (PGSE) sequences at varying diffusion times to probe the time dependence of the ADC in tissues, yielding multiple parameters—such as cell size and the intracellular volume fraction (fin)—that characterize microstructural alterations in cells. To date, Td-dMRI has shown promising results in computer simulations (12), ex vivo cell experiments (13), and in vivo animal studies (14), as well as in several solid tumors, including prostate cancer (15), glioma (16), and breast cancer (13). However, clinical research on its use in breast cancer remains exploratory, and has primarily focused on the differentiation of benign and malignant lesions (17-19). A limited number of studies have also explored the value of Td-dMRI in predicting immunohistochemical (IHC) receptor status, molecular subtypes, or response to neoadjuvant therapy (20-22). However, most of these studies have only analyzed the mean values of voxel-wise microstructural parameters, ignoring the heterogeneous distribution within tumors. A study by Xu et al. (13) of seven breast cancer patients is one of the few to have performed histogram analysis of these parameters.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of histogram-derived metrics from Td-dMRI for predicting Ki-67 expression status in breast cancer. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0309/rc).

Methods

Study population

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This prospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Basic and Clinical Research of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital [approval No. Ethics Review (Research) 2025-313], and all patients provided informed consent.

We prospectively recruited 159 consecutive female patients with known or suspected breast lesions who underwent breast MRI from April 2025 to September 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) no history of surgery or other treatment prior to the MRI examination; (II) surgery or needle biopsy performed within one month after the MRI scan; and (III) pathologically confirmed primary breast cancer with a lesion diameter of at least 10 mm. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) poor image quality (low signal-to-noise ratio or artifacts); and/or (II) incomplete pathological results.

MRI data acquisition

All scans were performed using a 3.0-T MRI scanner (uMR 880, United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China) with a maximum gradient strength of 80 mT/m and a maximum slew rate of 200 mT/m/ms. Participants were scanned in the prone position using an external 24-channel phased-array breast coil for signal reception. The OGSE sequences were acquired at oscillating frequencies of 17 Hz (one cycle, effective diffusion time =15 ms, b-values =0, 250, 500, and 1,000 s/mm2) and 33 Hz (two cycles, effective diffusion time =7.5 ms, b-values =0, 250, and 500 s/mm2) with a separation time and duration time of 60 and 70 ms, respectively. The PGSE sequences were acquired with effective diffusion times of 32 and 52 ms with separation and duration times of 32 ms/15 ms and 52 ms/15 ms, respectively, and b-values of 0, 250, 500, and 1,000 s/mm2. All sequences used the following acquisition parameters: four diffusion-encoding directions; repetition time/echo time (TR/TE) =4,000 ms/150 ms; flip angle =90°; field of view (FOV) =190 mm × 360 mm; section thickness =5 mm; in-plane resolution =2.5 mm × 2.5 mm.

The conventional imaging protocol consisted of the following sequences: (I) axial T1-weighted imaging (TR/TE =557 ms/7.84 ms, FOV =340 mm × 340 mm, slice thickness =5 mm); (II) axial fat-suppressed T2-weighted imaging (TR/TE =4,400 ms/55.52 ms, FOV =340 mm × 340 mm, slice thickness =5 mm); (III) sagittal fat-suppressed T2-weighted imaging (TR/TE =3,200 ms/66 ms, FOV =180 mm × 180 mm, slice thickness =4 mm); (IV) conventional DWI (b-values =0, 800, 1,000 s/mm2; TR/TE =2,273 ms/87 ms, FOV =320 mm × 320 mm, slice thickness =5 mm); (V) dynamic contrast-enhanced imaging, including one pre-contrast and six post-contrast phases (TR/TE =3.7 ms/1.5 ms, FOV =360 mm × 360 mm, slice thickness =1.5 mm).

MRI data analysis

Three radiologists (with 7, 3, and 2 years of breast MRI diagnostic experience, respectively), who were blinded to the pathological results and clinical information of the patients, analyzed and recorded the lesion characteristics on conventional imaging according to the Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS), 6th edition. Inconsistent results were resolved by consensus. For conventional DWI, ADC values (ADCDWI) were derived by manually delineating regions of interest (ROIs) slice-by-slice on ADC maps using U-Viewer software (United Imaging Healthcare). For Td-dMRI sequences, ROIs were manually drawn slice-by-slice on OGSE images (frequency =33 Hz, b-value =0 s/mm2) using ITK-SNAP software (version 3.6.0). In all cases, care was taken to exclude necrotic areas, hemorrhage, and intralesional vessels during ROI delineation. For patients with multiple lesions, the largest lesion was chosen. All lesions were initially delineated by reader 1. A randomly selected cohort of 30 patients was then re-delineated by reader 1 one week later, and independently delineated by reader 2.

The Td-dMRI data were fitted using a JOINT model (23) with a least-squares curve fitting toolbox in MATLAB (MathWorks, Inc.), which simultaneously estimated five parameters: mean cell diameter (d, µm), fin, intracellular diffusion coefficient (Din, 10−3mm2/s), extracellular apparent diffusion coefficient (Dex, 10−3mm2/s), and intracellular water pre-exchange lifetime (Tauin, ms). Cellularity was determined as fin/d × 100, and the intracellular water exchange rate (Kin) was calculated as 1/Tauin. ADCs for the PGSE and OGSE sequences were estimated by the equation: S/S0 =exp (−b × ADC), and were denoted as ADC32ms, ADC52ms, ADC17HZ, ADC33HZ, respectively.

Feature selection

First-order statistical features (i.e., histogram-based parameters) were extracted using the Pyradiomics toolkit (version 3.1.0; https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/) in Python (version 3.7; http://www.python.org/). For each of the seven microstructural parameters and four ADC parameters, 10 histogram features were derived, including the 10th percentile, 90th percentile, energy, interquartile range (IQR), kurtosis, maximum, mean, median, minimum, and skewness. Intra- and inter-observer reliability of the extracted features was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) in 10% of randomly selected ROIs, with ICC >0.90 indicating stable feature extraction.

The dataset was randomly split into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%) for internal validation. In the training set, among the 110 original histogram features, those with P<0.05 in the Mann-Whitney U test were retained. Subsequently, to reduce collinearity, redundant features with Spearman’s rank correlation >0.9 were recursively removed, with 20 features ultimately retained. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was then introduced for further dimensionality reduction, and the optimal regularization parameter λ was determined using 10‑fold cross‑validation.

Histopathology

IHC marker expression was assessed using surgical specimens or biopsy tissues. Two experienced pathologists, who were blinded to the clinical information of the patients, independently evaluated all samples and reached a consensus. The IHC criteria were defined as follows: estrogen receptor and progesterone receptor positivity: ≥1% of tumor nuclei staining at ×10 magnification; human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status: scores of “0” or “1+” were considered negative, scores of “3+” positive, and scores of “2+” positive after verification by fluorescence in situ hybridization showing amplification; and Ki-67 positivity (high expression): ≥20% staining (24).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using scikit-learn package (version 1.0.1) in Python and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0). The normality of the continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (Q1, Q3). Intergroup comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, and all resulting P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR) at 0.05. Categorical variables were reported as frequency (percentage). In the training set, univariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify the clinical and MRI characteristics that were independent predictors of Ki-67 status (retaining variables with P<0.1), and these selected variables were then combined with histogram features chosen by LASSO regression to construct a logistic regression-based predictive model. The discriminative performance of the model was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were calculated. The Youden index was used to determine the optimal cutoff in the training set, which was then applied to the test set to calculate sensitivity and specificity. The reproducibility of the ROI measurements between the two physicians was assessed by the ICC. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise stated.

Comparison of ADCDWI

The mean ADCDWI was 0.926 (0.8, 1.12) mm2/s in the Ki-67-negative group and 0.918 (0.837, 1) mm2/s in the Ki-67-positive group. No statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups (P=0.859).

Inter/intra-observer reproducibility of histogram parameter measurements by the two radiologists

The intra-observer ICCs for the histogram parameters ranged from 0.734 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.516–0.863] to 0.957 (95% CI: 0.912–0.979). The inter-observer ICCs ranged from 0.703 (95% CI: 0.461–0.847) to 0.919 (95% CI: 0.837–0.960).

Univariate analysis

A total of 22 parameters differed significantly between the Ki-67 groups. After Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons (FDR =0.05), nine features remained statistically significant (Q <0.05) (Figure 2). Representative histograms and OGSE images from patients in both groups are shown in Figure 3. The univariate regression analysis indicated that axillary lymph node metastasis (ALNM) was associated with Ki-67 expression (Table 2). The enhancement pattern analysis showed that rim enhancement occurred exclusively in Ki-67‑positive cases, while homogenous/inhomogeneous enhancement was associated with both Ki-67-positive and Ki-67-negative cases. Feature selection and model development

Based on LASSO regression, the optimal regularization parameter λ was determined to be 0.036. Under this optimal value, eight histogram features with the highest predictive value were ultimately extracted. According to the coefficient values from feature selection, Cellularity_P10 and ADC33Hz_Skewness contributed the most to the model (Figure 4). Finally, ALNM was combined with the eight histogram features to construct a logistic regression model. The model achieved an AUC of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.809–0.971) in the training set, with a sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of 89%, 65%, and 80%, respectively. The model achieved an AUC of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.712–1) in the test set, with a sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of 80%, 50%, and 73%, respectively (Figure 5).

Discussion

This study employed voxel-wise histogram parameters derived from Td-dMRI to predict Ki-67 expression in breast cancer. The proposed diagnostic model incorporating ALNM and eight histogram features showed good performance in both the training (AUC =0.89, sensitivity =89%) and test sets (AUC =0.87, sensitivity =80%). However, the specificity of the model was relatively low, suggesting a higher rate of false‑positive predictions. The prevalence of Ki-67 positivity in our cohort was approximately 66% (57/86), which could bias the model toward predicting the positive class. Moreover, the relatively small sample size may have led to unstable estimates of specificity. Nevertheless, this model still shows certain feasibility for predicting Ki‑67 expression in breast cancer.

Ki-67 is primarily used to assess prognosis in early stage breast cancer, guide decisions regarding chemotherapy, and predict response to chemotherapy (25). Breast cancers with high Ki-67 expression are often characterized by increased cellularity, nuclear enlargement, and atypia, which are associated with greater tumor heterogeneity (26,27). Highly heterogeneous tumors are more likely to harbor aggressive subclones and exhibit a greater propensity for ALNM. In the univariate regression analysis, only ALNM was retained for the subsequent analyses, demonstrating that ALNM positivity is associated with high Ki-67 expression status.

Increased cellularity also contributes to reduced ADC values; however, studies evaluating ADC for predicting Ki-67 expression have yielded inconclusive results. For example, Horvat et al. (28) reported that the maximum ADC value obtained by delineating a two-dimensional ROI in the darkest portion of the tumor on the largest tumor slice could distinguish between high and low Ki-67 expression. The region with the lowest ADC may represent the area of highest cell density and greatest heterogeneity. Conversely, Fan et al. (29) observed no significant intergroup difference in ADC values, which is consistent with our findings. Conventional ADC provides limited microstructural specificity, as its interpretation is confounded by concurrent pathological processes, such as angiogenesis, necrosis, and cellular proliferation. In contrast, Td-dMRI enables probing across a broader range of diffusion length scales by combining OGSE and PGSE acquisitions, although the JOINT model used to fit Td‑dMRI parameters has inherent limitations, including its simplification to a two‑compartment model that assumes spherical cell structures.

Imaging microstructural parameters using limited spectrally edited diffusion (IMPULSED), pulsed and oscillating gradient MRI for assessment of cell size and extracellular space (POMACE) (30), and vascular, extracellular, and restricted diffusion for cytometry in tumors (VERDICT) (31) are currently the three most widely applied geometric models for Td‑dMRI post‑processing. The VERDICT model is a three‑compartment model that provides richer microstructural information; however, it requires a long scan time and suffers from suboptimal stability due to the large number of fitted parameters. The POMACE model has so far been primarily used in animal studies. Our study employed the JOINT model, which integrates the IMPULSED model with a modified Kärger model to account for the transcytolemmal water exchange neglected by IMPULSED, and includes the Tauin as an additional parameter. Malignant lesions may exhibit a prolonged intracellular water residence time, as indicated by elevated Tauin, possibly due to their complex cellular architecture and altered membrane permeability (32). Using the JOINT model, Jiang et al. (33) observed a decrease in apparent Tauin in breast tumor cells following treatment, which confirms that Td-dMRI may serve as a non‑invasive tool for evaluating treatment response. Our predictive model also incorporated a Tauin‑related feature (tauin_Maximum), which was positively correlated with Ki-67 expression.

Given that mean values can obscure tissue heterogeneity, histogram features may better reflect tumor heterogeneity. Consequently, this study focused on the histogram characteristics of microstructural parameters. Nine histogram features differed significantly between the Ki-67‑positive and Ki-67‑negative groups in this study. All these features were related to energy, and their values were higher in the positive group. Energy was defined as the sum of the squared gray‑level intensities across all voxels. Although Ki-67‑positive tumors are expected to exhibit lower ADC values due to higher heterogeneity and cell density, the four ADC_Energy parameters were paradoxically higher in this group. This may be because energy also depends on the number of voxels: larger tumors contain more voxels, resulting in higher energy values. Thus, the observed difference may reflect the larger tumor volume in the Ki-67‑positive group [32 (25–47) vs. 30 (20–40) mm]. In this study, Tauin_Energy was higher in the Ki-67‑positive group, indicating that the voxel‑wise Tauin values were generally greater in the positive group, which is consistent with the findings of Jiang et al. (33) and Li et al. (19).

Controversy continues in relation to the d of benign and malignant breast lesions. Li et al. (19) found that the d value was lower in the malignant group, but the difference was not statistically significant (P>0.05), while another study reported opposite results (34). Wu et al. (35) compared the microstructural parameters derived from three models (IMPULSED, JOINT, and EXCHANGE) in breast cancer. In all three models, the d value was larger in the Ki-67‑positive group, but the difference only reached statistical significance in the EXCHANGE model. For the other parameters (Dex, Din, fin, and Kin), no significant differences were observed between the groups across the models. Ba et al. (21) also found that the IMPULSED-derived Td-dMRI parameters failed to predict Ki-67expression. Thus, the role of these parameters in differentiating between benign and malignant breast lesions and predicting Ki-67 expression still needs to be confirmed in further studies.

In this study, d_Energy was higher in the Ki-67‑positive group than in the Ki-67‑negative group, which may suggest stronger proliferative activity and larger d in Ki-67‑positive tumors, although it may also be related to volume effects. Further studies should adjust for volume effects to better reflect changes in d values. We did not fix Din in this study, as it is usually unknown in vivo, and no consensus exists regarding an appropriate fixed value. Xu et al. (13) showed that fixing Din >1.4×10−3 mm2/s did not significantly affect the fitting results. In our cohort, fitted Din ranged from 0.975 to 2.363 µm2/ms [(1.518±0.301)×10−3 mm2/s], indicating that most cases were not severely affected. Nevertheless, fixing Din (e.g., >1.4×10−3 mm2/s) could improve fitting stability and serve as a useful reference for future studies. In this study, the fin_Energy value was higher in the Ki-67‑positive group, which may reflect increased cellular packing density and reduced extracellular space in Ki-67‑positive cells. This finding is consistent with previous studies comparing fin between benign and malignant breast lesions (19,34). However, the results for Dex_Energy were inconsistent with the theoretical expectation that Dex decreases as cellular density increases and extracellular space becomes narrower. Among the features included in our predictive model, Cellularity_P10 and ADC33Hz_Skewnes had the largest weights. Theoretically, tumors with high Ki-67 expression should exhibit active proliferation and greater cellular density. However, based on the coefficient value, Cellularity_P10 was negatively correlated with Ki-67‑positive expression in this study. Wu et al. (35) also reported higher cellularity in the low Ki-67 group across all models (P<0.05, which is consistent with our findings. The underlying reasons for these observations remain unclear. The diversity of pathological types in our cohort may have introduced confounding effects from noncellular components, which could partly explain the discrepancy between our results and theoretical expectations (36). Skewness quantifies the asymmetry of the voxel intensity distribution within the ROI and serves as a key marker of tumor heterogeneity. The positive correlation between ADC33Hz_skewness and Ki‑67 expression in our model suggests that Ki-67‑positive tumors are more heterogeneous. This study employed oscillating frequencies of 17 Hz and 33 Hz due to the gradient limitations of the MRI scanner. Wu et al. (15) used the same frequencies to distinguish between clinically significant and non-significant prostate cancer, with cellularity achieving the highest AUC (0.96). Bao et al. (37) also used the same frequencies to differentiate between benign and malignant breast tumors. Although no standardized oscillating frequency has been established for OGSE, Ba et al. (21) found that shorter diffusion times reduced fitting errors in d estimation and suggested that an oscillation frequency as high as 50 Hz may be necessary. Differences in oscillation frequency may influence predictive outcomes, potentially contributing to discrepancies among studies. As the gradient performance of MRI systems continues to improve, higher oscillation frequencies are expected to further enhance diagnostic capability.

This study had several limitations. First, as a single-center prospective study, the sample size was small. Second, the OGSE sequence employed only one set of relatively low oscillation frequency, precluding comparisons across multiple frequencies. Third, the analysis focused exclusively on the histogram features derived from Td-dMRI, without directly analyzing the parameters themselves. Fourth, pathological confirmation in some patients relied on needle biopsy, which is susceptible to sampling error from tumor spatial heterogeneity. Fifth, due to the small sample size, subgroup analyses were not performed for specific pathological subtypes. Finally, the comparatively low spatial resolution of Td-dMRI images may have affected measurement accuracy in smaller lesions, particularly for statistics such as skewness and kurtosis. Therefore, our results should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with higher spatial resolution are needed to validate and extend our findings.

Conclusions

This study developed a model for predictng Ki-67 expression in breast cancer that integrated ALNM and whole-voxel histogram parameters derived from Td-dMRI. The model exhibited high diagnostic performance and represents a potential noninvasive adjunct tool for predicting Ki-67 expression, thereby providing more comprehensive insights into tumor heterogeneity. Future studies should aim to validate our model in larger, independent cohorts with more balanced class distributions.
