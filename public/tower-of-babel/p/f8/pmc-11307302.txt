Recovery of Cu, Co, and Fe from Pyrite Cinder Based on Mineral Phase Reconstruction
Authors: Hong Yu, Long Liu, Guanhua Chen, Xianlin Zhou, Manman Lu, Hanquan Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Pyrite cinder (PyC) containing polymetallics is difficult to use due to the low grade of metals and complex mineral phase composition, the low reutilized rate of which causes the wastage of resources. In this paper, a novel approach based on mineral phase reconstruction was proposed to recover Cu, Co, and Fe from PyC. A feasible reduction roasting process was developed for mineral phase reconstruction, followed by leaching with sulfuric acid to recover Cu and Co; finally, the leaching residue was separated by a magnetic tube to recover Fe. The maximum copper and cobalt extract rates of 86.15 and 79.61% were achieved respectively under the optimized conditions of reduction roasting for 30 min at 550 °C and 30% CO/N2 volume fraction, followed by leaching for 4 h at a liquid–solid ratio of 4:1 (mL/g), a mass concentration of 160 g/L sulfuric acid, and a temperature of 70 °C. The iron concentrate can be obtained with 63.08% Fe grade and 98.91% recovery rate by magnetic separation at a magnetic field strength of 28.26 kA/m. The mechanism analysis results of mineral phase reconstruction revealed that the primary copper sulfide in PyC was transformed into free copper oxide, combined copper oxide, and secondary copper sulfide without changing the valence state of copper by reduction roasting, resulting in a higher extraction rate of copper. Meanwhile, cobaltosic oxide and cobalt ferrite in PyC were transformed into cobalt sulfate and cobalt sulfide with the reduction of Co(III) to Co(II), improving the extraction rate of cobalt.

Introduction

Pyrite cinder (PyC) is the waste residue from pyrite oxidation roasting to produce sulfuric acid,1,2 and about 15 million tons of pyrite cinder were produced annually, accounting for approximately 30% by weight of the chemical waste produced in China.3 It has long been expected to be utilized as an ironmaking raw material as it contains a large amount of iron.4 Elements such as Cu, Pb, Zn, and Co are often associated with pyrite,5 which will eventually remain in the pyrite cinder after oxidation roasting, which are harmful elements for iron and steel smelting, also restricting the application of pyrite cinder in the iron industry.4,6 In comparison to iron, these nonferrous metals exhibit higher values; for instance, Co is widely used in batteries for new energy vehicles, medical and health materials, aerospace alloys, and catalysts.7,8 However, due to the low content and fine grain size, it is difficult to extract these high-value nonferrous metals from pyrite cinder with mainstream physical methods including magnetic separation, floatation, and gravity separation, resulting in the wastage of resources. Therefore, approaches such as acid-leaching, chlorination roasting–leaching,9 hydrothermal alkali melting–chlorination roasting,3 phosphoric acid-leaching,10 magnetizing roasting–magnetic separation,11 and direct reduction12 were proposed for the recovery of nonferrous metals from pyrite cinder and the provision of high-quality raw materials for ironmaking.

Acid-leaching methods with a low concentration of strong acids such as phosphoric acid, hydrochloric acid, and sulfuric acid were proposed to recover valuable metals from pyrite cinder in previous studies.10 After the removal of impurities and valuable metals, the leaching residue was used as an ironmaking raw material. However, the leaching selectivity was rather poor, a large amount of iron was dissolved out, and also the recovery rate for some metals was low as some mineral phases were insoluble in acids.13 In order to transform insoluble mineral phases into soluble mineral phases, pyrometallurgical processes, such as chlorination roasting, were used to separate valuable metals and heavy metals efficiently. However, grievous environmental pollution and serious equipment corrosion are unavoidable.10

In this research, a novel reduction method under a controlled atmosphere was adopted to reconstruct the mineral phases of iron, copper, and cobalt in the pyrite cinder. The refractory mineral phase was directionally transformed into an easily leachable phase through reduction roasting to recover valuable iron, copper, and cobalt resources in the slag. Effects of roasting conditions, including temperature, atmosphere, and time, and leaching conditions, including temperature, time, solid–liquid ratio, and acid concentration, on the recovery rate of copper and cobalt were investigated in this study. In addition, X-ray diffraction (XRD) and chemical phase analyses were performed to research the mechanism of mineral phase reconstruction.

Methods

Mineralogical Analysis

Characterization

An X-ray diffusion (XRD) test was performed to determine the phase of the pyrite cinder. An XRD test was performed with a diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (RIGAKU D/Max 2500, Tokyo, Japan). The tube current and voltage were 250 mA and 40 kV, respectively, the scanning range was 10–70°, and the step size was 0.02°. The data of the peaks were identified by referring to the JCPDS database. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) of the samples was performed using a ThermoFischer ESCALAB Xi+ X-ray photoelectron spectrometer. An Al Kα X-ray source (hv = 1486.6 eV) was employed, and the anode was operated at 12.5 kV and 16 mA (detailed operating conditions can be found in ref (14)). The binding energies were calibrated according to the binding energies of C 1s at 284.8 eV.

Chemical Phase Analyses

The contents of copper and cobalt in the pyrite cinder were very low compared with that of iron, which could not be detected by XRD. Therefore, it was necessary to analyze the phases of copper and cobalt by the chemical method. Copper phases generally involve copper sulfate, free copper oxide (e.g., malachite, azurite, cuprite, and tenorite), combined copper oxide (e.g., chrysocolla, copper combined with gangue), secondary copper sulfide (e.g., chalcocite, bornite, and covellite), and primary copper sulfide (e.g., chalcopyrite). The chemical phase analysis method of copper is described in detail in ref (15). Similarly, cobalt phases are generally classified into five phases, including cobalt sulfate (CoSO4), cobalt sulfide (CoS), cobaltous oxide (CoO), cobalt ferrite (CoFe2O4), and cobaltosic oxide (Co3O4). The analysis method was as follows: 0.5 g of sample was mixed with 100 mL of 5% acetic acid, leached for 1 h at ambient temperature, and then filtered and washed with deionized water; the content of cobalt sulfate was the cobalt content of the leachate determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). The leaching residue after the determination of copper sulfate was placed in a 250 mL shrink-flask, to which 60 mL of saturated bromine water was added, then filtered, and washed with deionized water after leaching for 1 h; the cobalt concentration in the leachate was the content of cobalt sulfide. Next, the residue after cobalt sulfide analysis was mixed with 100 mL of 10% acetic acid–5% hydrogen peroxide and leached in a near-boiling water bath for 1 h. The cobalt concentration in the leachate constituted cobaltous oxide. Then, the residue after cobaltous oxide analysis was mixed with 100 mL of 2% sulfuric acid–3% ammonium fluoride–5% hydrogen peroxide, leached in a near-boiling water bath for 1 h, and the result of cobalt concentration in the leachate represented cobalt ferrite. Finally, the cobalt content of the residue constituted cobaltosic oxide. The concentration of various elements was determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, 7700 series, Agilent Technologies).

Elemental Analysis

The iron grades of all samples were determined through the Chinese standard organization titrimetric method GB/T 6730.65–2009 with potassium dichromate as a titrant,16 while the copper and cobalt in the samples were analyzed by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, 7700 series, Agilent Technologies).

Reduction Roasting

40 g of pyrite cinder was placed in a corundum crucible. The corundum crucible with the sample was roasted in a tubular atmosphere furnace under certain conditions. Subsequently, the roasted sample was cooled in an inert atmosphere.

Leaching

Considering the loss of weight of the sample during reduction roasting, a part of the roasted sample (30 g) was taken for the leaching test. The sample was placed in a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask to carry out Cu and Co leaching tests under certain conditions. The Erlenmeyer flask was placed in a thermostated water bath to keep the leaching temperature constant. Results

Roasting Temperature

The reduction roasting experimental conditions were controlled at a roasting time of 60 min and roasting temperatures of 500, 550, 600, 650, and 700 °C. The roasting atmosphere was controlled at a CO/N2 ratio of 30%. Then, the leaching experiments of the roasted ore were carried out. The leaching conditions were as follows: the leaching temperature was 70 °C, the stirring rate was 400 r/min, the liquid–solid ratio was 4:1 mL/g, the sulfuric acid mass concentration was 160 g/L, and the leaching time was 4 h. The extraction rates of Cu, Co, and Fe are displayed in Figure 3a. Subsequently, the leaching slag was magnetically separated under a magnetic field strength of 28.26 kA/m; the results are displayed in Figure 3b.

Effects of roasting temperature on the extraction rate of metals (a) and magnetic separation of the leaching residue (b).

The results indicated that the roasting temperature had a remarkable effect on the extraction rate of copper and cobalt up to 550 °C. It can be seen in Figure 3a that the extraction rates of Cu and Co increase slowly with increasing temperature and then drop sharply when the roasting temperature exceeds a certain value. The extraction rate of Fe increases slowly as the temperature increases. The main reason for the cliff-like decline of Cu and Co extraction rates was that the mineral phase transformation of Cu and Co occurred with an increase in the roasting temperature, which will be discussed in detail in the following sections. At the same time, the phase of iron was gradually transformed from Fe2O3 to Fe3O4. Subsequently, the leaching slag was separated by magnetic separation to recover Fe, and the separation indexes are displayed in Figure 3b. The curves shown in Figure 3b indicate that the recovery rate and grade of Fe had already reached a high level at roasting temperatures of 500, 550, and 550 °C, respectively. When the roasting temperature increased, these values decreased slightly, but the fluctuations were not large. Considering the high extraction rates of Cu and Co, as well as the recovery rate and grade of Fe, the reduction roasting temperature of 550 was selected for subsequent experiments.

Roasting Time

To investigate the effect of roasting time on the cobalt, copper, and iron leaching efficiency and Fe recovery of magnetic separation, five time-intervals were employed at a roasting temperature of 550 °C. As can be seen in Figure 4a, the copper and cobalt extraction rates increased slightly at first and then declined with increasing roasting time. The extraction rates of Co and Cu reached the highest levels of 83.79 and 88.90% at roasting times of 60 and 75 min, respectively. In addition, the iron extraction rate increased from 38.18 to 53.37% at roasting times ranging from 30 to 90 min. It is clear that an appropriate roasting time was important for improving copper and cobalt extraction rates and reducing iron dissolution.

Effects of roasting time on the extraction rate of metals (a) and magnetic separation of the leaching residue (b).

Figure 4b shows the results of the magnetic separation of the leaching residue. The figure shows that the iron recovery rate did not undergo significant change while the iron grade decreased with increasing roasting time. In this range of roasting time, the iron recovery rate was greater than 98%, but the iron grade decreased from 63.77 to 57.57%. Combined with the results of the extraction rate and iron recovery of the leaching residue, the 60 min roasting time was appropriate.

Reducing Atmosphere

The composition of the atmosphere plays a significant role in reduction roasting, which in turn affects the leaching of copper and cobalt and iron recovery from the leaching residue. The leaching experiments on copper, cobalt, and iron and iron recovery from the reduction roasting of pyrite cinder were carried out in a reducing atmosphere varying from 10 to 40%, and the results are plotted in Figure 5. The results shown in Figure 5a demonstrate that a reducing atmosphere can transform the phases of copper, cobalt, and iron in reduction roasting to facilitate the leaching process. With an increase in CO from 10 to 30% in the reducing atmosphere, the extraction rates of copper, cobalt, and iron improved from 74.00 to 82.03%, 49.56 to 79.61%, and 39.12 to 47.87%, respectively. On a further increase in the CO content from 30 to 40%, the results showed no evident influence on the leaching process, indicating that the phases transformations of copper, cobalt, and iron were no longer affected by the composition of the atmosphere.

Effects of the reducing atmosphere on the extraction rate of metals (a) and magnetic separation of the leaching residue (b).

The effect of the reducing atmosphere on the recovery of iron from magnetic separation after leaching can be seen in Figure 5b. The data showed that the recovery rate of iron increased with an increase in the CO content from 10 to 20% and then underwent little change with a further increase in CO. However, the grade of the iron concentrate reached the maximum of 63.08% in a reducing atmosphere of 30% CO/N2. Considering the consumption of energy, reduction roasting efficiency, and environment, a reducing atmosphere of 30% CO/N2 was employed in the next experiments.

Leaching Temperature

The effect of temperature on the extraction rate with 160 g/L H2SO4 was investigated from 25 to 85 °C, while other leaching parameters remained constant. The results presented in Figure 6a show that the extraction rates of Co and Fe reached the highest values of 79.61 and 47.82%, respectively, at 70 °C after leaching for 4 h. As the leaching temperature was further increased, the extraction rates of Co and Fe started to decrease significantly. Nevertheless, the increase in the extraction rate of Cu was sustained with an increase in the leaching temperature from 25 to 85 °C.

Effects of leaching temperature on the extraction rate of metals (a) and magnetic separation of the leaching residue (b).

The Fe recovery rate and Fe grade of the iron concentrate obtained by magnetic separation with the leaching residue are illustrated in Figure 6b, which shows that the recovery rate of Fe was high throughout the leaching temperature range, exceeding 98%, and the grade of Fe was significantly increased from 60.10 to 63.08% in the temperature range of 25–70 °C. This implied that more impurity elements of reduction roasting products dissolved in the solution with increasing leaching temperature, and the grade of the iron concentrate obtained by magnetic separation after leaching was improved.

Liquid–Solid Ratio

The data presented in Figure 7 reveal the effects of the liquid–solid ratio on the extraction rates of Cu, Co, and Fe and Fe recovery from the leaching residue. It is noticed from Figure7a that the extraction rates of Cu and Co were as high as 86.15 and 79.61% at a liquid–solid ratio of 4:1, respectively. Further increasing the liquid–solid ratio showed no evident improvement in the extraction rate. The leaching process of Fe was significantly promoted with a liquid–solid ratio in the range of 3:1 to 6:1, and the extraction rate increased from 29.88 to 62.25%, which suggested that increasing the liquid–solid ratio can reduce the content of iron in the leaching residue, which in turn reduces the recovery of iron from the leaching residue by magnetic separation.

Effects of the liquid–solid ratio on the extraction rate of metals (a) and magnetic separation of the leaching residue (b).

In addition, Figure7b depicts that the Fe recovery rate from the iron concentrate decreased slightly with an increase in the liquid–solid ratio. The Fe grade reached the highest value of 63.08% at a liquid–solid ratio of 4:1 and then decreased with an increase in the liquid–solid ratio.

Sulfuric Acid Concentration

The influence of the initial sulfuric acid concentration on the extraction rates of Cu, Co, and Fe were investigated at sulfuric acid concentrations of 40, 80, 120, 160, and 200 g/L at 70 °C, as shown in Figure 8. Leaching results showed that the initial sulfuric acid concentration had a significant effect on Cu, Co, and Fe extraction rates (Figure 8a), and it could be seen that the extraction rates of Cu, Co, and Fe increased with increasing leaching time. Especially when the concentration of sulfuric acid increased from 40 to 200 g/L, the extraction rate of iron increased from 18.02 to 63.04%, which is unfavorable to the subsequent recovery of iron from the leaching residue. Results of Figure 8b clearly show that the grade and recovery rate of Fe both decreased within the sulfuric acid concentration range of 40–200 g/L. Considering the high copper and cobalt extraction rates and the high iron recovery rate from the leaching residue, 80 g/L sulfuric acid concentration is considered suitable.

Effects of sulfuric acid concentration on the extraction rate of metals (a) and magnetic separation of leaching residue (b).

Leaching Time

Figure 9 illustrates the effect of increasing the leaching time from 1 to 5 h on Cu, Co, and Fe extraction rates. As can be seen from Figure 9a, the extraction rates of Cu and Co were enhanced after 4 h of leaching, while the extraction rate of copper increased and that of cobalt decreased with further extension of the leaching time. However, the leaching rate of Fe showed no significant change.

Effects of leaching time on the extraction rate of metals (a) and magnetic separation of leaching residue (b).

Figure 9b shows that the grade and recovery rate of Fe increased with increasing leaching time up to 4 h, beyond which they decreased slightly as a function of time. The results may be due to the greater dissolution of impurity elements into the solution, while the dissolution rate of iron did not change significantly with increasing leaching time, thus improving the iron grade and recovery rate in the iron concentrate by magnetic separation. Combining Figure 9a and b, leaching for 4 h is appropriate, the extraction rates of copper and cobalt are 86.15 and 79.61%, respectively, the dissolution rate of iron is 47.82%, the grade of the iron concentrate obtained by magnetic separation is 63.08%, and the recovery rate is 98.91%. The dissolution rate of iron in sulfuric acid solution is more than 10%, as mentioned in ref (17). This indicated that the activity of iron in the PyC was enhanced after reduction roasting, resulting in an increased dissolution rate of iron in sulfuric acid solution.

Discussion

The results show that the maximum copper and cobalt extract rates of 86.15 and 79.61% were reached, respectively. The leaching residue was separated by a magnetic tube, and the iron concentrate was obtained with 63.08% Fe grade and 98.91% recovery rate. The data show a satisfactory result compared with previously reported studies.3,10,14 Thus, the high-efficiency synchronous recovery of iron, copper, and cobalt from the pyrite cinder under the optimized experiment conditions was realized.

In order to deeply understand the mechanism of the high recovery rate, the mineral phase transformations of Cu, Co, and Fe during the recovery process were analyzed in detail. Reduction roasting mainly influences metal extraction, as it can transform the phases of elements such as copper, cobalt, and iron and produce new mineral phases that are more easily dissolved in sulfuric acid solutions. In the process of reduction roasting, the most important factor is the temperature for the phase transformation of the minerals in the pyrite cinder. Therefore, the effects of the roasting temperature on the transformation of the mineral phase and the dissolution of minerals were investigated in the reduction roasting process.

Mineralogical Analysis

Iron

The XRD patterns of the pyrite cinder after reduction roasting in the range of 500–700 °C were determined by using 30% CO/N2 as reductants. As can be seen from the results of Figure 10, the diffractograms show the (220), (311), (222), (400), (422), (511), and (440) planes of a typical magnetite phase (JCPDS card 19-0629),18,19 and then the diffraction peaks can be indexed as hematite (JCPDS card 33-0664), with major diffraction peaks assigned to the (012), (104), (113), (024), (116), and (300) planes.18,20,21 The XRD spectrum indicates that the main phases in the samples were magnetite and hematite, containing a small amount of quartz, because iron was the main element in the pyrite cinder, constituting 54.87%. With an increase in the reduction roasting temperature, the intensity at a 2θ of 35.4° gradually increased and the intensity at a 2θ of 33° gradually weakened, which implied that Fe(III) was reduced to Fe(II). Thus, the content of Fe3O4 in RPyC increased gradually and the content of Fe2O3 decreased gradually. As Fe(II) dissolved more easily in sulfuric acid than Fe(III), the iron extraction rate increased from the roasted pyrite cinder (Figure 3a).

Copper

The main copper-bearing phases in samples were determined by chemical analysis, and the results are shown in Table 2, wherein copper was mainly present in four phases, namely, free copper oxide, combined copper oxide, and primary copper sulfide in PyC, as well as a small amount of secondary copper sulfide. Compared with PyC, the content of primary copper sulfide and copper sulfate in RPyC decreased in this temperature range, and other copper phases increased, indicating that copper sulfate and primary copper sulfide underwent phase transformation. The results shown in Table 2 indicate that copper sulfate is mainly converted to tenorite, which is one of the free copper oxides in a strong reducing atmosphere, as shown in eq 3, and the leaching rate of cuprite was improved by the oxidation of Fe3+ in the leaching solution. It is well-known that the primary copper sulfide is chalcopyrite, from which it is difficult for sulfuric acid to leach the copper.14,22 Therefore, the transformation of primary copper sulfide to other copper phases can improve the extraction rate of copper. After 4 h of leaching, the content of copper sulfate in ARPyC displayed that almost all of the copper sulfate was dissolved in the sulfuric acid solution. Furthermore, when the reduction roasting temperature was less than 550 °C, more free copper oxide and combined copper oxide in ARPyC dissolved, and when it exceeded 550 °C, more secondary copper sulfide and primary copper sulfide dissolved.3

Cobalt

The results displayed in Table 3 show that PyC contained a large amount of cobalt ferrite (CoFe2O4) and cobaltosic oxide (Co3O4), which made it difficult to be directly leached with sulfuric acid because cobalt ferrite has a spinel structure and they are both insoluble in sulfuric acid. Table 3 reveals that the cobalt in RPyC exists mainly in the forms of cobalt sulfate, cobalt sulfide, and cobalt ferrite, as well as a small amount of cobaltous oxide. However, the cobaltosic oxide was not detected in RPyC, which suggested that the trivalent cobalt in cobaltosic oxide was reduced to divalent cobalt, resulting in an improved extraction rate of Co after reduction roasting. With an increase in the roasting temperature, the content of cobalt sulfate in RPyC increases, but the extraction rate of cobalt decreases when the roasting temperature exceeds 600 °C (see Figure 3a), which indicates that cobalt sulfate may be coated with magnetite, which reduces the extraction rate of cobalt. This can be demonstrated by the cobalt sulfate content in the leaching residue (ARPyC). Typically, Figure 11b clearly shows that cobaltosic oxide disappeared, cobalt ferrite decreased from 32 to 18%, and cobalt sulfate increased from 43 to 75% in the pyrite cinder after reduction roasting at 550 °C. Up to 70% of cobalt ferrite remained in ARPyC, indicating that the phase of cobalt is difficult to extract by sulfuric acid.

XPS Analysis

In order to verify the behavior of the phases of copper and cobalt during the leaching process of the sample, XPS analysis of the sample was performed, and the results are shown in Figure 12. The Cu 2p core level spectra (Figure 12a) show four peaks at binding energies of 933.3, 938.9, 943.5, and 953.2 eV, respectively, corresponding to Cu 2p3/2, two satellite peaks, and Cu 2p1/2 in the sample of PPyC.23−25 The results reflect the presence of the Cu(II) state and the absence of the metallic state or the Cu(I) state in the sample of RPyC considering the shakeup satellite peaks at 938–946 eV and the lower binding energy of the metallic state or Cu(I) (normally the binding energy for Cu 2p3/2 is below 933.0 eV).25,26 In addition, the peak of the binding energy was not observed from the high-resolution spectra of Cu 2p for ARPyC, which suggested that the copper of RPyC was nearly dissolved in sulfuric acid. The results of XPS revealed that Cu(II) is not reduced to low-valence state copper but only undergoes a phase transition; it is possible that the reduction of copper requires a temperature higher than 550 °C.

High-resolution spectra of samples (a) Cu 2p and (b) Co 2p (reduction roasting: roasting for 30 min at 550 °C and 30% CO/N2 volume fraction; leaching: leaching for 4 h at a liquid–solid ratio of 4:1 mL/g, a mass concentration of 160 g/L sulfuric acid, and a temperature of 70 °C).

The spectrum of Co in Figure 12b depicts four peaks at 780.6, 783.3, 786.7, and 791.3 eV, respectively, corresponding to the Co 2p3/2 (low binding energy) and satellite peaks (high binding energy).27,28 According to ref (29), the Co in RPyC and ARPyC was divalent cobalt, consistent with Co 2p3/2 of 780.6 eV and the satellite peak of 786.7 eV. In addition, in ref (30), Co(II) has four peaks attributed to Co 2p3/2, the binding energy at 2p3/2 of high-valence cobalt (e.g., Co3O4) was less than 780 eV, and there was also a fifth peak, which proved that Co in the samples was bivalent cobalt, consistent with the results of the cobalt chemical phase analysis. Usually, the high-valence cobalt needs to be reduced to divalent cobalt; then, it can be easily extracted by sulfuric acid solution.31−33 The reduction roasting proved to be effective for the reduction of cobalt, and it transformed the high-valence phase of cobalt into the low-valence phase, thereby increasing the leaching rate of cobalt. Nevertheless, Co(II) still exists in the leaching residue, indicating that cobalt has not been fully extracted, which was because some cobalt may be wrapped by hematite. However, Co 2p1/2 was not observed in the range of 770–810 eV, which was different from the previously published literature.27,28,30 This may be caused by the low content of Co in the samples.

Mineral Phase Reconstruction Mechanism

Figure 13 shows the mineral phase reconstruction mechanism in the reduction roasting. In a strongly reductive atmosphere, the weakly magnetic mineral hematite is converted to strongly magnetic magnetite so that it can be separated by magnetic separation. In addition, cobaltosic oxide (Co3O4) is converted to cobalt sulfate (CoSO4). In contrast, the valence state of copper hardly changes but is converted from copper sulfate and primary copper sulfide to free copper oxide and combined copper oxide. CoSO4 and copper in copper oxide minerals can be simply extracted by sulfuric acid as shown in eq 4; meanwhile, minor amounts of CoFe2O4 and Fe3O4 were dissolved, as shown in eqs 5 and 6.456

Conclusions

Pyrite cinder, containing polymetallics, as an industrial waste slag, can be comprehensively utilized by mineral phase reconstruction with reduction roasting–leaching–magnetic separation. The results showed that maximum copper and cobalt extraction rates of 86.15 and 79.61% were achieved, respectively, under the conditions of reduction roasting for 30 min at 550 °C and 30% CO/N2 volume fraction, followed by leaching for 4 h at a liquid–solid ratio of 4:1 mL/g, a mass concentration of 160 g/L sulfuric acid, and a temperature of 70 °C. Furthermore, the leaching residue was separated by a magnetic tube; the iron concentrate can be obtained with 63.08% Fe grade and 98.91% recovery rate, which can be used as the raw material for ironmaking directly.

Mineralogical and XPS analyses indicated that reduction roasting can reconstruct the mineral phase. Under the conditions of reduction roasting at 550 °C, the primary copper sulfide and copper sulfate in PyC were transformed into combined copper oxide, free copper oxide, and secondary copper sulfide; in addition, the cobaltosic oxide and cobalt ferrite in PyC were transformed into cobalt sulfate and cobalt sulfide, which improved the extraction rates of copper and cobalt. Furthermore, reduction roasting changed the valence state of cobalt, reducing Co(III) to Co(II), which is easy to extract, while the valence state of copper did not change.
