Genome‐wide association analysis to identify QTLs and candidate genes associated with grain yield and its related traits under low light conditions in rice (Oryza sativa L.)
Authors: Swagatika Das, Soumya Mohanty, Darshan Panda, Nalini K Choudhury, Baneeta Mishra, Ranjan K Jena, Devanna BN, Reshmiraj KR, Awadesh Kumar, Khirod K Sahoo, Anil Kumar C, Rameswar P Sah, Sharat K Pradhan, Sanghamitra Samantray, Mirza J Baig, Lambodar Behera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLow‐light (LL) stress caused by persistent cloud cover during the Kharif season significantly reduces rice (Oryza sativa L.) grain yield (GY) by limiting photosynthesis, impairing assimilate production, and affecting reproductive development. To dissect the genetic basis of LL tolerance, 192 diverse rice genotypes were evaluated across contrasting light environments (LL and normal light under Rabi and Kharif seasons) and genotyped using a high‐density 44K single nucleotide polymorphism array. Integrating phenotypic and genomic data enabled a multi‐tiered analysis from quantitative trait locus (QTL) discovery to gene identification and haplotype dissection. Genome‐wide association analysis identified 305 QTLs associated with GY and 11 related traits, including 148 LL‐specific and 32 stable QTLs expressed across both seasons. Forty‐two candidate genes were localized within major QTL intervals, and 12 were identified as hub genes based on their key roles in photosynthesis, light perception, hormone signaling, and starch biosynthesis. These included Gn1a, OsPsbS1, OsAGPL2, OsLhcb1, OsAUX1, OsSBDCP1, OsNPF5.16, OsPHYA, OsPHYB, OsGIF1, HY5, and OsYUC11. Expression profiling confirmed stronger induction of OsPHYA (∼2.5‐fold) and OsPsbS1 (∼2.8‐fold) in LL‐tolerant genotypes like Purnendu and Swarnaprabha compared to susceptible lines. Haplotype analysis revealed several superior alleles, such as PHYA‐Hap2 and OsPsbS1‐Hap3, that were consistently associated with higher spikelet fertility, greater grain number, increased biomass, and improved GY under LL, with top‐performing haplotypes enhancing yield by 12%–18%. Genotypes carrying these haplotypes (e.g., Purnendu, Swarnaprabha, and Chamarmani) represent valuable breeding donors. Overall, this study provides the first genome‐wide identification of LL‐specific haplotypes in rice, together with biologically validated hub genes. These findings offer actionable genomic targets and donor resources for developing LL‐resilient, high‐yielding cultivars suited to changing climate and light‐limited environments.

INTRODUCTION

Low‐light (LL) stress is a major abiotic constraint affecting rice yield, particularly during the wet (Kharif) season in northeast and eastern India, Southeast Asia, and parts of China. This stress arises due to persistent cloud cover, which reduces photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) to as low as 600 µmol m−2 s−1, heavy rainfall, dense planting, and fluctuating light conditions (Murty & Sahu, 1987; Ramachandran & Kedia, 2011; Singh, 2005). LL stress negatively impacts photosynthesis by impairing the activity of Photosystem II and the Calvin cycle, leading to reduced carbohydrate production and energy availability for grain formation (Lichtenthaler & Burkart, 1999; Mathur et al., 2018). Under LL conditions, rice plants undergo various morphological, physiological, and biochemical changes, including increased height due to etiolation, delayed flowering, fewer tillers, and weaker stems, all of which arise from lower production of nonstructural carbohydrates like sucrose and cellulose. Therefore, LL stress leads to late flowering, reduced tillering, poor grain filling, and decreased spikelet fertility, causing yield losses ranging from 20% to 55% (Nayak & Murty, 1980; Voleti & Singh, 1996). In addition to direct physiological effects, LL conditions, often accompanied by elevated humidity and reduced canopy ventilation, create a microenvironment conducive to fungal pathogen proliferation. Increased susceptibility to diseases such as sheath blight and blast under LL stress further compounds yield losses, particularly in densely planted, irrigated field systems (Chakraborty et al., 2000). Further, these alterations make rice plants more prone to lodging (Hussain et al., 2019). The effects of LL stress are most pronounced during the reproductive phase, where limited energy supply results in fewer panicles, lower spikelet fertility, reduced grain number (GN), impaired grain filling, lower 1000‐grain weight (TGW), and decreased overall yield (Y. Chen & Tao, 2022; Panda et al., 2023).

LL stress is becoming increasingly common due to climate change‐driven factors such as heightened monsoon variability, prolonged overcast conditions, and irregular rainfall patterns. While much of the research has focused on South and Southeast Asia, LL stress is also emerging as a concern in other rice‐growing regions such as Sub‐Saharan Africa, Latin America, and East Asia, where cloud cover, shifting monsoons, and climate variability similarly disrupt light availability (Rebecca et al., 2018). This emphasizes the global relevance of identifying LL‐resilient rice traits for future‐ready breeding programs. These climatic shifts worsen LL stress during the Kharif season, especially in densely planted or poorly drained fields. Additionally, many high‐yielding modern rice varieties exhibit greater sensitivity to LL stress compared to traditional landraces, likely due to their narrow genetic base and breeding focus on high‐input systems (Ganguly et al., 2020). This emphasizes the urgency of identifying LL‐resilient traits for sustainable cultivation under climate uncertainty.

LL conditions also affect rice grain quality by reducing photosynthetic efficiency and carbohydrate synthesis, leading to smaller grains and lower yields (Shi et al., 2019). Starch composition is altered under LL, affecting the amylose‐to‐amylopectin ratio, which results in harder grains with inferior cooking properties. Essential micronutrients such as iron and zinc are also diminished in grains produced under LL stress (J. Chen et al., 2015). Some studies suggest a concentration effect that can give the illusion of increased protein content in grains (L. Zhang et al., 2018). Further, LL conditions lead to uneven grain filling and an increased number of chalky grains, which have lower quality and fetch lower market prices (Q. Li et al., 2022). Besides, the glycemic index of grains is increased under LL (A. Kumar et al., 2019). These findings highlight the importance of managing light conditions to ensure high‐quality rice production.

Molecular research has significantly advanced our understanding of how rice responds to LL stress, revealing a complex interplay of genes, transcription factors, photoreceptors, microRNAs, and metabolomic and proteomic changes. Transcriptomic analyses indicate that LL‐tolerant rice genotypes enhance the expression of genes encoding chlorophyll a/b‐binding proteins, light‐harvesting complexes (LHCs), and components of Photosystem I and II, all of which are essential for optimal light absorption and energy conversion (Sekhar et al., 2019). In light signaling pathways, transcription factors such as HY5 play a crucial role in regulating these genes, enabling plants to adapt to fluctuating light conditions (Lee et al., 2021). Photoreceptors like phytochromes (OsPHYA, OsPHYB, and OsPHYC) and cryptochromes (OsCRY1a and OsCRY1b) modulate physiological responses to improve light‐use efficiency under LL. Additionally, microRNAs such as miR156, miR160, miR399, miR167, and miR171 facilitate adaptive growth by regulating photosynthesis, hormone signaling, and glucose metabolism (Panigrahy et al., 2022; Sekhar et al., 2022; Y. Zhao et al., 2021). Metabolomic studies reveal changes in primary and secondary metabolites, including sugars, amino acids, and protective flavonoids, which support light absorption and stress resilience (K. Kumar et al., 2023). Proteomic analyses highlight the dynamic expression of proteins involved in photosynthesis, stress response, and energy management, demonstrating the plant's strategies to maintain homeostasis under LL conditions (Gupta et al., 2022). These findings highlight the highly coordinated molecular response that enables rice to preserve growth and yield despite LL stress. Recently, Panda et al. (2025) reinforced this by demonstrating that shade tolerance is not driven by a single leaf but results from a coordinated strategy involving improved radiation use efficiency, canopy‐wide foliar adaptations, and the robust expression of photosynthesis‐related genes.

Genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) have transformed plant genetics by providing a powerful approach to linking genetic variations with phenotypic traits. By leveraging natural genetic diversity, GWAS helps identify single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with key traits, including LL stress tolerance (H. Zhou et al., 2022). Rice, with its extensive genetic diversity shaped by widespread domestication and adaptation to various environments, is an ideal candidate for GWAS. High‐density SNP arrays such as the 44K, 50K, 90K SNP chips, and so forth, have enhanced GWAS resolution, allowing for the detection of major and minor effect loci. This precision is critical for pinpointing quantitative trait loci (QTLs) and candidate genes that confer LL tolerance and resilience to other environmental stresses (C. Li et al., 2022). Earlier GWASs identified numerous QTLs and candidate genes linked to important agronomic traits; however, most were constrained by small genotype panels or controlled experimental conditions that failed to capture the complexity of field environments, including variable light, soil heterogeneity, and natural diurnal cycles (Teng et al., 2023). Recent research has addressed these limitations. For example, Yadav et al. (2021) conducted GWAS on a small panel of 96 rice genotypes to identify marker‐trait associations (MTAs) for seedling stage salinity tolerance. Similarly, K. Liu et al. (2022) explored GWAS under different nitrogen levels, providing insights into nitrogen use efficiency. Integrating GWAS with functional genomics and transcriptomics offers a deeper understanding of the regulatory networks governing LL stress responses, opening new avenues for genetic engineering and molecular breeding (Tang et al., 2023).

To date, the genetic dissection of rice adaptation to LL conditions has primarily relied on biparental linkage mapping, and relatively few stable QTLs have been identified. One notable QTL identified through this approach, qTSN4, which is linked to the SPIKE gene, has been found to influence both carbon source traits (such as flag leaf size and leaf physiology) and sink traits (including panicle size and spikelet count per panicle) under LL stress (Fabre et al., 2016). Studies suggest that qTSN4 is a promising locus for further physiological research on rice adaptation to low‐radiation environments. Follow‐up investigations using near‐isogenic lines (NILs) carrying the qTSN4 locus in IR64 or IRRI146 backgrounds revealed that under LL conditions, the QTL's impact on spikelet number (SN) is mediated through its role in resource assimilation at the tiller level (Adriani et al., 2016). In our previous biparental study, we mapped 20 novel QTLs for yield and related traits under LL and normal light (NL) conditions using a recombinant inbred line (RIL) population derived from Swarnaprabha (LL tolerant) and IR8 (susceptible). Eleven QTLs were specific to LL, and nine were stable across environments, with major regions identified on chromosomes 1, 4, and 8. Candidate genes such as OsAUX1, OsSBDCP1, and OsNPF5.16 showed higher expression in the tolerant parent under shade, suggesting coordinated regulation of hormone signaling, carbohydrate metabolism, and nitrogen transport contributing to yield stability under LL stress (Mohanty et al., 2025). Similarly, utilizing biparental mapping, Saha et al. (2024) identified QTLs on chromosomes 1, 4, and 9 associated with spikelet fertility and grain yield (GY), including genes such as OsPsbS1, OsVDE, and OsZEP, which play roles in photoprotection and photosynthetic efficiency. Ganguly et al. (2024) also reported additional QTLs and genes linked to LL stress in rice through biparental mapping, notably a major QTL on chromosome 1, designated qPNLL1.1 or qGYLL1.1, which is essential for panicle number (PN) and GY under LL conditions. This QTL is associated with the MOC2 gene, which regulates shade response and is highly expressed in LL‐tolerant rice varieties. These studies provide valuable insights for marker‐assisted breeding to enhance rice resilience to LL stress; however, relatively little work has been conducted using GWASs on diverse panels, leaving the broader natural variation for LL tolerance largely unexplored.

Seasonal variations in light availability pose substantial challenges for rice cultivation. During the wet (Kharif) season, persistent cloud cover and dense planting contribute significantly to LL stress, which reduces PAR and is often accompanied by high humidity, pest infestations, and disease outbreaks, further compounding yield losses (S. Chen et al., 2022). In contrast, the dry (Rabi) season typically offers more stable light conditions but presents challenges such as cooler temperatures during vegetative growth and elevated temperatures during the reproductive phase, both of which interact with light availability to influence plant development. Understanding how different rice genotypes respond to these seasonal variations is crucial for breeding programs aiming to develop LL‐resilient varieties. This study addresses these challenges by evaluating a diverse set of rice genotypes across seasons to identify those that consistently perform well under LL stress. Understanding genotype performance across seasons is essential for developing season‐resilient rice varieties, particularly in double‐cropping systems common across Asia, where farmers depend on both Kharif and Rabi harvests for food and income security. Although significant advances have been made in characterizing rice responses to environmental stresses, critical knowledge gaps remain, especially regarding the genetic mechanisms underlying adaptation to LL stress across seasons.

While numerous molecular components involved in LL response, such as photoreceptors, light signaling pathways, and stress‐responsive transcription factors, have been identified, their associations with agronomic traits remain largely unvalidated at the haplotype level under field‐like LL conditions. Most prior studies have relied on limited or genetically narrow panels and controlled environments, which do not capture the complexity of genotype × environment interactions. Furthermore, the seasonal stability of allelic effects on performance‐related traits remains underexplored. To address these limitations, we conducted a GWAS on a panel of 192 genetically diverse rice genotypes evaluated under LL and NL conditions across two contrasting growing seasons (Kharif and Rabi). Twelve key agronomic traits were phenotyped, including GY, plant height (PH), flowering time, tiller number (TN), PN, GN, spikelet fertility, TGW, biomass, and harvest index, all of which are quantitatively inherited and environmentally responsive. The GWAS identified several trait‐associated markers, superior haplotypes, and key regulatory genes. The haplotypes on chromosomes 1 and 4 were associated with up to 30% higher GY and a 20%–25% increase in spikelet fertility under LL conditions. These findings provide valuable genomic resources to accelerate the molecular breeding of LL‐resilient rice cultivars suitable for diverse agro‐ecological zones and seasonal production systems.

The objectives of this study were to (i) identify genomic regions and candidate genes associated with LL tolerance and yield‐related traits across contrasting seasons and (ii) characterize superior haplotypes conferring stable performance under field‐based LL stress. These insights aim to provide molecular tools for breeding season‐resilient rice cultivars adapted to variable light regimes. Future research directions should focus on validating these haplotypes in multi‐location field trials and integrating these findings with functional genomics to elucidate the underlying mechanisms fully.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials

The study utilized a genetically diverse association mapping panel comprising 192 rice genotypes, including high‐yielding varieties, elite breeding lines from IRRI (International Rice Research Institute), traditional landraces, and genotypes commonly used as donor parents in breeding programs. In this panel, donor lines refer to genotypes known to harbor favorable alleles for key agronomic or stress‐responsive traits, which are routinely used as parental sources to introduce beneficial genetic variation into breeding populations. The panel represented a broad range of rice subpopulation backgrounds. Based on pedigree information and prior population structure analyses from IRRI, the majority of the genotypes belonged to the indica subgroup, with a smaller representation of indica‐derived improved lines, tropical japonica‐introgressed materials, and a few landraces with admixed ancestry. This subpopulation diversity was intentionally included to enhance allelic variation and improve the power of GWAS, while population structure was subsequently corrected in the association analysis. Among the 192 genotypes, 60 IRRI breeding lines were selected from a set of 72 Elite Core Panel genotypes, which collectively represent the genetic diversity of 15,286 IRRI breeding lines (including released varieties). Selection was based on breeding values for GY and high heritability estimates as reported by Juma et al. (2021). The widely grown variety Swarnaprabha was included as a check for LL tolerance, while IR8 served as a standard susceptible check.

Experimental design, treatments, management, and data collection

The field experiment was conducted at the ICAR (Indian Council for Agricultural Research)‐Central Rice Research Institute, Cuttack, Odisha, India, located in the deltaic plains of Eastern India (20°31′23′′ N, 085°47′17′′ E) at an altitude of 36 m above sea level. The soil at the site is alluvial with a neutral pH of 6.8. The region experiences a tropical wet and dry climate, with the Kharif season (June–November) characterized by an average temperature of 30°C and annual rainfall of approximately 1440 mm due to the southwest monsoon. In contrast, the Rabi season (December–May) features temperature fluctuations between 12 and 40°C, with minimal rainfall. The association panel was evaluated during both the Rabi and Kharif seasons of 2022 using an alpha‐lattice design with two replications. For the Rabi season (December–May), seeds were sown on December 10, 2021 and transplanted on January 10, 2022, whereas for the Kharif season (June–November), seeds were sown on June 17, 2022 and transplanted on July 18, 2022. Seasonal environmental variability was carefully documented because fluctuations in temperature, rainfall, and cloudiness can influence LL stress. The alpha‐lattice design consisted of 16 incomplete blocks per replication, each containing 12 genotypes (16 × 12 = 192). Each genotype was planted in a single 20‐plant row within its block, with 20 cm × 15 cm spacing. Seeds were sown in nursery beds, and 25‐day‐old seedlings were transplanted into experimental plots. Gaps were filled within 7 days to maintain uniform plant density. Standard agronomic practices and pest control measures were followed, and fertilizers were applied at 80 kg N, 40 kg P2O5, and 40 kg K2O ha−1. Half the nitrogen and all phosphorus and potassium were applied before transplanting, and the remaining nitrogen was split between the tillering and grain‐filling stages. To evaluate tolerance to LL conditions, one set of the 192 genotypes was grown under shade nets supported by wooden or bamboo frames (3.9 m × 3.9 m × 1.7 m), reducing incoming PAR by 50% in Rabi and 25% in Kharif, beginning 10–12 days after transplanting. A parallel set was grown under open‐field conditions (100% sunlight). Both treatments were replicated twice to allow direct comparison across seasons.

To minimize environmental variability and avoid confounding, shade structures were erected at uniform heights, and LL and control plots were placed adjacently within the same field so that all genotypes experienced similar soil, temperature, humidity, and pest pressure. Light intensity (PAR) was monitored every 5–7 days using a handheld quantum sensor to verify uniform shading and to document natural fluctuations. Based on long‐term climatological records for Odisha (IMD, 2022; IMD Climate of Orissa), the Kharif season is characterized by frequent cloud cover associated with the southwest monsoon, resulting in approximately 12–18 cloudy or overcast days per month. This prolonged reduction in natural irradiance is typical of the June–November period, during which intermittent rainfall and persistent monsoon clouds limit direct sunlight. In contrast, the Rabi season (December–May) remains predominantly clear, with only about 1–4 cloudy or partly cloudy days per month, as this period corresponds to the dry, post‐monsoon climate of eastern India. This seasonal contrast in sky conditions is well documented for Odisha and was considered while interpreting the imposed LL treatments to ensure that natural fluctuations in sunlight did not mask or confound treatment effects.

These natural fluctuations in the control plots were recorded and used to ensure that any brief reductions in ambient light did not mimic the sustained LL imposed by shade nets. Management operations (irrigation, fertilizer application, and pest control) were synchronized across treatments, and all phenotypic observations were carried out between 9:00 a.m. and 11:00 a.m. to reduce diurnal variation. Together, these measures ensured that observed phenotypic differences were primarily due to controlled light variation rather than uncontrolled environmental effects.

Phenotypic data were collected for GY and 11 related traits from five plants per replication and averaged across replications. Recorded traits included days to 50% flowering (DFF), PH, TN, PN, panicle length (PL), GN per panicle, SN per panicle, spikelet fertility percentage (SFP), panicle weight (PW), TGW, biomass (BIOM), and GY.

Statistical analyses such as descriptive statistics, analysis of variance (ANOVA), Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD), Pearson correlations, and principal component analysis (PCA) were performed using XL‐Stat software (ADDINSOFT, 2023). Genotyping was carried out using the Affymetrix Axiom 44K SNP array, with stringent filtering retaining 19,613 high‐quality SNPs. Genetic diversity and population structure analyses included Nei's genetic distance, fixation index (Fst), PCA, STRUCTURE (Pritchard et al., 2000), ADMIXTURE (Alexander et al., 2009), analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) (Smouse & Peakall et al., 2012), and linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay (C. Zhang et al., 2019). GWAS was conducted to identify significant MTAs and QTLs using GAPIT 3.0 software (Lipka et al., 2012), validated using multiple statistical models including compressed mixed linear model (CMLM), multi‐locus mixed model (MLMM), fixed and random model circulating probability unification (FarmCPU) (X. Liu et al., 2016), and Bayesian information and linkage association mapping (BLINK) (M. Huang et al., 2019). Candidate genes near significant MTAs were identified via bioinformatics analyses utilizing Rice Genome Annotation Project Database (RGAP) (Kawahara et al., 2013), QTARO, and Gramene databases, along with gene ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment. Expression of candidate genes under LL stress was quantified through quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR), normalized against actin. Superior haplotypes linked to beneficial phenotypes were identified using Haploview v4.2 (Barrett et al., 2005), aiding in the selection of donor genotypes for breeding purposes.

Genotyping of association mapping panel

Genomic DNA was extracted from a single 1‐month‐old seedling; leaves from each genotype using the GenElute Plant Genomic DNA Miniprep Kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturer's protocol. The DNA concentration was determined using both a Qubit Fluorometer and a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, while its quality and integrity were evaluated via 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Only high‐quality genomic DNA samples (>90% purity, fragment size >10 Kb) with OD260/OD280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 and OD260/OD230 ratios above 1.5 were selected and diluted to a final concentration of 50 ng µL−1 in Tris‐EDTA buffer for genotyping.

Genotyping was performed using the Affymetrix Axiom GeneChip Rice 44K SNP array on the GeneTitan platform. SNP clustering was conducted with Genotype Console Software (v4.1), ensuring a dish quality control value of at least 0.82 and an SNP call rate of 95% or higher. Stringent filtering criteria were applied, retaining only SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) of 0.05 and a maximum missing data threshold of 10%, resulting in a final set of 19,613 high‐quality SNPs. The SNP genotypes coded as −1/0/1 were analyzed under an additive genetic model, where −1 and 1 represent the two homozygous allelic classes and 0 denotes the heterozygote. Missing values were imputed using the mean genotypes for further analyses, including PCA. The genotyping and data processing were conducted by Nucleome Informatics Pvt. Ltd., Hyderabad, India.

Molecular diversity in association mapping panel

Genetic diversity and population structure were analyzed using various methods. Nei's genetic distance and the fixation index (Fst) were computed to evaluate genetic diversity and differentiation among populations, with pairwise Fst values providing insights into genetic divergence (Perrier & Jacquemoud‐Collet, 2006). PCA was conducted using R/GAPIT 3.0 (Lipka et al., 2012) to explain variance within the population, and the number of significant principal components (PCs) was determined through scree plots. Tukey's multiple comparison test was employed to assess differences between subpopulations. To examine population structure, 19,613 high‐quality SNPs from 192 rice genotypes were analyzed using STRUCTURE v2.3.4 (Pritchard et al., 2000) and ADMIXTURE v1.3.0 (Alexander et al., 2009), assuming correlated allele frequencies. The optimal number of subpopulations (K) was identified using Evanno's test (Evanno et al., 2005) in STRUCTURE HARVESTER (Earl & VonHoldt, 2012), and the results were visualized with R/pophelper (Francis, 2017). AMOVA was performed using GenAlEx 6.5 (Smouse & Peakall, 2012). LD was assessed using r 2 with a sliding window of 100 SNPs in GAPIT v3 (Lipka et al., 2012), considering SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) of at least 0.05. LD decay was analyzed using PopLDdecay (C. Zhang et al., 2019), which estimated the genomic distance at which r 2 declined to half its maximum value (X. Huang et al., 2010).

Genome‐wide association analysis for identification of QTLs associated with yield and related traits

A GWAS was conducted to identify genomic regions associated with GY and 11 related traits, utilizing 19,613 high‐quality SNPs and the GAPIT 3.0 software (Lipka et al., 2012). Multiple analytical models were employed, including the CMLM (Z. Zhang et al., 2010), MLMM, FarmCPU (X. Liu et al., 2016), and BLINK (M. Huang et al., 2019).

To minimize spurious associations arising from population stratification and relatedness, both population structure (Q) and kinship (K) matrices were integrated into all mixed‐model analyses. Population structure was captured using PCA implemented in GAPIT, where the first few PCs explaining major axes of genetic variation were included as fixed covariates. The optimal number of PCs for each trait was determined using the Bayesian information criterion and genomic inflation factors derived from quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots. Additionally, ancestry coefficients generated from STRUCTURE and ADMIXTURE analyses were used to validate the PCA‐derived population structure. Kinship (K) was estimated using the VanRaden algorithm, producing an additive relationship matrix that was incorporated as a random effect to control for cryptic relatedness among genotypes. In CMLM, kinship was further compressed by grouping genetically similar individuals, whereas in MLMM, FarmCPU, and BLINK, K was used iteratively to separate true marker effects from background polygenic noise. This Q + K framework ensured robust genomic control and reduced false‐positive associations.

Significant MTAs were identified using a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of ≤0.05 (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). Only MTAs surpassing this significance threshold and consistently detected across at least two models were considered reliable. The phenotypic variance (PV) explained by each significant SNP was estimated by calculating the squared correlation between genotype and phenotype using R scripts. Results were visualized through Q–Q plots and Manhattan plots generated with the R package qqman (Turner, 2014). QTLs were designated based on the associated traits and the chromosomal positions of significant SNPs, with numbering assigned according to chromosomal order. Additionally, chromosomal maps illustrating QTLs and MTAs were developed using MapChart v2.32 (Voorrips, 2002), with annotated loci and QTL intervals depicted as vertical bars along the chromosomes.

In silico analysis to identify candidate and hub genes

The most significant QTLs or MTAs, identified particularly under LL and both conditions, based on their consistency across different seasons and/or environmental conditions, were used for the identification of candidate genes. Genes located within a 200 kb region flanking the peak SNP were identified using the RGAP genome browser (Kawahara et al., 2013; https://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp/). To assess whether the identified SNPs or LD regions overlapped with previously reported QTLs or cloned genes, a literature review was conducted using the QTARO database (http://qtaro.abr.affrc.go.jp) and the Gramene database (http://www.gramene.org). Candidate genes from these regions were selected for further investigation to identify hub genes, including GO enrichment analysis, KEGG pathway analysis, and QTARO QTL associations. A comprehensive in silico analysis was performed using gene expression databases such as RiceXpro (https://ricexpro.dna.affrc.go.jp/), RED IC4R (http://expression.ic4r.org/), RGI Rice Gene Index (https://riceome.hzau.edu.cn), and NetREx (https://bioinf.iiit.ac.in/netrex/index.html). Only significant candidate genes (hub genes) meeting the FDR (≤0.05) threshold were considered for subsequent haplotype‐phenotype analysis.

Expression analysis of hub genes

To analyze the expression of the hub genes identified in this study, total RNA was extracted from both control (T0) and LL‐treated leaf tissues of tolerant and susceptible genotypes at the flowering stage for T1 (1 day), T5 (5 days), and T10 (10 days) using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer's protocol (Chomczynski & Sacchi, 1987). RNA integrity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and agarose gel electrophoresis (Wilfinger et al., 1997). For each genotype and treatment, three biological replicates (leaf tissue from independent plants) were collected, and cDNA was synthesized from 2 µg of total RNA using the High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen). qRT‐PCR was performed using the Agilent Brilliant III Ultra‐Fast SYBR Green Master Mix on a Bio‐Rad CFX Connect Real‐Time PCR Detection System. Gene‐specific primers were designed using Primer3 software (Untergasser et al., 2012) and validated for specificity and amplification efficiency, which ranged between 90% and 110%, falling within the MIQE‐recommended acceptable range for accurate ΔΔCT quantification (Bustin et al., 2009). A melt‐curve analysis was included at the end of each qPCR run to confirm single, specific amplicon formation and to eliminate primer–dimer artifacts (Taylor et al., 2010). Each reaction was performed with three technical replicates per biological replicate. Actin was used as the internal reference gene, and relative transcript levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method (Livak & Schmittgen, 2001). This workflow ensures reliable and reproducible quantification of gene expression changes under LL stress.

Haplotype analysis to identify superior haplotypes and donors

Haplotypes for each candidate hub gene were constructed using Haploview v4.2 (Barrett et al., 2005) by incorporating all polymorphic SNPs located within the gene body and its immediate flanking regions. To ensure adequate statistical power for comparison, major haplotypes were defined as those represented by at least ten genotypes in the association panel. Phenotypic variation among these haplotypes was evaluated using ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD test to identify statistically significant differences in trait performance.

Superior haplotypes were selected based on a combined criterion of statistical significance and biological relevance, rather than statistical tests alone. First, haplotypes that showed significant differences (p ≤ 0.05) for key agronomic traits under LL and combined LL–NL conditions were shortlisted. Second, among these statistically significant haplotypes, preference was given to those supported by functional or mechanistic evidence. This included haplotypes containing SNPs predicted to cause biologically meaningful changes, such as non‐synonymous substitutions, promoter or regulatory variants, or SNPs previously reported to influence gene function. The known or predicted biological roles of the corresponding genes in photosynthesis, photoprotection, hormone signaling, nutrient transport, starch biosynthesis, or reproductive development were also considered. Additionally, haplotypes that displayed consistent phenotypic advantages across both seasons and light environments were prioritized to ensure environmental stability.

Thus, superior haplotypes were identified only when they satisfied both statistical robustness and functional plausibility, ensuring that selected alleles represented genuine, biologically meaningful contributors to LL tolerance. Finally, donor genotypes carrying these superior haplotypes were identified. Priority was given to genotypes that consistently exhibited favorable performance under both LL stress and LL–NL conditions, enabling their use as reliable donors for marker‐assisted breeding aimed at improving LL resilience.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, ANOVA, Tukey's HSD tests, Pearson correlation analysis, and PCA were performed using XLSTAT (ADDINSOFT, 2023) to evaluate phenotypic variation across environments and treatments. ANOVA was conducted using a mixed‐model framework with genotypes considered as fixed effects and replications and blocks as random effects, appropriate for alpha‐lattice designs (Federer et al., 1975; Patterson & Williams, 1976). Trait means two replications were used for downstream genetic analyses. To enhance the statistical interpretation of treatment effects, effect sizes (Cohen's d) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for all LL–NL pairwise trait comparisons. Cohen's d quantified the magnitude of LL‐induced changes independent of sample size, whereas the CI provided an estimate of the precision of mean differences. These metrics were incorporated alongside p‐values to distinguish statistically significant but biologically small effects from large, meaningful changes in trait performance. Effect sizes and CIs were computed using pooled standard deviations and standard errors derived from trait variances across genotypes. Genotyping was performed using the Affymetrix Axiom 44K SNP array, and stringent filtering retained 19,613 high‐quality SNPs with minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.05 and missing data ≤ 10%. Genetic diversity and structure were assessed using Nei's genetic distance (Nei, 1972), fixation index (Fst) (Weir & Cockerham, 1984), PCA, STRUCTURE (Pritchard et al., 2000), ADMIXTURE (Alexander et al., 2009), and AMOVA (Smouse & Peakall, 2012). The optimal number of subpopulations (K) was inferred using the ΔK method (Evanno et al., 2005), and results were visualized using pophelper (Francis, 2017). LD was estimated using pairwise r2 values in GAPIT v3 (Lipka et al., 2012). LD decay was assessed with PopLDdecay (C. Zhang et al., 2019), defining the genomic distance at which r 2 dropped to half of its maximum as the LD decay threshold (X. Huang et al., 2010). Genome‐wide association study (GWAS) analysis was performed using GAPIT 3.0 with multiple models, CMLM (Z. Zhang et al., 2010), MLMM (Segura et al., 2012), FarmCPU (X. Liu et al., 2016), and BLINK (M. Huang et al., 2019). Both population structure (Q) and kinship (K) matrices were incorporated to control spurious associations due to stratification and relatedness, following standard Q + K GWAS methodology (Yu et al., 2006). Significant MTAs were identified using a FDR (≤0.05) (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). MTAs detected consistently across at least two models were considered reliable. Phenotypic variance explained (PVE) by each significant SNP was calculated as the squared correlation coefficient (R 2) between SNP genotype and trait value. Visualization of GWAS outputs, including Q–Q plots and Manhattan plots, was performed in R using the qqman package (Turner, 2014). Significant SNPs were organized into QTLs according to chromosomal positions, and QTL maps were generated using MapChart v2.32 (Voorrips, 2002).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Variations in GY and related traits

The analysis demonstrated that LL stress had a significant adverse effect on rice GY and 11 associated traits (Table 1; Figure 1). To confirm the consistency of the imposed LL treatment across seasons, PPFD (Photosynthetic Photon Flux Density) was monitored monthly at 12:00 p.m. and 3:00 p.m. (Figure S1). During Rabi‐2022, ambient PPFD ranged from ∼700 to 1450 µmol m− 2 s− 1, while LL plots consistently maintained a 45%–50% reduction. In Kharif‐2022, ambient PPFD was substantially lower (∼500–900 µmol m− 2 s− 1) due to monsoon‐associated cloud cover, and the LL treatment imposed a stable ∼25%–30% reduction relative to ambient light. These measurements confirm that the LL stress was applied consistently and not confounded by natural fluctuations in irradiance, aligning with earlier findings that rice genotypes exhibit marked physiological and agronomic sensitivity to prolonged shading or reduced light availability (Dutta et al., 2018; Das et al., 2025; Saha et al., 2024). Under LL conditions, the DFF decreased by 1.93% during the Kharif season (p = 0.0184), although the small effect size (d = −0.055; 95% CI: −1.85–1.05) suggests limited biological impact. A similar pattern occurred in Rabi (−1.29%, p = 0.089; d = −0.096; 95% CI: −2.13–0.75). While early flowering may appear advantageous, premature transition to reproduction can reduce vegetative growth and ultimately limit yield (Ranawake et al., 2014). PH increased significantly under LL in both seasons, by 6.21% in Rabi (p < 0.0001; d = 0.473; 95% CI: 5.63–10.99) and 1.10% in Kharif (p = 0.0416; d = 0.028; 95% CI: −2.10–3.50). Such elongation reflects a classic shade‐avoidance response (etiolation), where plants grow taller to capture limited light (J. Yang et al., 2022).

In contrast, traits directly linked to yield formation showed large negative responses. TN declined by 27.75% in Rabi (p < 0.0001; d = −0.665; 95% CI: −1.77 to −0.98) and by 16.24% in Kharif (p = 0.0001; d = −0.828; 95% CI: −2.03 to −1.31). Similarly, PN showed reductions of 32.36% in Rabi (p < 0.0001; d = −0.735; 95% CI: −1.77 to −1.12) and 20.41% in Kharif (p = 0.0001; d = −1.014; 95% CI: −2.35 to −1.27). These large effect sizes indicate strong biological inhibition of tillering and reproductive branching under LL, consistent with Ganie et al. (2024). Importantly, the magnitude and consistency of decline across seasons suggest that TN and PN are reliable early‐stage phenotypic markers for selecting LL‐tolerant genotypes in breeding nurseries. Reproductive growth was further constrained, as PL, GN, and SN declined significantly under LL. Spikelet fertility—a key determinant of grain formation—decreased by 17.39% in Rabi and 8.42% in Kharif (both p < 0.0001; d = −1.180 and −0.520, respectively). The wide CIs (Rabi CI: −12.48 to −8.40; Kharif CI: −9.95 to −2.65) reinforce the strong and consistent negative impact of LL on reproductive success. This aligns with B. Qi and Wu (2022), who noted that reduced irradiance impairs anther development and fertilization. Because spikelet fertility remains highly correlated with GY under LL, it serves as a high‐value secondary trait for screening genotypes capable of maintaining reproductive efficiency despite reduced light, making it ideal for marker‐assisted phenotyping.

TGW showed smaller declines of 1.02% in Rabi (p = 0.003; d = −0.085) and 3.69% in Kharif (p = 0.0064; d = −0.239), suggesting grain filling is moderately affected compared to source‐driven traits like tillering. Although TGW is less sensitive, it can complement primary LL‐indicator traits by identifying genotypes that sustain grain filling under stress, which is valuable for fine‐scale selection. GY dropped sharply by 27.79% in Rabi and 18.21% in Kharif (both p < 0.0001; d = −0.391 to −0.429; Rabi CI: −3.05 to −0.99; Kharif CI: −2.88 to −1.05). The most substantial reduction occurred in biomass, which declined by 39.98% in Rabi (p < 0.0001; d = −1.246; CI: −6.77 to −4.90) and by 28.49% in Kharif (p < 0.0001; d = −0.860; CI: −8.86 to −5.52), highlighting LL stress as a major constraint on photosynthesis and whole‐plant vigor (Toscano et al., 2021). Given biomass's strong relationship with carbon assimilation under LL, it represents a robust selection trait for identifying genotypes with superior photosynthetic capacity in shaded environments.

Overall, traits such as TN, PN, spikelet fertility, biomass, and GY displayed large negative effect sizes, indicating they are particularly sensitive to reduced light availability. These traits therefore offer a practical, trait‐based selection framework for early‐generation screening and can be used to guide both breeding decisions and field management strategies. Their sensitivity also emphasizes the importance of developing LL‐tolerant rice varieties and implementing improved agronomic practices such as optimized spacing, reduced plant density, and reflective mulches to enhance resilience under cloudy, shaded, or densely planted conditions (Ramegowda et al., 2024). Ensuring stable production under LL conditions is essential for sustaining rice yields in suboptimal growing regions.

Analysis of variance

The three‐way ANOVA conducted on 192 rice genotypes from a GWAS panel across the Rabi and Kharif seasons revealed significant effects of light, genotype, and season on various growth and productivity traits, emphasizing the intricate interactions among these factors (Table 2). The results indicated that light significantly influenced most traits, except for DFF (F = 1.23, p = 0.27), suggesting that this trait is relatively unaffected by changes in light conditions. In contrast, traits such as PH (F = 58.86, p < 0.0001), TN (F = 79.21, p < 0.0001), and PN (F = 92.73, p < 0.0001) exhibited strong responses to light variations. Because TN and PN consistently showed both strong LL sensitivity and large effect sizes, they represent robust early‐stage phenotypic selection markers for identifying LL‐tolerant genotypes during preliminary screening cycles. GY (F = 244.48, p < 0.0001) and biomass (F = 309.09, p < 0.0001) were highly sensitive to light, emphasizing its crucial role in optimizing rice productivity (Sarma et al., 2023). The strong light dependence of biomass also indicates its utility as a proxy trait for whole‐plant photosynthetic resilience under LL conditions.

Reproductive traits like GN (F = 6.06, p < 0.0001) and SN (F = 5.63, p < 0.0001) were particularly influenced by genotype, highlighting their potential as key targets for genetic improvement (Bhoite et al., 2023). Because GN and SN showed high genotypic variability and strong correlations with yield under LL, they serve as reliable genetic indicators for selecting superior alleles and haplotypes associated with LL tolerance. Additionally, variability in TN, PN, and TGW suggests that breeding programs could focus on these traits to enhance yield stability and adaptability. TGW, showing moderate but consistent variation, is particularly useful as a fine‐scale selection trait, helping breeders differentiate genotypes that maintain grain‐filling capacity under LL stress.

Seasonal variations had a substantial impact on all measured traits, particularly reproductive traits such as PN (F = 228.89, p < 0.0001), PL (F = 227.47, p < 0.0001), and GY (F = 36.93, p < 0.0001), which showed strong seasonal dependencies. Overall, the three‐way ANOVA results highlight the critical role of light, genotype, and seasonal conditions in shaping key agronomic traits in rice. The significant genotype–environment interactions suggest that breeding efforts should prioritize traits suited to specific light conditions and seasons to enhance productivity. The identification of TN, PN, spikelet fertility, GN, biomass, and TGW as consistently responsive to LL stress underlines their usefulness as practical, multi‐tiered selection markers for both early‐ and late‐stage variety development. Moreover, the strong influence of light on biomass and GY emphasizes the importance of optimizing light capture through improved management practices, such as adjustments in planting density or row orientation, to maximize light interception (Kakar et al., 2021).

Phenotypic correlation and PCA

The correlation analysis revealed that GY was consistently and positively associated with key agronomic traits such as biomass, GN, SN, SFP, and PW in both seasons (Figure 2a,b). These relationships underline the central role of assimilate production and reproductive efficiency in sustaining yield under LL stress (Table S1). Notably, correlations were stronger during the Kharif season, when LL stress coincides with critical growth phases such as tillering, flowering, and grain filling. This seasonal disparity suggests that Kharif‐grown plants are more sensitive to reduced light availability, resulting in greater dependence on biomass accumulation and reproductive robustness to maintain yield (Swathi et al., 2023). TGW also showed season‐specific effects, with stronger correlations in Kharif than in Rabi, indicating that grain filling is more vulnerable to light fluctuations during the monsoon season (Baillot et al., 2018). Likewise, TN and PN demonstrated stronger associations with GY in Kharif, supporting the hypothesis that vegetative and reproductive branching traits are more influential under variable light conditions.

Distribution of high‐quality SNPs used for genotyping the association mapping panel

The analysis of the distribution of 19,613 high‐quality SNP markers across the 12 rice chromosomes (1 Mb windows) revealed substantial variation in SNP density (Figure 3). The heatmap showed distinct regions of high and low SNP concentration, with red regions representing SNP‐rich segments and yellow‐to‐green regions indicating lower densities. Prominent SNP‐rich intervals were observed on chromosomes 1, 2, 3, 6, and 9. These regions are particularly valuable for QTL discovery because high SNP density increases the likelihood of capturing markers in strong LD with causal genes, thereby improving the precision and power of GWAS. As a result, SNP‐rich segments provide finer mapping resolution, enable clearer separation of tightly linked loci, and facilitate more reliable identification of QTLs associated with LL stress tolerance and yield‐related traits. The clustering of SNPs in these genomic intervals suggests that they may harbor critical genes or regulatory elements influencing stress adaptation, making them especially informative for marker‐assisted selection (MAS). These observations are consistent with Yu et al. (2023) and Windhorst et al. (2024), who highlighted the importance of dense SNP regions for detecting stress‐responsive loci. Similarly, Saha et al. (2024) demonstrated that SNP‐rich areas greatly enhance the efficiency of high‐resolution mapping and the discovery of candidate genes controlling complex traits. Together, these findings underscore the value of SNP‐dense genomic regions in improving the accuracy of QTL detection and advancing breeding strategies aimed at enhancing resilience to LL stress in rice.

Genetic diversity of the association‐mapping panel

The genetic analysis of 192 rice genotypes using 19,613 high‐quality SNP markers identified five distinct genetic clusters, as demonstrated through STRUCTURE analysis and kinship heatmap assessment. This structured population differentiation was confirmed by a peak in the delta K plot at K = 5 (Figure 4a–d). The clusters comprised 25, 38, 42, and 50 genotypes in clusters I, II, III, and IV, respectively. This classification provides valuable insights into the genetic diversity of these rice genotypes. Cluster V includes 37 admixed genotypes, shown by mixed colors in the STRUCTURE plot, indicating past genetic mixing that may explain their varied responses to low‐light stress (Smith et al., 2015). This visualization enhances the understanding of genetic relationships and spatial distribution within the population. Further supporting this classification, the neighbor‐joining tree distinctly grouped the genotypes into five genetic clusters, reflecting shared ancestry and genetic proximity. The clear clustering of related genotypes provides crucial information on genetic relationships, helping to identify genotypes that may demonstrate resilience or susceptibility to LL stress, which is essential for guiding breeding strategies to enhance stress tolerance (Felsenstein, 1993).

The kinship matrix, visualized as a heatmap, revealed pairwise genetic relatedness, displaying dark‐colored blocks that represented strong genetic similarities within clusters I, II, III, IV, and V. This representation offers an in‐depth view of the genetic landscape within the studied population. Additionally, the AMOVA provided key insights into genetic differentiation within and among the clusters. The results indicated that only 3% of the total genetic variation was attributed to differences among clusters, while 97% of the variation was found within clusters (Table 4). The sum of squares values for variation among populations and within populations were 39.546 and 825.892, respectively, indicating that the majority of genetic variation exists within populations. The estimated variance components were 0.156 for among populations and 4.417 for within populations, with mean squares values of 9.886 and 4.417, respectively, both of which were statistically significant (p = 0.001) (Excoffier et al., 1992).

These findings provide a strong foundation for breeding programs focused on enhancing LL tolerance in rice. By targeting genomic regions with high SNP density, utilizing the genetic diversity within clusters, and maintaining within‐cluster variation, breeders can develop rice varieties that are more resilient to fluctuating light conditions. The integration of genomic tools such as STRUCTURE analysis, PCA, and AMOVA into breeding strategies will be pivotal in ensuring sustainable rice production under challenging environmental conditions (Y. These insights support the significance of genetic diversity in improving the adaptability of rice varieties to environmental stressors, particularly LL conditions that frequently occur during the monsoon season.

Identification of QTLs associated with GY and related traits

The GWAS analysis performed using GAPIT 3.0, employing ECMLM (Enriched Compressed Mixed Linear Model), MLMM, FarmCPU, and BLINK models, identified a total of 305 QTLs associated with GY and 11 related agronomic traits (DFF, PH, TN, PN, PL, GN, SN, SFP, TGW, PW, and BIOM) under LL and NL conditions across the Rabi and Kharif seasons of 2022 (Table S2). These QTLs accounted for PV ranging from 4.01% to 65.17%. Of these, 148 QTLs were detected under LL, 125 under NL, and 32 were common across both light conditions. A total of 17 QTLs were consistently identified under LL stress in both seasons, including qDFF2.4RKL (PVE: 49.03%), qDFF4.1RKL (PVE: 7.51%), and qDFF11.4RKL (PVE: 14.80%) for DFF; qGN1.5RKL (PVE: 31.18%), qGN1.6RKL (PVE: 20.08%), and qGN2.1RKL (PVE: 12.31%) for GN; qSN2.2RKL (PVE: 21.56%), qSFP7.2RKL (PVE: 24.04%), and qSFP7.3RKL (PVE: 38.20%) for SN and SFP; qPW1.2RKL (PVE: 20.51%), qPW3.3RKL (PVE: 18.13%), and qPW11.1RKL (PVE: 38.05%) for PW; qGY1.1RKL (PVE: 28.05%), qGY8.2RKL (PVE: 32.40%), and qGY10.1RKL (PVE: 23.52%) for GY; and qBIOM1.1RKL (PVE: 20.09%) and qBIOM12.2RKL (PVE: 16.10%) for biomass (Table 5). These 17 QTLs are considered novel because no previous studies have reported LL‐specific QTLs in these genomic intervals. Importantly, the recent RIL‐based LL‐QTL mapping study by Mohanty et al. (2025) identified 20 novel QTLs distributed across chromosomes 1, 3, 4, 8, and others, but none of these overlapped with the chromosomal regions identified in our association panel, confirming that the QTLs discovered in this study represent newly uncovered LL‐responsive loci not previously reported in earlier mapping efforts. This includes LL‐specific QTLs such as qDFF2.4RKL , qGN1.5RKL , qSFP7.3RKL , qPW11.1RKL , and qGY8.2RKL , whose chromosomal positions do not coincide with the LL QTL hotspots (on chromosomes 1, 4, and 8) reported by Mohanty et al. (2025). This confirms that our GWAS‐identified loci expand the genomic landscape of LL tolerance beyond the previously known QTL clusters. Ganguly et al. (2024) identified 44 putative QTLs for GY, PN, and related traits under ambient and LL conditions, including qPN_LL1.1 and qGY_LL1.1 on chromosome 1 (35.82–37.69 Mb), and the stable LL‐QTL qGY_LL6.1 on chromosome 6 that controlled 8%–12% of PV. These earlier findings align with our results, yet none of our 17 novel LL‐stable QTLs map to these previously known intervals, highlighting the unique contributions of this study. Similarly, Dutta et al. (2018) identified simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers (e.g., HvSSR02‐54, HvSSR06‐69, HvSSR09‐45) associated with GY, spikelet fertility, and biological yield under LL conditions, many located near genes regulating leaf senescence, hormone signaling, and defense pathways. However, their marker positions do not overlap with the QTL regions discovered in the present analysis, further supporting the novelty of our findings. Thus, the 17 LL‐specific QTLs identified here are novel because no QTL for these 12 traits have previously been mapped in these chromosomal regions exclusively under LL stress. Their consistency across seasons underscores their value as stable genomic targets for improving LL tolerance. A total of 12 QTLs were identified under both LL and NL conditions during Rabi, including qSN1.1RLN (PVE: 0.026%), qPN2.2RLN (PVE: 17.74%), qPN2.3RLN (PVE: 12.79%), qSN3.2RLN (PVE: 4.91%), qPW4.1RLN (PVE: 7.32%), qGY5.2RLN (PVE: 33.33%), qTGW7.6RLN (PVE: 5.29%), qBIOM8.2RLN (PVE: 16.41%), and qDFF12.2RLN (PVE: 7.94%). During Kharif, 20 QTLs were detected under both conditions, such as qSN1.4KLN (PVE: 16.74%), qPW1.3KLN (PVE: 14.04%), qPL3.1KLN (PVE: 28.74%), qGY3.1KLN (PVE: 21.31%), qGY4.2KLN (PVE: 38.68%), qTN10.1KLN (PVE: 18.06%), and qTGW12.1KLN (PVE: 31.17%). Previous studies show that stress‐specific QTLs often exhibit higher PVE because selective pressure intensifies trait expression under adverse environments (Kadam & Fakrudin, 2017). In our study, DFF exhibited the highest number of QTLs (44 total; 25 under LL), highlighting the strong genetic control over flowering time plasticity. GY was associated with 36 QTLs (12 under LL), consistent with its known complexity and central role in LL adaptation. Traits such as PL and SN displayed higher PVE values, reinforcing their importance for yield stability under suboptimal light (J. Wang et al., 2021; N. Zhang et al., 2020).

Identification of candidate genes

A total of 42 candidate genes have been identified within QTL regions based on their predicted functions, which were inferred from biological databases, literature, and functional genomics data. These genes are associated with key biological processes such as photosynthesis, starch biosynthesis, phytochrome interactions, hormonal pathways, and stress responses. Each candidate gene is linked to a specific QTL region, as illustrated in the chromosomal map (Figure 5). The analysis of QTL regions associated with GY and other traits under LL (148 QTLs) and combined conditions (both LL and NL, 32 QTLs) focused on identifying candidate genes within a 200 kb upstream and downstream region. This led to the identification of 42 genes involved in essential physiological and regulatory processes that influence yield and stress response (Table 6). Among them, OsPIL15, linked to qSN1.9RL , functions in the phytochrome‐interacting factor‐like pathway and plays a crucial role in light signal transduction. Similarly, NFR, associated with qTN1.8KL , was identified as a key regulatory gene, while OsLIR1, linked to qSN1.1RLN , is involved in photosynthesis, highlighting its importance in enhancing yield under challenging conditions. Other genes related to photosynthesis include OsLhcb1, associated with qGN1.4KL and qSN1.3KL , which contributes to the photosynthetic pathway. Additionally, OsAGPL2, linked to qGY1.1RKL , is involved in starch biosynthesis, emphasizing the importance of photosynthetic efficiency and starch accumulation in improving yield traits. OsYUCC9, associated with qPW1.3RLN , plays a significant role in auxin (IAA) biosynthesis, a key regulator of plant growth and development. Furthermore, Gn1a/OsCKX2, linked to qTGW1.1KL , is known to influence GN and consequently influences grain weight and yield in rice through modulation of cytokinin levels. OsPsbS1, associated with qBIOM1.1RKL , is involved in the photosynthetic pathway, suggesting its potential role in enhancing productivity under LL conditions. Other identified genes include OsSBDCP1 (qGN1.4KL ), which encodes a starch‐binding domain protein crucial for starch metabolism, and OsNPF5.16 (qSN1.3KL ), a nitrate/peptide transporter that plays a key role in nitrogen metabolism, crucial for grain filling and yield. Photoreceptor genes such as OsCRY1a (qPN2.2RLN ) and OsPHYB (qSFP3.1RLN ) were also identified, emphasizing their role in light response, particularly under LL conditions. Additional genes like OsBEIIb (qTN2.4KL ) and OsNF‐YB1 (qSN2.8KL ) are involved in starch biosynthesis and regulatory pathways essential for biomass production and stress adaptation. Other genes linked to photosynthesis and regulatory functions under diverse environmental conditions include Lhca5 (qPN2.3LN ), OsPHYA (qSN3.1KL ), OsPHYC (qSN3.2RLN ), OsPIL1 (qSFP3.3RL ), and OsbHLH101 (qDFF4.4KLN ). Notably, RUBISCO Activase large (beta), associated with qPW4.1RLN , highlights the importance of carbon fixation in maintaining productivity under stress conditions.

Functional annotation of the QTLs and candidate genes highlighted critical pathways involved in stress adaptation. Genes such as OsPIL15 and photoreceptors like OsPHYA (qSN3.1KL ) and OsPHYB (qSFP3.1RLN ) regulate light signaling and photomorphogenesis, which are crucial for adapting to LL stress (Jung et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2021). Similarly, photosynthesis‐related genes like OsLhcb1 and OsPsbS1 are essential for light harvesting and photoprotection, maintaining photosynthetic efficiency under stress (R. Zhou et al., 2019). Genes like OsAGPL2 and OsSBDCP1 are involved in specific steps of starch biosynthesis. OsAGPL2, for example, is involved in the conversion of glucose‐1‐phosphate into ADP‐glucose, a crucial precursor for starch synthesis. OsSBDCP1 might be involved in the activity of starch‐branching enzymes, which help create the branching structure of starch molecules, conforming to findings of Teng et al. (2022). Hormonal pathways, including auxin signaling via OsYUC9 and OsAUX1, were found to be critical for growth and resource allocation under stress conditions. Additionally, transcription factors like HY5 integrate light signaling with hormonal pathways, underscoring their regulatory role in stress adaptation (B. Chen et al., 2018).

GO enrichment and pathway analysis of the 42 candidate genes revealed several biological processes associated with GY and its component traits under LL and common (LL–NL) conditions during both Rabi and Kharif seasons (Figure 6; Table 7). The significantly enriched pathways, including cytokinin metabolism, photorespiration, light harvesting, auxin signaling, nutrient transport, and reproductive development, reflect core mechanisms required for sustaining plant performance under reduced irradiance. Similar LL‐responsive pathways have been reported in earlier studies (Saha et al., 2024), supporting the robustness of our findings.

Expression analysis of hub genes

The expression patterns of 12 key hub genes, Gn1a, OsPHYA, OsPsbS1, OsAGPL2, OsLhcb1, OsAUX1, OsSBDCP1, OsNPF5.16, OsPHYB, OsGIF1, HY5, and OsYUC11, were examined using real‐time PCR under LL and NL conditions at the reproductive (flowering) stage. The analysis was conducted at three time points: 1 day (T1), 5 days (T5), and 10 days (T10) of LL exposure across three tolerant rice genotypes (Purnendu, Swarnaprabha, and VLDhan‐209) and three susceptible genotypes (GR4, IR8, and IR64) (Figure 8). The results showed that tolerant genotypes consistently exhibited higher expression levels of these genes compared to their susceptible counterparts, particularly under LL conditions. This suggests their crucial roles in light perception, photosynthesis, nutrient assimilation, and hormone signaling. Among the tolerant genotypes, Gn1a, a key regulator of GN, showed the highest expression in Purnendu at T5 (∼1.8‐fold), followed by Swarnaprabha (∼1.5‐fold) and VLDhan‐209 (∼1.4‐fold). In contrast, susceptible genotypes like IR64 and GR4 exhibited lowest expression (∼1.0‐fold). Gn1a, which encodes cytokinin oxidase, is a critical determinant of GN in rice and appears to be differentially regulated under LL stress, potentially influencing the varying degrees of tolerance among genotypes (Lu et al., 2022). OsPHYA, a major phytochrome involved in photomorphogenesis, exhibited peak expression levels of ∼2.5‐fold in Purnendu, ∼2.2‐fold in Swarnaprabha, and ∼2.0‐fold in VLDhan‐209, whereas susceptible genotypes such as IR64 and IR8 showed only ∼1.2‐fold expression. This gene is essential for perceiving and responding to light signals, impacting tillering patterns in rice (L. Zhang et al., 2018). Under low red/far‐red light conditions, typical of shaded environments, OsPHYA activity is suppressed, leading to increased stem elongation and reduced tillering, characteristic of the shade avoidance response (H. Peng, Phung, et al., 2020). Genes associated with photoprotection and photosynthesis, including OsPsbS1 and OsLhcb1, were significantly upregulated in tolerant genotypes. OsPsbS1, which plays a vital role in non‐photochemical quenching and photoprotection, reached peak expression of ∼2.8‐fold in Purnendu, followed by ∼2.5‐fold in Swarnaprabha and ∼2.3‐fold in VLDhan‐209, while susceptible genotypes showed only ∼1.0‐fold expression. Similarly, OsLhcb1, a crucial component of the LHC, exhibited ∼2.7‐fold expression in Purnendu, ∼2.4‐fold in Swarnaprabha, and ∼2.2‐fold in VLDhan‐209, whereas IR64 displayed lowest expression (∼1.1‐fold). These findings emphasize the critical role of photosynthetic genes in sustaining productivity under LL conditions, consistent with previous research highlighting their importance in photoprotection and energy transfer (Kasajima et al., 2011). Genes related to hormonal regulation, such as OsAUX1 and OsYUC11, showed marked upregulation in tolerant genotypes. OsYUC11, which is involved in auxin biosynthesis, exhibited a peak expression of ∼5.5‐fold in Purnendu, ∼4.8‐fold in Swarnaprabha, and ∼4.2‐fold in VLDhan‐209, while susceptible genotypes IR64 and GR4 displayed significantly lower expression (∼1.5‐fold and ∼1.4‐fold, respectively). Similarly, OsAUX1, essential for auxin transport, was most highly expressed in Purnendu (∼3.5‐fold), followed by Swarnaprabha (∼3.0‐fold) and VLDhan‐209 (∼2.8‐fold), with IR64 showing only ∼1.2‐fold expression. The enhanced expression of auxin‐related genes in tolerant genotypes aligns with previous studies emphasizing auxin's role in adaptive growth responses and resource reallocation under stress (Q. Zhang, Gong, et al., 2022). Transcription factors such as HY5, a central regulator of light signaling, and OsGIF1, which influences grain filling, were strongly expressed in tolerant genotypes. HY5 reached a peak expression of ∼4.5‐fold in Purnendu, compared to ∼4.0‐fold in Swarnaprabha and ∼3.8‐fold in VLDhan‐209, while susceptible genotypes such as IR64 and GR4 exhibited minimal (∼1.0‐fold) expression. Similarly, OsGIF1 peaked at ∼3.2‐fold in Purnendu, ∼2.9‐fold in Swarnaprabha, and ∼2.7‐fold in VLDhan‐209, compared to only ∼1.2‐fold in susceptible genotypes. These findings reinforce the regulatory roles of transcription factors in coordinating stress adaptation and growth under suboptimal light conditions, consistent with previous reports (B. Chen et al., 2018). Genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism (OsAGPL2) and nutrient assimilation (OsNPF5.16) were also upregulated in tolerant genotypes. OsAGPL2, a key regulator of starch biosynthesis, showed peak expression of ∼3.2‐fold in Purnendu, ∼2.8‐fold in Swarnaprabha, and ∼2.6‐fold in VLDhan‐209, whereas IR64 exhibited only ∼1.1‐fold expression. Similarly, OsNPF5.16, a nitrate transporter essential for nitrogen uptake, was highly expressed in tolerant genotypes, reaching ∼3.0‐fold in Purnendu, ∼2.7‐fold in Swarnaprabha, and ∼2.5‐fold in VLDhan‐209, while IR64 showed only ∼1.0‐fold expression. These findings emphasize the significance of starch metabolism and nutrient assimilation in maintaining energy balance and metabolic functions under LL stress (Raghavan et al., 2017).

These insights provide valuable targets for breeding programs aimed at developing rice varieties with enhanced tolerance to LL conditions.

LD heatmaps and identification of superior haplotypes and donors for selected traits

Haplotype analysis serves as a valuable approach for deciphering complex traits and pinpointing superior allelic combinations (W. Huang & Mackay, 2016). In this study, haplotype analysis of 12 key rice genes revealed significant correlations with essential yield‐related traits, including GY, GN, SN, SFP, TGW, and biomass (BIOM). LD heatmaps were utilized to identify genomic regions where strong SNP linkages influenced these traits, leading to the discovery of superior haplotypes along with their associated QTLs and SNPs (Table 8). These findings offer a valuable genetic foundation for breeding programs focused on enhancing rice yield potential (Figure 9). This methodology enables precise selection of beneficial alleles to improve crop performance (Bernardo, 2016; Collard & Mackill, 2008). For GY, several superior haplotypes were identified within QTLs qGY1.1RKL , qGY3.2KL , qGY4.2KLN , and qGY6.9RL , which were associated with notable yield improvements. For instance, Hap2 of PHYA (AA) at qGY3.2KL (mean = 15.84 g plant−1, p = 4.8e‐05), Hap1 of OsGIF1 (TT) at qGY4.2KLN (mean = 21.52 g plant−1, p = 0.045), Hap2 of HY5 (GG) at qGY6.9RL (mean = 16.32 g plant−1, p = 0.01), and Hap2 of OsAGPL2 (GG) at qGY1.1RKL (mean = 16.14 g plant−1, p = 0.029) demonstrated significant yield enhancements. PHYA encodes a phytochrome photoreceptor crucial for light signaling and plant development, impacting various growth parameters, including yield (Takano et al., 2005). Similarly, OsAGPL2 is integral to starch biosynthesis, directly influencing grain filling and final grain weight (Feng et al., 2022). The presence of these favorable haplotypes in high‐performing donor genotypes like Purnendu, Swarnaprabha, and multiple IRRI genotypes suggests their potential for broad adaptability and yield improvement (B. Peng, Guan, et al., 2020). Regarding GN, superior haplotypes were associated with QTL qGN1.4KL , with peak SNPs, Affx‐122674971 and Affx‐122674236, playing a key role. Notably, Hap2 of OsSBDCP1 (GG) (mean = 94.51, p = 0.014) and Hap3 of OsLhcb1 (GG) (mean = 96.41, p = 0.013) were found in donor varieties such as Swarnaprabha and Bhasamanik, along with several regional lines, emphasizing their potential for increasing GN. OsSBDCP1 is implicated in starch metabolism, affecting carbon partitioning and potentially influencing GN (Cakir et al., 2019). OsLhcb1, which encodes an LHC protein, is essential for efficient photosynthesis and resource acquisition, which indirectly enhances GN by supporting overall plant growth (Jansson, 1994). For SN, superior haplotypes such as Hap2 of PhyA (GG) (mean = 146.2, p = 0.01) and Hap2 of OsNPF5.16 (TT) (mean = 87.41, p = 4.6e‐07) within qSN1.3KL were prevalent in donor genotypes like Purnendu, Swarnaprabha, and Rudra, indicating their role in increased spikelet production. Gn1a is a crucial regulator of panicle branching, directly influencing the number of spikelets per panicle (Ashikari et al., 2005). OsNPF5.16, a nitrate transporter gene, is essential for nitrogen uptake and assimilation, which in turn supports the growth and development of more spikelets (J. Wang et al., 2012). SFP was significantly improved with Hap1 of PhyB (AA) in qSFP3.1RLN (mean = 76.33%, p = 0.006) and Hap3 of OsYUC11 (TT) in qSFP12.1RL (mean = 82.59%, p = 0.0086), with these haplotypes being found in donor varieties like Chamarmani and Mahisugandh. PhyB, another phytochrome involved in light signaling, influences SFP by regulating flowering time and other developmental pathways (Takano et al., 2005). Meanwhile, OsYUC11, a key gene in auxin biosynthesis, plays a critical role in reproductive development, including spikelet fertility regulation (X. Xu et al., 2021). For TGW and biomass (BIOM), key associations were observed, particularly with Hap1 of Gn1a (CC) in qTGW1.1KL (mean = 24.59 g plant−1, p = 0.011) and Hap3 of OsAUX1 and OsPsbS1 (CC) in qBIOM1.1RKL (mean = 36.41 g plant−1, p = 0.01). These haplotypes were found in donor genotypes such as Barhabali Dhan, Malikshali, and Swarnaprabha, indicating their role in overall plant growth and resource utilization. OsAUX1 is an auxin transporter gene involved in grain filling and final grain size regulation (L. Liu et al., 2015), while OsPsbS1 plays a role in photoprotection and photosynthesis regulation, directly influencing biomass accumulation (Hubbart et al., 2012). The heatmap analysis provides a clear visualization of the presence of hub genes across various rice donors under LL stress conditions (Figure 10). Particularly, genes such as OsAGPL2, OsGIF1, and HY5 show a widespread presence across multiple donors like Purnendu, Swarnaprabha, and Chamarmani, indicating their potential robustness in responding to LL stress. In contrast, genes like OsLhcb1, OsNPF5.16, and OsYUC11 exhibit a more selective distribution, suggesting a possible donor‐specific adaptation to LL conditions. The presence of these genes in certain donors but not others could point toward genetic variations that might confer an advantage under LL stress, highlighting potential targets for breeding programs aimed at improving LL tolerance in rice.

This study presents a comprehensive approach to understanding and mitigating LL stress in rice, a major constraint in rice‐growing regions facing prolonged cloud cover, dense planting, double‐cropping systems, and climate‐driven reductions in solar radiation (Fabre et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2022). By integrating GWASs, QTL mapping, candidate gene analysis, and haplotype profiling, this work identifies key genomic regions and regulatory pathways governing LL stress resilience. The discovery of 17 stable and novel QTLs associated with critical agronomic traits, including GY, PN, GN, spikelet fertility, and biomass, provides a robust genetic foundation for breeding high‐yielding rice under LL environments. Expression profiling of 42 candidate genes, including 12 hub genes such as OsPHYA, OsSBDCP1, OsPsbS1, OsAGPL2, and OsYUC11, further highlights their regulatory roles in photomorphogenesis, photoprotection, hormonal balance, carbohydrate metabolism, and nutrient assimilation under reduced irradiance (Murchie et al., 2005; Saha et al., 2024). These pathways collectively help maintain photosynthetic efficiency and reproductive stability under LL stress. Haplotype analysis revealed that several superior allelic combinations provide significant yield advantages. For instance, favorable haplotypes of OsPsbS1 increased GY by 22%–28%, consistent with its role in non‐photochemical quenching and photoprotection (X. P. Li et al., 2002). Similarly, superior OsYUC11 alleles enhanced spikelet fertility by 12%–16%, aligning with its established role in auxin‐mediated reproductive development. Haplotypes of OsAGPL2 improved biomass accumulation by 15%–21%, supporting previous findings on its function in starch biosynthesis and energy allocation (Tuncel & Okita, 2013). Several high‐performing genotypes like Purnendu, Swarnaprabha, Chamarmani, and TRB466 harbored multiple superior haplotypes, confirming their potential as elite donor lines for LL‐tolerance breeding (Dutta et al., 2018; Ganguly et al., 2024). These donors represent valuable resources for introgressing LL‐tolerance traits through MAS and genomic selection (GS) approaches. The implications of these findings extend globally. LL stress increasingly affects rice production in Southeast and East Asia, East Africa, Japan, and South America due to climate variability and intensified cultivation (Wassmann et al., 2009). The QTLs and haplotypes identified here, many with moderate to high PVE and cross‐season stability, provide transferable genomic targets for international pre‐breeding, hybrid development, and genomic prediction platforms. The conservation of genes such as OsPHYA and OsPsbS1 across diverse rice subpopulations further strengthens their global breeding relevance (Figure 11). Future research should validate these QTLs and haplotypes through multi‐location, multi‐year field trials and functional genomics approaches such as genome editing CRISPR/Cas‐mediated validation. Developing NILs, integrating transcriptomic and metabolomic data, and incorporating LL‐tolerance loci into machine‐learning‐driven GS models will accelerate the development of high‐yielding, LL‐resilient cultivars suitable for diverse agro‐ecological zones.
