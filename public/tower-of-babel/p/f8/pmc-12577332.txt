Salivary gland cell senescence in Sjögren’s syndrome: current research and novel therapeutic directions
Authors: Xiaoyu Tang, Jingjin Hu, Xinying Fan, Haodong Su, Minglu Li, Liyun Zhang, Dan Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sjögren's syndrome (SS) is an autoimmune disease primarily affecting exocrine glands, characterized by dry mouth and eyes, with an unclear pathogenesis. Recent studies suggest that salivary gland (SG) cell senescence is critical in SS pathogenesis. The senescence mechanism may serve as a critical pathological nexus linking molecular abnormalities to clinical phenotypes. Under assaults from infections, environmental factors, and immune dysregulation, SG cells undergo oxidative stress, telomere shortening, and DNA damage, leading to senescence. Senescent cells lose proliferative capacity and secrete senescence-associated cytokines, triggering inflammation and immune responses that exacerbate disease progression. Elucidating the mechanisms of SG cell senescence may advance our understanding of SS pathogenesis and identify novel therapeutic targets, offering new avenues for SS treatment.

Introduction

Sjögren's disease (SS) is a chronic autoimmune disease that primarily affects exocrine glands such as the salivary and lacrimal glands. The prevalence of this disease is higher in Asian and European populations, with female patients accounting for approximately 93.2%, and the peak age of onset is between 40 and 50 years. Viral infections and genetic factors are its known risk factors [1]. Notably, the 2023 international Rome consensus recommended using "Sjögren's disease" instead of "Sjögren's syndrome," abbreviated as "SjD," to more accurately reflect the nature of the disease and eliminated the distinction between "primary" and "secondary" in clinical diagnosis [2]. The core pathological process begins with salivary gland (SG) involvement and can progress to affect multiple systems throughout the body, manifesting as interstitial lung disease, renal tubular acidosis, neuropathy, among others. The most severe complication of this disease is a significantly elevated risk of lymphoma development, the mechanism of which is related to abnormal B-cell proliferation due to chronic immune stimulation. Such complications are more commonly observed in patients with longer disease duration and high disease activity [3]. Under physiological conditions, SG maintain oral health and function through saliva secretion, which aids digestion, lubrication, defense, signaling, and pH regulation [4]. In SS, however, SG undergo pathological changes, including lymphocytic infiltration, glandular cell damage, interstitial fibrosis, altered saliva composition, and glandular atrophy, leading to hyposalivation, immune activation, and systemic complications [5].

Cellular senescence is an irreversible cell cycle arrest that leads to​​ a decline in physiological function and metabolic activity, driven by a complex interplay of biological processes. Specifically, senescence arises in response to stressors such as telomere attrition, genotoxic agents, radiation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, oncogene activation, hypoxia, and dietary factors [6]. While senescent cells are typically cleared by the immune system [7], their accumulation can impair tissue repair and regeneration, exacerbating disease progression [8]. Traditional methods for identifying senescent cells primarily rely on a limited set of markers and homogeneous samples, making it difficult to achieve universal applicability. Researchers have developed the Human Universal Senescence Index (hUSI) by integrating multi-source transcriptomic data with machine learning models, thereby addressing the challenge of detecting heterogeneity in cellular senescence. This provides novel diagnostic tools and therapeutic targets for senescence-related diseases (such as autoimmune diseases and tumors) [9].

SG are central to SS pathogenesis, as their dysfunction—linked to cellular senescence—directly contributes to symptom manifestation and disease progression [10, 11]. Emerging evidence highlights SG cell senescence as a key driver of SS, underscoring the importance of unraveling its mechanisms to advance therapeutic strategies. However, the diagnosis of SS still faces challenges. The 2016 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) classification criteria are based on a scoring system where a total score of ≥ 4 points confirms primary Sjögren's syndrome (pSS). Core items include anti-SSA/Ro antibody positivity (weighted at 3 points) and labial gland biopsy showing focal lymphocytic sialadenitis with a focus score of ≥ 1 focus/4 mm2 (weighted at 3 points). Additional assessments incorporate the ocular staining score (OSS ≥ 5, weighted at 1 point), Schirmer's test (≤ 5 mm/5 min, weighted at 1 point), and unstimulated salivary flow rate (≤ 0.1 ml/min, weighted at 1 point) [12]. Although this criteria set demonstrates relatively high specificity and sensitivity, false-negative results frequently occur in early disease due to seronegativity for antibodies and negative labial gland biopsy findings, and it can be easily confused with conditions such as age-related xerostomia. In such scenarios, the detection of SG senescence markers may provide auxiliary diagnostic value.

Mechanisms of salivary gland cell senescence in SS

Human major SG include the parotid, submandibular, and sublingual glands [13], minor SG include labial glands, palatal glands, buccal glands, and lingual glands. Labial glands comprise secretory acinar cells, myoepithelial cells, and ductal cells. SG cells are categorized into stem cells (SGSCs), progenitor cells (SGPCs), and epithelial cells (SGECs) based on differentiation and functional states. These cells serve as targets for autoimmune attacks [14] and secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, amplifying inflammation and glandular dysfunction [15]. SGSCs, located at the acinar-ductal junction, exhibit self-renewal and multipotent differentiation capacities [16]. After disease or SG damage, they can be differentiated into different cell types to maintain the structure and function of the SG. For instance, ductal basal stem cells (Krt5⁺/Krt14⁺) differentiate into acinar cells after radiation-induced damage, thereby restoring secretory function [17]. SGPCs are differentiated from stem cells, but the two differ in characteristics and functions [18]. SGSCs possess the capacity for unlimited division and multidirectional differentiation, whereas SGPCs can only differentiate into specific cell types [19], such as acinar cells and ductal cells. SGPCs has specific molecular markers, such as KIT, K5, K14, SOX2, and SOX9, which can be used for the identification and isolation of SGPCs [20],during embryonic SG development, cells expressing SOX2 have the potential to differentiate into acinar cells and ductal cells [21]. SGECs include acinar cells (saliva production) and ductal cells (saliva transport and ion regulation) [22]. Oxidative stress

In SS, oxidative stress drives SG dysfunction through multiple cascades. The core mechanism initiates with mitochondrial structural abnormalities (swelling, membrane rupture) and endoplasmic reticulum stress, leading to excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [23, 24], which disrupts the redox balance [25, 26]. Excess ROS directly attacks key cellular components: Firstly, it oxidizes DNA bases (e.g., e.g., guanine to 8-hydroxy-2'-deoxyguanosine (8-oxoG)), induces DNA double-strand breaks and activates the damage response (DDR) [25, 27]. Unrepaired damage accelerates cellular senescence; aged mice exhibit reduced salivary flow and elevated salivary/serum 8-OHdG [28, 29], and 8-OHdG is significantly expressed in the ductal cells of the SG in SS patients [30]. Secondly, it oxidizes proteins (e.g., carbonylation) [31], forming insoluble lipofuscin deposits—a marker of senescence [32, 33]. SS patients exhibit significant lipid metabolism disorders, which form the basis for lipofuscin formation [34]. Thirdly, it damages the mitochondria themselves (proteins/lipids/DNA), creating a vicious cycle [35]. In SS, mitochondrial dysfunction leads to excessive ROS production, which activates the NLRP3 inflammasome and releases pro-inflammatory factors like IL-1β; the resulting chronic inflammatory environment feedback exacerbates mitochondrial damage (manifested as swelling, cristae structure disruption, and other ultrastructural abnormalities), ultimately forming a self-perpetuating vicious cycle of "ROS → mitochondrial damage → inflammation → ROS" [24]. Fourthly, ROS activates pro-inflammatory and senescence signaling pathways (ERK/JNK/p38 MAPK, NF-κB, p53) [36], interfering with cell metabolism and differentiation, and promoting chronic inflammation [37]. In SS, ROS impairs SG function by affecting key signaling pathways (e.g., JNK-NF-κB and PKA-CREB) [38]. Furthermore, evidence indicates that downregulation of Foxo3a expression in SS weakens antioxidant defense, leading to functional exhaustion of SGSCs/SGPCs (CD133 +) [39]. Foxo3a is a senescence-associated gene that suppresses oxidative stress. An SS mouse model with Sod2 knockout in ductal cells recapitulates exacerbated oxidative stress [40]. SOD2 is a key mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme whose function is to convert O₂ to H₂O₂, which is then decomposed into water and oxygen by catalase. This process is one of the core mechanisms by which cells defend against oxidative stress. The above confirms that oxidative stress indeed affects SG function in SS.

Telomere shortening

Telomeres, repetitive DNA sequences at chromosome ends [41], shorten with each cell division. Critical shortening disrupts protective caps, mimicking DNA double-strand breaks and activating DDR, Although telomerase replenishes telomeric repeats, its activity declines with aging, while no study has directly demonstrated this phenomenon in SS, the regenerative capacity of SGSCs declines with age, which may be associated with telomerase activity [42]. Telomere shortening suppresses the NAD + -SIRT1-PGC1α axis, linking DNA damage to mitochondrial dysfunction and accelerated aging [43]. In SS, SGSCs exhibit markedly shortened telomeres [42], and ATM—a gene involved in telomere maintenance and senescence—is upregulated in labial glands of primary SS patients [42], suggesting excessive replication stress and senescence-driven glandular dysfunction. Firstly, the sustained inflammatory microenvironment (e.g., pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-α, TNF-α, and IL-6) accelerates abnormal proliferation and telomere attrition of SGSCs by activating the DNA damage response (DDR) pathway (e.g., the ATM/p53 axis). Secondly, although the upregulation of ATM attempts to repair telomere damage, under chronic inflammatory stress, it instead exacerbates cell cycle arrest and senescence-associated phenotypes (e.g., p16 expression). Ultimately, telomere dysfunction leads to the exhaustion of the regenerative capacity of SGSCs, preventing their differentiation into acinar cells and causing them to be arrested at the intercalated duct stage, resulting in the accumulation of p16 + senescent cells. This "inflammation-telomere attrition-senescence" cascade explains the pathological basis for the progressive loss of SG function in patients with pSS.

DNA damage

DNA damage is a key pathway driving cellular senescence, yet the mechanistic link between DNA damage and senescence in SG cells of SS patients remains incompletely elucidated. Studies indicate that factors such as telomere shortening and oxidative stress can induce DNA damage and activate the DDR [44]. This process activates ATM kinase, leading to phosphorylation of the H2AX protein (a modified form of histone H2AX) and the formation of γH2AX foci. These foci serve as both markers of DNA damage and determinants of cell fate [36]. In radiation-exposed SG organoids, significant upregulation of p16, p21, and senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors (e.g., IL-6, MCP1, CXCL1, GDNF) was observed [45]. Both p16 and p21 are core regulators of the cell cycle and senescence [46], and DDR activation triggers cell cycle arrest through signaling pathways such as the p53 and p16 axes [47–49]. Notably, p16 exacerbates chromosomal ubiquitination and impairs DNA repair efficiency via the HUWE1-USP7-TRIP12 cascade [50]. Furthermore, the accumulation of HIF-1α under hypoxic conditions further promotes p16/p53 expression [51], collectively accelerating the senescence process.

In SS patients, pro-inflammatory cytokines released by infiltrating immune cells within the SG can induce the expression of p16 and p21 in SGSCs [42], leading to an accumulation of p16-positive cells in the basal striated duct (BSD) region—the microenvironment for SGPCs [52, 53]—as reported in [54]. This finding aligns with the abnormally active DDR response observed in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of SS patients (e.g., enhanced G1 phase arrest and elevated p53 phosphorylation) [55], suggesting that defective DNA repair may contribute to SS pathogenesis. The abnormal accumulation of p53 in the labial glands of SS patients [56] further supports the association between DNA damage and glandular dysfunction. Elevated expression of TET2 enzyme, which catalyzes the conversion of 5mC to 5hmC during DNA demethylation, is observed in the SGECs of SS patients. This enzyme may also influence DNA damage repair [57]. Interestingly, activation of the Sonic hedgehog (Shh) signaling pathway can partially restore radiation-induced SG dysfunction by upregulating DNA repair genes such as Chek1 and Egfr [58]. Whether this approach could be applied to SS patients requires further investigation.

Although current research has revealed the general mechanisms linking the DDR pathway to cellular senescence, the SS-specific causal chain remains inadequately delineated. The molecular events connecting DNA damage in SGSCs/SGPCs to DDR activation and ultimately to glandular functional decline have not been fully. Future research should focus on identifying the triggers of DNA damage (e.g., chronic inflammation or autoantibodies) within the SS microenvironment and their downstream effects, to establish more direct mechanistic links.

Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype (SASP)

Senescent cells secrete a complex array of factors, collectively termed the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which includes cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, proteases, coagulation factors, ceramides, bradykinin, extracellular matrix (ECM) components, and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [59]. These molecules modulate the cellular microenvironment and intercellular communication [60], while also serving as potential biomarkers of senescence [61]. The role of SASP in cellular senescence is multifaceted. For instance, the SASP component IL-6 is linked to oncogene-induced senescence, where it not only sustains senescence but also promotes tumor cell proliferation [62]. In SS SG, elevated inflammatory cytokines [63] drive senescence-associated gene expression in SGSCs, accelerating their senescence and skewing differentiation toward ductal rather than acinar cells, thereby impairing glandular function [42]. SS mouse models exhibit increased senescence-associated T cells (SA-Ts) expressing the CXCL12 receptor CXCR4. CXCL12, a pro-inflammatory mediator upregulated in salivary ductal epithelial cells [64], exacerbates disease progression. Recent studies have further revealed that CCR1hi/CCL5hi macrophages in the SG mediate the chemotaxis of T and B cells through CCL5, establishing a positive feedback loop that accelerates the pathological progression of SS [65]. Macrophage infiltration in SG of pSS patients correlates with elevated SASP components and senescence-related genes (p16 and p53) [66]. Paracrine senescence, mediated by SASP factors such as TGF-β ligands, VEGF, CCL2, and CCL20, describes how senescent cells propagate senescence to neighboring healthy cells via secreted factors [67]. Beyond its pro-inflammatory effects, SASP in SG senescence amplifies inflammation by activating nearby immune cells and elevating oxidative stress in adjacent cells [68]. In summary, SASP primarily functions to signal to diverse immune cells, including natural killer cells, macrophages, and T cells [69].

SASP induction and expression are regulated by multiple factors, including DDR, inflammatory signaling pathways, epigenetic modifications, transcription factors, metabolic shifts, and RNA-binding proteins [69]. The DDR and inflammatory pathways (e.g., cGAS/STING) are the primary triggers. The cGAS/STING pathway, a key regulator of SASP [70], is activated when cGAS detects cytoplasmic DNA, synthesizes cGAMP, and stimulates STING to initiate SASP. Compared to healthy controls, pSS patients exhibit elevated lactate levels in labial glands, correlating with inflammatory cytokine levels, in the labial glands of patients with pSS, there is substantial lymphocytic infiltration. These activated immune cells (e.g., CD4 + T cells) undergo metabolic reprogramming during the autoimmune response, characterized by significantly enhanced glycolytic activity, which leads to the substantial accumulation of lactate as an end-product of glycolysis. Furthermore, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) damage and leakage in SGECs under high lactate conditions, activating the cGAS-STING pathway and driving inflammation to exacerbate pSS progression [71]. Senescent cells display metabolic dysfunction, including inhibition of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, impaired ATP production, and elevated AMP/ADP ratios [72]. These conditions favor lactate accumulation, suggesting that SG cell senescence contributes to SS pathogenesis. The mTOR pathway and metabolic alterations further regulate SASP composition and function [73]. In SG cells, mTOR hyperactivation promotes BAFF overexpression, driving excessive B-cell activation and autoantibody production [74], which exacerbates SS-related inflammation. mTOR may also directly mediate SG senescence. Klotho-deficient mice exhibit accelerated aging phenotypes (e.g., shortened lifespan, xerostomia), and soluble Klotho protein modulates mTOR pathway components, implicating mTOR in SG senescence [75]. mTOR signaling activation alters cellular morphology and function [76]; while organelles such as mitochondria and lysosomes [77, 78]contribute to senescence.

Autoimmunity

In patients with autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and axial spondyloarthritis, premature thymic dysfunction can lead to accelerated telomere shortening in T cells, exacerbating T cell premature senescence and the accumulation of terminally differentiated T cells, ultimately resulting in persistent autoimmune activation [79]. In SS, CD4⁺CD28⁻ T cells have been found to contribute to SGECs senescence and glandular function loss through direct cytotoxicity (e.g., perforin release) and secretion of pro-fibrotic factors (e.g., TGF-β). Concurrently, CD8⁺ TEMRA cells promote these processes via replicative senescence due to telomere shortening and the production of ROS [80]. B cells play a more critical role in the pathogenesis of SS. On one hand, B cells act as antigen-presenting cells that capture antigens and present them to CD4⁺ T cells, promoting T cell secretion of IL-21, which further activates B cells and stimulates antibody production. Antibody deposition triggers mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to ROS accumulation and DNA damage (γH2AX focus formation), thereby accelerating cellular senescence. Studies in BALB/c mouse models of SS have shown that anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB antibodies can be internalized into SG cells via endocytosis, resulting in reduced salivary secretion function [81]. On the other hand, B cells excessively secrete B-cell activating factor (BAFF), which promotes the survival of autoreactive B cells and simultaneously stimulates SGECs to secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby exacerbating tissue damage [80]. The combined effects of direct damage by autoantibodies, the shaping of an inflammatory microenvironment, and the synergistic amplification by T and B cells collectively play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of SS. Synergies between mechanisms

In SS, the synergistic interaction between oxidative stress, telomere shortening, DNA damage, and the SASP forms a vicious cycle that directly drives SG dysfunction and disease progression. Specifically, in SS patients, SGSCs and SGPCs accumulate oxidative damage (e.g., 8-oxoG) due to mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive ROS production. This leads to persistent activation of the DDR and induces cellular senescence via the p16/p53 pathway. Senescent acinar and ductal epithelial cells secrete SASP factors (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α, CXCL12), which not only recruit and activate CD4 + T cells and macrophages to infiltrate the glands but also accelerate the senescence of neighboring cells through paracrine effects. Furthermore, elevated lactate levels in SS activate the cGAS-STING pathway by inducing mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) leakage, which further amplifies the inflammatory response and creates a positive feedback loop between inflammation and senescence. This cycle is exacerbated by autoimmune attack: B cells overexpressing BAFF produce autoantibodies, while damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) exposed by senescent SGECs activate pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) to sustain chronic inflammation [24]. Autoimmunity is inextricably linked to inflammation. These findings strongly suggest a close association between SG cell senescence and the pathological process of SS.

Prospects of targeting cellular senescence in SS therapy

Senolytic drugs

Senolytics are pharmacological agents that selectively eliminate senescent cells [82]. Current research on senolytics focuses on metabolic disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer. Dasatinib and Quercetin are two such drugs capable of targeting senescent cells. Dasatinib, a multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor, blocks Eph kinase-dependent anti-apoptotic signaling via EFNB [83], a dependency receptor linked to senescent cell survival. EFNB-targeted therapies show efficacy in autoimmune diseases such as chronic lymphocytic leukemia [84]. In collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mice, Dasatinib treatment significantly alleviates arthritis symptoms [85], however, its therapeutic potential and value in SS require further validation through in-depth empirical research and rigorous systematic evaluation. Quercetin, a natural flavonoid, selectively eliminates senescent cells in vitro and in vivo by inhibiting PI3K and other kinases [86, 87]. The combination of Dasatinib and Quercetin broadens senolytic targeting across diverse senescent cell populations, enhancing physiological function and extending healthspan [88]. High-dose Quercetin increases salivary flow in NOD mice [89]. In IFN-γ and leptin (LP)-treated salivary epithelial cells from BALB/c mice, Quercetin reverses apoptosis-related protein alterations (e.g., upregulated Bax and downregulated Bcl-2), significantly reducing apoptosis [89]. Leptin (LP) plays a critical role in inflammatory and autoimmune diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease, systemic sclerosis, and psoriasis [90]. LP drives the accumulation of senescent cells expressing senescence markers, while senescence enhances leptin secretion and regulation, creating a pathological loop in multiple diseases [91]. Elevated serum leptin levels are observed in NOD mice [92]. Researchers also found that in NOD mice, the D + Q combination therapy significantly restored the salivary flow rate, reduced lymphocytic infiltration in the SG, and alleviated glandular destruction [9]. Thus, Dasatinib and Quercetin hold significant potential for treating SS SG cells via senolytic activity. Navitoclax (ABT-263), a senolytic drug targeting BCL-2 family proteins, is another widely studied anti-senescence agent [93]. In tauopathy-driven neurodegenerative mouse models, Navitoclax exhibits effects comparable to Dasatinib + Quercetin [94], though its clinical use is limited by thrombocytopenia risk [95]. ABT-263 reduces senescent CD4 + T-cell frequency in SLE mouse models, alleviating symptoms such as proteinuria and renal injury [96], Similarly, ABT-263 decreases senescent cells in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) mice [97], underscoring its relevance for SS therapy as another autoimmune disorder. Among the current senotherapeutic agents, only Quercetin has been demonstrated to exert anti-senescence effects in NOD mice, while the other two drugs require further investigation. However, chemotherapeutically-derived drugs such as Dasatinib and Navitoclax are limited in SS treatment due to systemic toxicities (e.g., myelosuppression) and thrombocytopenia [98]. Intermittent dosing regimens can effectively balance senescent cell clearance efficacy with toxic side effects [99]. Close monitoring of blood counts and liver function indices during treatment is essential to allow for timely dose adjustment or drug suspension.

Signaling pathway modulators

The senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) is closely linked to cellular senescence. Suppressing SASP can mitigate inflammation and alleviate senescence-related tissue dysfunction [100]. Rapamycin, an mTOR inhibitor, modulates SASP through mTOR signaling, offering therapeutic potential for senescence-related disorders [101]. Studies demonstrate that Rapamycin improves salivary flow in NOD mice by regulating autophagy [102]. Topical Rapamycin eye drops also alleviate dry eye symptoms in NOD mice [103], highlighting its therapeutic promise for SS. Beyond mTOR inhibition, Metformin suppresses cellular senescence by blocking IKK/NFκB activation, thereby reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine production [104]. NFκB is sequestered in the cytoplasm as an inactive complex bound to its inhibitor, IκB. Upon cellular stimulation, IκB undergoes ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, allowing free NFκB to translocate to the nucleus. There, it binds specific DNA sequences to regulate genes involved in immune responses, inflammation, proliferation, and survival [105], Metformin additionally delays cellular senescence by suppressing mitochondrial ROS generation [106]. Metformin has been shown to significantly enhance salivary flow in NOD mice [107, 108].

Anti-inflammatory strategies

Inflammation is both a hallmark and driver of cellular senescence, while senescence reciprocally amplifies inflammation. Thus, anti-inflammatory approaches hold promise for targeting senescence. Aspirin has been shown to reduce oxidative stress and attenuate senescence [109], though optimal dosing and efficacy vary among individuals, and long-term use carries potential side effects. Given the central role of inflammation in SS pathogenesis, the alleviation of sicca symptoms primarily relies on topical lubricants (e.g., artificial tears for dry eyes; saliva substitutes or stimulants like pilocarpine for dry mouth [110]); for local inflammation, anti-inflammatory therapies such as cyclosporine eye drops can be used. Systemic interventions include glucocorticoids, disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), or B-cell targeted biologics [111], Glucocorticoids may reduce endothelial senescence and SASP to prevent cardiovascular complications in COPD patients [112]. While their efficacy against SG senescence remains unproven, this represents a promising avenue for future investigation.

Antioxidant interventions

Oxidative stress is a major driver of cellular senescence, impairing cellular function through diverse mechanisms. Antioxidant strategies can counteract senescence [113]. Classic antioxidants include vitamin C, vitamin E, and N-acetylcysteine. Melatonin exerts antioxidant effects via free radical scavenging. Experimental studies demonstrate that melatonin reduces lymphocyte infiltration in the submandibular glands of NOD mice, likely linked to its ROS-neutralizing properties [114]. Additionally, melatonin enhances the activity of endogenous antioxidant enzymes (e.g., superoxide dismutase, catalase), bolsters systemic antioxidant capacity, and synergizes with vitamins C and E to amplify antioxidant efficacy [115]. In vitro studies show that cordycepin rescues oxidative stress-induced submandibular gland cell dysfunction by reducing ROS levels and restoring the expression of salivary proteins (e.g., AMY, AQP5), highlighting its protective role [116]. Nanozymes—enzyme-mimicking nanomaterials—exhibit peroxidase-, catalase-, and superoxide dismutase-like activities to clear excess ROS and mitigate inflammation. When combined with conventional drugs or mesenchymal stem cells, nanozymes reduce local ROS levels, enhancing therapeutic efficacy. While explored in rheumatoid arthritis [117], their application in SS remains unreported. Notably, oxidative stress markers (e.g., 8-OHdG, 4-HNE, nitrotyrosine) are elevated in salivary ductal cells of SS patients compared to healthy controls [118], underscoring oxidative stress’s pathogenic role. Nanozymes have been found to demonstrate promising efficacy in treating dry eye disease by targeting oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [119, 120]. Nanozyme-based therapies may emerge as a novel strategy for SS. These antioxidant approaches offer promising avenues to advance SS therapeutics.

Gene therapy targeting senescence

Telomere shortening is a hallmark of cellular senescence, progressing with successive cell divisions until critical attrition triggers senescence. Strategies to extend telomere length or activate telomerase hold therapeutic potential. Telomere length is significantly reduced in autoimmune diseases like SS [121], we speculate that telomere maintenance may represent a promising therapeutic target. SS patients exhibit elevated telomerase activity in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) compared to healthy controls, likely a compensatory response to telomere attrition [122]. Further research is needed to harness telomerase activation for autoimmune diseases. Regular endurance and interval training enhance telomerase activity, offering a non-pharmacological approach to delay senescence [123].

Cell-based therapies

Cell therapy employs living cells to treat complex or refractory diseases, leveraging their regenerative capacity to repair, replace, or enhance tissue and organ function. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), derived from mesodermal tissues (e.g., bone marrow, adipose, umbilical cord, dental pulp, labial glands) [124],exhibit multipotent differentiation into osteocytes, adipocytes, and chondrocytes [125]. MSCs combat senescence through dual mechanisms: secreting growth factors and cytokines to promote proliferation/differentiation [126], and expressing antioxidant enzymes to neutralize free radicals [127]. MSC-based therapies show broad anti-senescence effects: MSC-liposome conjugates (Lip@NCAF) enhance lung regeneration in aged mice [128]; MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) reduce senescence markers (p15) and oxidative stress (SOD2) in osteoarthritis models [129]; human amniotic MSCs and their exosomes suppress ROS overproduction in age-related macular degeneration [130]; and MSCs improve oocyte maturation and embryonic development in female reproductive aging [131]. In SS, intravenous infusion of umbilical cord-derived MSCs improves salivary flow and reduces disease activity [132], while patient-specific iPSC-derived salivary progenitor cells restore glandular function [133]. However, the role of senescence modulation in these effects requires further elucidation. Although MSCs possess intrinsic antioxidant properties, oxidative stress in the local microenvironment may impair their survival, proliferation, and regenerative capacity [134], Nanozyme-engineered MSCs mitigate oxidative stress and enhance therapeutic efficacy [135], though this approach remains unexplored in SS. Recent advances in CAR-T therapy target senescent cells via surface-specific markers. NKG2D-CAR T cells selectively eliminate senescent cells with minimal off-target effects in C57BL/6 J mice, demonstrating therapeutic potential [136]. A case report of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma with concurrent SS achieved complete remission (CR) after anti-CD19 CAR-T therapy, accompanied by seronegativity for anti-nuclear and anti-Ro-52 antibodies and resolution of xerostomia [137]. Prospects and challenges

SS significantly impairs patients' quality of life as the disease progresses, frequently leading to anxiety, depression, insomnia, and even an increased risk of suicide [138, 139]. Current management of SS primarily relies on DMARDs and biologic agents. The application of biologics represents a significant clinical advancement, particularly those targeting B cells. Rituximab, by specifically binding to the CD20 molecule on B cells and promoting B cell apoptosis, significantly reduces serum IgG levels and ESSDAI scores in SS patients. However, its improvement in glandular function indicators (e.g., salivary flow rate, Schirmer's test) and ESSDAI scores remains limited [140]. However, a phase 2 study of rituximab combined with belimumab (anti-BAFF) demonstrated that the combination therapy was more effective than monotherapy in significantly reducing ESSDAI scores and improving SG function [141]. Furthermore, in a small-sample study, 62% (8/13) of rituximab-resistant SS patients treated with obinutuzumab (a novel anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody) achieved an ESSDAI score reduction of ≥ 3 points [142]. Telitacicept is a recombinant dual-target fusion protein of B-lymphocyte stimulator (BLyS) and a proliferation-inducing ligand (APRIL). It simultaneously inhibits the binding of BLyS and APRIL to their receptors on B cells, thereby preventing aberrant B cell differentiation and maturation. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 clinical trial of Telitacicept for primary SS, the agent significantly reduced immunoglobulin levels, disease activity, and fatigue in SS patients. However, its efficacy and safety require further validation [143]. Other biologic agents showing potential in SS treatment include: epratuzumab (targeting CD22 on B cells), ianalumab (blocking the BAFF receptor), PI3K inhibitors seletalisib and parsaclisib, nipocalimab (targeting FcRn), and agents targeting co-stimulatory receptors such as abatacept, iscalimab, and dazodalibep [80, 144–150]. In recent years, the use of JAK inhibitors has shown a trend of rapid growth and expanding indications, particularly in autoimmune diseases, including exploration in SS. Studies in SGECs have found that JAK inhibitors act directly on the JAK-STAT pathway downstream of IFN signaling, inhibit STAT3 phosphorylation, and can significantly reduce H₂O₂-induced ROS generation, thereby hindering disease progression. However, they also increase the risk of infection [151]. Additionally, CAR-T (Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-cell) therapy represents another novel treatment approach. These therapeutic methods require further investigation, making the exploration of other biological targets essential. An important emerging challenge is the occurrence of Sjögren's syndrome-like manifestations in cancer patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy. Treatment with topical fluorometholone and dequalinium chloride resulted in the resolution of sicca symptoms while maintaining cancer immunotherapy [152]. But balancing the treatment for cancer and SS poses a significant challenge. Furthermore, whether these immune checkpoint inhibitors can induce SG cell senescence is a key focus for our future research. Other than that, targeting SG cell senescence—or combining senescence-targeted approaches with existing treatments—may offer novel therapeutic options for SS patients. Establishing in vitro models of SG senescence, investigating senescence mechanisms through cellular and animal experiments, screening potential therapeutic agents, and conducting preclinical/clinical trials are critical steps to validate the safety and efficacy of these innovative therapies. Of course, SG senescence is not exclusive to SS. Chronic hyperglycemia in diabetes disrupts cellular metabolism, potentially accelerating SG cell senescence through ROS overproduction [153]. Similarly, radiation therapy for head and neck cancers irreversibly damages irradiated SG, leading to xerostomia [11], all of the above reveals therapeutic potential for targeting cellular senescence. It is important to note that while senolytic therapies show potential in SS, several key challenges remain. Firstly, off-target effects may lead to the unintended elimination of non-senescent cells (e.g., stem/progenitor cells), impairing tissue regenerative capacity [154]. Secondly, immune modulation requires careful evaluation, as SASP factors (e.g., IL-6, TGF-β) possess dual roles in both promoting inflammation and facilitating tissue repair [155]. Thirdly, the heterogeneity of senescent cells in SS necessitates the development of cell-type-specific targeting strategies to avoid broad toxicity.

However, anti-aging drugs also exhibit certain drawbacks. SASP factors secreted by senescent cells can activate stem cells and promote tissue repair [156]. When senolytics eliminate these cells, stem cells may lose essential signaling support, potentially compromising tissue regenerative capacity. During medication, fibrosis and alterations in the ductal structure can impair parenchymal drug penetration. Utilizing hydrogel-loaded senolytics to achieve precise drug release within the SG region can reduce systemic exposure. For instance, embedding Navitoclax nanoparticles into hydrogels forms an injectable localized drug delivery system, effectively addressing its hydrophobicity and systemic toxicity [157]. While clearing pathogenic senescent cells and reducing inflammation, there is a potential risk of impairing tissue regenerative capacity. Modifying administration strategies—such as intermittent dosing, localized targeted delivery (to reduce systemic exposure), or considering combination therapy with pro-regenerative factors—may help mitigate these potential adverse effects [154]. And the efficacy and safety of senolytics are highly contingent upon the intervention timing [154], where variations in dosing frequency, therapeutic window, and individualization significantly impact treatment outcomes. ​Additionally, whether organoid models can be leveraged to screen Senolytics candidates with lower toxicity to SGSCs remains an area requiring further investigation.

Similar to how exosomal miRNA-24-3p in saliva offers novel insights for diagnosing oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) [158], could we likewise develop a salivary-gland-specific senescence biomarker? This would hold immense significance for diagnosis and treatment. This article provides a comprehensive summary of the potential mechanisms underlying SG cell senescence and the corresponding therapeutic agents targeting these processes. However, most of these drugs have only been tested in animal studies. Translating these findings into human applications represents a significant leap and poses a formidable challenge, underscoring the substantial difficulties inherent in this translational process. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms of SG senescence and developing senescence-targeted drugs hold significant clinical implications for patients with SS and other salivary SG.
