Reply
Authors: Peter N Fiorica, Lawrence H Kushi, Heather Greenlee, Marilyn L Kwan, Song Yao
Published: 2026

We thank Dr Govind for his thoughtful comments on our work.1 We agree on the need to include subcontinental Asian populations in polygenic score (PGS) research for cardiovascular disease (CVD). Here, we respond to the key points raised in the letter.

First, the lack of statistically significant PGS performance in the East Asian subgroup in our analysis was likely due to limited sample size and lack of inclusion of ancestry-specific variants. We caution against referring to population-specific genetic risk for CVD among breast cancer survivors until more research has been published. Large-scale breast cancer consortia such as the Confluence Project may be well positioned to answer questions on ancestry-specific genetic risk for CVD after breast cancer treatment. Given the high risk of cardiotoxicities from coadministration of trastuzumab and anthracyclines, there is an urgent need to characterize the incidence of CVD in patients with breast cancer in South Asia, who are more commonly treated with anthracyclines in the context of dual-human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 blockage.

Second, there is no obvious explanation for why some of the European-derived PGS outperformed the multiancestry PGS. This surprising finding contradicts other work using up-to-date multiancestry PGS.2 However, the ischemic heart disease PGS that is referred to in Dr Govind's comment was not developed with up-to-date multiancestry PGS tools. When these tools were used in developing PGS for diabetes, the resultant score performed marginally better than single-ancestry PGS in our study.

Third, with respect to the point suggesting clinical implementation of PGS to predict cardiotoxicity when access to serial cardiac imaging is limited, our results showed that adding PGS to clinical risk models did not significantly improve prediction of incident CVD. It is thus likely neither informative nor cost-effective to use PGS in resource-limited areas until more definitive clinical research has been conducted with demonstrated cost savings.3

Lastly, we advocate for more intentional language when using population descriptors for patients of South and East Asian ancestries. Although clinical registries such as Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program use the descriptor of “Asian,” applying this label to PGS incorrectly assumes genetic homogeneity. Given the complex population structure within Asia, there is a need to explicitly explore how subcontinent-specific genetic variants impact risk for CVD in breast cancer survivors because differences in survival may exist among subcontinental Asian populations.4,5
