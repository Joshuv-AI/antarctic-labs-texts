Fluctuating hydrogen-bond network of the Hv1 ion channel
Authors: Honey Jain, Michalis Lazaratos, Peter Pohl, Ana-Nicoleta Bondar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The human voltage-gated proton channel Hv1 has an intriguing architecture, as (i) its voltage-sensing unit contributes to the proton permeation pathway and (ii) the single channel proton flux is too large to be supplied solely by diffusion from the aqueous surroundings, and it likely involves both titratable residues and protein-bound water molecules. The paths followed by protons being transferred through the channel, and the identity of the protein groups that can transiently bind protons, remain unclear. To investigate the dynamic hydrogen-bond paths sampled at the channel mouths and inside the pore we carried out extensive atomic-level molecular dynamics simulations and graph-based analyses. We found that the inter-helical region of the channel hosts an extensive water-mediated hydrogen-bond network that includes the key aspartic residue essential for proton selectivity and additional titratable sidechains. We implemented a graph-based protocol to identify continuous hydrogen-bond paths that can connect the aspartic residue to either side of the membrane. We report that the internal H-bond network connects to lipid headgroups at both membrane interfaces, which could provide a mechanism for (i) the surrounding lipid membrane to influence the protein conformational dynamics and the internal hydrogen-bond network, and (ii) channeling rapidly diffusing protons from the membrane surface into and out of the Hv1 pore.

Introduction

The human voltage-gated proton (H+) channel isoform 1, hHv1, is expressed in immune tissues [1], [2] and essential for the optimal function of phagocytes during the respiratory burst [3], [4]. Hv1 is overexpressed in highly metastatic cancer [5] and linked to a worsening of brain damage during early stages of ischemia [6], such that inhibitors of Hv1 are of interest to develop new therapeutics [7], [8].

Voltage-gated proton channels have two distinctive features: (i) the same protein domain serves for voltage sensing and proton permeation [1], and (ii) their unitary conductivity is larger than what can be explained by the diffusional supply of protons to the channel mouth at pH values > 6.0. Depending on pH, the estimated unitary proton flux may exceed the diffusion limit by up to two orders of magnitude [9]. The underlying molecular mechanisms of these phenomena remain insufficiently clarified. A plausible explanation is that a protein-spanning hydrogen(H)-bonded network, responsive to voltage-dependent conformational changes, connects to the membrane surface, where it can tap into the pool of rapidly migrating surface protons [10], [11] to boost the Hv1-mediated proton flux. The channel has strong proton selectivity, without detectable currents that would be due to the passage of other ions [12]. Essential for this selectivity is an aspartic acid residue located at approximately the middle of the channel – D112 in human Hv1, hHv1, corresponding to D108 in the mouse Hv1, mHv1. When D112 is substituted by residues other than Glu, the proton specificity is lost, and the D112A and D112H mutants are selective for chloride ions [12], [13], [14]. A salt bridge between D112 and R208 (R204 in mHv1, Fig. 1) of the Voltage Sensing Domain (VSD) is thought to function as the selectivity filter [12], and electrostatic interactions between D112 and Arg sidechains shape the proton-transfer energetics [15]. The voltage-dependent gating of Hv1, whereby upon membrane depolarization the channel opens to extrude protons, depends on the pH gradient: Hv1 is sensitive to the pH at both the intracellular and extracellular sides, but it opens when the proton electrochemical gradient favors proton transfer to the extracellular side [12]. For the isolated cytoplasmic C-terminal domain, circular dichroism measurements indicated pH-dependent α-helical content (94 % at pH = 6 vs. 79 % at pH = 8) [16]. The architecture of Hv1 and the location of functionally important residues are presented in Fig. 1 based on the structure of the mouse Hv1 chimera, mHv1cc, which probably represents a closed state of the channel [17]. This chimeric ion channel consists of the mHv1, an insertion in the cytoplasmic halves of helices S2 and S3 taken from Ci-VSP, and C-terminal cytoplasmic coiled coil region taken from the yeast transcription factor CGN4 [17]. For simplicity, for the remaining of the manuscript we will primarily use the residue numbers from mHv1. A summary of the numerical keys for selected amino acid residues of Hv1 from different species was presented in ref. [18].

D108, D149 and D181 (D112, E153 and D185 in hHv1), are located at approximately the middle of the membrane plane (Fig. 1), close to the VSD residues R201 (R205 in hHv1) and R204. D149 is within one helical turn of F146 of the so-called hydrophobic gasket, which is a cluster of four hydrophobic sidechains that block the flow of water and protons when the channel is closed [19]. In hHv1 D185 can be mutated to Asn, Ala, Met, or Val, and the ion channel remains proton selective [13] but, at least in the case of the D185A mutant, the voltage at which the channel opens shifted to more positive values [20]. Mutation of E153 to Ala or Asn had the opposite effect, as the transmembrane voltage at which hHv1 has a detectable tail current was shifted to more negative values [20]. When D170 (D174 in hHv1) was mutated to Ala or Asn the transmembrane voltage threshold shifted significantly towards more negative values [20] and the unitary channel conductivity was significantly reduced [21]. These observations from site-directed mutagenesis data suggest that, except for D108 (D112), the positions occupied by other carboxylic sidechains along the internal region of the channel, although important for the voltage response, are not essential for a proton current to be established.

We wondered whether this robustness of the ion channel towards mutation of internal carboxylic sidechains could arise from the carboxylic sidechains being part of a common, dynamic H-bond network.

We studied the dynamics of mHv1cc embedded in a hydrated POPC lipid membrane and implemented a graph-based protocol to identify continuous H-bond paths that can connect D108 to titratable residues close to either of the membrane interfaces. Considering the observations from the site-directed mutagenesis experiments summarized above, we wondered whether the D108 H-bond network we identified is robust towards protonation changes – which may occur during the proton-transfer functionality of the channel. We found that, although details can depend on the protonation of specific carboxylic and histidine sidechains of the internal H-bond network, D108 can connect to residues closer to either side of the membrane in all conditions used.

Our H-bond network graph analyses on Hv1 embedded in POPC led to the unanticipated finding that the internal H-bond network of Hv1 can connect to lipid headgroups. This could be the key for understanding the high unitary channel conductivity, as it provides a plausible pathway for the pool of protons otherwise confined to the membrane surface [22] to enter the pore. That lipids influence membrane protein function, including of voltage-gated ion channels, has been shown with experiment and computation [23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], however for Hv1 there is a gap in understanding the role of specific lipid interactions. Distinct experiments on Hv1 reconstituted in liposomes used different lipid composition and addressed distinct questions. The paper reporting that Hv1 is a voltage-gated proton channel without a dedicated pore domain used Human Embryonic Kidney (HEK) cells [1], whose membranes contain a complex lipid mixture–PC, PS, PE, PI, PG, sphingomyelin [31]. Compared to the native cells, in HEK (and in the fibroblas-like COS-9 cells), the threshold voltage of Hv1 is shifted by 30 mV towards more negative values [32]. A 3:1 POPE:POPG membrane was used to show that cholesterol inhibits Hv1 by interfering with the conformational dynamics of helix S4 [33], whereas adding arachidonic acid and zinc ions reverses this effect [34], a 3:1 POPC:POPG membrane, for Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) measurements that found water entering deep inside the channel [35] and a 6:6:3:3:1:1 POPC:POPE:POPS:SM:PI membrane model of the human neutrophil plasma membrane, to verify the reconstitution of the channel and measure its conductance [36]. Hv1 expressed in Escherichia coli and reconstituted in liposomes composed of POPC and cholesterol lacked proton channel activity, but was active in POPC:POPG liposomes; a puzzling observation was that the proton transfer rate estimated for the heterologously expressed Hv1 reconstituted in POPC:POPG:cholesterol, of about 10 protons per channel per second, was ‘at least three orders of magnitude smaller than that of native Hv1 channels’ [37]. Atomic-level molecular dynamics (MD) simulations studies of Hv1 are typically performed in POPC [20], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], though DOPC [44] and DMPC [45] were also used, and earlier coarse-grain simulations of Hv1 used DOPC and DOTAP membranes [46].

Motivated by our serendipitous finding that, in a POPC membrane, POPC headgroups connect to the H-bond network of Hv1, we carried out simulations in model membranes composed of POPE, POPG, POPE:POPG, and a lipid mixture that models the E. coli membrane. The H-bond network analyses we report indicate that, for some of the protein-lipid interactions, sampling depends on the lipid membrane composition.

Methods

Starting protein coordinates, protonation states, and lipid membrane composition. Protein coordinates were taken from the crystal structure of the mouse Hv1 chimeric channel mHv1cc, Protein Data Bank (PDB) [47] ID: 3wkv [17]. Hv1 is a dimer and the opening of the protomers is cooperative [48], but each monomer has its own proton-conduction path and can function on its own [49], [50]. The crystallographic structure of mHv1cc is a trimer. Experimental data indicated that an artificially-induced trimer of mHv1 is functional, albeit with somewhat different kinetics [17], [51]. For simplicity, and since the monomer is functional, we performed all computations on a single copy of mHv1cc, and in what follows we refer to the mHv1cc structure as ‘Hv1’.

We used Modeller [52], [53] version 9.25 to generate the missing internal loops (Figure S1), the Orientations of Proteins in Membranes web server, OPM [54] for the membrane protein orientation, and CHARMM-GUI [55], [56] to prepare starting coordinates of membrane-embedded Hv1.

We prepared 14 independent simulation systems distinguished by the protonation of selected titratable sidechains and/or the composition of the lipid bilayer, as summarized in Table 1. As a reference simulation we consider Hv1 embedded in POPC, and with all titratable sidechains described with standard protonation states, i.e., all Asp and Glu sidechains are negatively charged, all Arg and Lys, positively charged, and all His sidechains are neutral Nδ1 tautomers. All simulations in which at least one titratable sidechain had a protonation state distinct from the reference simulation also used a hydrated POPC bilayer. The non-standard protonation states we considered here are as follows. (i) H95 described as an Nε2 tautomer (Figures S1 A-B); (ii) H136 positively charged (protonated at both the Nδ1 and Nε2 atoms); (iii) D108, E115, D119, D149, E167, D170, or D181, were neutral (protonated) in 7 independent simulations (Table 1, Figure S1).

Simulations that probe the dynamics of Hv1 in membranes with different lipid composition use Hv1 with standard protonations for all titratable protein sidechains (the same as in the reference simulation above), but the membrane is composed of lipids other than POPC. We considered the following 4 lipid bilayers. (i) 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (POPE) lipids. Although both POPE and POPC lipids are zwitterionic, compared to the choline group of POPC the amine group of POPE has greater propensity to directly H-bond to protein groups; (ii) 5:1 POPE:POPG (1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphatidylglycerol), which introduces negatively charged POPG lipids, as a model for bacterial membranes [57]. (iii) 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-L-serine (POPS) lipids, to verify protein-lipid H-bonding in a membrane composed of negatively-charged lipids; iv) the E. coli membrane model Top6 composed of 6 PE and PG lipid types, to study Hv1 dynamics in a more realistic cell membrane which is also thinner than 5:1 POPE:POPG [57].

Protocol for MD simulations. We used CHARMM36 for the protein, lipids, and ions [58], [59], [60], [61], [62], the TIP3P water model [63], and NAMD version 2.13 to perform the simulations [64], [65], [66]. We used the smooth particle mesh Ewald summation [67], [68] for Coulomb interactions, and a switch function between 10 Å and 12 Å for real space interactions. An initial equilibration with velocity rescaling and weak harmonic constraints, ∼1 ns in total, was performed according to the standard CHARMM-GUI protocol. All constraints were then switched off for the production runs, which were performed in the NPT ensemble (constant number of particles N, constant pressure P = 1 bar, and constant temperature T = 310.15 K) using a Langevin dynamics scheme [69], [70] with an oscillation period of 200fs, a damping time scale of 100fs, and a damping coefficient of 5 ps−1. The lengths of covalent bonds to H atoms were kept fixed [71]. Equilibration and the first 1 ns of the production runs were performed with an integration step of 1fs; all remaining production runs were performed with a multiple time step integration scheme [72], [73] with integration steps 1fs for the bonded interactions, 2fs for short-range nonbonded forces, and 4fs for long-range electrostatics. Coordinates were saved every 10 ps.

Cα Root-mean-squared distances (RMSD) profiles. Time series of the Cα RMSD profiles were monitored separately for the entire protein, for the transmembrane (TM) helical region and for the loops and termini of Hv1 (Figure S2). The time series were monitored along the entire production runs, and average RMSD values were computed from the last 200 ns of each simulation (Table S1).

H-bond criteria, H-bond occupancy, H-bond graphs, local H-bond clusters, and H-bond paths. We used standard distance and angle criteria for H-bonding, whereby two groups were considered as H-bonded if the distance between the donor and acceptor heteroatoms was within 3.5 Å, and the H-bond angle, within 60º. The occupancy of an H-bond gives the percentage of the coordinate sets used for analyses during which the H-bond criteria are met [74], [75], [76]. An H-bond graph consists of nodes, which here are the protein H-bonding sidechains, and edges, which are direct or water-mediated H-bonds between protein sidechains. The local H-bond cluster or local network of a specific protein sidechain consists of the nodes and edges that connect to that specific group of interest [75], [76].

A shortest-distance H-bond path between two nodes (protein groups) of interest consists of the nodes and edges that inter-connect those two protein groups via intermediate H-bonds [75]. The length of the shortest distance path between graph nodes ni and nj is given by the number of path segments between them. The Joint Occupancy (JO) of the path between nodes ni and nj is given by the percentage of coordinate sets in which all intermediate path segments are sampled at the same moment of time [75].

All H-bond graph computations were performed with Bridge/Bridge2 [75], [76]. H-bond clusters and H-bond paths were extracted from the H-bond graphs using Connected Component Analyses [77] as implemented in Bridge. Unless specified otherwise, water-mediated bridges between protein sidechains include up to three H-bonded water molecules, and H-bond connections included in the graphs shown have occupancies of at least 30 %. The path JO computations were performed on the H-bond graphs with minimum H-bond occupancy of 30 %. Each H-bond graph computation is based on 20.000 equally spaced coordinate sets from the last 200 ns of the simulation.

To compute lipid-protein-water H-bond graphs, lipids within 10 Å of protein were manually selected and their headgroups treated in the graph computation with the same H-bond criteria as described above. The phosphate and ester groups were included in the computation for all lipids; for the other lipids, we additionally included H-bonding groups as follows: the ethanolamine groups for phosphatidylethanolamine lipids, glycerol for phosphatidylglycerol, and the serine groups for phosphatidylserine lipids.

Thickness of the lipid bilayer. We used the MEMBPLUGIN [78] in Visual Molecular Dynamics, VMD [79] to estimate the average thickness of lipid bilayers. Computations were performed for the lipid phosphate atoms using the last 200 ns of each simulation.

Number of lipid phosphate groups close to protein sidechains. For selected H-bonding protein sidechains we monitored the time series of the number of lipid phosphate atoms within 3.5 Å distance of the sidechain’s H-bonding hetero-atoms. We counted the number of phosphate groups for which at least one oxygen atom is within 3.5 Å distance of any of the oxygen or nitrogen atoms of the selected protein sidechains. From the last 200 ns of the simulations we then computed (i) the average number of lipid phosphate atoms within 3.5 Å distance of the sidechain of interest; (ii) the frequency with which at least one lipid phosphate atom is within 3.5 Å distance of the sidechain of interest.

Results and discussion

To investigate the dynamic H-bond network of Hv1 we performed atomic-level simulations and graph-based analyses (Table 1). For each of the 14 independent simulations we monitored the overall protein structural stability and conformational dynamics (Figures S2, S3, Table S1), computed the protein-water H-bond graphs to study the water-mediated H-bond networks, and analyzed the lipid-protein interactions. Below we largely focus our discussion on the dynamic H-bond networks that can connect the critical D108 to either side of the membrane.

The channel hosts an extensive water-mediated H-bond network that inter-connects functionally important protein groups. Numerous water molecules visit the inter-helical region of the channel and mediate H-bond bridges between protein sidechains such that, at any moment of time along the simulations, there are ∼10 direct H-bonds between protein sidechains and ∼40 H-bonded water bridges between pairs of sidechains (Fig. 2A&D, 3 A&D). In the reference simulation of the POPC-embedded channel, D108 has highly persistent (>80 % occupancy), mostly direct, H-bonds with R201 and R204, and a water-mediated bridge with D181. D181 and R204 have additional water bridges with S177, which has a mostly direct H-bond with W203; these connections are somewhat more dynamic, with average H-bond occupancies of about 39–58 % (Figures S4, S5). Thus, negatively charged D108 is part of a local inter-helical H-bond cluster of direct and water-mediated bridges that includes W103 (Figures S5, S6). W103 is highly conserved (Figure S7) and its substitution with Ala, Phe, or Ser, accelerates channel opening up to 100-fold and perturbs the ΔpH-sensitivity of the channel [80].

The inter-helical H-bond cluster of D108 extends to H136 at the extracellular side but, except for an infrequently sampled W203-R207 water bridge (Figures S4, S5), it lacks persistent H-bonds with the D170 H-bond cluster. Instead, D170 connects to R207 directly and to E167 and N210 via water bridges (Figs. 2, S4, S5). Since N210 further connects to Q98 and N160, the local cluster of D170 appears a central connection node of the channel, a site where all four transmembrane segments inter-connect.

An important role of D170 for the local H-bond connections within the channel would be compatible with the experimental observation that the D170A mutation significantly reduces the unitary channel conductance [21]. Moreover, our finding that N210 is part of the local inter-helical H-bond cluster of D170 could help explain why the N210A and N210C mutants have defective proton permeation [18]. When N210 is mutated to Ala or Cys, the absence of the carboxamide group could lead to a reorganization of the inter-helical H-bonding network. This, in turn, may affect the local water and protein dynamics, ultimately hindering proton conduction [12], [20]. We suggest that N210, as part of the local inter-helical cluster around D170, could contribute to proton permeation.

As we illustrate with the color-coding of the graph nodes in Fig. 2A, the H-bond network of the channel contains other Asp, Glu, and His sidechains in both the D108 and D170 clusters, and some of these sidechains are close to the channel mouths. Bulk-exposed clusters of carboxylic groups, with or without histidine sidechains, are present in e.g., bacteriorhodopsin [81], cytochrome c oxidase [82], [83], the soluble PsbO subunit of photosystem II [84], [85], [86], [87], [88], and the proton-sensing G protein-coupled receptor GPR68 [89], where they are commonly thought to function as proton-collecting antennas. Bulk-exposed proton antennas could help deliver protons to residues at the entrance of a membrane transporter [81].

H-bond connections of D108 and other carboxylic and histidine sidechains of the H-bond network . Our classical mechanical simulations are performed with fixed protonation states. During ion channel function, as protons are transferred across the channel, protons may transiently bind to titratable protein sidechains that delineate the water-filled interior of the channel (Fig. 1A). The identity of protein groups that participate in proton transfers, and the sequence of events along the reaction path of the channel’s pH sensing and proton transfer, are largely unclear. To verify whether H-bond paths of D108 may be sensitive to protonation changes of the network, we studied the H-bond network of the channel with distinct protonation states (Table 1, Fig. 3). We present details of the average H-bond occupancies and average water bridge lengths for the H-bond networks in Figures S4 and S8-S16, and summarize average occupancies of selected H-bonds in Tables S2 and S3. The total number of H-bonds and water bridges of the network is about the same when D108 is negatively charged or neutral (Figs. 2D, 3D). However, there are a number of H-bond network rearrangements when D108 is neutral, including: (i) the persistent connection between negatively charged D108 and R204, which is sampled in the reference simulation (Fig. 2A), is absent when D108 is neutral (Fig. 3A); (ii) R204 connects to D170; (iii) D108 samples a water-mediated bridges with E115 and S139 (Fig. 3, S8); (iv) the extracellular water-mediated H-bond cluster of E115, D119, E192, and H190, which is sampled in the reference simulation (Fig. 2A), now includes the D126-E127-Q128-D129 stretch and H189 (Fig. 2C); (v) at the cytoplasmic side, the H-bond between D170 and N210 is no longer sampled (compare Figs. 2A and 3A); (vi) the local H-bond network of N210 rearranges such that N210 now has only one persistent H-bond connection, with R207; (vii) the connection between helix S3 E167 and helix S2 Y157, sampled when D108 is negatively charged (Fig. 2A), is absent from the H-bond graph of the channel with neutral D108 (Fig. 3A). Helix dynamics associate with these inter-helical H-bond changes (Figure S3B).

For all other protonation states considered, though details of the local H-bond network depend on the protonation state, D108 is part of a water-mediated H-bond network that effectively spans most of the transmembrane region of the channel (Fig. 4D-H). For the path JO values summarized in Fig. 6 we used the H-bond graphs presented in Figures S4 and S8-S16, in which the minimum H-bond occupancy is 30 %, and considered only shortest distance H-bond paths with a minimum JO value of 5 %. With these criteria, we aimed to find out whether there are relatively long H-bond paths for which nevertheless all intermediate path segments (H-bonds and water bridges) are sampled simultaneously. The residues that are intermediate nodes along the path are listed in Table S5. Note that, for the path between two graph nodes ni and nj (e.g., D108 and D181) there can be more than one shortest-distance path of same length but going via distinct intermediate nodes (protein residues).

Generally, and as anticipated, the largest JO values are found for the shorter paths. The short H-bond paths between D108 and D181, with path length = 2 (two H-bonds or two water-mediated bridges between D108 and D181) have the largest JO values regardless of the protonation states considered, though there are also alternative paths between D108 and D181 with somewhat smaller JO (Fig. 6). Thus, in all simulations performed with distinct protonation states there is more than one H-bond path that connects D108 to D181, and at least one of these paths has very large JO values > 90 % (Fig. 6A-J); in several simulations, H-bond paths between D108 and D181 can also have lower JO values < 40 %, e.g., when D149 (Fig. 6B), D181 (Fig. 6F), or E167 are neutral (Fig. 6H). The longest shortest-distance H-bond path found here is from D108 to H95 in simulations of the POPE-embedded channel with standard protonation states: it involves no fewer than 9 path segments, yet its JO value is > 5 % (Fig. 6K).

Compared to negatively-charged D108 (Fig. 6A), neutral D108 (Fig. 6B) has several additional H-bond paths with longer path segments and noticeable JO values, including to D149 and E167 at the cytoplasmic side. Dynamic H-bond paths with JO values between 10 % and 38 % can also connect neutral D108 to N164 at the cytoplasmic side (Fig. 1B, Table S5). This amino acid residue position is potentially important for the functioning of the channel, as in hHv1 the corresponding residue, H168, was proposed to function as the internal pH-sensing group [90]. All H-bond paths between neutral D108 and N164 pass via D170 and E167. Negatively charged D108 lacks continuous H-bond paths to N164 when either D170 or E167 are neutral (Table S5), though it can still connect to N164 in simulations with neutral D149, with H95-Ne2, or with positively charged H136, these connections are somewhat more infrequent (Table S5). Continuous connections between D108 and E167 are also sampled when D181 is neutral (Fig. 6F) and with H95-Ne2 (Fig. 6I). By contrast, neutral D170 associates with several connections with JO > 20 % being sampled between D108 and D119 (Fig. 6E). Although D119 is close to the extracellular side (Fig. 1B), H-bond paths between D108 and D119 can be influenced by the protonation states of residues in both halves of the channel. Only one H-bond path with Path Length = 2 connects D108 and D119 when either residue is neutral (Figs. 6B, 6C), when D181 (Fig. 6F) or E115 (Fig. 6G) are neutral, and when H95 is described as -Nε2 tautomer (Fig. 6I); likewise, only one path with length 3 connects D108 and D119 in the reference simulation (Fig. 6A) and when H136 is positively charged (Fig. 6J). The sensitivity of the protein-lipid H-bonds to lipids, as illustrated above, likely arises from differences in both the H-bonding capabilities of the lipid headgroups and the bilayer thickness. Compared to POPE, the Top6 E. coli membrane is about 4 Å thinner, that is, about one helical turn thinner; this could help explain why it is easier for R204 to snorkel towards the interface and anchor to lipids in the E. coli membrane. POPS and POPE have very similar bilayer thickness yet lipid anchoring of R201 and R207 occurs in POPE, but not in POPS. Both POPE and POPS headgroups can water bridge to residues of the internal H-bond network at both sides of the membrane (Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9, S26, Table S4): for example, POPE/POPS H-bonding is sampled by S3-E167, and helix S1 residues Q98 and R96 (Fig. 9 O, S26, Table S4).

Conclusions

The aspartic residue essential for proton selectivity in Hv1, D108, is located roughly at the middle of the channel. How D108 connects to either side of the membrane is largely unclear. From MD simulations and graph-based analyses of the H-bond network of the POPC-embedded closed channel we found that a dynamic water-mediated H-bond network connects D108 to other polar and charged residues and water molecules (Fig. 9). Such an H-bond network may facilitate proton entry into the channel from the interface. Since interfacial protons can differ in both mobility and concentration from bulk protons [11] —due to an energy barrier that impedes exchange with the bulk phase [91], [92]—the resulting increase in local proton availability could significantly enhance proton flux. This mechanism may help explain the high unitary conductance of Hv1, which has been reported to exceed the classical diffusion limit [9].

To evaluate H-bond paths that can connect D108 to residues closed to either side of the bulk we quantified the frequency with which continuous H-bond paths between D108 and other residues are sampled (i.e., all H-bonds of the path are sampled at the same moment of time along a simulation trajectory), and represented this frequency (JO) as a function of the number of H-bonds (length) of the path. To verify whether the protonation state of other titratable sidechains could influence the dynamics of H-bond paths of D108, we studied the H-bond networks for several hypothetic protonation states of the channel. The protonation states we have chosen are not meant to represent an actual proton transfer path or the pKa values of the residues. Nevertheless, the computations of the H-bond network analyses are useful as a glimpse into the remarkable flexibility of the internal H-bond network of Hv1, which allows titratable sidechains located remote from D108 to influence the paths connecting D108 to either side of the bulk.

A serendipitous finding from our analyses of H-bond networks of POPC-embedded Hv1 was that lipid headgroups can connect to residues that are also part of the internal H-bond network. While a systematic experimental characterization of Hv1 function in membranes with distinct lipid composition remains an important avenue to be explored in the future, we note that lipid interactions are known to be essential for ion transporters. For the well-studied KvAP voltage-gated potassium ion channel, experimental measurements using black lipid membranes and lipid vesicles led to the conclusion that ‘the channel is gated by its annular lipids’ [28]. More recently, lipid mediated gating has been demonstrated using photoswitchable lipids [93]. In a mutant Kv1.2 channel, phosphatidic acid from the intracellular leaflet interacts with VSD arginine residues, possibly via H-bonding, and stabilizes the channel in the closed conformation [94]. An H-bond network involving water, lipid phosphate groups, and the VSD Arg sidechains, has been observed before in simulations of the S4 voltage-sensing helix of the KvAP potassium ion channel [30]. The unrelated proton-coupled multidrug transporter LmrP requires interactions between the conserved D68 and PE lipids for its proton sensing activity [26]. D68 is located at the cytoplasmic tip of a transmembrane helix [95], where it is part of a local protein H-bond network and its specific interactions with lipid headgroups stabilize the inward-facing conformation of the transporter [96]. In the case of Hv1, a characterization of its function in liposomes shows a strong dependence on lipid composition with reported conductances of the reconstituted channel ranging from very small in synthetic lipids [37] to more appreciable values in natural lipid mixtures [21]. Hv1 reconstituted in 9:1 POPC-cholesterol liposomes, POPC being the typical choice for MD simulations of Hv1, lacked activity, which could be established by adding POPG or POPG and cholesterol [37]. Our computations of the H-bond network of Hv1 in hydrated membranes with different lipid composition indicate that the lipid membrane composition influences the dynamics of the internal H-bond network of the channel.

The starting mHv1cc protein structure likely represents a closed state of the ion channel [17]. In the absence of an experimental high-resolution structure of the open channel, one can assume that the opening of the ion channel involves a protein structural rearrangement that associates with the formation of H-bond paths that allow protons to be transferred via a Grotthus mechanism, with the water wire interrupted by at least one intervening amino acid side chain [21]. Supporting this finding, recent reactive MD simulations and free energy computations on human Hv1 have likewise called ‘into question the plausibility of a purely water-wire conduction mechanism’ [40].

Formation of a persistent water wire across the channel was observed during earlier MD simulations of a homology model of the hHv1 monomer based on the Kv1.2-Kv2.1 paddle-chimera structure as a template [38]; the sampling of this water wire depended on details of water-protein interactions, particularly with the VSD arginine sidechains [38]. In the simulations we present here, water-mediated H-bond networks across the channel are sampled in a protonation-dependent manner. We think, however, that these simulations in which water-mediated H-bond path cross the pores should not be described as open-like states: such an assignment would require computations of the proton transfer energetics and of the dynamic rearrangements of the H-bonded waters and protein sidechains during proton transfer. Computations of the channel in its open state would be required to evaluate the H-bond networks of the open channel. It would be of interest, for example, to use the protocol presented here to evaluate how D108 connects to either side of the membrane in the open state of the channel, and to extend our H-bond network analyses to quantify the lifetime of the water bridges.

Another important aspect that would need to be addressed in the future concerns the influence of Zn2+ ion binding to Hv1. Atomic-level simulations of hHv1 suggested that Zn2+ binding associates with decreased protein flexibility [41]. However, as accurate description of Zn2+ coordination geometries, including at histidine binding sites, remains challenging for standard force-fields [97], further developments in force-fields for Zn2+-protein interactions and structural biology of Hv1 will be needed to characterize the interplay between Zn2+ binding and protein dynamics during Hv1 function.

The protocol we presented here to identify continuous H-bond paths of D108 could be extended to study H-bond paths from combined quantum mechanical/molecular mechanical simulations (QM/MM) of the proton transfer reactions, or from constant pH simulations of the protonation-coupled channel dynamics. Unlike in standard MD simulations, in which protonation states are assigned from the start remain unchanged over the course of one simulation, with constant pH simulations protonation states are allowed to change and one can sample the conformational dynamics of the protein at pH values of interest. Also, although the monomeric channel is functional, the coupling between the lipid bilayer and protein conformational dynamics indicated by the simulations with distinct lipid bilayers suggests that studies of the dimeric ion channel could be useful to verify the protein conformational dynamics. We envision that an important avenue for future research on Hv1 will be to study with experiment and computation the role of the lipid-protein interactions for proton transfers. A role of the lipid membrane in a local proton antenna cluster [81] and for lateral proton transfer [98] was discussed in seminal work on the bacteriorhodopsin proton pump. More recently, a proton antenna effect was discussed for the interface of DOPG liposomes [99]. In the future, methodological and computing infrastructure developments could allow, for example, QM/MM computations to probe protonation dynamics at the Hv1/membrane interface.
