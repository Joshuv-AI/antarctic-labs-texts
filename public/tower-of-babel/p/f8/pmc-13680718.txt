Transient high-output heart failure documented by echocardiographic cardiac index measurement in a pregnant cat
Authors: Yasutomo HORI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A female cat (approximately 3 weeks of gestation) was referred for respiratory distress and pleural effusion. Echocardiography revealed dilation of the left atrium and left ventricle, and the cardiac index was markedly elevated (0.39 L/kg/min), raising suspicion for high-output heart failure (HOHF). The cat was treated with furosemide and enrofloxacin. Pleural effusion completely resolved in late pregnancy, and 45 days after delivery the cardiac index decreased to 0.22 L/kg/min. To our knowledge, this is the first report describing a transient HOHF associated with pregnancy in a cat, highlighting the clinical utility of echocardiographic cardiac index measurement for identifying this condition. Further accumulation of cases is needed to better characterise this condition in feline patients.

High-output heart failure (HOHF) is a rare form of heart failure characterized by eccentric hypertrophy and an abnormally elevated cardiac output (CO) beyond the normal range [8]. Pregnancy is associated with increased circulating blood volume and may contribute to the development of heart failure in humans [1, 10, 15]. Only a few cases have been reported in cats, including two cases of congestive heart failure (CHF) occurring in late pregnancy [7, 14], and detailed clinical characteristics of pregnancy-associated heart failure in cats remain poorly described. This report describes a cat that developed transient HOHF accompanied by increased CO during pregnancy.

A 5.5-year-old female Sphynx cat was presented to a local veterinary clinic with a 3-day history of decreased appetite and tachypnoea. At the referring veterinary clinic, enrofloxacin (4.5 mg/kg q24 hr) had been prescribed empirically because an infection could not be excluded in the presence of an increased serum amyloid A concentration (41.8 mg/L; reference interval, <3.75 mg/L). The cat was subsequently referred to our hospital for evaluation of suspected cardiac disease and possible pericardial effusion. On presentation, the cat weighed 3.3 kg (body condition score 5/9), with a heart rate of 220–230 bpm and a respiratory rate of 44 breaths/min. No cardiac murmur was auscultated. Abdominal distension was noted; however, the cat was approximately 3 weeks pregnant. Based on these findings, the cat was tentatively diagnosed with an unclassified cardiomyopathy phenotype. In addition, stroke volume and CO were calculated using both left and right ventricular outflow tract methods, as previously described [2, 9]. For calculation of stroke volume, the aortic velocity–time integral was obtained by pulsed-wave Doppler from the left apical long-axis view at the level of the aortic valve, whereas the pulmonary velocity–time integral was obtained from the right parasternal short-axis view at the level of the pulmonary valve. Briefly, stroke volume was calculated as outflow tract cross-sectional area × velocity–time integral. Cardiac output was calculated as stroke volume × heart rate, and cardiac index (CI) was calculated by dividing CO by body weight or body surface area. Pulmonary-to-systemic blood flow ratio (Qp/Qs) was calculated using Doppler-derived flow measurements as previously described [11]. Pulmonary and systemic blood flow were derived from the right and left ventricular outflow tracts, respectively, and Qp/Qs was calculated as the ratio of pulmonary to systemic blood flow. The left ventricular CI calculated from the body weight at presentation was 0.39 L/kg/min, which exceeds the normal feline CI (0.112 ± 0.038 L/kg/min, [2]), and was therefore considered elevated. The Qp/Qs ratio was within the normal range (Table 1). Thus, HOHF was suspected.

Initial treatment consisted of furosemide (1.52 mg/kg q12 hr) and enrofloxacin (4.5 mg/kg q24 hr) administered for 7 days. After 7 days, the cat’s respiratory status and appetite had improved; therefore, the furosemide dosage was gradually reduced to q24 hr. In late pregnancy, the pulmonary oedema and pleural effusion had completely resolved; therefore, furosemide was discontinued. Forty-three days after the first presentation, the cat delivered seven kittens, one of which was stillborn.

On day 89 (45 days after delivery), ultrasonographic examination showed improvement in dilation of both atria and ventricle (Fig. 3C, 3D). The CI calculated from the left ventricular outflow tract decreased from 0.39 L/kg/min at baseline to 0.22 L/kg/min (Table 1). Following the present pregnancy, the cat underwent three additional pregnancies without recurrence of CHF. At the most recent recheck on day 1,033, the cat was in good general condition. The most notable finding in this case was a markedly elevated CI measured using Doppler echocardiography, which strongly suggested HOHF. HOHF is a rare form of CHF characterised by eccentric hypertrophy induced by volume overload and a CO abnormally elevated beyond the normal range [8]. In a previously published box plot, the interquartile ranges of stroke volume and CO derived from the left ventricular outflow tract in healthy cats appear to be approximately 5.0–5.5 mL and 1.0–1.15 L/min, respectively [3]. The CI derived from the left ventricular outflow tract in healthy cats has been reported as 0.112 ± 0.038 L/kg/min [2]. Although direct comparison is not possible, the CO and CI in the present case were approximately 1.2- and 3.5-fold higher, respectively, than previously reported values. Because the CI is normalised to body weight, this marked increase reflects a disproportionately high CO relative to body size. Although definitive diagnostic criteria for HOHF in cats have not been established, the development of oedema despite increased CO is consistent with HOHF as defined in human medicine [8].

Other causes of increased CO, such as hyperthyroidism, arteriovenous fistula, and sepsis, were considered unlikely based on the clinical course and resolution after delivery. Serum thyroxine concentration was not measured in this case because hyperthyroidism is uncommon in cats of this age and because the clinical signs resolved completely after delivery.

Pregnancy is associated with increased circulating blood volume, which may contribute to elevated CO [1]. In healthy cats, stroke volume increases from approximately 5.5 mL to 6–7 mL and CO from approximately 1.0 L/min to 1.4–1.8 L/min from mating to the first and second trimesters [3]. In two previously reported cats with pregnancy-associated CHF, these variables were not assessed [7, 14]. Although baseline CO and CI were unknown in the present case, CO and CI at the initial visit were approximately 1.6–2.0 times higher than the postpartum values, when clinical signs and cardiac enlargement had resolved. Taken together, pregnancy-associated volume overload likely contributed to CHF and pleural effusion through a marked increase in CO.

In human medicine, peripartum cardiomyopathy develops in late pregnancy or the postpartum period and is characterized in part by left ventricular systolic dysfunction [13]. In the present case, fractional shortening was higher than normal reference values, consistent with previous case reports [7, 14], and may reflect pregnancy-related volume overload in cats [3]. This finding suggests that the pathophysiology of CHF during pregnancy in cats may differ from that of human peripartum cardiomyopathy [13]. In addition, unlike previously reported cats that developed CHF in late pregnancy [7, 14], the present cat developed CHF during mid-pregnancy. The reason for this discrepancy remains unclear but may reflect individual variation in cardiovascular adaptation to pregnancy.

In a previously reported cat, pimobendan was administered during late pregnancy and discontinued after resolution of CHF postpartum [7]. In the present case, although both furosemide and enrofloxacin may pose potential risks to fetuses, treatment was considered necessary because CHF represented a greater immediate risk to both the queen and the fetuses. Enrofloxacin had been prescribed by the referring veterinarian because infection could not be excluded. Although enrofloxacin has been associated with abnormalities in articular cartilage development in juvenile animals, no apparent musculoskeletal abnormalities were observed in the surviving kittens; however, one kitten was stillborn. Diuretics such as furosemide are commonly used for the management of peripartum cardiomyopathy in human medicine [16]. Furosemide was discontinued after resolution of CHF, and enrofloxacin after completion of the treatment course. These findings suggest that pregnancy-associated CHF in cats may resolve with minimal medical intervention. However, because safe and effective treatment protocols have not been established, treatment duration should be minimized whenever possible.

The cat in this case has remained asymptomatic without treatment for approximately 3 years following the initial visit. Similarly, in two previously reported cats with pregnancy-associated CHF, heart failure resolved postpartum, with asymptomatic periods of 9 years and 688 days [7, 14], consistent with the present case. These findings suggest that pregnancy-associated CHF in cats may have a favorable long-term prognosis; however, continued long-term follow-up may be warranted.

In conclusion, this case describes a cat that exhibited transient HOHF associated with increased circulating blood volume during pregnancy. This case highlights the potential utility of echocardiographic CI measurement for identifying HOHF in cats.
