The relationship between sleep quality and nomophobia among medical students
Authors: Narges Sadeghi, Shahab Rezaeian, Maryam Janatolmakan, Mohammad Moein Mohammadi, Pegah Heidarian, Alireza Khatony
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedical students often experience sleep disturbances and maladaptive behavioral patterns, such as problematic smartphone use and nomophobia, due to the demanding nature of their studies and the stressful academic environment. This study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of poor sleep quality among medical students at Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences in western Iran, identify associated factors, and explore its relationship with nomophobia. By focusing on this population, we aim to highlight the unique challenges medical students face concerning their sleep health.MethodsThis descriptive-analytical cross-sectional study was conducted from August 25, 2023, to March 7, 2024. A total of 419 medical students were recruited through convenience sampling. Data were collected using a demographic information form, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) (with a cut-off score > 5 indicating poor sleep quality) to assess sleep quality, and the Nomophobia Questionnaire (NMP-Q) to evaluate nomophobia. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Scheffé post-hoc tests, Pearson’s correlation, and multiple logistic regression performed using STATA version 14. In the logistic regression, both odds ratios (OR) and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) were reported. Adjusted odds ratio (AOR) means that the effects of other variables under study, which may influence the relationship between a specific variable and sleep quality, have been controlled for in the analysis.ResultsMore than half of the medical students (53.7%) reported poor sleep quality (cut-off for poor sleep quality > 5). A significant proportion (80%) exhibited moderate to severe nomophobia. Multiple logistic regression analysis revealed that checking smartphones immediately upon waking (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.30–4.07, P < 0.001) and higher nomophobia scores (AOR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.01–1.02, P = 0.012) were independently associated with poor sleep quality after adjusting for potential confounders.ConclusionsThe high prevalence of poor sleep quality and nomophobia among medical students is concerning and highlights the urgent need for targeted interventions. Implementing educational programs on sleep hygiene and smartphone use, along with regular screening for sleep disturbances and accessible psychological support services, could effectively improve student well-being. Addressing academic stressors through institutional policy changes may also help reduce the burden of poor sleep quality in this population.Keywords: Sleep, Sleep quality, Medical students, Nomophobia, Smartphone, Prevalence

Introduction

Adequate and quality sleep plays a crucial role in maintaining health and improving mental and physical performance, while insufficient sleep can have detrimental effects on both physical and mental well-being [1, 2]. Poor sleep quality is a common problem among university students [3], and medical students, in particular, appear to be more susceptible to sleep disorders compared to students in other fields of study [4]. To demonstrate the widespread nature of this issue and to reflect the variability in prevalence estimates reported globally, studies from different countries have been cited. These studies were selected based on their focus on medical or healthcare students, the recency of publication, and their investigation of sleep quality or its associated behavioral and contextual factors, such as smartphone use, stress, or academic load. The prevalence of poor sleep quality among medical students in Rwanda (2023) and Egypt (2023) has been reported to be 80% and 63.1%, respectively, while among nursing students in India (2021), it was reported to be 82.42% [5–7]. Similarly, the prevalence of poor sleep quality among medical students in Nepal (2022) and Iran (2020) has been reported to be 38.2% and 40%, respectively [8, 9]. These discrepancies in prevalence rates may be attributed to various factors, including cultural norms related to sleep and mental health, differences in educational systems (e.g., intensity of training or clinical exposure), and methodological aspects such as sample size, sleep assessment tools, and diagnostic thresholds used in the studies. For example, regions with more rigorous academic environments, higher clinical workloads, or limited mental health support may exhibit higher rates of poor sleep quality. Likewise, cultural stigma surrounding sleep issues or mental health may lead to underreporting in certain settings [1, 7, 10]. Evidence suggests that poor sleep quality can negatively impact academic performance and mental health in students [3].

Several factors are associated with poor sleep quality among medical students, including a stressful learning environment, a heavy academic workload, and long clinical hours [1, 10]. In addition to academic factors, psychological factors such as smartphone addiction and nomophobia can also influence sleep quality in students [11–13]. Nomophobia, short for “No Mobile Phone PhoBIA,” is defined as the irrational fear of being without or unable to access one’s smartphone and its associated services [13–15]. Studies indicate that students are among the groups with the highest prevalence of nomophobia. For instance, a 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of 23 articles reported a nomophobia prevalence of nearly 100% among students [13].

Problematic smartphone use can affect psychological processes and brain function, disrupting sleep patterns and leading to sleep-wake cycle disturbances, insomnia, and reduced sleep quality [3, 16]. The impact of nomophobia on insomnia is attributed to feelings of fear and helplessness when not using the phone, leading to psychological changes [15]. Several studies have reported a significant statistical association between nomophobia, insomnia, and student sleep quality [15–19]. However, some studies have not found a significant relationship between smartphone addiction, nomophobia, insomnia, and sleep quality [8, 20, 21].

Given the impact of sleep quality on physical and mental health, particularly on the academic performance of medical students [1], and the inconsistencies in the findings regarding the relationship between sleep quality and nomophobia [15–21], the present study was designed and conducted. This study aimed to determine sleep quality and its associated factors, focusing on the role of nomophobia, among medical students in Kermanshah, a western province of Iran. The findings of this study can be beneficial in gaining a better understanding of sleep problems and their associated factors, especially nomophobia, among medical students in Kermanshah, and in developing effective strategies to improve their sleep quality.

The decision to conduct this study in Kermanshah was based on several important considerations. Although the general relationship between nomophobia and sleep quality has been studied internationally, there is a notable lack of research investigating this relationship specifically among Iranian medical students and more broadly in the Middle East region. Cultural, educational, and regional differences may substantially influence sleep behaviors and smartphone use patterns. In particular, stronger societal stigma regarding psychological disorders, including sleep-related issues, may exist in this region, potentially discouraging individuals from seeking help. Furthermore, universities in different parts of Iran may follow diverse educational policies and training structures, impacting students’ stress levels and, consequently, their sleep quality. Kermanshah, as a culturally diverse and socioeconomically varied region in western Iran, presents unique conditions that may shape both smartphone dependency and sleep patterns among university students. Medical students in this city are often subject to intensive academic pressure in settings that may lack adequate psychological support services, which could exacerbate nomophobia and its impact on sleep. Additionally, there is a lack of research investigating the prevalence of nomophobia and its association with sleep quality among medical students, specifically in Kermanshah. No published studies, to our knowledge, have explored this relationship in this population, highlighting a significant gap in the national literature. Considering the limited and sometimes contradictory findings regarding the relationship between nomophobia and sleep quality in the Iranian context, further investigation in this region is essential to provide more localized insights and inform appropriate interventions. Therefore, the main hypothesis of this study is that nomophobia is significantly associated with poor sleep quality among medical students in Kermanshah. Materials and methods

Study design

This descriptive-analytical cross-sectional study was conducted from August 25, 2023, to March 7, 2024. The reporting of this study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [22].

Sample and sampling method

In Iran, the Doctor of Medicine (MD) program consists of four phases: Basic Sciences, Physiopathology, Clerkship (Stager), and Internship (Intern), lasting two, one and a half, two, and one and a half years, respectively. During the Clerkship phase, students enter clinical settings and receive training in hospitals without on-call duties. In contrast, in the Internship phase, students are required to complete multiple on-call shifts. The target population of this study included all medical students enrolled in the MD program at Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences. The total number of medical students, categorized by academic phase, was as follows: 485 in Basic Sciences, 260 in Physiopathology, 290 in Clerkship, and 361 in Internship.

The sample size was determined based on a study by Shadzi et al. (2020), which reported a 40% prevalence of poor sleep quality among medical students [9]. This relatively high estimate was chosen to ensure sufficient statistical power and precision. Using this estimate, along with a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, the minimum required sample size for the descriptive objective (estimating prevalence) was calculated to be 369 participants. Since one of the analytical objectives involved multivariable logistic regression, we ensured that the sample also adhered to the widely accepted rule of having at least ten events per predictor variable. With six predictors included in the final adjusted model, a minimum of 60 events was required to obtain stable estimates. As the descriptively calculated sample size (n = 369) already exceeded this requirement, no additional increase was necessary for the regression analysis. However, the sample was expanded to 419 participants to enhance statistical power, allow for subgroup comparisons, and improve the generalizability of the results.

The inclusion criteria were: consent to participate in the study, being a current MD student, and owning a smartphone. Additionally, participants were required to have no self-reported history of chronic illnesses, major physical disorders, or severe psychological conditions that could significantly impact sleep quality. Incomplete completion of questionnaires was considered an exclusion criterion from the study. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method. While this approach facilitated data collection, it may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings.

Instruments

Data were collected using a demographic information form, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and the Nomophobia Questionnaire (NMPQ). The demographic information form included items on age, gender, living arrangements, family income, academic phase, grade point average (GPA) (reported on a scale of 0 to 20, as per the Iranian grading system), and five questions regarding smartphone usage patterns.

PSQI, developed and validated by Buysse et al. (1989) [23], was used to evaluate sleep quality over the past month. The Persian version of this instrument has demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties, with a reported Cronbach’s alpha of 0.77 in previous research [24]. In the present study, the internal consistency of the PSQI was recalculated using the current sample, and the Cronbach’s alpha was found to be 0.79, indicating good reliability.

The PSQI comprises 19 self-report items, which are grouped into seven components: subjective sleep quality (1 item), sleep latency (2 items), sleep duration (1 item), habitual sleep efficiency (3 items), sleep disturbances (9 items), use of sleeping medication (1 item), and daytime dysfunction (2 items). Although the questionnaire contains 19 questions, the scoring system condenses them into seven components, each scored from 0 to 3. The sum of these component scores yields a global PSQI score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality [23]. Scoring is based on a combination of Likert-type and open-ended items. Fourteen questions are rated on a 4-point Likert scale, typically ranging from 0 (no difficulty or never) to 3 (severe difficulty or three or more times per week). The remaining five questions are open-ended and require respondents to report quantitative details such as sleep duration, bedtime, and sleep latency. These responses are converted into standard scores according to the PSQI scoring protocol to compute their corresponding component values. A global PSQI score greater than 5 is indicative of poor sleep quality and has been shown to distinguish between good and poor sleepers with high sensitivity and specificity [23, 24].

NMPQ, developed by Yildirim and Correia (2015) [25], has been validated in various studies [14, 15]. The questionnaire was previously psychometrically tested in Iran in 2018, demonstrating acceptable reliability [26]. In the current study, we calculated the internal consistency of the NMPQ using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a value of 0.78, indicating good reliability. The NMPQ consists of four dimensions: not being able to communicate, losing connectedness, not being able to access information, and giving up convenience. This 20-item instrument uses a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The total score ranges from 20 to 140, with higher scores indicating greater nomophobia. A score of 20 indicates no nomophobia, 21–59 reflects mild nomophobia, 60–99 indicates moderate nomophobia, and a score of 100 or above denotes severe nomophobia [14].

Data collection

Upon obtaining ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences, the researcher visited the School of Medicine, various departments of affiliated hospitals, educational classes at these hospitals, and student dormitories to recruit participants. After explaining the study objectives and addressing any queries, the questionnaires were distributed to students willing to participate. Completed questionnaires were collected, and the data were entered into STATA for analysis.

To ensure data quality and minimize potential errors, the following measures were implemented: Participants received clear and comprehensive explanations about the questionnaires and their completion. All their questions were answered thoroughly. Anonymity and confidentiality of responses and participant information were emphasized. Questionnaires were administered at a convenient time and place for participants, allowing them sufficient time to complete the forms. The individual responsible for data collection received training on data collection procedures and data entry into STATA.

In the present study, there was no missing data. Additionally, the response rate was 100%, as all 419 medical students who were approached agreed to participate and completed the questionnaires. The research team regularly reviewed the collected data throughout the study to identify and address any inconsistencies or outliers.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using STATA version 14. Descriptive statistics included frequencies (percentages) and means (standard deviations). Chi-square tests were used to compare the frequencies of demographic variables and smartphone usage characteristics based on sleep quality (categorized as good or poor). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to examine differences in the mean global PSQI score and its component scores across different levels of nomophobia. Scheffé post-hoc tests were performed for pairwise comparisons when significant differences were observed. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess the correlation between sleep quality and nomophobia.

To further investigate the association between demographic variables, smartphone usage characteristics, and nomophobia (as independent variables) with sleep quality (dichotomous dependent variable), binary multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed. Variables with a p-value < 0.2 in bivariate (crude) analyses were selected for inclusion in the multivariable model, in accordance with best practices to avoid excluding potentially important predictors [27]. Crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs and AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). All VIF values were below the commonly accepted threshold of 10, indicating no significant multicollinearity. To evaluate model fit and predictive power, Nagelkerke R² was reported in the footnote of Table 4.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences (approval number: IR.KUMS.REC.1402.183). Before completing the questionnaires, the study objectives, procedures, potential risks, and benefits were clearly explained to all participants. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. They were informed that participation was voluntary and that they had the right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty.

Anonymity was ensured by avoiding the collection of any identifying information, such as names or student IDs. Confidentiality of responses was strictly maintained. Data were stored securely on password-protected computers accessible only to the research team, and hard copies of completed questionnaires were kept in locked cabinets in a secure location. All data will be retained for five years and then securely destroyed.

Results

The mean age of the participants was 23.6 ± 2.9 years, with 67.8% (n = 284) aged 24 years or younger. Females were slightly more than males (57.0%, n = 239). Regarding living arrangements, 46.1% (n = 193) of the students resided in dormitories. Most participants (86.2%, n = 361) reported that their family income was sufficient to cover living expenses. Among the participants, 34.8% (n = 146) were pursuing basic sciences degrees, and 74.9% (n = 314) had a GPA above 15. The majority of the participants reported checking their smartphones before sleep (92.4%, n = 387) and immediately after waking up in the morning (82.6%, n = 346). Regarding smartphone ownership duration, 79.0% (n = 331) of the students had owned a smartphone for 10 years or less. Furthermore, 58.2% (n = 244) and 72.1% (n = 302) of the participants reported using their smartphones and mobile internet for 5 h or less per day, respectively (Table 1).

The results indicated a statistically significant difference between the good and poor sleep quality groups in terms of checking smartphones immediately after waking up in the morning, the number of years of smartphone ownership, and the number of hours of mobile internet use per day (P < 0.001, P = 0.019, and P = 0.015, respectively). However, there was no statistically significant association between sleep quality and other variables (Table 1).

The mean global PSQI score among participants was 6.3 ± 3.0, which exceeds the cutoff of 5, indicating poor sleep quality. The mean scores for the seven components of the PSQI were as follows: subjective sleep quality: 1.3 ± 0.8, sleep latency: 1.2 ± 0.9, sleep duration: 1.2 ± 0.8, habitual sleep efficiency: 0.4 ± 0.7, sleep disturbances: 1.1 ± 0.5, use of sleeping medication: 0.2 ± 0.7, and daytime dysfunction: 1.0 ± 0.8. Each component is scored on a scale from 0 (no difficulty) to 3 (severe difficulty), with higher scores reflecting greater impairment.

A total of 27% (n = 113) and 6.9% (n = 29) of students described their subjective sleep quality as fairly poor and very poor, respectively. Sleep latency exceeded 30 min in 14.8% (n = 64) of students. Additionally, 28.6% (n = 120) reported sleeping less than six hours per night. During the past month, 15.5% (n = 65) of students had used sleep medication at least once. Based on the global PSQI score, 53.7% (n = 225) of students were identified as having poor sleep quality (PSQI score > 5).

The mean total nomophobia score was 83.2 ± 25.4. The mean scores for the four nomophobia subscales were as follows: not being able to communicate: 26.0 ± 9.3 (range 6–42), losing connectedness: 16.6 ± 7.7 (range 5–35), not being able to access information: 18.5 ± 5.7 (range 4–28), and giving up convenience: 22.1 ± 7.5 (range 5–35). Although mean scores are reported for each subscale, it is important to note that no validated cutoff points currently exist for defining severity levels within individual subscales. Instead, overall nomophobia severity is classified using the total NMPQ score. In this study, participants were categorized as having no nomophobia (0.5%, n = 2), mild nomophobia (19.6%, n = 82), moderate nomophobia (52.3%, n = 219), and severe nomophobia (27.7%, n = 116) according to the total score classification. Furthermore, the prevalence of poor sleep quality among students increased with nomophobia severity: 0.0% in those with no nomophobia, 39.0% in mild, 54.3% in moderate, and 63.8% in severe nomophobia groups.

The mean global PSQI score, as well as the component scores for subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, and daytime dysfunction, were significantly higher in students with moderate and severe nomophobia compared to those with mild nomophobia (P < 0.05). The mean sleep disturbances component score was significantly higher in students with severe nomophobia compared to those with mild nomophobia (P < 0.05). Additionally, the mean scores for the subjective sleep quality and daytime dysfunction components were significantly higher in students with severe nomophobia compared to those with moderate nomophobia (P < 0.05). However, there was no statistically significant association between the sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, and use of sleep medication components and the severity of nomophobia (Table 2).

There was a significant positive correlation between the global PSQI score and the total nomophobia score (r = 0.305, p < 0.001), indicating a moderate association, such that higher levels of nomophobia were related to poorer overall sleep quality. The global PSQI score was also significantly correlated with all four nomophobia subscales (all p < 0.001). Among the PSQI components, subjective sleep quality (r = 0.327, p < 0.001) and daytime dysfunction (r = 0.334, p < 0.001) showed moderate correlations with the total nomophobia score. Sleep latency (r = 0.153, p = 0.002) and sleep disturbances (r = 0.205, p < 0.001) demonstrated small but statistically significant correlations. The correlation with use of sleep medication was small and marginally significant (r = 0.094, p = 0.056). In contrast, sleep duration (r = 0.069, p = 0.157) and habitual sleep efficiency (r = 0.071, p = 0.148) were not significantly correlated with the total nomophobia score. Across the nomophobia subscales, similar patterns emerged. For instance, the “Not being able to access information” subscale had a moderate correlation with daytime dysfunction (r = 0.347, p < 0.001) and a small but significant correlation with sleep duration (r = 0.105, p = 0.032). The “Losing connectedness” subscale was significantly but weakly correlated with use of sleep medication (r = 0.129, p = 0.008). Other correlations between nomophobia subscales and PSQI components were generally in the small range (r = 0.10 to 0.29), though many were statistically significant (Table 3; Fig. 1).

To determine the association of demographic variables, smartphone usage characteristics, and nomophobia with sleep quality, a multivariable logistic regression model was used. In the crude analysis, checking smartphones immediately after waking up in the morning (P < 0.001), the number of years of smartphone use (P = 0.020), the number of hours of mobile internet use per day (P = 0.015), and nomophobia (P < 0.001) were significantly associated with sleep quality. The odds of having poor sleep quality were 2.83 (95% CI: 1.66, 4.82) times higher among students who checked their smartphones immediately after waking up in the morning compared to those who did not. Participants who had owned a smartphone for 11 years or more had 1.78 (95% CI: 1.10, 2.91) times higher odds of experiencing poor sleep quality compared to those who had owned a smartphone for 10 years or less. Students who used mobile internet for 6 h or more per day had 1.72 (95% CI: 1.11, 2.67) times higher odds of having poor sleep quality compared to those who used it for 5 h or less.

Variables with a P value less than 0.2 in the unadjusted (crude) model—including age, checking smartphones before sleep, checking smartphones immediately after waking up, number of years of smartphone use, daily duration of mobile internet use, and nomophobia—were entered into the adjusted regression model. After controlling for the effects of these variables, students who checked their smartphones immediately upon waking had 2.31 times higher odds of reporting poor sleep quality (95% CI: 1.30–4.07; P = 0.004), compared to those who did not engage in this behavior. Additionally, higher nomophobia scores were statistically associated with greater odds of poor sleep quality (OR = 1.02; 95% CI: 1.01–1.02; P = 0.034). However, the associations between the remaining variables and poor sleep quality were not statistically significant in the adjusted model (Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the prevalence of poor sleep quality, its associated factors, and the relationship between sleep quality and nomophobia among medical students. Our results revealed that 53.7% of the students suffered from poor sleep quality. This figure is comparable to the prevalence reported among medical students in France (49.7% in 2023) and medical sciences students in India (51.3% in 2019) [28, 29]. Similarly, a 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of seven studies reported a 55.6% prevalence of poor sleep quality among medical students [1]. However, lower prevalence rates were reported among medical sciences students in Nepal (38.2% in 2022) and among medical students in Iran (40% in 2020) [8, 9]. Conversely, some studies reported higher prevalence rates than those found in the present study, such as 80% in Rwanda (2023) and 63.1% in Egypt (2023) [5, 7]. Several factors may contribute to these differences in reported prevalence, including cultural and social contexts, physical and mental health status, degree of dependence on digital devices, and timing of data collection. For example, the season during which data were collected (summer vs. winter) or whether the study was conducted during or outside exam periods may influence sleep patterns and perceived sleep quality [2].

In addition to these methodological and environmental factors, deeper systemic and cultural influences should also be considered. Differences in university educational systems—such as variations in student responsibilities, levels of academic rigor, institutional expectations, and competitiveness—may also explain discrepancies in sleep quality across different settings [1, 5, 10]. Academic pressure, particularly in highly competitive or resource-limited educational environments, may exacerbate stress and disrupt sleep among students [1, 3]. Furthermore, cultural expectations regarding productivity and rest differ across societies. For instance, in some countries, late-night studying and minimal sleep are normalized or even valorized as signs of dedication, while in others, sleep hygiene and mental well-being are more strongly emphasized [30]. Previously mentioned socio-cultural factors may also significantly influence sleep quality. For instance, in low-income or post-conflict countries such as Rwanda, students may be more prone to sleep disturbances due to trans-generational trauma and psychological disorders stemming from past traumatic events [7]. Healthcare system differences may also play a role. Limited access to mental health services in certain countries could leave sleep disorders undiagnosed and untreated, contributing to their persistence and severity [3, 10]. In the context of Iran, several unique behavioral and cultural factors may influence sleep quality among students. For example, the consumption of tobacco and alcohol is generally discouraged both culturally and religiously, particularly within family structures. As a result, students may be less likely to engage in such behaviors, which are known risk factors for poor sleep [8, 11]. On the positive side, strong familial bonds and collectivist values in many parts of Iran—especially in the western regions—may serve as a protective factor. Students often rely on their families for emotional and practical support, which could buffer stress and improve sleep quality. Research has shown that higher levels of social support are associated with better sleep quality and longer sleep duration [31, 32]. However, cultural stigma around mental health remains a challenge in Iran. Sleep disorders and psychological conditions may go unacknowledged or untreated due to societal reluctance to seek psychiatric or psychological help, especially among young adults. This underreporting may lead to more severe or persistent sleep problems [10]. These interwoven cultural and behavioral factors may help explain both the observed patterns in our study and inconsistencies in the global literature.

Psychological mediators such as anxiety and stress may play a pivotal role in the association between nomophobia and poor sleep [33]. These factors may not only co-occur with nomophobia but also mediate its impact on sleep by intensifying hyperarousal and cognitive rumination. In particular, anticipatory anxiety about missing messages or being offline may disrupt sleep patterns and reduce overall sleep quality [15]. To address these challenges, practical interventions should be implemented at the university level. These include integrating sleep hygiene education into medical school curricula, focusing on the importance of sleep, and teaching relaxation and mindfulness techniques. Digital detox programs—such as smartphone-free hours or group-based disconnection activities—may help reduce smartphone dependency and improve sleep. Peer support groups can offer shared coping strategies and enhance social connectedness. In addition, awareness campaigns and counseling services can destigmatize help-seeking and ensure timely access to mental health support. By promoting well-being through structured interventions, medical schools can support students’ academic performance, mental health, and future professional competence.

Overall, the findings indicate a significant prevalence of poor sleep quality among medical students. Medical students experience high levels of stress due to their demanding academic workload, multiple responsibilities in clinical settings, exposure to illnesses, and interactions with critically ill patients. Consequently, obtaining sufficient sleep may not be prioritized in their daily schedules, leading to poor sleep quality in this student population [3, 7, 8]. Poor sleep quality is associated with physical and psychological disorders, impaired learning ability, and reduced productivity in daily activities and work [7]. Sleep deprivation can negatively impact cognitive function and, consequently, students’ performance in clinical settings and patient safety [2, 11]. Given these considerations, policymakers need to prioritize the improvement of sleep quality and psychological well-being among medical students. Implementing appropriate programs and interventions in this area is crucial.

This study found no significant association between age and sex with sleep quality. These findings align with several studies that reported no significant relationship between age [5, 6, 10, 12, 34, 35] and sex [9, 10, 34, 35] and sleep quality. However, a 2024 study in China found a significant association between female sex and sleep quality, with female students having 1.238 times higher odds of poor sleep quality than male students [11]. Similarly, a 2020 study in Jordan identified female sex as a predictor of poor sleep quality [36]. The higher prevalence of sleep disorders in women could be attributed to psychological, biological, and sociocultural factors, such as a higher likelihood of depression and hormonal fluctuations throughout their lifespan [36]. However, a 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis found no significant difference between male and female students regarding the prevalence of poor sleep quality [37]. Interestingly, another systematic review and meta-analysis in 2024 reported that female sex was associated with an increased risk of insomnia disorders, while male sex was associated with an increased risk of obstructive sleep apnea [4]. These conflicting findings highlight the need for further investigation into the relationship between sex and sleep quality. Future studies could employ objective measures like actigraphy and polysomnography to compare and analyze specific sleep disorder patterns in men and women.

This study found no statistically significant association between living arrangements and family socioeconomic status with sleep quality. These findings are consistent with a 2022 Iranian study that reported no significant association between living arrangements and sleep quality among dental students [38]. Another 2024 Iranian study found that medical students living in independent houses had significantly poorer sleep quality than those living with their families. However, logistic regression analysis did not reveal a significant association between living arrangements (living with family, independent house, dormitory) and sleep quality [10]. While some studies report no significant association between socioeconomic status and sleep quality [6, 11], others suggest a significant relationship between these variables [39].

Contrary to our findings, an Egyptian study found that students residing in hostels experienced poorer sleep quality than those living with their families [5]. Similarly, a 2020 Iranian study reported poorer sleep quality among students living in dormitories [9]. Factors such as family environment, student personality traits, interactions with roommates in dormitory settings, and dormitory living conditions might have influenced the lack of association between living arrangements and sleep quality in our study. Moreover, other relevant variables such as psychological disorders—including anxiety and depression [8, 40]—and lifestyle factors such as physical activity [41], which are known to affect sleep quality, were not considered in the present study.

Our study found no statistically significant differences in sleep quality across the different academic phases of medical education. This finding is consistent with previous research conducted among medical students in Iran (2024) and Egypt (2023) [5, 10]. Similarly, a systematic review and meta-analysis (2020) found no significant association between year of study and sleep quality among medical students [37]. However, our results contradict those of a study conducted in Iran (2020), which reported significantly poorer sleep quality among students in the clinical phases (Clerkship and Internship) compared to those in pre-clinical phases [9]. Likewise, a study in Rwanda (2023) found the poorest sleep quality among final-year students, followed by first-year students. The authors attributed this pattern to the cumulative effect of frequent night shifts and financial burdens among final-year students, and the challenges of adaptation, increased academic workload, and lifestyle changes among first-year students [7]. These contrasting findings underscore the complex interplay of factors influencing sleep quality across academic phases. While each phase presents distinct stressors, individual coping mechanisms and personality traits likely mediate the impact of these stressors on psychological well-being and sleep patterns. For instance, first-year students must adjust to a new academic environment and greater workload than in high school, which may lead to elevated stress levels and difficulty in maintaining healthy sleep schedules. Pre-clinical students face academic pressure from a high volume of coursework and examinations, often sacrificing sleep to meet academic demands. Clinical-phase students experience the additional stress of working in hospital settings, encountering patient suffering, and managing increased clinical responsibilities. Interns, in particular, are subjected to continuous clinical duties and frequent night shifts, all of which may adversely affect sleep quality. It is also important to note that several other variables—such as lifestyle factors (e.g., physical activity and exercise [41], alcohol consumption [8, 11], smoking [11], and psychological factors including stress [3, 42], anxiety [40], and depression [8]—have been shown to influence sleep quality. However, these data were not collected in the present study, and therefore, their potential confounding effects were not controlled for in the regression analysis. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the results.

Our study also revealed no significant difference in sleep quality based on GPA. This finding aligns with a study in Saudi Arabia (2021) that reported no association between GPA and sleep quality among medical students [35]. However, another Saudi Arabian study found that students with the lowest GPA had significantly poorer sleep quality compared to those with the highest academic performance [42]. This discrepancy might be attributed to variations in study habits and coping mechanisms among high-achieving students. While some prioritize sleep alongside their academic commitments, others might sacrifice sleep to maintain their high GPA, leading to inconsistent findings across studies. Additionally, the pressure of examinations often disrupts sleep patterns for many students, regardless of their academic standing, further contributing to the complex relationship between GPA and sleep quality. Therefore, future longitudinal studies are needed to comprehensively assess the long-term impact of academic performance on sleep quality.

Furthermore, our study found no significant difference in sleep quality based on checking smartphones before bed, contradicting previous studies reporting a significant association between pre-sleep smartphone use and poorer sleep quality [3, 5, 43, 44]. In those studies, the odds of having poor sleep quality were significantly higher among individuals who used smartphones before sleep compared to those who did not. This discrepancy might be attributed to the multifaceted nature of smartphone use and its impact on sleep. While the blue light emitted from smartphones can interfere with melatonin secretion and alter brain activity, potentially disrupting sleep [3, 45], the specific content accessed and duration of use might also play a significant role. For instance, a Danish study (2021) found no significant association between sleep duration and pre-sleep smartphone use; in fact, individuals who used smartphones before bed once a week had a 20% lower chance of short sleep duration compared to those who never did [46]. Similarly, a Finnish study (2023) reported no significant association between pre-sleep smartphone use and sleep quality, despite observing poorer sleep quality among participants who used smartphones in bed. However, they found a significant association between sleep latency, wake-after-sleep onset, and smartphone use in bed, highlighting the influence of specific usage patterns on sleep parameters. Moreover, the study suggested that the purpose of smartphone use before bed might influence its impact on sleep, with communication being associated with better sleep quality [45]. Our study did not account for factors such as duration, frequency, and purpose of pre-sleep smartphone use, which could have influenced our findings. Notably, we found a significant association between checking smartphones immediately after waking up and poorer sleep quality. Students who engaged in this behavior had a 2.31 times higher chance of experiencing poor sleep quality compared to those who did not. This finding aligns with a recent study from India (2023) reporting a significant association between these variables [47]. Although the relationship between checking smartphones immediately after waking up and sleep quality requires further investigation, it is plausible that this behavior reflects heightened anxiety levels related to academic or personal obligations and potentially indicates problematic smartphone use, both of which could contribute to poorer sleep quality. Future research should explore these potential mechanisms to better understand the observed association.

Our study revealed a significant difference in sleep quality across varying durations of smartphone use. Specifically, students who had used smartphones for 11 years or more were 1.78 times more likely to experience poor sleep quality compared to those with 10 years or less of usage. However, this association lost statistical significance after adjusting for covariates. This finding contrasts with a 2020 study from China, which reported a significant association between sleep quality and years of smartphone use even after controlling for confounding variables. In that study, the odds of poor sleep quality were 3.38 times higher among students who had used smartphones for more than four years compared to those with four years or less of use [12].

Additionally, our study found no significant association between the number of hours of daily smartphone use and sleep quality. This result diverges from previous studies [12, 39, 48, 49], which consistently reported a higher likelihood of poor sleep quality among students who used smartphones for longer periods each day. However, a 2023 German study supports our findings, reporting no significant association between sleep quality and screen time. Interestingly, that study did find that increased screen time was associated with reduced sleep duration among students using digital devices for work or study purposes, while no such association was found for leisure-related use. Moreover, greater leisure screen time during the day was significantly associated with later bedtimes, an effect not observed in the context of academic or work-related use [2].

Our findings also indicated a significant difference in sleep quality based on the duration of mobile internet use; however, this relationship also became statistically insignificant after adjusting for covariates. A 2020 Chinese study similarly reported a significant association between mobile internet use and sleep quality, although this was not confirmed in their univariate analysis, and mobile internet use was excluded from their multivariable regression model [12]. In contrast, other studies have demonstrated a significant association between prolonged internet use and poor sleep quality even after adjusting for confounding variables [40, 50]. These studies found that individuals with longer daily internet use were significantly more likely to report poor sleep quality. It is important to note that these associations, while statistically significant in some cases, do not imply causality. Due to the cross-sectional nature of our study, we cannot determine the direction of these relationships. For instance, although smartphone use before bedtime may contribute to poor sleep quality, it is equally plausible that individuals with existing sleep problems tend to use their smartphones more frequently at night. This potential bidirectional relationship must be acknowledged when interpreting the results.

Our findings, along with previous research, suggest that total hours of smartphone or internet use alone may not adequately predict sleep quality. Other factors such as the purpose and timing of use (e.g., nighttime use or social media engagement) were not assessed in this study and may mediate the relationship. Additionally, biological, psychological, and lifestyle variables not included in the analysis could have influenced the results.

A majority of students in our study exhibited moderate levels of nomophobia, with approximately 80% experiencing moderate to severe nomophobia. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting a high prevalence of moderate nomophobia among medical students [13, 20, 51, 52]. In line with some previous research [17, 51], severe nomophobia was the second most common level observed, which is cause for concern. While technological advancements have undoubtedly enhanced various aspects of life, particularly in education and communication, excessive dependence on smartphones may lead to maladaptive cognitive and emotional responses. One such consequence is nomophobia, which has been shown to negatively affect mental health and overall well-being [17, 51, 53].

In our study, an increase in nomophobia scores was significantly associated with a decline in sleep quality. The findings of the present study revealed a significant positive association between nomophobia and poor sleep quality among medical students. Specifically, the total nomophobia score showed a moderate correlation with the global PSQI score, indicating that higher levels of nomophobia are linked to reduced overall sleep quality. This association was particularly evident in key components such as subjective sleep quality and daytime dysfunction. This finding is consistent with existing literature that highlights a meaningful relationship between nomophobia, insomnia, and overall sleep disturbances [15–19]. For instance, Lin et al. (2021) identified nomophobia as a significant longitudinal predictor of insomnia [18]. Conversely, Farchakh et al. (2021) reported insomnia as a predictor of nomophobia, indicating a possible bidirectional relationship [15]. Supporting this perspective, Al-Mamun et al. also confirmed a bidirectional association between these two variables through correlational analysis [17]. These results suggest that the psychological distress associated with excessive mobile phone dependence—especially fear of disconnection or inability to access information—may negatively impact the sleep experience and daily functioning of students. Given the increasing prevalence of nomophobia among young adults and students in medical fields, these findings underscore the need to address smartphone-related behaviors as a potential risk factor for sleep disturbances in this population. Psychological factors such as anxiety and rumination—previously identified as predictors of nomophobia [15, 53]—may partly explain the link between higher nomophobia scores and poorer sleep quality observed in our study. Furthermore, a considerable portion of the students in our sample experienced severe levels of nomophobia. The psychological distress and emotional responses triggered by separation from one’s mobile phone, such as fear and anxiety, may further contribute to sleep disruption [15]. Moreover, nomophobia has been reported as a predictor of anxiety, depression, and stress in several studies [54, 55].These mental health conditions are well-documented risk factors for impaired sleep. Thus, it is plausible that the observed association between nomophobia and sleep quality may be mediated by psychological distress, a possibility that merits further investigation.

While our findings align with a number of previous investigations, some studies have failed to find a significant relationship between nomophobia and sleep-related issues [20, 21]. These discrepancies may be due to differences in study design or the presence of uncontrolled confounding variables. Given that sleep quality is influenced by various factors, including physical health, psychological well-being, and lifestyle habits, such inconsistencies are not unexpected.

Although the association between nomophobia and sleep quality in our study was statistically significant, the observed odds ratio (OR = 1.02) reflects a small effect size. This highlights the important distinction between statistical significance and clinical significance. While the effect size is modest, even small associations may carry practical implications at the population level, particularly among vulnerable groups such as medical students, where sleep quality is tightly linked to academic performance and mental health. As previously noted, sleep quality is shaped by multiple biological, psychological, and environmental factors. For example, lifestyle behaviors such as physical activity [41], smoking [11], and alcohol consumption [8, 11], as well as mental health conditions like stress, anxiety, and depression [3, 8, 40, 42], have all been shown to influence sleep. However, data on these potential confounders were not collected in the present study and therefore were not included in the regression model. The absence of these adjustments may have influenced the observed association. Future research should aim to incorporate and control for these factors to better understand the true magnitude and mechanisms of the relationship between nomophobia and sleep quality.

Strengths and limitations of this study

Standardized instruments were used to assess sleep quality and nomophobia, ensuring measurement reliability and validity. The sufficient sample size ensured adequate power for statistical analyses. The absence of attrition throughout the study contributed to the findings’ reliability. Adherence to the STROBE guidelines for comprehensive and transparent reporting enhanced the study’s rigor.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design prevents any inference of causality. Although we observed associations between nomophobia, patterns of smartphone use, and sleep quality, it remains unclear whether these variables influence each other directly or whether their relationship is bidirectional. For example, poor sleep quality may lead to increased smartphone use as a coping mechanism, just as excessive smartphone use may negatively affect sleep. These interpretations should be approached with caution, as they do not reflect causal pathways. Future longitudinal or experimental research is strongly recommended to clarify the directionality and underlying mechanisms of these associations.

Additionally, the use of convenience sampling may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings. Although we attempted to ensure diversity across academic phases—reflected in the proportional representation of students from basic sciences, pathophysiology, externship, and internship—representativeness across other demographic or psychosocial variables could not be guaranteed. We also aimed to include a balanced gender distribution; however, the final sample may not fully reflect the overall demographics of the medical student population at Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences. Future research may benefit from employing stratified random sampling based on academic stage, gender, and other relevant variables to enhance generalizability and reduce bias.

A further limitation of this study is the reliance on self-report questionnaires to assess both primary variables—sleep quality and nomophobia. This approach may introduce recall bias, where participants inaccurately recall past experiences, as well as social desirability bias, in which responses are influenced by the desire to present oneself favorably. Although participant confidentiality was ensured, these biases remain possible and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Moreover, the exclusive use of subjective sleep assessments, without objective tools such as actigraphy or digital screen-time logs, may have reduced the precision of the reported sleep and smartphone use patterns. Future research should incorporate objective monitoring methods to improve data accuracy and validate self-reported outcomes.

Additionally, the data collection period spanned from August 25, 2023, to March 7, 2024, overlapping with the final exam season for students. This timing may have led to an overestimation of sleep disturbances, particularly among students who completed the questionnaires during pre-exam or exam periods, when academic stress is typically elevated. Moreover, the inclusion of both late summer and winter months raises the possibility of seasonal influences on sleep patterns, which were not specifically accounted for in the analysis.

Sleep quality is influenced by numerous psychological, environmental, and biological factors, many of which were not assessed in this study. Specifically, variables such as stress, anxiety, depression, caffeine intake, and screen time before bed were omitted due to time and resource constraints, and to reduce participant fatigue. This omission may have affected the findings and should be addressed in future research for a more comprehensive understanding.

Although we included variables with P < 0.2 from bivariate analysis in the multivariable model, we acknowledge that important confounders—particularly mental health and behavioral variables—were omitted due to practical constraints. Future studies should include a broader range of such variables to improve confounder control and analytical robustness.

Conclusion

This study revealed a concerning prevalence of poor sleep quality (53.7%) among medical students. Significant associations were found between poor sleep quality, nomophobia, and the habit of checking one’s smartphone immediately upon waking. Notably, 80% of participating students exhibited moderate to severe nomophobia. Given the detrimental effects of both poor sleep quality and nomophobia on physical and psychological health, implementing preventative measures is crucial. These measures should focus on raising students’ awareness about the negative consequences of smartphone dependency, promoting sleep hygiene, and encouraging healthy lifestyle choices. Improving the overall learning environment and reducing academic pressures may also have a positive impact on students’ sleep quality. Practical recommendations include enhancing student-faculty relationships, improving dormitory conditions and campus facilities, and ensuring access to adequate clinical and counseling services that support student well-being. At the policy level, universities and health policymakers should consider implementing institutional guidelines that promote sleep health. For example, universities could incorporate sleep hygiene education into the academic curriculum, especially during the early years of medical training, to emphasize the relationship between quality sleep, academic performance, and long-term well-being. They might also adjust academic schedules to reduce late-night academic obligations, minimize early-morning exams or lectures, and allow for adequate rest periods. Furthermore, introducing mandatory orientation sessions for new students focusing on digital health, stress management, and time management skills could be beneficial.

Finally, future research employing objective measures and longitudinal designs is recommended to further investigate the impact of smartphone use patterns, including specific activities and the duration of use throughout the day and night, on sleep quality and various sleep parameters (e.g., sleep onset latency, latency to persistent sleep, sleep episodes). Given the cross-sectional nature of the current study, causal inferences cannot be made, and the directionality of associations remains unclear. Future studies should specifically aim to clarify whether smartphone use leads to poor sleep quality, whether poor sleep encourages increased smartphone use, or whether the relationship is bidirectional. Additionally, the impact of interventions aimed at reducing nomophobia on sleep quality should be explored through clinical trials.
