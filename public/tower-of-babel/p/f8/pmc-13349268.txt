Delayed Detection of Atrial Fibrillation After Cryptogenic Stroke and Risk of Recurrent Ischemic Stroke: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Sadiq A Shah, Taimoor Khan, Khadija Syed, Safi Ullah, Muhammad Bilal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Cryptogenic stroke (CS) accounts for a significant percentage of ischemic strokes. Undetected atrial fibrillation (AF) is a major underlying cause, and delayed identification increases recurrence risk. This study evaluated the timing of AF detection after CS and its impact on stroke recurrence and secondary prevention in a tertiary care setting.Methods:This retrospective observational analysis comprised 355 adult patients with CS hospitalized from January 2020 to June 2024. Demographic data, AF detection, timing, monitoring options, anticoagulation status, and stroke recurrence were all recorded. A multivariable Cox regression analysis identified predictors of recurrent stroke. SPSS version 23.0 was used for analysis.Results:Among 355 CS patients, 115 (32.4%) were diagnosed with AF during the follow-up period. The median time to AF detection was 5.5 months. Modalities for detection included Holter monitoring 55 (47.8%), electrocardiography 48 (41.7%), and implantable loop recorders 12 (10.4%). Among those with AF, 34 (29.6%) suffered a recurrent stroke; only 12 (35.3%) were on anticoagulation at the time of recurrence. Independent predictors of recurrence included age >65 years (hazard ratio [HR] 2.29; P = 0.012), delayed AF diagnosis >6 months (HR 2.51; P = 0.003), non-adherence to anticoagulation (HR 3.59, P = 0.002), and left atrial enlargement (HR 2.31, P = 0.017).Conclusions:Delayed AF diagnosis in CS is prevalent and has been linked to a significantly greater risk of recurrent stroke. Early and extended cardiac monitoring, combined with early anticoagulation, is critical for improving patient outcomes in CS.

Introduction

Cryptogenic stroke (CS), which accounts for 20%–30% of all ischemic strokes, poses a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge because of the lack of a recognized etiology.[1] Paroxysmal or subclinical atrial fibrillation (AF), a potential hidden etiology, has been identified as a significant cause of CS and often remains undiagnosed at the time of initial presentation.[2]

The most common cardiac arrhythmia is AF and is a major risk factor for ischemic stroke, which affects up to 1% to 2% of the general population.[3] Ischemic stroke, the most terrible consequence of AF, affects 10–20% of AF patients.[4]

It is essential to detect AF in CS patients early since it significantly changes secondary preventive tactics. In patients with AF-related stroke, early introduction of oral anticoagulation has been demonstrated to approximately two-thirds lower the incidence of further ischemic episodes.[5] However, conventional electrocardiography (ECG) or short-term Holter monitoring typically misses intermittent or silent episodes of AF, which delays diagnosis and prevents anticoagulation chances.[6]

The recent developments in extended cardiac monitoring, such as implantable loop recorders (ILRs), have shown increased efficiency in detecting subclinical AF, their application in low-resource settings is still restricted.[7] Furthermore, growing evidence suggests that delayed identification of AF may be associated with inferior clinical outcomes and an increased risk of stroke recurrence.[8]

Limited local data from Pakistan are available on the timing of AF detection following CS and its effects on anticoagulation status, functional outcomes, and recurrent stroke rates. By analyzing the frequency and timing of AF diagnosis in patients with CS, identifying risk factors for subsequent stroke, and assessing adherence to secondary prevention approaches, this study aims to address this gap.

This is one of the few studies conducted in Pakistan that systematically assesses the relationship between the timing of AF identification and recurrent stroke outcomes in CS. Although previous local studies have examined stroke risk factors,[910] few have directly addressed CS, time of AF diagnosis, or secondary prevention using anticoagulation. By documenting functional outcomes and employing multivariable analysis to identify factors of recurrence, this study addresses the gap and offers practical insights for enhancing post-stroke care in resource-limited settings.

Methods

Study design, setting, and ethics

This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at the Departments of Neurology and Cardiology at Lady Reading Hospital Peshawar, Pakistan, a tertiary care hospital. Consecutive patients were identified over 4.5 years (January 2020–June 2024).

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethical Board of Lady Reading Hospital Peshawar, Pakistan (Ref: 332/LRH/MTI). Informed consent was not required because the study involved retrospective analysis of de-identified clinical data. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational studies.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults (≥18 years) hospitalized with acute ischemic stroke and discharged with CS, which is defined according to the Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) classification as an ischemic stroke without an identified etiology after standard diagnostic evaluation, including neuroimaging, vascular imaging, transthoracic ECG, and routine cardiac rhythm assessment.[11]

At our hospital, the routine etiological workup of acute ischemic stroke includes brain imaging (computed tomography [CT] or magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]), vascular imaging of intra- and extracranial vessels (computed tomography angiography [CTA] or magnetic resonance angiography [MRA]), transthoracic ECG, baseline 12-lead ECG, and in-hospital rhythm monitoring when available. Stroke subtype classification is performed according to the TOAST criteria before discharge.

All patients met the TOAST criteria for CS after a comprehensive diagnostic workup, including vascular imaging and ECG. Patients with identified large-artery atherosclerosis, small-vessel disease, or established cardioembolic sources were excluded. Therefore, cases with dual-mechanism strokes were not included in this cohort at baseline.

The duration for AF detection was defined as an episode of irregular rhythm lasting more than 30 seconds, documented on ECG, Holter monitoring, or ILRs. However, detailed information regarding AF burden (including cumulative episode duration or stratification into <6 minutes vs ≥6 minutes) was not consistently available across monitoring modalities due to the retrospective study design. Therefore, duration-based stratified analyses could not be reliably performed.

Covariates

Baseline covariates included demographics (age and sex), vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and smoking), and ECG data. Left atrial enlargement was recorded based on transthoracic ECG, as documented in clinical reports.

Outcome

Follow-up

Patients were followed longitudinally through outpatient visits and hospital readmissions for a minimum of 18 months after the index stroke. Outcome events were identified through a systematic review of clinical documentation, electronic medical records, and imaging reports.

Patients with no available clinical information beyond discharge and who could not be reliably tracked through hospital records were considered lost to follow-up and were excluded if follow-up was less than 18 months.

Sample size

The study sample included all eligible patients during the study period. The number of outcome events was considered sufficient for multivariable survival analysis in accordance with established recommendations for events per predictor variable.[12]

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for analysis. Data were summarized using descriptive statistics. For this cohort of 355 patients with CS, continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical variables, whereas Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test was used for continuous variables based on data distribution.

Time-to-event analysis was performed using Cox proportional hazards regression for recurrent stroke in individuals with AF, with time zero defined as the date of AF diagnosis. Cox regression analysis was conducted among patients with AF to identify predictors of stroke recurrence. The multivariable model included variables associated with recurrence at P < 0.10 in univariate analysis. Results are expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).[1314]

The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using graphical and analytical methods. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided P value <0.05. Missing data were handled using a complete-case analysis approach.

Results

The study included a total of 355 patients diagnosed with CS [Figure 1]. During follow-up, AF was detected in 115 patients, and 34 patients experienced recurrent ischemic stroke. The flow diagram of patient selection and follow-up is summarized in Figure 1.

AF was found in 115 patients (32.4%) during follow-up, of whom 70 (60.9%) were male. The median time to AF detection was 5.5 months. AF was most frequently identified by Holter monitoring in 55 patients (47.8%), followed by standard ECG in 48 (41.7%) and ILRs in 12 (10.4%).

Discussion

Our results support the growing body of data worldwide that CS frequently has a cardioembolic cause, with paroxysmal AF playing a significant role. In the present study cohort, 32.4% of patients with CS developed AF during follow-up, consistent with findings from studies such as The Cryptogenic Stroke and Underlying AF (CRYSTAL AF) trial) and 30-Day Cardiac Event Monitor Belt for Recording Atrial Fibrillation After a Cerebral Ischemic Event (EMBRACE) trial.[25]

The median delay to AF detection in our sample was 5.5 months. Although short-term Holter monitoring detected a substantial number of AF cases, intermittent monitoring may still fail to capture paroxysmal AF early in some patients, especially when arrhythmic burden is low. Our findings therefore support structured and, where feasible, extended rhythm monitoring strategies rather than suggesting inadequacy of Holter monitoring itself. This result is consistent with earlier research that supported extended cardiac monitoring in patients with CS to detect subclinical AF.[25] However, ILRs are still not widely available in Pakistan and low- and middle-income countries due to financial and infrastructure limitations.

Post-stroke autonomic dysregulation, especially involving regions such as the insular cortex or brainstem, has been proposed as a potential mechanism for arrhythmogenesis, including AF.[15] However, detailed lesion mapping was not consistently available in our retrospective dataset. Therefore, we could not ascertain whether AF detected during follow-up in some patients was stroke-induced rather than due to primary atrial pathology. Future prospective studies combining advanced neuroimaging with continuous rhythm monitoring are needed to further explore this association.

Among patients with recurrent stroke (n = 34), only 12 (35.3%) were on anticoagulation at the time of recurrence, indicating a low rate of anticoagulation use. These findings highlighted the consequences of inadequate anticoagulation in patients with AF and reveal a substantial gap in secondary stroke prevention.[16] This reflects both delays in diagnosis and gaps in treatment. Adequate anticoagulation has been shown to reduce the risk of recurrent stroke in AF patients by up to 60% in studies from high-income nations.[6]

Our multivariable analysis further underscores the importance of clinical vigilance. Older age, left atrial hypertrophy, and non-adherence to anticoagulation were significant predictors of recurrence. The association between delayed AF diagnosis (>6 months) and recurrence does not suggest that low-burden AF is protective. Rather, delayed detection likely reflects prolonged exposure to untreated cardioembolic risk and delayed initiation of anticoagulation. In settings with limited structured post-stroke surveillance, patients may remain unprotected for extended periods, increasing recurrence risk.[17]

Echocardiographic parameters such as left atrial size may help identify patients at risk for delayed-onset AF who require closer monitoring.[56] Multivariable Cox regression was used to estimate HRs for individual predictors.[8] In many hospitals in Pakistan, formal post-stroke surveillance is lacking, and follow-up is often irregular.[910] This contributes to under-detection of AF and suboptimal secondary prevention.

The STROKE-AF randomized clinical trial demonstrated that long-term continuous cardiac monitoring significantly improves AF detection in patients with prior ischemic stroke due to large- or small-vessel disease.[17] These findings support the need for extended cardiac monitoring in CS populations and emphasize the importance of prolonged rhythm surveillance, especially in patients who were initially diagnosed with non-cardioembolic stroke.

The necessity for early intervention is further highlighted by the high proportion of patients with poor functional outcomes (mRS >3) at follow-up; 61.8% in the recurrent group). This illustrates the severe impact of recurrent strokes.[1819]

Although the association between AF and CS is well established, our study offers valuable real-world evidence from a resource-limited setting, demonstrating that delayed AF detection (>6 months) is an independent predictor of recurrent stroke. The observed median detection delay of 5.5 months and the high proportion of patients not receiving anticoagulation at recurrence (64.7%) underscore a clinically significant gap in secondary prevention. Unlike randomized controlled trials from high-income countries, our results reflect routine clinical practice and emphasize the need for systematic post-stroke rhythm surveillance protocols in low- and middle-income regions.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective, single-center study, which limits generalizability. Second, the limited use of ILRs may have underestimated the true prevalence of paroxysmal AF. Third, the AF-positive subgroup (n = 355) was relatively small, warranting larger prospective multicenter investigations. Data on AF burden, cumulative episode duration, and exact monitoring periods were not consistently recorded. Therefore, we could not stratify patients based on AF episode duration (e.g., <6 minutes vs ≥6 minutes) to evaluate its association with recurrence risk. Previous studies, such as the Asymptomatic Atrial Fibrillation and Stroke Evaluation in Pacemaker Patients and the Atrial Fibrillation Reduction Atrial Pacing Trial (ASSERT), have shown that greater AF burden is associated with increased thromboembolic risk. However, such data were not consistently available in our cohort.[20] Additionally, although all cases were classified as cryptogenic according to TOAST criteria, the presence of subclinical competing stroke mechanisms cannot be entirely excluded, given the retrospective design.

Finally, we were unable to evaluate each patient’s exact timing of anticoagulation initiation and adherence patterns in detail, which may have influenced the outcomes.

Conclusions

Delayed AF diagnosis is a common and preventable cause of recurrent CS. Proactive and extended cardiac monitoring is essential, particularly in older patients and those with left atrial enlargement. Early initiation of and adherence to anticoagulation significantly reduce the risk of recurrence and functional deterioration. Our findings underscore the need for structured post-stroke surveillance programs and timely anticoagulation, particularly in resource-limited settings such as Pakistan.
