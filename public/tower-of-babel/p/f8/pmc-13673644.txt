“Now they just say it will get better on its own.”: a qualitative exploration of discontinuation of facility-based tele-mental health services in Bangladesh
Authors: Ishrat Jabeen, Mohammad Sohel Shomik, Sahadat Hossain, Tasnu Ara, Ridwana Maher Manna, S M Hasibul Islam, Tahmina Akter, Nasimul Ghani Usmani, Pradip Chandra, Bibek Ahamed, Md Abdullah Al Mamun, Md Shariful Islam, Md Robed Amin, Mohammad Muntasir Maruf, Shams El Arifeen, Ahmed Ehsanur Rahman, Aniqa Tasnim Hossain, Janet E Perkins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiscontinuation of mental health care is a persistent global challenge, with up to one-third of patients dropping out before treatment completion. Evidence on discontinuation from facility-based tele-mental health services in low- and middle-income countries remains limited. This study explores why patients disengage from facility-based tele-mental health services delivered through Wellbeing Centres in Bangladesh.MethodA qualitative study was conducted between October 2024 and February 2025 using mobile phone interviews. Participants were service recipients who had discontinued follow-up services provided by the Wellbeing Centres. A total of 26 interviews were conducted, and the data were analyzed thematically.ResultsDiscontinuation was shaped by dynamic and sometimes contradictory patient experiences. First, perceptions of treatment outcomes influenced disengagement in complex ways: both perceived improvement and perceived lack of benefit led to discontinuation, reflecting shifting expectations of care. Second, everyday constraints—including fluctuations in mental wellbeing, competing life demands, distance from facilities, and communication gaps with providers—disrupted continuity of care. Third, family dynamics played a critical role, with limited support and motivation reducing sustained engagement with services.ConclusionDiscontinuation from facility-based tele-mental health services is not solely driven by access barriers but emerges from the interaction between perceived recovery, unmet expectations, and socio-cultural influences, particularly family involvement. Interventions should move beyond access-focused models to incorporate continuous patient engagement, clearer communication of treatment pathways, and active involvement of family members. These findings have implications for the design and scale-up of facility-based tele-mental health services in low- and middle- income countries (LMICs).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15109-6.Keywords: Facility-based tele-mental health services, Service recipients, Discontinuation, Qualitative exploration

Background

Discontinuation of mental health services refers to a situation in which a person who has initiated mental health treatment stops attending mental health consultations before a mental health practitioner deems treatment complete [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that 31% of mental health patients globally discontinue outpatient mental health care prematurely, with rates of 26.3%, 45.1% and 37.6% in high-income countries, upper-middle-income countries and low/lower-middle-income countries, respectively [1]. This has been identified as a major concern, as it may result in incomplete treatment and less efficient outcomes from the therapy [2]. Furthermore, patients may later resume treatment with a deteriorated mental health condition [3, 4]. Some studies have found that patients who discontinued mental health services have poorer social functioning capacity compared to those who attend follow-up care [4]. Identifying the reasons for discontinuation is important to minimize the negative effects of mental health service discontinuation and to provide successful mental health treatment.

A body of qualitative and quantitative scholarship has examined the causes of discontinuation of both in-person and technology-enabled mental health services. Several quantitative studies considered demographic characteristics such as age, sex, socioeconomic status, and ethnicity as associated with discontinuation [5–14]. Additionally, individual causes- such as the patients’ perceptions of mental health problems and their treatment, fear of admission, social stigma, and denial of the problem have been identified as reasons for withdrawing therapy in both qualitative and quantitative findings [2, 4, 5]. Patients’ unstable physical and mental conditions have also been found to be a common reason for missing appointments [4, 15]. Additionally, (dis)satisfaction with services has been identified as influencing decisions to discontinue care [4, 5, 8, 14, 16, 17]. Environmental factors, such as distance to facilities from home and weather, have also emerged as significant issues [2]. Furthermore, technology-related factors, including- poor connectivity and lack of communication devices are frequently cited as key barriers for disengagement from the technology-based services [18, 19]. However, much of the existing evidence originated from high-income countries (HICs), where mental health service delivery systems are relatively well-established. Furthermore, evidence of technology-enabled mental health services has often been generated during emergency situation, such as the COVID-19 pandemic. However, we found very limited evidence regarding how these factors influence continuity of care in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly within newly introduced facility-based tele-mental health services.

Limited evidence from Bangladesh is available on why individuals discontinue mental health treatment after initiating care. In Bangladesh, 19% of the adult population is estimated to have a mental health disorder [20, 21]. The unmet need for mental health services is estimated at 92.3% [22]. Limited availability and accessibility to mental health services, including the paucity of government facilities and an inadequate number of service providers, significantly impede mental health care-seeking in the country [23, 24]. In addition to addressing existing barriers to accessing mental health care, identifying and mitigating barriers to continuity of follow-up services is essential for reducing the treatment gap.

The Government of Bangladesh (GoB) is committed to expanding mental health services in the country and aims to establish a comprehensive, community-based, and rights-based mental health system in alignment with the World Health Organization (WHO) strategy [22, 25]. However, the expansion of these services remains challenging due to the limited availability of trained mental health professionals and their geographic concentration in urban centres that restricts access to care for rural population. Recognizing the challenges of accessing mental health services, the government introduced facility-based tele-mental health services through Wellbeing Centres embedded within district and sub-district hospitals, where patients attend designated centres and receive remote consultations with psychologists and psychiatrists via video conferencing [22]. Facilitation support for the project was provided by the International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr, b), the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH), and the Department of Clinical Psychology at the University of Dhaka. Together, they pooled a team of trained psychologists and psychiatrists to provide mental health support remotely.

Overall, seven Wellbeing Centres were established in hospitals across Bangladesh, including two district hospitals and four sub-district hospitals in Dinajpur, Netrokona, Sherpur, Noakhali, and Matlab. Each Wellbeing Centre consisted of a private room within a hospital, equipped to facilitate videoconferencing between patients physically attending the centers and remotely located psychologists and psychiatrists. Patients were referred by doctors, and registered by health workers via an online system. Appointments were scheduled with available psychologists, while health workers assisted patients in preparing for their video calls and maintaining privacy during sessions. During the sessions, psychologists used screening tools like Patient Health Questionnaire- 9 (PHQ-9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item (GAD-7) to assess depression and anxiety. Based on the patient’s condition, they provided counseling or referred more serious cases to psychiatrists at the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) and Sylhet MAG Osmani Medical College Hospital (SOMCH). These referred sessions were also delivered remotely with support from the health workers.

To strengthen service delivery of Wellbeing Centres, healthcare administrators with mental health expertise, alongside healthcare facility managers, collaborated with icddr, b to conduct training and workshops for district- and sub-district-level healthcare providers, including Resident Medical Officers (RMOs), physicians, gynaecology consultants, and health workers. Training covered patient screening, appointment scheduling, virtual consultations procedures, technology use, emergency management, and patient follow-up.

Together, these Wellbeing Centres were designed to improve access to mental health services. A previous paper demonstrated that Wellbeing Centres were both practical to run and highly valued by users because of their privacy, free services, and accessibility [24].

Despite the high satisfaction and acceptability reported by users of the Wellbeing Centres, previous research suggested challenges in maintaining continuity of care, with few patients seeking follow-up visits when advised to do so by the mental health professionals [24]. Furthermore, internal program monitoring data indicated that 4895 patients were advised to return for follow-up between January 2023 and March 2025; however, only 19.4% returned for the recommended follow-up care. These findings suggest that improving access to mental health care through facility-based tele-mental health services does not necessarily guarantee continued engagement with treatment. Although the services are designed to reduce geographical and access barriers through virtual consultation delivered within health facilities, little is known about why patients discontinue follow-up care within this specific facility-based tele-mental health delivery model in low- and middle-income settings. Understanding the factors that contribute to discontinuation after initial service use is crucial for strengthening continuity of care and maximizing the benefits of mental health service expansion in Bangladesh and other LMICs. Accordingly, this qualitative study aimed to explore the context and reasons for discontinuation of follow-up mental health care among service recipients of the Wellbeing Centres.

Method

Study design and settings

This was an interview-based qualitative study conducted between October 2024 and February 2025 to explore the reason for discontinuation of follow-up care among service recipients of facility-based tele-mental health services delivered through Wellbeing Centres in Bangladesh. The study reporting adheres to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [26] and the criteria recommended by Sheard for reporting on qualitative studies in implementation science [27]. (related file 1).

Data were collected remotely through telephone interviews, with a small number of face-to-face interviews to complement and validate findings.

Sampling procedure

We used purposive sampling to select study participants from among those who received an initial mental health consultation at the Wellbeing Centres, but did not seek follow-up mental health services after being advised to do so by the mental health professionals.

Between January 2023 and March 2025, 7379 patients attended the Wellbeing Centres for tele-mental health consultations (Fig. 1). Among them, service providers recommended 4895 patients return for follow-up services. However, only 19.4% returned for follow-up care, leaving 3945 individuals who discontinued recommended follow-up services.

Number of discontinued patients from wellbeing centers

Afterwards, we purposively selected participants based on severity GAD-7 scores (range between 10 to > 15, indicating moderate to severe anxiety) and PHQ-9 scores (range from 10 to > 27, suggesting moderate to severe depression). From this sampling frame, we then contacted potential participants via telephone. Potential participants were contacted sequentially using the contact information available in program records until information sufficiency was achieved. In total, 26 interviews were conducted for this study.

Data collection

Three trained qualitative researchers conducted the interviews, including twenty-three telephone and three face-to-face interviews. Because participants were geographically dispersed across multiple districts, telephone interviews were used as the primary mode of data collection. This approach enabled timely engagement with participants while reducing travel burden and logistical constraints [28, 29]. In two interviews, service recipients requested that a guardian or attendant contribute to the discussion alongside them. The service recipients remained the primary participants in these interviews, while the accompanying guardians provided additional contextual information regarding the circumstances surrounding discontinuation of follow-up care. Information from these interviews was retained because the discussion focused on the service recipient’s experiences and decision-making progress. Later, three face-to-face interviews were subsequently conducted to compare findings across interview modes and to enhance the credibility of the findings.

A semi-structured interview guideline was developed specifically for this study (supplementary file 1). Both face-to-face and mobile phone interviews were conducted using this guideline. The guide explored participants’ experiences with Wellbeing Centre services, reasons for discontinuing follow-up care, perceived outcomes after the initial consultation, and experiences following discontinuation.

Interviews were conducted in Bangla, lasted approximately 25 min, and were undertaken in private settings whenever possible. No prior relationship was established between the researchers and participants before data collection. Researchers had no role in participants’ clinical care or service delivery at the Wellbeing Centres.

To minimize participant discomfort and protect confidentiality, interviews were not audio-recorded. Instead, trained qualitative researchers documented detailed contemporaneous notes during interviews. While participants were speaking, interviewers documented responses whenever feasible, including notable participant statements and explanations to minimize reliance on recall. Immediately following each interview, the notes were reviewed, expanded, and entered electronically while the discussion remained fresh in memory. These expanded interview notes served as the primary analytic dataset. This approach is consistent with previous qualitative research demonstrating that detailed interview notes and scripts prepared by experienced interviewers can provide rich and analytically useful data when audio recording is not feasible [30]. First author (IJ) held regular debriefing discussions with the researchers to review interview documentation, discuss emerging findings, and promote consistency in data capture.

Data collection and analysis occurred concurrently. Ongoing review of interview notes, emerging codes and preliminary themes informed decisions regarding recruitment. Recruitment continued until information sufficiency was reached, defined as the point at which no substantially new concepts, explanations, or perspectives emerged from successive expanded interview notes. To explore potential differences between interview modes, data from telephone and face-to-face interviews were compared during coding and theme development by examining whether codes or themes were uniquely associated with either mode. No systematic differences in patterns of meaning were identified between the two modes; therefore, findings were analyzed collectively.

No intervention or comparison group was applied, as this was an exploratory qualitative study.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was initiated to identify inductive themes from the data. The thematic analysis was led by the first author (IJ) and the senior author (JEP), both of whom have social science background and extensive experience in qualitative research. Preliminary data were analyzed iteratively during data generation. The analysis began with repeated reading of the expanded interview notes to achieve familiarization with the data. After this initial familiarization with the data, relevant segments of text relating to participants’ experiences of discontinuing follow-up mental health services were extracted into Microsoft Excel spreadsheets. Initial inductive codes were then assigned to the extracted data and compared across interviews to identify similarities and differences in participants’ experiences. Related codes were grouped into broader categories, which were subsequently reviewed, refined, and organized into overarching themes. The coding and theme development process was led by the first author (IJ) with ongoing input from the senior author (JEP). Emerging interpretations, categories, and themes were regularly discussed with the wider research team to enhance analytic rigor and ensure that the findings accurately reflected participants’ accounts. Any differences in interpretation were discussed among the research team until consensus was reached.

Ethical approval and informed consent

The Ethical Review Committee of the International Center for Diarrhoeal Diseases Research, Bangladesh (icddr, b) approved the study protocol (PR-22103), following scientific review by the Research Review Committee. The study involves no more than minimal risk of participants. We limited our discussion with participants to issues related to the study objectives and participants’ experiences with follow-up mental health services. We obtained verbal consent via telephone after briefing participants on the study objectives, potential risks and benefits, and right to withdraw or skip questions without penalty. Moreover, we kept participants’ personal information anonymous and confidential, and all data were anonymized prior to analysis and future use. All eligible individuals who were successfully contacted agreed to participate in the study; no refusals were recorded.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

A total of 26 participants were interviewed for this study (Table 1). Among them, 16 patients received only psychological counseling, whereas 10 patients also received both psychiatric and psychological counseling. Patients had their first appointment with a psychologist for their primary assessment in the facility-based tele-mental health services. Psychologists then decided whether psychological counselling was sufficient for patients or if they also required services from a psychiatrist who could prescribe medication, referring patients to the psychiatric services when needed. The average age of participants was 28 years, with all patients aged 19–58. Among 26 participants, 19 were married and 7 were unmarried. Of the 26 participants, 11 had received services through district hospitals and 15 through Upazila Health Complexes.

Findings

Three interrelated themes were generated through thematic analysis to explain discontinuation of mental health service: Perceived service outcomes, everyday constraints and lack of social support for continued mental health care. However, we observed that participants’ experiences often overlapped across themes. For analytic purposes, experiences were grouped according to the primary reason participants described as influencing their decisions to discontinue follow-up care.

Perceived service outcomes shape decisions to continue or discontinue follow-up care

The decision by Wellbeing Centre service recipients and their caregivers not to seek follow-up care was heavily influenced by their pre-existing expectations of tele-mental health care and their perceptions of the service outcomes in relation to those expectations. These perceptions were primarily based on whether they considered this first consultation effective or ineffective. Both effectiveness and ineffectiveness were provided as reasons for discontinuing services.

Some service recipients perceived that their mental health condition had improved after their initial consultation and following the mental health service providers’ advice. As a result, they saw no further need for follow-up. One service recipient stated:

I would not talk, or smile, I would just be there [emotionless] before treatment. They (the service provider) prescribed me medicine from there (the Wellbeing Centre), and suggested I go around and talk. My brothers took me to different places for one month, and I stayed in my parents’ house. I was cured. Therefore, I did not visit the hospital [Female, Age-24y].

For this research participant and others, the single consultation at the Wellbeing Centre was considered sufficient for addressing the mental health problems that had brought them there in the first place. This positive outcome meant they did not feel the need to seek a follow-up consultation, despite the mental health service provider’s recommendations.

However, some of the patients who initially believed that they had recovered from their mental health problems after the first consultation found that their suffering later returned. According to one female service recipient, she initially followed the service provider’s advice and reported some improvement, including better sleep and reduced headaches. As a result, she did not attend follow-up visits as advised. After some time, her previous symptoms reappeared. Further discussion revealed that her mental health issues primarily stem from ongoing family conflicts with her husband, which remain unresolved. Moreover, she has not been able to internalize strategies to cope with the situation or improve her mental well-being.

Another group of service recipient who expressed positive experiences with facility-based tele-mental health services decided not to return for follow-up visits because they felt they could not implement the mental health service providers’ advice. One service recipient mentioned that,

I cannot follow the suggestions provided by the psychologist, as I do not get help from my family to change my lifestyle. Therefore, I stopped visiting the hospital for counseling because I know that the problem cannot be solved unless I can maintain it. [Female, Age- 26y].

These service recipients understood that the potential benefit of mental health services was contingent on their ability to follow the mental health service provider’s suggestions. However, as they did not feel they could do this, they saw no reason to return for a follow-up visit only to obtain further advice that they would be unable to implement.

Findings also revealed a discrepancy between a patient’s a priori expectations of mental health services and the actual services delivered. This also resulted in deciding not to seek further services through the Wellbeing Centres. For example, service recipients who had previously sought mental health care had anticipated receiving different treatment from the Wellbeing Centre than what they had received in the past. One young female participant expressed that she discontinued the services as the mental health service providers did not prescribe a new treatment for her anxiety, but rather suggested that she continues taking the previous medication prescribed by another psychiatrist from a different hospital. In her experience, this medication had not improved her mental health issues, as she still engages in suicidal ideation. According to her,

I didn’t really benefit from the video counselling. They just listened to what I had to say about my problems. Then they asked which medications I had been given by the previous doctor. After hearing that, they only told me not to keep anything near me that could be dangerous or used for suicide—like rope, scissors, or knives. A little while after the conversation, they sent me a message on WhatsApp telling me to continue the medication prescribed by the previous doctor. [Female, Age- 24y].

Although clients’ initial perceptions were shaped during their first counseling session, whether they saw a need to return for follow-up sessions depended on whether the counseling led to observable improvements in their daily lives. Such improvements are closely influenced by their individual circumstances, which play a key role in their decisions to continue or discontinue follow-up visits for mental health services. Interestingly, the decision not to seek follow-up services was justified by both the perception that the session had resulted in the intended effect —i.e., an improvement in mental wellbeing and the perception that it had not i.e., no improvement in mental wellbeing. In both cases, respondents felt that these outcomes rendered a follow-up visit unnecessary. These perceptions shaped participants’ decision regarding continuation of follow-up care.

Everyday life constraints limit continuation of care

In contrast to participants who saw no need for follow-up services, others felt they needed further mental health support, but found it difficult to attend another session. They described several complexities of daily life that hindered their pursuit of follow-up services. These included the distance of the Wellbeing Centre from their residence, compromised communication with health services providers to understand how to seek services, limiting mental and physical health conditions, and prioritization of other responsibilities.

Some of the initial service recipients described not seeking follow-up treatment despite a psychologist’s recommendation due to the extended distance of the health facility from their residence. They visited the hospital while suffering from severe mental health problems, and were satisfied with the counseling. However, they discontinued the services later due to the distance to the health facility. One participant explained that she lives in a different district from the hospital, and was therefore not interested in traveling to the hospital only for follow-up mental health services. According to the her:

I took mental health services while staying at my relatives’ home in Dinajpur. Now, I am with my parents in Gaibandha. I understand the importance of follow-up care, but I find it difficult to travel to Dinajpur only for this purpose. I am considering seeking further mental health support from Bogura Medical College Hospital, and I would attend follow-up sessions if Wellbeing Corner services were available in Bogura. [Female, Age- 19y].

A few patients reported that they were socially dependent on family members or attendants to accompany them if they were to seek follow-up services. In rural Bangladesh, adolescents, particularly, girls, are generally not allowed to travel alone due to prevailing social norms and security concerns. They often have to rely on others to accompany them when leaving home. Therefore, some missed follow-up visits in the Wellbeing Centre, as their companions were occupied with their daily activities. According to one attendant of the service recipients:

My sister is still suffering from mental illness. She still has headaches and has bad dreams during her menstrual period. But I haven’t been able to take her to ‘X’ hospital for the last two months because it is too far from our house and takes at least one and a half hours. Also, I became a mother very recently, so it’s difficult for me to go out anywhere now. [Attendant of a 19-year-old female service recipient].

Thus, the service recipient’s possibilities to seek follow-up services were shaped not only by their daily responsibilities and context, but also by those on whom they depended to accompany them to health facilities.

Moreover, some participants mentioned that they did not seek follow-up support from the Wellbeing Centres due to a physical and/or mental condition that impeded their ability to continue treatment. In some cases, however, they sought further mental health services from alternative institutions better accommodated to their specific needs. For instance, one participant reported that she was suffering from other physical complications for which she had been admitted to another hospital. In addition to the treatment for this physical condition, she also received mental health support from the service providers of that hospital. Another participant stated that her headaches became so severe that her parents and relatives decided to take her to a private clinic for mental health services, and she is still continuing his treatment there.

Furthermore, personal responsibilities-such as school examinations, household work, the sudden death of relatives, and family members’ marriages-were also mentioned as reasons for not seeking follow-up consultations at the Wellbeing Centres. We found that service recipients often became occupied with unavoidable life events and routine responsibilities, which led them to deprioritize mental health concerns. For example, one middle-aged participant (male, 51 years old) reported that he was so busy with his daughter’s marriage preparations that he was unable to visit the Wellbeing Center. However, as his headaches persisted, he attempted to adopt an alternative strategy to manage and improve his mental health. He began using Ayurvedic medicine to address his symptoms, although he found it to be of limited effectiveness. He also expressed confusion about whether he should continue taking the medication prescribed by the Wellbeing Center alongside the Ayurvedic treatment. Another service recipient believed that the root of her mental health problems stemmed from ongoing issues in her conjugal life. Instead of seeking follow-up psychological counseling, she chose to focus on resolving those relationship issues first.

As the Wellbeing Center was a new concept among the patients, two service recipients seemed confused about the re-appointment procedure when they decided to go for follow-up visit. One of the participants reported that she visited the Wellbeing Center for treatment of headache and heart palpitations, but later experiencing severe side effects that led her to seek emergency hospital care. During this process, she was unable to identify a designated focal person at the Wellbeing Center for follow-up support or mental health emergencies. Additionally, the loss of her appointment slip further discouraged her from returning for follow-up care. She also expressed uncertainty about when and how she could access services, as mental health care was only available on specific days and hours. Her account illustrates that the practicalities of communicating with the Wellbeing Center stood in the way of repeat use of services. Research participants were not sure when they could return for follow-up care. Since mental health services were only provided during specific hours and days, participants could not find services to be available outside of this time. She stated that,

I have also lost my slip given from Wellbeing Centre. Can I visit there without the slip?… Also, I went to hospital five days after her psychological counseling because I had a side effect of medicines provided by doctor (psychiatrist), such as severe dizziness and being unable to stand. But I did not find anyone in Wellbeing Centre. Therefore, I went to the emergency doctor for treatment. [Female, Age-25y].

Her account illustrates that the practicalities of communicating with the Wellbeing Centre stood in the way of repeat use of services. Research participants were not sure when they could return for follow-up care. Since mental health services were only provided during specific hours and days, participants could not find services to be available outside of this time.

As participants faced various constraints in their daily lives and were unable to attend the Wellbeing Center regularly, they attempted to adopt alternative strategies to manage and improve their mental health.

Although participants had a positive view of mental health services, many could not continue follow-up visits due to everyday constraints. Instead, some chose other ways to cope, such as Ayurvedic treatment or getting help from different service providers instead of going back to the Wellbeing Centre.

Lack of social support undermines continued follow-up care

Limited social support emerged as another barrier to follow-up visits at Wellbeing Centres. Household members’ negative perceptions and attitudes toward mental health services often discouraged participants from returning for recommended follow-up visits. In many ways, the extent of support participants received was shaped by prevailing gender norms and household financial pressures, both of which significantly constrained individuals’ possibilities for attending follow-up visits.

In rural Bangladesh, where society is largely patriarchal, women often move into their husbands’ households after marriage. They are expected to follow social norms, which include relying on their husbands and in-laws for decision-making and taking on extensive household duties [31, 32]. These responsibilities frequently lead to neglect of their own physical and mental health. Women research participants shared that family members overlooked their mental health concerns. Family members discouraged them from seeking services as they believed that the treatment was unnecessary. One female participant mentioned that,

If my mother-in-law had been taken to the hospital, and the doctors [psychologists] had explained how necessary counseling is for my mental condition, then they would have taken my illness seriously and encouraged me to go to the hospital. Now they just say it will get better on its own. [Female, Age-26y].

She further shared that her husband’s attitude toward her became visibly negative after she sought care at the Wellbeing Centre. As she stated,

Since coming back from the hospital, my husband always says that, “You have become mad. The doctor has already declared. You can’t do anything.” This kind of attitude demotivated me to visit the hospital for mental help… I cannot follow the suggestions provided by the psychologist, as I do not get the help from my family to change my lifestyle. [Female, Age-26y].

Among our research participants, autonomy to seek follow-up services through the Wellbeing Centres was fundamentally shaped by the social context in which the service recipient was embedded. As this research participant’s account illustrates, without the support of her family, and her mother-in-law and husband specifically, she was unable to seek the follow-up mental health care she desired. Another participant shared that her husband is currently unemployed, and the family relies on her father-in-law for financial support. This situation creates a sense of inferiority and pressure within the household. During rice harvesting season, she must assist with drying rice, and any absence results in criticism from her mother-in-law. As a result, she is unwilling to travel three hours for counseling, fearing household disapproval.

Women in Bangladesh often remain heavily dependent on their husbands and in-laws for decision-making, including matters related to their own health. This dependency frequently suppresses their personal health priorities, resulting in the interruption or discontinuation of mental health care.

Discussion

The findings of the study revealed the reasons why first-time Wellbeing Centre service recipients did not return for follow-up facility-based tele-mental health services. The exploration identified three main reasons for not seeking follow-up services: misalignment between the service provided and service recipients’ perceptions, real-life constraints on care, and a lack of support from the household and family to seek services. The exploration provides an in-depth understanding of the reasons for discontinuation from mental health services and offers a useful direction for developing a modified, improved intervention plan to strengthen mental health services in low- and middle-income country contexts like Bangladesh.

Our findings reveal a fundamental mismatch between how mental health service implementers conceptualize care as a structured process requiring regular follow-up and how many service recipients perceive it as a one-time solution aimed at immediate relief. This divergence often renders the concept of “discontinuation” irrelevant or inapplicable from the patients’ perspective, shifting the focus from dropout to perceived closure. Both favorable and unfavorable experiences with services contributed to termination decisions, a pattern supported by literature across diverse settings [5, 33–39]. For instance, a study conducted in the UK by Tony Hostick found that patients often discontinued services after perceiving an improvement in their condition, concluding that further care was unnecessary [38]. Similarly, research from Australia showed that approximately 30% of patients exited treatment early, not due to dissatisfaction, but because they felt the intervention had already been effective, expressing satisfaction and a sense of completion [39]. In contrast, a study in the United States found that adolescents often discontinued follow-up care because they perceived the services as unhelpful [5]. These findings align with our own, indicating that both favorable and unfavorable outcomes contribute to service withdrawal. While prior assessments of the Wellbeing Centres reported a 99% acceptability rate [24], our qualitative data highlight that perceived outcomes are deeply intertwined with service users’ individual practices and expectations. A recurring theme was discordance between providers’ recommendations and patients’ or families’ beliefs, which frequently led to service withdrawal. Supporting literature emphasizes that satisfaction and alignment of treatment expectations significantly influence continuity of care [35, 40, 41]. A recent qualitative study further recommends formal interventions that address the underlying reasons for dropout, suggesting that tailored approaches may enhance retention and long-term engagement [41].

Our findings show that although facility-based tele-mental health services have been introduced in district-level health facilities to improve access for individuals facing mental health challenges, these services may still fall short of true accessibility due to the complexities of people’s everyday lives. Although the Wellbeing Centres use telecommunication technologies, they represent a facility-based telepsychiatry model rather than direct-to-home tele-mental health care. Patients were required to physically attend designated hospital-based Wellbeing Centres to receive remote consultations with psychologists and psychiatrists via videoconferencing. Consequently, challenges related to travel distance, dependence on attendants, and scheduling constraints should be interpreted as barriers to accessing this facility-based service model, despite the remote delivery of specialist consultations. Supporting this, a study of outpatient drug treatment clients in Baltimore City found that traveling more than one mile to a treatment Centre was associated with nearly a 50% decrease in treatment completion rates [42]. Similarly, a cross-sectional telephonic survey of 158 outpatient dropouts from the Karwan-e-Hayat Institute for Mental Health Care in Karachi, Pakistan, reported that 8.9% of patients discontinued treatment specifically due to distance-related barriers, highlighting the significant role geographic accessibility plays in treatment continuation [43]. Another study in Finland’s Kainuu region by Tina showed that extended travel times reduced access to both in-person and home-based mental health services by as much as 83%, underscoring distance as a critical obstacle, particularly in remote areas [44].

While the cited studies employed quantitative methods and did not explore in depth how distance specifically led to treatment discontinuation, our qualitative findings provide insight into this. Participants emphasized that everyday realities, such as age, physical limitations, availability/unavailability of attendants, and daily work responsibilities, significantly influenced their ability to access services. This reveals a more nuanced picture of the barriers to continued care, extending beyond mere travel distance to include the complex interaction of personal and contextual factors that contribute to discontinuation. Evidence showed that using a home computer for therapy is useful for the patients, as they can fit therapy into their daily schedule at their convenience [45]. Furthermore, mental health services delivered via smartphones provide additional advantages, including greater accessibility, convenience and improved communication [46]. Similarly, the modification of the facility-based tele-mental health services at the Wellbeing Centres tele-mental health services could allow patients to access them from their homes. However, further investigation is required into the accessibility of the internet or devices at patients’ residences and patients’ ability to operate the services.

We also found that the family relationships, household decision-making, and social support play a crucial role in shaping continuity of mental health care. In many cases, service recipients’ decisions regarding follow-up care were not made independently but were influenced by family members, attendants, and broader social circumstances. In collectivist societies like Bangladesh, where people’s lives are deeply embedded in family and community networks, interventions such as facility-based tele-mental health services may be less effective if local cultural contexts, family dynamics and social support systems are not adequately considered [47]. Global evidence supports the need for culturally sensitive mental health care. For example, a study in Turkey by Yildiz emphasized how cultural values shape the understanding and treatment of conditions like ADHD and depression, underscoring the need for context-specific interventions [48]. Ahmedani further noted that despite the availability of effective treatments, access to mental health care remains limited due to different forms of stigma, including societal stigma, especially in low-resource settings [49]. Similarly, Jacob and Christopher both argued that Western models of mental illness may not always fit non-Western contexts [50, 51], and applying them without adaptation can sometimes do more harm than good [51]. A study conducted across 23 countries found that an internet-based program was effective in preventing postpartum depression, but it failed to prove to be a universally strong prevention tool in this initial evaluation [52]. Mabunda’s scoping review in Africa demonstrated that lay health workers could deliver culturally adapted therapies with promising results [53].

Taken together, these findings collectively point to the need for blending clinical interventions with cultural adaptation to ensure long-term success and better access to mental health care in settings like developing countries [53]. Similarly, our findings suggest that continuity of care within the Wellbeing Centres was strongly influenced by family relationships and social support. This is particularly true for women in rural areas, who often depend on their husbands or in-laws to make health-related decisions, making it harder for them to attend follow-up visits. In this context, future adaptation of the Wellbeing Centres model may benefit from greater engagement of family members and caregivers to better align service delivery with social realities. Several studies revealed that the tendency to continue therapy is higher among patients who have good social support compared to those who have poor social support [9, 54]. To address these issues, intervention strategies should emphasize including families in discussions about the importance of regular follow-ups for the patient’s complete recovery [55].

Limitation of the study

The interpretation of these results must be considered in light of several study limitations. First, to facilitate a rapid assessment of reasons contributing to patient discontinuation of follow-up care across geographically dispersed participants, most interviews were conducted by telephone interviews. Although this approach enabled timely data collection, the absence of face-to-face interaction may have limited rapport-building and opportunities for probing, potentially resulting in less detailed responses than might have been obtained through in-person interviews. Second, we recruited the participants based on the availability of valid contact information and participants’ willingness to participate in an interview. Consequently, individuals with different experiences of service discontinuation may not have been represented. In addition, participants were not equally distributed across all selected sub-districts, and female participants were more highly represented than male participants. Third, the interviews were not audio-recorded because of concerns regarding participant comfort and confidentiality. As a result, some nuances of participants’ language, tone, and expression may not have been fully captured, which may have affected data completeness and the precision of quoted statements. To mitigate this limitation, trained qualitative researchers documented detailed contemporaneous notes during interviews, expanded and reviewed these notes immediately following each interview, and engaged in regular debriefing discussions to promote consistency in data capture and interpretation. Data collection and analysis occurred concurrently, allowing emerging findings to be reviewed and refined throughout the study. Nevertheless, the absence of audio recordings may have limited the depth of analysis compared with studies based on verbatim transcripts.

Despite these limitations, the study provides important insights into factors influencing discontinuation of follow-up mental health services and identifies issues with potential relevance for programmatic improvement and future research.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that discontinuation from facility-based tele-mental health services is shaped by the interaction of perceived recovery, unmet expectations, everyday constraints, and family influence, rather than by access barriers alone. These findings highlight the importance of context-responsive mental health services that support continuous patient engagement, clearer communication regarding treatment pathways, and greater involvement of family members in follow-up care. Strengthening continuity of care through flexible and patient-centered follow-up mechanisms may improve sustained engagement with facility-based tele-mental health services in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Further research is needed to identify effective strategies for reducing treatment discontinuation and improving long-term engagement in mental health care.
