Integration of mobile CT and basal anesthesia for pain-free preoperative localization of small pulmonary nodules: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mengxu Yao, Yungang Sun, Qiang Zhang, Feng Shao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the application of mobile CT combined with basal anesthesia in preoperative painless localization of small pulmonary nodules and evaluate its safety and effectiveness.MethodsThe clinical data of patients who underwent precise localization of pulmonary nodules via mobile CT combined with basal anesthesia in the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Affiliated Nanjing Brain Hospital, Nanjing Medical University October 2024–February 2025 were retrospectively analyzed. The basic information and perioperative outcomes of the entire group were analyzed.ResultsA total of 100 patients, 33 males and 67 females aged 19–86 years, were included. The preoperative localization success rate for 100 patients with small pulmonary nodules was 100.0%, with an average localization time of 14.51 ± 4.01 min.The localization-related complications included 1 case of intrapulmonary hemorrhage, 1 case of pneumothorax, and 2 cases of localization needle displacement, with no complications such as hemoptysis or pleural reaction. The time interval from completion of localization to the start of surgery was 28.83 ± 3.55 min. All patients underwent successful single-port thoracoscopic resection of pulmonary nodules without conversion to open thoracotomy. The average operative time was 62.72 ± 41.50 min. The surgery-related complications included one case of intraoperative bleeding, which was promptly controlled via endoscopic hemostasis. The average postoperative chest tube retention time was 3.60 ± 1.30 days, and the average hospital stay was 6.12 ± 1.15 days. Postoperative pathological results confirmed that all the nodules had a margin distance greater than 2 cm from the resection edge. The 100 patients were divided into two groups: one group underwent mobile CT-guided localization under lidocaine local anesthesia, while the other group received mobile CT-guided localization under basal anesthesia. Analysis of postlocalization pain scores revealed that basal anesthesia achieved superior analgesic efficacy (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe combination of mobile CT with basal anesthesia enables painless and precise preoperative localization of pulmonary nodules, effectively reducing the incidence of localization-related complications, shortening surgical waiting times, and ensuring the safety and efficacy of preoperative localization in patients with pulmonary nodules. This approach further enhances patient comfort during the procedure and demonstrates certain clinical application value.Keywords: Pulmonary nodule, Localization, Mobile CT, Basal anesthesia, Retrospective cohort study

With the widespread use of low-dose spiral CT and increased awareness of health check-ups, the detection rate of small pulmonary nodules has been increasing. In particular, with the detection of some high-risk pulmonary nodules, the diagnosis and treatment of small pulmonary nodules have become a focus for clinicians. Thoracoscopic surgery has become an important method for the diagnosis and treatment of small pulmonary nodules. However, owing to the small size of the nodules, which cannot be palpated during surgery, the importance of preoperative auxiliary localization techniques has increased in prominence [1, 2].

Currently, the most commonly used localization method in clinical practice involves first performing a CT scan of the pulmonary nodule in the radiology department to determine its position, followed by percutaneous localization under local anesthesia. After localization, the patient is transferred back to the ward to wait for surgery [2]. However, this method has certain limitations: it requires coordination among multiple departments, which is clinically challenging and increases the workload; it involves multiple transfers of the patient, increasing the risk of localization complications (such as pneumothorax, hemorrhage, and pleural reaction); the waiting time for surgery is long, further increasing the risk of needle dislodgement, migration, or dye diffusion, leading to inaccurate identification of the nodule during surgery; additionally, localization under local anesthesia can cause fear, tension, and other negative emotions in patients, with some patients experiencing severe pain, which can lead to incomplete or inaccurate localization [3, 4]. Therefore, we innovatively used mobile CT combined with basal anesthesia for precise preoperative localization of small pulmonary nodules, overcoming the shortcomings of the above localization methods and providing strong support for safe, painless, and precise resection of small pulmonary nodules. This method was successfully applied in 100 patients who underwent single-port thoracoscopic surgery for small pulmonary nodules in our hospital and achieved good clinical outcomes, as summarized below.

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The clinical data of patients who underwent localization of pulmonary nodules via Body Tom® mobile CT combined with basal anesthesia at the Department of Thoracic Surgery of our hospital from October 2024–February 2025 were retrospectively analyzed. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) follow-up time ≥ 3 months, with no change or increase in nodule size, and clinical assessment suggesting a high possibility of malignancy; and (2) pulmonary nodule size ≤ 30 mm located in the periphery of the lung. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with poor cardiopulmonary function who could not tolerate surgery; (2) patients whose nodules were located too deep, close to the hilum; (3) patients who were allergic to iodine; and (4) patients who were allergic to anesthetic drugs or had contraindications to anesthetic drugs (e.g., glaucoma) [4].

Methods

Body Tom® mobile CT localization room

The SAMSUNG Body Tom® 32-slice mobile whole-body CT has an 85 cm large bore and a 60 cm field of view (FOV). The built-in battery power and 130° wide-angle camera allow the Body Tom® to be conveniently moved from one operating room to another. Body Tom® has performance comparable to that of fixed CTs and can perform axial, spiral, and dynamic scans. It can perform 2D, 3D, and multiplanar reconstruction image processing, providing high-quality CT imaging for pulmonary nodules ≥ 4–5 mm and soft tissues such as the lung parenchyma. The open platform is compatible with all DICOM formats and can easily import 3D reconstruction systems to assist in preoperative 3D modeling of pulmonary nodules and guide intraoperative navigation. The Body Tom® complies with the American College of Radiology (ACR) guidelines. The entire machine is built with 0.75 mm lead equivalent shielding, which significantly reduces the amount of scattered radiation by five times compared with traditional fixed CTs. For patients of different ages and weights, the Body Tom® can set specific scanning protocols, reducing the scanning dose through dose control software, thereby significantly lowering the risk of radiation exposure for patients.

Basal anesthesia

Basal anesthesia in this study refers to the administration of pharmacological agents to induce a foundational sedative state in patients, achieving conscious sedation while preserving responsiveness and cooperative capacity during localization.The basal anesthesia methods used in this study can be divided into two main types: (1) combined use of midazolam + oxycodone and (2) oxycodone alone. When midazolam is used for the preoperative localization of pulmonary nodules, the dose should be individualized and gradually titrated. Midazolam must be administered slowly, with an administration time of more than 2 min, and wait for 2 min or longer to fully evaluate its sedative effect. The recommended dose is 1 mg/mL for slow injection, which can be diluted with 0.9% sodium chloride and 5% glucose solutions. The application dose of oxycodone is 2 mg (concentration of 1 mg/mL), and the dose adjustment needs to be individualized to ensure adequate sedation and reduce adverse reactions [5]. Basal anesthesia requires the patient to be responsive, awake, and able to change positions and cooperate with the localization operation.

Patients were non-randomly assigned to basal or local anesthesia groups based on preoperative anesthesiologist assessment of anxiety levels and comorbidities. Group allocation aimed to minimize procedural risks while ensuring equipoise.

After entering the mobile CT localization preparation room, patients received intravenous access and mask oxygen. Continuous monitoring of electrocardiogram, blood pressure, and pulse oximetry (SpO₂) was maintained. The anesthesiologist administered basic anesthesia based on the patient’s baseline condition, with the following details:

Sedation depth assessment: The Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) was used to evaluate sedation levels. RASS scores range from − 5 (unarousable) to + 4 (combative), with a target sedation level of −1 to 0 (calm, awake, and cooperative).

Dose Adjustment: Midazolam and oxycodone doses were dynamically adjusted based on RASS scores. If RASS > 0 (agitated), an additional 0.5 mg midazolam was administered; if RASS < −2 (oversedation), drug administration was paused, and respiratory function was monitored.

Tolerance Criteria: Patients were deemed to tolerate the procedure well if:

RASS scores remained within − 1 to 0;

No physical resistance or verbal protests occurred;

Patients could follow commands for positional adjustments (e.g., deep breathing, lateral positioning).

Mobile CT localization operation process

After basal anesthesia is complete, a professional radiologist performs mobile CT: (1) Based on the preoperative CT image of the location, size of the nodule, and its relationship with adjacent tissues, the appropriate position is selected, and the approximate scanning range is determined; (2) After the body surface surface marker is placed, a mobile CT image is used to perform a plain scan to determine the best needle entry point, angle, and depth; (3) After routine disinfection and draping, 2% lidocaine is used for local infiltration anesthesia, and a hookwire puncture needle is inserted; under ECG monitoring, the puncture localization operation is performed, and lidocaine (5 mL: 0.1 g) is used for local infiltration anesthesia, especially given that the local anesthesia operation should allow lidocaine to act on the pleura as much as possible so that the patient can achieve satisfactory analgesic effects; and (4) the mobile CT scan can be used again to determine the relationship between the puncture needle and the nodule. If the position is appropriate, 0.1 mL of indocyanine green (2.5 mg/mL) is injected, the hook wire is released, the sleeve needle is removed, sterile gauze is used to cover and fix the soft wire on the body surface. (5) After localization is successfully completed, the patient is immediately transferred to the operating room for thoracoscopic wedge resection or segmentectomy (Fig. 1).

Single-Port thoracoscopic surgery under general intravenous anesthesia

After mobile CT combined with basal anesthesia localization is completed, the patient is pushed into the operating room on a flat car, and general intravenous anesthesia induction with double-lumen tracheal intubation is continued. All surgeries were performed by the same medical team and were completed via single-port thoracoscopy. A single incision of approximately 3–5 cm is made in the 4th or 5th intercostal space of the anterior axillary line as the observation and operation port. A wound protector is routinely used to avoid damage to the intercostal muscles and nerves. Under the guidance of the localization needle or dye, the pulmonary lesion is resected under single-port thoracoscopy, and intraoperative frozen section examination is routinely performed. Typically, if the frozen pathological diagnosis is invasive primary lung cancer, lobectomy + systematic lymph node dissection is performed; for patients with peripheral lung cancer with a maximum diameter ≤ 2 cm or sufficient margin (margin > 2 cm or > the maximum diameter of the tumor), sublobar resection + systematic lymph node dissection or sampling is performed. After surgery, a 24-gauge chest tube is routinely placed, and a chest X-ray is performed postoperatively to determine whether the lung is fully re-expanded. When the drainage volume is ≤ 200 mL/day and there is no air leakage, the chest tube is removed [6]. All postoperative pathological results were analyzed according to the 9th edition of the TNM staging system.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 22.0 software was used for statistical analysis. The normality of the measurement data was tested. If it conformed to a normal distribution, it was described by the mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s). If it did not conform to a normal distribution, it was described by the median. Categorical variables are described as frequencies and percentages (%). Intergroup comparisons were performed via the independent samples t test for normally distributed data with equal variances or the Mann‒Whitney U test for nonnormally distributed or heteroscedastic data. A p value < 0.05 was defined as the threshold for statistical significance.Tolerance Metrics: RASS scores are reported as median (interquartile range, IQR), with intergroup comparisons using the Mann-Whitney U test.Adverse Events: Categorical variables are described as frequencies (percentages), with group differences analyzed via Fisher’s exact test.Non-parametric tests were selected due to the ordinal nature of pain scores and non-normal distribution, ensuring robust analysis of clinical significance.

Results

Basic clinical data

This study enrolled 100 patients with pulmonary nodules requiring preoperative localization, including 33 males and 67 females aged 19–86 years. All patients successfully completed preoperative localization, achieving a 100% success rate. Among the 100 pulmonary small nodules, the mean size was 12.96 ± 6.02 mm, and the mean distance from the parietal pleura was 18.33 ± 11.44 mm. The preoperative CT imaging characteristics of the nodules included 49 pure ground-glass nodules (GGNs), 31 mixed GGNs, and 20 solid nodules. Localization data

Preoperative localization of small pulmonary nodules was considered successful if the following two conditions were met: (1) the Body Tom® mobile CT-guided localization was accurate, and the distance between the localization needle or dye area and the nodule position was ≤ 10 mm; (2) the lesion was projected and resected under thoracoscopy under the guidance of the localization mark, without localization needle shedding, migration, or obvious indocyanine green overflow/disappearance [7].

All 100 patients successfully underwent precise localization and resection of pulmonary small nodules without conversion to thoracotomy or perioperative mortality. Under basal anesthesia, all patients cooperated fully with positional adjustments and localization procedures, with no interruptions due to pain, anxiety, or fear. Postlocalization complications included intrapulmonary hemorrhage in 1 patient and small pneumothorax (approximately 20% lung collapse) in 1 patient; both resolved spontaneously after surgical resection. No complications, such as hemoptysis or pleural reactions, occurred. Displacement of the localization needle was observed in 2 patients during intraoperative exploration, but the nodules were successfully identified and resected on the basis of the position of the staining agent.

Sedation depth and tolerance

The median RASS score across all patients was 0 (IQR: −1 to 0), with 94% achieving the target sedation level (−1 to 0). No significant difference in RASS scores was observed between the basic and local anesthesia groups (*p*= 0.15).

Adverse events

Hypotension occurred in 2.0% (1/50) of the basic anesthesia group versus 4.0% (2/50) in the local anesthesia group, with no statistical significance (*p*= 0.56). No respiratory depression or severe agitation events occurred in either group.

Postoperative pathological results

Postoperative pathological examination revealed no cases of positive margins, with a margin distance exceeding 2 cm in all 100 patients. The mean margin width was 3.23 ± 0.83 cm, and no patients required extended lung resection or conversion to thoracotomy due to insufficient margins.

The final pathological diagnoses included the following:

••Adenocarcinoma in situ: 27 cases.

••Minimally invasive adenocarcinoma: 22 cases,

••Invasive adenocarcinoma: 34 cases,

••Benign lesions: 17 patients.

Discussion

The primary goal of preoperative auxiliary localization is to precisely guide the resection of pulmonary small nodules while maximizing the preservation of lung function [6, 8]. Currently, various preoperative localization techniques are clinically available, including:

① CT-guided percutaneous localization (e.g., placement of Hookwire, coils, or methylene blue dye),

② Bronchoscopy-assisted localization (e.g., electromagnetic navigation or virtual bronchoscopic navigation),

③ CT-based 3D virtual localization [7].

Each technique has advantages and limitations.

In this study, we innovatively employed mobile CT combined with basal anesthesia for precise preoperative localization of pulmonary small nodules. This approach establishes a one-stop streamlined workflow integrating “basal anesthesia– precise localization– minimally invasive surgery,” enhancing procedural efficiency and patient comfort (Table 5).

With the development and updating of minimally invasive techniques and related guidelines, for smaller pulmonary nodules, sublobar resection (wedge resection or segmentectomy) is often performed, especially for lesions located in the outer one-third of the lung tissue, which are considered “advantageous locations [9]. The basic principle of minimally invasive surgery is to ensure complete resection of the pulmonary nodule while preserving as much normal functional lung tissue as possible, thereby improving the patient’s postoperative lung function and quality of life. Owing to the small size and low density of pulmonary nodules, it is difficult for surgeons to palpate the nodules during single-port minimally invasive surgery, which may lead to insufficient resection margins, tumor residue, or excessive resection of normal lung tissue and prolonged operation time. Therefore, to quickly and accurately locate and resect pulmonary nodules during minimally invasive single-port thoracoscopic surgery, preoperative localization is needed. Currently, the most commonly used localization method is CT-guided percutaneous lung localization under local anesthesia [10]. The routine use of lidocaine hydrochloride for local infiltration anesthesia can cause strong breath-holding, stress cough, nausea, vomiting, and even pleural reactions during the puncture operation [11]. In particular, the series of stress stimuli generated during the operation can cause severe fluctuations in the patient’s hemodynamic indicators, such as increased heart rate and blood pressure, making it difficult for the patient to cooperate during the examination and even causing certain harmful stimuli. Some patients may also have adverse memories, and during the localization process under local anesthesia, patients often experience high levels of tension, panic, anxiety, and other negative emotions, and the pain is still quite strong. Therefore, patients often complain that the degree of pain at the location point is greater than that associated with the minimally invasive incision used during surgery, which we call “memory pain“ [10]. Preoperative localization can cause fear, affecting subsequent surgery and rapid postoperative recovery. Therefore, adequate and accurate anesthesia during preoperative localization is an important part of successful localization and surgery. To effectively eliminate this local pain and fear, we have innovatively improved the preoperative localization anesthesia scheme, choosing a suitable basal anesthesia scheme for individual patients, which can effectively maintain good sedation and analgesia during the localization process; improve patient cooperation and satisfaction; and eliminate patient pain, discomfort, and tension and anxiety [11]. All patients in this study did not experience any adverse consequences related to basal anesthesia, and none of them were unable to complete localization or were unable to be accurately located due to intolerance to pain, tension, fear, or other negative emotions. None of the 100 patients experienced adverse reactions such as hypertension, hypotension, tachycardia, bradycardia, or respiratory depression. After localization, patients were immediately transferred to the operating room for double-lumen endotracheal intubation and general intravenous anesthesia. The average time from the end of localization to the beginning of surgery in this study was 14.51 ± 4.01 min, accelerating the progress of surgery and significantly improving surgical efficiency.

Traditional localization methods require coordination among multiple departments. First, patients need to undergo preoperative lung nodule puncture localization in the radiology CT room. Then, they are transferred to the general ward to wait for surgery. The localization process is complex, and the waiting time for surgery is long. The process of transferring patients from the radiology CT room after localization not only reduces surgical efficiency but also poses significant safety risks for patients with pneumothorax, intrathoracic bleeding, and pleuritis after localization [12, 13]. According to the relevant literature, the incidence of preoperative localization pneumothorax is 7.5%~49.1%, the incidence of pulmonary hemorrhage is 10.6%~29.7%, and the detachment rate of hookwire localization needles is 0.8%~6.9% [14].In comparison, the incidence rates of related complications in this study were significantly lower than the average levels (the incidence rate of pneumothorax was 1%, displacement of the localization needle was 2%, and pulmonary hemorrhage was 1%). The Body Tom® mobile CT in our hospital is located next to the anesthesia preparation room and adjacent to the operating room, with minimal transfer distance, reducing transfer-related risks. It prevents patients from traveling back and forth between the radiology CT room and the operating room. After localization is complete, patients can be sent directly to the operating room, effectively shortening the ineffective waiting time from localization completion to surgery start, reducing the probability of complications such as hookwire detachment and displacement, indocyanine green leakage and diffusion, and significantly reducing serious complications such as pleuritis, allergic shock, etc. This approach also alleviates the medical burden on nursing staff and reduces negative emotions such as tension, anxiety, and pain experienced by patients during the waiting process. It improves the comfort and safety of patient care [15, 16]. In this study, displacement of the localization needle was observed in 2 patients during intraoperative exploration, but the nodules were successfully identified and resected on the basis of the position of the staining agent.One patient in this study experienced pulmonary hemorrhage, which may be related to the proximity of the nodule to the pulmonary vessels. However, the patient underwent surgery immediately after localization, and the symptoms were relieved immediately after surgery, without any adverse consequences. In all patients, precise resection of small lung nodules was successful. Postoperative pathology revealed that the margins were sufficient, and there were no patients with insufficient margins, leading to conversion to thoracotomy or expanded pulmonary resection. There were no perioperative deaths.

Sedation Management and Patient Tolerance: By quantifying sedation depth via RASS scores, this study confirmed that basal anesthesia effectively maintained calm, cooperative states (median RASS = 0) without oversedation-related complications. Compared to local anesthesia, the basal anesthesia group showed significantly lower pain scores (*p*< 0.001) and no increased risk of hypotension or respiratory depression, highlighting its safety.

This study has certain limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study based on data from a single medical group, which may introduce some errors. Second, it requires multidisciplinary coordination, which is challenging. However, our hospital is a thoracic specialty hospital, with close collaboration between departments. We have also established a dedicated mobile CT positioning room and coordinated with the radiology and anesthesia departments. Furthermore, positioning under basal anesthesia increases the workload of anesthesiologists and clinicians, but it can improve patient comfort and safety. Additionally, mobile CT is expensive, and not all medical institutions have the resources to implement it, which limits its widespread adoption.While mobile CT requires significant upfront investment, its integration into a streamlined workflow reduces patient transfers, staff workload, and postoperative complications, potentially offsetting costs over time. This study lacks concurrent comparative studies with common positioning techniques, such as CT-guided positioning and magnetic navigation positioning under general anesthesia. This will be further investigated and supplemented in subsequent studies. The sample size of this study is relatively small, but it can preliminarily demonstrate the advantages of this technique. We hope that future multicenter, large-sample, and prospective studies will further validate the safety and effectiveness of this technique.
