Acridine-derived small molecule associates with VEGF and is linked to reduced CAM vascularization: a combined in silico and CAM study
Authors: Sayantani Karmakar, Shuvojit Moulik, Semanti Ghosh, Gunjan Uttam, Ashwag Alsharidah, Ranjay Kumar Choudhary, Moattar Raza Rizvi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcridine derivatives exhibit diverse biological activities, yet their potential to modulate angiogenesis remains underexplored. This study evaluated 9-phenyl acridine (ACPH) as a small-molecule ligand capable of engaging vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and altering conformational features relevant to receptor recognition, with the working hypothesis that ACPH may modulate VEGF structural dynamics in regions involved in VEGF-VEGFR interaction rather than directly inhibiting receptor binding.MethodsStructure-based binding was assessed using AutoDock, PatchDock, and GOLD, with VEGF as the molecular target. Complex stability and conformational effects were examined by 50-ns molecular dynamics simulations, including RMSD, residue-level flexibility, compactness, solvent exposure, and interaction energetics. Functional activity was evaluated using the chick embryo chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) assay at 0.04 nM, 0.4 nM, and 0.8 nM, with ImageJ-based quantification of vascular features over 24–48 h.ResultsDocking consistently positioned ACPH within a VEGF pocket, with predicted contacts involving Phe36, Ile46, Phe47, and Lys48 (chain A) and Cys60, Cys61, Asn62, Asp63, Glu64, and Glu67 (chain B). Across simulations, ACPH association was associated with reduced conformational variability of VEGF, particularly in regions relevant to protein-protein interaction, supported by an estimated ligand-protein interaction energy of approximately − 26.77 kcal/mol derived from trajectory-based and interpreted qualitatively rather than as a rigorous thermodynamic binding free energy. In ovo, ACPH exposure was associated with a dose-dependent reduction in CAM vascularization, with observable effects at 0.4 nM and more pronounced effects at 0.8 nM; however, this represents a phenotypic observation and does not establish VEGF-specific inhibition or a defined anti-angiogenic mechanism. Prolonged exposure at 0.8 nM was also associated with reduced embryo viability, indicating that non-specific toxicity may contribute at the highest dose.ConclusionsDocking and MD simulations consistently positioned ACPH within a transient VEGF cavity and were associated with reduced conformational drift and residue-level fluctuations. In the CAM model, ACPH exposure was associated with dose-dependent vascular reduction, with reduced embryo viability at 0.8 nM suggesting possible non-specific toxicity; these findings warrant receptor-facing validation and cytotoxicity-controlled endothelial assays.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40360-026-01148-6.Keywords: 9-phenyl acridine, VEGF, Angiogenesis, Molecular docking, Molecular dynamics, CAM assay, Conformational dynamics

Introduction

Pathological angiogenesis underpins a wide range of high-burden diseases by supplying abnormal tissues with oxygen and nutrients, increasing vascular leakiness, and shaping microenvironments that enable persistence and progression [1]. In cancer, the shift from a contained lesion to invasive, metastatic disease is often accompanied by an angiogenic switch driven by hypoxia-responsive programs and sustained activation of VEGF-VEGFR signalling [2]. In the retina, excessive VEGF activity promotes fragile neovessels that bleed and leak, contributing to vision-threatening conditions such as neovascular age-related macular degeneration and diabetic macular oedema [3]. These clinical realities have made angiogenic signalling a major therapeutic target, supported by a broad portfolio of anti-angiogenic agents and combination strategies, including integration with immunotherapy in oncology [4, 5].

Among pro-angiogenic pathways, VEGF signalling remains the most clinically validated axis for controlling pathological neovascularisation. VEGF ligands regulate endothelial proliferation, migration, survival, and permeability through receptor engagement; VEGF-A signals primarily via VEGFR2, while other family members and co-receptors shape angiogenic and lymphangiogenic outputs [6]. In practice, VEGF pathway inhibition has delivered meaningful benefits in neovascular retinal disease and has become a core component of treatment for multiple solid tumours [7]. However, important constraints remain. In ophthalmology, durable benefit frequently requires repeated intravitreal injections and close monitoring, creating sustained burden for patients and health systems [8]. In both ocular and oncology settings, responses may be partial or wane over time, and resistance can emerge through redundant pro-angiogenic signalling and microenvironmental adaptation [3]. Safety is also a continuing consideration; ocular complications are recognised, and systemic adverse-event signals have been reported in susceptible groups, particularly individuals with diabetes or cardiovascular risk [9, 10]. Together, these issues continue to motivate approaches that preserve efficacy while improving durability and reducing treatment burden [11].

Small molecules offer a complementary route because they can be optimized for oral delivery, scalable synthesis, and flexible use in combination regimens [4]. Most clinically advanced small-molecule anti-angiogenic agents act downstream by inhibiting receptor tyrosine kinases such as VEGFR, PDGFR, and FGFR, often with multi-target profiles that broaden pathway coverage and partly buffer compensatory signalling [2, 12]. At the same time, multi-kinase inhibition can be limited by off-target effects and dose-limiting toxicities, which complicate sustained administration [13]. This has sharpened interest in alternative intervention points that may modulate the VEGF system with greater precision. Structural insights into VEGF-VEGFR recognition, together with evidence for druggable regulatory sites and conformational control within VEGFR2-associated signalling, support the plausibility of approaches that influence signalling states rather than relying solely on blockade [11, 14]. These concepts are consistent with broader advances in engineering ligand and receptor systems to achieve signalling bias and therapeutic precision [15].

Acridine is an established pharmacophore with diverse biological activities and a long-standing role in anticancer drug discovery [16, 17]. Substitution at the C-9 position has been particularly important for modulating biological activity, although C-9 phenyl-substituted acridines remain less explored than several other chemotypes [17]. Our laboratory has focused on C-9 phenyl-substituted acridines, and prior work demonstrated that 9-phenyl acridine (ACPH) can interact with DNA and preferentially sensitize cancer cells to apoptosis [18]. ACPH has also been shown to act synergistically with cisplatin at non-toxic concentrations, potentiating cisplatin-induced cytotoxicity at lower doses [19].

Beyond their anticancer relevance, rigid aromatic scaffolds such as acridines provide defined shape complementarity and hydrophobic surface characteristics that may be suitable for probing transient or non-canonical interaction regions on conformationally flexible proteins. Structural and biophysical studies indicate that vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) exhibits conformational plasticity and contains hydrophobic and aromatic residue-rich regions that contribute to dimer stability and receptor-recognition interfaces [11, 20, 21]. In addition, protein-protein interfaces are increasingly recognized to harbor small but ligandable cavities that can be engaged by appropriately shaped small molecules [22]. Consistent with this concept, small-molecule ligands capable of modulating VEGF function or interfering with VEGF-VEGFR interactions have been reported, supporting the plausibility of direct VEGF engagement by non-peptidic compounds [23, 24].

On this basis, ACPH was selected as a representative probe within this scaffold class. The present study was therefore intentionally designed as an exploratory investigation, rather than a structure-activity relationship or lead-optimization study, to assess whether ACPH can associate with VEGF and influence its conformational dynamics, and whether such association corresponds to a measurable vascular phenotype, without presuming VEGF-specific inhibition. Within this framework, ACPH is hypothesized to engage transient or regulatory regions of VEGF and modulate conformational features relevant to VEGF-VEGFR recognition, rather than directly blocking receptor binding. The study further examines whether such molecular interactions correspond to a measurable phenotypic effect in an in ovo angiogenesis model. Importantly, this work does not seek to establish definitive VEGF pathway inhibition but to evaluate the biological plausibility of VEGF conformational modulation by a small aromatic scaffold under defined experimental conditions.

Materials and methods

Protein and ligand preparation

The three-dimensional (3D) structure of the target protein, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 2VPF) [20]. Prior to docking, the structure was prepared by removing crystallographic water molecules, heteroatoms, and any co-crystallized molecules that could interfere with binding-site recognition. Polar hydrogen atoms were added and Kollman charges were assigned using AutoDock Tools to ensure appropriate electrostatic configuration. Putative binding-site residues were identified using a stepwise approach involving (i) structural inspection of the VEGF dimer using PyMOL, (ii) manual identification of surface-exposed cavities based on geometric accessibility and residue composition, and (iii) refinement using literature-reported reports regions associated with VEGF conformational flexibility and transient pocket formation [21, 22]. Docking-site selection was therefore not performed as a fully blind search, but was defined through cavity-guided and literature-informed definition of a candidate VEGF surface region considered structurally permissive for small-molecule association. No automated pocket-detection software was used; cavity identification was performed manually to maintain consistency across docking platforms.

The ligand molecule, 9-phenyl acridine (ACPH), was generated and optimized in three-dimensional space using ACD/ChemSketch. The initial geometry was inspected in PyMOL 7.2 to confirm structural integrity. ACPH was then energy-minimized using the Conjugate Gradient (CG) algorithm in Accelrys Discovery Studio 2.5. The CHARMM force field was applied solely for ligand geometry optimization until the energy gradient converged to 0.001 kcal/mol [25]. The neutral (unprotonated) form of ACPH was selected for docking and molecular dynamics simulations to minimize artificial electrostatic stabilization and to allow unbiased exploration of non-covalent interactions within the VEGF binding region. Acridine derivatives are weakly basic heterocycles and may exist in partially protonated form under physiological conditions; however, the neutral state was selected here as a simplified representation to enable comparative evaluation of binding poses across docking platforms without introducing charge-dependent bias. Protonated states of ACPH were not explicitly evaluated in this study, and therefore potential effects of ligand protonation on electrostatic interactions with charged VEGF residues (e.g., Asp, Glu, Lys) are not captured in the present analysis.

Docking studies

Energy-minimized coordinates of VEGF and ACPH were used for molecular docking using multiple platforms (AutoDock 4.2, PatchDock, and GOLD) to obtain comparative interaction profiles [26–28]. For AutoDock 4.2, docking simulations were performed using the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm (LGA). The docking region was defined by first identifying a candidate surface cavity from the prepared VEGF structure, then refining that candidate region using residue-level structural context and published information on VEGF conformational plasticity and ligandable pockets [21, 22]. The grid box was centered on this preselected candidate region and configured to encompass the pocket to allow ligand flexibility. Grid dimensions were set to X = Y = Z = 126 points with a spacing of 0.375 Å, thereby covering the full extent of the pre-identified cavity and adjacent residues potentially contributing to ligand accommodation. Accordingly, the docking workflow consisted of candidate-region identification by structural/cavity inspection, refinement of that region using literature-guided VEGF residue context, and focused docking within the resulting pocket-centered search space rather than an unconstrained blind-docking protocol. Ten conformations were generated, and the pose with the lowest predicted interaction energy was selected for further analysis. Docked complexes were evaluated based on predicted binding affinity and the presence of stabilizing interactions, including hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts. To cross-validate the docking results, the same protein and ligand structures were docked using the PatchDock server, which applies a geometry-based molecular shape complementarity algorithm [28]. GOLD docking was performed using a genetic algorithm for flexible ligand docking, and poses were ranked using GoldScore and ChemScore [27]. Finally, top-ranked docked complexes from each method were refined by post-docking energy minimization using the Conjugate Gradient algorithm in Accelrys Discovery Studio 2.5 with the CHARMM force field until the energy gradient converged to 0.001 kcal/mol, to remove atomic clashes and stabilize the complex geometry prior to downstream analyses [25].

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation study of VEGF and VEGF-ACPH complex

To evaluate dynamic stability, conformational behaviour, and interaction persistence under near-physiological conditions, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed for VEGF in the absence of ligand (apo VEGF) and in complex with ACPH (VEGF-ACPH) under identical conditions. Simulations were carried out using GROMACS version 5.0.5 on an HP Z840 workstation, a validated platform widely used for biomolecular simulations with established force-field compatibility [29]. Energy-minimized coordinates of apo VEGF and the VEGF-ACPH complex obtained from docking were used as starting structures. Each system was placed in a triclinic simulation box with a minimum solute-to-boundary distance of 10 Å and solvated using the SPC/E water model. Counter-ions (Na⁺/Cl⁻) were added to neutralize the systems. The GROMOS96 53a6 force field was used for the protein, and ligand topology parameters for ACPH were generated using the Automated Topology Builder (ATB) server [30]. Partial atomic charges and bonded parameters were assigned according to the GROMOS-compatible parameterization scheme provided by ATB, ensuring consistency with the parent force field.

Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm until the maximum force was < 1000 kJ/mol/nm. Equilibration was conducted in two stages with position restraints applied to protein backbone heavy atoms: (i) NVT equilibration at 310 K using the Berendsen thermostat, followed by (ii) NPT equilibration at 1 atm using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat. Each equilibration stage was run for 100 ps. All bond lengths were constrained using the LINCS algorithm [31]. Long-range electrostatics were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method with a 10 Å cutoff for Coulombic and van der Waals interactions, and periodic boundary conditions were applied throughout [32].

Unrestrained production simulations were then performed for 50 ns for both apo VEGF and the VEGF-ACPH complex as single continuous trajectories under identical initial conditions, using a 2 fs integration time step at 310 K and 1 atm. All reported structural and energetic analyses are based on these single production trajectories; replicate simulations were not performed in the present exploratory study. Trajectory analyses were conducted over the equilibrated portion of each simulation rather than fixed time-window intervals, as the primary objective was to assess overall conformational stability and interaction patterns within a 50 ns exploratory framework. Trajectories were analysed for structural deviation (RMSD), residue-level flexibility (RMSF), compactness (radius of gyration; Rg), and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA). Two-dimensional plots were generated using Microsoft Excel, and molecular interaction visualizations were prepared using Discovery Studio 2.5 and LigPlot+ v1.4.5. A detailed summary of molecular dynamics simulation parameters is provided in Supplementary File S2.

Influence of ACPH on angiogenesis in the in ovo chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) assay

The in ovo chick chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) assay was performed following published protocols [33]. Fertilized chicken eggs were selected for the assay. Before incubation, eggshell surfaces were cleaned using dry tissue paper to remove surface debris. Eggs were incubated at 37 °C for 72 h in a sterile incubator, rotated three times daily, and maintained at 60–62% humidity. On day 4, a square window was created in the eggshell using a sterile No. 11 surgical blade, and the opening was resealed with tape to maintain sterility. Once prominent vascularization was observed surrounding the embryo, ACPH was applied at 0.04 nM, 0.4 nM, and 0.8 nM using sterile filter-paper strips placed adjacent to newly formed blood vessels.

ACPH was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock solution and subsequently diluted to the final working concentrations in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) ensuring that the final DMSO concentration was kept constant across all treatment groups (typically ≤ 0.1% v/v). A vehicle-only control (filter paper treated with solvent without ACPH) was included to account for any solvent-related effects. The concentration range was selected based on preliminary observations and prior studies on acridine derivatives, with the aim of evaluating low-nanomolar exposure while minimizing overt developmental toxicity. The effect of treatment on blood vessel formation was observed for up to 48 h. Angiogenesis was quantified using ImageJ software [34]. ImageJ-based vessel quantification was performed using randomized image ordering, and analysis was conducted in a blind manner with a treatment group label concealed during quantification to minimize potential bias. For each experimental condition, blood vessel length was measured in CAM images obtained from six independent embryos per group. Data are presented as mean±standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s multiple-comparison test to compare each treatment group with the control. Significance was defined at p < 0.05, and exact p-values are reported where applicable.

At the experimental endpoint, embryos were humanely terminated by rapid cooling at 4 °C prior to disposal. No anesthesia was administered, as the study was conducted at early developmental stages where embryos are not considered capable of pain perception, in accordance with institutional and internationally accepted guidelines for embryonic research.

Results

Molecular docking

Docking analyses suggested that ACPH was predicted to be accommodated within a preselected transient cavity on the VEGF surface through hydrophobic packing, van der Waals contacts, and electrostatic complementarity (Fig. 1). The predicted binding poses positioned ACPH within ≤ 5 Å of residues in both VEGF chains. In chain A, the local environment included Phe36, Ile46, Phe47, Lys48, Pro49, Ser50, and Cys51, whereas in chain B the ligand was proximal to Tyr21, Ser24, Cys60, Cys61, Asn62, Asp63, Glu64, Leu66, Glu67, and Lys107 (Table 1). Docking scores were therefore used to identify residues contributing to ligand accommodation, highlighting Ile46 (chain A) and Asp63, Glu64, and Glu67 (chain B) as frequent contributors within the predicted binding mode. A summary of docking outcomes across the different docking platforms (AutoDock, PatchDock, and GOLD) is presented in Table 2. Because the docking platforms employ different scoring functions, absolute score values are not directly comparable across methods; therefore, docking results were interpreted based on consistency in binding region, pose stability, and interaction patterns rather than numerical equivalence of scores. These results were used to define a plausible VEGF-ACPH binding configuration for subsequent molecular dynamics simulations and are not interpreted as evidence of functional VEGF inhibition.

Molecular dynamics simulation of VEGF and the VEGF-ACPH complex

To compare conformational behaviour under identical simulation conditions, 50 ns molecular dynamics simulations were performed for unbound VEGF and the VEGF-ACPH complex. Backbone RMSD analysis (Fig. 2a) indicated that both systems approached a stable regime after approximately 30 ns. Accordingly, structural analyses were interpreted based on the equilibrated phase of the trajectory rather than discrete interval sampling. Following equilibration, the ligand-bound system exhibited lower backbone deviation (≈ 0.1–0.2 nm) than unbound VEGF (≈ 0.3–0.6 nm), consistent with reduced overall conformational variability in the ligand-associated system under the simulated conditions.

Residue-level flexibility analysis (RMSF; Fig. 2b) showed comparatively dampened fluctuations in regions adjacent to the predicted binding site. In particular, residues 63–67 in chain B displayed reduced mobility in the ligand-bound simulation, consistent with localized differences in conformational dynamics associated with ligand presence within the simulated system.

Global conformational descriptors were evaluated using the radius of gyration and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA). Relative to unbound VEGF, the VEGF-ACPH complex exhibited a slightly reduced radius of gyration (Fig. 3a) and lower SASA values (Fig. 3b). These differences are consistent with modest changes in protein compactness and solvent exposure associated with ligand accommodation within the simulated system.

Collectively, the MD simulations indicate that ACPH association is accompanied by measurable changes in VEGF conformational dynamics and interaction patterns within the simulated system. These observations support the plausibility of VEGF conformational modulation by ACPH but do not establish functional inhibition or biological specificity.

In ovo chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) assay

ACPH exposure was associated with a dose-dependent reduction in vascularization in the chick embryo CAM model at 0.04 nM, 0.4 nM, and 0.8 nM, as indicated by decreased vessel density in treated embryos (Fig. 8). Quantitative ImageJ analysis showed a statistically significant reduction in blood vessel length at 24 h and 48 h following treatment at 0.4 nM and 0.8 nM compared with untreated controls (Fig. 9). At the highest concentration (0.8 nM), reduced vascularization coincided with decreased embryo viability (assessed during the 48 h observation period), indicating that non-specific developmental toxicity likely contributes to the observed phenotype at this dose. Accordingly, the CAM findings are interpreted as phenotypic observations that support further investigation rather than definitive evidence of VEGF-specific anti-angiogenic activity, and they do not distinguish between VEGF-related effects and broader vascular suppression or developmental toxicity.

Discussion

Angiogenesis is essential for normal development and tissue repair, yet sustained or dysregulated vascular growth contributes to major disease burdens, including cancer and chronic inflammatory states. VEGF-particularly VEGF-A-remains a dominant driver of pathological neovascularization and a longstanding therapeutic target because it promotes endothelial proliferation, migration, survival, and vascular permeability [35, 36]. In cancer, hypoxia further reinforces VEGF-dependent programs and shapes permissive microenvironments that support progression and treatment resistance [37]. Despite the clinical success of anti-VEGF biologics and VEGF/VEGFR-directed strategies, continued interest exists in identifying alternative or complementary modalities that could reduce treatment burden, expand combination options, or provide additional mechanistic insight into VEGF-driven biology [38, 39].

This study explored whether a bioactive acridine derivative, 9-phenyl acridine (ACPH), can associate with VEGF and influence its conformational behavior, with corresponding phenotypic effects assessed in an in ovo angiogenesis model. Acridine scaffolds are well established in anticancer drug discovery and are notable for their capacity to support diverse interactions depending on substitution patterns and physicochemical tuning [16, 17]. Prior evidence that 9-phenyl acridine can induce apoptosis and synergize with cisplatin in cancer cells supports its broader biological tractability as a small-molecule scaffold [18, 19]. Building on this background, computational docking analyses suggested that ACPH may be accommodated within a defined transient cavity on VEGF, with predicted interactions involving hydrophobic packing, van der Waals contacts, and electrostatic complementarity rather than canonical hydrogen bonding. Such interaction features are consistent with the physicochemical characteristics of planar aromatic scaffolds, in which shape complementarity and non-polar contacts can contribute to ligand accommodation without requiring extensive hydrogen bonding.

Molecular dynamics simulations provided complementary computational observations, in which ACPH association with VEGF was associated with reduced structural variability after equilibration and dampened residue-level fluctuations in interface-adjacent regions under the simulated conditions. Such effects are consistent with ligand-associated differences in a subset of VEGF conformational states, a concept that has been emphasized in prior work examining VEGF dynamics in a bound contexts [21]. The observed localized changes in flexibility near residues implicated by docking and energy profiling suggest that ACPH association may be associated with altered VEGF surface dynamics; however, these findings are derived from computational simulations and do not establish functional effects of VEGF-VEGFR interactions.

Although VEGF is not a classical small-molecule target, increasing evidence indicates that conformational plasticity and transient cavity formation can permit ligand association in defined regions, supporting exploratory structure-based approaches. However, because the present simulations did not include VEGF receptors, the data support conformational modulation rather than direct evidence of inhibited VEGF-VEGFR binding. Accordingly, these findings are consistent with a plausible association between ACPH and VEGF but do not establish VEGF-specific pathway inhibition or functional modulation. In this regard, the study provides a mechanistically informed starting point for receptor-facing validation rather than a definitive receptor-blockade claim.

The CAM assay findings provide phenotypic observations consistent with reduction in vascularization at low nanomolar concentrations, with visually apparent changes and quantitative decreases in vessel length at 24 h and 48 h. However, the CAM assay was not benchmarked against a reference anti-angiogenic agent, which limits direct comparison of the magnitude of the observed effects with established VEGF or VEGFR inhibitors [40]; therefore the extent of vascular reduction observed in this study should be interpreted in relative rather than quantitative terms when compared with known anti-angiogenic agents. In addition, as a phenotypic model, the CAM assay cannot distinguish between VEGF-related effects and broader suppression of vascular development or embryo viability, particularly at higher concentrations where non-specific toxicity is evident. Reduced embryo viability with prolonged exposure at the highest concentration therefore introduces an important interpretive constraint: at elevated dose, suppression of vascularization may reflect a combination of angiogenesis-associated effects and non-specific developmental toxicity [33, 41]. This distinction is central to translational interpretation and is therefore treated as a key observation rather than a limitation to be minimized. The most defensible interpretation of the in ovo data is that ACPH exposure was associated with a dose-dependent reduction in CAM vascularization, with an increasing likelihood of toxicity contributing at the upper dose range, such that the pronounced vascular suppression observed at the highest concentration likely reflects a combined effect of vascular changes and non-specific developmental toxicity rather than a mechanism-specific anti-angiogenic response.

From a therapeutic-development perspective, the results motivate continued exploration of acridine-derived scaffolds as candidate VEGF-engaging molecules. The broader VEGF targeting landscape includes biologics and engineered binders as well as structure-informed approaches that exploit defined VEGF/VEGFR interaction features [38, 39]. Small-molecule engagement of VEGF remains conceptually attractive but practically challenging, particularly as a strategy to modulate ligand conformational states that may influence receptor recognition, making early indications of VEGF association and phenotypic vascular changes of interest within an exploratory context [23, 24]. The present study positions ACPH as a tractable starting scaffold rather than a validated lead, particularly if future structure-activity optimization can strengthen VEGF-directed effects while widening the safety window.

Several limitations define the next experimental steps. First, computational docking and molecular dynamics provide supportive evidence for a plausible ligand-protein association and altered conformational dynamics but do not establish pathway specificity and therefore mechanistic conclusions should be interpreted with caution. Specifically, no VEGF-VEGFR binding/competition assays or downstream receptor signaling readouts (e.g., VEGFR2 phosphorylation) were performed in the present study. Second, CAM outcomes alone cannot resolve whether the observed phenotype is driven by VEGF modulation versus broader cytotoxicity at higher dose. Third, direct benchmarking against established VEGF inhibitors was not performed, limiting comparative claims. Molecular dynamics results are derived from single simulation trajectories for each system; while sufficient for exploratory conformational analysis, future studies should incorporate replicate simulations to assess trajectory convergence and improve statistical robustness. In addition, advanced free-energy calculations (e.g., metadynamics or umbrella sampling) were not performed; therefore, the reported interaction energy values should be interpreted as qualitative estimates rather than rigorous thermodynamic binding free energies. Furthermore, ligand protonation states were not explicitly explored; given that acridine derivatives can undergo protonation under physiological conditions with potential coexistence of neutral and protonated species, future studies should evaluate alternative protonation states to determine their impact on electrostatic interactions, binding orientation, and interaction energetics. Future work should therefore prioritize: (i) receptor-engagement or competition assays to test whether ACPH interferes with VEGF binding to VEGFRs; (ii) endothelial functional assays (migration, tube formation, and permeability-related readouts) paired with cytotoxicity controls; (iii) expanded CAM quantification with explicit viability scoring and standardized imaging endpoints; and (iv) scaffold optimization guided by computational dynamics and binding-site mapping, informed by prior modelling strategies used to design VEGF-targeting binders and ligands. Together, these steps will clarify whether ACPH is best understood as a VEGF-associated conformational modulator with angiogenesis-related effects or as a broader bioactive scaffold whose anti-vascular effects observed in ovo partially reflect dose-limiting toxicity.

Importantly, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence of direct VEGF inhibition, VEGF-VEGFR interference, or a defined anti-angiogenic mechanism. The computational analyses provide structural hypotheses of ligand association, while the CAM assay reflects a phenotypic outcome that may arise from multiple biological processes, including non-specific developmental toxicity at higher concentrations. Therefore, the observed associations should be considered hypothesis-generating and require validation using receptor-level assays and endothelial functional studies.

Conclusions

This study presents exploratory computational and in ovo observations suggesting that 9-phenyl acridine (ACPH) is predicted to associate with VEGF and is associated with differences in conformational behaviour in regions implicated in protein-protein interactions. Multi-platform docking and molecular dynamics simulations consistently positioned ACPH within a transient VEGF cavity, with reduced conformational variability and dampened residue-level fluctuations observed in the ligand-associated system under simulated conditions. In parallel, ACPH exposure was associated with a dose-dependent reduction in CAM vascularization, with observable effects at 0.4 nM and more pronounced reduction at 0.8 nM. Importantly, reduced embryo viability at the highest concentration indicates that non-specific developmental toxicity is likely to contribute at this dose, thereby limiting mechanistic interpretation. Collectively, these findings do not establish VEGF-specific anti-VEGF activity, but instead identify ACPH as a candidate small-molecule scaffold associated with VEGF interaction and phenotypic vascular reduction, with potential relevance to conformational features involved in receptor recognition, warranting further mechanistic validation. Accordingly, the computational results are interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Direct VEGF-VEGFR binding or competition assays, quantitative endothelial functional studies, inclusion of reference anti-angiogenic compounds, and systematic cytotoxicity profiling will be essential to determine specificity, mechanism, and therapeutic relevance.
