SnRNA-seq and genome-wide CRISPR screening define the complete transcriptional trajectory and functional drivers of podocyte stress
Authors: Jianbo Qing, Jiaying Hu, Xiao Wang, Xiaoyun Shen, Yimiao Ma, Mingjing Gao, Junnan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Podocyte injury is a central driver of proteinuria and progressive kidney dysfunction. Although podocytes are continuously exposed to diverse stressors in both physiological and pathological contexts, the dynamic processes underlying their adaptation and eventual failure remain poorly defined. Here, we performed integrative single-nucleus RNA sequencing of kidney tissues from patients with six types of representative chronic glomerulonephritis, capturing a spectrum of podocyte injury states. We identified distinct podocyte subpopulations and reconstructed a dynamic trajectory characterized by an initial adaptive activation followed by progressive functional decline. Integration with time-resolved transcriptomics identified 778 candidate genes associated with podocyte stress responses. To distinguish putative functional drivers from secondary transcriptional changes, we integrated these candidates with a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen, prioritizing genes required for podocyte survival under stress conditions. Subsequent siRNA-mediated validation of five representative candidates—BST1, TALDO1, ATP6V1E1, PPP2R1A and CHL1—showed that knockdown of these genes significantly compromised cell viability and accelerated apoptosis, highlighting a coordinated survival network spanning metabolic regulation, autophagy, and cytoskeletal stability. Our findings define a dynamic framework of podocyte stress adaptation and failure, and suggest that targeting stress-response pathways may prolong podocyte survival, thereby extending the therapeutic window for intervention in chronic kidney disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06335-6.

Introduction

Podocytes play a central role in maintaining the integrity of the glomerular filtration barrier [1]. Podocyte injury is a defining feature of a wide range of kidney diseases, particularly chronic glomerulonephritis, and is closely associated with proteinuria and progressive loss of renal function [2]. Notably, podocytes are terminally differentiated cells with limited regenerative capacity, rendering them especially susceptible to sustained stress and cumulative injury [3].

Within both physiological and pathological microenvironments, podocytes are continuously exposed to diverse stressors, including mechanical strain [4], metabolic disturbances such as hyperglycemia and lipotoxicity [5, 6], hemodynamic alterations such as hypertension [7], and immune-mediated insults involving cytokines and circulating antibodies [8]. Consequently, cellular stress responses are not sporadic events but constitute an intrinsic and persistent feature of podocyte biology [9]. The capacity of podocytes to tolerate and adapt to these stressors is essential for preserving foot process architecture and maintaining glomerular function. Enhancing podocyte stress resilience may therefore extend the therapeutic window, providing critical time for clinical intervention to prevent irreversible injury and disease progression.

Despite its fundamental importance, the dynamic nature of podocyte stress responses remains poorly defined. Previous studies, including those employing single-cell transcriptomics, have largely focused on identifying genes that are differentially expressed between healthy and injured states [10, 11]. However, such static comparisons fail to capture the temporal complexity of stress responses. Many stress-associated genes exhibit non-monotonic expression dynamics, including transient induction followed by repression or oscillatory patterns over the course of injury [12, 13]. As a result, analyses restricted to endpoint comparisons may overlook key regulators that function at specific phases of stress adaptation, potentially leading to incomplete or even misleading interpretations. Furthermore, defining discrete stages of podocyte injury in clinical and experimental settings remains inherently challenging, further complicating efforts to resolve the temporal sequence of molecular events.

Here, we integrated single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) of kidney tissues from patients with six types of representative chronic glomerulonephritis with time-resolved bulk RNA sequencing from in vitro podocyte injury models. Through pseudotemporal analysis, we reconstructed the dynamic transcriptional trajectories of podocytes under stress and identified candidate genes associated with stress resilience during early and transitional phases of injury. By integrating these candidates with a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen, we prioritized genes required for podocyte survival under stress. Finally, we performed siRNA-mediated functional validation in podocyte cell lines, confirming the roles of key candidates in modulating stress tolerance (Fig. 1A).

Results

Single-nucleus transcriptomic landscape across glomerular diseases

We generated snRNA-seq data from kidney tissues of patients with five representative glomerular diseases associated with prominent podocyte injury, including minimal change disease (MCD), membranous nephropathy (MN), focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), obesity-related nephropathy (ORG), and diabetic nephropathy (DN). Adjacent non-tumor kidney tissues from three patients with renal carcinoma were included as controls. To further account for potential confounding effects associated with surgical sampling, such as acute ischemia during nephrectomy, we additionally incorporated publicly available snRNA-seq datasets from three patients with IgA nephropathy (IgAN) [14]. These IgAN cases were characterized predominantly by mesangial alterations with minimal podocyte injury, thereby providing a complementary reference for relatively preserved podocyte states. All included individuals had an eGFR > 90 mL/min/1.73 m² (Table S1). It is worth noting that, regardless of disease stage, podocytes detected by snRNA-seq are those that remain attached to the glomerulus. Severely injured podocytes are likely to have already detached and are therefore absent from the dataset. As a result, the captured podocytes predominantly represent cells that are still viable but undergoing stress, making them particularly informative for studying early injury responses and adaptive states.

After stringent quality control and data integration, a total of 168,850 high-quality nuclei were retained for downstream analysis. Dimensionality reduction using 32 principal components enabled the identification of ten major renal cell types based on established marker genes [15], including podocytes (Fig. 1B-C and Fig. S1A-B). Among the 2,138 podocytes, canonical markers including WT1, NPHS1/2 and PODXL were highly expressed only in a subset of cells [16], while a substantial proportion showed reduced expression of these markers, consistent with an injured podocyte state (Fig. 1D-E). Cross-sample comparison further revealed marked transcriptional similarities across disease groups: podocytes from DN and MN showed highly similar expression profiles, whereas those from FSGS, ORG and MCD clustered more closely (Fig. 1F).

Consistently, density distributions in UMAP space showed that podocytes from different disease origins occupied partially distinct regions, despite all samples being derived from patients with preserved renal function. These findings suggest that distinct etiologies drive divergent transcriptional states in podocytes, even at early stages of disease (Fig. 1G).

Identification of podocyte subpopulations

To further investigate early functional changes during podocyte injury, we re-performed dimensionality reduction on 2,138 podocytes using 22 principal components (Fig. 2A and Figure S1C). Following re-embedding, podocytes were broadly separated into two regions based on the expression levels of canonical markers (Fig. 2B). Subsequent clustering at a resolution of 0.05 yielded stable results (Fig. 2C-D), identifying four distinct clusters with good purity as assessed by ROGUE (Fig. 2E). Canonical podocyte markers, particularly WT1 and NPHS1/2, showed relatively higher expression in POD1 and POD4 compared to POD2 and POD3. This suggests that POD2 and POD3 may represent relatively injured or stressed states (Fig. 2F). Notably, when examining absolute expression levels, the differences among the four clusters were not pronounced, which is consistent with an early stage of podocyte injury (Fig. 2G). Overall, POD1 and POD4 represented relatively healthy or less-injured podocyte states. Notably, POD4 was enriched in IgAN samples, consistent with a milder degree of podocyte injury. In contrast, POD2 and POD3 were preferentially enriched in disease conditions associated with more pronounced podocyte injury, such as MN and DN (Fig. 2H).

Functional enrichment analysis further revealed distinct biological programs across podocyte subpopulations. POD2 was enriched for metabolic processes, including organic anion and carboxylic acid transport, as well as pathways related to cellular metabolism such as butanoate and pyrimidine metabolism. In contrast, POD3 showed enrichment in developmental and signaling pathways, including nephron epithelium development, MAPK signaling, and cGMP-PKG signaling, suggesting activation of stress-responsive and remodeling programs. POD4 was associated with processes related to cellular structure and turnover, such as actin nucleation, reticulophagy, and endocytosis, as well as pathways involved in cell adhesion and genome maintenance. Notably, POD1 was enriched for pathways linked to glomerular and nephron development, cytoskeletal organization, and cell projection assembly, consistent with a relatively preserved or homeostatic podocyte state [17] (Fig. 2I).

Stress, autophagy and apoptosis programs vary across podocyte states and disease conditions

Podocyte stress involves multiple interconnected processes, including necroptosis, inflammatory and profibrotic signaling, cytoskeletal remodeling, endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, as well as autophagy and apoptosis, all of which can contribute to podocyte injury and compromise of the glomerular filtration barrier. To systematically evaluate stress responses in our study, we focused on pathways that have been particularly highlighted in prior literature, namely autophagy and apoptosis. Accordingly, we curated three functionally defined gene sets representing key stress-related processes, including autophagy, apoptosis, and cellular stress responses, based on prior literature [18]. Autophagy-related genes capture adaptive and protective mechanisms involved in cellular homeostasis, whereas apoptosis-related genes reflect activation of cell death pathways. In addition, a comprehensive stress-response gene set was compiled, encompassing genes involved in oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, hypoxia, and chaperone-mediated responses [19, 20] (Table S2).

We next quantified autophagy, apoptosis and stress activities at the single-cell level in podocytes. All three programs showed marked variation across the four podocyte subpopulations, with POD2 exhibiting the highest activity, followed by POD3, whereas POD1 and POD4 showed relatively lower levels (Fig. 3A). At the disease level, podocytes from MN and DN displayed the highest levels of apoptosis and stress signatures. In contrast, IgAN and control samples exhibited relatively low activity, while FSGS and MCD showed intermediate levels (Fig. 3B). Notably, autophagy and stress activities were positively correlated (Fig. 3C).

Figure 3D and Fig. S2 show the expression patterns of genes associated with autophagy, apoptosis and stress programs across podocyte subpopulations and disease conditions. We further examined the association between podocyte state dynamics and clinical parameters. Notably, both autophagy and stress activity scores in podocytes were positively correlated with 24-hour proteinuria levels. In addition, the relative abundance of the POD2 subpopulation was also significantly associated with increased proteinuria (Fig. 3E).

We next validated these findings using the KPMP dataset, comprising patients with acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD). While apoptosis activity showed only modest variation across conditions, autophagy and stress programs displayed striking differences. Podocytes from AKI exhibited significantly elevated autophagy and stress activities compared to controls and CKD, consistent with an active adaptive response to acute injury [21]. In contrast, CKD podocytes showed markedly reduced autophagy activity and only modest stress activation, suggesting impaired adaptive capacity in chronic disease state [22] (Fig. 3F).

Pseudotemporal trajectories reveals dynamic transitions of podocyte states

We first evaluated the relationships among podocyte subpopulations using cosine similarity and partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) analysis. Cosine similarity showed that POD1 and POD4 were transcriptionally more similar, whereas POD2 and POD3 formed a separate, closely related group (Fig. 4A). Consistent with this, PAGA revealed stronger connectivity between POD1 and POD4, and between POD2 and POD3, indicating two major podocyte state modules (Fig. 4B). We further evaluated cell cycle activity across podocyte subpopulations using “S” and “G2/M” scores. Notably, no substantial differences were observed among the four groups, with all subpopulations exhibiting consistently low cell cycle activity (Fig. 4C). This is consistent with the terminally differentiated nature of podocytes and indicates that the observed transcriptional heterogeneity is not driven by cell cycle effects.

To delineate the dynamic progression of podocyte states, we reconstructed pseudotemporal trajectories using multiple complementary approaches, including CytoTRACE2 [23], Monocle2, Monocle3 [24], Slingshot [25], and VIA [26]. Notably, CytoTRACE2 estimates differentiation potential in a root-independent manner, thereby providing an initial framework for trajectory orientation. CytoTRACE2 analysis revealed that POD2 exhibited the lowest differentiation potential (most differentiated state), followed by POD3, whereas POD1 and POD4 displayed higher differentiation potential, indicative of earlier or less differentiated states (Fig. 4D-F). At the disease level, podocytes from control samples exhibited the highest differentiation potential, followed by those from IgAN, whereas podocytes from MN and DN showed the lowest differentiation potential. Other disease conditions displayed intermediate levels. These patterns are consistent with our previous observations of autophagy, apoptosis and stress activities (Fig. 4G).

Consistently, trajectory inference using Monocle2 (Fig. 4H-I), Monocle3 (Fig. 4J-K), Slingshot (Fig. 4L-M) and VIA (Fig. 4N-O) demonstrated a coherent transition from POD1 and POD4 toward POD2 and POD3. Across all methods, POD2 was consistently positioned at the terminal end of the trajectory, suggesting that it represents a relatively late-stage, highly differentiated or injury-associated podocyte state.

Pseudotime analysis of snRNA-seq reveals non-monotonic gene expression programs in podocytes

To characterize gene expression dynamics along the inferred trajectory, we applied our previously developed scTrends framework to model transcriptional changes as a function of CytoTRACE2-derived pseudotime [27]. A total of 2,138 podocytes were ordered into 40 pseudotemporal bins, and 6,120 expressed genes were included for downstream modeling (Fig. 5A).

Based on the optimal model parameters (Fig. S3), genes were classified into five major dynamic patterns, including “Up”, “Down”, “Up-then-Down”, “Down-then-Up”, and “complex” trends (Table S3). Notably, canonical podocyte markers such as WT1, NPHS1, SYNPO and PODXL exhibited an “Up-then-Down” pattern, whereas NPHS2 showed a gradual decline along the trajectory (Fig. 5B). Figure 5C illustrates the overall fitted trend across all genes, as well as representative trajectories of the top 50-ranked genes in each category. Strikingly, the global expression profile of all 6,120 genes displayed a general “Up-then-Down” pattern, suggesting a coordinated transcriptional response during podocyte state transitions. Figure 5D further presents the classification of gene expression trends and their distribution across pseudotime, cell states, and disease conditions.

Functional enrichment analysis revealed that “Up” genes were enriched in pathways related to cell development and differentiation, suggesting a potential involvement in the progressive activation of repair or remodeling programs [28]. In contrast, “Down” genes were associated with stress response and repair pathways, including endoplasmic reticulum stress and DNA damage repair, indicating that these protective processes may diminish along the pseudotemporal trajectory. Notably, genes exhibiting an “Up-then-Down” pattern were enriched in cytoskeletal organization and autophagy-related processes, consistent with transient activation of adaptive responses during early or intermediate stages of injury [29]. Conversely, “Down-then-Up” genes were enriched in RNA processing and signaling pathways (Fig. 5E), suggesting that this trajectory may reflect early suppression followed by later reactivation of RNA regulatory and signaling programs during podocyte injury.

Time-resolved bulk RNA-seq reconstructs the complete gene expression trajectory of podocyte injury

Given that snRNA-seq predominantly captures podocytes with relatively mild injury in vivo, we sought to reconstruct the full spectrum of podocyte stress responses using an in vitro injury model. We systematically evaluated multiple stimuli previously reported to induce podocyte injury, including adriamycin (ADR), lipopolysaccharide (LPS), palmitic acid (PA), high glucose (GLU), IL-1β, TNF-α, TGF-β, and angiotensin II (ANG-II). Among these, ADR induced the most pronounced cytotoxicity, with approximately 50% cell death at 48 h, and was therefore selected for subsequent time-course experiments (Fig. 6A).

We performed bulk RNA sequencing across a temporal series (0 h, 30 min, 2 h, 6 h, 12 h, 18 h, 24 h, 36 h, and 48 h), capturing acute, early, intermediate, and late stages of podocyte injury. Principal component analysis revealed clear temporal separation among samples collected at different time points (Fig. S5A). Consistent with our single-cell analysis, dynamic changes were observed across autophagy, apoptosis, and stress programs. Autophagy displayed a characteristic pattern of early activation followed by late attenuation, with elevated activity during 0–6 h and a progressive decline beginning at 12 h, reaching a nadir at later time points. In contrast, apoptosis exhibited an inverse trend. Apoptotic activity remained suppressed during early stages, reaching its lowest level at 12 h, followed by a marked increase from 18 h onwards and peaking at 48 h. Stress responses showed a more complex, multiphasic pattern (Fig. 6B). In addition, across the full time course, apoptosis activity showed a strong inverse correlation with autophagy and stress programs (Fig. 6C). These findings recapitulate a temporal transition from early adaptive responses toward progressive injury and cell death, consistent with the trajectory inferred from in vivo analyses.

Similarly, we applied the scTrends framework to the bulk RNA-seq dataset to characterize gene expression dynamics across the time course (Fig. S4). A total of 6,048 expressed genes were modeled from 0 to 48 h and classified into six distinct patterns, including “Up”, “Down”, “Up-then-Down”, “Down-then-Up”, “complex”, and “stable” trends (Table S4). The global distribution of these expression patterns is shown in Fig. 6D, and representative fitted curves for each category are presented in Fig. S5B.

Notably, genes exhibiting an “Up-then-Down” pattern are most consistent with transient activation during early stress responses followed by attenuation at later stages, suggesting a potential role in adaptive or protective processes. Thus, we intersected the “Up-then-Down” gene sets derived from both snRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq analyses. This integrative approach yielded 778 concordant genes displaying consistent temporal dynamics across in vivo and in vitro systems (Fig. 6E, and Table S5).

Genome-wide CRISPR screening identifies key functional regulators of podocyte stress responses

Although transcriptomic analyses identified 778 stress-associated genes, it remained unclear whether these genes represent downstream consequences of injury or functional regulators of podocyte survival. To systematically identify key drivers of stress responses, we performed a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen.

We generated podocyte cell lines stably expressing Cas9 and transduced them with a genome-wide sgRNA library. Cells were then subjected to ADR-induced stress, and sgRNA abundance was quantified following selection. Using a positive selection framework, sgRNAs that were depleted or enriched after ADR treatment were used to infer genes that modulate podocyte stress sensitivity (Fig. 6F). Quality control analyses confirmed the robustness of the screen. Principal component analysis demonstrated clear separation between control and ADR-treated samples, indicating substantial perturbation of sgRNA representation following stress exposure (Fig. S5C). In addition, Gini index distributions were highly consistent among biological replicates within both the control and ADR-treated groups (Fig. S5D). Specifically, ADR treatment resulted in a distinct sgRNA abundance distribution compared with controls, indicating effective selection during the screening process (Fig. S5E).

To identify key functional drivers of podocyte stress responses, we performed an integrative analysis by intersecting the 778 dynamically regulated genes with candidate genes identified from the genome-wide CRISPR screen. Using criteria (|log2 fold change| > 0.5 and P < 0.05), we identified 32 high-confidence candidate genes. Based on Gene Ontology annotation, these candidate genes were classified into ten functional categories (Table S6). These genes were primarily enriched in pathways related to organelle function (n = 9), cytoskeletal organization and extracellular matrix remodeling (n = 8), and metabolic processes (n = 6), with additional contributions from immune response (n = 2), protein phosphorylation (n = 2), and aging/fibrosis-related pathways (n = 1), as well as other functional categories (n = 4), consistent with our earlier observations of altered autophagy and structural remodeling during podocyte stress (Fig. 6G).

Notably, we successfully recovered several well-established podocyte regulators, including the key transcription factor WT1, as well as MYH9 and ITGA9 [16], which are closely associated with podocyte structural integrity, supporting the robustness of our screening strategy. The temporal expression patterns of these 32 genes across both snRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq datasets are shown in Fig. 6H.

We then performed secondary selection based on phenotypic potency to further prioritize the most functionally relevant candidates, defined by the extent of sgRNA depletion under ADR-induced stress. From the ten functional categories, we selected five representative genes, including ATP6V1E1, PPP2R1A, TALDO1, CHL1, and BST1, which exhibits strong depletion signals across their respective modules. These genes showed relatively low expression in POD1 and POD4, but were preferentially expressed in POD2 and POD3 (Fig. S5F). Notably, ATP6V1E1 and PPP2R1A exhibited the most pronounced depletion (LFC < − 5), suggesting particularly strong protective effects (Fig. 6I). In addition, for all five candidates, multiple sgRNAs showed concordant depletion patterns supporting on-target effects and minimizing the likelihood of off-target or stochastic bias (Fig. 6J).

To further validate the functional relevance of the candidate genes, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown of ATP6V1E1, PPP2R1A, TALDO1, CHL1 and BST1 in podocytes, followed by treatment with or without ADR for 24 or 48 h (Fig. 7A-B). Under basal conditions, knockdown of all five genes resulted in a modest reduction in cell viability at 24 h, as assessed by CCK-8 assay (Fig. 7C). Upon ADR exposure, however, the decrease in cell viability induced by knockdown of each gene was significantly greater than that observed in the si-control group, and this effect became more pronounced at 48 h (Fig. 7D).

We next assessed apoptosis under the same conditions. At 24 h in the absence of ADR, only ATP6V1E1 and TALDO1 knockdown caused a mild increase in apoptosis, reaching approximately 6%. In contrast, after ADR treatment, knockdown of each of the five genes significantly increased apoptosis relative to the si-control group, in which the mean apoptotic rate was approximately 15.7%. At 48 h, silencing of any of the five genes markedly increased apoptosis even in the absence of ADR, with apoptotic rates approximately 2- to 3-fold higher than those in control cells (Fig. 7E). Following ADR treatment for 48 h, the apoptotic rate in the si-control group was approximately 45%, whereas knockdown of each gene further increased apoptosis to > 60%; notably, ATP6V1E1 and TALDO1 silencing increased apoptosis to > 80% (Fig. 7F-G). Together, these findings indicate that the identified genes are not only involved in the cellular stress response but also function as key protective regulators that preserve podocyte viability and limit apoptosis in response to injury.

Discussion

Here, we integrated clinical snRNA-seq data with in vitro time-course profiling to reconstruct the dynamic transcriptional landscape of podocyte stress responses. By coupling this approach with genome-wide CRISPR screening, we bridged transcriptional dynamics with functional causality, identifying 32 core regulators of podocyte stress resilience, five of which were further functionally validated.

Podocytes have extremely limited regenerative capacity [30]. Although recent studies have proposed potential transdifferentiation mechanisms, these are insufficient in both scale and efficiency to compensate for podocyte loss [31]. From an evolutionary perspective, podocytes are continuously exposed to multiple stressors over the lifespan, including hemodynamic forces, metabolic disturbances, and immune-mediated injury [32]. The ability to maintain structural and functional stability under such sustained stress suggests that podocytes are not inherently fragile, but instead possess robust stress resilience and adaptive capacity, and this intrinsic resilience is fundamental to the long-term maintenance of glomerular filtration function [33]. However, CKD is not simply a consequence of insufficient defense, but rather reflects a progressive breakdown of this finely tuned adaptive balance. Under persistent stress, adaptive responses may gradually transition into maladaptive states, ultimately leading to irreversible injury and podocyte loss [34, 35].

Current therapeutic strategies for podocyte injury primarily rely on hemodynamic modulation, such as RAS inhibition, and immunosuppressive treatments [16]. Although these approaches can partially delay disease progression, they are often insufficient to rapidly reverse podocyte injury and loss. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop strategies that extend the “survival window” of podocytes under stress, thereby complementing existing therapies.

Motivated by this clinical need, we integrated snRNA-seq datasets from patients with multiple glomerular diseases and stratified podocytes into four subpopulations representing a continuum from relatively healthy to progressively injured states. Functional scoring revealed coordinated activation of autophagy, stress, and apoptosis programs along this transition, suggesting that podocytes maintain a highly sensitive and dynamically regulated homeostatic balance under stress conditions. Notably, autophagy, which has been widely recognized as a critical mechanism for maintaining proteostasis and mitigating oxidative damage in terminally differentiated podocytes, was activated at early stages, consistent with an adaptive protective response [36].

Furthermore, by reconstructing pseudotemporal trajectories, we identified non-monotonic gene expression patterns that are largely overlooked by conventional static differential analyses. Notably, a subset of key podocyte regulators and structural genes, such as WT1, NPHS1, and SYNPO, exhibited a consistent “up-then-down” dynamic pattern along the trajectory. These genes are central to maintaining podocyte identity and structural integrity, and WT1 has traditionally been regarded as a stable marker of podocyte differentiation [37], our findings suggest that it undergoes transient upregulation during early stress. This dynamic behavior may reflect a compensatory response aimed at preserving podocyte identity and reinforcing structural stability under initial injury conditions [38, 39]. Such a response likely involves coordinated activation of cytoskeletal and slit diaphragm-associated programs, which are essential for maintaining filtration barrier integrity [40]. However, this transient adaptive response appears unsustainable under prolonged stress, ultimately leading to a decline in these key regulatory and structural components.

Although snRNA-seq provides powerful resolution for dissecting cellular heterogeneity in human tissues, it is inherently subject to a “survivor bias,” as severely injured or detached podocytes are less likely to be captured. This limitation constrains the ability to fully resolve the complete trajectory of podocyte injury. To address this, we established a 0–48 h in vitro time-course injury model to reconstruct the full spectrum of podocyte responses from acute stress to terminal injury. Within this framework, autophagy activity exhibited an early increase followed by a progressive decline, whereas apoptosis displayed an inverse temporal pattern. These dynamics closely recapitulate the trends inferred from in vivo analyses. Consistently, key genes such as WT1 also showed a similar “up-then-down” pattern in vitro, further supporting a coordinated, temporally regulated response that underlies podocyte adaptation to stress.

By integrating in vivo and in vitro datasets, we identified a set of core genes exhibiting consistent dynamic patterns across both systems. These genes were significantly enriched in biological processes related to actin cytoskeleton remodeling, autophagy regulation, cell polarity maintenance, and kidney development, which are essential for maintaining podocyte structural integrity and glomerular filtration function [18], suggesting that podocytes may activate coordinated structural and metabolic programs to preserve cellular homeostasis during early stress.

However, changes in gene expression do not necessarily equate to functional necessity. By integrating genome-wide CRISPR screening with dynamic trajectory analysis, we identified 32 core candidate genes, only a small fraction of which (WT1, MYH9, and ITGA9) have been previously linked to podocyte biology. This observation further suggests that conventional endpoint-based differential expression analyses may overlook genes that play critical protective roles during early stress responses.

The five representative genes (BST1, TALDO1, ATP6V1E1, PPP2R1A, and CHL1) span multiple functional domains, including metabolic regulation, redox homeostasis, lysosomal function, and cytoskeletal organization, indicating that podocyte stress resilience is supported by coordinated regulation across diverse cellular processes. For instance, TALDO1, a key enzyme in the pentose phosphate pathway, may contribute to redox balance by supporting NADPH production, which is essential for counteracting oxidative stress [41]. Similarly, NAD⁺ metabolism–related pathways, potentially involving BST1, have been implicated in renal injury and cellular stress responses [42, 43]. In addition, ATP6V1E1, as a component of the V-ATPase complex [44], is required for lysosomal acidification and may influence autophagic flux [45], a process critical for podocyte homeostasis. PPP2R1A encodes a scaffold subunit of the protein phosphatase 2 A holoenzyme, which has been shown to play a critical role in maintaining cytoskeletal integrity and normal function in podocytes [46], while CHL1 may contribute to cell adhesion and structural integrity [47]. Collectively, these findings suggest that podocyte resilience under stress is not governed by a single pathway, but rather by an integrated network linking metabolism, proteostasis, and structural stability.

This study has several limitations. First, although adjacent normal kidney tissue from renal carcinoma patients was used as a reference, it cannot fully substitute for truly healthy human kidney tissue, as renal biopsy is rarely performed in completely healthy individuals. Second, although the IgAN cases included here exhibited relatively preserved podocyte morphology, subclinical podocyte stress may still occur and contribute to albuminuria; thus, IgAN should be considered a reference for a relatively preserved podocyte state rather than a disease completely independent of podocyte involvement. Finally, although genome-wide CRISPR functional screens and ADR-induced podocyte injury models provided insights into candidate genes promoting stress resilience, these in vitro systems cannot fully recapitulate in vivo disease progression or represent the broader spectrum of podocyte injury in DN, ORG and other complex glomerular diseases. Future studies using disease-specific in vivo models will be needed to delineate causative mechanisms and therapeutic windows.

In summary, our study supports a model in which podocyte injury represents a staged transition driven by dynamic regulatory networks, where early adaptive responses may shape cellular fate. These findings highlight a shift in perspective from passive damage mitigation toward enhancing podocyte resilience, and provide a framework for identifying potential therapeutic targets before irreversible injury.

Methods

Patients

Kidney tissues were obtained from patients with five types of representative chronic glomerulonephritis at Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. All cases were confirmed by clinical and pathological evaluation, with podocyte injury further verified by electron microscopy. Adjacent normal kidney tissues from three patients with renal cell carcinoma were included as controls. None of the participants had received corticosteroids or immunosuppressive therapy within six months prior to biopsy or surgery, and all had an eGFR above 90 mL/min/1.73 m². The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (ID: 2024 − 0529).

Cell culture

Conditionally immortalized human podocytes (HPCs; F-biology; FBCL2160) were cultured at 33 °C in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS to maintain proliferation. To induce differentiation, cells were transferred to 37 °C and cultured for 10 days before subsequent experiments. Differentiated podocytes were exposed to eight commonly used injury stimuli: ADR (0.4µM; MCE, HY-15142) [48], ANG II (100ng/ml; MCE, HY-13948) [48], glucose (30mmol/L; MCE, HY-B0389) [49], IL-1β (50 ng/ml; MCE, HY-P7028) [50], LPS (25 µg/ml; MCE, HY-D1056) [51], PA (750 µM; MCE, HY-N0830) [52], TGF-β (5 ng/mL; MCE, HY-P70543) [53], TNF-α (50ng/ml; MCE, HY-P7058) [10], and, to model diverse stress conditions. Cell viability was calculated as the percentage of unstained (viable) cells relative to total cells. Cell viability was further quantified using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (KeyGEN BioTECH, KGA9310-500). siRNAs targeting the genes of interest were purchased from Obio Technology (Shanghai, China, Table S7). Cells were transfected at approximately 50% confluency according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 h of transfection, cells were treated with or without ADR for subsequent experiments.

snRNA-seq

Nuclei were isolated from human kidney tissues using a commercial nuclei isolation kit (Shbio, 52009-10). Single-nucleus suspensions were processed using the 10x Genomics Chromium platform (Chromium Chip K) to generate barcoded libraries following the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis, amplification, and library preparation were performed according to standard protocols. Library quality was assessed using Qubit and an Agilent Bioanalyzer, and sequencing was performed on an Illumina platform with paired-end reads.

Bulk-RNA seq

Podocytes treated with ADR for different durations were collected for bulk RNA sequencing. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal V10 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Vazyme, NR606-01/02) following the manufacturer’s protocol and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads.

CRISPR screening and library preparation

Podocytes were transduced with a genome-wide human CRISPR knockout pooled library (Ubigene, LIBR-H001A-P100), which targets 19,050 protein-coding genes with three sgRNAs per gene, to generate a stable knockout population. Detailed library information is available from the manufacturer’s website. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from cell pellets using a commercial kit (Vazyme, DC112) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. sgRNA sequences were amplified from gDNA by PCR using primers compatible with the lentiviral library vector. PCR products were purified and used for next-generation sequencing library preparation. Libraries were sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform with a paired-end 150 bp (PE150) sequencing strategy, and sgRNA abundance was quantified based on sequencing reads for downstream enrichment and depletion analysis.

RT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from tissues or cells using a commercial extraction kit. cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of RNA using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green chemistry on a QuantStudio 6 Flex system (Applied Biosystems). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Detailed procedures are provided in the Supplementary Methods, and primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 7.

Apoptosis analysis by flow cytometry

Treated cells were collected and stained with an Annexin V-APC/7-AAD Apoptosis Detection Kit (Lianke, AP105) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were analyzed on a Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX flow cytometer, and the data were processed using FlowJo software (version 10.9). Because ADR exhibits intrinsic fluorescence that partially overlaps with the 7-AAD channel, cells were washed with PBS before acquisition to remove surface-associated ADR. Despite this step, residual intracellular ADR fluorescence remained; therefore, fluorescence compensation was applied during flow cytometric analysis.

Bioinformatic and statistical analyses

All bioinformatics analyses were performed using R (v4.3.0) and Python (v3.10), and detailed computational procedures are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Experimental data are presented as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using two-tailed Student’s t-tests. For comparisons among multiple groups, ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. Graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism (Version 9.5). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
