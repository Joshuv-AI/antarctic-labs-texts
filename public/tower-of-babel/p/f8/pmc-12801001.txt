All-Arthroscopic Reconstruction of the Medial Patellofemoral Ligament Using Artificial Ligament for Recurrent Patellar Dislocation
Authors: Zirong Huang, Wenhan Yang, Yubo Niu, Hanyu Yao, Wenzhe Feng, Yankan Ou, Weimin Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Medial patellofemoral ligament injury is the primary stability barrier after patellar dislocation, with high recurrence rates with conservative treatment, making reconstruction surgery increasingly mainstream. This article describes an all-arthroscopic technique for medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction using high-strength synthetic suture tape combined with suture anchors, eliminating the need for tendon harvesting. It involves anatomic localization of the patellar and femoral insertion sites, with appropriate tensioning and fixation at 30° of knee flexion to restore medial stability. This method shortens operative time and avoids donor-site complications (e.g. neurovascular injury, hematoma, and pain) and patellar fracture risks. Biomechanical studies show that the tensile strength of synthetic suture tape is comparable to that of autologous tendons, with clinical outcomes showing low recurrence rates and good functional recovery, making this method particularly suitable for children, patients with connective tissue disorders, or cases of multiple surgical failures. As a safe and efficient alternative, this technique warrants further clinical validation and implementation.

Patellar instability, encompassing dislocation and subluxation, remains a prevalent orthopaedic challenge, with an annual incidence of 23.2 per 100,000 person-years overall and up to 147.7 per 100,000 in adolescents aged 14 to 18 years.1,2 Recurrent dislocations affect 17% to 22.7% of patients after a primary event, rising to 63% with conservative treatment alone, and are associated with medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) rupture in nearly all cases.3 The MPFL serves as the primary soft-tissue restraint, contributing 50% to 60% of the medial stabilizing force against lateral patellar translation from 0° to 30° of knee flexion.4 Initial management typically involves nonoperative strategies such as physical therapy, bracing, and vastus medialis obliquus strengthening to reduce swelling, improve range of motion, and enhance gluteal activation.5 However, these approaches often fail in patients with risk factors such as patella alta, trochlear dysplasia, increased tibial tuberosity–trochlear groove distance, or open physes, leading to recurrent instability, patellofemoral osteoarthritis, and functional decline.6,7

Surgical intervention is indicated for recurrent dislocations, osteochondral injuries, or loose bodies, with MPFL reconstruction (MPFLR) emerging as a cornerstone treatment, often combined with bony procedures such as tibial tuberosity transfer or trochleoplasty.8 A variety of techniques exist, including repair, reconstruction with autografts (e.g. semitendinosus or gracilis tendons), allografts, or synthetic materials, using fixation methods such as interference screws, suture anchors, or through-tunnel approaches.9 Although autografts yield favorable outcomes with redislocation rates of 0% to 4%, they are associated with complications including donor-site morbidity (e.g. neurovascular injury, hematoma, or pain), patellar fracture, infection, stiffness, and potential graft stretching over time, affecting up to 26.1% of cases.10,11 Allografts mitigate some risks but introduce concerns of cost, availability, and immunogenicity.12

Recent advancements highlight synthetic suture tape, such as ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene with a braided polyester jacket, as a viable alternative.13 Biomechanical studies show that its tensile strength (937 ± 93 N) surpasses that required for MPFL function, and clinical reports indicate comparable or superior outcomes to autografts, with reduced recurrence and no need for tendon harvesting—thus minimizing operative time and donor-site risks.14,15 Systematic reviews confirm that synthetic grafts offer reliable stability for primary or recurrent patellofemoral instability, particularly in challenging populations such as children with connective tissue disorders, patients with generalized hypermobility, or cases of prior surgical failure, in whom autologous options may be suboptimal.16,17

We present a simple, all-arthroscopic technique for MPFLR using high-strength synthetic suture tape with knotless anchors. This approach addresses MPFL laxity anatomically, restores patellar stability, and aligns with evolving evidence favoring synthetic materials for improved safety and efficacy. By detailing the surgical steps, indications, and potential advantages, we aim to provide orthopaedic surgeons with a reproducible method to enhance patient outcomes in patellar instability management.

Surgical Technique

Step 1: Patient Positioning

The patient undergoes combined spinal-epidural anesthesia and is positioned supine (Video 1). The affected limb is positioned to allow easy figure-of-4 positioning of the knee for femoral tunnel preparation and to allow the knee to hang off the bed edge for intraoperative manipulation.

Step 2: Graft Preparation

A 6-mm universal artificial ligament (Movmedix) is selected (Fig 1). As the original ligament has a traction suture on only one end and is relatively thin, both ends are braided with high-strength sutures over a 1.5-cm length. Sutures of different colors are used for easy differentiation. The ligament is folded in half, and the midpoint is marked with a surgical pen for reference.Fig 1Graft preparation. Step 3: Patellar Insertion Creation Using Socket-Anchor Technique

With assistance, the operative knee is flexed to 30° and stabilized to position the patella within the trochlear groove. The patella is grasped with one hand, and a 1-cm incision is made at the mid-superior patella. To assess patellar thickness and guide positioning, the surgeon first uses the tip of a Kirschner wire (K-wire) to sequentially touch the inferior and superior surfaces of the patella to directly appreciate the cortical spacing. To minimize the risk of tension-type patellar fracture, the medial patellar socket is positioned within the posterior half of the patellar thickness and the target is centered on the junction of the middle and posterior halves in the axial plane. Then, a 2.0-mm K-wire is inserted as a guide (Fig 2A) to a depth of approximately 2 to 3 cm, keeping the drilling trajectory parallel to the patellar articular surface with a slight posterior bias to avoid breaching the anterior cortex. After K-wire placement, the surgeon should ensure that the wire tip is not palpable at the skin surface and perform a patellar grind test; if no frictional sensation suggestive of wire penetration into the articular surface is detected, the position is deemed satisfactory. A 5-mm-deep socket is created using a 5-mm cannulated drill. The K-wire is removed, and a tap corresponding to the anchor size is inserted. One suture anchor is screwed in with trailing sutures (Fig 2B). The sutures are pulled to confirm patellar mobility in the direction of pull without anchor extrusion, indicating satisfactory fixation (Fig 2C).Fig 2Patellar-side socket-anchor technique. Step 4: Femoral Guidewire Placement

With the knee fixed at 30° of flexion, the adductor tubercle on the medial femoral epicondyle is palpated. A small incision is made, and a 2.0-mm K-wire is inserted as an arthroscopic landmark point (Fig 2D).

Step 5: Establishment of Arthroscopic Portals

A 5-mm medial portal is created at the depression slightly lateral to the space between the medial femoral condyle and medial tibial plateau. A straight clamp is inserted toward the lateral femoral condyle guidewire to establish a deep subcutaneous tunnel (Fig 3A). Step 6: Exposure of Femoral Anatomic Landmarks

A 70° arthroscope is inserted through the medial portal, and a shaver is inserted through the patellar portal. Centering on the medial femoral condyle guidewire, the surgeon gradually dissects the deep fascia circumferentially, alternating with radiofrequency for hemostasis. The deep surface of the vastus medialis obliquus is exposed to aid in identifying the adductor magnus (Fig 4A). The adductor magnus is followed to its insertion at the adductor tubercle on the medial femoral epicondyle (Fig 4B), and the medial collateral ligament insertion is exposed (Fig 4C). The femoral attachment for MPFLR is the intersection of the adductor magnus course and the horizontal line from the medial collateral ligament insertion. This point is marked with radiofrequency coagulation and verified by switching portals. The 2.0-mm K-wire at the medial epicondyle is adjusted to position its tip at the marked point, drilling perpendicular to the femur. Step 7: Patellar-Side Graft Fixation

The 2 trailing anchor sutures are sutured to the midpoint of the artificial ligament (Fig 5A). The opposite ends of the sutures are pulled to slide the ligament into the patellar socket under tension (socket technique), and knots are tied for fixation. The process is repeated with the second suture (Fig 5B). Curved forceps are used through the patellar portal to retrieve the looped guidewire. The sutures at both ends of the braided artificial ligament are threaded into the guidewire loop, and the lateral guidewire is pulled to advance the ligament through the subcutaneous tunnel and femoral tunnel. Step 8: Graft Tensioning and Fixation

The arthroscope is inserted through the medial portal into the joint cavity. The graft is tensioned by pulling both ends while monitoring patellar tracking during knee flexion-extension. The surgeon should ensure that the patella tracks within the trochlear groove without impingement on the medial condyle or dislocation and should confirm full range of motion. The arthroscope is withdrawn to the medial subcutaneous tunnel, and a guide pin is inserted into the femoral tunnel and secured with an interference screw.

Step 9: Lateral Retinacular Release

The lateral portal is established at the lateral soft spot of the patellar tendon for optimal visualization and working space. The surgeon bluntly dissects subcutaneous tissue with scissors and transects the lateral retinaculum, using radiofrequency under arthroscopic guidance for thorough release (Fig 5C). Hemostasis in the joint and subcutaneous fascial layers is achieved with radiofrequency (Fig 5D). Postoperatively, compressive cotton padding is applied to the medial and lateral patella to minimize subcutaneous hematoma formation. Postoperative Rehabilitation

On postoperative day 2, the knee is braced at 0° of extension and full weight-bearing ambulation is allowed. Progressive knee flexion exercises are initiated, advancing to 90° within 2 weeks. The brace is removed at 1 month for unrestricted activities. Jogging and swimming are resumed at 3 months, with a return to competitive sports at 6 months.

Discussion

The evolution of MPFLR techniques has highlighted several limitations in traditional femoral tunnel positioning methods, such as the Schöttle radiographic method and the saddle-region palpation approach. The Schöttle method relies on fluoroscopic imaging to identify the femoral insertion point based on anatomic landmarks such as the adductor tubercle and medial epicondyle, but it is prone to inaccuracies due to variations in patient anatomy, radiographic projection errors, and challenges in obese patients or those with trochlear dysplasia.18,19 Similarly, the saddle-region palpation technique, which involves manual identification of the “saddle” between the adductor tubercle and medial epicondyle, introduces subjectivity and operator dependency, potentially leading to nonanatomic placement. These inaccuracies can result in complications such as tunnel malposition, altered patellar tracking, overconstraint of the patella, and increased risk of medial patellofemoral joint overload or early osteoarthritis.9 In our all-arthroscopic approach, we address these drawbacks by prioritizing direct visualization, which may improve precision and reduce revision rates.

Compared with the traditional Schöttle positioning method, our technique emphasizes arthroscopic identification of anatomic landmarks for femoral tunnel placement, which minimizes surgery-related complications arising from erroneous positioning. By directly visualizing the MPFL native femoral insertion site under arthroscopy—typically located at the intersection of the adductor muscle extension line and the horizontal line from the femoral insertion of the medial collateral ligament—we achieve more reliable anatomic reconstruction without relying on intraoperative fluoroscopy, thereby reducing radiation exposure and operative time.20 Studies have shown that malpositioned femoral tunnels, common in radiographic methods, can lead to graft isometry issues, resulting in complications such as persistent instability or patellar fracture.10,11 In contrast, arthroscopic guidance allows for real-time adjustment based on soft-tissue landmarks and knee kinematics, potentially decreasing these risks. Preliminary data from similar minimally invasive techniques indicate improved patient outcomes, with lower complication rates and better restoration of patellar stability.21

The use of artificial ligaments, such as high-strength synthetic suture tape (e.g. FiberTape; Arthrex), provides superior initial reduction tension compared with autografts, mitigating ligament laxity due to non-isometric behavior during knee motion while eliminating donor-site complications. Biomechanical analyses show that synthetic tapes offer tensile strengths exceeding 900 N, surpassing the native MPFL requirements and reducing the risk of elongation or failure under cyclic loading.13,14 This contrasts with autografts such as semitendinosus tendons, which may stretch over time because of viscoelastic properties, leading to recurrent instability in 0% to 4% of cases.8 Additionally, by avoiding tendon harvesting, our method circumvents donor-site morbidity, including neurovascular injury, hematoma, pain, and infection, which affects up to 26.1% of autograft procedures.22 Clinical reports support these advantages, showing comparable or superior functional scores and low recurrence in diverse populations, including adolescents and patients with connective tissue disorders.15

Lateral retinacular release remains a controversial aspect of MPFLR, yet in the context of robust medial fixation, it provides a margin of error for optimizing patellar tracking. Proponents argue that releasing the lateral retinaculum addresses tight lateral structures contributing to instability, particularly in cases with an increased tibial tuberosity–trochlear groove distance or patella alta, potentially improving outcomes without increasing complications.23 However, critics highlight risks such as medial subluxation or over-release leading to instability, with some studies reporting no added benefit in isolated MPFL procedures.24 In our technique, the strong medial stabilization afforded by synthetic tape and suture anchors creates a forgiving environment, allowing selective lateral release to correct maltracking while minimizing adverse effects. This approach aligns with evidence from systematic reviews suggesting that combined medial reconstruction with judicious lateral release yields recurrence rates as low as 2% to 5% and enhanced patient satisfaction, especially in recurrent or complex cases.17,25

In summary, this all-arthroscopic MPFLR with synthetic suture tape and suture anchors represents a refined, efficient alternative that addresses key limitations of traditional methods. Future prospective studies are needed to validate long-term outcomes in larger cohorts.
