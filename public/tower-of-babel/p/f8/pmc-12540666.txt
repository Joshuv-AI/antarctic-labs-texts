Socioeconomic disparities impacting breast cancer care and survival in Iran
Authors: Mojtaba Vand Rajabpour, Saeed Nemati, Monireh Sadat Seyyedsalehi, Azin Nahvijou, Sepideh Abdi, Maryam Garousi, Ramesh Omranipour, Alireza Abdollahi, Mahdi Aghili, Ali Motlagh, Shaghayegh Haghjooy Javanmad, Kazem Zendehdel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women. However, individuals with a lower socioeconomic status (SES) tend to experience poorer prognosis. We aimed to study disparities in diagnosis, treatment, quality of care, and survival by SES in Iran. Data from breast cancer patients at the Cancer Institute of Iran between 2014 and 2016, registered in the clinical breast cancer registry of Iran (CBCR-IR), were used. Clinical data were obtained from CBCR-IR, while follow-up data and information on SES, including 10 household assets and education level, were gathered through telephone interviews. The primary outcome was survival, determined through active follow-up. Additionally, we studied delays in treatment and the quality of care based on European Society of Breast Cancer Specialists (EUSOMA) standards. A Cox proportional hazard model was used to investigate the association between SES and survival in women with breast cancer. The inequality in breast cancer survival was analyzed using the Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition model. The 5-year overall survival rates were 87.7% (95% CI: 83.9–90.7) for low SES patients, 90.2% (95% CI: 86.7–92.8) for mid SES, and 92.1% (95% CI: 89.0–94.3) for high SES patients (log-rank test P-value = 0.077). A 3.3% gap in breast cancer mortality was observed between high and low SES patients (95% CI: 1.3–5.5), with the model explaining 2.2% of this difference. Differences in delay in treatment and stage at diagnosis accounted for 0.5% (95% CI: 0.2–0.7) and 1.8% (95% CI: 1.2–2.3) of the disparity. Breast cancer mortality is higher in low SES patients, primarily due to late diagnosis and delays in treatment initiation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20638-x.

Introduction

Breast cancer, as the leading cancer among women, accounted for over 2 million new cases and nearly 700,000 cancer-related deaths worldwide in 20201. The majority of breast cancer-related deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where the incidence of breast cancer is on the rise2. The same socioeconomic disparity has been evident in Iran, where patients in more developed provinces with a high Human Development Index (HDI) have shown improved survival compared to those in provinces with lower HDI3. In an ecologic study utilizing nine population-based cancer registries in Iran, more than 13% gap in the 5-year survival rate of breast cancer was identified between provinces with high and low HDI3. Various individual and health system-related factors, such as higher comorbidities, lower cancer awareness, limited access to screening services, diagnoses at advanced stages, challenges in access to diagnostic centers and conventional treatment, contribute to the elevated breast cancer mortality observed in individuals with lower socio-economic status (SES) and those residing in more deprived areas4–7.

Disparities in the diagnosis and treatment of cancer significantly impact the survival outcomes of women diagnosed with breast cancer8. It is evident that women with higher SES are diagnosed at earlier stages and have better access to treatment, which contributes to their improved survival rates compared to women from more deprived backgrounds9–11. Despite the significance of these issues, there is limited evidence on the impact of socioeconomic inequality on the diagnosis, treatment, and survival of breast cancer in Iran. The previous study conducted in Iran was ecological, limiting our ability to interpret the findings at an individual level3. Furthermore, previous studies have predominantly focused on survival outcomes, with comprehensive research covering various aspects of breast cancer—including diagnosis, treatment delays, treatment quality, and survival—remaining unexplored. Quantifying socioeconomic disparities related to delays in diagnosis and treatment, the quality of treatment provided, and ultimately survival outcomes is a crucial first step in addressing any existing inequalities. It is also necessary to identify the role of each contributing factor in the observed disparities. The present study made a comprehensive effort to measure socio-economic inequality across various aspects of breast cancer management. This included examining delays in the initiation of treatment, the quality of care provided, and, ultimately, the survival outcomes of Iranian women diagnosed with breast cancer. The study’s objective was to quantify socio-economic inequality concerning breast cancer survival and to break down this observed inequality into its contributing factors, utilizing data from a hospital-based cancer registry in Tehran.

Material and methods

Study participants

The present study was conducted based on patients diagnosed with breast cancer between 2014 and 2020 and recruited from the Cancer Institute to the Clinical Breast Cancer Registry of Iran (CBCR-IR)12. The primary diagnosis of the recruited patients was ductal/lobular carcinoma infiltrative/invasive. Data were extracted (overall N = 3291) from the CBCR-IR. Patients aged over 79 and those with distant metastasis at the diagnosis were excluded from the study (N = 10)12. We also excluded cases without SES information (N = 1817), those who censored before the end of follow-up(N = 7), and those with missing diagnosis date (N = 64). Additionally, the study protocol underwent ethical review and was approved by the Ethics Committee at Tehran University of Medical Sciences (Ethics code: IR.TUMS.VCR.REC.1398.1015).

Clinical data and patient’s follow-up

Clinical data for each patient were extracted retrospectively from their medical records and subsequently updated during their follow-up phone calls. We gathered data on age, stage at diagnosis, year of diagnosis, education level, and molecular subtype which represents the patient’s status concerning estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) and KI67 protein.

SES information

We performed telephone interview we collected information about the SES using a questionnaire that comprised information about the patients assets including ownership of side-by-side freezer, microwave, TV, personal computers, dishwasher, vacuum cleaner, and washing machine, access to the internet, laptop. In addition, we collected information about the insurance coverage. Subsequently, we conducted a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to transform the dataset of assets into a new composite variable representing the socioeconomic status of each patient. The composite variable also incorporated education level. We utilized the coefficients of the first component of PCA, responsible for 33.7% of the overall variance. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 78.1%, and the Bartlett test of sphericity yielded a statistically significant result (P-value < 0.001). Subsequently, patients were categorized into three tertiles—low, mid, and high SES—based on their scores derived from the PCA analysis. All study participants provided verbal consent before the interview.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was vital status, and we conducted phone-based active follow-ups to determine the outcome. Patients or their families were contacted after fifth years from their cancer diagnosis to gather information on the vital status of the patients. All follow-up processes were meticulously carried out by trained interviewers. The secondary outcomes included delay in treatment initiation and the quality of care. Delay in treatment initiation was defined as the duration between cancer diagnosis and the date of surgery. To assess the quality of care, we employed the 21 indices introduced by European Society of Breast Cancer Specialists (EUSOMA) in 201713. High-quality care was defined as adherence to 80% or more of the investigated indexes, while any value less than 80% was considered indicative of low-quality care14. The term "delay in treatment" was defined as a gap of more than 60 days between the cancer diagnosis and the day of surgery. Patients undergoing surgery more than 60 days after their cancer diagnosis were classified as experiencing delayed treatment8.

Statistical analysis

Survival probabilities were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier approach, and the log-rank test assessed the differences in survival estimates across SES groups. Subsequently, a simple Cox proportional hazard model was employed to investigate the association between SES and overall mortality in breast cancer women. This association was further explored after adjusting for potential confounders, such as stage at diagnosis, age, molecular subtype, and year of diagnosis, in a multivariate Cox proportional hazard model. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using the Schoenfeld residuals global test. Any covariate that violated this assumption was included in the Cox regression model as a time-varying covariate.

To examine the association between delay in treatment and quality of care, we utilized two separate logistic regression models. Initially, the association was investigated in a simple model, and then adjustments for potential confounders were made in a multiple logistic regression model.

Finally, we investigated the disparity of different SES groups in overall mortality of breast cancer patients and identified the drivers of this inequality using the Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition model. This model divides the difference between high and low SES groups into two distinct parts known as the explained part and the unexplained part. The explained part quantifies the extent to which differences in the outcome between the compared groups can be attributed to variations in the mean of the variable x, which was introduced into the model as a covariate. Meanwhile, in the unexplained part, we aim to understand how the differing effects of each independent variable in the compared groups contribute to the observed difference in the investigated outcome. Results

Discussion

The study aimed to evaluate socio-economic inequalities in breast cancer survival, treatment initiation, and the quality of care using data from a hospital-based cancer registry in Iran. Surprisingly, our observations revealed no significant difference in the quality of care between more and less deprived groups. However, we observed a discernible delay in treatment and poorer survival outcomes in the low SES group compared to their high SES counterparts. Furthermore, the low SES group exhibited a higher probability of being diagnosed at more advanced stages. Our decomposition model shed light on the factors contributing to the observed gap in breast cancer mortality between high and low SES groups and showed that late diagnosis and delayed treatment jointly accounted for 66.6% of the observed inequality.

The overall 5-year survival rate was higher (87.7%) in the high SES group compared to the low SES group (82.4%). The primary factor contributing to this inequality was the stage at diagnosis, predominantly associated with diagnosis of the low SES group in a more advanced stage. Several factors contribute to this discrepancy, including limited access to financial resources, lower cancer awareness, and a lack of health insurance covering mammography expenses18. These issues result in lower mammography rates or follow-up on mammography, leading to diagnoses at more advanced stages in the low SES group19. One study conducted in Saudi Arabia revealed a lower uptake of breast cancer screening among more deprived women. Another study conducted in European countries, confirming these findings, demonstrated a reduced likelihood of breast cancer testing in women with lower SES20. The absence of a widespread screening program in Iran may intensify the disparity in mammography uptake in favor of women with higher SES. This disparity arises because the high SES women are generally more aware of the benefits of breast cancer screening and can afford the costs of opportunistic screening.

Delay in treatment was identified as another contributing factor explaining part of the observed socioeconomic inequality in breast cancer survival. Previous studies have reported a higher probability of treatment delay in patients residing in more deprived and low SES areas21,22. Financial issues and lack of awareness play the key roles in delaying cancer treatment in low SES patients21,22. The cost of cancer treatment can be prohibitive, imposing a significant financial burden on low SES families, especially in the absence of proper insurance—something more common among low SES women with breast cancer22. Low cancer awareness also contributes to delays in treatment, as it is associated with higher refusal and non-adherence to treatment23,24. A study conducted in the UK reported lower help-seeking behavior in low SES women with breast cancer20,25. Patients with lower SES generally have lower levels of education, which impairs their access to health information26. This lack of awareness affects their risk perception and increases the likelihood of delayed treatment. Furthermore, in communities like Iran, there is a positive attitude towards non-scientific traditional medicines, such as herbal medicine, for cancer treatment27. The current study was conducted in an educational referral health center that welcomes patients from across the country. According to a previously published study, 35% of the admitted patients in this center originated from provinces other than Tehran. These individuals had to travel more than 450 km because primary conventional care, such as chemotherapy or radiotherapy, was unavailable in their place of residence28. The necessity of traveling from other provinces and the need for accommodation in Tehran during prolonged treatments like chemotherapy impose a substantial financial burden. Consequently, these financial barriers could lead to delays in the initiation of treatment for such patients. It could also increase probability of incomplete treatment that could eventually affect their survival28.

While literature mentions provider bias as another explanation for survival inequality in breast cancer29, our observations found no significant difference in the quality of care provided between high and low SES groups. The concept of inequality in quality of provided care and access to the treatment strongly correlates to the setting14. For instance, a study conducted in China have shown significantly higher odds of receiving high-quality treatment in wealthier patients14. Meanwhile, another study in Italy has reported no disparity regarding access to good quality breast cancer care. One explanation for this finding is the low proportion of high-quality care for all patients30. The distribution of low-quality care appears to be uniform across SES groups, and we observed no disparity in this regard affecting the survival outcome of breast cancer patients.

The current study was a unique and comprehensive attempt to measure inequality in breast cancer diagnosis, delay in treatment, quality of provided care, and survival outcomes. We collected SES data on an individual level using a valid and reliable questionnaire. Furthermore, the study was conducted on a comprehensive sample of patients, representing breast cancer patients at a referral center in Tehran, with a prolonged course of follow-up. The inclusion of clinical data, such as stage at diagnosis and hormone receptor status, added strength to the study. However, it is essential to acknowledge some limitations in this study that should be considered in data interpretation. Data on certain potential confounders, such as comorbidities and residence provinces, were not collected. Additionally, there were missing values for a couple of other confounding factors like stage at diagnosis and hormone receptor status. To address this, we employed the MICE approach to manage the missing values. Another limitation of our study is that censoring was modestly associated with some variables related to quality of care (e.g., delay in treatment), which may introduce a degree of informative censoring; however, baseline clinical characteristics such as age, stage, and molecular subtype were largely balanced between censored and non-censored groups, suggesting minimal bias in outcome estimation.(Table S1).

Low external generalizability was another limitation of the current study, as we only recruited cases from one center in Tehran. Therefore, these findings cannot be generalized to the entire country. Being a single-center study might be one of the main potential reasons for the low inequality observed in the treatment and survival of women with breast cancer in this study. A multi-center study including patients from other centers in low-resource regions of Iran might increase the observed inequality in this regard.

In conclusion, the study revealed a moderately higher survival rate among breast cancer women with high SES compared to those with low SES. This difference was primarily attributed to late diagnosis and delays in treatment initiation. Importantly, the quality of care provided was equally low for all patients, and it did not contribute to the observed inequality in breast cancer survival. Further multi-center studies with bigger sample size are required.
