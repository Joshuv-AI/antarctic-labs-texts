Spatial proteomics reveals four-stage molecular evolution in cancer immunotherapy-related gastritis
Authors: Lei Shi, Zhongqiao Lin, Huishan Zhang, Yuping Lu, Zequn Sun, Bin Lan, Huang Xia, Shuimei Luo, Ling Chen, Zhida Wu, Yuyang Huang, Mengru Quan, Xuefeng Wang, Jing Lin, Yu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed cancer treatment, yet immune-related adverse events (irAEs) including immunotherapy-related gastritis (IRAEG) pose significant clinical challenges—often necessitating treatment interruption that may compromise antitumor efficacy. IRAEG presents with atypical symptoms, lacks specific biomarkers, and shows histopathological overlap with other forms of gastritis, complicating diagnosis and management. Despite increasing clinical recognition, a systematic understanding of spatial molecular alterations across the full disease course remains limited. Here, we used spatial proteomics to map the molecular landscape of IRAEG during disease progression and to define stage-specific patterns of molecular evolution relevant to cancer immunotherapy management.MethodsWe analyzed tissue samples from seven patients, including four non-immunotherapy-related gastritis controls and three cancer patients who developed IRAEG following ICI therapy for solid tumors, sampled longitudinally across four disease stages: baseline (G1), acute severe inflammation (G2), early recovery (G3), and complete recovery (G4). Using laser capture microdissection coupled with data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry, we profiled 177 spatially resolved gastric tissue regions. Multiplex immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence characterized features of the immune microenvironment, while Gene Ontology, KEGG pathway analysis, Gene Set Variation Analysis, and xCell inference enabled functional, metabolic, and immune profiling. Key immune and NET-related findings were further validated by multiplex immunofluorescence in an independent, expanded cohort of IRAEG and non-immunotherapy-related gastritis samples.ResultsIRAEG was characterized by widespread HLA molecule activation and enhanced antigen processing, resembling the immune phenotype observed in organ transplant rejection. The acute G2 stage exhibited excessive neutrophil extracellular trap formation, profound metabolic suppression, and collapse of immune homeostasis—features that may inform early intervention strategies to preserve ICI treatment continuity. During early recovery (G3), inflammatory injury transitioned toward repair, marked by activation of fatty acid metabolism and PPAR signaling. Notably, even at complete clinical recovery (G4), more than 1,000 proteins remained differentially expressed, reflecting sustained enhancement of metabolic and immune functions and establishing a distinct molecular "memory" state with implications for ICI rechallenge decisions.ConclusionsThese findings define four molecularly distinct stages of IRAEG progression and recovery. The stage-specific signatures identified here serve as candidate biomarkers for diagnosis, disease staging, and therapeutic response assessment, and may guide clinical decisions regarding irAE management, treatment modification, and safe ICI rechallenge to support continued antitumor therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12943-026-02737-z.Keywords: Cancer immunotherapy, Immunotherapy-related gastritis, Spatial proteomics, Immune-related adverse events, Molecular staging

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have fundamentally transformed cancer therapy by targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 pathways to restore T cell-mediated antitumor immunity [1–6]. As a result, ICIs are now widely used to treat melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and multiple other solid tumors [7–9]. However, by releasing immune inhibitory constraints, ICIs can also provoke immune-related adverse events (irAEs) that affect normal tissues [10–12]. Although most irAEs are mild, severe manifestations can be life-threatening, limit treatment continuity, and substantially impair patient quality of life [13]. Consequently, standardized strategies for recognizing and managing irAEs have become increasingly important as ICI use expands [14].

Among gastrointestinal irAEs, immunotherapy-related gastritis (IRAEG) remains particularly challenging [15–17],often necessitating ICI dose reduction or treatment interruption that may compromise antitumor efficacy. Clinical presentation is often subtle and heterogeneous, ranging from mild dyspepsia to severe gastrointestinal bleeding or perforation [18]. Histopathologically, IRAEG overlaps extensively with drug-induced gastritis, Helicobacter pylori-associated gastritis, and autoimmune gastritis [19], complicating accurate diagnosis. Therapeutic responses are similarly variable: while some patients respond rapidly to corticosteroids, others develop steroid dependence, resistance, or disease recurrence. These observations suggest that IRAEG encompasses distinct molecular states that are not adequately captured by conventional histology alone, highlighting the need for molecular-level characterization to guide treatment decisions in cancer patients.

Current models of IRAEG pathogenesis propose that ICI-induced T cell hyperactivation leads to inappropriate recognition of gastric self-antigens, potentially through molecular mimicry, resulting in autoimmune inflammation [20]. Host factors such as HLA genotype, genetic susceptibility, gut microbiota composition, and pre-existing gastric pathology likely modulate disease risk and severity. However, despite these insights, the dynamic molecular changes that occur during IRAEG onset, progression, and recovery remain poorly defined, particularly at the tissue and spatial levels.

Spatial proteomics, which integrates laser capture microdissection with high-resolution mass spectrometry, enables quantitative protein profiling while preserving tissue architecture and spatial context [21–23]. This approach has been successfully applied to studies of tumor heterogeneity, mechanisms of inflammatory disease, and therapeutic target discovery [24–26]. By capturing both molecular composition and spatial localization, spatial proteomics provides a powerful framework for dissecting dynamic tissue remodeling processes. To date, however, this strategy has not been systematically applied to IRAEG, and longitudinal, stage-resolved molecular analyses of the complete disease course are lacking.

In this study, we present the first comprehensive spatial proteomic characterization of IRAEG across its full clinical trajectory. By comparing IRAEG tissues with non-immunotherapy-related gastritis (NOTG) controls and longitudinally tracking individual patients from baseline through acute inflammation and recovery, we define a four-stage molecular progression model. Our data reveal an autoimmune-like molecular phenotype marked by broad HLA upregulation and enhanced antigen presentation, identify neutrophil extracellular trap formation as a central feature of acute tissue injury, and uncover profound, stage-specific metabolic reprogramming. Importantly, even after apparent clinical and histological resolution, IRAEG tissues retain a distinct molecular signature characterized by sustained activation of lipid metabolism and detoxification pathways, suggesting the establishment of a new homeostatic state rather than simple reversion to baseline. These findings provide a mechanistic framework for understanding IRAEG pathogenesis and recovery and offer a molecular basis for improving diagnosis, staging, and long-term management of this increasingly common complication of cancer immunotherapy.

Methods

Sample collection and processing

We collected 15 FFPE (formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded) tissue specimens comprising 177 distinct gastric tissue regions. Tissue sections were deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Stained slides were digitally scanned to identify and annotate the target region. All procedures involving human samples were approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Fujian Cancer Hospital (K2024-262–01). Raw mass spectrometry data files were processed through the iProteome cloud platform for database searching and quantitative analysis.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

FFPE tissue Sects. (4 μm thick) were deparaffinized in xylene (3 changes, 5 min each) and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 85%, 75% ethanol, 3 min each), followed by rinsing in distilled water. Sections were stained with hematoxylin solution for 3–5 min to visualize nuclei, then rinsed in running tap water for 5 min to develop the blue color. After a brief differentiation in 1% acid alcohol (1% HCl in 70% ethanol) for 10–30 s, sections were rinsed again in tap water and placed in Scott's tap water substitute or 0.2% ammonia water for 30 s to enhance nuclear staining (bluing). Following a second water rinse, sections were counterstained with eosin Y solution (0.5% aqueous or alcoholic eosin) for 30 s to 1 min to stain the cytoplasm and extracellular matrix. Sections were then dehydrated through graded ethanol (75%, 85%, 95%, 100%, 1 min each), cleared in xylene (3 changes, 2 min each), and mounted with neutral resin mounting medium. Stained slides were digitally scanned at 20 × magnification using a whole-slide imaging system for precise identification of the target region, pathological evaluation, and subsequent guidance for laser capture microdissection.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

To validate spatial proteomics findings, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) on key proteins. FFPE tissue Sects. (4 μm thick) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using a pressure cooker. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min, followed by blocking with 5% normal serum for 30 min at room temperature. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (see reagent list for details), then for 1 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Chromogenic detection was performed using the DAB substrate, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. PBS was used to replace primary antibodies in negative controls. Detailed antibody information is provided in Supplementary Materials.

Quantification of immunohistochemistry

Positive cell detection was performed using QuPath (version 0.7.0). For each whole-tissue region, cells were detected based on optical density, and positive cells were identified using the DAB optical density for each marker (CD3, CD4, CD8, CD20, PD-1, PD-L1). Cell density was calculated as the number of positive cells per unit area and compared between IRAEG and NOTG groups.

Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF)

FFPE Sects. (4 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval. Sequential multiplex staining was performed using tyramide signal amplification. Quantification was performed in QuPath as the positive area ratio for each marker; colocalization (CD4/FOXP3, CD66B/MPO/CitH3) was quantified as the colocalized positive area ratio.

Laser microdissection and protein extraction

Target regions on H&E-stained sections were precisely dissected and collected using laser capture microdissection (LCM). For each tissue section, regions of interest (ROIs) were sampled using a standardized grid (443.75 µm × 443.75 µm per unit; approximately 0.197 mm2 per unit); the spatial layout and size of all sampled ROIs are shown in Supplementary Figure S2. Collected tissues were processed using the FFomic method (PMID: 35,659,036) for lysis, decrosslinking, and in-gel digestion. Digested peptides were extracted with 50% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid, vacuum-dried, resuspended in 0.1% formic acid, purified using C18 desalting columns, and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis

Peptide separation was performed using an EASY-nLC 1200 liquid chromatography system with a nanoliter flow rate. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water; mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile. Samples were loaded onto a trap column (2 cm × 100 μm, 3 μm C18) and separated on an analytical column (15 cm × 150 μm, 1.9 μm C18) at 600 nL/min using gradient elution. Mass spectrometry was performed using an Orbitrap HFX mass spectrometer in positive-ion mode. Parent ion scanning ranged from 300–1400 m/z with MS1 resolution of 60,000. Data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode was employed with 30 DIA windows per full scan, MS2 resolution of 15,000, and HCD collision energy of 27%.

Protein identification and quantification

Raw mass spectrometry data files were processed through the iProteome cloud platform for database searching and quantitative analysis. Search parameters included trypsin digestion with a maximum of 2 missed cleavages; carbamidomethylation of cysteine as a fixed modification; oxidation of methionine and N-terminal acetylation as variable modifications; peptide mass tolerance of 20 ppm; and fragment ion mass tolerance of 0.05 Da. Peptide identification employed stringent quality control with FDR ≤ 0.05. The protein expression matrices are provided in Supplementary Tables 1–2.

Spatial proteomics data processing

Spatial location information was preserved for all identified proteins, with each collection point's protein expression profile linked to its spatial coordinates on the tissue section at 100–300 μm resolution. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to assess tissue heterogeneity. Unsupervised clustering analysis of collection points was performed using K-means and graph-based clustering algorithms, with the number of clusters (k) set to 2–5.

Differential protein analysis

Multiple statistical methods were used to identify differentially expressed proteins between spatial regions or cluster subgroups. For pairwise comparisons, Student's t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (for non-normally distributed data) was used to assess the statistical significance of differences in protein expression. For multi-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis’s test was applied, followed by Tukey's HSD or Dunn's post-hoc test for multiple comparison correction. Differential proteins were defined by fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67 (i.e., |log₂FC|≥ 0.585) and corrected P-value (q-value) < 0.05. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Differentially expressed proteins were visualized using volcano plots, with log₂ fold change (log₂FC) on the x-axis and -log₁₀(P-value) on the y-axis. Significantly upregulated and downregulated proteins were highlighted in pink and green, respectively.

Spatial expression projection analysis

Spatial distributions of key differential proteins, pathway activity scores, and cell infiltration scores were visualized using spatial projection heatmaps. Quantitative values from each spatial collection point were mapped back to their original coordinates on tissue sections, and pseudo-color heatmaps were generated using the ggplot2 package (version 3.4.4, RRID: SCR_014601). Color gradients represent protein expression levels, pathway activity, and cell infiltration scores, intuitively displaying spatial distribution patterns and regional heterogeneity of molecular features across tissue.

A detailed description of the other Materials and Methods used in this study is provided in the Supplementary Files.

Results

Immune checkpoint inhibitor-induced gastritis exhibits a distinctive T cell-dominant immune microenvironment

We analyzed gastric tissue samples from seven patients, including four non-immunotherapy-related gastritis controls (NOTG1-4; including Helicobacter pylori-associated gastritis) and three patients with solid tumors who developed immunotherapy-related gastritis (IRAEG1-3) after PD-1 inhibitor treatment (mucosal melanoma, renal cell carcinoma, and conjunctival melanoma) (Fig. 1A). All IRAEG patients developed gastritis after multiple cycles of anti-PD-1 therapy, had non-gastric primary tumors, and received corticosteroids alone or in combination with cytokine antagonists (detailed clinical characteristics and treatment courses are provided in Supplementary Figure S1). Endoscopic evaluation revealed more pronounced mucosal congestion, edema, and erosion in IRAEG than in NOTG. Consistent with these findings, H&E staining demonstrated extensive inflammatory infiltration, glandular architectural disruption, and widespread epithelial apoptosis in IRAEG tissues (Fig. 1B).

Multiplex immunohistochemistry revealed a distinct immune microenvironment in IRAEG compared with NOTG (Fig. 1B). CD3⁺ T cells showed higher infiltration density in all IRAEG cases, with a diffuse distribution across the lamina propria and epithelial compartments, representing a prominent immunological feature of IRAEG. In contrast, CD20⁺ B cells exhibited divergent patterns: NOTG controls (NOTG1 and NOTG2) showed dense infiltration with lymphoid follicle-like structures, whereas IRAEG samples demonstrated reduced B cell presence. CD4⁺ helper T cell density did not differ significantly between groups, suggesting that despite the overall increase in T cells, the CD4⁺ compartment remained relatively stable. By contrast, CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cell infiltration displayed notable interpatient heterogeneity, with high densities in NOTG1, IRAEG2, and IRAEG3, but lower levels in NOTG2 and IRAEG1, potentially reflecting differences in disease severity or progression stage. Notably, PD-1 and PD-L1 expression remained low in IRAEG gastric mucosa, despite systemic PD-1 blockade. Collectively, these findings suggest that IRAEG represents a T cell-dominant inflammatory condition characterized by increased CD3⁺ infiltration, variable CD8⁺ responses, and relative B cell depletion, with an immune composition distinct from NOTG. Quantification of marker densities confirmed these patterns, with higher CD3⁺ density and lower CD20⁺ density in IRAEG, comparable CD4⁺ density, and low PD-1/PD-L1 across groups (Fig. 1C). Given the limited number of patients in this initial analysis, individual variation may contribute to some of these patterns; we therefore validated the key immune populations by multiplex immunofluorescence with quantification in an expanded, independent cohort (Fig. 1D, E), which confirmed the higher CD3⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and the relative reduction of CD20⁺ B cells in IRAEG.

Spatial proteomics defines a distinct molecular identity and immune remodeling program in IRAEG

To delineate the molecular features underlying this immune phenotype, we established a spatial proteomic workflow that integrates laser capture microdissection with data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry. Using this approach, we analyzed four non-immunotherapy-related gastritis (NOTG) cases and three IRAEG patients sampled across four disease stages (G1-G4), generating spatially resolved proteomic profiles spanning the entire disease course (Fig. 2A-B). Each region of interest was sampled using a standardized grid and annotated by anatomical compartment based on the corresponding H&E images (Supplementary Figure S2, Supplementary Table S3). Quality control and principal component analyses showed clear separation between IRAEG and NOTG samples (Supplementary Figure S3A-C), confirming the robustness of disease-specific proteomic signatures.

Differential expression analysis identified 692 proteins that were significantly altered in IRAEG relative to NOTG, with a pronounced bias toward upregulation (606 upregulated versus 86 downregulated). This extreme activation pattern, with an approximately 7:1 ratio of upregulated to downregulated proteins, is unprecedented among gastritis subtypes and suggests that IRAEG represents a molecularly distinct inflammatory entity rather than a quantitative variant of chronic gastritis. Functional enrichment analysis showed that upregulated proteins were predominantly associated with immune activation, inflammatory signaling, and antigen processing pathways (Fig. 2C, E). Gene Ontology analysis also showed enrichment of RNA splicing and mRNA processing pathways in IRAEG (Fig. 2D).

Three core molecular features emerged from this analysis. First, IRAEG exhibited broad activation of antigen-recognition machinery, including marked upregulation of STAT1, classical MHC molecules (HLA-A, HLA-DPB1, HLA-DRA), and non-classical HLA molecules (HLA-E, HLA-G) (Fig. 2G, K, L). Such widespread activation of antigen presentation is rare in conventional gastritis and indicates a systemic enhancement of immune recognition (Supplementary Figure S3D). Second, IRAEG displayed coordinated pro-inflammatory signaling, with concurrent activation of IL-17 pathway components (e.g., VCAM1, S100A8, S100A7, MAPK, and NF-κB signaling molecules) and leukocyte transendothelial migration programs, providing a molecular basis for extensive immune cell infiltration (Supplementary Figure S3G-M). Third, GSVA revealed comprehensive metabolic reprogramming, with increased activity across glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, and amino acid and lipid metabolism (Fig. 2F, H), supporting the high energetic demands of sustained immune activation.

Consistent with these molecular signatures, xCell analysis inferred a pro-inflammatory immune composition characterized by increased CD8⁺ naïve T cells, Th1 cells, and M1 macrophages, and reduced regulatory T cells and mast cells (Fig. 2I, Supplementary Figure S3E-F). ROI-level niche analysis further revealed distinct cellular microenvironments between IRAEG and NOTG (Supplementary Figure S4). Together, these findings establish IRAEG as a rejection-like inflammatory state marked by hyperactivation of immune recognition, coordinated inflammatory signaling, metabolic upregulation, and loss of immunoregulatory balance, providing molecular insights into this increasingly recognized complication of cancer immunotherapy.

Acute IRAEG Is defined by NET overactivation, metabolic collapse, and immune homeostasis disruption

To characterize dynamic molecular changes during disease progression, we performed a longitudinal analysis of three IRAEG patients across four defined stages: baseline (G1), acute severe inflammation (G2), early recovery (G3), and complete recovery (G4) (Fig. 3A). Principal component analysis confirmed clear stage-specific molecular segregation (Supplementary Figure S5A-C). Unsupervised clustering identified six protein expression clusters with distinct temporal trajectories, corresponding to nucleotide metabolism, ATP and glucose metabolism, fatty acyl-CoA metabolism, oxidative stress handling, nucleotide sugar metabolism, and nitric oxide signaling (Supplementary Figure S5D-F).

Comparison of G2 with G1 revealed extensive molecular disruption, with differentially expressed proteins enriched for antimicrobial responses, cell killing, and tissue destruction (Fig. 3B-D). KEGG pathway analysis highlighted strong activation of neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation and glutathione metabolism (Fig. 3E). Indeed, the G2 stage was characterized by coordinated upregulation of 20 NET-associated proteins, including S100A8/A9, PADI4, CAMP, AZU1, and ELANE (Fig. 3F). Spatial projection showed widespread distribution of NET markers throughout the gastric mucosa during G2 (Fig. 3G-H), implicating NET-mediated injury as a dominant mechanism of acute tissue damage.

Concurrently, cellular metabolism was profoundly suppressed. GSVA analysis indicated that approximately 70% of metabolism-related pathways were significantly downregulated in G2, including ATP synthesis, glycolysis, and oxidative phosphorylation, whereas stress-associated detoxification pathways were selectively activated (Supplementary Figure S6A). Spatial analysis revealed marked differences in glycogen breakdown, gluconeogenesis, amino acid metabolism, nucleotide sugar metabolism, and carbohydrate processing between G1 and G2 (Supplementary Figure S6B). Immune composition analysis further demonstrated a collapse of immune homeostasis, with massive neutrophil infiltration and dysregulation of ion transport and gastric secretory proteins, including ATP4A, TFF2, MUC6, and SLC12A2 (Supplementary Figure S6C-I). We further validated NET overactivation at the histological level by multiplex immunofluorescence for CD66B, MPO, and citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3), comparing the baseline (G1) and acute severe (G2) stages. Compared with G1, G2 showed a significant increase in MPO and, most importantly, in the colocalization of these NET-defining markers (CD66B/MPO, CD66B/CitH3, and triple CD66B/MPO/CitH3), providing direct evidence of bona fide NET structures during acute IRAEG (Fig. 3J, K).

Together, these findings define the acute G2 phase as a state of excessive NET activation, metabolic exhaustion, and immune imbalance.

Early Recovery Is Marked by a Coordinated Shift from Inflammatory Injury to Metabolic Repair

To elucidate recovery mechanisms, we compared the early recovery stage (G3) with the acute severe stage (G2) (Fig. 4A). This transition was marked by broad molecular reprogramming, including downregulation of inflammatory mediators and induction of repair-associated proteins (Fig. 4B). Functional enrichment analyses revealed activation of detoxification, small-molecule metabolism, and redox homeostasis (Fig. 4C), while KEGG pathways were dominated by fatty acid metabolism, peroxisome function, and glutathione metabolism (Fig. 4D). GSVA confirmed suppression of inflammatory signaling and activation of DNA repair, lipid metabolism, and oxidative stress response pathways (Fig. 4E).

Notably, fatty acid metabolism and PPAR signaling emerged as central drivers of recovery. Key enzymes involved in β-oxidation and lipid handling (ACAA2, ACSL5, CPT1A) were significantly upregulated and spatially redistributed during G3 (Fig. 4F-H, Supplementary Figure S7A-B). Immune profiling showed partial restoration of immune balance, with reductions in neutrophils and eosinophils and recovery of M2 macrophages and regulatory populations (Fig. 4I-K, Supplementary Figure S7C). Comparison with the baseline further revealed that G3 represents a distinct metabolic state rather than simple normalization, characterized by enhanced lipid oxidation and detoxification capacities that exceed pre-disease levels (Supplementary Figure S7F-P).

Molecular and immune stabilization during transition from early to complete recovery

To define the transition from early to complete recovery, we compared G4 with G3 (Fig. 5A). In striking contrast to earlier transitions involving hundreds of differentially expressed proteins, only 56 proteins were significantly altered between these stages, with a marked bias toward downregulation (45 downregulated versus 11 upregulated), indicating that complete recovery is defined by molecular stabilization rather than continued reprogramming (Fig. 5B). Functional enrichment analysis supported this interpretation: GO analysis identified only 106 enriched biological processes centered on fatty acid metabolism and lipid catabolism, while KEGG analysis revealed 28 significant pathways dominated by PPAR signaling and peroxisome function (Fig. 5C-D). GSVA confirmed fine-tuning of lipid oxidation and cholesterol metabolism rather than large-scale metabolic reconstruction (Fig. 5E-F), and spatial analysis demonstrated coordinated expression patterns of CPT1A, fatty acid β-oxidation, and glycosaminoglycan metabolism pathways across tissue regions (Fig. 5G-H).

Importantly, immune profiling revealed near-complete stabilization between G4 and G3, with no significant differences across T cell subsets, innate immune cells, or adaptive immune populations, except for minor changes in CD4⁺ memory T cells and M1 macrophages (Fig. 5I-K). Together, these findings establish that complete recovery represents a state of molecular and immune stabilization, marking successful transition from active repair to restored homeostasis.

Complete recovery establishes a dual state of injury resolution and persistent molecular remodeling

To define complete recovery, we compared G4 with both G2 and G1. Compared with G2, G4 showed near-complete reversal of acute injury signatures, with downregulation of inflammatory and antigen-presenting proteins and restoration of pathways involved in wound healing, complement activation, cytoskeletal organization, and metabolic homeostasis (Fig. 6A-J). Immune profiling confirmed normalization of pro-inflammatory cell populations and recovery of regulatory immune components.

Unexpectedly, comparison of G4 with baseline revealed extensive molecular remodeling beyond pre-disease states. More than 1,000 proteins remained differentially expressed, with enrichment of pathways involved in nucleotide-sugar biosynthesis, mitochondrial function, lipid metabolism, and antigen processing (Fig. 6K-U). Immune composition analysis indicated a more mature and balanced immune state than in G1. These findings demonstrate that complete clinical recovery is accompanied by durable molecular reprogramming rather than full molecular reversion, establishing a new homeostatic state characterized by enhanced metabolic and immune capacity—a molecular "memory" that may inform decisions regarding safe ICI rechallenge.

IRAEG recovery proceeds through a staged molecular repair trajectory

Tracking abnormally expressed proteins from G2 through subsequent stages revealed a stepwise repair process (Figure S8A-E). Most proteins dysregulated during acute inflammation progressively normalized by G4, with early recovery restoring basic stress-response and metabolic proteins and late recovery reinstating complex structural and regulatory networks. Pathway-level analysis showed a similar staged pattern of restoration, with gradual normalization of inflammatory signaling followed by reactivation of developmental and repair pathways, some of which exceeded baseline activity (Figure S8F-G). Spatial analyses confirmed coordinated tissue-level reconstruction, delineating a structured molecular trajectory from acute injury to advanced repair (Figure S8H).

Discussion

NETs overactivation: a critical pathological feature of acute IRAEG

Using spatial proteomics, we identified significant neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) activation in IRAEG in cancer patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors. During the acute severe phase (G2), 20 NET-related proteins were coordinately upregulated and showed widespread gastric mucosal distribution. Compared with NOTG, IRAEG displayed distinct features, including massive CD3⁺ T cell infiltration with relative B cell depletion, broad activation of HLA-A and HLA-DRA, extreme molecular imbalance, and an organ transplant rejection-like phenotype. Together, these findings suggest that NET-mediated immunopathological injury is a key mechanism underlying acute IRAEG, reflecting a rapid, dysregulated immune effector response triggered by checkpoint blockade.

NETs are DNA–protein complexes released by activated neutrophils in response to infection or sterile inflammation [27–30]. Although initially considered host-protective, NETs have context-dependent pathogenic roles in immune-mediated diseases [31–34]. In systemic lupus erythematosus, NETs provide autoantigens and activate plasmacytoid dendritic cells [35, 36]; in rheumatoid arthritis, NET-associated citrullination promotes autoantibody formation [37, 38]; and in ANCA-associated vasculitis, NETs directly drive tissue damage and disease progression [39, 40]. These precedents support the biological plausibility that NET pathways contribute to tissue injury in IRAEG, although their involvement in immune-related adverse events during cancer immunotherapy has remained largely unexplored. Whether NET-targeting approaches could complement immunosuppressive therapy in IRAEG remains to be tested in future mechanistic studies.

Dual characteristics of metabolic reprogramming in IRAEG: activation, collapse, and repair

Beyond immune dysregulation, our data reveal a stage-dependent metabolic logic that underlies IRAEG progression in cancer patients receiving ICIs. At baseline, compared with NOTG, IRAEG exhibits broad activation of glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and lipid metabolism, consistent with heightened immunometabolic demand following checkpoint inhibition. However, longitudinal analysis uncovered a striking paradox: during the acute severe phase (G2), approximately 70% of core metabolic pathways were profoundly suppressed, including ATP synthesis, glucose metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation. This acute metabolic suppression was accompanied by concurrent activation of glutathione metabolism and amino acid pathways, suggesting compensatory stress responses.

Recovery from this state is not a passive return to baseline metabolism. Instead, the early recovery phase (G3) is marked by a coordinated shift toward lipid-centered metabolic programs, including fatty acid β-oxidation and PPAR signaling. This glucose-to-lipid metabolic transition aligns with established immunometabolic paradigms in which the resolution of inflammation is coupled to oxidative and lipid-based energy utilization [41, 42]. The therapeutic relevance of these stage-specific metabolic changes warrants future investigation.

The paradox of complete recovery: persistent molecular remodeling and “molecular memory”

A particularly unexpected finding of this study is the dissociation between clinical remission and molecular normalization in cancer patients recovering from ICI-induced gastritis. Despite near-complete resolution of symptoms, endoscopic findings, and histopathology at the complete recovery stage (G4), spatial proteomics revealed persistent differential expression of more than 1,000 proteins relative to baseline. This indicates that IRAEG recovery culminates not in molecular reversion but in the establishment of a new homeostatic state.

This persistent remodeling is characterized by sustained upregulation of lipid-metabolism enzymes such as ACSL5 and CPT1A [43, 44], along with enhanced detoxification and antigen-presenting programs. These persistent changes, which we refer to as a form of "molecular memory," are distinct from both acute inflammatory activation and early repair programs. Whether this molecular memory is protective or maladaptive, and whether it gradually normalizes over a longer timescale beyond the complete recovery stage or persists as a stable new equilibrium, remains an open question that warrants extended longitudinal follow-up and functional studies.

Clinically, these findings suggest that patients who appear fully recovered may remain in a state of molecular adaptation, underscoring the potential value of longer-term monitoring and molecular biomarkers to assess recovery quality.

Limitations and future directions

Although the cohort size is limited, the longitudinal, spatially resolved design provides high internal consistency and mechanistic resolution. Methodologically, the study's strength lies in its depth-first, longitudinal design, which enables high-resolution spatiotemporal dissection of IRAEG progression across the full disease course. By integrating spatial proteomics with immune and metabolic inference, we demonstrate that IRAEG is a dynamic, staged process rather than a static diagnosis.

The identified molecular markers, particularly HLA-related proteins, NET-associated components, and fatty acid metabolic enzymes, provide a foundation for developing molecular staging frameworks and precision diagnostic tools. Future multicenter studies will be required to validate their robustness and clinical utility across broader patient populations.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cohort size is limited by the rarity of longitudinal IRAEG sampling; however, the consistency of stage-specific signatures across patients supports the biological validity of the findings. As an exploratory study, it remains to be determined whether the identified molecular features are broadly generalizable or partly patient-specific, which will require validation in larger cohorts. Additionally, causal relationships between identified pathways and tissue injury cannot be inferred solely from observational profiling, underscoring the need for mechanistic validation in experimental models. Furthermore, all patients were of the same ethnicity and received corticosteroid treatment, and the heterogeneity in tumor type, age, and sex within this small cohort means that the potential influence of these clinical factors cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusions

In summary, this work establishes a molecular framework for understanding IRAEG as a staged immunometabolic disease, characterized by NET-driven acute injury, metabolic collapse and repair, and durable molecular remodeling that persists beyond clinical recovery. These insights provide a foundation for improving diagnosis, monitoring, and therapeutic intervention in immune checkpoint inhibitor-related gastritis and potentially other immune-related adverse events.
