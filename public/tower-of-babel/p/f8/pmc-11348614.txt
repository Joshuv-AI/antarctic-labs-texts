The somatic genome of Eptatretus okinoseanus reveals the adaptation to deep-sea oligotrophic environment
Authors: Mengke Shi, Qi Li, Yong Wang, Li-Sheng He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHagfishes are fascinating creatures that typically inhabit the deep sea. The deep sea is characterized by its lack of sunlight, primary productivity, and diminishing biomass with increasing ocean depth. Therefore, hagfishes living in this environment must develop effective survival strategies to adapt to the limited food supply. Deep-sea hagfishes have been observed to survive without food intake for up to one year. In this study, we have assembled a high-quality somatic genome of the deep-sea hagfish (Eptatretus okinoseanus) captured below 1,000 m. We compared the genome of E. okinoseanus with the genomes of inshore hagfish, lampreys, and other related species to investigate the genetic factors underlying the deep-sea hagfish adaptations to the environment.ResultsThe E. okinoseanus somatic genome was estimated to be 1.89 Gb and assembled into 17 pseudochromosomes. Phylogenetic analysis showed that shallow-sea and deep-sea hagfishes diverged approximately 58.8 million years ago. We found Perilipin gene family was significantly expanded in deep sea E. okinoseanus, which promotes triacylglycerol storage. Furthermore, a series of genes involved in fatty acid synthesis and metabolism, blood glucose regulation, and metabolic rate regulation were also expanded, rapid evolution or positive selection, and these changes contribute to their efficiency in energy utilization. Among these genes, the positively selected gene JNK may play an important role in energy metabolism. In addition, the olfactory receptors of the deep-sea hagfish were significantly expanded to 86, and three conserved motifs present only in hagfishes olfactory receptors were identified, which may facilitate the rapid localization of carrion.ConclusionsThis study provides valuable genomic resources for insights into the survival strategies of deep-sea hagfishes in oligotrophic environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10727-y.Keywords: Hagfish, Chromosome-level genome, Environment adaptation, Olfactory receptors

Background

Hagfishes and lampreys are distinct from jawed vertebrates, due to their lack of jaws, bones, and dentine [1]. They have been grouped together as cyclostomes [2], the sister group to gnathostomes. As part of the two remaining jawless fish lineages, hagfishes provide a unique comparative perspective on early vertebrate evolution. Hagfishes exhibit an eel-like body shape, unique in anatomy and physiology, and notably lack jaws, eyes, and fins. Morphological and molecular characteristics revealed that hagfishes comprise three subfamilies, Eptatretinae, Myxininae, and Rubicundinae, and 6 genera and 81 species. Their morphological differences are most evident in their branchial pores and horny teeth, which are often used to identify species. Eptatretinae and Rubicundinae have more than five pairs of pores, while Myxininae only have one pair [3].

Hagfishes typically inhabit in the deep sea, but Eptatretus burgeri is an exception as it lives in shallow waters. Other hagfishes, like E. okinoseanus, Eptatretus atami, and Eptatretus multidens, are found at varying depths ranging from 300 to 1020 m [4]. The deep sea is distinguished by its lack of sunlight and primary productivity [5], and the concentration of particulate organic carbon decreases with increasing ocean depth and distance from the land, resulting in a declining trend in biomass along the depth [6]. Hagfishes living in the deep-sea have to face the limited food environment and develop effective survival strategies to adapt to oligotrophic environments. Deep sea organisms mainly rely on their sense of smell in foraging. Therefore, the olfactory organ is an important part of deep-sea organisms [7]. Hagfishes have anterior nostrils located above the mouth, leading to the olfactory organs. The olfactory sensory epithelium is on the front of the olfactory bulb. Studies have found that hagfishes have a very sensitive sense of smell [8]. Hagfish have been observed to be excellent at localizing decaying carcasses; they are always the first to appear beside decaying bait, with the next fish arriving in twice as much time [9]. Besides, hagfishes are capable of prolonged fasting. Deep-sea hagfishes specializing in nekton falls rely more on prolonged fasting [10], as they may not frequently encounter nekton falls at their depth, while shallow hagfishes feed opportunistically on benthic fauna and do not rely as heavily on fasting [11]. In addition, hagfishes can turn energy into fat more efficiently after feeding, and they store large amounts of lipid droplets in their tissue cells that can be used as a source of energy when food is scarce [12]. Hagfishes can survive for up to one year without eating any food, relying on stored lipids [13]. Researches have shown that the hagfish that living survive only in deep waters have a much higher fat content than those that survive in shallow waters. For example, the muscle of Eptatretus deani, a deep-living species, comprised an average of 34% lipids (dry weight). The shallower species Eptatretus stoutii had a much lower lipid content in their muscle of 8% [14]. However, it is unclear what genomic changes occurred in the deep-sea hagfish as it adapted to the food-scarce deep-sea environment, as well as what adaptive evolution occurred in terms of lipid storage and metabolic mechanisms.

E. okinoseanus is distributed mainly in the northwest Pacific region, the Sea of Japan, and Taiwan. The body is dark purplish-brown in colour, cylindrical at the front and laterally flattened at the back, with eyes reduced to skin and eight gill holes on each side, widely spaced and arranged in a longitudinal line. It is currently found only in deep waters at depths of 300–1000 m, and is rare because of its deep habitat [15]. In this study, E. okinoseanus was collected (a depth of 1000 m) during an expedition in the South China Sea, we combined Illumina short reads, PacBio long reads, and Hi-C reads to construct the chromosomal-level somatic genome of E. okinoseanus and to analyse the genome evolution of E. okinoseanus and perform comparative genomic analysis with the inshore hagfish E. burgeri and other shallow-sea lampreys to elucidate the adaptation mechanisms of Cyclostomata to the deep-sea environment.

Results and discussion

Genome assembly

To estimate the somatic genome size of E. okinoseanus, a total of 88.94 Gb Illumina short reads were generated for genome survey analysis. K-mer analysis indicated that the somatic genome of E. okinoseanus is approximately 2.09 Gb in size, with a high proportion of repetitive sequences (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). For de novo assembly of the somatic genome of E. okinoseanus, 254.85 Gb of PacBio long reads were generated. After assembly, we obtained a somatic genome of size 1.89 Gb with a PacBio read coverage depth of 134.84 × . The genome contig N50 reached 2.77 Mb. The ratio of A, T, G and C bases was normal, and the percentage of GC was 43.82%, which is in the acceptable range (< 10%). The ratio of all small fragment reads to the genome was approximately 98.37%, and the coverage was approximately 98.02%, indicating that the reads and the assembled genomes were in good agreement. Finally, 200.17 Gb of HiC reads generated were used for chromosome construction. For haploid, in E. okinoseanus, the germline genome has 27 chromosomes, and after chromosome elimination, the somatic genome has 17 chromosomes. In this study, a total of 17 somatic chromosomes were anchored, which is consistent with the results of Kubota et al. [16]. The N50 of this chromosome-level genome was 110 Mb. The length of chromosomes ranged from 60.4 Mb to 172.1 Mb, with a mount rate of 95.58% (Fig. 1; Additional file 1: Fig. S2; Additional file 2: Table S1-7). We determined the completeness of the assembled genome by BUSCO. BUSCO analysis of the metazoan (obd10) gene set showed that the assembled E. okinoseanus somatic genome contained 92.70% of the total complete single-copy homologous genes. (Additional file 2: Table S8; Additional file 1: Fig. S3).

Structural characteristics of the E. okinoseanus somatic genome

The synteny analysis between E. okinoseanus D. rerio, B. floridae, E. burgeri, E. tridentatus, L. camtschaticum, L. reissneri, and P. marinus showed a low percentage of synteny conservation between Cyclostomata and Cephalochordata as well as between Osteichthyes, which could be attributed to their distant evolutionary distance. The percentages of collinear homologous pairs between the six species within Cyclostomata are relatively high, reaching approximately 60% within lampreys and ranging from 10–15% between the deep-sea hagfish E. okinoseanus and the four lampreys but only 5–9% between the inshore hagfish E. burgeri and the four lampreys, while the percentage of collinear homologous pairs between the deep-sea and inshore hagfishes was approximately 43.83% (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). This phenomenon may be related to the more extensive genomic rearrangements that exist between cyclostomata [17, 18]. Additionally, we conducted a comparative analysis of collinearity between Eptatretus atami and E. okinoseanus. We found a strong collinearity relationship between the two species, indicating a conserved chromosome evolution since the divergence of the two species, and showing the high quality of the assembled genome as well as the accuracy of the genome annotation of E. okinoseanus. But large size inversions were found in chromosomes 7, 12, and 14, suggesting structural variations between the two species of hagfish, and that may be related to the differences in phenotype or adaptation to the environment. The mechanism of variation formation and possible functions require further study (Fig. 2).

Hox functions in the development of organisms, play a crucial role in determining the structure of the body axis in the early stages of embryonic development in bilaterian animals. Hox genes are usually clustered in the same genomic region, and the duplication of the number of Hox clusters is often used to characterize whole-genome duplication (WGD) events during the evolution of invertebrates to vertebrates [19]. In this study, six Hox gene clusters containing a total of 39 Hox genes were found in the genome of E. okinoseanus. We referenced the ordering of the Hox gene clusters by E. burgeri. The genome of E. burgeri [18] and E. atami both have 40 Hox genes [17]. We found that E. okinoseanus lacks Hox-α11 compared to E. burgeri and E. atami. Hox-α11 was not seen by direct TBLASTN or by examining the region of the E. okinoseanus genome between the Hox-α9 and Hspbb gene (Hspbb is the closest gene upstream to Hox-α11 in E. burgeri). This difference may be due to the misassembly of the E. okinoseanus genome or to gene loss that occurred during the evolution of the E. okinoseanus genome. (Additional file 2: Table S9; Additional file 1: Fig. S5).

Genome annotation

Among the species in Cyclostomata, the genome size of hagfishes is larger than that of lampreys [20] (Additional file 2: Table S10). Through the identification of repetitive elements, E. okinoseanus repetitive elements consist mainly of transposable elements (TEs), which account for 66.10% of its genome size, with DNA transposons (5.90% of the genome), long interspersed elements (LINEs; 7.26% of the genome), short interspersed elements (SINEs; 0.02%), long terminal repeats (LTRs; 59.40%), and unknown TEs 3.01% (Additional file 2: Table S11; Additional file 1: Fig. S6). The statistical results show that the percentage of each type of TE in the genome is not significantly different between lampreys [21], but the percentage of LTRs in the genome of E. okinoseanus is significantly higher than that of lampreys, reaching 59.40%, which could explain hagfishes' larger genome size. Our analysis of the E. okinoseanus genome revealed the presence of 2.15 Mb of noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs), which include microRNAs (miRNAs), ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs), small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), and transfer RNAs (tRNAs). These ncRNAs constitute approximately 0.11% of the genome assembly (Additional file 2: Table S12). A total of 20,518 protein-coding genes were predicted and annotated in the E. okinoseanus genome (Additional file 2: Table S13). A total of 97.5% of these protein-coding genes were functionally annotated (Additional file 2: Table S14; Additional file 1: Fig. S7).

Phylogenetic analysis

To study the evolutionary history of E. okinoseanus, an ML phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using 482 single-copy orthologues of E. okinoseanus with 12 other species, including Cyclostomata, Hemichordata, Cephalochordata, Urochordate, Osteichthyes, and Chondrichthyes. Our results showed that hagfish and lamprey clustered together as a clade, supporting a monophyletic group of Cyclostomata [17, 18]. The divergence of hagfish and lamprey was estimated to have occurred approximately 461 million years ago (Fig. 3A and B), around the Ordovician period, a time similar to that inferred by cDNA [22], and is similar to the findings of Yu et al. [18]. The divergence between deep-sea and inshore hagfish occurred 58.8 million years ago, during the Paleocene period, before the divergence of the four species of lamprey was 21.8 million years ago.

Comparative genomics

For genomic comparison, the gene families of these 13 selected species were analysed. 18,789 gene families were constructed in total and 7423 gene families were shared in Chordates, Cephalochordata, Urochordate, and Hemichordata (Fig. 3C). 1020 species-specific gene families were found in both hagfish, which were significantly enriched mainly in transmembrane signalling receptor activity, olfactory receptor activity, pheromone receptor activity, and signal transduction (Additional file 2: Table S15), and 196 species-specific gene families in E. okinoseanus were significantly enriched in both DNA helicase activity and DNA repair (Additional file 2: Table S16). Specific gene families were thought to play an important role in hagfish's unique evolution. Because changes in gene copy number may be associated with species adaptive evolution, we investigated gene family expansion in E. okinoseanus to reveal the underlying mechanisms of their adaptation. We found 88 significantly expanded gene families in the E. okinoseanus somatic genome. A notably large number of expanded gene families were enriched for GO terms related to the olfactory receptor activity, scavenger receptor activity, DNA helicase activity, potassium channel activity, lipid droplets, negative regulation of insulin secretion, and regulation of circadian rhythm (Additional file 2: Table S17). The KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway-based analysis revealed that these expanded gene families were significantly enriched in pathways involving olfactory transduction (Additional file 2: Table S18). We next evaluated the adaptive divergence between E. okinoseanus and the other five Cyclostomata species that live in shallow seas or rivers, using both the branch and the branch-site models implemented in PAML. We identified 26 positively selected genes (PSGs) (Additional file 2: Table S19) and 985 rapidly evolving genes (REGs) (Additional file 2: Table S20) in E. okinoseanus. Among these genes, we identified 28 genes related to lipid metabolism, 20 genes related to replication and repair of DNA, and several key genes related to insulin resistance (Additional file 1: Fig. S8). The expansion of these gene families and the PSGs and REGs genes may reflect the evolution of E. okinoseanus adaptations to the deep-sea environment.

Demographic inference

The pairwise sequential Markovian coalescent (PSMC) was used to determine the dynamic effective population size (Ne) (Fig. 3D). The findings indicate that the deep-sea and inshore hagfish had similar population dynamics. The end of the Pliocene (~ 1.6 Ma) [23] marked the beginning of a period of severe climate change and sea level fluctuations, with dramatic climate change that led to mass extinctions of marine organisms and population declines that were hardly recovered for a long time afterwards [24]. In this study, the population sizes of both deep-sea and inshore hagfish declined over time. However, we observed an earlier (~ 0.3 Ma) recovery of population size in the shallow water species, presumably arising from the Interglacial period climate warming and rising sea levels, which made the ecosystem in the shallow waters more suitable for species survival. In contrast, the deep-sea ecosystem was harsher and less susceptible, hence the slow recovery of population sizes of deep-sea species. During the Last Glacial Maximum, shallow species continued to be more affected than deep-sea species, which is probably also attributed to the more stable environment in the deep sea.

Strategies for responding to oligotrophic environments

Deep-sea hagfish, unlike shallow hagfish, cannot obtain sufficient food at any time. According to the reports, deep-sea hagfish can survive without food for up to a year. The deep-sea hagfish had a significantly higher fatty acid content than the shallow hagfish, leading to the hypothesis that it may have a high capacity for fatty acid synthesis and storage [14]. Lipid droplets are essential for cellular lipid homeostasis because they store triacylglycerols, which serve as energy substrates. Perilipin, located on the surface layer of intracellular lipid droplets in adipocytes, is the most abundant lipid droplet-associated protein and promotes the storage of triacylglycerols in adipocytes [25, 26]. Studies have shown that perilipin null mice are slender and possess approximately 30% less adipose tissue compared to their wild-type counterparts [27]. Notably, our genomic analyses found that the Perilipin gene experienced significant expansion with copy number up to 22 in E. okinoseanus relative to other species having a copy number of 1–9 (Fig. 4A) (Additional file 2: Table S21, S22). The expanded copies of perilipin consisting of both Perilipin-1 and Perilipin-2 were tandemly duplicated. Perilipin-1 primarily governs the regulation of adipose tissue triglyceride hydrolysis, overall adipose tissue function and the resulting maintenance of systemic glucose and lipid homeostasis [28]. Perilipin-2 reduces the association of adipose tissue triglyceride lipase (ATGL) with cytosolic lipid droplets, and overexpression of Perilipin-2 enhances the accumulation of cytosolic lipid droplets in hepatocytes and other cells [29].

Among the REGs, six genes are associated with lipid biosynthesis including ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY), 3-oxoacyl-[acyl-carrier-protein] synthase, mitochondrial (OXSM), Long-chain-fatty-acid–CoA ligase (ACSBG2), Peroxisomal trans-2-enoyl-CoA reductase (PECR), Glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase 4 (GPAT4), and Glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase 1, mitochondrial (GPAM) (Fig. 4C). ACLY cleaves citric acid to produce acetyl-CoA outside the mitochondria for glucose-dependent de novo lipogenesis, and the acetyl-CoA produced is a central metabolite that is the cornerstone of de novo fatty acid synthesis [30]. OXSM, ACSBG2, and PECR are involved in the biosynthesis and elongation of acetyl-CoA based fatty acids [31, 32]. GPAT4 and GPAM are involved in the synthesis of triglycerides, and these genes play an important role in lipid synthesis and energy storage. In addition, carnitine palmitoyltransferase I a (CPT1a) is a positively selected gene and is the key rate-limiting enzyme of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation (FAO). CPT1a regulates FAO, enabling the maintenance of body energy homeostasis during fasting [33]. Two REGs 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase (HADHB) and acetyl-Coenzyme A acyltransferase 1A (Acaa1a) are involved in the metabolism of fatty acids. HADHB is a major component of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein, an essential mitochondrial metabolism involved in the degradation of long-chain fatty acids in the fatty acid β-oxidation metabolic pathway [34]. ACAA1, also known as peroxisome 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase, is a key enzyme in the β-oxidation of fatty acids and plays an important role in their metabolism [35]. These findings suggest that E. okinoseanus has undergone adaptive evolution in the storage and use of fatty acids, allowing them to fast for longer periods of time (Fig. 4D).

Insulin promotes the utilization and storage of blood glucose while also maintaining blood glucose stability. Insulin has been shown to have no effect on free fatty acid concentrations and has a lower physiological effect on hagfish than in other vertebrates [12]. But the reason is unclear. In this study, the extended gene families in the genome of E. okinoseanus was significantly enriched in the pathway of negative regulation of insulin secretion (GO:0046676). PSGs cJun-N-terminal-kinase (JNK) and ATP-sensitive inward rectifier potassium channel 11 (KCJ11) and REGs Protein kinase C delta type (Prkcd), Max-like protein X (MLX), glucose transporter member 1 (GLUT1) associated with insulin resistance and negative regulation of insulin secretion were also identified in E. okinoseanus (Fig. 4C). The expanded gene Potassium voltage-gated channel subfamily B member 1 (KCNB1) is highly enriched in islet b-cells, which can control hormone release by regulating cell membrane potential, and it has been shown that germline null mutation of KCNB1 promotes insulin secretion [36]. The positively selected gene KCJ11 is involved in the composition of the ATP-sensitive potassium (KATP) channel, and increased KATP channel activity may lead to impaired insulin secretion [37].

The JNK gene has detected amino acid substitutions in deep-sea hagfish, which may affect the 3-D structure. The gene was at the crossroads of obesity and insulin resistance (Fig. 4B). JNK plays an important role in the development of insulin resistance because it directly inhibits the tyrosine phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrates (IRS) 1 and 2, which are essential components of the insulin signaling pathway [38]. Due to the efficient storage and usage of lipids by E. okinoseanus, the free fatty acids in the blood may remain consistently high, and JNK is thus in an activated state. In addition, the rapidly evolving gene Prkcd can also inhibit insulin-stimulated IRS-1 tyrosine phosphorylation, further inhibiting IRS activity [39]. Inhibition of IRS1 diminishes the IRS1-PI3K-AKT cascade reaction, resulting in decreased expression of GLUT4 and consequently the amount of GLUT4 on the plasma membrane [40]. MLX has also been shown to be involved in insulin resistance and is associated with the accumulation of triglycerides [41]. The combination of these factors may cause E. okinoseanus to remain hyperglycemic for an extended period of time after feeding, similar to cavefish. Cavefish, like deep-sea hagfish, face periodic food shortages. Insulin resistance in cavefish is thought to be a response to a nutritionally limited environment in which reduced insulin signaling is advantageous [42]. However, it is currently unclear whether dysregulation of blood glucose will have adverse effects on these organisms, or whether there is a compensation mechanism for these organisms. GLUT1 is insulin-independent for glucose transport and is phosphorylated by hexokinase I, forming glucose 6-phosphate, which can be used in all metabolic pathways, including the hexosamine pathway. Hexosamine has a negative feedback effect on GLUT4, and GLUT4 activity is further reduced [43]. We hypothesize that the blood glucose may eventually enter the cell via GLUT1. The glucose entering the cell passes through the glucose-6-phosphate pathway or the glycolytic pathway to form pyruvate and other products, which then form acetyl-CoA, which enters the fatty acid synthesis pathway to form fatty acids and ultimately stores energy in lipid droplets as triglycerides (Fig. 4D).

The metabolic rate and the minimum amount of oxygen required for the survival of deep-sea hagfish are lower than those of shallow-sea living hagfish [14]. Studies on Mexican tetra cavefish have shown that when eyed surface forms are tested in constant darkness, they consume 38% more energy than eyeless cave forms in the corresponding conditions. Metabolic homeostasis and circadian rhythms are two closely related biological processes, and the elimination of metabolic rhythms is likely a common feature of animals that live in permanently dark, food-limited environments such as caves or the deep sea [42, 44]. The expanded genes KCNB1 of E. okinoseanus are potassium channel proteins, and a study has shown that targeted expression of K+ channels electrically silences Drosophila circadian pacing neurons, causing severe defects in free-running circadian locomotion under conditions of complete darkness [45]. The positive selection genes Prospero homeobox protein 1 (PROX1) and JNK are also involved in circadian regulatory networks, with PROX1 having all the necessary attributes as an essential factor linking metabolism and circadian rhythms, controlling biological clocks and metabolic gene networks in the liver. PROX1 is a corepressor of ERRα, and mice lacking ERRα exhibit lower activity levels over 24 h. Furthermore, cells lacking PROX1 had greater cellular respiratory capacity [46, 47]. JNK inhibition or knockdown prolongs the circadian rhythm of cells, and mice exhibit longer free-running times in constant darkness [48]. The rapidly evolving gene Period circadian protein homolog 2 (PER2) controls lipid metabolism by directly regulating PPARγ [49]. The above evidence suggests that E. okinoseanus may regulate the metabolic efficiency of the organism through the evolution of these biological clock-related genes, which is more conducive to its living in the deep sea (Fig. 4D).

Adaptation of olfactory receptors to the deep sea

To detect food in a timely manner, aquatic organisms usually have an acute/enhanced sense of smell, and olfactory receptors were examined in this study. There are 86 complete olfactory receptors (OR) genes identified in the deep-sea hagfish E. okinoseanus, and the OR genes occur in clusters on chromosomes. OR genes are predominantly present in clusters on putative chromosomes 1, 8, and 11 (Additional file 1: Fig. S9), suggesting that tandem duplications may be the mechanism for the amplification of this gene family. We similarly characterized them in other cyclostome species as well as amphioxus. Phylogenetic analysis of these ORs revealed that the OR genes in hagfish clustered into two clades (Fig. 5A). To estimate the gene gain and loss events of ORs during the evolution of these species, we reconciled the gene tree with the species tree to derive the gene gains and losses. Our analyses show (Fig. 5B) that the number of ORs changed highly dynamically during the evolution of Cyclostomata and that the deep-sea hagfish has the highest number of ORs among these Cyclostomata. The common ancestor of Cyclostomata is estimated to be seven OR genes, and the increase or decrease in various genes seems to have occurred after the divergence of the hagfish and the lamprey. The inshore hagfish has fewer OR genes than that in the deep-sea hagfish; the common ancestor of the two species had 70 OR genes, of which the deep-sea hagfish gained 24 OR genes and lost 5, whereas the inshore hagfish gained 2 OR genes but lost 34. We hypothesize that this numerical difference may be related to the ecological position of the two species of hagfish. In order to further understand the structural characteristics of these hagfish OR genes, we performed a motif search and identified a total of ten motifs in all analysed species (Additional file 1: Fig. S10). Among these, three conserved motifs are present only in hagfishes (Additional file 1: Fig. S11). Hagfishes have been observed to always be the first to appear near decaying bait, and the next fish takes twice as long to arrive; thus, hagfishes have a strong ability to locate decaying carcasses [9]. So, we propose that these three motifs unique to hagfishes may be related to the recognition of chemical molecules emitted by decaying carcasses.

Conclusions

The somatic chromosomal-level genome of a deep-sea hagfish E. okinoseanus was successfully assembled. This study provides valuable insights into the genomic-level understanding of the survival strategies employed by deep-sea hagfishes in oligotrophic environments. The expansion of the Perilipin gene family, as well as gene evolution related to fatty acid metabolism and energy regulation highlights the deep-sea hagfish's adaptations for storing energy and maintaining metabolic homeostasis. The significant expansion of olfactory receptors aids in the detection of carrion in an oligotrophic environment. Furthermore, the somatic genome provides reliable data resources for research into Cyclostomata ecology, evolutionary biology, and biological conservation.

Methods

Sample collection and genome sequencing

The specimens in this study were trapped using the Feng–Huang lander at a depth of 1000 m in the South China Sea (110.4240 E, 17.5029 N). The E. okinoseanus was captured in the trap and recovered with the lander to the deck of the mothership. This sample was dissected and then immediately stored in liquid nitrogen and brought back to the specimen room of the protein research lab of the Institute of Deep-sea Science and Engineering, Chinese Academy of Sciences, where it was stored at -80 °C until use.

Total genomic DNA was extracted from muscle tissue using the E.Z.N.A.® Tissue DNA Kit (OMEGA, China). The quality and quantity of DNA were measured by standard agarose gel electrophoresis and a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen). Total RNA was extracted from the same individual using TRIzol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) from four different tissues/organs, including liver, testis, tongue and muscle. Sequencing libraries with an insert size of 350 bp were constructed using the Library Construction Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (Illumina, San Diego, CA). These libraries were then sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform. Fastp v0.20.1 [50] default parameters were used to filter 88.94 Gb raw reads to generate Illumina clean reads for subsequent analysis. (Additional file 2: Table S1). A 20-kb insert size library was constructed with at least 10 μg of sheared DNA, and we performed real-time DNA sequencing of the 20-kb single-molecule DNA library on the PacBio Sequel platform (Pacific Biosciences, USA), generating approximately 254.85 Gb of PacBio data (Additional file 2: Table S2). For Hi-C sequencing, DNA was extracted from muscle tissue for library preparation and sequenced on the Illumina platform PE150, and a total of 200.17 Gb of Hi-C data was generated (Additional file 2: Table S3). The species of the examined hagfish in this study were identified according to their morphological characteristics and molecular analysis by the mitochondrial genome. The results showed that the specimen has the same morphological characteristics as E. okinoseanus and similar sequences of 12S, 16S, and COX1 to E. okinoseanus with 100%, 100%, and 99.19% identity, respectively.

Genome survey and assembly

Using all Illumina filtered reads, the size and heterozygosity of the genome were estimated using the k-mer frequency distribution method with the formula: genome size = (total number of 17-mers)/(position of the peak depth). We filtered the Illumina raw reads using fastp v0.20.1 [50] to remove low-quality reads and splice sequences to obtain clean and high-quality reads. For the PacBio raw data, to obtain enough corrected reads, the longest coverage of subreads was first selected as seed reads to correct sequence errors. The clean PacBio reads were used to assemble the studied genome with wtdbg2 (version 2.5) [51]. We then used Arrow v.2.3.3 (SMRTTools v7) to correct the contig version of the genome using the PacBio data, followed by Pilon v1.22 [52] polishing the assembled genome using the Illumina reads after quality control. Subsequently, the Hi-C sequencing data were aligned to the assembled scaffolds by BWA-mem, and the scaffolds were clustered onto chromosomes with 3D-DNA pipeline version 190,716 [53] under default settings for diploid genomes. Finally, Juicebox v1.6 [54] was used to check and manually correct pseudochromosome linkage groups. To evaluate the quality of the genome assembly, we used Bowtie2 to map Illumina sequencing reads to the genome and calculate genome coverage [55]. The genome was also assessed for completeness and quality using the Metazoa database (obd10) of BUSCO (v5.2.2) [56] software.

Repeat elements and gene annotation

We first built a repetitive library of the deep-sea hagfish E. okinoseanus genome using RepeatModeler v2.0.1 [57], merged it with the repetitive consensus library in RepBase, annotated all the repetitive elements in the genome using RepeatMasker v4.1.0 [58] and replaced the repetitive sequences with Ns to generate a repeat-masked genome. After obtaining all repeat annotation results, we calculated the sequence divergence rate for each TE family. The noncoding RNA (ncRNA) genes in the E. okinoseanus genome, comprising microRNAs (miRNAs), ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs), small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), and transfer RNAs (tRNAs), were identified through Infernal v1.1.2 [59]. The software utilized alignment with the Rfam ncRNA database [60] as a reference to predict these genes. For structural annotation of genes, we used the GETA v2.5.6 pipeline (https://github.com/chenlianfu/geta), a workflow that integrates ab initio gene prediction, transcript evidence, and homology-based analyses, the three prediction methods. We provided the pipeline with RNA-seq reads of pooled tissue samples (liver, testis, muscle and tongue) to assemble the transcriptomes. The RNA-seq data were first trimmed using Trimmomatic [61], and the trimmed data were compared to the reference genome using HISAT2 v2.2.1 [62]. The scripts in GETA can be used to predict introns and to optimize transcripts. Based on the high-quality transcripts from these processes, the gene model was trained iteratively using AUGUSTUS v3.4.0 [63] for ab initio prediction. Homology with sequences from closely related species E. burgeri, Lethenteron reissneri, and Petromyzon marinus; from Hemichordata Saccoglossus kowalevskii, Cephalochordata Branchiostoma floridae, and Urochordate Ciona intestinalis; and from other vertebrates representative species Danio rerio, Takifugu rubripes, Callorhinchus milii, and Homo sapiens (Additional file 2: Table S23) was used as protein evidence to predict the gene set with GeneWise v. 2.4.1 [64]. Finally, the gene models predicted by these three methods were combined into a nonredundant gene set, and the Pfam database was used to screen for high-quality gene models. All the parameters of the software used above are stored in conf_for_big_genome.txt. Gene functions were annotated using databases including InterPro, eggNOG-mapper, SwissProt, NCBI NonRedundant Protein Database (NR), and KEGG Automatic Annotation Server (KAAS). Protein domains were annotated by mapping the genome to the InterPro databases using InterProScan v5.50 [65]. Database alignments for SwissProt and NR were performed using the BLASTp program using an E-value threshold of 1e-5. Mapping of the KEGG database using the KEGG Automated Annotation Server (KAAS) for annotation of pathways that may involve genes (https://www.genome.jp/kaas-bin/kaas_main). GO classification of genes was extracted from the corresponding InterProScan and eggno-emapper [66] results.

Hox gene annotation and genome synteny analysis

To identify Hox genes, the NR and SwissProt functionally annotated protein sets for E. okinoseanus were searched using BLAST software with e-values < 10–5. All Hox genes annotated by NR and SwissProt were extracted. Finally, their locations were determined by structural annotation files. The E. okinoseanus genome sequences were compared with those of D. rerio, B. floridae, E. burgeri, E. tridentatus, L. camtschaticum, L. reissneri, and P. marinus to determine the percentages of collinear homologous pairs. Synteny blocks with at least three collinear homologous genes were detected using MCScanX software [67]. As for the analysis of the colinearity of E. atami and E. okinoseanus, the MUMmer (4.0.0.beta2) [68] was used to perform the pairwise alignments using the whole genomes with the parameter “-b 400”. The alignments were filtered to retain the one-to-one best hits using the program of delta-filt in the MUMmer package. Unanchored scaffolds were excluded from the alignments. The alignments were then formatted for use in the MCScan pipeline for synteny visualization.

Phylogenetic analysis, gene expansion and contraction

The genomes selected in this study for comparative analysis included the inshore hagfish E. burgeri and eleven other species, including four species of lamprey P. marinus, L. reissneri, Lethenteron camtschaticum and Entosphenus tridentatus of Cyclostomata; S. kowalevskii of Hemichordata; B. floridae of Cephalochordata; C. intestinalis of Urochordate; D. rerio and T. rubripes of Osteichthyes; and C. milii and Carcharodon carcharias of Chondrichthyes. A total of 482 single-copy genes were identified in 13 species using the default parameters of OrthoFinder v2.3.8 [69]. Multiple alignment analysis was carried out using MAFFT v7.490 [70], and all alignments were combined into one supergene to construct a phylogenetic tree using RAxML v8.2.12 [71] under the PROTGAMMAJTT model with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Divergence times were estimated using MCMCTree in the PAML package v4.9j [72] and corrected by four potential fossil records, which were downloaded from the TIMETREE databases (Additional file 2: Table S24). Homologue clusters that contained over 100 gene copies in species were excluded from the Orthofinder results. The filtered homologue clusters were analysed for expansion and contraction of the gene family using CAFÉ v.4.2 [73].

Demographic history analysis

The demographic history of deep-sea and inshore hagfish was inferred by pairwise sequentially Markovian coalescent (PSMC) analysis [74] to show the changes in effective population size (Ne) over 10,000 to 1,000,000 years. E. burgeri of paired-end Illumina reads downloaded from the NCBI SRA database (ERR10906840). Paired-end Illumina reads of E. okinoseanus were sequenced using another body different from the genome assembly. Reads were filtered and trimmed with fastp [50]. Short reads were aligned to the two hagfish genomes using the default parameters of BWA-MEM [75]. To generate diploid consensus sequences for the individuals, we utilized the SAMtools v1.11 mpileup pipeline with BCFtools and vcfutils.pl. Subsequently, we converted the consensus fastq files into psmcfa format, which is required input for PSMC, using the fq2psmcfa program. The PSMC method was utilized to infer the effective population history, and the resulting data were visualized using the psmc_plot.pl pipeline. The substitution mutation rate and generation time of hagfish were set to 2.4e-9 and 1 year, respectively [4], according to a previous study.

Identification of positively selected genes (PSGs) and rapidly evolving genes (REGs)

Six species of Cyclostomata were selected to explore the evolutionary features of E. okinoseanus deep-sea hagfish. All orthologues from the results of the reciprocal best hit (RBH) method were used to test for positive selection and detect rapidly evolving genes for E. okinoseanus. The ratio (ω) of nonsynonymous substitutions (dN) to synonymous substitutions (dS) was calculated by using the PAML4 software package. RAxML generated species trees were used as input trees for CodeML, and E. okinoseanus was selected as a foreground branch. The two-ratio branch model (model = 2, NSsites = 0) with a null model (model = 0, NSsites = 0) was employed to detect rapidly evolving genes and the branch-site model (model = 2, NSsites = 2, fix_omega = 0) and null model (model = 2, NSsites = 2, fix_omega = 1) were employed to detect positively selected genes. The significance of disparities between the two models was evaluated through likelihood ratio tests (LRTs). These tests involved computing the twofold logarithm of the likelihood differences (2DL) utilizing the chi-square distribution. We considered genes as rapidly evolving when the FDR-adjusted P value < 0.05 and having a high ω-ratio in E. okinoseanus. Genes with FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 were considered positively selected genes.

Olfactory receptor gene identification and analysis

The identification of OR genes from the genome was performed as described by Niimura [76]. The known OR genes of zebrafish were searched in NCBI and used as query sequences, and the collected OR protein sequences were searched using cd-hit to remove redundant sequences, tblastn (E-value = 1e-10). The best matches with the lowest e-value and the longest alignments were selected for subsequent analysis. If the fragmented gene sequences belonged to the same query protein, they were merged using Solar and the gene structure was predicted using GeneWise [64]. The final OR gene selected was the best match to the OR gene in the nonredundant protein database, with a sequence length between 250–500 bp and seven transmembrane structural domains. A maximum likelihood tree was constructed using IQtree [77]. The motifs in OR genes were predicted by MEME with lengths from 6 to 100 aa and a maximum of 10 motifs. To assess the occurrences of gene gain and loss events in OR genes during the evolutionary process of Cyclostomata species, we utilized the tree reconciliation method available in NOTUNG 2.9 [78]. The species tree topology is taken from the study above, while the gene tree topology is taken from the ML tree of ORs.
