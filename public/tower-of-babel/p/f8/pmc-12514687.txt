Metastatic subcutaneous Ewing sarcoma mimicking a synchronous second primary neoplasm: a case description
Authors: Jiro Ichikawa, Koshi Akahane, Minori Tamai, Kojiro Onohara, Masaki Matsuda, Masanori Wako, Hirotaka Haro, Tomonori Kawasaki
Published: 2025

Introduction

Ewing sarcoma (EWS) is a small round sarcoma presenting gene fusion between genes in EWSR1 and ETS families. EWS occurs in the diaphysis and diaphyseal-metaphyseal portions of long bones, pelvis, or ribs in young individuals. However, extra-skeletal EWS occurs in approximately 12% of cases, and among these, subcutaneous manifestations are rare (1,2). Compared to EWS in the bone, subcutaneous EWS is smaller and has a better prognosis (2-4). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) are effective for evaluating the characteristics of the primary and metastatic sites in EWS (5). The roles of molecular confirmation have been developed for the diagnosis of EWS (6). Additionally, diagnosing sarcomas through imaging is often challenging; thus, combining imaging findings and pathological features is essential for a correct diagnosis. Here, we report a case of lymph node metastatic subcutaneous EWS that presented with imaging findings suggesting two different tumors. Performing biopsies in two locations led to an accurate diagnosis and adequate treatment.

Case presentation

A 9-year-old boy, with no medical history, presented with a mass in the right inguinal region, first noticed 2 months before his initial visit. Pediatricians initially suspected lymphadenitis. However, as the mass increased in size, the patient was referred to our hospital for further evaluation. Examination revealed another mass in the right lumbar region, which had appeared about 5 months prior to the initial visit. It was diagnosed by a dermatologist as an atheroma and treated with ointment, but no change was observed in size. Blood tests revealed white blood cell levels of 6,310/µL (normal range: 4,000–8,000/µL), platelet number of 346 ×103/µL (normal range: 158×103/µL–348×103/µL), C-reactive protein of 0.1 mg/dL (normal range: 0.02–0.5), and alkaline phosphatase of 258 U/L (normal range: 38–113 U/L), lactate dehydrogenase 217 U/L (normal range: 124–222 U/L) which were all within normal limits. Hemoglobin was 12.4 g/dL, indicating slight anemia. Both the masses in the right inguinal region and the right lumbar region were elastic and soft, with no redness or swelling. Neither of the tumors showed pain or a positive Tinel’s sign. Inguinal (Figure 1A-1C) and lumbar (Figure 1D-1F) tumors showed low intensity on T1 MRI (Figure 1A,1D); however, the inguinal mass showed an uneven faint high signal on short-tau inversion recovery (Figure 1B) without enhancement with 40 mm × 36 mm × 22 mm in size (Figure 1C), while the lumbar mass showed a uniform faint high signal 16 mm × 16 mm × 10 mm in size (Figure 1E) with mild enhancement (Figure 1F). Differential diagnosis was lymphatic malformation or lymphadenitis for the right inguinal region, and vascular malformation for the right lumbar region.

Both the right inguinal (Figure 2A) and right lumbar (Figure 2B) tumors showed low uptake on PET-CT, with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 1.8 and 1.37, respectively, and no uptake except for these two tumors. These findings suggested malignant tumors, such as low-grade malignant lymphoma and myxoid liposarcoma, or benign tumors, such as lymphatic malformation, lymphadenitis, or vascular malformation. Given the broad differential diagnoses and increasing size of the masses, open biopsies were conducted.

PET-CT showed low uptake in the (A) inguinal tumors (red arrow) and (B) lumbar tumors (asterisk). PET-CT, positron emission tomography-computed tomography.

Histopathological findings of inguinal (Figure 3A-3D) and lumbar (Figure 3E-3H) masses showed uniform proliferation of small round cells (Figure 3A,3E), but the inguinal mass showed myxomatous stroma (Figure 3A). Immunohistochemistry was positive for CD99 (Figure 3B,3F) and NKX2.2 (Figure 3C,3G), and negative for CD34, desmin, S100, and myogenin (data not shown). Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis showed a split signal of EWSR1 (Figure 3D,3H), confirming the diagnosis of EWS. Based on the tumor locations and progression, the subcutaneous lumbar EWS possibly metastasized to the inguinal lymph nodes. Regarding the post-diagnosis course, four cycles of chemotherapy with vincristine, doxorubicin, and cyclophosphamide (VDC) plus ifosfamide and etoposide (IE) were administered. CT confirmed the absence of new lesions and MRI confirmed a reduction in the lumbar region to 16 mm × 13 mm × 8 mm and the disappearance of the inguinal region, leading to a wide resection. Histopathological findings from resected specimens confirmed that the lumbar tumor was the primary site and the inguinal tumor was a lymph node metastasis with negative margins. Subsequently, two additional cycles of VDC + IE were administered, completing the treatment. Six months after treatment, no recurrences or new metastases occurred.

Discussion

Most EWS cases originate in bones, with approximately 80% occurring in individuals under the age of 20, and a slightly higher prevalence in men (1). Extraskeletal EWS refers to EWS arising outside of bones, and it occurs in 12% of cases (1). Reported sites of occurrence include the kidneys, breasts, and lungs, among which the subcutaneous tissue is an example (7). Subcutaneous EWS has a wide anatomical distribution, but it is most commonly found in the lower extremities, chest wall, and shoulders, in that order (4). Compared to bone EWS, subcutaneous EWS is characterized by a smaller tumor size, localized occurrence (fewer metastases), pre-diagnostic surgeries (unplanned surgery), and a lower rate of local recurrence. However, there is no established consensus regarding age or sex differences (2-4). Pre-diagnostic surgeries depend on the tumor size and in our case, the tumor might have been resected if it had been limited to either the lumbar or inguinal region. Although pre-diagnostic surgeries are often performed, they do not significantly impact prognosis, and chemotherapy or additional surgeries are considered effective (3). However, these findings are based solely on retrospective reports, and further detailed investigation into the etiology of subcutaneous EWS is needed.

Reports regarding subcutaneous EWS are lacking; therefore, we elaborated on MRI findings of osseous EWS. CT and MRI are beneficial for local staging; however, MRI is mainly targeted at detecting intraosseous and extraosseous extensions with higher sensitivity (5,8). EWS on MRI typically shows T1-low and T2-high signals, with varying degrees of contrast enhancement (5,8). Although MRI findings in the lumbar mass were similar to those previously reported, we suspected a vascular malformation because of the small size and uniform STIR and contrast enhancement. However, because of the location and poor contrast enhancement of MRI findings, it was initially misdiagnosed as lymph node inflammation, leading to the identification of two distinct lesions. Lesions that do not show internal enhancement have been reported in cases of carcinoma with inguinal lymph node metastasis, which is consistent with our case. Additionally, differentiation from lymphadenitis and hernia may be necessary (9). Recently, the effectiveness of PET-CT in evaluating systemic staging has been reported, particularly regarding sensitivity and specificity for detecting bone metastases compared to conventional bone scintigraphy (10). Additionally, it is equally effective in assessing lymph node metastases and recurrence. However, while PET-CT is useful for detecting pulmonary metastases, it may not detect small tumors; therefore, combining it with diagnostic CT is recommended (10). Bone and soft tissue EWS exhibit high uptake on PET, with reported SUVmax values ranging from 2 to 18.4 (mean 8.3), 4.3 to 8 (mean 6.4), with no difference in SUVmax between bone and soft-tissue EWS (11). In our case, the SUVmax values were 1.8 and 1.37, respectively, both relatively low. However, the relationship between SUVmax and prognosis in EWS remains unclear (11,12). Since our case reported low SUVmax values, further investigation is needed to determine whether low SUVmax reflects the characteristics and biology of subcutaneous EWS, which is considered to exhibit a favorable prognosis (3). The diagnosis of sarcomas, including EWS, is prone to misinterpretation based on imaging alone.

There have been no reports of characteristic pathological findings specific to subcutaneous EWS, and it is considered similar to osseous EWS. Histologically, it is a uniform small round cell tumor with round nuclei. Immunohistochemically, CD99 is frequently used and is positive, but it is nonspecific. NKX2-2 and PAX7 have high specificity, with positive rates exceeding 90% (6). Other markers, such as keratin, ERG, and FLI, are also utilized (6). However, for accurate diagnosis, molecular confirmation via FISH or polymerase chain reaction has become essential in recent years. In EWS, EWSR1 or FUS, along with ETS, is observed in over 80% of cases, with EWSR1-FLI1 fusion being the most common, followed by EWSR1-ERG (6). In a smaller proportion, EWSR1-ETV1 and FUS-ERG can be detected. Recently, EWS and Ewing-like sarcoma have been further classified to include CIC-rearranged sarcomas, emphasizing the increasing diagnostic significance of molecular confirmation (6).

In the whole EWS, chemotherapy, surgery, and radiation therapy improve prognosis, with a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate of approximately 70% in non-metastatic cases. However, in metastatic cases, the 5-year survival rate is reported to be around 15–40% (13). The VDC + IE regimen is superior to VIDE (vincristine, ifosfamide, doxorubicin, and etoposide), which was used for our case (13). Additionally, subcutaneous EWS has a better prognosis compared to osseous EWS, with a reported 5-year survival at 93.8% and 5-year event-free survival at 88.5% (4). Chemotherapy plays a role in EWS in both localized and metastatic tumors (13). The fundamental strategy involves chemotherapy, followed by surgical resection of both primary and metastatic lesions if feasible. However, considering the number and location of metastases, radiation therapy and/or surgery may be used (13). Based on this approach and the generally favorable prognosis, just as for non-metastatic cases of EWS, we performed both the adjuvant and neoadjuvant chemotherapy and surgery. Despite the favorable prognosis of subcutaneous EWS, cases with metastases at initial diagnosis are reported to have significantly poorer outcomes, necessitating careful follow-up in the future (3). There are some limitations in our case, including (I) short-term follow-up, (II) no confirmation of the details of fusion type.

Conclusions

We report a challenging case of metastatic subcutaneous EWS. Imaging initially suggested two distinct benign neoplasms; however, pathological examination confirmed that they were the same tumor, further identified as EWS. Subcutaneous EWS is considered a subtype of EWS with a favorable prognosis, and even in metastatic cases, aggressive treatment involving chemotherapy with surgery or radiation is recommended, similar to non-metastatic cases. However, metastases at initial diagnosis correlate with prognosis, necessitating long-term follow-up.
