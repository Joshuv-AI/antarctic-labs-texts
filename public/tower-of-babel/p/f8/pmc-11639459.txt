The correlation between parenting styles and children’s anxiety in the COVID-19 pandemic in Bandar Abbas in Iran
Authors: Roghayeh Ezati Rad, Shokrollah Mohseni, Laleh Hassani, Nahid Shahabi, Fatemeh Moltafet, Maryam Shabrava
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Anxiety is considered a prevalent mental disorder during childhood. Due to the significance of child psychology in critical psychological and health conditions and the fact that parenting styles may affect child health and behavioral issues in prospective life, the present study intends to explore the association of parenting styles and anxiety level among children in the COVID-19 pandemic.MATERIALS AND METHODS:The present cross-sectional study used a convenient sampling of all mothers with 5 to 12 children in Bandar Abbas in southern Iran. The target population included 346 mothers. A web-based self-administered questionnaire was used to collect data. The questionnaire survey consisted of three parts, demographic information, Baumrind parenting styles, and Spence children’s anxiety scale-parent. Analysis of quantitative data was done using a t-test, and association of qualitative variables was tested using the Chi-square test. Regression analysis was run on the significant values of the univariate analysis. The data were analyzed in SPSS 21.RESULTS:The findings revealed that the most frequent parenting style was authoritative (69.6%). The state of child anxiety in the authoritative style was kept to the minimum (24.07 ± 13.14). The highest anxiety score belonged to the permissive style (49.50 ± 37.67). Moreover, the results indicated that separation anxiety and fear of physical harm accounted for the most anxiety levels in the target research population. The four constructs of authoritative and authoritarian parenting styles, mother’s education, and father’s age accounted for 11.9% of the anxiety (P ≤ 001).CONCLUSION:In light of the present findings, it can be concluded that psychologists and health care providers can raise parents’ awareness of what each parenting style means and can help parents provide adequate care, love, and support for children and develop a sense of independence in them too.

Introduction

In COVID-19 pandemic, child and parent mental well-being is threatened by the psychological pressure on families; restrictions for children to stay home and play just at home, spend time far from school and friends, and lose fun; stress; and dozens of other adverse effects.[1]

Anxiety is a prevalent psychological disorder in children and adolescents, and its prevalence is between 8.3% and 27% among those under 18 years.[2] The highest prevalence of anxiety disorders is that of social disorders (66%), followed by generalized anxiety disorder with a prevalence rate of 38%.[3]

Many psychological disorders are caused by parents’ inappropriate behaviors and attitudes.[4] Parent–child affairs are a supportive factor against children’s mental and behavioral issues.[5]

Thus, family-related variables comprise a main cause of emotional and behavioral problems. Among the family factors that determine individual and social behavior and other behavioral and personality traits of an individual are the parenting styles. Parenting style is one factor affecting the formation of children’s personality. The use of inefficient styles can have several negative effects such as behavioral problems.[6] Several studies showed that the mother’s parenting style plays a fundamental role in the emergence of children’s external and internal behaviors.[7]

Parenting style includes the methods and behaviors that parents adopt in raising their children and can affect the child’s growth. Parenting styles are classified into three types depending on the (child’s) needs and (the parent’s) responsiveness: authoritarian, authoritative, and permissive. Authoritative parents are concerned about children’s needs and their supportive organizing method. Authoritarian parents are often harsh and use rigid punishment. Permissive ones are parent-oriented and are rarely involved in child-rearing relations.[8]

Many studies have been conducted on the psychological and social damages to children during the COVID-19 pandemic. Yet, a few studies addressed the parenting style and anxiety of children during the pandemic. These studies have been conducted in a wide age range (e.g. under 18 years old).[91011] To the best of our knowledge, the present study pioneers in studying parenting style and children’s anxiety during the pandemic in Iran. As the behavior of parents has changed during the pandemic,[910] the parenting style of parents has changed too, and the intensity of anxiety disorders in children increased during the pandemic.[12] Psychological distress has disrupted the ability of parents to take care of their children and aggravated the destructive effect of the pandemic on children.[9] Thus, the present study aimed to determine the association between parenting style and children’s anxiety during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

The present cross-sectional study was conducted from June to September 2022 in Bandar Abbas with a web-based self-administered questionnaire. Bandar Abbas is the capital of Hormozgan Province. Hormozgan is a southern province of Iran located on the coast of the Persian Gulf.

The research population was composed of all mothers to 5–12-year children in Bandar Abbas City. The reason for including mothers (rather than fathers) was that previous studies had shown that mothers’ parenting style played a fundamental role in the development of children’s external and internal behaviors.[9] Children spend more time with their mothers, and mothers’ parenting style significantly affects the children’s behavioral disorders.[10]

The data were collected using a questionnaire designed on the Pors Line platform, a web-based survey platform in Iran (https://survey.porsline.ir), and were provided to the target group through social media. Participation was quite voluntary, and on the first page of the questionnaire, after explaining the objectives of the study, the consent form was signed by the participants.

Study participants and sampling

The present cross-sectional study used a convenient sample of all mothers with 5 to 12 children in Bandar Abbas in southern Iran. Because of the existing restrictions of the pandemic and the impossibility of using printed questionnaires, the data were gathered via different social networks (WhatsApp, Telegram, Linkedin), emails, channels, news institutes, public relations of the University of Science Medicine, Red Crescent, Municipality, and University Student Research Committee in the target city.

The inclusion criteria were all mothers the aged over 18 years, living in bandar abbas, having children aged 5–12, and no mental illness of the mother or child (based on self-report). The exclusion criterion was incomplete questionnaires.

To estimate the sample size, a correlation value of 0.15 was considered. Then, the G.POWER software was used to estimate a sample size of 346. Having collected the data, there were 33 incomplete questionnaires excluded from the analysis, and the response rate was 90%.

Data collection tool and technique

A tripartite parent-reported questionnaire was used to collect the data. The first part included demographic information such as sex, age, duration of child’s contact with family per day, parents’ occupation, and education. The second part included Baumrind parenting style questionnaire, with its reliability and validity already substantiated in a previous study.[12] In Iran, the validity and reliability of the Persian questionnaire were substantiated.[13] This Spence children’s anxiety scale-parent (SCAS-parent) has 30 questions, 10 related to the parents’ permissive style, 10 to the authoritarian style, and 10 to the authoritative style. The authoritarian parenting style involved questions 1, 6, 10, 13, 14, 17, 19, 21, 24, and 28; the authoritative parenting style involved questions 2, 3, 7, 9, 12, 16, 18, 25, 26, and 29; and the permissive parenting style involved questions 4, 5, 8, 11, 15, 20, 22, 23, 27, and 30. They are all rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging between 0 and 4. A Likert rating was used to explore the participant’s evaluation on five points: 5: always, 4: frequently, 3: occasionally, 2: seldom, and 1: never.[14]

The scores of the questions related to each style were summed up and divided by the number of questions. Three separate scores were obtained for permissive, authoritative, and authoritarian styles for each individual participant. The maximum score would represent the participant’s main parenting style.

The third part of the questionnaire was the Spence Children’s Anxiety Scale (SCAS), a questionnaire with 38 scored items. This questionnaire was developed by Spence in 1999 to assess the symptoms of anxiety in children in the general population. The parent version of the Spence questionnaire was used in this study. Scoring follows a 4-point Likert scale (never (0), sometimes (1), often (2), and always (3)). Also, six subscales are included: separation anxiety, social phobia, obsessive compulsive disorder, panic/agoraphobia, physical injury fears, and generalized anxiety.

The reliability of the general anxiety is estimated at 0.92. That of the subscales is reported to range between 0.60 and 0.82.[15]

In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of internal consistency for Spence children’s anxiety questionnaire were 0.88 and 0.81 for the parenting style questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

Frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation were utilized to describe the data. T-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were run to test the hypotheses and examine the relationship between the mean anxiety score and demographic variables as well as the parenting styles. The variables with a P value less than 0.25 in the univariate analysis were punched into the multiple linear regression model. Chi-square test was used to test the relationship between demographic variables and parenting styles. The significance level for hypothesis testing was considered to be 0.05.

Ethical considerations

All procedures abided by the Declaration of Helsinki. The research was confirmed by the ethics committee (ethical code: IR.HUMS.REC.1400.330). The committee has agreed to carry out the study online and get the participantsd by the ethics. All subjects who gave online consent for the study were ensured that participation was quite voluntary, they could withdraw from the study when they wanted, and the data were anonymized, securely saved, and considered for publication.

Discussion

The present study showed that the authoritative parenting style was the most prevalent, and the permissive parenting style was the least prevalent parenting style in the participants. The child’s anxiety level is the lowest in the authoritative style and the highest in the permissive style. Besides, the results showed that separation anxiety and physical injury anxiety had the highest scores in the research population. Moreover, authoritative and authoritarian parenting style, mother’s education, and father’s age were the predictors of child’s anxiety.

In this research, the authoritative parenting style showed to be the most prevalent parenting style. Previous studies showed authoritative parenting style was the most dominant style.[16] A dominant authoritarian parenting style in Iran shows a direct cultural component guided by religion.[14] Mothers with an authoritative parenting style enjoy more positive interactions with their children. Children brought up in authoritative families are often motivated to practice verbal reasoning with their parents. They enjoy more adept emotional and communication skills, have more ability to access emotion regulation strategies, and can show more socially adaptive behavior.[17]

There was a significant correlation between parents’ education and parenting styles, and the authoritative parenting style is significantly higher in families where parents hold a university degree. This finding is consistent with another study.[18] This could be because families with a university degree knew more about parenting styles and received the necessary advice for proper parenting styles.

In our study, no statistically significant correlation was found between students’ age and anxiety, which is consistent with other studies.[1920] This finding is not consistent with Muris et al., which reported a decrease in anxiety symptoms with an increasing age. Perhaps this inconsistency is because the research population in the present study was composed of 5- to 12-year-old children, whereas the research population of the afore-mentioned study was 7- to 19-year-old children. Also, people of older age groups were included.[21]

In this study, no statistically significant correlation was found between students’ gender and anxiety. However, in another study, in which gender differences were observed, girls reported more anxiety symptoms.[22] This could be because during the pandemic, both girls and boys were at home for long hours and staying home can contribute to the better social relationships among family members and similarly close relationships between children and other family members can prevent physical and mental problems.[23] However, due to a common life-threatening illness, children’s anxiety and stress levels at home may increase.[24]

Anxiety showed to have the lowest score in the authoritative style and the highest score in the permissive style, which is consistent with several other studies.[2526] One reason that the permissive style may be related to child anxiety is that a lack of parental intimacy and a pattern of parental neglect or lack of involvement can lead to feelings of insecurity in children.

Permissive parents do not monitor their children’s behavior or support their interests. Therefore, due to the lack of discipline and guidance, these children face problems in different areas of emotional development.[2728]

Permissive parents fail to set limits and do not expect developmentally appropriate behavior from children, which limits the development of children’s social skills and emotional regulation and increases sensitivity to anxiety.[29]

Besides, the findings showed that separation anxiety and physical injury anxiety have the highest anxiety scores in the research population. This result is consistent with another study by Kolcakoglu et al. in Turkey,[24] which could be due to the lack of information about the pandemic. Children faced with unexpected and unknown events usually have their own beliefs about the virus and blame themselves for the current situation, resulting in greater fear of physical harm and anxiety.[30] Previous research showed that quarantine conditions can lead to separation anxiety and fear of harm in children, which may affect the child’s mental health in the long run.[31] Anxiety is defined as feelings of helplessness, fear, and dissatisfaction in a seemingly threatening environment. If children’s anxiety can be properly controlled by families, they will be able to overcome their distress by creating stable emotional and physiological conditions even during the quarantine in the COVID-19 pandemic.[32]

Also, authoritative parenting style could negatively predict child anxiety, and anxiety in children with authoritative parents was significantly reduced compared to the permissive parents. This finding is consistent with another study.[33] Children with authoritative parents are better able to regulate their stress. Hence, they have less internal distress.[34] An authoritative parenting style refers to parenting behaviors that include warmth, reasoning, democratic participation, and benevolence or the easy-going nature of parents.

The present findings showed that increasing the mothers’ authority is related to a lower probability of developing anxiety disorders in children.[35] A possible explanation for why an authoritative parenting style may be associated with better outcomes for children is that an authoritative parenting style creates a warm, loving, and mutual understanding in the family and fosters stable behavior in children. Authoritative families show clear boundaries, which allow for a supportive yet independent environment.[36] This helps the child internalize the importance and reasonableness of the demands and makes the child more self-confident, socially competent, independent, and social. Therefore, children raised by authoritative parents have more self-confidence, have more self-control, have higher social and cognitive skills, and, as a result, have less anxiety.[3738] This style (authoritative) has been recognized as a suitable educational style in a previous study.[39] In the present study, although the level of anxiety was significantly higher in children with authoritarian parents, regression analysis did not correlate it with anxiety disorders in children, and anxiety in children with authoritarian parents was significantly reduced compared to permissive parents. The authoritarian parenting style refers to a parental behavior that includes hostility, corporal punishment, irrational punishment strategies, and orders. Studies have shown that parents’ lack of anger control, which is related to the authoritarian style, is positively related to children’s anxiety.[40] These divergent findings can be due to the small sample size of parents with a permissive style (n = 8) in our study. Arguably, we should not neglect the cultural similarities and differences of different regions of the country and other parts of the world. Researchers believe that according to the concept of parenting styles, the effect of each style can be different according to the type of culture that dominates the society. However, the authoritarian parenting style has historically been associated with children’s emotional disturbances and both internalizing and externalizing problems.

In Sahithya et al.’s study, permissive parenting style was associated with increased likelihood of anxiety disorders in children, which is consistent with the present study.[35] The present findings are consistent with the study of Dougherty et al., reporting that children with anxiety disorders are more likely to have parents who are less supportive during parent–child interactions,[25] and they also rate themselves as more permissive. These studies, along with the findings of the present study, support the association between anxiety disorders and the permissive parenting style. One reason that neglect may be related to child anxiety is that a lack of parental intimacy and a pattern of parental neglect or lack of involvement can lead to the feelings of insecurity in children. Permissive parents do not monitor their children’s behavior or support their interests. Therefore, due to the lack of discipline and guidance, these children face problems in different phases of emotional development.[2728]

The findings of the present study support the association between anxiety disorders and the permissive parenting style. Permissive parents fail to set limits and do not expect developmentally appropriate behavior from their children, which limits the development of children’s social skills and emotional regulation and increases their susceptibility to anxiety.[29] Negligence is probably an important factor in the development of anxiety disorders because it may cause the child to perceive the environment as fundamentally hostile and threatening, leading to feelings of low self-worth and competence.[41]

Limitations and recommendation

Since in the present study the participants were selected through an online survey, it is possible that those with no access to online surveys, for example, rural residents, those with no access to the Internet, and those with low literacy levels have been underrepresented.

The present study did not examine the different parenting styles of children’s mothers and fathers separately. There may be sex differences in their parenting style.

The anxiety data were obtained only through parent reports, and the present study did not use a clinical interview.

The small sample size in the “permissive style” resulted in large confidence intervals, and the effect of this category on children’s anxiety should be interpreted with caution.

Also, the convenience sampling method used to recruit participants might lead to a selection bias, which, in turn, could have shown a biased estimation of the study variables concerning the study population. Last, the observational design of the study does not allow us to infer any causality; thus, interpretation of the results should be made in the light of this information.

Further research is required with a prospective design to assess a wider range of potential mediating factors. Also, designing a qualitative study can provide more in-depth information on parents’ parenting styles and their perception of this important issue. Moreover, future studies should examine a broader set of family factors such as socioeconomic status, level of social support, and cultural influences such as race and ethnicity to further identify potential mediators and moderators that may underlie the relationships between parenting styles.

Conclusions

In general, the tragic COVID-19 pandemic has collateral effects extending beyond those of direct viral infection. According to these findings, the present research highlighted the need to evaluate 5–12-year children who experience a permissive parenting style for the possible presence of anxiety disorders. In addition, students should be educated about the risk factors associated with anxiety as well as the supplementary resources available to them.

It is also recommended to include sufficient numbers of trained mental health professionals at school to identify and help students with mental health problems such as anxiety disorders as well as their parents. It is recommended that parents examine their parenting styles and if necessary change their parenting styles to ensure the best outcomes for their children. To do so, psychologists and health care providers can inform parents of what each parenting style is like and guide parents to provide adequate care, affection, and support for their children while maintaining their independence.
