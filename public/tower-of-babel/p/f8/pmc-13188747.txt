Forgetting or blanking? Evaluating the role of a forgot option in a thought sampling method for mind blanking research
Authors: Toshikazu Kawagoe, Keiichi Onoda, Shinpei Yoshimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMind blanking (MB) describes a state in which a person reports an absence of thought, sensation, or awareness. Although thought sampling methods (TSMs) are widely used to capture MB during sustained attention tasks, concerns remain about whether individuals’ reports of MB during the task reflect genuine blankness or failures in memory retrieval.MethodsWe investigated whether adding a “Forgot” option, designed to capture metacognitive uncertainty, to TSM probes improved the interpretability of MB assessments. Japanese participants (n = 183) completed a sustained attention to response task (SART) in a within‑subject crossover study with two probe conditions: one with five common categorical options and one with an added sixth Forgot option.ResultsThe Forgot option was selected infrequently, especially in the first session, yet its inclusion clarified introspective reporting without disrupting behavioral performance (hit response time, variability, and d′). State frequencies showed moderate-to-high session‑to‑session stability, indicating reliable individual reporting tendencies. A correlation analysis revealed the expected associations between the participants’ reports of mind-wandering (MW) and questionnaire-based trait MW scores across both conditions. Critically, MB reports correlated with trait MB scores only when the Forgot option was available, suggesting that the additional option reduced misclassification of retrieval‑based uncertainty as MB.ConclusionsIncorporating a Forgot option enhances the construct validity of MB measurement by distinguishing genuine experiential absence from memory‑based uncertainty, while preserving task performance and the stability of state reports. This refinement improves the precision of TSM‑based assessments and offers a practical tool for advancing research on cognitive absence and attention–memory interactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04517-2.Keywords: Mind blanking, Thought sampling method, Options, Sustained attention to response task, Forgetting

Introduction

Much of cognitive science has focused on the contents of thought (i.e., what we think, why we think it, and how attention fluctuates) but there are moments when the human mind appears to contain nothing at all. These episodes, often described as “mind blanking” (MB), reflect a state characterized by a reported absence of conscious awareness and by a lack of sensory input, bodily sensations, and identifiable thoughts [1]. Although the MB state was mentioned in early studies [2, 3], it has remained largely unexplored due to its elusive nature and to methodological challenges such as how to empirically capture a moment defined by absence.

Ward and Wegner’s seminal work [1] provided a conceptual and empirical foundation for MB, distinguishing it from mind wandering (MW), which also involves disengagement from external stimuli but typically with retention of internal thought content. Ward and Wegner used a thought sampling method (TSM), asking participants to retrospectively report their exact thoughts just prior to a probe presented randomly during a tedious challenge such as a sustained attention to response task (SART). Their findings demonstrated that MB is not merely a variant of MW, but rather a distinct cognitive state. Since then, the research has expanded to examine the clinical relevance and neural underpinnings of MB. For example, Van den Driessche et al. [4] reported that individuals with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) described experiencing MB more frequently compared with control subjects, and that stimulant medication reduced the frequency of MB moments to normative levels.

Neuroimaging studies have also begun to characterize the neural signature of MB, with findings suggesting reduced activity in regions such as Broca’s area and the hippocampus [5]. More recent work indicated that this reduced activity (i.e., deactivation) is not limited to these regions; instead, whole-brain deactivations precede MB experiences [6] and are accompanied by increased interareal-phase coherence—akin to deep sleep—during prolonged MB episodes [7].

Despite these advances, methodological questions remain, particularly regarding how MB reports should be interpreted in TSMs. Although TSMs are easy to implement, prior studies have noted potential sources of response bias and other methodological issues ( for a review, see [8]). Reported MB rates during the SART range from 3% [7] to 15% [1], raising questions about how accurately individuals can identify and report MB. In particular, it seems possible that some MB reports reflect a failure in memory retrieval rather than genuine experiential absence. Consistent with this concern, a 2024 study found that some participants—especially those from the U.S.—reported that they could not understand and/or had never experienced MB [9]. Participants in that study also appeared to have difficulty in discriminating MB from sleepiness or dozing off, as indicated by a significant correlation between trait MB and daily sleepiness. Because sleepiness is known to reduce attentional stability and alter self-reported mental states, we expected higher sleepiness to decrease Focus reports and potentially increase MB reports or Forgot responses.

In addition, even though TSMs target the moment just before the probe is administered to reduce memory effects, they still rely on retrospective recall, which can be demanding for some individuals. People occasionally fail to retrieve the content of very recent experiences, and such failures need not reflect attentional lapses. For example, older adults with subjective memory complaints may forget what they have just read or heard [10], and “white dream” experiences illustrate that one may know a mental event occurred without being able to recall its content [11]. Thus, we expected older adults to select the “Forgot” option more frequently than younger participants. Importantly, these retrieval failures do not necessarily indicate sustained attentional decline or reduced working‑memory capacity. Rather, they reflect limitations in metacognitive access to the immediately preceding mental state—a process that is intrinsic to retrospective thought‑sampling. Thus, when participants are forced to choose among content‑based categories (e.g., MW, MB), uncertainty about their own recent experience may be inadvertently reported as MB.

Theoretically, the “Forgot” response can be understood by distinguishing between attentional and memory-related failures, as emphasized by Smilek et al. [12]. They argued that attention-related cognitive errors are conceptually distinct from general memory failures. Applying this distinction to TSM, we propose that “Forgot” reports capture momentary retrieval failures—instances where a mental state was present but could not be effectively encoded or retrieved due to the inherent demands of retrospective recall. This mechanism is distinct from MB, which represents a genuine absence of conscious content, and from the stable working memory capacity limits discussed by Adam et al. [13]. By providing a “Forgot” option, we isolate these memory-related retrieval errors from MB reports, thereby enhancing the construct validity of MB as a measure of experiential absence.

The present study addressed this ambiguity by systematically investigating whether including a Forgot option in thought-sampling probes can capture metacognitive uncertainty and thereby enhance the validity and interpretability of MB assessments. By disentangling memory-based uncertainty from genuine MB states, we sought to refine MB measurement and clarify the boundaries of MB within the spectrum of conscious experience.

Methods

Participants

The study participants were recruited via a data collection agency (CrowdWorks: https://crowdworks.co.jp/en). We aimed to recruit 200 participants, based on three considerations: (1) prior online studies using the SART on platforms such as MTurk and Prolific typically analyzed samples in the range of 100–200 participants (e; g; [12, 13]); (2) online experiments are known to incur substantial data loss due to failed attention checks or inadequate task performance, with exclusion rates of approximately 15%–30% being common; and (3) MB tends to occur at relatively low base rates compared with MW, with only ~ 15% of probe responses typically classified as MB [1]. We thus set a recruitment goal of 200 participants and ultimately obtained usable data from 203 participants naïve to the concept of MB. Among the 203 participants, 20 were excluded due to their satisficing behavior, fast responding, or low response accuracy based on common criteria (reaction time [RT] < the mean – 3 × the standard deviation (SD); hit rate < 0.5, and false alarm rate > 0.5). A final total of 183 Japanese participants were thus included in the present analyses (mean age, 43.6; SD, 8.9; range, 21–70; 146 women). All participants were included in the analyses regardless of whether they selected the Forgot option. All participants provided informed consent.

The sustained attention to response task (SART) with thought sampling

We administered an SART to the participants in order to assess fluctuations in sustained attention and momentary experiential state. Participants viewed a continuous stream of single-digit numbers (1–9) displayed centrally on a computer monitor at 100 px font size for 1500 ms, followed by a fixation cross (+) presented for 1000 ms. Participants were instructed to press the spacebar for all digits except 3, which served as the No-Go stimulus requiring response inhibition.

To capture participants’ internal states during the task, we incorporated a TSM using graphical probes. At random time points, participants were presented with a probe consisting of either five or six images, each representing a distinct experiential category (Fig. 1). Participants received prior instruction regarding the cognitive content represented by each image. The five options were: (1) Focused attention (Focus), (2) Task-related thoughts (TRTs), (3) External distraction (ED), (4) MW, and (5) MB. These categories were determined based on a commonly used classification of mental states described by van den Driessche et al. [14]. In the six-option condition, a sixth option (i.e., “Forgot”) was added, allowing participants to indicate that they could not recall or verbalize their mental state immediately prior to the probe. Participants selected the image that best described their mental state immediately preceding the probe by clicking directly on the image. After this selection, participants rated how sleepy they felt just before the probe on a nine-point Likert scale.

The choices in the six-option condition. The arrangement of each choice was randomly determined for each sampling. In the five-option condition, the “Forgot” choice was excluded. MB, mind blanking; MW, mind wandering

The task was administered in a within-subject crossover design with two sessions. Half of the participants (n = 94) completed the five-option condition first, followed by the six-option condition; the remaining participants (n = 89) completed the conditions in the reverse order. This design allowed direct within-participant comparisons between conditions, thereby controlling for between-participant variability and increasing the statistical power. Each session comprised 440 SART trials, yielding an average interval of 20 trials between probes. Probes were distributed pseudo‑randomly, with a minimum spacing of 18 trials to prevent clustering.

Questionnaires

We also investigated the association between the results obtained with the TSM and trait-level indices of MW and MB, anticipating significant positive correlations between the frequency of MW and MB reports and their corresponding trait measures.

Mind Wandering Questionnaire (MWQ)

The Mind Wandering Questionnaire (MWQ) is a single-factor questionnaire that assesses an individual’s tendency towards MW [15]. Its total score ranges from 5 to 30 points, with higher scores indicating higher levels of MW tendency. The Japanese version of the MWQ used in this study was that of Kajimura and Nomura [16].

Mind Blanking Questionnaire (MBQ)

The MB Questionnaire (MBQ) was developed to assess the MB tendency at the trait level, and it is patterned after the MWQ [9]. The MBQ was created and validated in both Japanese and English, showing measurement invariance across languages, genders, and age groups. The total score ranges from 5 to 30 points, with higher scores indicating higher levels of trait MB.

Statistical analyses

We conducted a quasi-binomial logistic regression analysis to assess the effects of probe-option condition and session on the distribution of reported mental states. Because the response data were overdispersed (variance exceeding the mean), we specified a quasi-binomial family in order to adjust standard errors accordingly. The dependent variable was the proportion of probes classified as each mental state (e.g., Focus, MW, MB) within each participant and condition (i.e., the number of probes within each category divided by the total number of probes). The external distraction (ED) category was set as the reference level because it was least frequently selected and showed minimal variation across experimental conditions. Model 1 included fixed effects for mental state, number of Options (5 and 6), and Session (1 vs. 2), as well as all factorial interactions to evaluate potential carryover effects in the crossover design. Model 2 added age to the factors examined in Model 1, and Model 3 added sleepiness to the factors examined in Model 1. We calculated McFadden’s pseudo-R2 to assess the explanatory power of the models relative to a null model.

For the behavioral data, we calculated the hit response time (hit RT) and the d′ in the period immediately preceding each probe to reflect participants’ cognitive state at the time of reporting. Specifically, those behavioral measures were computed from the three trials that immediately preceded each probe. This short window was chosen to capture performance fluctuations most proximal to the reported mental state while also balancing the need for temporal precision against having sufficient data for analysis. Given the relatively high probe frequency (5%), using a longer window (e.g., 10 trials) would have increased overlap between pre-probe periods and reduced the temporal specificity of the behavioral measures. For d′, No‑Go trials were treated as the signal: correct inhibitions were counted as hits and commission errors as false alarms. A log‑linear correction was applied when hit or false‑alarm rates were 0 or 1. To compare the performance across option conditions, sessions, and reported mental states, a linear mixed model with random intercepts for participants was used. Because Task‑Focus exhibited substantial session‑related variability in our dataset, External Distraction (ED) was used as the reference category to provide a stable baseline for interpreting state‑related effects. This approach enabled us to account for individual variability as a random effect while isolating the influences of mental state and task conditions on the behavioral performance.

Finally, we conducted rank-order correlation analyses between the external psychological scales and the TSM-derived measures to evaluate trait–state associations. To assess the robustness of those associations, we also performed nonparametric bootstrap analyses of the correlations. Specifically, 2000 bootstrap samples were generated by resampling participants with replacement within each option condition, and the resulting empirical distributions were used to derive 95% percentile-based confidence intervals (95%CIs).

Results

Frequencies of reported mental states

The mean frequencies (± SE) of reported mental states across conditions are shown in Fig. 2. Most participants did not select the Forgot option, especially in session 1; the frequency of the Forgot option was 2.2 ± 0.5% in session 1 and 7.2 ± 1.2% in session 2. A direct comparison among only participants who reported the ‘Forgot’ option at least once revealed a significant difference between sessions: t[73] = 8.96 (p < 0.001). Among the other five options, a similar pattern was observed across the five- and six-option conditions: Focus was reported most frequently, followed in order by TRT, ED, MB, and MW (Fig. 2). A complementary one-sample one-tailed t-test indicated that the proportion of the Forgot responses was significantly greater than zero across participants (t[182] = 6.99, p < 0.001), suggesting that memory-based uncertainty was meaningfully present in the sample.

Frequencies of reported mental states during the sustained attention to response task (SART) (n = 183). ED, external distraction; Fgt, Forgot; MB, mind blanking; MW, mind wandering; TRT, task-related thought

The state frequencies were moderately to highly stable across sessions. Spearman rank correlations between session‑wise proportions (Session 1 vs. Session 2) were ρ = 0.73 for Focus, 0.64 for TRT, 0.52 for ED, 0.67 for MW, and 0.59 for MB (all ps < 0.001). The Forgot option was presented in only one session and therefore was not included in this analysis. These results indicate that relative individual differences in reporting patterns are reproducible across sessions.

Logistic regression: effects of options and sessions

The logistic regression analysis (Model 1) tested whether the six-option condition (i.e., inclusion of the Forgot option) altered the distribution of mental‑state reports. Relative to the reference category (ED), Focus (β = 1.88 [OR = 6.57, 95%CI = 4.64–9.46], t = 10.39, p < 0.001) and TRT (β = 0.56 [OR = 1.75, 95%CI: 1.21–2.58], t = 2.89, p = 0.004) were more likely. Because Forgot was not available in the five-option condition, interaction terms involving Forgot (e.g., Forgot × option condition; Forgot × option condition × session) were not estimable. It is noteworthy that, even with the absence of the Forgot option in half of the trials (i.e., in the five-option condition), the coefficient for the Forgot option was not significant (β = −0.64 [OR = 0.53, 95%CI: 0.26–1.01], t = − 1.84, p = 0.065), indicating that we obtained no reliable evidence of a difference from ED option, which is one of the common choices in TSMs [14].

We also observed no effect of Option (five vs. six options: β = −0.15 [OR = 0.86, 95%CI: 0.54–1.36], t = − 0.65, p = 0.514) and its interactions with each choice (ps > 0.327), and Session (1st vs. 2nd session: β = −0.21 [OR = 0.81, 95%CI: 0.51–1.28], t = − 0.90, p = 0.37) and its interactions with each choice (ps > 0.106), indicating no carryover effects in the data. No other significant interaction was identified in Model 1 which showed acceptable fit (McFadden’s pseudo-R2McF = 0.39).

Effects of age and sleepiness

Model 2 extended Model 1 by adding age as a fixed effect. Although overall model fit did not surpass that of Model 1 (McFadden’s pseudo-R2McF = 0.38), age significantly influenced Focus reports (β = 0.03 [OR = 1.03, 95%CI: 1.01–1.05], t = 2.64, p = 0.008), indicating that the older participants were more likely to report being focused. No significant main effect of option condition or its interaction was observed.

Model 3 extended Model 1 by adding sleepiness as a fixed effect. Sleepiness significantly reduced the likelihood of Focus reports (β = −0.27 [OR = 0.74, 95%CI: 0.67–0.82], t = − 3.62, p < 0.001). Again, no significant main effect of option condition or interaction effects were detected, and the explanatory power did not exceed that of Models 1 and 2 (McFadden’s pseudo-R2McF = 0.41).

Task performance across probe conditions

To examine whether the Forgot option influenced sustained-attention performance, we applied the same model structure as in Model 1 to the task performances indices (i.e., hit RT, RT variability, and d′). The behavioral data were not explained by mental state or its interactions with option condition and session (the marginal R2 values were 0.004, 0.004, and 0.091 for the hit RT, RT variability [SD], and d′, respectively). In contrast, individual differences explained a substantial proportion of the variance in hit RT (conditional R2s = 0.502) but not in RT variability (SD) or d′ (the conditional R2 values were 0.114 and 0.153, respectively). No significant effects of option condition, session, or their interaction on hit RT, RT variability (SD), or d′ were observed (ps > 0.167; Fig. 3). As further detailed in the Supplementary Material (see Table S1 for hit RT, Table S2 for RT variability (SD), and Table S3 for d′), these results indicate that the inclusion of the Forgot option did not affect task performance.

Trait–state associations

Finally, we examined whether including the Forgot option improved the specificity of MB reports. The expected association was confirmed in both option conditions for MW, whereas it differed between option conditions for MB (Table 1). Bootstrap analyses revealed that the MBQ–MB correlation was substantially more stable in the six‑option condition: its 95% bootstrap confidence interval did not include zero, whereas the interval for the five‑option condition did. This pattern suggests that the Forgot option clarified the boundaries of MB by absorbing ambiguous responses.

Discussion

We investigated whether the inclusion of a Forgot option in thought sampling probes could improve the interpretability of MB reports by distinguishing “pure” blank states from memory retrieval failures. Although the Forgot option was selected infrequently overall by the participants, the presence of the Forgot option revealed subtle but meaningful patterns in introspective reporting.

First, the low frequency of Forgot responses suggests that the participants rarely defaulted to this option. However, the significant result of the one-sample t-test confirms that the Forgot option was meaningfully selected across the participants, demonstrating that memory‑based uncertainty is a non‑negligible component of introspective experience. Importantly, the logistic regression revealed no significant difference between the Forgot option and the baseline category (i.e., external distraction, one of the common choices in TSMs [14]), which suggests that Forgot responses may occupy a certain amount of an individual’s mental status during an SART. The reduction in MB reports when “Forgot” was available suggests that some ambiguous cases—likely reflecting uncertainty in reconstructing the prior mental state—had previously been classified as MB and were reallocated to the “Forgot” category. Theoretically, this reallocation aligns with the distinction between attention-related and memory-related errors. As Smilek et al. [12] argued that these cognitive failures are conceptually distinct, providing an explicit “Forgot” option allows for the isolation of memory-related retrieval failures from genuine attentional or experiential absences (i.e., MB). This reallocation clarifies the boundaries of MB by providing an explicit outlet for the metacognitive uncertainty and thereby increases the specificity of its measurement.

The absence of significant effects of option condition and session suggests that including the Forgot option did not systematically distort the distribution of the other mental states. This is particularly relevant given prior concerns about response biases in thought-sampling methods [8]. In addition, behavioral performance (the hit RT, RT variability, and d′) did not vary significantly across the conditions. These results support the inclusion of the Forgot option as a meaningful category, rather than a disruptive or confusing addition. The overall pattern of reports (i.e., high frequencies of Focus and task‑related thought, and relatively low frequencies of MW and MB) closely aligns with previous SART findings in Japanese samples [17, 18], further supporting the robustness of the present data. Moreover, the moderate-to-high between-session stability observed for most state frequencies indicates that participants’ reporting tendencies were not driven solely by momentary fluctuations or task‑specific noise, but instead reflect reproducible individual patterns. This reliability further supports the interpretive validity of the state proportions used in the present analyses and indicates that the state categories adopted from a previous study [14] function robustly in our sample as well.

Finally, we observed that older adults were more likely to report being focused, and that increased sleepiness was associated with reduced focus. Although we predicted that older adults might select the Forgot option more frequently due to age-related reduction in memory and/or metacognitive access, the age effect on Focus aligns with previous findings that older adults often show greater attentional stability during SART [19]. Likewise, the association between higher sleepiness and reduced focus is consistent with prior evidence that momentary drowsiness impairs attentional control [20].

Our trait-level analyses revealed expected associations between participants’ MW reports and their MWQ scores across both probe conditions, consistent with previous works showing modest state–trait associations in mind-wandering (typically around r = 0.2–0.35 [15, 18, 21]). In contrast, MB reports were associated with MBQ scores only in the six-option condition. This supports our current hypothesis that traditional MB measures may conflate genuine blankness with memory lapses. According to Smallwood & Schooler [22], when attention decouples from the task, the internal monitoring of one’s mental state is diminished, leading to impoverished encoding of thoughts. When a probe occurs, this lack of encoded content manifests as a “Forgot” report—a momentary failure to retrieve what was just present. Thus, incorporating a Forgot option strengthens the construct validity of the SART. Because sustained attention and memory for recent experience are closely linked [23], retrieval failures may be misinterpreted as attentional lapses (i.e., MB) when no explicit alternative is provided. A further consideration concerns the content of the MBQ itself. One of its items (i.e., “There are moments that I can’t remember what I was just thinking about”) reflects a trait‑level tendency toward reduced metacognitive access, rather than a momentary retrieval failure. In contrast, the Forgot option in our TSM captures state‑level uncertainty on specific trials. Without such an option, these momentary retrieval failures may be forced into content‑based categories, weakening the correspondence between trait MBQ scores and state MB reports. Although the absence of reportable content in both MB and “Forgot” states could lead to inverse misclassifications (i.e., reporting “Forgot” when MB was the actual state), the improved state-trait correlation observed in the six-option condition suggests that the “Forgot” option primarily functioned to isolate retrieval-based noise. By filtering this state-level noise, the “Forgot” option ensures that MB reports more accurately reflect the experiential void, thereby enhancing the psychological precision of MB assessments.

Taken together, our findings suggest that the inclusion of a Forgot option enhances the interpretive clarity of MB assessments without introducing systematic bias. By refining the taxonomy of experiential states, this study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of MB and its measurement. The Forgot option offers a practical tool for disentangling introspective uncertainty from genuine blankness, especially in populations whose memory reliability and/or metacognitive access may be compromised. To our knowledge, no prior studies have directly examined the correspondence between trait MB (as measured by the MBQ) and state MB reports during a task. The present findings therefore provide an initial demonstration of this trait–state link. Future research should explore the generalizability of this study’s findings across cultural contexts and clinical populations, and further investigations should examine the neural correlates of Forgot versus MB states by using neuroimaging or electrophysiological methods. Ultimately, improving the granularity of thought sampling not only deepens our grasp of cognitive lapses but also enhances the methodological precision with which we study the boundaries of conscious experience.

Despite these contributions, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the frequency of Forgot responses was relatively low, particularly in the initial session, which may limit the statistical power to detect subtle effects or interactions. Second, although the inclusion of the Forgot option was designed to disentangle memory failure from MB, the retrospective nature of the TSM still relies on the participants’ metacognitive access and interpretive judgment, which may vary across individuals. Third, the study was conducted with a Japanese population that was recruited online, and cultural or linguistic factors may influence the interpretation and reportability of MB and Forgot experiences. Finally, although this study’s behavioral measures did not show significant variation across conditions, the absence of valid neurophysiological and/or ecological data limits our ability to validate the experiential categories with objective markers. These limitations highlight the need for multimodal approaches including thought sampling in daily life situations, and cross-cultural replications to further refine the measurement of MB.
