Trends in 10th Revision of the International Classification of Diseases–Coded CKD Incidence Among Chinese Adults
Authors: Zhiqing Zeng, Si Cheng, Yizhen Hu, Chao Yang, Canqing Yu, Dianjianyi Sun, Pei Pei, Ling Yang, Yiping Chen, Huaidong Du, Kaifei Zhu, Dan Schmidt, Junshi Chen, Zhengming Chen, Liming Li, Luxia Zhang, Jun Lv, China Kadoorie Biobank Collaborative Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a growing public health concern, yet evidence on its incidence is limited in China.MethodsUsing data from the China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB), which recruited 512,723 participants aged 30 to 79 years from 10 areas across China, we analyzed 5 major CKD subtypes: diabetic kidney disease (DKD), hypertensive nephropathy (HTN), glomerulonephritis (GN), chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis (CTIN), and obstructive nephropathy (ON). Total CKD included the aforementioned subtypes, CKD due to other reasons, or chronic kidney failure. Outcomes were ascertained through linkages to local death and disease registries and health insurance databases until December 31, 2018. Poisson regression model was used to assess secular trends and population variations.ResultsOver a mean of 9.5 years of follow-up, 5662 total CKD cases were identified. The crude incidence rate (per 100,000 person-years) of total CKD was 119.7 (95% confidence interval: 116.6–122.8) between 2009 and 2018, with an annual increase of 8.9% (7.9%–10.0%) after adjusting for age, sex, and study area. The crude incidence rates were highest in GN, whereas biggest increases were observed in ON, DKD, and HTN. Total CKD incidence rate was similar between urban and rural areas; however, those of different subtypes varied: DKD or CTIN were higher in urban areas, and ON was significantly higher in rural areas. Males, older adults, and participants with hypertension or diabetes mellitus were at higher risks.ConclusionDespite the underestimation of CKD incidence because of outcome ascertainment methods, the incidence of total CKD and its subtypes increased in the CKB population during 2009 to 2018. Measures should be taken against major driving factors, such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and kidney stones.Keywords: adult, Chinese, chronic kidney disease, disease subtype, incidence rate, secular trend

CKD has received widespread attention over the past decades as a growing global public health concern.1 CKD is characterized by high prevalence rate, poor prognosis, and high medical costs.2 Patients with CKD are at significantly higher risk of developing kidney failure, cardiovascular disease, and dying prematurely, thus imposing a heavy disease and economic burden.2,3

Based on clinical etiologies, there are 5 major subtypes of CKD, namely DKD, HTN, GN, CTIN, and ON.4 GN used to be one of the leading causes of CKD worldwide5; however, the CKD spectrum has shifted in recent decades because of changes in risk factors and the rising prevalence of diabetes mellitus and hypertension.6 According to the latest study of the Global Burden of Disease, the age-standardized incidence rates of CKD increased from 217.6 to 233.7 per 100,000 persons between 2010 and 2019, with its rank rising from 53rd to 51st among causes of disease burden.7 The age-standardized incidence rates of CKD owing to type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension increased during the time.7 Previous studies, mostly conducted in developed countries such as Europe and Japan, focused on the patterns of incident kidney failure due to different causes.8, 9, 10 Only a few studies have reported trends in the overall incidence of CKD,11, 12, 13 none of which differentiated between CKD subtypes, and the results varied across regions. There have been reports on the prevalence of CKD in the Chinese population14; however, research on the trends in incidence of CKD and its subtypes was inadequate. Understanding the epidemiological characteristics of CKD and its subtypes could aid in the identification of health care priorities and the development of effective public health strategies, adding value to the present evidence on CKD prevalence because it might overrepresent participants with better prognoses.

Using data from a nationwide prospective cohort of Chinese adults, this study aimed to quantify the incidence of total CKD and its subtypes based on the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) diagnosis from 2009 to 2018; and examine the secular trends as well as regional and population variations.

Methods

Study Population

We used data from the CKB, a large nationwide prospective cohort study that enrolled 512,723 participants aged 30 to 79 years between 2004 and 2008 from 5 urban and 5 rural areas across China. Details of the study design, methods, and procedures have been reported previously.15,16 In brief, all participants signed informed consent forms, completed an interviewer-administered questionnaire, and had anthropometric measurements taken at baseline. Long-term follow-up was started for all participants soon after the baseline survey. By December 31, 2018, only 4027 (0.8%) participants were lost to follow-up. The Ethical Review Committee of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (Beijing, China) and the Oxford Tropical Research Ethics Committee, University of Oxford (UK) approved the study.

Outcome Ascertainment and Definitions

The mortality, morbidity, and all hospitalization events were identified through linkages with local death and disease registries and health insurance claim databases using unique personal identification numbers, supplemented by annual active follow-ups. The ICD-10 was used to code all disease diagnoses and causes of death by trained staff who did not know the baseline information.

The primary CKD outcomes were DKD, HTN, GN, CTIN, ON, and CKD due to other reasons (others). We referred to the China Kidney Disease Network 2016 Annual Data Report to identify the above-mentioned outcomes with ICD-10 codes (Table 1).4 Membranous nephropathy (MN), an important subtype of primary GN, was considered in the analysis. In addition, chronic kidney failure (ICD-10 code, N18) was commonly used as a definition of CKD in previous epidemiologic studies.17 Therefore, we defined total CKD as having any of the following diagnoses: DKD, HTN, GN, CTIN, ON, others, and chronic kidney failure. Only the first documented diagnosis of the same disease subtype was considered in the corresponding analysis. Participants with multiple diagnoses of different CKD subtypes were considered separately in the corresponding analyses.

Assessment of Covariates

A laptop-based questionnaire was used to assess baseline sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age and sex) and personal medical history, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and CKD. Built-in functions were set to avoid missing items and logic errors. Blood pressure was measured using calibrated instruments following standard procedures. Random plasma glucose was measured immediately on-site. The measurement procedures have been described previously.18,19 Participants were considered having hypertension at baseline if they reported physician-diagnosed hypertension, had measured systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mm Hg, or were taking antihypertensive medication.18 Participants were considered having diabetes mellitus at baseline if they reported a physician-diagnosed diabetes, had a random plasma glucose level ≥ 11.1 mmol/l, or a fasting plasma glucose level ≥ 7.0 mmol/l.19 The disease status of diabetes (E10–E14) and hypertension (H35.0, I10–I12, I15.0, I15.1, I15.9, I67.4, and O13) was updated through established registries during long-term follow-up.

Statistical Analysis

The baseline survey was completed in July 2008. All participants were fully linked to the health insurance database from 2009 onwards. We hence restricted the study period from January 1, 2009 to December 31, 2018. We excluded the following participants: (i) those who reported physician-diagnosed CKD at baseline (n = 7574); (ii) those who died (n = 6456) or were lost to follow-up (n = 180) before January 1, 2009. In the analysis of total CKD, we excluded participants who had any newly documented subtype of CKD or chronic kidney failure before 2009 (n = 357), and a total of 498,291 participants were left. In analyses for different subtypes of CKD, we only excluded participants who had newly documented corresponding subtype or chronic kidney failure before 2009.

The background characteristics of participants with or without total CKD and its subtypes were compared with adjustment for age, sex, and study area using a logistic regression model for categorical variables and analysis of variance for continuous variables to control the confounding effects of age, sex, and study area. The Poisson regression model was used to examine the incidence rates of total CKD and its subtypes. The follow-up data of the study population were transformed into panel data based on calendar year. Participants who developed the outcomes of interest were followed-up with until the index date of outcomes. Participants who did not develop the outcomes of interest were followed-up with until death, loss to follow-up, or December 31, 2018, whichever came first. The status of incident total CKD and its subtypes (dependent variable) and the duration of follow-up were calculated in each calendar year. Participants’ ages and disease statuses for hypertension and diabetes were updated at the start of each year from 2009 to 2018. We analyzed incidence rates and annual changes for total CKD and its subtypes overall and by the following factors: region (urban and rural residency), sex (male and female), age groups (< 60, 60–69, and ≥ 70 years), and disease status (without diabetes and hypertension, diabetes alone, hypertension alone, and both diabetes and hypertension). Models were adjusted for annually updated age, sex, and 10 study areas, as appropriate. We applied the direct standardization method to obtain age-, sex-, and region-standardized incidence rates and annual changes, with the CKB population in 2009 as the reference population in the corresponding analyses. In addition to further strengthen the robustness of the incidence modelling, we performed analyses using Poisson regression models with robust standard errors.

In addition, we examined regional and population variations in the incidence rates of total CKD and its subtypes during the study period. In addition to annually updated age, sex, and 10 study areas, models were adjusted for calendar year in the analyses. The proportions of different CKD subtypes in total newly incident cases were reported each year, both overall and by residency. Only the first documented diagnosis of each CKD subtypes was considered, and those with chronic kidney failure alone were excluded from the analyses as they could not be classified into any subtypes of CKD.

All statistical analyses were done with Stata Statistical Software: Release 17.0 (StataCorp, 2021). Graphs were plotted using R (version 3.5.3 and version 4.2.3), with R packages Jasper (version 2-239), ggplot2 (version 4.0.1), and reshape2 (version 1.4.5). A 2-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant Characteristics

The mean age (SD) of the 498,291 participants included in the analysis of total CKD at the beginning of 2009 was 53.7 (10.6); 40.8% were males, and 55.8% were rural residents. Between 2009 and 2018, 5671 total CKD cases were identified during a mean follow-up of 9.5 (SD: 1.6) years. We recorded 1277 DKD, 412 HTN, 1511 GN, 469 CTIN, 612 ON, and 535 others during the study period.

Participants who developed total CKD during follow-up were older and had a higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes at the beginning of the study than those who did not. For these newly identified total CKD cases, the mean age (SD) at the first documented diagnosis was 63.9 (10.7) (Table 2). Regarding different CKD subtypes, the mean age at corresponding disease diagnosis was older in DKD and HTN, but younger in ON. In comparison to other CKD subtypes, a significantly higher proportion of CTIN cases (77.3%) were females, and a higher proportion of ON cases (83.2%) dwelled in rural areas (Supplementary Table S1). The background characteristics of male and female participants with incident treated CKD subtypes during 2009 to 2018 were listed in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3, respectively.

Secular Trend in Incidence of Total CKD and its Subtypes

The crude incidence rate (per 100,000 person-years) for CKD was 119.9 (95% confidence interval: 116.8–123.0) between 2009 and 2018 (Table 3). After adjusting for age, sex, and study area, the incidence rates increased from 76.1 (67.7–84.5) in 2009 to 166.4 (155.1–177.6) in 2018 accompanied by an annual increase of 8.9% (7.9%–10.0%) (Figure 1a). Similar results were obtained when the standardized method was applied (9.7% [7.1%–12.4%]) (Supplementary Figure S1A).

From 2009 to 2018, the crude incidence rates (per 100,000 person-years) for different CKD subtypes, in descending order, were as follows: GN (31.9 [30.2–33.5]), DKD (26.9 [25.4–28.4]), ON (12.9 [11.9–13.9]), CTIN (9.9 [9.0–10.8]), and HTN (8.7 [7.8–9.5]) (Table 3). During the study period, the highest increase in incidence occurred in ON, with an adjusted annual increase of 35.6% (31.1%–40.3%). It should be noted that the incidence rate of ON steeply increased from 2015 to 2018, reaching 47.4/100,000 person-years in 2018 (Figure 1c). The adjusted annualized increase for DKD was 8.4% (6.2%–10.6%), with a similar increasing trend from 2015 to 2018 (Figure 1b). The incidence rate of GN had shifted from increasing to decreasing, with a minor overall increase of 3.4% (1.5%–5.3%) (Figure 1b). However, the incidence rate of MN increased over the study period, with an adjusted annual increase of 19.8% (9.5%–31.0%) (Supplementary Table S4). Besides, the incidence rates of HTN (adjust annual increase: 7.0%), CTIN (3.8%), and others (12.5%) all steadily increased during follow-up (Figure 1c). Little discrepancies in annualized changes were found after the standardized method was applied (Supplementary Figure S1). Further sensitivity analysis with Poisson regression using robust standard errors showed no significant changes in incidence rates and temporal trends (Supplementary Figure S1).

From 2009 to 2018, rural residents had a greater increment in annualized change in total CKD than urban residents (11.4% vs. 5.9%), mostly because of the faster growth of DKD and ON in rural areas (Supplementary Figure S2). Male participants had higher annual increases in total CKD and its subtypes than females, except for CTIN (Supplementary Figure S3). Compared with participants aged ≥ 70 years, those aged < 70 years had higher annual increases in total CKD and its subtypes, except for HTN, where a higher annualized increase was observed in the ≥70-year group (Supplementary Figure S4). The secular trends in incidence rates for total CKD and its subtypes were generally consistent across different comorbidity conditions (Supplementary Figure S5).

In 2009, the top 3 CKD subtypes in descending order were GN, DKD, and ON, accounting for 73.5% of all incident cases (Supplementary Figure S6). Subsequently, the proportions of GN decreased, whereas the proportion for DKD and ON increased. Since 2016, DKD has become the leading cause of total CKD, followed by GN and ON, accounting for 76.5% of all incident cases in 2018. The compositions of CKD subtypes differed significantly between urban and rural residencies. DKD and GN remained the leading causes among urban residents. GN ranked top among rural residents until 2015, followed by significant increases in DKD and ON, which accounted for 64.0% of all incident cases in 2018.

Regional and Population Variations in Incidence of Total CKD and its Subtypes

The adjusted incidence rates of total CKD (per 100,000 person-years) were similar between urban (122.3 [117.7–127.0]) and rural residents (118.8 [114.5–123.0]) (Table 3). However, the incidence rates of different subtypes varied across regions. The DKD and CTIN had greater incidence rate in urban than in rural areas. In contrast, rural residents had a significantly higher incidence rate of ON than urban residents.

Male participants had a higher adjusted incidence rate of total CKD (129.0 [123.9–134.1]) than female participants (114.5 [110.5–118.5]) (P < 0.001) (Table 3). Such a sex distinction was observed for ON and others. In contrast, female participants had a significantly higher incidence rate of CTIN than males.

The incidence rates of total CKD increased steadily with age, with adjusted rates (per 100,000 person-years) of 78.9 (75.5–82.4), 139.8 (133.3–146.3), and 219.2 (208.9–229.5) in the age groups < 60, 60–69, and ≥ 70 years, respectively (Table 3, Figure 2). Except for ON, which had its highest incidence in the group aged 50 to 54 years and then tended to decrease with age, the incidence for all CKD subtypes tended to increase with age.

Participants with either hypertension or diabetes had higher incidence rates of total CKD and almost all subtypes when compared to those without either disease (Table 3). The incidence rate of total CKD was highest among participants with both hypertension and diabetes (520.3 [489.4–551.1] per 100,000 person-years), 7-fold greater than that among participants without either disease (64.9 [61.8–68.0] per 100,000 person-years). The difference in incidence rates because of comorbidity conditions was even more pronounced in DKD.

Discussion

In this large prospective cohort of Chinese adults, the incidence rates of total CKD and its subtypes increased from 2009 to 2018, with ON, DKD, and HTN showing the greatest increases during the study period. The incidence rates of coded GN shifted from rising to declining, with only a minor overall increase. The 3 most common CKD subtypes were GN, DKD, and ON, accounting for approximately three-quarters of all incident cases in 2009. However, the proportion of GN declined over time, with DKD and ON accounting for the biggest proportions in urban and rural areas, respectively, by the end of the study.

Comparison With Other Studies

The crude incidence rate from 2009 to 2018 for total CKD in the current population was 119.9/100,000 person-years, lower than that in cohorts from Japan20 and South Korea21,22 at the same period. Aside from the actual differences in incidence levels between populations, the various definitions of outcomes used across studies may explain at least some of the observed difference. The current study identified CKD cases based on ICD-10 codes in medical records. A previous study of type 2 diabetes patients from the UK primary care database suggested that this method would underestimate the incidence of coded CKD when compared with confirmed CKD cases (≥ 2 estimated glomerular filtration rate measurements < 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 ≥ 90 days apart, ≤ 15 months apart).23 Similarly, another study of CKD hospitalizations in Chinese adults found that, when compared with the golden criteria, CKD identification based on ICD-10 codes was low in sensitivity (34.2%) but high in specificity (97.8%), indicating an underestimation of CKD in practice.24 In contrast, when CKD is diagnosed based solely on a single estimated glomerular filtration rate or urinary albumin measurement, as in the aforementioned studies,20, 21, 22 the incidence rates tended to be overestimated.25 However, an analysis of the Integrated Primary Care Information database in the Netherlands found that even when 2 consecutive laboratory test results (≥ 90 days apart) were used to define CKD, the incidence rate (479/100,000 person-years) was still higher than that in our study.26

The current population experienced an increase in the incidence of coded CKD from 2009 to 2018, with the incidence rate of total CKD reaching 166.4/100,000 person-years in 2018. This figure is comparable with the age-standardized rate of CKD (161.52/100,000 person-years) reported in the 2019 Global Burden of Disease study, which also indicated an increasing trend in the age-standardized rate of CKD in China between 1990 and 2019.27 Only a few studies have examined the temporal trends in CKD incidence, and the results were mixed. From 2008 to 2017, the hospitalization rate for CKD in Espírito Santo, Brazil, increased by 6.23%/yr, and when stratified by age, the increases for those aged 20 to 49 years and ≥ 50 years were 3.63% and 6.96%, respectively.11 Another study from Northern Brazil during the same period showed that the hospitalization rate for CKD among those aged 20 to 49 years remained stable overall, but with an inconsistent trend across states.12 According to an analysis of national discharge databases in Spain, the hospitalization rate for CKD decreased from 2011 to 2017, reaching 10.72 of 10,000 people in 2017.13

In recent decades, diabetes mellitus and hypertension epidemics have resulted in rapid growth of DKD and HTN in all developed countries and many developing countries, and GN is no longer the leading cause of CKD.1 The epidemiologic transition started earlier in the Western. According to data from the European Renal Association-European Dialysis and Transplant Association Registry, the proportion of GN among kidney failure patients decreased from 1965 to 2015, with a ≤ 51% decrease from 1965 to 1974 and 10% from 2005 to 2015, whereas the proportions of DKD (0.5% vs. 24%) and HTN (2% vs. 11%) increased rapidly.28 According to the Korean Society of Nephrology end-stage renal disease registry, the proportion of GN among patients with kidney failure had decreased continuously over the past 30 years, from approximately 40% in 1985 to 8.2% in 2014. Although there has been a significant increase in the proportion of DKD, accounting for 48.0% of kidney failure cases in 2014,29 the proportion of HTN increased from 1985 to 1994, before stabilizing at approximately 20%.29

A study based on the Hospital Quality Monitoring System, a national database covering 74% of tertiary hospitals in China, included 1.8 million patients hospitalized for CKD.24 The results showed that the proportion of GN among CKD hospitalizations decreased from 23.3% in 2010 to 15.1% in 2015.24 In contrast, the proportions of DKD (19.5% vs. 24.3%) and HTN (11.5% vs. 15.9%) increased between 2010 and 2015, with urban residents experiencing greater increases than rural residents.24 Simultaneously, the proportion of ON has increased rapidly over time, particularly among rural residents, where it became the leading cause of hospitalized CKD in 2015.24 The findings from the current population-based study differ slightly from those mentioned above. In our population, GN remained the most common subtype from 2010 to 2015, accounting for around one-third of all incident cases. The difference in the findings between these 2 studies can be explained to some extent by the different geographical and hospital-level coverage. Moreover, the Hospital Quality Monitoring System–based study reported annual prevalent cases and might have overrepresented subtypes that are incurable. We further added evidence of secular trend in incidence of total CKD and its subtypes. The highest increase in incidence was observed in ON, especially among rural residents after 2015, which might be associated with the increasing prevalence of kidney stones among Chinese adults. A meta-analysis indicated that the prevalence rates of kidney stones in China were 5.95%, 8.86%, and 10.63% during the periods of 1991 to 2000, 2001 to 2010, and 2011 to 2016, respectively.30 In addition, with the advancement of China’s health care system reform (e.g., the "Healthy China 2030" initiative launched in 2016) , the capacity for screening kidney diseases in rural areas has progressively improved. This enhancement may have increased the detection rate of asymptomatic or mild kidney stone–induced obstruction, leading to more documented ON cases after 2015. Although the incidence rate of GN shifted from increasing to declining during the study period, the incidence of MN continued to increase. This trend may be attributed to increased awareness, better diagnosis, and improved access to health care in China.31 In addition, a large-scale study of 71,151 renal biopsies from 2004 to 2014 across 282 Chinese cities reported an annual increase of 13% in the frequency of MN. Long-term exposure to high levels of PM2.5 and higher average air quality index has been linked to an elevated risk of MN, whereas the proportions of other major glomerulopathies remained stable.32 Although the proportions of DKD and HTN to total incident cases did not appear to be increasing in the CKB population, their incidence rates did. Potential explanations for long-term transitions in different subtypes of CKD included increasing prevalence of diabetes and hypertension,33,34 widespread adoption of western lifestyles,35 the obesity epidemic,36 and the prevalence of kidney stones.30

A steep increase in overall CKD incidence with age has been observed in almost all studies.11,12,25,26,37,38 In the present study, with the exception of ON, all CKD subtypes had age-related increases in incidence rates, particularly observed in DKD, HTN, and GN. Multiple aspects of body function declined with age, and older adults experienced a physiological decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate.39 Simultaneously, the increasing prevalence of major comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension can lead to long-term kidney damage,40 as evidenced by significantly higher incidence rates of total CKD and the 3 aforementioned subtypes in subpopulations with diabetes and/or hypertension.

Established studies yielded mixed results regarding sex differences in overall CKD incidence: males higher than females,11,13,37 vice versa,25,26,38 or no difference.12 One possible explanation is that those analyses did not differentiate between CKD subtypes. In our population, CTIN had a higher incidence among female participants, whereas ON and others had a higher incidence among male participants. Although there was no statistically significant sex difference in DKD, HTN, and GN, their numerical incidence rates were all higher in males than in females, resulting in total CKD being higher in males than in females. Kidney stone is one of the most common causes of ON, and the prevalence of kidney stones was higher in Chinese men than in women,30,41 which may contribute to the sex difference in ON incidence. Physiological differences between males and females, such as the protective effects of estrogens in women and the damaging effects of testosterone,42 and men being more likely to adopt unhealthy lifestyles, may also contribute to the higher incidence of CKD in men.

Similarly, we found that the differences in CKD incidence between urban and rural areas varied by subtypes. The incidence of coded DKD and CTIN was higher among urban residents than among rural residents, whereas ON was the inverse. To some extent, this difference was consistent with the distributions of risk factors for each subtype across regions. Diabetes mellitus was more prevalent in urban areas of China,43 whereas kidney stones were more common in rural areas.41 The uneven distribution of environmental nephrotoxins (e.g., aristolochic acid, heavy metals) may also contribute to the observed disparities in CKD incidence between urban and rural areas. Medications, such as antibiotics, nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs, and antivirals, were one of the most common causes of CTIN.44 There had been no previous evidence on sex or regional differences in CTIN incidence, and more studies are expected.

Strengths and Limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first population-based study that provides detailed information on secular trends as well as regional and population variations in CKD incidence owing to different causes. Even though the prospective cohort was not intended to be representative of the general population, the inclusion of a large number of participants from diverse populations should aid in understanding the distributions of CKD and its subtypes in the Chinese population. The linkage to local death and disease registries and health insurance claim databases enables us to identify as many incident CKD cases as possible and differentiate between CKD subtypes. However, we identified the outcomes using ICD-10 codes for clinical disease diagnoses rather than relying on a sustained reduction in estimated glomerular filtration rate for > 3 months. According to a previous study, the former method had low sensitivity but high specificity, thus some patients with early CKD may go undetected.24 In addition, the proportions of missing patients may differ by CKD subtypes. For the health insurance claim system, which was one of the 3 sources of CKD cases, we only included patients who required hospitalization. This made the disease diagnoses more reliable but might also miss mild outpatients. As a result, the incidence of coded CKD and its subtypes in our population may be underestimated to a different extent. Our findings are more likely to show an increase in the incidence of more severe CKD, which posed a greater burden on public health and required more medical resources.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate an increasing incidence of clinically coded CKD and its subtypes among Chinese adults from 2009 to 2018, with the greatest increases observed in ON, DKD, and HTN. There were significant differences in the incidence of CKD subtypes across subpopulations. These results merely reflect the burden of more advanced or clinically detected disease. Nevertheless, the finding underscores the urgent need for prevention and earlier detection strategies.
