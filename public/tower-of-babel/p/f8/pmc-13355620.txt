Supernumerary kidney associated with horseshoe kidney: A very rare congenital renal anomaly
Authors: Bedilu Zewdu Asmare, Mulualem Amare Woldemichael, Ibrahim Hussen Kedir, Rebi Ali Oumer, Getachew Sahile Alemu, Dereje Assefa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supernumerary kidney is a rare congenital anomaly, and its coexistence with a horseshoe kidney is exceedingly uncommon. We report the case of an 18-year-old female who presented with a 1-month history of non-specific left flank pain. Abdominal ultrasound revealed a left native kidney with mild hydronephrosis and a caudally located accessory kidney fused superiorly to the native kidney and inferiorly to the lower pole of the right kidney, forming a horseshoe configuration. Computed tomography (CT) urography confirmed a small caudal supernumerary kidney contributing to the parenchymal isthmus and showed a malrotated accessory renal pelvis, while the native left and right renal pelvises were normally oriented. Mild hydronephrosis was limited to the native left kidney, with no evidence of obstruction. The patient was managed conservatively with clinical and imaging follow-up. Follow up ultrasound after 2 weeks demonstrated complete resolution of hydronephrosis and the patient remained asymptomatic. This case highlights an exceptionally rare combination of a supernumerary kidney with horseshoe fusion and underscores the critical role of imaging in accurate diagnosis, anatomical delineation and ongoing follow-up.

Introduction

Supernumerary kidney is an extremely rare congenital renal anomaly defined by the presence of an additional kidney with a distinct blood supply, collecting system, and renal capsule. Its occurrence in combination with a horseshoe kidney is even more uncommon. Although many individuals remain asymptomatic, this anomaly may present with complications such as hydronephrosis, infection, stones, or tumors. We report the case of an 18-year-old female patient with a left-sided supernumerary kidney forming horseshoe configuration, identified on ultrasound and further characterized by computed tomography (CT) urography.

Case presentation

An 18-year-old female patient presented to Wolkite University Specialized Hospital with a 1-month history of vague, intermittent left flank pain. Her vital signs were stable, and physical examination was unremarkable. Initial laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, urinalysis, and renal function tests (blood urea nitrogen 15 mg/dL and creatinine 0.9 mg/dL), were within normal limits. She had no significant past medical history, including urinary tract infections, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or prior renal disease, and no history of previous surgery. She was not taking any regular medications and denied the use of nephrotoxic drugs or herbal remedies.

Abdominal ultrasound revealed a normal right kidney with preserved corticomedullary differentiation and no evidence of stones or hydronephrosis. On the left side, 2 renal units were identified. The orthotopic left kidney was normal in size and echogenicity, with mild pelvicalyceal dilatation and no evidence of calculi. Imaging findings were consistent with a left-sided supernumerary kidney associated with an atypical horseshoe configuration. The primary differential diagnosis is a duplex kidney, in which a single renal unit contains 2 pelvicalyceal systems with either a single or duplicated ureter. However, in the present case, the presence of a distinct accessory renal parenchyma with its own collecting system supports the diagnosis of a supernumerary kidney. The patient was managed conservatively. On follow-up renal ultrasound, the mild hydronephrosis of the native left kidney had completely resolved, confirming unobstructed urinary drainage (Fig. 2B)

Case discussion

Supernumerary kidney is defined as the presence of one or more additional kidneys [1]. It is a rare renal congenital anomaly, and the true incidence is not known, with fewer 100 cases reported in the literature [[1], [2], [3],4]. During embryological development, the supernumerary kidney arises from the aberrant division of nephrogenic cords into 2 or more metanephric blastema’s, resulting in a partially or completely duplicated ureteric bud at 5th to 7th weeks of gestation [5]. The accessory kidney may be separated from the normal kidney or connected through loose areolar tissue, and it is usually small in size with decreased function [5,6].

The coexistence of a supernumerary kidney with a horseshoe kidney is exceptionally uncommon. While horseshoe kidney occurs in approximately 1 in 500 individuals, only 6 cases of supernumerary kidney associated with horseshoe fusion have been described [2,5,6,[7], [8], [9]].

Differentiation from the more common duplex kidney is clinically important. A duplex kidney contains 2 pelvicalyceal systems within a single renal unit, with either a single or double ureter, whereas a supernumerary kidney has a distinct collecting system, separate arterial and venous drainage, and an independent renal capsule, confirming its unique embryological origin [1,2,10].

Most reported supernumerary kidneys are unilateral and located on the left side [2,10,11]. Bilateral supernumerary kidneys are extremely rare, only 4 cases have been reported [1]. Two types are described: drainage via a bifid ureter or via an entirely separate ureter [9]. The position of the supernumerary kidney is related to the ureter; it is located cranially to the normal kidney when a separate ureter is present and caudally when a bifid ureter is present [1,4,9]. A completely separated ureter may drain ectopically into the bladder, in accordance with the Wiegert–Meyer rule [9,11]. Our case demonstrated a caudally located supernumerary kidney draining into bifid ureter. Vascular supply is usually separate from the ipsilateral kidney and may show considerable anatomical variation, underscoring the importance of vascular mapping [5,6].

The most common congenital anomalies associated with supernumerary kidneys include horseshoe kidney, urethral or vaginal atresia, imperforate anus, coarctation of the aorta, ventricular septal defect, meningomyelocele, complete duplication of the urethra and penis and ectopic ureteral opening [6,10,12]. The associated anomaly in our case is horseshoe kidney.

Approximately two-thirds of patients with a supernumerary kidney present with symptoms. When symptoms occur, they are usually secondary to associated conditions such as hydronephrosis, recurrent urinary tract infections, nephrolithiasis, pyonephrosis, or, rarely, tumors including renal cell carcinoma, papillary cystadenoma, and Wilms tumor [2,9,13]. Our patient presented with left flank pain due to transient non-obstructive hydronephrosis of the left native kidney.

The diagnosis relies on imaging, including intravenous pyelography (IVP), ultrasound, CT, MRI, and nuclear scan [[1], [2], [3], [4],5]. A nuclear scan is employed to assess renal cortical function and parenchymal damage, particularly following renal infection or in cases involving a supernumerary kidney associated with horseshoe kidney formation. It also helps determine the functional contribution of both the supernumerary kidney and the horseshoe kidney isthmus. This information is crucial for preoperative planning, as surgeons must be aware of functioning renal tissue during surgical intervention. However, a nuclear scan was not performed on this patient due to its unavailability in our facility. For focal renal parenchymal lesions, especially when renal cell carcinoma is suspected, contrast-enhanced CT provides superior lesion characterization and evaluation. Furthermore, it facilitates the assessment of the renal vasculature.

If surgical intervention is considered, CT angiography (CTA) or MR angiography (MRA) is recommended to delineate vascular anatomy and avoid inadvertent injury [10]. In our case, CTA and MRA were not done because they were not available in our setup.

Management depends on symptoms and complications. Asymptomatic patients may be monitored with periodic follow-up, whereas nephrectomy may be indicated for non-functional kidneys or in cases complicated by obstruction, infection, calculi, or malignancy [1,2,5]. In our patient, the mild hydronephrosis resolved within 2 weeks. At 3-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with normal urinalysis, renal function tests, and ultrasound findings. Ongoing surveillance with ultrasound every 6 months has been advised. The patient was also counselled regarding potential future risks (eg, stones, infection) and instructed to seek early medical attention if symptoms such as flank pain or urinary complaints develop.

This case report has some limitations. CT angiography or MR angiography was not performed due to limited availability in our setting. Nuclear renal scan is valuable for assessing cortical function, detecting congenital anomalies, and determining the functional contribution of a supernumerary kidney; however, it was not performed in our case due to unavailability.

Conclusions

Supernumerary kidney is a rare congenital anomaly, and its coexistence with a horseshoe kidney is exceptionally uncommon. Diagnosis depends on detailed imaging, particularly CT angiography and MR angiography, to define anatomy and vascular supply. Management should be tailored to symptoms and renal function, with conservative follow-up for asymptomatic cases and nephrectomy reserved for non-functional or complicated kidneys. Recognizing this anomaly is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and ensure safe clinical decision-making.
