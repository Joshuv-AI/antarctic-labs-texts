Ribbon constrains dendritic pruning via actin scaffolding and exocyst complex
Authors: Wanting Wang, Su Wang, Yuhao Yuan, Menglong Rui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDuring animal development, neurons selectively remove superfluous synaptic connections, strengthen key synapses, and optimize neural circuits in the brain, which is a core mechanism for fine-tuning the development of the nervous system. Drosophila class IV dendritic arborization (C4da) sensory neurons undergo dendrite-specific pruning during development. Nevertheless, the cell-autonomous inhibitory mechanisms of dendritic pruning in C4da neurons are largely unknown.Methods and resultsHere, we discovered Ribbon (Rib), a nuclear BTB-domain protein, whose malfunction in C4da neurons causes a precocious occurrence of dendritic pruning. Our study further shows that the regulation of dendritic pruning by Rib is dependent on Akt/Tor signaling. Moreover, actin polymerization factors and exocyst complex subunits are also involved in repressing dendritic pruning and function as downstream effectors of Rib and Akt/Tor signaling.ConclusionsOverall, the present study reveals a cell-autonomous inhibitory mechanism of Rib in dendritic pruning, with a perspective to provide new insights into neurodevelopment and the pathogenesis of relevant neurological disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-026-00903-1.

Introduction

During early development, the number of neurons and synapses increases rapidly, but not all connections are necessary. Synaptic pruning makes neural networks more elaborate and efficient by removing redundant and weak synapses. Pruning of inactive or unstable synaptic connections helps to enhance the functional stability of the remaining synapses. This results in more accurate and reliable signaling between neurons, which improves the functional stability of the entire nervous system. Neurons in different brain regions form specific neural circuits through complex synaptic connections, and synaptic pruning plays a key role in this process. It helps to shape the specific structure and function of neural circuits so that different brain regions can perform their respective roles and coordinate with each other to accomplish a variety of complex physiological and psychological functions, including sensation, movement, learning, and memory [1–3].

Many larval-born neurons in the central and peripheral nervous systems of the holometabolous insect Drosophila go through systematic pruning during metamorphosis in order to remove their larval dendrites or axons [4]. On the basis of the shape of their dendrites, Drosophila dendritic sensory neurons are divided into four subtypes (I to IV). During early metamorphosis, class IV dendritic arborization (C4da) sensory neurons experience dendrite-specific pruning [5]. The early thinning and blebbing around 4 h after puparium formation (APF) results in dendrite severing, subsequent fragmentation, and eventual debris cleaning (Fig. 1A). Dorsal class I sensory neurons (ddaD and ddaE) prune their larval dendrites but not their axons, similar to C4da neurons [5, 6], whereas class II (ddaB) and class III (ddaA/ddaF) da neurons are removed by apoptosis [6]. It has been demonstrated that brain diseases, including schizophrenia and autism spectrum disorder (ASD), are linked to abnormalities in neural pruning [7, 8]. We may thus be able to understand more regarding the underlying mechanisms of human neurological disorders by investigating the molecular and cellular processes of developmental pruning. Previous studies have uncovered a series of crucial signaling pathways and biological processes participating in dendritic pruning of C4da neurons [9]. These include, ecdysone signaling [5], JNK signaling [10], calcium transients [11], caspase activity [12, 13], microtubule polarity [14, 15], endocytosis pathway [16], protein secretion pathway [17–19], peripheral glial cells [20], epithelial cells [21], and others. We carried out a genome-wide RNA interference (RNAi) screen to identify new modulators necessary for dendritic pruning in order to thoroughly comprehend the processes underlying dendritic pruning. On the basis of this screen, we isolated Ribbon (Rib), a nuclear BTB-domain protein, as a repressor of dendritic pruning in C4da neurons. Joseph et al. originally showed that Rib is required for normal morphogenesis of tubular epithelia in Drosophila [22]. Subsequent work implicated Rib in maintaining cell shape via cytoskeletal regulation, with aberrant myosin-driven cortical actin contraction contributing to mutant phenotypes [23]. In addition, Rib has been found to be involved in specific tracheal branch migration processes [24]. Complementary studies indicate that Rib coordinates epithelial cell body and apical–basal movements during branching and is a key regulator of epithelial morphogenesis [25]. Furthermore, Rib was identified to interact with Lola Like to promote tube elongation in Drosophila salivary glands and trachea by regulating the apical membrane dynamics of epithelial cells [26]. Finally, multiple reports support a role for Rib as a transcriptional regulator that controls tissue-specific gene programs driving cell growth and organ shape, including activation of ribosomal protein gene expression in the salivary gland [27, 28]. To our understanding, however, whether Rib has a function in controlling neuronal remodeling, including pruning, is entirely unknown.

In this study, we report that Rib is required for dendritic pruning in C4da neurons. In contrast to most of the previously identified pruning promoters, the loss of function of Rib resulted in precocious dendritic pruning, and Rib overexpression caused a significant defect in dendritic pruning, implying a negative regulatory role during dendritic pruning. Interestingly, Akt/Tor signaling is genetically interacting with Rib and may be located downstream of Rib to antagonize dendritic pruning. Further studies showed that both actin polymerization-associated protein and vesicle transport complex subunits are implicated in dendritic pruning. Remarkably, these factors act downstream of Rib and stabilize dendrites during pruning. Taken together, our results indicate, for the first time, that Rib maintains the normal actin scaffolding and exocyst complex in dendrites to suppress dendrite-specific pruning via Akt/Tor signaling in C4da sensory neurons.

The following stocks were obtained from the Tsinghua RNAi Stock Center: Rib RNAi #2 (TH02000.N), Rac1 RNAi (THU1493), Rac2 RNAi (TH201501064.S), RalA RNAi #1 (THU3168), Mtl RNAi (THU3024), Sec6 RNAi (THU2636), Exo70 RNAi (THU2880), Exo84 RNAi (THU3087), Pi3k RNAi #1 (THU2776), Pi3k RNAi #2 (TH201501070.S), Akt RNAi #1 (THU0552), Akt RNAi #2 (THU4852), Tor RNAi #1 (THU1291), Tor RNAi #2 (TH201501087.S), and Unc-115b RNAi (THU4082), Cofilin/Tsr (THU0972), and S6k RNAi (THU0572). The PPK-Gal4 (II) and PPK-Gal4 (III) are gifts from Dr. Yan Zhu (Institute of Biophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences). The UAS-Dicer2, UAS-CD4-tdGFP, and UAS-CD4-tdTomato are gifts from Prof. Wei Xie (Southeast University, China). All flies were raised at 25 °C on standard medium.

Generation of transgenes

For the rescue experiment, RNAi-resistant UAS-Rib was made by cloning the full-length cDNA with modifications to be resistant to RNAi into the transformation vector pUAST-attB. Transgenic fly lines were generated by standard germline transformation. cDNA of Rib was obtained from the genome of UAS-Rib via PCR. The transgenic line was generated by UniHuaii Inc.

Generation of antibody against Rib

A polyclonal antibody against Rib was generated in rabbits. A purified glutathione S-transferase fusion fragment (aa482–661) of Rib protein was used as an antigen. The antibody was purified using an affinity column generated by coupling a Rib fragment (aa482–661) to Sepharose 4B. The efficacy of the antibody has been confirmed through RNAi-based immunofluorescence staining experiments (Supplementary Fig. S1A–C).

Live imaging analysis

Drosophila at second and third instar larval, white prepupal (WP), 4 h APF, and 6 h APF stages were first washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer briefly for three times and subsequently immersed with 90% glycerol. Pupal cases at the 10 h APF, 12 h APF, 13 h APF, and 16 h APF were carefully removed before being mounted with 90% glycerol. Images of C4da neurons were captured with a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope.

Immunohistochemistry and antibodies

For immunostaining, larvae and WP were dissected in precooled PBS and fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 30 min. The experimental and control samples were incubated synergistically in the same tubes. Mounting was conducted in the mounting medium of VectaShield, and the samples were directly visualized by a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope. The following primary antibodies were used for immunohistochemistry at the indicated dilution: mouse anti-Nrg (1:50, BP104, DSHB, Iowa City, IA, USA) and mouse anti-EcR-B1 (1:20, DSHB, Iowa City, IA, USA). The following secondary antibodies were used for immunohistochemistry at the indicated dilution: Alexa Fluor 488- and Alexa Fluor 555- conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA).

Quantification of dendrites

The structure of the C4da neuron dendrite at the WP, 10 h APF, 12 h APF, 13 h APF, and 16 h APF stages was captured through live confocal imaging. The length of unpruned dendrites is utilized to evaluate the dendrite pruning phenotype. The length of unpruned dendrites is the sum length of remaining dendrites at WP, 10 h APF, 12 h APF, 13 h APF, and 16 h APF, measured in a 275 μm × 275 μm region derived from the dorsal dendritic field of C4da neurons, ranging from abdominal segment 2 to 4.

Statistics

For pairwise comparison, the two-tailed Student t-test was used to determine statistical significance. For multiple groups comparison, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni’s test was used to determine significance. Error bars in all graphs represent the standard error of the mean (s.e.m.). Statistical significance is shown as ****P < 0.0001, ***P < 0.001, **P < 0.01, *P < 0.05, and ns, not significant. The number of neurons (n) in each group is shown on the bars.

Results

Rib suppresses dendritic pruning in C4da neurons

In Drosophila C4da neurons, dendritic pruning is an essential biological mechanism that removes superfluous dendritic branches to improve neuronal circuits, and it ensures accurate connections and appropriate neural function during development. This process is regulated by multiple signaling pathways and mechanisms [9, 29]. Of these, the majority of findings have centered on mechanisms that promote dendritic pruning, whereas little is known about inhibitory signals of dendritic pruning. On this basis, we performed a genetic screen to identify unknown regulatory signals of dendritic pruning during development, paying particular attention to negative regulators. By utilizing the C4da neuron-specific Gal4 (PPK-Gla4) to drive knockdown of candidate genes, we discovered two independent RNAi strains, V103977 (#1) and TH02000.N (#2), both targeting the same gene, ribbon (rib). Drosophila rib encodes a nuclear BTB/POZ domain protein that plays crucial roles in epithelial morphogenesis and directed cell migration during development [24, 25]. Here, we showed that deletion of Rib in C4da neurons resulted in a precocious occurrence of dendritic pruning. As demonstrated at 10 and 12 h APF, downregulation of Rib displays significantly reduced dendritic branching residues relative to wild-type (Fig. 1B–F). To rule out that the Rib-associated dendritic precocious pruning is caused by an off-target effect, we subsequently overexpressed the full-length RNAi-targeting site mutation (RNAi-resistant) of Rib in an RNAi background. The results showed that reintroducing Rib RNAi-resistant at both 10 and 12 h APF could rescue the phenotype of precocious pruning (Fig. 1G–J), indicating an inhibitory role for Rib in dendritic pruning of C4da sensory neurons.

Subsequently, we generated an anti-Rib antibody to test whether Rib is expressed in C4da neurons and how its level changes during development. Using immunostaining, we confirmed that Rib is indeed expressed in C4da neurons. Because of the technical limitations of whole-mount C4da immunostaining in pupal stages, reliable detection by our assay is currently feasible up to 6 h APF. Accordingly, we compared Rib expression in three developmental stages: third-instar larvae (3rd Larvae), white prepupae (WP, 0 h APF), and 6 h APF. The immunostaining results show a clear downregulation in Rib signal as development proceeds: expression is highest in 3rd Larvae, reduced at the WP stage, and further diminished by 6 h APF (Supplementary Fig. S1A–C). These data indicate that Rib is developmentally downregulated in C4da neurons during the window that precedes and accompanies pruning onset, consistent with a model in which Rib acts to oppose pruning.

Rib overexpression leads to defective dendritic pruning in C4da neurons

To further validate the role of Rib in dendritic pruning, we next wanted to know whether excessive Rib modulates dendritic pruning in an opposite direction. Consistent with our expectation, overexpression of full-length Rib without mutation in C4da neurons revealed a substantial number of unpruned dendrites by observation of dendrites at 16 h APF, whereas almost all dendrites in the control group were cleared at this phase (Fig. 2E–I). Of note, we noticed that excess Rib actually led to a significant decline in dendritic branching complexity during the WP stage, a period prior to the onset of dendritic pruning, suggesting that overexpression of Rib may have a severe impact on dendritic growth. To further clarify whether overabundant Rib interferes with dendritic growth, we further examined dendritic branching in both second and third instar larvae, and the results showed a dramatic decrease in the number of dendritic branches in overexpression of Rib compared with the wild-type (Fig. 2A–D). Then, to exclude that the phenotype of 16 h APF dendritic pruning defect is actually related to the previous dendritic growth abnormality, we next utilized temperature-sensitive GAL80ts to temporally and spatially genetically regulate Rib. At 18 °C, the activity of GAL4 was repressed by GAL80, and the target gene could not be expressed normally; at 29 °C, the activity of GAL4 was restored, and the target gene was expressed normally (Fig. 2J). On this basis, we cultured the control and experimental groups at 18 °C and shifted them to 29 °C at the wandering third instar larval stage (wL3). The degree of dendritic pruning of 13 h APF at 29 °C corresponded to 16 h APF at 25 °C. The results showed that dendrites were completely pruned in the control group, whereas a large number of residuals remained after overexpression of Rib (Fig. 2K–M). Additionally, we found that the phenotype overexpressing Rib from the wL3 stage was weaker compared with those from the embryonic stage, with the average residual dendrite length shrinking from 1009 to 527 μm (Fig. 2H–M), suggesting that there is a temporal dosage effect on the regulation of dendritic pruning. Furthermore, to confirm that this pruning-defective phenotype is indeed caused by Rib, we performed Rib knockdown in the context of Rib overexpression and showed that silencing Rib almost completely rescued the dendritic pruning phenotype caused by expressing Rib full-length without mutations (Fig. 2N–P). Unc-115b serves as a potential downstream target of Rib during dendritic pruning

Subsequently, we attempted to understand the underlying mechanisms by which Rib represses dendrite-specific pruning in C4da sensory neurons. We first focused on some previously identified classical regulators of dendritic pruning, for instance, the ecdysone signaling receptor EcR-B1 and the L1-type cell adhesion molecule Neuroglian (Nrg). To this end, we knocked down Rib in C4da neurons and observed the expression of EcR-B1 and Nrg. The results showed that deletion of Rib did not have a significant impact on EcR-B1 and Nrg (Figure S2A-F). To further characterize the downstream mechanism of Rib-mediated dendritic pruning, we chose the Drosophila brain as a sample, considering too few C4da neurons, and extracted RNA from control and rib-silenced brains for transcriptome sequencing. It was found that deficiency of Rib resulted in upregulation of 152 genes and downregulation of 199 genes (Fig. 3A, B). GO and KEGG analyses of differentially expressed genes revealed enrichment in fatty acid metabolism, glycan synthesis, chromatin structure composition, and so on (Fig. 3C, D). Among these differentially expressed genes, we selected 16 up- and downregulated genes that may be involved in neurodevelopment for dendritic pruning phenotype screening, respectively (Fig. 3E, F). Interestingly, we identified Unc-115b, a human ABLIM3 (actin binding LIM protein family member 3) ortholog, with actin filament binding activity, which regulates actin assembly. In Caenorhabditis elegans, UNC-115b acts autonomously in specific neurons as a downstream effector molecule of the Rac signaling pathway to regulate the actin cytoskeleton of growth cones by binding to actin, thereby mediating axon guidance [30, 31]. Knockdown of Unc-115b in C4da neurons triggered a significant precocious pruning phenotype as compared with the control, similar to Rib (Fig. 3H–I, L). Subsequently, we asked whether UNC-115b is involved in Rib-mediated dendritic pruning process. We conjectured that Rib, as a transcription factor, whose overexpression may drive transcriptional activation of its downstream target genes, for instance UNC-115b, then silencing UNC-115b by RNAi in this context should theoretically be able to rescue the phenotype caused by excess Rib. To this end, we utilized PPK-Gal4 to specifically overexpress full-length Rib without mutation sites in C4da neurons. Consistent with previous results, excess Rib led to substantial dendritic residues at 16 h APF. Interestingly, downregulation of UNC-115b in this background significantly ameliorated the dendritic pruning defect caused by Rib overexpression (Fig. 3J, K, M). In summary, these results suggest that UNC-115b suppresses dendritic pruning and functions as a downstream target of Rib.

Rib stabilizes dendrites via AKT/TOR signaling during pruning

Next, we were interested in whether there are downstream mechanisms other than UNC-115b for the regulation of dendritic pruning by Rib, and whether these mechanisms are intercorrelated with UNC-115b. A previous report illustrated that the PI3K/AKT/TOR signaling pathway controls the regrowth of C4da neuron dendrites after dendritic pruning by selectively stimulating the translation of actin-associated proteins and exocyst complex components, revealing a pivotal role of TOR signaling in dendritic regrowth [32]. We hypothesize that dendritic regrowth and pruning are essentially two contrasting events in neuronal development and that there is a high probability that they share analogous molecular regulatory mechanisms in opposite directions. Moreover, the activity of the PI3K/AKT/TOR pathway, a key regulatory network in neural development, is suppressed by the Cullin1-based SCF E3 ubiquitin ligase, and dendritic pruning defects caused by Cullin1 deficiency can be rescued by downregulating key components of the PI3K/AKT/TOR pathway [33]. These results imply that the PI3K/AKT/TOR pathway may negatively regulate dendritic pruning during development, similar to Rib. On this basis, we speculated that PI3K/AKT/TOR could be a potential mechanism for Rib to modulate dendritic pruning. First, we utilized PPK-Gal4 to silence pi3k, akt, and tor within C4da neurons and observed dendritic pruning at 12 h APF. Statistical results showed that knockdown of pi3k had no significant influence on pruning in comparison with controls (Fig. 4A–C, P). However, downregulation of akt and tor remarkably accelerated the process of dendritic pruning (Fig. 4D–G, P). To determine whether a canonical TOR downstream effector also participates in dendritic pruning, we next knocked down S6k in C4da neurons. S6k downregulation produced a pruning phenotype that closely resembled that of Rib (Fig. 4H, P). These results suggest that AKT and TOR are involved in the dendritic pruning process and perform a negative regulatory role. Notably, pruning was not affected after silencing pi3k. Actin polymerization factors antagonize dendritic pruning

Previous studies have shown that, during regrowth after dendritic pruning, AKT activates TORC1, which selectively stimulates the translation of regrowth factor mRNAs related to actin cytoskeleton regulation and polarized exocytosis while not affecting pruning factors such as as Mical, thereby regulating dendrite regrowth [32]. In another study, the protein phosphatase PP2A was found to mediate actin dynamics and participate in the regulation of dendritic pruning through genetic interactions with the actin-severing enzymes Mical, Cofilin, and other actin regulators [35]. Also, research has revealed that rib-mutant Drosophila has an abnormal morphology of Malpighian tubules with defective cytoskeleton and, in particular, dysfunctional myosin-driven contraction of the cortical actin [22, 23, 36]. Furthermore, in combination with our previous finding of an important role for actin-binding protein UNC-115b in dendritic pruning, it is implied that the actin filament skeleton may serve as a pivotal hub to maintain the normal occurrence of dendritic pruning during development. To confirm this conjecture, we wondered whether attenuating the key factors of actin polymerization produces a dendritic pruning phenotype consistent with Rib. Rac1 and Rac2 are Rho family GTPases that regulate actin cytoskeleton dynamics by cycling between GTP-bound (active) and GDP-bound (inactive) states. Active Rac recruits the WAVE regulatory complex to activate the Arp2/3 complex and drive branched actin polymerization for lamellipodia and membrane ruffling [37, 38]. Mtl (Mig-2-like) is also a Drosophila Rho-family GTPase orthologous to C. elegans MIG-2 and synergizes with Rac1 and Rac2 to control the actin cytoskeleton by activating downstream effector pathways [39]. We thus exploited RNAi to downregulate the expression of Mtl, Rac1, and Rac2 in C4da neurons, and by observing 12 h APF dendrites, we found that silencing of mtl, rac1, and rac2 consistently causes a dendritic precocious pruning phenotype analogous to that of Rib RNAi when compared with control (Fig. 5A–D, Q). To further clarify the role of the actin scaffold in dendritic pruning, we next utilized PPK-Gal4 to specifically drive Rac1 expression in C4da neurons to facilitate actin polymerization, and the results showed that the process of dendritic pruning was severely restricted, as evidenced by the fact that substantial dendritic branches were uncleared at 16 h APF (Fig. 5E, F, R). Actin scaffolding works as a potential target of Rib in dendritic pruning

Given our previous findings, there is a high probability that the actin polymerization event acts downstream of Rib to suppress dendrite pruning. To this end, we attempted to demonstrate their upstream–downstream relationship through genetic interactions. On the one hand, in the context of Rib RNAi, we simultaneously knocked down Rac1, Rac2, and Mtl, respectively. The results showed that double knockdown of Rib with any of these actin polymerization factors exhibited more significant precocious pruning phenotype at 12 h APF compared with the control group (Fig. 5G–J, S). To further validate the genetic interaction between Rib and actin-related pathway, we subsequently performed additional experiments using established alleles including rib1 and mtlDelta. The double mutants of rib and mtl showed no appreciable enhancement of the pruning phenotype relative to the rib single mutant (Fig. 5K–N, T), revealing that loss of Mtl does not further exacerbate the Rib phenotype. On the other hand, overexpressing full-length Rac1 in the background of Rib RNAi, we observed that the total length of unpruned dendrites was longer than that of the control at 12 h APF (Fig. 5O, P, U), suggesting that activation of Rac1 has a certain degree of rescuing effect on pruning defects. These results suggest that Rib acts synergistically with the actin polymerization factors Mtl, Rac1, and Rac2 to suppress the pruning of dendrites. To further elucidate the regulatory role of Rib on actin, we next expressed lifeact:GFP in C4da neurons, which was previously revealed to be able to label and track filamentous-actin (F-actin) dynamics in real time [40]. At the third instar larval and WP stages, throughout the dendritic coverage, we observed that the overall fluorescence intensity of the dendrites after knockdown of Rib was lower than that of the control. To quantify this phenotype, we selected two 20 μm lengths of proximal dendrites within a 50 μm radius centered on the cytosol and statistically analyzed their average fluorescence intensity. The results showed that, during the third instar larval period, knockdown of Rib led to a significant decrease in the normalized fluorescent intensity of lifeact:GFP compared with the control group (Fig. 5V–V′, X), indicating that Rib is required for maintaining the normal dendritic F-actin. Analogously, lifeact:GFP normalized values were significantly reduced in the condition when rib was silenced during the WP period (Fig. 5W–W′, Y). Taken together, these findings indicate that Rib is involved in actin organization during dendritic pruning.

Exocyst complex subunits suppress dendritic pruning

The exocyst is a key tethering complex that sits at the interface between secretory vesicles and their target membrane, playing a central role in regulated exocytosis and overall membrane trafficking [41, 42]. It is another key biological event in the regulation of dendritic regrowth by Akt/Tor signaling and has previously been found to play an important role in dendritic growth and maintenance [32, 43]. In addition, previous studies have shown that Rib contributes to the morphogenesis of the Drosophila tracheal epithelium by regulating the expression of the transmembrane protein Crumbs and maintaining apical delivery of vesicles mediated by the small GTPase Rab11 [26]. On this basis, we asked whether the exocyst complex is also involved in Rib-mediated dendrite pruning. The small GTPase RalA has been demonstrated to mediate integrin-dependent membrane raft exocytosis via interacting with the exocyst complex [44, 45]. In Drosophila larval peripheral nerves, Ral GTPase acts through the exocyst complex in wrapping glial cells to regulate their growth and development [46]. Also, in the PC12 cells, the interaction between RalA GTPase and the exocyst complex is essential for GTP-dependent exocytosis [45]. Therefore, we selected the small GTPase RalA as well as some specific subunits of the exocyst complex, Sec5, Sec6, Exo70, and Exo84, and analyzed their dendritic pruning phenotypes. Discussion

Selective removal of redundant or inefficient neural connections in the developing nervous system enhances information processing efficiency and cognitive function by optimizing synaptic network structure [2, 3]. This mechanism potentiates brain plasticity and facilitates efficient resource allocation and adaptive learning, while its abnormalities may contribute to neurodevelopmental disorders or psychiatric disorders [47, 48]. During Drosophila C4da neuronal dendrite development, ecdysone binds to EcR/Usp co-receptors and promotes the expression of downstream genes, including sox14 and hdc, to initiate dendritic pruning [9, 29], while at the same time, inhibitory signals such as the endocytosis and degradation of cell adhesion molecule Nrg and the repression of the InR/PI3K/TOR signaling pathway are also required to promote dendritic pruning [16, 33]. This suggests that the initiation of dendritic pruning requires a fine balance of positive and negative regulatory signals.

In this study, using the C4da neuron model, a genetic screen revealed that rib is involved in dendritic pruning as a novel negative regulator. Neuron-specific knockdown or overexpression experiments confirmed that Rib is an important molecule for maintaining pruning homeostasis. Transcriptome analysis of Drosophila brain tissue showed that Rib was associated with the actin polymerization gene UNC-115b. Deletion of Rib led to a decrease in the transcriptional level of UNC-115b, and downregulation of UNC-115b also triggered the dendritic precocious pruning phenotype. Further genetic rescue experiments showed that knockdown of UNC-115b could rescue the dendritic pruning defect caused by Rib overexpression, and these results implied that UNC-115b might act downstream of Rib to participate in the regulation of dendritic pruning. However, future labeling and sorting of C4da neurons will be required to further confirm this mechanism using single-cell sequencing. Besides, whether UNC-115b serves as a direct transcriptional regulatory target of Rib also needs to be further verified using the chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) technique and electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA). Interestingly, downregulation of other actin polymerization-related genes in C4da neurons consistently resulted in a phenotype of precocious dendritic pruning. Moreover, double knockdown of these genes with Rib all triggered a more severe phenotype, and a defect that was markedly ameliorated by overexpression of the actin polymerization factor Rac1. Importantly, F-actin levels in C4da neurons undergo a significant decrease after downregulation of Rib. Previous studies have shown that the stability of C4da dendrites depends on the “structural scaffold” formed by actin filaments. During pruning, when the actin cytoskeleton undergoes continuous disassembly and disrupts structural support within dendritic branches, dendrites are severed in their proximal regions, followed by further fragmentation, and are ultimately phagocytosed and cleared by epidermal cells (a complete degeneration process) [35, 49]. To test whether Rib affects proximal dendrite severing, a hallmark of pruning onset that occurs normally at 5–8 h APF, we examined dendritic integrity at 4 h APF. At this earlier time point, most proximal dendrites in wild-type animals remained intact, whereas knockdown of Rib in C4da neurons caused proximal dendrite severing to occur precociously. To determine whether Rib suppresses pruning onset by maintaining F-actin stability, we overexpressed Rac1 in the Rib-knockdown background. Overexpression of Rac1 substantially rescued the precocious severing phenotype induced by Rib downregulation (Supplementary Fig. S3A–D, L). In contrast, overexpression of Rib prevented proximal dendritic breakage at 6 h APF, while Rac1 knockdown in this context induced the occurrence of severing (Supplementary Fig. S3E–H, M). Furthermore, we knocked down Cofilin and Mical, two actin disassembly factors, in C4da neurons and observed a phenotype consistent with Rib overexpression in proximal dendrites at 6 h APF (Supplementary Fig. S3I–K, N). Collectively, our results, together with previous studies, indicate that Rib promotes assembly and stability of the F-actin cytoskeleton in C4da dendrites, thereby attenuating dendritic structural disruption during pruning and decelerating subsequent degeneration and clearance. Nevertheless, whether Rib modulates the expression of these actin polymerization-associated genes other than UNC-115b, and the detailed regulatory mechanisms, remain to be further clarified.

PI3K/TOR signaling was previously considered to play a key role in dendrite regrowth after pruning, and its activation promotes dendrite regrowth through actin assembly and exocyst complex-mediated membrane trafficking [32]. Dendritic regrowth and pruning are essentially contrary processes, and there is a high probability that they share the same regulatory mechanisms in different directions. On this basis, we conjecture that the regulation of actin assembly by Rib may simultaneously exist in both direct and indirect forms. It is manifested as direct transcriptional modulation of UNC-115b or indirect control of other actin-related genes translation through PI3K/AKT/TOR. To test this hypothesis, we first need to understand whether PI3K/AKT/TOR signaling is involved in dendritic pruning and its role as a downstream target of Rib in dendritic pruning. Gene silencing experiments revealed that Akt and TOR, as core components of the pathway, play a negative regulatory role in dendritic pruning. However, knockdown of the pi3k gene did not significantly alter dendritic pruning. We speculate that the underlying mechanism may be attributed to the compensatory effects of other downstream molecules induced by the absence of PI3K function. These molecules may maintain the homeostatic balance of the pruning regulatory network by reorganizing the signaling pathways, thus avoiding the precocious pruning phenomenon. In addition, downregulation of Akt and TOR also alleviated the dendritic pruning phenotype caused by Rib overexpression, further confirming that this signaling pathway may function downstream of Rib. However, it is unclear how Rib modulates Akt/TOR signaling, and it is warranted to demonstrate whether Rib is directly involved in the transcription of key components of Akt/TOR signaling. In fact, we found that the mRNA levels of PI3K, Akt, and TOR were almost unchanged after deletion of Rib by RT-PCR (data not shown), suggesting that its regulation of Akt/TOR signaling may occur posttranscriptionally or indirectly. Is Akt/TOR signaling activity regulated by Rib? Future work is also required to further explore whether deletion or overexpression of Rib in C4da neurons influences the levels of nonphosphorylated as well as phosphorylated forms of Akt or TOR.

The mechanism by which Akt/TOR promotes dendritic regrowth involves an exocyst complex-mediated exocytosis in addition to actin polymerization. Here, we wondered whether Rib inhibits dendrite pruning by promoting the assembly of exocyst complex through Akt/TOR signaling. The results show that deficiency of RalA, Sec5, Sec6, Exo70, and Exo84 promotes dendritic pruning, whereas persistent activation of RalA suppresses this process, indicating that these exocyst complex genes may function as negative regulators of pruning. Additionally, knockdown of these genes further exacerbated the precocious pruning caused by Rib deletion, and overexpression of RalA partially rescued the rib-silencing phenotype. Live imaging results showed that Rib downregulation in C4da neurons caused a decrease in the levels of Sec5 and Sec6. Taken together, Rib may be involved in the assembly of the exocyst complex through the Akt/TOR pathway, which ultimately suppresses dendritic pruning in C4da neurons. How Rib inhibits dendritic pruning during development through exocyst complex is a fascinating direction of research that is still unclear. We hypothesize that membrane trafficking may antagonize the pruning process by complementing the dendritic membrane structure, or that membrane trafficking inhibits dendritic pruning by increasing the localization of some unknown membrane protein at the cell membrane. This membrane protein is required for degradation by endocytosis from the cell membrane at the onset of dendritic pruning. The first one that comes to mind is Nrg; however, the knockdown of Rib does not influence it (Supplementary Fig. S2D–F). Therefore, in the future, we can screen other cell adhesion molecules or receptor proteins to identify downstream targets of Rib mediating dendritic pruning through the exocyst complex.

Considering the potential influence of Akt/Tor signaling, actin assembly, and the exocyst complex on cell growth, we sought to rule out the possibility that the effects of genes associated with these pathways on dendritic pruning might be partially attributable to their regulation of dendritic growth prior to pruning. To this end, we used the temporal control strategy (tub-Gal80ts + PPK-Gal4), as shown in Fig. 2J, to restrict RNAi expression to defined developmental windows. Drosophila carrying downstream-gene RNAi were maintained at 18 °C through the WP stage (Gal80ts active, Gal4 repressed), and under these conditions no dendritic morphology defects were observed at the WP stage compared with controls, indicating that early dendritic growth and arborization were not perturbed by the manipulations when RNAi expression was suppressed during the growth phase. To test effects specifically during pruning, we next shifted animals to 29 °C (Gal80ts inactive, allowing Gal4-driven RNAi) after completion of larval to early pupal growth and assayed dendritic structure at 10 h APF (approximately equivalent to 12 h APF at 25 °C developmental timing). Under these conditions, knockdown of the Rib downstream genes all produced precocious pruning phenotypes closely resembling Rib RNAi, indicating that these genes act to inhibit pruning after dendritic growth is complete (Supplementary Fig. S4A–M).

Developmental dendritic pruning primarily occurs through progressive retraction or local degeneration, and these modes appear to be at least partially conserved across species. In Drosophila sensory neurons, dendrite removal involves both branch retraction and local fragmentation driven by ecdysone-triggered local cytoskeletal disassembly and compartmentalized degeneration [6, 13, 50]. Importantly, vertebrate studies also identified retraction-type mechanisms driven by actin dynamics, and activity-dependent stabilization versus elimination of dendrites has been linked to differential regulation of small GTPases and actin regulators [51–53]. For example, previous work emphasizes activity-dependent, synaptotrophic control of dendritic arbor stability [54], while recent analyses in mammalian mitral cells highlight competing Rac1 versus RhoA signaling as a molecular switch between dendrite stabilization and pruning [55]. Moreover, in the mouse hippocampus, induction of long-term depression (LTD) activates autophagic vesicles within postsynaptic dendrites; these autophagic pathways operate in parallel with endocytic mechanisms to traffic AMPA receptors and other key synaptic proteins to lysosomes for degradation, thereby promoting spine elimination and supporting a role for local degradative processes in synaptic pruning [56]. Together, these findings suggest that the cellular modes and some upstream signaling pathways we observe in Drosophila might have parallels in mammalian systems, supporting the broader relevance of our results.

In conclusion, we have identified a novel suppressor of dendritic pruning, revealing that it promotes the polymerization of actin as well as the assembly of exocyst complex through the activation of Akt/Tor signaling, which ultimately inhibits the occurrence of dendritic pruning (Fig. 8).
