Identification and mapping of mHealth interventions applied in palliative care in Sub Saharan Africa: a scoping review:
Authors: John Bosco Ndinawe, Jerome Kabakyenga, Elizabeth Namukwaya, Edgar Mugema Mulogo, Francisca Nagujja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough palliative care is very essential for improving quality of life (QOL) by providing relief from pain and other distressing symptoms, its coverage in sub Saharan Africa remains remarkably low. Utilization of mHealth could be a fundamental healthcare system strengthening approach to enhance and promote palliative care in the region. There is paucity of evidence regarding evidence on the use of these interventions in SSA because it is a relatively new concept in the region. Therefore, there was need to identify and map the available/ applied mHealth interventions for palliative care in SSA basing on published and grey literature.AimTo examine the nature and use of mHealth interventions for palliative care and factors influencing their use in sub Saharan Africa (SSA).MethodsThe review was based on the JBI methodology for scoping review. PUBMED, CINAHL, Embase and Google Scholar databases were searched for published articles with no time restrictions. Unpublished and grey literature was also searched for. Data was extracted from the retrieved sources and synthesized. The findings were reported using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) in a comprehensive and transparent way.ResultsA total of 224 articles were identified and 35 of these met the inclusion criterion and were considered for the review. The results show use of mHealth interventions for palliative care in 12 countries of the SSA. Only 12 (35.3%) were app-based, 9(26.5%) were SMS-text-based, 4(11.8%) were telemedicine-based,5(14.7%) were phone call-based, 3(8.8%) were mixed while 1(2.9%) used wearable-sensor-based mHealth interventions. Key uses of mHealth interventions in palliative care included health strengthening, psychosocial support to patients and families, enhancement of compliance to appointments for patients, promotion of adherence to medication, health education, monitoring, evaluation and research and capacity building for palliative care health professionals. A number of influencing factors were identified and they included wide-spread telephone ownership and access, great robustness of telephone and internet connectivity, high degree of perceived usefulness and ease of use, feasibility and acceptability of mHealth. Also, vital barriers such as low levels of technological advancement/expertise, irregularity and paucity of electric power supply, high likelihood to compromise patients’ privacy and confidentiality and high illiteracy rates among the population were articulated by the articles.ConclusionAbout a quarter of SSA countries have incorporated mHealth interventions for palliative care, underscoring the need for further research and development of the technologies. These approaches are quite beneficial to SSA, where there is a disproportionate disease burden and an inadequate healthcare system.Keywords: mHealth interventions, Palliative care, Sub Saharan Africa, Life-limiting illnesses

Background

Utilization of mHealth has been recognized as a strategy to enhance palliative care for patients with life-limiting illnesses, such as; cancer, HIV/AIDS, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases and advanced kidney disease with an aim of ameliorating their quality of life. This is due to its potential to increase accessibility and effectiveness of this modality of care [1, 2].

The World Health Organization (WHO) explains mHealth as a medical or public health practice aided by portable devices such as mobile phones, patient monitoring devices, personal digital assistants and other wireless devices [3, 4]. These interventions have been demonstrated to be effective in improving patient outcomes such as better pain control and improved communication between patients and health professionals, when integrated into palliative care across the USA and Europe [5–7]. They promote rapid access to clinical and social networks thereby increasing health communication between patients and their caregivers [8, 9]. They could be fundamentally beneficial to Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), which is grappling with a disproportionate disease burden (25% of the global disease burden) and scarcity of trained healthcare workforce (1.3% of the world’s trained health personnel) [10].

WHO defines palliative care as “an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families facing problems associated with life-limiting illnesses through prevention and relief of suffering by means of early identification and impeccable assessment and treatment of pain and other problems, physical, psychosocial and spiritual [11, 12]. ” There is a great emphasis on the importance of palliative care in the management of the above patients with an aim of improving their quality of life [13, 14].

Currently, there is an acute need for palliative care globally due to increases in the aging population and non-communicable diseases [15]. The estimated number of people with palliative care needs is over sixty million globally, three-quarters of whom live in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including SSA [15]. Nevertheless, this modality of care is not available and/or accessible to most patients in need, especially those in LMICs [16–18]. In SSA particularly, this modality of care remains deplorably low; less than 5% of the patients in need, access the care [7].

Therefore, mHealth presents a strong promise to scale up palliative care services to a larger population in need especially, the developing world such as SSA and also improve the quality of palliative care where it already exists.

An earlier search of PROSPERO, PUBMED and MEDLINE was done to establish existing evidence on the topic. Three systematic reviews were found to have studied about mHealth in SSA; one illustrated the guidance on the development of mHealth as informed by earlier research [19]. Another one was about the strategic framework to highlight evidence gaps about mHealth [20]. These two, however, don’t focus on palliative care. The one that focused on palliative care highlighted that the state of mHealth in palliative was still in infancy [7]. This, however, did not spell out the nature and use of mHealth for palliative care in SSA. Since then, a number of developments in information communication and technology have occurred; there has been increased phone ownership and penetration from 5% to 70% in the last one decade in the region [21]. There is a great dearth of evidence regarding these mHealth interventions for palliative care in SSA because these approaches are a relatively new development in the region. The depth and breadth of evidence of mHealth applied in palliative care in the Sub Saharan African context is not well known. There is therefore, need to identify and map out mHealth interventions that have been applied in palliative care in SSA basing on published and grey literature and hence the essence of this scoping review. This study aimed to examine mHealth-based intervention for palliative care for patients with life-limiting illnesses in SSA with a specific focus on their nature, use and influencing factors.

This review was guided by the following questions:

How does the existing literature characterize the types and applications of mHealth interventions in the context of palliative care in SSA?

Methods

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology [22] was applied during this review. The findings were reported following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [23].

Inclusion

Inclusion Criteria

This was developed by the research team by consensus using the participants, concept and context (PCC) framework [24].

Participants

This review focused on studies that involved key stakeholders in palliative care such as; patients with life-limiting illnesses (e.g. cancer, renal disease, chronic obstructive respiratory disease, cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, degenerative diseases), health professionals, community volunteer workers and community leaders. There were no restrictions in terms of parameters such as age, sex, diagnosis, disease stage, ethnicity or sampling technique. The population was deliberately made broad in order to capture all the possible mHealth research findings across the spectrum of stakeholders in the palliative care fraternity, as it is a relatively novel program in the SSA region.

Concept

The review considered research studies that involved application of mHealth approaches in a palliative care setting. The included studies had to involve use of mHealth either in the design, the implementation or the evaluation of palliative care services. There were no restrictions to content and duration of the intervention. To be considered as an mHealth intervention, an interaction with a mobile device by health professionals or community volunteers or patients to communicate about their care, has to be documented. Additionally, the studies had to describe either the type or use of mHealth interventions, or the factors affecting their adoption for palliative care, or a combination of two or three of these aspects.

Context

The review considered only studies carried out in all the countries of the Sub Saharan African region. These include: Angola, Benin, Botswana, Burkina Faso, Burundi, Cape Verde, Cameroon, Central African Republic, Chad, Cameroon, Comoros, Democratic Republic of Congo, Republic of the Congo, Djibouti, Equatorial Guinea, Eritrea, Eswatini, Ethiopia, Gabon, Gambia, Ghana, Guinea, Guinea Bissau, Ivory Coast, Kenya, Lesotho, Liberia, Madagascar, Malawi, Mali, Mauritius, Mozambique, Namibia, Niger, Nigeria, Rwanda, Sao Tome and Principe, Senegal, Seychelles, Sierra Leone, Somalia, South Africa, South Sudan, Sudan, Tanzania, Togo, Uganda, Zambia and Zimbabwe.

Types of sources

During the scoping review, the reviewers focused on studies with qualitative, quantitative and mixed methods study designs. Review papers were excluded because their constituent papers were already included. Only articles published in English were included due to limited resources to engage in translation from other languages and also because the number of non- English language articles was low, as English is widely spoken in the SSA region and is the lingua franca for science communication [25].

Search strategy

A search strategy, focusing on locating both published and unpublished grey primary studies and reviews was employed. An initial limited search was carried out in PubMed with a goal of identifying relevant studies on the topic (appendix I). The titles and abstracts of the above articles were helpful in generating a list of keywords or terms that were used in the development of the main search strategy later on. The key words that were applied in this search included: mHealth, digital health, telemedicine, mobile applications, individual countries of SSA, palliative care, terminal care and hospice. The databases that were searched from included: CINAHL(EBSCohost), PUBMED (ovid), Embase (Ovid), and Google Scholar (first 10 pages). Reference lists of papers generated above were screened for more studies relevant to the review questions and objectives using ascendancy and descendancy approach. There was no time restriction about studies to be included because mHealth in a palliative care setting is a relatively new concept in SSA. Also, sources of unpublished and grey literature were searched and these included; government reports and policy documents, dissertations, websites of palliative care organizations, academic theses, conference proceedings and abstracts. The above search processes were carried out in consultation with senior librarians at Mbarara University of Science and Technology (MUST) and Hospice Africa Uganda (HAU) from the 22nd August 2025 to 30th September 2025.

Selection of studies

The studies identified from the above search process were uploaded onto EndNote version 21 and the duplicates were eliminated manually. Using our established inclusion criteria, titles and abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers. Relevant papers were downloaded in full text and together with their citation details were exported into Covidence software for screening. The full text articles retrieved were assessed in detail for inclusion by 3 independent reviewers; J.B.N, F.N and E.M using the drawn inclusion criteria. The number and reasons of the excluded full-text papers were documented and noted. In cases of discordance between the 2 reviewers at any stage during the selection process, they were harmonized through discussion or through engagement with a third independent reviewer. Also, these search findings were presented in a detailed format and reported in the PRISMA flowchart.

Data extraction

Data were extracted from the included studied by 2 independent reviewers; NJ and NF by using a modified JBI data extraction form for scoping reviews (appendix II). The extracted data included key details such as author, title, year of publication, the study population, context, concept, objectives, methodology and key findings relevant to the review questions. The extraction form was pilot tested by the review team using 10% of the retrieved articles. During this process, the data from the articles was entered into the data extraction form to assess its suitability, relevance or adequacy in answering the questions and objectives of the scoping review. The tool was modified accordingly after the preliminary trial. There were five episodes of disagreement among the above reviewers regarding what material to extract, three of these were resolved through consensus by the duo while two of such were settled by involving a third reviewer, EM. In situations of missing information, authors of articles were contacted for that missing or additional information that was required.

Registration of the review protocol

The protocol for this review was registered with Open Science Framework (DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/GBQCN). Furthermore, a manuscript for the protocol was prepared, submitted and accepted for publication at the JBI Evidence Synthesis Journal.

Results

The three database and hand searches yielded a total pool of 224 articles of which, 34 duplicate records were noted and removed. Two reviewers (NJ and NF) independently and separately screened the remaining 190 titles and abstracts and excluded 125 articles. The remaining 65 articles were further subjected to full-text screening and this process generated 35 articles for inclusion in the review, where 30 articles were excluded for various reasons; 12 were not focusing on palliative care or management of life-limiting illnesses, 10 were about interventions implemented outside Sub Saharan Africa, while 8 were reviews reporting on primary studies that were already included in the 35 above. Study characteristics

Several study designs/ methodologies were reported in the studies that were included. These include; rapid evaluation methods [26], qualitative design using interviews [26], mixed methods [27–29], descriptive-analytical design [30], quasi-experimental designs [31], prospective cohort study design [32], randomized controlled studies [21, 33], pilot validation study design [34], cross-sectional qualitative study design [35], observational qualitative study design [36], two- phase co-design approach [37], human centered-iterative design framework [38], case studies [39] and qualitative secondary analysis study design [40].

The most dominating study design were qualitative studies n = 10 [35, 36, 40–46], followed by mixed methods n = 5 [27–29, 34, 47], prospective cohort studies n = 4 [32, 48–50], randomized trials n = 4 [21, 33, 51, 52], cross-sectional studies n = 4 [27, 36, 42, 43], pre- & post- test evaluation studies n = 1 [53], quasi-experimental studies n = 2 [31, 54], case studies n = 1 [39] and rapid evaluation methods n = 1 [26].

Distribution of publications about mHealth Interventions by year

About the type of mHealth interventions/ approaches, a number of them were reported by the studies and they are categorized as; SMS-based [7, 21, 29, 31, 45, 46, 51–53, 55], Telephone call-based [26, 30, 32, 49, 50, 56], Telemedicine-based [34, 42, 48, 54], wearable sensor-based [34], app-based [28, 33, 35–39, 44, 57, 58] and mixed interventions [27, 47, 51]. SMS-based mHealth interventions refers to the utilization of text messages as a modality of delivery of healthcare services through mobile phones. App-based approaches involve use of mobile applications (apps) to offer health services through tablets, smart phone and other movable telecommunication devices. Telemedicine-based interventions utilize mobile devices such as smart phones, tablets or computers and telecommunication to offer virtual consultation between healthcare providers and patients or amongst health care providers. Telephone call-based interventions basically are those approaches mediated by voice telephone calls. Wearable sensor-based mHealth interventions are those that use an imbedded sensor-wearable device in mobile applications or online platforms. Then, mixed mHealth interventions is an amalgamation of two or more of the mHealth approaches discussed above.

Our findings show that app-based interventions 35.3% (n = 12) were the commonest type of mHealth applied in palliative care in SSA followed by SMS-based interventions 26.5% (n = 9), phone call-based interventions 14.7% (n = 5), telemedicine-based interventions 11.8% (n = 4), mixed mHealth interventions 8.8% (n = 3) and wearable sensor-based intervention were the least type applied.

A very sizeable number of articles/ studies (94%, n = 33) articulated the uses for which the mobile health interventions were utilized are as shown in the figure. They included the following: Improving access to care through health system strengthening [35, 36, 43, 58], psychosocial support to patients, caregivers and family [26, 29, 42, 46, 53, 56], enhancing compliance to appointments for patients [31, 47, 55], Promoting adherence to medication among patients [27, 31, 33, 45, 51, 52, 55], health education [21, 30, 53, 56], monitoring, evaluation and research [28, 37, 48–50, 57], supporting symptom management general clinical care [21, 32, 36, 38, 39, 41, 43] and capacity building to health professionals in palliative care [35, 41] .

Factors influencing use of mHealth in palliative care in Sub Saharan Africa

Over 40% (40%) of the included studies (42.9%, n = 15) highlighted factors that influence the effective utilization of mHealth in palliative care in Sub Saharan Africa. Facilitating factors articulated include wide spread telephone ownership and access, the great robustness of telephone and internet connectivity [26], high degree of perceived usefulness and ease of use among the users [59], abundant willingness of health professionals to develop and adopt digital approaches to palliative care [43] and high degree of feasibility and acceptability of mHealth in a palliative care setting [38].

The highlighted barriers include; low levels of technological advancement/expertise [44], irregularity and paucity of electric power supply in some areas [42, 48]. Other hindrances are premised on perceived concerns about the high likelihood to compromise patients’ privacy and confidentiality [40], high illiteracy rates among the population in the region [21] and the perceived high likelihood to disrupt other processes of patient care, for example doctors/nurses receiving calls or notifications when they are engaged in clinical procedures and other processes of patient care [35].

Discussion

This scoping review was performed to establish a comprehensive evidence about the nature, use of mHealth interventions in palliative care as well as factors influencing their utilization.

In terms of the types of the mHealth interventions, namely; SMS-text-based, app-based, phone call-based, telemedicine-based, wearable sensor-based and mixed approaches, the majority of the interventions mentioned by the articles were app-based approaches, followed by SMS text-based interventions. This is somehow consistent with findings of another scoping review by about the use of mHealth approaches in SSA [60]. In the above study, telemedicine-based, app-based and SMS text-based interventions were the most commonly utilized mHealth approaches. The slight variation between the two studies can be attributed to the differences in the study setting; whereas the latter study considered the use of mHealth approaches in Sub Saharan Africa, our study restricted itself to the use of mHealth in palliative care in Sub Saharan Africa.

According to the World Bank, Sub Saharan Africa is made up of 48 countries [61]. Less than half of these (21) have integrated mHealth interventions in their general healthcare delivery systems [60] but only 12 have embedded it in palliative care according to this study. When analyzing the distribution of published mHealth approaches in the region, Uganda stands out as the most represented with the highest number of the interventions followed by Nigeria. This finding could be explained by the fact that Nigeria and Uganda were some of the first countries in Africa to start modern palliative care. The above phenomenon is elucidated by the fact that countries involved in mHealth for palliative care are few and still at a young stage where much input from governments, researchers and innovators is still expected and wanted. This is similar to results of an earlier systematic review which highlighted that the state of mHealth development for palliative care in Sub Saharan Africa was still nascent [7].

When examining the period of publication, the majority of the studies (n = 23, 65.7%) were published after 2017, only 12 (34.3%) were published between 2000 and 2016. Before 2000, no studies about mHealth interventions were identified in this scoping review, a clear indication that innovation, adoption and spread of mHealth for palliative care is by and large a new phenomenon that has gained prominence in the recent past [7].

With regard to the distribution of mHealth approaches by the target population, the majority of them focused on patients with life-limiting illnesses such as cancer followed by those centering on health professionals with the least proportion focusing on palliative care facilities.

Considering the uses for which mHealth interventions were instituted, twenty-five (25) of the studies reported on them. These included; improving access to care through health system strengthening [46, 53], psychosocial support to patients, caregivers and family [26, 29, 42], enhancement of compliance to appointments for patients [47, 55], promotion of adherence to medication [31, 45, 52], health education [30, 46], supporting health system management and capacity building to health professionals and other stakeholders in palliative care [21, 35, 39, 41]. The above findings concur with previous research work that mHealth has a fundamental role in palliative care and cancer management in general [62–64]. Furthermore, this is an affirmation to the WHO mHealth and Information Communication Technology (ICT) framework which underscores all the above uses as a means to enhance access, efficiency, coverage and high quality of health services especially in low resource settings [65].

In terms of factors influencing utilization of mHealth interventions for palliative care in the region, twenty-five (25) of the articles highlighted them. Some of the enabling factors mentioned include widespread ownership and access [26, 35, 45], robustness of internet and telephone connectivity [26, 40] and a high degree of perceived usefulness and ease of use of the interventions among the users [38, 57]. Notable hindrances articulated include low levels of technological advancement or expertise [56], irregularity and paucity of power supply and limited internet connectivity [42], perceived negative impact on patients’ confidentiality and privacy [35] as well as a seemingly disruptive nature of some interventions such as phone calls towards routine clinical care [35]. Building on the above strengths and solving the above weakness could help in buttressing mHealth for palliative care in the SSA region.

All in all, the results of the reports indicate that mHealth interventions have the potential to enhance ways of delivering palliative care by leveraging their feasibility and capacity to circumvent geographical barriers. It is prominently evident that mHealth approaches can reduce healthcare costs as compared to traditional methods [66] Ghani et al. 2020. This is because costs that would be incurred during long distance travels, complications that would occur due to patients’ lack of knowledge about a disease condition or inadequate patient-healthcare provider can be curtailed through embracing mHealth interventions in palliative care delivery system.

Future research opportunities

Like other developing regions of the world, SSA is grappling with a disproportionate burden of non-communicable diseases especially cancer that requires palliative care, the provision of which is woefully low in the region. This is further exacerbated by acute shortage of skilled health professionals [60]. However, with the rapid penetration of mobile devices and internet in the region, mHealth approaches are envisioned to provide a seminal game changer in scaling up the service as well as enhancing its quality.

The findings of this scoping review provide a firm bedrock for further robust research that will ultimately lead to further adoption of the technologies in the region. Nevertheless, giving information about medication adherence, life style and clinic appointment schedule to patients and their caregivers is not adequate enough to ensure efficient self-management among patients with life-limiting illnesses. Therefore, there is need for a system that assesses and evaluates important parameters that are directly related to the disease/ disorder in order to optimize the use of these mHealth approaches. There is also need to review and formulate policies on mHealth interventions for palliative care in SSA.

Strengths

This review applied a systematic and transparent methodology aligned with the JBI framework and PRISMA-ScR guidelines, which ensured great rigor and reliability. The broad exploration/ search across multiple electronic databases and grey literature sources curtailed bias and captured a broad spectrum of studies relevant to mHealth interventions in SSA. By incorporating a variety of research designs; qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods—the review provides a comprehensive understanding of the types, applications, and factors influencing mHealth in palliative care. The detailed mapping of interventions across countries and populations offers valuable insights for stakeholders, highlighting regional trends and gaps that can guide future research and policy development.

Limitations

Despite the above strengths, the review does have some weaknesses. One major limitation is the possibility of selection bias due to language restrictions, if any, which could have excluded relevant studies published in languages other than English. Additionally, the reliance on mainly published literature may lead to publication bias, where studies with positive findings are more likely to be included. The heterogeneity of included studies, in terms of design, population, and interventions, might have limited the ability to synthesize findings comprehensively. Moreover, as a scoping review, it did not assess risk of bias or the quality of individual studies, which could affect the interpretation of the overall evidence. Finally, given the rapidly evolving nature of mHealth technology, some recent developments would not have been captured if they were not yet published at the time of this study.

Conclusion

Approximately one-quarter of countries in the SSA region have integrated mHealth interventions into palliative care, highlighting the great need for further research and technological development in this sector. In order to better understand the scope and effectiveness of these interventions, future research, including systematic reviews could be conducted, which will ultimately inform policy development for palliative care. These innovative approaches could hold a vital promise for the development and scaling up of palliative care services in the region grappling with disproportionate disease burden and a fragile healthcare system.
