Changes in Respiratory Function over Time after Radiofrequency Ablation for Lung Tumors
Authors: Takaaki Hasegawa, Noriaki Sakakura, Yozo Sato, Shinichi Murata, Hidekazu Yamaura, Yoshitaka Inaba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose: To investigate changes in respiratory function after radiofrequency ablation for lung tumors.Material and Methods: Data from 21 patients who underwent respiratory function tests before radiofrequency ablation, during the early period (20-57 days; median, 34 days), and during the late period (104-692 days; median, 264 days) after lung radiofrequency ablation were retrospectively assessed. The index tumors included 10 primary lung cancers and 11 metastatic lung tumors, with a median tumor size of 12 mm (range, 4-21). Seventeen patients had a history of smoking, and the median Brinkman Index was 500 (range, 0-1,200). Percentage changes in vital capacity and forced expiratory volume in 1 s relative to baseline values were calculated and compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Risk factors for decreased vital capacity and forced expiratory volume in 1 s were examined by univariate analysis using the Mann-Whitney U test.Results: All radiofrequency ablation procedures were completed as planned. Vital capacity and forced expiratory volume in 1 s showed significant decreases to 98.0% (p = 0.048) and 96.8% (p = 0.048), respectively, in the early period, but recovered to 100.2% (p = 0.89) and 98.1% (p = 0.28), respectively, in the late period. Forced expiratory volume in 1 s decreased significantly in the early period among patients with a Brinkman Index >500 (98.9% for Brinkman Index ≤500 and 91.2% for Brinkman Index >500, p = 0.002).Conclusions: Respiratory function decreased temporarily within 2 months after lung radiofrequency ablation but recovered several months thereafter. Caution is needed because forced expiratory volume in 1 s tends to decrease in the early period after radiofrequency ablation in patients with a smoking history and a Brinkman Index >500.Keywords: lung tumor, radiofrequency ablation, respiratory function, smoking

Introduction

RFA has become widely used as a locoregional treatment for malignant lung tumors [1-3]. RFA is a less invasive treatment than surgery, and it can be applied to nonsurgical candidates. In particular, RFA has a minimal effect on posttreatment respiratory function; thus, patients with poor respiratory function are considered good candidates for lung RFA [4]. Several reports have indicated that respiratory function does not decrease after treatment [5-7], whereas a few reports have shown some decrease [8, 9]. Only a few reports have assessed changes in respiratory function over time after lung RFA [10]. Furthermore, the risk factors for decreased respiratory function have not been evaluated.

This study aimed to investigate the changes in respiratory function during the early and late periods after RFA for malignant lung tumors and to determine the factors contributing to decreased posttreatment respiratory function.

Material and Methods

Patients

This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board, which waived the need for informed consent for study inclusion. This study included patients who received lung RFA at our institution from April 2009 to November 2022. The inclusion criteria were patients (a) who underwent RFA for lung tumors and (b) who received respiratory function tests four weeks before RFA, during the early period, and during the late period. The early and late periods were defined as 14-60 days and 100-730 days after RFA, respectively. Patients who underwent locoregional treatment such as surgery, RFA, and radiotherapy between RFA and respiratory function test in the late period were excluded. A total of 21 patients were included in this study (Figure 1). All participants underwent routine physical examinations, laboratory tests, and imaging studies, including chest radiography and CT, within four weeks before treatment. Vital capacity (VC) and forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) were evaluated as respiratory function using a spirometer (Chestac 8900, CHEST M.I., Inc., Tokyo, Japan). We do not routinely measure the diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide in respiratory function tests before or during follow-up after lung RFA; therefore, only VC and FEV1 were evaluated in this study. The treatment strategy was determined through multidisciplinary team discussion involving thoracic oncologists, thoracic surgeons, radiotherapists, and interventional radiologists. A VC of 1 L or more and an FEV1 of 0.8 L or more were used as threshold values for respiratory function tests for indication of lung RFA at our institution.

Flowchart of patients who underwent lung RFA at our institution during the study period and those included in this study.

Table 1 summarizes the patients' background characteristics. The participants included 15 men and six women, with a median age of 63.5 years (range, 40-76). Respiratory function tests were evaluated at 20-57 days (median; 34) after RFA during the early period and at 104-692 days (median; 264) during the late period. The interval between the early and late periods was 56-664 days (median; 117). Seventeen patients (81%, 17/21) reported a smoking history, with a median Brinkman Index (BI) of 500; however, all had discontinued smoking before RFA. Although the number of tumors treated at each session was not considered as an inclusion or exclusion criterion, all patients had a single lung tumor treated with RFA.

RFA procedure

Lung RFA was performed percutaneously with the patient under moderate sedation using a sedative (PrecedexⓇ Injections; Pfizer Japan Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and local anesthesia (Xylocaine; Astellas Pharma Inc., Tokyo, Japan) on an inpatient basis. Real-time CT fluoroscopy (Aquilion LB or Aquilion Prime; Canon Medical Systems Corp., Otawara, Japan) was used for image guidance. All procedures were performed with an internally cooled electrode (STAR RF Electrode; STARmed Co., Seoul, Korea). Generally, the active tip length was set to the index tumor diameter plus 10-15 mm. Radiofrequency energy was applied for 12 minutes or until five breaks (steep impedance increases) occurred using an impedance-control algorithm. When the ablation time was too short due to consecutive breaks related to a rapid increase in tissue impedance, manual ablation was applied. Chest CT was performed immediately after RFA to assess adverse events (AEs), and additional ablation was applied at the discretion of the operator when the ablation margin was considered insufficient on CT images. Chest X-ray was followed-up 2 h, 1 day, and 2 days after RFA to assess AEs.

After RFA, physical examinations, laboratory tests, and CT were performed within 2 months, then every 3-4 months for 2 years, and every 6 months thereafter. Respiratory function testing was not performed routinely, but was conducted at the discretion of the attending physician or when additional treatment was required. Data were followed until death or March 31, 2025.

Assessment

All variables are presented as median values. Technical success of the RFA procedure was defined as completion of the RFA treatment protocol. Percentage changes in VC and FEV1 from baseline were calculated, and their medians were compared with baseline values using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Further, median percentage changes in VC and FEV1 from baseline were compared between subgroups (primary lung tumor vs metastatic lung tumor; tumor size ≤10 mm vs >10 mm; tumor location in the left lung vs right lung; hilar location [located in the inner half of the lung] vs peripheral location [located in the outer half]; active tip length of 20 mm vs 30 mm; presence vs absence of emphysema; BI ≤500 vs >500; previous ipsilateral lung surgery vs none; previous contralateral lung surgery vs none; and development of AE vs none) using univariate analysis with the Mann-Whitney U test. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using commercially available software (SPSS for Windows, version 24; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

RFA procedure

RFA was performed in a single session for each patient. All RFA procedures were completed as planned (technical success rate, 100%). Self-limiting pneumothorax developed in five cases (24%, 5/21). One patient (5%, 1/21) developed pneumothorax requiring temporary chest tube insertion and recovered without further intervention. No additional AEs, including pneumonia, developed within 6 months after RFA.

During the median follow-up period of 2601 days (range, 685-5,566), no local tumor progression was observed.

Respiratory function after lung RFA

The median VC and FEV1 were 3.11 L (range, 1.79-4.01) and 1.99 L (range, 0.83-2.80) at baseline; 3.04 L (range, 1.74-3.90) and 1.88 L (range, 0.80-2.79) in the early period; and 3.08 L (range, 1.83-4.02) and 2.02 L (range, 0.81-2.83) in the late period, respectively. Figure 2 shows the changes in VC and FEV1. The median percentage changes in VC and FEV1 showed significant decreases to 98.0% (p = 0.048) and 96.8% (p = 0.048), respectively, in the early period, but recovered to 100.2% (p = 0.89) and 98.1% (p = 0.28), respectively, in the late period. No patients complained of respiratory discomfort throughout the follow-up period.

Discussion

This study showed changes in respiratory function over time after RFA for lung tumors. VC and FEV1 decreased significantly in the early period, but the change was <5%, and no patients complained of respiratory discomfort after RFA. Furthermore, the decrease recovered in the late period. Thus, lung RFA can be said to have a minimal effect on respiratory function.

The results of the present study are consistent with previous reports in that respiratory function was impaired within 3 months but recovered during long-term follow-up after lung RFA [5-9]. In particular, a recent study evaluated respiratory function tests before and after RFA for ground-glass nodules [10]; it showed that respiratory function decreased temporarily within 1 month after treatment but recovered after 6 months. In the present study, the index tumors were solid, but the results were similar. Moreover, Wang et al. [11] evaluated respiratory function tests 3 days and 6 months after microwave ablation (MWA), rather than RFA, for lung tumors. They also reported that respiratory function was impaired initially but later returned to the preoperative state. Since RFA and MWA are both heat-based ablation therapies, similar effects on respiratory function are reasonable. One explanation for the early decreases in VC and FEV1 may be treatment-related alveolar hemorrhage, inflammation, and pain. The effects of pneumothorax and pneumonia may have been other contributing factors, but no severe AEs were observed in the participants of the present study. In any case, assessment in the early period may underestimate respiratory function after RFA.

The present study found a significant decrease in FEV1 after lung RFA in patients with a BI >500. This finding indicates the detrimental effect of heavy smoking on respiratory function in the context of antitumor interventions such as thermal ablation. The decreased FEV1 can be attributed to several mechanisms. First, heavy smoking induces persistent airway inflammation, which can be intensified by ablation treatment [12]. The procedure itself may trigger an inflammatory response and further impair lung function in smokers. Furthermore, smoking causes structural changes in the bronchial walls, including thickening and fibrosis [12, 13]. These changes reduce airway elasticity and diameter, contributing to the decreased FEV1.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center, retrospective study; thus, selection bias could not be avoided. The decision to perform respiratory function testing and its timing was discretionary, which may have exacerbated the selection bias. Second, the sample size was small. We could not conduct multivariable analysis due to the insufficient sample size. Therefore, large studies are required to validate the results. Third, since no patients developed severe AEs, the effects of AEs on respiratory function could not be assessed. Finally, all cases had only one lesion with a relatively small size; thus, the ablation area was small, and the effect on respiratory function may have been minimal.

In conclusion, lung RFA is considered a less invasive treatment, since respiratory function decreased temporarily in the early period but recovered to the preoperative state in the late period. However, caution is needed, because FEV1 tends to decrease in the early period after RFA in patients with a smoking history and a BI >500.
