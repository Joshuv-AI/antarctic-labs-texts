The recovery of lower limb function in stroke patients: which sequence of transcranial direct current stimulation combined with lower limb rehabilitation robot is most effective—A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Kai Wan, Qiaojun Zhang, Peng Li, Yumeng Xu, Libo Li, Yaxin Yang, Xiaohui Lei, Ben Ma, Lei Lu, Siduo Zhang, Weiqi Li, Peijia Fu, Xin Gao, Hongfei Qiao, Bin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to directly compare the effects of synchronous versus sequential tDCS combined with RAGT on lower limb motor function, balance, and walking ability in stroke patients with hemiplegia through a randomized controlled trial.MethodsThis study was designed as a prospective, single-blind, randomized controlled trial. A total of 60 eligible stroke patients with hemiplegia were recruited and randomly assigned to three groups, with 20 patients in each group. The synchronous group received anodal tDCS stimulation concurrently during lower-limb robotic-assisted gait training (RAGT). The sequential group received tDCS stimulation first, followed immediately by RAGT. The sham stimulation group received sham tDCS stimulation during RAGT. All groups also received conventional rehabilitation training. The intervention was administered five days per week for four weeks. The primary outcome measure was the Fugl-Meyer Assessment of Lower Extremity (FMA-LE) score. Secondary outcome measures included the Berg Balance Scale (BBS) and the Functional Ambulation Category (FAC). All assessments were conducted at baseline (T0), after two weeks of intervention (T1), and after four weeks of intervention (T2) by assessors blinded to group allocation.ResultsData analysis of the 54 study completers showed that repeated measures ANOVA indicated significant main effects of time, group, and time × group interactions for both FMA-LE and BBS scores (p < 0.001). Simple effects analysis revealed that at T1, the synchronous group had significantly higher scores than the sequential group (FMA-LE p = 0.002; BBS p = 0.006) and the sham stimulation group (p < 0.001). By T2, the synchronous group’s scores were significantly better than those of the sequential group (p < 0.001), while the sequential group also significantly outperformed the sham stimulation group (p < 0.001). Generalized estimating equations analysis showed that the group, time, and interaction effects for FAC were all significant (p < 0.01), with the synchronous group having the highest cumulative odds ratio (47.893), significantly better than the sequential group (18.379) and the sham stimulation group.ConclusionThe combination of tDCS and lower limb robotic-assisted gait training effectively improves lower limb function in stroke patients, and the timing of combination significantly influences the treatment outcome. Specifically, the synchronous combination mode demonstrates faster and greater improvements in lower limb motor function, balance, and walking ability, significantly outperforming the sequential mode, making it a superior “brain-limb collaborative” rehabilitation strategy.Trial registration This study was preregistered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration number: ChiCTR2400086567).Keywords: Transcranial direct current stimulation, Stroke, Central modulation, Robotic-assisted gait training, Lower limb motor function

Introduction

Stroke is the leading cause of long-term disability and functional impairment in adults worldwide, with its high incidence and disability rates placing a substantial social and economic burden [1]. Among stroke survivors, more than half are left with significant lower limb motor dysfunction, characterized by symptoms such as decreased muscle strength, impaired balance control, and abnormal gait [2]. These deficits severely compromise patients’ ability to walk independently, limit their participation in daily activities, and ultimately lead to an overall decline in quality of life [3]. Therefore, exploring and optimizing effective lower limb rehabilitation strategies to maximize the recovery of walking function has become a core challenge and an urgent need in the field of neurorehabilitation.

Conventional rehabilitation therapies, such as physical therapy and occupational therapy, form the cornerstone of stroke rehabilitation. However, their efficacy is often limited by factors including therapist experience, insufficient training intensity and repetition, and patient compliance, leading many patients to reach a recovery plateau prematurely [4]. To overcome these limitations, lower-limb robotic-assisted gait training (RAGT) has emerged [5]. It can provide high-intensity, highly repetitive gait training with precisely controllable parameters. By simulating the physiological gait cycle, it delivers regular and rich proprioceptive input to the central nervous system, thereby promoting the recovery of motor control and enhancement of muscle strength [6]. Substantial evidence indicates that RAGT positively improves walking speed, endurance, and overall motor function in post-stroke patients [6, 7]. A recent Cochrane systematic review further confirmed that, compared to conventional rehabilitation, electromechanically-assisted training significantly increases the proportion of patients achieving “independent walking” [8]. However, robotic training is inherently a “bottom-up” intervention model, primarily driving adaptive changes in the central nervous system through peripheral sensory input, with relatively limited direct and active modulation capability over cortical neuroplasticity [9].

Although numerous studies confirm the positive effects of RAGT on functional recovery in stroke patients, a critical question arises: Has functional recovery in patients receiving robotic training reached its individual peak? Is there room to further enhance the efficiency of neural remodeling and the upper limit of functional recovery? This question is supported by empirical research.

An early study by Mirelman et al. [10] provided key evidence, finding that RAGT combined with virtual reality (VR) led to significantly greater improvements in gait performance in stroke patients compared to RAGT alone. This finding profoundly reveals that interventions relying solely on peripherally-driven robotics have an efficacy ceiling; rehabilitation effectiveness can be significantly enhanced when a centrally-oriented technology that increases brain engagement and sensory integration is introduced. This strongly suggests that suboptimal outcomes in some patients are precisely because peripheral-only interventions fail to fully exploit and drive the reorganization potential of the central nervous system.

In recent years, non-invasive neuromodulation techniques, represented by transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), have provided a new “top-down” approach to enhance stroke rehabilitation outcomes [11]. tDCS modulates the resting membrane potential of cortical neurons via low-intensity direct current applied to the scalp. Specifically, anodal tDCS can significantly increase the excitability of the primary motor cortex in the lesioned hemisphere, while cathodal tDCS can help suppress overactivity in the non-lesioned hemisphere, thereby restoring the post-stroke imbalance in interhemispheric inhibition [12]. Research shows that tDCS is not only non-invasive, portable, and safe, but the neuromodulatory effects it induces can persist for a period after the stimulation ceases. This after-effect makes it an ideal tool for synergistic application with rehabilitation training [13–15]. When tDCS is combined with robotic training, it forms a “brain-limb closed-loop” synergistic system. The robot provides precise peripheral sensory feedback and motor output, while tDCS creates optimal conditions for the reorganization of neural circuits underlying motor learning and memory by enhancing synaptic plasticity and long-term potentiation (LTP)-like effects [16].

Based on this, the novel “brain-limb collaborative” rehabilitation model, which combines the central neuromodulation of tDCS with the peripheral training of lower limb robot-assisted gait training (RAGT), demonstrates great potential. This combined strategy aims to “prime” or “modulate” the brain’s motor network either prior to or during training, enabling more efficient information integration and encoding when receiving precise gait input from the robot, thereby producing a synergistic effect [17, 18]. Regarding tDCS combined with RAGT, the study by Kim et al. [17] demonstrated that simultaneous high-definition tDCS combined with RAGT significantly improved walking speed and motor function in stroke patients. The randomized controlled trial by Zhang et al. [18] also confirmed that tDCS combined with RAGT effectively enhanced lower limb motor function, accompanied by changes in motor cortex activation patterns. In addition to tDCS, the combined application of other neuromodulation techniques with RAGT has also gained increasing attention. In terms of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) combined with RAGT, the study by Jehangir et al. [19] showed that high-frequency rTMS applied to the ipsilesional M1 area enhanced the efficacy of RAGT, improving walking ability and balance function. Furthermore, the recent network meta-analysis by Huang et al. [20] systematically compared the efficacy of various neuromodulation techniques combined with RAGT and found that tDCS combined with RAGT had advantages in improving lower limb motor function, providing higher-level evidence-based support for the combined application of tDCS and RAGT. However, a critical and underexplored scientific question emerges: What is the optimal timing for combining tDCS with robotic training? Different intervention timings may influence rehabilitation outcomes through distinct neural mechanisms. Currently, rigorous research specifically addressing the timing issue is scarce. Theoretically, two primary models exist: The first is the synchronous mode, where tDCS is applied concurrently with RAGT. This mode is theoretically grounded in Hebbian theory [21, 22], emphasizing that the precise temporal coincidence of central neuronal activity and peripheral afferent signals is key to maximizing synaptic connection strength. The second is the sequential mode, where tDCS is applied first, followed immediately by RAGT within the window of cortical excitability after-effects. This mode aims to “prepare” a brain in an optimally excitable state for subsequent motor learning. Yet, direct evidence from well-designed randomized controlled trials comparing the relative effectiveness of these two modes in inducing lower limb functional recovery is currently lacking.

Therefore, this study aims to conduct a prospective, single-blind, randomized controlled trial to directly compare the effects of synchronous versus sequential tDCS combined with lower limb robot-assisted gait training on lower limb motor function, balance, and walking ability in stroke patients with hemiplegia. Three groups were established: (1) Synchronous group: received anodal tDCS concurrently during robot-assisted gait training; (2) Sequential group: received anodal tDCS first, followed immediately by robot-assisted gait training; (3) Sham stimulation group: received sham tDCS during robot-assisted gait training to strictly control for placebo effects. We hypothesized that both active tDCS interventions would improve lower limb function more effectively than the sham stimulation group. We further hypothesized that, due to the immediate coupling of central modulation and peripheral input, the synchronous mode would demonstrate superior effects in improving lower limb motor function, balance, and walking ability compared to the sequential mode. The results of this study are expected to provide high-level evidence-based medical evidence for formulating optimized, timing-based “brain-limb collaborative” rehabilitation protocols, thereby advancing lower limb stroke rehabilitation towards greater precision and individualization.

Methods

Study design

This is a prospective single-blind randomized clinical controlled trial. All reports from this trial were prepared in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement [23]. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (Approval No. 2024096) and was pre-registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No. ChiCTR2400086567). This study adheres to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants received detailed oral and written information about the study and provided written informed consent. Before signing, the research team ensured that participants fully understood the study purpose, intervention content, potential risks, and personal privacy protection measures. An independent statistician, not involved in the study, allocated all subjects using a random number table method, ensuring the independence and transparency of the randomization process, with an allocation ratio of 1:1:1 to the Synchronous Group (simultaneous RAGT and tDCS), Sequential Group (tDCS followed immediately by RAGT), and Sham Stimulation Group (wearing a non-activated tDCS device while undergoing RAGT). Due to differences in the total duration and sequence of interventions, participants and therapists in the synchronous and sequential groups could not be blinded to group allocation. In the sham stimulation group, a standardized sham stimulation protocol was used to ensure that participants remained blinded to the authenticity of the stimulation. All outcome assessors and statistical analysts were kept blinded to group allocation throughout the study. All data analyses were performed by a statistician independent of the trial process to ensure the impartiality of the results.

Study setting and participants

The study was conducted in the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. Between July 2024 and July 2025, patients were recruited from the department itself and various communities in Xi’an City through instant messaging tools (WeChat, QQ, WeChat public platform) and paper notices.

Inclusion criteria: ① Time since stroke onset ≥ 1 month, with stable medical condition, and a unilateral lesion (cortical or subcortical); ② Age between 45 and 75 years; ③ Brunnstrom stage of the affected lower limb ≥ Stage II; ④ Clear consciousness, stable vital signs, and good cooperation with training; ⑤ Willingness to provide signed informed consent for this study. Exclusion criteria: ① Presence of impaired consciousness, cognitive impairment, mental disorders, or mixed aphasia affecting assessment and treatment; ② Presence of concurrent cerebral hemorrhage; ③ Severe dysfunction of important organs such as liver, kidney, or heart; ④ Presence of musculoskeletal diseases affecting lower limb mobility; ⑤ Implanted cardiac pacemaker or other metal medical devices in the skull; ⑥ Modified Ashworth spasticity grade of the affected lower limb ≥ 3; ⑦ Any condition identified by the investigator that could harm the subject or prevent them from meeting or complying with the study requirements.

We determined the locations of stroke lesions based on structural brain MRI or CT images obtained during the acute hospitalization period and classified the lesions in two steps. First, lesions were categorized by anatomical level into cortical only, subcortical only, or mixed cortical–subcortical involvement (i.e., simultaneous involvement of cortical and deep structures). Second, lesions were annotated according to motor-related regions, allowing multiple regions to be assigned to the same patient when applicable. These regions included cortical areas—the primary motor cortex (M1) and the premotor and supplementary motor areas (PMC/SMA)—as well as subcortical areas, including the basal ganglia, internal capsule, thalamus, brainstem, and cerebellum. Acute-phase stroke management

All participants received guideline-recommended standard acute stroke management during their hospitalization prior to enrollment. Acute-phase treatments included stabilization of medical condition, secondary prevention, prevention of complications, and early mobilization as tolerated.

Basic acute-phase rehabilitation interventions primarily consisted of positioning as clinically indicated, range-of-motion exercises, and early bedside mobilization. It is important to emphasize that during the acute phase, none of the participants received transcranial direct current stimulation, robot-assisted gait training, or other neuromodulation interventions. The acute-phase management strategies were essentially consistent across all study groups.

Medication use

The medication regimen of all participants upon enrollment was documented. Commonly used medications included antiplatelet agents (such as aspirin or clopidogrel), statins, antihypertensive drugs, and glucose-lowering agents as clinically indicated. None of the participants were taking medications known to directly influence cortical excitability, such as antiepileptic drugs or centrally acting neurostimulants. The distribution of medications was comparable among the synchronous stimulation, sequential stimulation, and sham stimulation groups, and all medications were continued according to standard clinical practice throughout the study period.

Intervention

Conventional rehabilitation protocol

The conventional rehabilitation program consisted of two components: physical agent therapy and therapeutic exercise. Physical agent therapy primarily included low-frequency electrical stimulation and functional electrical stimulation. Therapeutic exercise encompassed muscle strength training, neurodevelopmental techniques, and trunk muscle and pelvic control training. Building on this, focused training was implemented for trunk balance control in sitting and standing positions, enhancement of lower limb weight-bearing capacity, and clearance training for the affected lower limb. The training was conducted for 60–90 min per day, 5 days per week, for a total of 4 weeks.

Transcranial direct current stimulation protocol

The tDCS protocol was administered using the 8060 tDCS stimulator manufactured by Wuhan Yimai Medical Technology Co., Ltd. Electrode sponges (50 mm × 70 mm) were soaked in saline solution. The anode was placed over the primary motor cortex (M1 region) of the lesioned cerebral hemisphere, and the cathode was fixed above the contralateral orbit. For the Synchronous Group, real stimulation was applied with a current intensity of 2 mA for 20 min per session, once daily, 5 days per week. For the Sham Stimulation Group, a sham stimulation protocol was used: the current ramped up to 1.4 mA over 10 s at the beginning, then immediately ramped down to 0 mA and remained there until the end of the session; all other parameters were identical to the Synchronous Group. The sequential group received the same anodal tDCS protocol as the synchronous group, but the stimulation was administered immediately before, rather than concurrently with, the RAGT session.

Robotic-assisted gait training protocol

The RAGT intervention was delivered using the lower limb exoskeleton walking rehabilitator (DH-A1) from Guangzhou Dinghe Technology Co., Ltd. The specific procedures were as follows: The therapist first recorded the patient’s basic information, including height, bilateral thigh length, calf length, and lower limb length, into the system to create a personal database. The thigh and calf segments of the robot were adjusted according to the leg lengths to align the patient’s hip and knee joints with the corresponding robot joints, and the data were updated. The patient’s lower limbs were secured to the exoskeleton frame, with feet placed securely on the footplates and heels positioned snugly. Straps were used to fasten the waist, knees, and feet, checking for proper fit and comfort. The therapist adjusted parameters such as step frequency, speed, and length based on the patient’s functional status. The device offers six movement environment modes; the therapist selected the appropriate mode according to the patient’s walking ability. During training, patients were continuously guided to actively participate in walking, enhancing their perception and control of gait. The training regimen consisted of 20-minute sessions, once daily, 5 times per week, for 4 weeks. Patient responses were monitored throughout the session. Training was immediately stopped if significant discomfort occurred, the patient’s condition was assessed, and the training plan was adjusted accordingly. The RAGT intervention for all participants was jointly administered by two rehabilitation therapists who had received unified training, both possessing over five years of experience in neurorehabilitation. To ensure intervention consistency, the therapists underwent a two-week training session on standard operating procedures prior to the study and passed an assessment. During the study period, weekly quality control meetings were held to review the operational procedures and discuss implementation, thereby ensuring standardized delivery of the intervention.

Synchronous Group (tDCS Synchronous with RAGT): The patient was first prepared and positioned on the RAGT device according to the standard operating procedures. Another rehabilitation practitioner, following the tDCS operation manual and brain localization markings, applied the tDCS electrodes to the patient and set the tDCS to the predetermined parameters. The RAGT and tDCS were started simultaneously. The intervention was administered once daily, with a total session duration of 20 min, 5 consecutive days per week, for 4 weeks.

Sequential Group (tDCS Sequential to RAGT): In addition to conventional rehabilitation training, this group received both tDCS and RAGT, but the two treatments were separated in time. They first received 20 min of tDCS, followed immediately by 20 min of RAGT. This was administered once daily, resulting in a total daily intervention time of 40 min, 5 days per week, for 4 weeks.

Sham Stimulation Group (RAGT with Sham tDCS): Patients in this group underwent RAGT while simultaneously wearing the tDCS equipment, which was set to the sham stimulation mode (i.e., no actual current delivery after the initial ramp-up/ramp-down) to control for psychological and non-specific effects.

Outcomes

In this study, a set of fundamental assessment tools was employed to evaluate patients’ lower limb physical function and activity levels. The selection of these tools was based on international consensus recommendations and clinical practice guidelines for stroke rehabilitation research [24]. All patients were assessed by therapists with six years of rehabilitation experience from the research team at three time points: before treatment (T0), two weeks after treatment (T1), and four weeks after treatment (T2). The assessment tools included the Fugl-Meyer Assessment for Lower Extremity (FMA-LE), the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), and the Functional Ambulation Category (FAC), which were used to evaluate patients’ lower limb motor function, balance ability, and walking capacity, respectively. All assessors were blinded to the participants’ group allocation.

Primary outcome

Fugl-Meyer motor function assessment–lower extremity

The FMA-LE is a standardized evaluation tool widely used in the field of neurorehabilitation, particularly for assessing lower limb motor function in stroke patients [25]. This rating scale comprehensively evaluates various aspects of lower limb function through multiple items, including voluntary movement, muscle strength, coordination, reflex activity, and gait control. Each item in the assessment utilizes a 0-to-2-point scoring system, where 0 indicates an inability to perform the task or a complete absence of function, and 2 represents the ability to perform the task fully and normally.

Secondary outcomes

Berg balance scale

The BBS is used to assess a patient’s static and dynamic balance abilities, particularly suitable for individuals with lower limb motor impairments [26]. This scale consists of 14 items, with a scoring range of 0 to 4 points for each item. It primarily measures the patient’s stability and coordination in various postures. The total score obtained from the BBS can be used to evaluate the patient’s balance control capacity.

Functional ambulation category

The FAC is used to assess a patient’s walking capacity across different environments, with a specific focus on the level of independence during ambulation [27]. This scale rates patients based on the degree of physical assistance required, if any, to perform walking tasks. Scoring ranges from 0 (indicating complete dependence on physical support from another person) to5 (representing the ability to walk independently on various surfaces).

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation

The primary outcome measure of this study was the FMA-LE score. Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1). The parameters were set as follows: number of groups = 3 (Synchronous group, Sequential group, Sham group), number of measurements = 3 (T0, T1, T2), effect size f = 0.20 (medium), significance level α = 0.05, statistical power (1 − β) = 0.80, assumed within-group correlation coefficient r = 0.5, and non-sphericity correction ε = 1. The calculation indicated a minimum requirement of 15 participants per group, totaling 45 participants across all three groups. Accounting for an estimated dropout rate of approximately 30%, the final planned sample size was 60 participants, with 20 participants allocated to each group.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous data conforming to a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed continuous data are presented as median (interquartile range). Categorical data are presented as number (percentage). The significance level (α) was set at 0.05 (two-tailed). The normality of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For baseline comparisons of continuous variables across the three groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used if the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were met; otherwise, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was employed. Categorical data were compared across groups using the chi-square test. For the primary outcome measure (a continuous variable) and secondary continuous outcome measures, a repeated-measures analysis of variance (Repeated-Measures ANOVA) was used to evaluate the main effects of time, group, and the time-by-group interaction effect. If a significant interaction was found, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted. Both within-group comparisons across different time points and between-group comparisons at the same time point used the Bonferroni correction to control for Type I error. For the ordinal secondary outcome measure (FAC), a Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) model was employed. As the FAC is an ordinal categorical variable (scores 0–5) and repeated measurements from the same participant across different time points are inherently correlated, the GEE model is well suited to handle this data structure. In the model, the FAC was specified as following a multinomial ordinal distribution with a cumulative logit link function. Group, time point, and their interaction term were included as fixed effects. This model was used to estimate the cumulative odds ratios and their 95% confidence intervals for each intervention group compared to the reference group (sham stimulation group), thereby quantifying the effects of different intervention modes on the improvement of walking function levels.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

A total of 112 stroke patients were screened for this study, of which 60 met the inclusion criteria. These participants were randomly allocated into three groups: 20 to the synchronous group, 20 to the sequential group, and 20 to the sham stimulation group. During the study period, a total of 6 participants did not complete the full intervention. The reasons for withdrawal and the number of dropouts in each group were as follows: in the synchronous group, 1 participant withdrew due to recurrent stroke and 1 was discharged early before completing the intervention; in the sequential group, 1 participant withdrew due to recurrent stroke; in the sham stimulation group, 1 participant withdrew due to recurrent stroke and 2 withdrew due to pulmonary infection. Consequently, 54 participants completed the entire study protocol and were included in the final data analysis (see Fig. 1). There were no statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics among the three groups, including age, gender, body weight, hemiplegic side, time since stroke onset, lesion type, and distribution of motor-related lesion regions (p > 0.05). A Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) model was employed to analyze the effects of group, time, and their interaction on the FAC. The results indicated significant main effects of group (Wald χ² = 13.978, p = 0.001) and time (Wald χ² =131.271, p < 0.001), as well as a significant group × time interaction (Wald χ²=17.473, p = 0.002). This confirms that the trends in gait function improvement over time differed significantly among the groups. Parameter estimates revealed that the cumulative odds ratio for an improvement in FAC level was 47.893 for the synchronous group compared to the sham group, and 18.379 for the sequential group compared to the sham group. Compared to the 4-week post-treatment assessment, the odds ratio for FAC improvement was only 0.006 at pre-treatment and 0.059 at the 2-week post-treatment assessment. At the 2-week post-treatment mark, gait function significantly improved from baseline in both the synchronous and sequential groups, with the most substantial improvement in the synchronous group, while the sham group showed no significant change. Discussion

It must be emphasized that all participants in this study were in the early subacute phase of stroke recovery, with an onset duration of less than 70 days. This period is characterized by heightened neuroplasticity and enhanced responsiveness to interventions that couple central neuromodulation with peripheral task-specific training. Within this well-defined population, our randomized controlled trial compared the effects of different temporal sequences of tDCS combined with RAGT on lower limb motor function, balance, and gait recovery. The results clearly demonstrate that while all interventions promoted functional improvement to some extent, the magnitude and trajectory of recovery differed significantly across groups. The primary finding is that synchronous tDCS combined with RAGT exhibited the fastest and most pronounced therapeutic advantages in improving functional gait, lower limb motor function, and balance. This superiority emerged as early as 2 weeks and continued to expand at 4 weeks. These results not only confirm our primary hypothesis but also provide strong empirical evidence for optimizing the timing of combined tDCS and robotic training. This finding suggests that the synchronous application of these two neurorehabilitation techniques produces a significant synergistic effect, rather than a simple additive effect. Our findings align with, yet importantly deepen, previous exploratory research on the timing of combining tDCS with motor training. Early studies preliminarily suggested the potential of synchronous intervention. For instance, research by Lindenberg et al. found that applying tDCS bilaterally over M1 areas synchronously with upper limb rehabilitation training led to better motor recovery compared to training alone [28].Similarly, work by Bolognini et al. also supports that applying tDCS during task execution might be more beneficial for motor learning [29].However, these studies predominantly focused on the upper limb and did not directly compare the “synchronous” and “sequential” timing strategies head-to-head. By directly comparing synchronous, sequential, and sham stimulation control conditions, our study suggests the crucial role of the “time-by-mode” interaction in reshaping the recovery trajectory. It indicates that different tDCS application modes do not simply produce additive effects but, acting as a neuromodulator, may profoundly alter the rate and trajectory of functional recovery.

The pronounced early advantage demonstrated by the synchronous intervention is the core finding of this study. From the perspective of neuroplasticity theory, this early advantage may stem from the optimal utilization of Hebbian plasticity principles [21].In the synchronous mode, anodal tDCS delivers depolarizing stimulation to the primary motor cortex (M1) precisely while the patient is actively engaged in robot-guided lower limb movement. In this scenario, the tDCS-induced increase in cortical excitability [17, 18]and the peripheral proprioceptive input generated from motor execution, along with the efferent motor commands, achieve precise “co-activation” in time [20]. This immediate coupling of central modulation and peripheral input is believed to maximally enhance synaptic connection strength within the relevant neural circuits of the motor cortex [30],thereby more efficiently consolidating motor memory and facilitating the rapid rebuilding of functional circuits [31]. In contrast, the sequential mode temporally separates the central modulation from the peripheral input. Although tDCS preconditioning might “pre-activate” the motor cortex to some extent, creating a background state of excitability favorable for learning [32, 33],the half-life of this state is limited. Its coupling with the subsequent specific motor task execution is inevitably less tight than in the synchronous mode [34],potentially leading to the loss of some “priming effect” and consequently delaying the manifestation of functional improvements. It is important to emphasize that the overall effect of the sequential intervention was still superior to the sham group, indicating its clinical value; future studies could explore optimizing stimulation parameters or identifying more suitable patient subgroups to further enhance its efficacy.

The advantage of the synchronous mode also stems from the task-dependent nature of tDCS-induced plasticity [35]. Previous research has demonstrated that tDCS-induced changes in cortical excitability are highly task-specific, with their direction and magnitude depending on the specific activity performed during stimulation [36]. When electrical stimulation is strictly synchronized with a meaningful motor task directly relevant to the target function, the excitability changes are more likely to be “locked” onto the specific neural networks involved in that task, achieving targeted remodeling [35]. In this study, patients in the synchronous group received tDCS while their brains were simultaneously processing sensorimotor information directly related to gait and balance control; therefore, the modulatory effects of tDCS may have been more precisely directed towards and potentiated the core networks essential for lower limb functional recovery [37]. In contrast, the sequential mode lacks this immediate task context, resulting in more diffuse excitability changes and less efficient binding to the specific lower limb functional circuits.

In this study, besides comparing the synchronous and sequential groups, we also included a sham stimulation group as a control to further validate the actual effect of tDCS in the rehabilitation intervention. Patients in the sham group wore the tDCS equipment but did not receive actual current stimulation, while undergoing standard robotic training. Although the sham group received the same robotic training as the synchronous and sequential groups, their recovery outcomes were significantly lower due to the absence of current stimulation. From a neurophysiological mechanism perspective, the sham group presumably failed to modulate cortical excitability via tDCS, thus suggesting a lack of effective activation of neural plasticity in the brain, which may have contributed to slower progress in functional recovery. In contrast, the synchronous group, through the immediate coupling of tDCS stimulation with training, significantly enhanced cortical excitability highly coupled with the motor task, thereby promoting neural network remodeling and functional recovery [17]. Although the sequential group also received tDCS stimulation, its effect was inferior to the synchronous group, and the recovery rate was slower, due to the temporal separation of stimulation and training. This result further verifies the role of tDCS in promoting neuroplasticity, indicating that the synchronous mode is significantly superior to both the sequential mode and the sham group in accelerating functional recovery, underscoring the critical role of actual current stimulation in enhancing neural excitability and functional recovery.

Corroborating the aforementioned neurophysiological mechanisms, our clinical data also consistently demonstrated a pattern of synchronous advantage. The synchronous group showed the most significant improvements in the FAC, FMA-LE, and BBS at both the 2-week and 4-week post-treatment assessments. This aligns with previous studies reporting that tDCS can improve post-stroke gait [17, 38, 39] and balance function [40], but our research further clarifies the timing conditions for achieving optimal efficacy. In the synchronous mode, the immediate coupling of tDCS with rehabilitation training may enable more precise and efficient neuromodulation of the brain’s control over gait, movement, and balance, which is associated with enhanced cortical activity observed using techniques like functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) [18]

In addition to statistical significance, assessing whether the improvements in clinical outcomes reach the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) is equally critical for determining the clinical value of an intervention. Previous studies have reported that the MCID for the FMA-LE in post-stroke patients ranges from 4 to 6 points [41], the MCID for the BBS ranges from 5 to 6 points [42], and a one-level increase in the FAC is generally considered clinically meaningful [27]. In the present study, the synchronous group achieved a mean FMA-LE improvement of 15 points, and the sequential group achieved 11 points, both exceeding the MCID threshold; the sham group improved by 3.82 points, falling below the lower limit of the MCID. For the BBS, the synchronous group improved by 22 points and the sequential group by 14 points, both surpassing the MCID; the sham group improved by 7 points, only slightly exceeding the lower MCID limit. Regarding the FAC, 94% of patients in the synchronous group achieved independent walking (FAC ≥ 4) at 4 weeks post-treatment, which was significantly superior to the sequential group (47%) and the sham group (6%). These results indicate that synchronous tDCS combined with RAGT not only produced statistically significant improvements but also met established criteria for clinically important differences, further supporting the practical value of this intervention model.

In summary, based on both neurophysiological mechanisms and clinical data analysis, the synchronous tDCS combined with lower limb robotic-assisted gait training mode is superior to the sequential mode in improving lower limb motor function, balance, and gait symmetry in stroke patients with hemiplegia. This superior efficacy is likely the result of the combined action of two key neuroplasticity principles: “temporal synchrony” and “task specificity”. By precisely aligning central neuromodulation with peripheral task execution in time, the synchronous intervention optimizes the conditions for inducing neuroplasticity, successfully accelerating the process of lower limb functional recovery post-stroke. This provides a theoretical basis and practical direction for clinical practice, offering solid support for selecting more effective rehabilitation treatment plans and formulating optimal combination strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small (n = 54), and the lack of direct neurophysiological measurements limits the interpretation of proposed mechanisms such as “brain-limb closed-loop effects.” Second, there was an imbalance in total intervention duration: the sequential group (40 min) received 20 min more than the synchronous group (20 min), which may have induced fatigue or reduced attention, and the confounding effect of this “additional time burden” cannot be completely ruled out. Third, all participants were in the early subacute phase (onset < 70 days) with only 4 weeks of follow-up; caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to other stages or long-term outcomes. Fourth, due to differences in intervention timing, participants and therapists in the active groups could not be blinded, which may introduce performance bias despite blinded outcome assessors.

Based on these limitations, future multicenter, large-sample randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate the robustness of our findings. Studies should balance total intervention duration across groups to more cleanly disentangle the “timing effect” from the “duration effect.” Longer follow-up periods are recommended to evaluate long-term efficacy and its impact on quality of life. Additionally, future research should incorporate techniques such as transcranial magnetic stimulation, functional neuroimaging, or electroencephalography to directly monitor changes in cortical excitability and neural network connectivity, thereby further elucidating the underlying neurophysiological mechanisms and exploring differential responses across patients at different stroke stages and with different lesion locations.

Conclusions

The combination of tDCS and lower limb robotic-assisted gait training can effectively improve lower limb function in stroke patients, and the combination mode exhibits a significant timing effect. The synchronous combination mode demonstrates faster onset of efficacy and greater improvement magnitude in enhancing lower limb motor function, balance, and walking capacity, significantly outperforming the sequential mode. This indicates that the precise temporal synchronization of central modulation and peripheral task execution can produce a synergistic effect. From a strategic perspective, the synchronous combination mode, through precise “brain-limb” interaction, maximizes rehabilitation benefits, representing a currently superior, evidence-based “brain-limb collaborative” rehabilitation strategy and providing a clear direction for optimizing lower limb rehabilitation protocols for stroke.
