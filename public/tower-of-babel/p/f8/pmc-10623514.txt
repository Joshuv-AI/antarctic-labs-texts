Characterization of pediatric transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients in a large academic center
Authors: Dorsa Zarrabian, Mirette Hanna
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients are at high risk of transfusion‐related complications. Yet, there is scanty data on the frequency of transfusion reactions, particularity alloimmunization among pediatric transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients. In addition, there is no consensus on the prophylactic antigen matching for prevention of alloimmunization or the extent of antigen matching for alloimmunized thalassemia patients.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study to assess the frequency and specificity of alloimmunization among pediatric transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients receiving ABO, RhD, and K‐matched red blood cell units. In addition, we studied the association between patients' characteristics and alloimmunization. The clinical and transfusion records of transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients followed up at our institution between July 2018 and June 2022 were reviewed.ResultsNinety‐two transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients having mean age of 13.37 years (SD, 5.56) were included in our study. Eight patients (9%) had developed clinically significant alloantibodies; six patients (6%) developed alloantibody against E antigen while two patients (2%) developed more than one alloantibody. Of alloimmunized patients, five patients had received transfusion outside Canada. Patients' sex, age, having a genotype variant, total number, and duration of transfusion received were not associated with the risk of alloimmunization. The transfusion‐recipient's diagnosis of β‐thalassemia, having developed autoantibody, and history of receiving transfusion outside Canada were associated with alloimmunization.ConclusionBlood matching for ABO, RhD, and K antigens resulted in, although not eliminated, lower frequency of alloimmunization than that previously reported among pediatric thalassemia patients. Extending matching to include Rh antigens could further reduce the rate of alloimmunization.Keywords: alloantibodies, alloimmunization, red blood cell transfusion, thalassemia, transfusion‐related complications

INTRODUCTION

Thalassemia syndromes are heterogenous group of disorders that include a wide range of genetic disturbances in α and β genes. Transfusion of red blood cells (RBCs), either prophylactic or therapeutic, is the mainstay of treatment for thalassemia. 1 , 2 Transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients require life‐long regular transfusion for survival while nontransfusion‐dependent patients receive transfusion only during exacerbation of anemia. 3 , 4 Frequent transfusions, however, are not without risk and complications form transfusion constitute major causes of morbidity in transfusion‐dependent patients. 5 , 6

One of the major adverse events of chronic transfusion is the formation of new RBCs alloantibody in response to foreign antigen exposure and subsequent delayed hemolytic transfusion reactions. 4 , 5 A wide range of alloimmunization, ranging from 3% to 37%, was reported among 9256 transfusion‐dependent children and adults thalassemia patients from different ethnic background in a systematic review. 7 The antigenic disparity between donor and recipient population is considered one of the major risk factors for alloimmunization. 8 , 9 , 10 In addition, partial antigen expression resulting from an underlying genetic variant in either blood donor or recipient also predisposes to alloimmunization. 11 , 12 Therefore, prophylactic phenotypic matching between donor RBC unit and recipient has been recommended for prevention of alloimmunization. 13 , 14 , 15 However, there is no consensus on the extent of matching. 16 This has led to inconsistency in transfusion practice for thalassemia patients. Diversity of transfusion practice across centers, in addition to the limited matched blood availability and challenges in blood bank inventory management, especially in small centers, all have contributed to the wide range of alloimmunization reported in the literature.

In Canada, transfusion policies for thalassemia patients are largely set by the blood bank in each institution. Therefore, there is a wide range of variation in transfusion practice for thalassemia patients across institutions. Local practice includes matching for ABO, RhD, and K; ABO, Rh (D, C, E, c, and e), and K; or additional extended matching to include Kidd (Jka and Jkb), Duffy (Fya and Fyb), and Ss. Lack of standardized transfusion protocol for thalassemia patients presents a challenge for the continuity of health care for patients receiving transfusion in multiple institutions. One of the main challenges for developing nation‐wide guidelines is the lack of precise data on the frequency and specificity of alloimmunization among pediatric transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients especially with population changes due to immigration.

We aimed to determine the characteristics of pediatric transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients, the frequency and specificity of alloimmunization and factors associated with alloimmunization at the Hospital for Sick Children, the largest pediatric academic hospital in Canada. Gaining knowledge about the frequency and specificity of alloantibodies developed by transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients, will allow the establishment of cost‐effective evidence‐based recommendations for RBC prophylactic matching for these patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We performed a retrospective chart review of children diagnosed with transfusion‐dependent thalassemia and followed up at the Hospital of Sick Children from July 2018 to June 2022. Transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients were considered those needing regular transfusion for survival. Patients diagnosed with either β‐thalassemia, hemoglobin E/β‐thalassemia, δβ‐thalassemia, or α‐thalassemia were considered in this study. Patients with sickle β‐thalassemia disorders were excluded from the study due to different pathophysiology. Additional exclusion criteria included infants aged less than 1 year old, those followed up for less than a one‐year period at the Hospital of Sick Children, and nontransfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients. The end point for analysis was June 30, 2022, ensuring more than 1 year's follow‐up for each patient. Transfusion history was traced back to the date of first transfusion received at our institution. For each participant, all RBC transfusions received at our institution were considered in the analysis.

Data collected by review of electronic medical records included patients' demographics, age as of June 30, 2022, primary diagnosis, blood group, transfusion history, antibody screen and antibody panel results, RBC phenotype, and RBC genotyping. Transfusion history included the total number of RBC transfusions, first and last date of transfusion received at our institution as of June 30, 2022, as well as history of receiving transfusion outside Canada at any point of their treatment and reported transfusion‐related reactions. The exact number of transfusions received outside Canada was not available in patients' chart. In addition, we attempted to collect data on ethnic background; however, this information was not always available in the patient's electronic file.

All pretransfusion samples were routinely tested for ABO and RhD antigens using the column agglutination technology from Ortho Clinical Diagnostics, USA, and standard tube test from Novaclone, Canada. The antibody screen was performed using two screening cells and positive sera were analyzed using 11‐cell panel (panel A, B, and/or C) from Ortho Clinical Diagnostics, USA. More complex antibody investigation was performed at the reference laboratory of the Canadian Blood Services (CBS). In addition, polyspecific direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and, if needed, monospecific DAT from Ortho Clinical Diagnostics, USA, were performed for all patients having positive antibody screen. For samples positive for monospecific DAT in the presence of anti‐IgG, eluate, performed using Elu KIT™ PLUS, Canada, was tested against panel of cells. Per institutional blood bank protocol, thalassemia patients were prophylactically matched for ABO, RhD, and K. In addition, alloimmunized patients received antigen‐negative units to the corresponding antibody. All RBC units transfused were provided by the CBS and were leukocyte‐reduced by pre‐storage filtration.

For the purpose of this study, an “alloimmunized” patient was defined as a patient who developed, at any point of their treatment, at least one clinically significant alloantibody induced by transfusion. Clinically significant alloantibodies included anti‐D, C, c, E, e, K, Jka, Jkb, Fya, Fyb, M (reactive at 37°C), and S. Patients who developed only warm autoantibodies (WAA), cold autoantibodies (CAA), or only a nonclinically significant alloantibody not requiring matching were not considered as alloimmunized. A “non‐alloimmunized” patient was defined as a patient who never had positive antibody investigation due to any of the clinically significant antibody.

The primary outcome of interest in this study was to determine the frequency of developing a clinically significant alloantibody among transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients receiving ABO, RhD, and K‐matched RBC. To better understand the risk of alloimmunization, we examined its association with the number of transfusions, total duration of treatment received at our institution, and receiving transfusions outside Canada. Secondary outcomes included the association between the presence of clinically significant alloantibodies and the age, sex of the patient (F/M), initial diagnosis (either β‐thalassemia, hemoglobin E/β‐thalassemia, δβ‐thalassemia, α‐thalassemia, or β‐thalassemia with α‐gene(s) deletion), Rh phenotype (R1R1, R1r, R1R2, R0r, R0R0, rr, or R2r), presence of genotype variants (variants in RHD, RHCE, or Duffy system) or deletions, development of other antibodies (WAA, CAA, or nonclinically significant alloantibodies), and transfusion‐related reactions (minor allergic reactions, febrile nonhemolytic transfusion reactions, transfusion‐related acute lung injury [TRALI], and anaphylactic reactions). Patient's related factors were compared between alloimmunized and nonalloimmunized patients using the Student's test for continuous variables and Chi‐square test for categorical variables. Univariate and multivariate models were applied. Age and sex were considered as potential confounders and were included in the models. Subsequent models restricting analysis to patients diagnosed only with β‐thalassemia were used. Restricting the analysis to other subtypes of thalassemia was not possible due to small number of patients diagnosed with a thalassemia other than β‐thalassemia. A p‐value <0.05 was considered significant. All analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.). This study was approved by the Sick Kids Research Ethic Board.

RESULTS

Study population

Over the period from July 2018 to June 2022, a total of 108 transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients received transfusion at the Hospital for Sick Children. Of these, 10 patients were followed for less than 1 year at our institution, and five were younger than 1 year old. These patients were consequently excluded from the analysis. In addition, one patient, originally diagnosed with hereditary persistence of fetal hemoglobin, was subsequently found to have a de novo β‐globin mutation at the age of seven. This patient was excluded from the analysis due to unusual presentation of the disease.

Characteristics of the 92 patients included in the analysis are presented in Table 1. The mean age is 13.37 (SD, 5.56) years. More than half of the transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients included in the study were males (n = 55, 60%). Patient's diagnosis included β‐thalassemia (n = 76, 83%), hemoglobin E/β‐thalassemia (n = 7, 8%), δβ‐thalassemia (n = 2, 2%), α‐thalassemia (n = 5, 5%), and β‐thalassemia with α‐gene(s) deletion (n = 2, 2%). Characteristics of patients by subtype of thalassemia are presented in Table S1. Approximately one‐third of patients had blood group A (33%), 28% were blood group B, 29% were blood group O, and 10% were blood group AB. The majority were RhD positive (n = 88, 96%) while only four patients were RhD negative. Information on ethnic background could not be retrieved for 41% of the study participants and was excluded from the analysis.

Transfusion history

Transfusion history was traced back to August 2000, the date of first transfusion for patients included in this study. The collective RBC exposure for this cohort was 14,021 transfusions for all patients included in this cohort (median and mean were 117.5 and 152.4 transfusions, respectively). One patient followed up at our institution received five transfusions at our institution over a 3‐year period while receiving regular transfusion at a community hospital due to proximity and was kept in the analysis. Information on prophylactic matching outside of our institution was lacking. Data were not much different when considering only patients diagnosed with β‐thalassemia. The collective RBC transfusion was 11,056 transfusions for patients diagnosed with β‐thalassemia (median and mean were 114 and 145.5 transfusions, respectively) (Table 1). The collective red cell transfusion was 1056 transfusions, the median was 116 transfusions, and the mean was 132 transfusions for alloimmunized patients (Table 2). There was no statistically significant difference between alloimmunized and nonalloimmunized patients for the number of transfusions received or duration of transfusion at our institution (p = 0.63 and 0.48 for number of transfusions and the duration of transfusion, respectively) (Table 2).

Alloimmunization and autoimmunization

In total, 12 patients had positive antibody screen (Figure 1). Eight patients (9%) had formed clinically significant alloantibodies while receiving prophylactic ABO, RhD, and K‐matched transfusions (Figure 1). Three of these patients had also formed warm autoantibodies. The frequency of alloimmunization was higher among male patients, the male to female ratio being 3:1. However, there was no statistically significant difference between alloimmunized patients and nonalloimmunized patients for sex (p = 0.35) (Table 2). Contrary, there was a statistically significant association between alloimmunization and the thalassemia subtype (p = 0.01) (Table 2). Fifty percent of alloimmunized patients were diagnosed with β‐thalassemia (n = 4) while two patients had hemoglobin E/β‐thalassemia and two patients had δ/β‐thalassemia (Table 2). None of the α‐thalassemia or β‐thalassemia with α‐gene(s) deletion patients included in our study had developed a clinically significant alloantibody. Alloantibodies were predominantly directed against RH antigens (Tables 2 and 3). Six patients (75%) developed antibodies directed against E antigen while one patient had developed alloantibodies directed against C and Cw antigen. Patients typed negative for corresponding antigens. One patient had developed anti‐Wra, ‐V, ‐Vs, and ‐Sc2 in addition to a warm autoantibody (Table 3).

The rate of alloimmunization was estimated at 0.3% per transfusion. There was no statistically significant difference between alloimmunized patients and nonalloimmunized patients for age, ABO blood group, RhD typing, Rh phenotype, or transfusion‐related reactions (all p > 0.05). Among alloimmunized patients, five patients (62.5%) had received transfusions outside Canada (Table 3). Although the difference did not reach statistical significance, alloimmunized patients had higher history of transfusion outside Canada compared to nonalloimmunized patients (p = 0.07) (Table 2). Interestingly, all alloimmunized patients diagnosed with β‐thalassemia (n = 4) had received transfusion outside Canada (p = 0.007 for history of receiving transfusion outside Canada for β‐thalassemia patients only) (data not shown).

In total, six patients had formed autoantibodies (five patients formed WAA and one patient formed CAA) while three patients formed nonclinically significant alloantibodies. One patient developed anti‐Leb, or ‐Lua, each, while one patient developed multiple alloantibodies against the Knops system including, anti‐Sia, ‐Kn, and ‐McCa (Figure 1). Alloimmunized patients were more likely to form autoantibodies compared to nonalloimmunized patients (p = 0.0002) while there was no statistically significant difference in the rate of formation of nonclinically significant antibodies (p = 0.12) (Table 2). The former association was no longer significant when restricting the analysis to β‐thalassemia patients (p = 0.73) (data not shown). In addition, none of the patients diagnosed with β‐thalassemia had developed nonclinically significant alloantibody (data not shown).

Transfusion‐related reactions

Sixteen transfusion‐related reactions were reported over the duration of the study period (Table 1). The majority were minor allergic reactions (87% of all reported transfusion‐related reactions). Febrile nonhemolytic transfusion reaction and anaphylaxis comprised less than 5% for all the study population and were not reported among alloimmunized patients. Overall, the transfusion‐related reactions were not significantly different between alloimmunized and nonalloimmunized patients (p = 0.88) (Table 2). Restricting the analysis to β‐thalassemia patients did not change the results (data not shown).

Genotyping

Per institutional protocol, all thalassemia patients were phenotyped at the first presentation, unless transfused during the preceding 3 months. Extended genotype was performed for patients who could not be phenotyped due to recent history of transfusion and to identify those who express partial antigens and could be at risk of alloimmunization. Extended genotype was performed at the reference laboratory of the CBS using ID CORE XT and Immucor's RHD BeadChip Test. Phenotyping or extended genotyping results were available for 56 (61%) and 71 (77%) patients, respectively. Genotype results for RHD were available for 48 (52%) patients (Table 1). Among patients with genotyping available on file, two patients had DV type 2 and were homozygous for RHCE*ce(733G). Among these later, one developed anti‐C and Cw. One patient had RHCE*ceAR and one patient had RHD deletion. GATA mutation, that results in a lack of Fyb expression on RBCs but not in other tissues, was detected in five patients, with two patients having coexisting RHCE*ceAR or RHCE*ce(733G). Having a genotype variant was not associated with alloimmunization (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Restricting the analysis to β‐thalassemia patients yields similar results except for the association between alloimmunization and history of having autoantibody that was no longer statistically significant (data not shown). Multivariate analysis did not change any of the association observed (data not shown).

DISCUSSION

This is the first report focusing on pediatric transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients in Canada. A total of 92 transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients were included in the study. Eight patients developed clinically significant alloantibodies while receiving ABO, RhD, and K‐matched transfusion. The rate of alloimmunization for clinically significant antibody was estimated at 0.3% per transfusion. All alloimmunized patients included in our study, except one, developed antibodies against the Rh system. Anti‐E was the most frequent alloantibody detected. A diagnosis of β‐thalassemia, having an autoantibody, and a history of receiving transfusions outside Canada, although this last did not reach statistical significance, were associated with alloimmunization. There was no difference between alloimmunized and nonalloimmunized patients for age, sex, having genetic variants, number of transfusions received, the duration of transfusion, or transfusion‐related adverse reactions.

The frequency of alloimmunization observed in our study was lower than that reported in previously published studies including pediatric transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients. A recent retrospective study conducted in Pakistan reported a frequency of alloimmunization of 26% among 84 child aged between 6 months and 16 years and diagnosed with β‐thalassemia major. 17 The extend of matching was not indicated in the study. 17 An alloimmunization rate of 18% was demonstrated among 217 pediatric patients aged <18 years included in a cross‐sectional study of pediatric and adult patients receiving regular ABO, RhD‐matched transfusion for β‐thalassemia in Egypt. 18 Similarly, Azarkeivan et al. reported a frequency of alloimmunization of 21% among 287 pediatric patients diagnosed predominantly with thalassemia major and receiving ABO‐matched RBCs in a multicentric study conducted among pediatric and adult patients in Iran. 19 The prevalence of alloimmunization was even higher in a retrospective study including 40 pediatric chronically transfused thalassemia patients. 11 The authors reported that 13 patients (32.5%) were alloimmunized with 18 alloantibodies detected despite receiving ABO‐, Rh‐ (D, C, and E), and K‐matched transfusions. 11 Difference in the rate of alloimmunization observed across studies might be attributed, at least in part, to the disparity between the donor and recipient populations included in each study. In keeping with our findings, alloantibodies against the Rh system were the most commonly identified alloantibodies in the three studies. 11 , 17 , 18 Variant Rh alleles did not explain any of the Rh antibodies detected in one study. 11 Unlike our study, anti‐D and anti‐K were detected in all studies. 11 , 17 , 18 , 19 This provides a support to our practice of performing RHD genotyping and matching for K antigen for all thalassemia patients.

One study reported almost the same frequency of alloimmunization as that observed in our study. 20 Six out of 70 pediatric patients (8.5%) were found to have alloantibodies after receiving ABO and RhD‐matched RBC transfusions in India over a 1‐year period. 20 Identified alloantibodies included anti‐D (17%), anti‐K (33%), and anti‐E (50%). Lower frequency of alloimmunization observed in this study compared to other studies might be attributed to the relatively short duration of the study and inclusion of patients diagnosed with thalassemia intermedia or minor with less transfusion requirement (n = 8 and 3 for thalassemia intermedia and minor, respectively).

Various patient‐related factors may influence the risk of alloimmunization. Female gender, history of splenectomy, white ethnicity, frequency, and duration of transfusion have been linked to higher risk of alloimmunization in systematic reviews including pediatrics and adults. 7 , 21 The increased risk of alloimmunization previously reported among females may be attributed to increased antigenic exposure during pregnancy, which is not the case for pediatric population. In our study, although not statistically significant, the frequency of alloimmunization was higher among male patients. Information on ethnic background was missing from the electronic medical records for many patients included in our study, as these patients are long known to their treating physicians. Consequently, we could not include ethnicity in the analysis. Splenectomy for thalassemia patients is not a standard practice in Canada. Only two patients included in our study had history of splenectomy performed before joining our institution. In this study, number of transfusions or duration of transfusion received at our institution were not significantly associated with the risk of alloimmunization. This is in agreement with previous studies reporting no statistically significant association between alloantibody formation and the number of transfused RBC units. 22 , 23

In addition, receiving transfusion at less than 4‐week interval was associated with increased risk of alloimmunization among transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients, having mean age ± SD, 23.9 ± 15.4 years, and treated in eight university hospitals in Thailand. 24 Conversely, receiving first transfusion before the age of 1 year was associated with lower risk of alloimmunization in a report from Center for Disease Control Thalassemia Blood Safety Network. 5 This reduced risk observed among children who began transfusion before 1 year of age might be explained by the immune tolerance developing in infants under 1 year of age. 10 , 12 Unfortunately, we could not assess the association between age at first transfusion and the development of alloantibody, as many of the patients included in our study were immigrants, and age at first transfusion received outside of our institution was not precisely indicated in the patient's electronic medical record. Interestingly, receiving transfusion outside Canada was associated with increased risk of alloimmunization, although association was only significant when restricting analysis to β‐thalassemia patients and did not reach statistical significance considering all patients in the analysis. Five out of the eight patients who developed alloantibodies (62.5%) had received transfusion outside Canada. Difference in matching protocols in other countries might have contributed to the high prevalence of history of receiving transfusion outside Canada among alloimmunized patients. Unfortunately, due to our study design, we could not assess the temporal relationship between detection of alloantibody and receiving transfusion outside Canada. In addition, we did not have information on the level of matching for transfusion received outside Canada.

Contrary to our results, the rate of alloimmunization was not significantly different by diagnosis among 697 patients included in the Thalassemia Clinical Research Network. 25 In our study, the diagnosis of β‐thalassemia was significantly associated with the risk of alloimmunization. Although the pathophysiology is different for each subtype of thalassemia; however, transfusion matching, nonetheless, remains the same. Therefore, all transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients followed up at our institution were considered in the analysis regardless of the subtype of thalassemia. Moreover, restricting the analysis to patients diagnosed with β‐thalassemia only yields almost similar results except for the association between developing alloantibody and autoantibody that was no longer statistically significant. On the other hand, considering the small number of patients diagnosed with thalassemia other than β‐thalassemia in our study, we could not perform subgroup analysis for these patients.

The association between alloimmunization and autoantibody formation has been previously reported in studies including pediatric and adult patients. 9 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 The underlying mechanisms for autoantibody formation after alloimmunization are not well understood. One possible mechanism is that conformational changes of RBC antigenic epitope resulting from binding of alloantibody to the RBC membrane may simulate autoantibody production. On the other hand, the presence of autoantibody in the recipient sample might interfere with pretransfusion crossmatching and complicates further transfusion. 30

Frequency and severity of transfusion‐related reactions in transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients have not been well studied, particularly in the pediatric population. In our study, minor allergic reactions were the most commonly reported reactions followed by alloimmunization (15% and 9% for minor allergic reaction and alloimmunization, respectively). Only one patient was reported to have anaphylaxis while none of the patients had TRALI. Unlike our study, Teawtrakul et al. reported alloimmunization to be the most commonly documented transfusion‐related complication followed by allergic reactions among 421 transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients in Thailand (15.6% and 13.0% for alloimmunization and allergic reactions, respectively). 24 Seven patients included in their study had major transfusion‐related reactions (n = 6 and 1 for anaphylaxis and TRALI, respectively). 24 This difference observed in the frequency of major transfusion‐related reactions might be due to difference in the transfused RBC products. In Canada, all RBC units provided by CBS are leukocyte‐reduced by filtration at the time of collection.

In conclusion, in our study, a relatively low frequency of alloimmunization was detected among pediatric transfusion‐dependent thalassemia patients receiving RBCs‐matched for ABO, RhD, and K. Alloantibodies against the Rh system, particularly anti‐E, were the most frequently detected antibodies. Therefore, additional matching to include the Rh system would probably decrease the frequency of alloimmunization. However, extended phenotype‐matching beyond ABO, K, and Rh system would not have greatly affected the risk and would not be cost‐effective. However, it is important to note that results observed in our study and conclusions withdrawn may not be generalized. National recommendations should be tailored according to the recipient and donor population.
