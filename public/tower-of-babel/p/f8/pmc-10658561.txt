Microbiome analysis of bile from apparently healthy cats and cats with suspected hepatobiliary disease
Authors: Tanner S. Slead, Benjamin J. Callahan, Megan E. Schreeg, Gabriela S. Seiler, Devorah M. Stowe, Maria Andrea Azcarate‐Peril, Megan E. Jacob, Jody L. Gookin
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBacterial infection of bile is a common cause of hepatobiliary disease in cats. Whether bile harbors a core microbiota in health or in cases of suspected hepatobiliary disease in cats is unknown.ObjectivesEstablish if gallbladder bile in apparently healthy cats harbors a core microbiota composed of bacterial taxa common to many individuals. Compare results of bile cytology, bile culture, and 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing in apparently healthy cats and cats with suspected hepatobiliary disease.AnimalsForty‐three client‐owned cats with suspected hepatobiliary disease and 17 control cats.MethodsBile was collected by ultrasound guided cholecystocentesis (cats with suspected hepatobiliary disease) or laparotomy after euthanasia (controls). Bile samples underwent cytologic examination, aerobic and anaerobic culture, and DNA was extracted for 16S rRNA gene amplification and sequencing.ResultsMicrobiome sequencing did not identify a core microbiota in control cats or cats having bile sampled because of clinical suspicion for hepatobiliary disease. Microbiome profiles from control cats were indistinguishable from profiles obtained from sampling instruments and reagents that were not exposed to bile (technical controls). Bacterial taxa that could not be explained by contamination or off‐target amplification were identified only in samples from cats with bactibilia and positive bile culture results for Escherichia coli. In several E. coli positive samples, microbiome sequencing also identified a small number of potentially co‐infecting bacterial genera not identified by culture.Conclusions and Clinical ImportanceCat bile does not harbor a core microbiota. Uncultured bacteria may contribute to pathogenesis of hepatobiliary disease in cats with bile E. coli infection.

INTRODUCTION

Neutrophilic cholangitis is the most common inflammatory hepatobiliary disease in cats and generally is attributed to ascending infection or translocation by enteric bacteria (ie, bacterial cholangitis). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Definitive diagnosis of neutrophilic cholangitis requires collection of a liver biopsy sample. Given the prevalence of neutrophilic cholangitis and the invasiveness and cost of liver biopsy, clinicians increasingly perform ultrasound‐guided cholecystocentesis to obtain bile for cytology and bacterial culture before recommending biopsy. Positive results of bile cytology or culture then are used as a surrogate for presumptive diagnosis and treatment of neutrophilic cholangitis. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7

In humans, standard culture of bile in cases of acute cholecystitis fails to detect >60% of the microbes identified by sequencing and there is conflicting evidence regarding existence of a core bile microbiome (ie, a set of microbial taxa found in a substantial fraction of the population in the absence of clinical infection). 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 In veterinary practice, 67%‐79% of cats undergoing cholecystocentesis have negative results on bile culture, despite compelling clinicopathologic, ultrasonographic, and sometimes even bile cytologic evidence for hepatobiliary disease or inflammation. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 It is unknown in these cases if bacteria are simply not playing a role in the underlying hepatobiliary disease, culturable bacteria are missed because of factors such as poor sensitivity or exposure to antibiotics, or the disease process is mediated by bacteria that cannot be cultured. It is also unknown if cat bile harbors a core microbiome that may influence the pathogenesis of biliary disease or confound interpretation of the results obtained when bile culture is performed.

Accordingly, our primary objectives were to (a) establish if apparently healthy cats harbor a core microbiota in gallbladder bile and (b) evaluate the agreement among results of bile cytology, bile culture, and 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing in apparently healthy cats and cats with suspected hepatobiliary disease.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals and samples

Cats with suspected hepatobiliary disease (clinical cats)

Criteria for inclusion in the study were any cat for which bile sampling was performed at the discretion of the attending veterinarian, and with owner consent, as part of a diagnostic plan for suspected hepatobiliary disease. Patient bile samples were collected by means of percutaneous ultrasound‐guided cholecystocentesis (PUC) from cats presented to the North Carolina State University Veterinary Hospital over the period from December 1, 2015 to January 31, 2020. Percutaneous ultrasound‐guided cholecystocentesis was performed after shaving and sterile preparation of the abdominal skin with betadine scrub. After sterile preparation, a 22‐gauge needle was attached to sterile collection tubing and gallbladder bile was removed. Bile was prepared for cytological examination by smearing a single drop of uncentrifuged bile onto each of 2 glass microscope slides. Slides were air‐dried, fixed, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and examined by an American College of Veterinary Clinical Pathology (ACVP) boarded specialist. Approximately 4 mL of each bile sample was cultured under aerobic and anaerobic conditions by the North Carolina State Veterinary Hospital Microbiology Laboratory using methods previously described. 13 Bile in excess to that utilized for cytology and culture was kept refrigerated for <48 hours before being aseptically aliquoted into sterile, 2 mL, RNAse/DNAse‐free microcentrifuge tubes and stored frozen at −80°C.

Control cats

Control bile samples were collected from young adult, feral cats immediately after humane euthanasia by animal control for purposes unrelated to the study. Samples subsequently were excluded if moderate to severe histopathologic evidence of underlying hepatobiliary, pancreatic, or duodenal disease (ie, inflammation, infection, or neoplasia) was identified. A ventral midline incision was made immediately after confirmation of death and bile obtained by aseptic needle aspiration of the gallbladder under direct visualization. As with clinical cats, each bile sample was processed for cytological examination by an ACVCP‐boarded specialist and aerobic and anaerobic culture was performed by the North Carolina State Veterinary Hospital Microbiology Laboratory. Bile samples were transferred on ice to the laboratory where they were aseptically aliquoted into 2 mL RNAse/DNAse‐free microcentrifuge tubes, frozen, and stored at −80°C within 30 minutes of collection.

All control cats underwent necropsy examination for collection of tissue samples from the liver (peripheral lobe and porta hepatis), gallbladder, pancreas (left limb), major duodenal papilla (including right pancreatic limb), duodenum, jejunum, and ileocecocolic junction. Tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, trimmed, and embedded in paraffin before sectioning at 7 μm. Sections were stained using Mayer‐Harris hematoxylin and alcoholic eosin before examination by a 3rd year anatomic pathology resident and supervising ACVP board‐certified pathologist.

Technical control samples

Technical control samples were obtained from a subset of clinical and control cats. For clinical cats, technical controls were obtained in the small animal ultrasonography suite using the same sample collection materials and process as for routine cholecystocentesis, with the needle touching but not penetrating the body wall. For control cats, technical controls were obtained using the same laparotomy approach with the needle touching, but not penetrating, the wall of the gallbladder. In both clinical and control cats, the needle and syringe were used to aspirate and re‐inject sterile saline into the same lot of 2 mL RNAse/DNAse‐free microcentrifuge tubes as used for storage of study samples.

Collection of medical record data

Medical records for clinical patients were retrospectively reviewed to extract data including signalment, medical history, treatment history (eg, antibiotics), clinical signs (eg, fever), hematological (eg, inflammatory leukogram, anemia, thrombocytopenia) and serum biochemical abnormalities (eg, liver enzyme activities, and total bilirubin, albumin, globulin, BUN, creatinine, glucose, and cholesterol concentrations), results of bile cytology and aerobic and anaerobic culture, and ultrasonographic findings. Patterns of increased liver enzyme activities were determined by calculation using the formula: (R = [ALT/upper limit of normal]/[ALP/upper limit of normal]), with R > 5 indicating hepatocellular injury, R < 2 indicating cholestatic injury, and R 2‐5 indicated a mixed pattern where ALT = alanine aminotransferase and ALP = alkaline phosphatase. 18 For control cats, signalment and complete history were collected but inconsistently available.

Ultrasonographic images of the abdomen obtained from each clinical patient in the study were reviewed by a single ACVR‐boarded radiologist for standardization of findings. Ultrasonographic abnormalities were categorized by their anatomic location (eg, liver, gallbladder, pancreas, gastrointestinal tract) and specific echogenicity features of disease within each organ.

DNA isolation, 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, and analysis

DNA isolation

Bile and technical control samples were thawed and mixed by vortexing. Samples (600 μL) were transferred to 2 mL tubes containing 200 mg of 106/500 μm glass beads (Sigma, St. Louis, Missouri). Samples were centrifuged at maximum speed for 5 minutes, and 500 μL of supernatant was removed and discarded without disrupting the pellet. Qiagen InhibitEX buffer (1.0 mL, Valencia, California) was added. The suspension then was agitated for 40 minutes using a Digital Vortex Mixer at 3000 rpm. After a 3‐minute centrifugation at maximum speed, 600 μL of supernatant was aspirated and transferred to a new tube containing 0.6 mL Qiagen AL buffer supplemented with 20 μL of proteinase K. The suspension was incubated at 50°C overnight. After a brief centrifugation, supernatant was aspirated and transferred to a new tube containing 0.6 mL of ethanol. The DNA was purified using a standard on‐column purification method with Qiagen buffers AW1 and AW2 as washing agents and eluted in 30 μL elution buffer (TRIS pH 8). 19 , 20 , 21 Three extraction reagent control tubes treated with only PCR‐grade water were included in all steps.

16S rRNA amplicon sequencing

Total DNA (12.5 ng) was amplified using universal primers targeting the V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene as previously described. 22 Briefly, amplicons were generated with primers F515/R806. 19 , 20 Primers contained overhang adapters appended to the 5′ end of each primer for compatibility with the Illumina sequencing platform. The master mixes contained 12.5 ng of total DNA, 0.5 μM of each primer and 2× KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, Massachusetts). Each 16S rRNA amplicon was purified using the AMPure XP reagent (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, Indiana). In the next step, each sample was amplified using a limited cycle PCR program, adding Illumina sequencing adapters and dual‐index barcodes (index 1(i7) and index 2(i5); Illumina, San Diego, California) to the amplicon target. The final libraries were again purified using the AMPure XP reagent (Beckman Coulter), quantified and normalized before pooling. The DNA library pool then was denatured using NaOH, diluted with hybridization buffer and heat denatured before loading on the MiSeq reagent cartridge (Illumina) and on the MiSeq instrument (Illumina). Automated cluster generation and paired‐end sequencing with dual reads were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Bioinformatics analysis

Sequencing output from the Illumina MiSeq PE250 platform was converted to fastq format and demultiplexed using Illumina Bcl2Fastq 2.18.0.12. The resulting paired‐end reads were processed using QIIME 2 2018.11. 20 Index and linker primer sequences were trimmed using the QIIME 2 invocation of Cutadapt. The resulting paired‐end reads were processed with DADA2 through QIIME2 including merging paired ends, quality filtering, error correction, and chimera detection. 21 Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) from DADA2 were assigned taxonomic identifiers against the Silva v138.1 reference database using the naïve Bayesian classifier method as implemented in the DADA2 R package. 23 , 24 In the analysis of the microbiome sequencing data, only samples with at least 500 reads and ASVs present in at least 5 samples or with total study‐wide abundance of at least 1000 reads were considered.

Statistical analysis

The presence of a core microbiota not explained by contamination was assessed using the decontam prevalence method, as implemented in the decontam R package. 25 All negative control types (technical control samples, reagent controls, and negative library controls) were pooled for decontam analysis. Multiple hypothesis testing was controlled using the Benjamini‐Hochberg method. 26 Alpha‐diversity of microbiome profiles was measured by the Shannon diversity index, and beta‐diversity between microbiome profiles was measured by the Bray‐curtis community dissimilarity metric. 27 , 28 Data visualization was performed using phyloseq and ggplot2. 29 , 30 All analyses were performed in the R statistical computing environment and are available as reproducible scripts at the Github repository associated with this manuscript: https://github.com/benjjneb/FelineBileManuscript.

RESULTS

Control cats

A total of 34 cats were screened for inclusion as controls in the study. The final control group contained 17 domestic cats (5 intact males and 12 intact females). The remaining 17 cats were excluded because of abnormal results of bile cytology, positive results of bile culture, evidence of underlying hepatobiliary, pancreatic, or duodenal disease on histopathological analysis or some combination of these (Figure 1). Only 2 control cats had positive results on bile culture. Both cats were excluded based on the presence of severe diffuse vacuolar hepatopathy in 1, and moderate lymphoplasmacytic inflammation of the major duodenal papilla in the other. For all control cats, medical and medication history was unknown. Ages were unavailable for cats in the control group, but 2 females and 1 male were estimated to be <8 months of age based on dentition at euthanasia.

Disposition of control cats in the study. Note that cats may have been excluded based on more than one criteria.

Cats with suspected hepatobiliary disease (clinical cats)

A total of 43 cats had bile sampled as part of a diagnostic investigation for suspected hepatobiliary disease. The group included 20 neutered males and 23 spayed females. The average and median ages were 9.7 and 10.0 years, respectively, with a range of 1.0 to 18.0 years. Breeds included 31 (72%) domestic, 3 Siamese, 2 Maine Coon, and 1 each of Burmese, Australian Mist, Himalayan, Norwegian Forest Cat, Ocicat, Ragamuffin, and Snowshoe.

Clinical findings

The most common clinical signs reported for cats with suspected hepatobiliary disease included hyporexia or anorexia in 27 (62%) cats, lethargy in 21 (48%) cats, and vomiting in 21 (48%) cats. Ten (23%) cats had an increased rectal temperature (range, 103‐104.3°F). Three (6%) cats were described as icteric. Body condition scores were graded out of 9 and ranged from 1.5 to 9 with a median of 4/9.

Clinicopathologic findings

A CBC was performed for 41/43 cats. Time between performance of the CBC and bile sampling ranged from 3 days before to 2 days after (average, same day). The most common hematologic abnormalities included anemia (n = 23; 56%), leukocytosis (n = 16; 39%), segmented neutrophilia (n = 29; 70%), and presence of band neutrophils (n = 24; 55%; Table 1).

Ultrasonographic findings

Ultrasonographic imaging of the entire abdomen was performed in 41/43 cats. One cat underwent focal biliary and pancreatic imaging. For the remaining cat, abdominal ultrasound examination was performed elsewhere, and images were not available.

Ultrasonographic abnormalities associated with the biliary tract were observed in 40/42 (95%) cats. The most common abnormality was gallbladder sludge (n = 38), followed by gallbladder wall hyperechogenicity (n = 13), common bile duct dilatation (n = 13), and gallbladder wall thickening (n = 11). Hepatic abnormalities were observed in 16/41 (39%) cats with hyperechoic parenchyma occurring most commonly (n = 12). Abnormalities associated with both the biliary tract and the liver were observed in 15/41 (36%) cats. All cats underwent PUC for collection of a bile sample. No adverse events associated with PUC for any cat.

Ultrasonographic abnormalities associated with the pancreas were observed in 22/42 (52%) cats. Ultrasonographic abnormalities associated with the intestinal tract were observed in 18/41 (43%) cats. Ten of the cats with intestinal abnormalities also had intestinal lymphadenopathy. A summary of ultrasound findings is presented in Table 3.

Bile cytology and culture

Cytologic examination was performed on bile collected from 42/43 (97%) cats with suspected hepatobiliary disease and 17/17 (100%) control cats. Nine (21%) cats with suspected hepatobiliary disease had bacteria observed in bile, among which 8 also had inflammatory cells present. Inflammatory cells alone were observed in the bile of 1 additional culture‐negative cat. Neither bacteria nor inflammatory cells were observed in bile from any control cats.

Aerobic and anaerobic bacterial culture was performed on bile samples collected from 41/43 (95%) cats with suspected hepatobiliary disease and 17/17 (100%) control cats. Positive culture results were obtained for 11/41 (27%) bile samples collected from cats with suspected hepatobiliary disease, among which 9 samples also had bacteria or inflammatory cells identified on cytology. Culture results included Escherichia coli alone (5 cats), E. coli with Clostridium spp. (1 cat), E. coli with Enterococcus spp. (1 cat), E. coli with Streptococcus spp. (1 cat), Klebsiella spp. (1 cat), Staphylococcus epidermis (1 cat), and Peptostreptococcus (1 cat; Table 4). Twelve cats with suspected hepatobiliary disease were receiving antibiotics at the time of or within 1 month of bile sample collection. Four of these cats were among those that had abnormal results of bile cytology, culture or both (Table S1). All 17 control cats had negative results for aerobic and anaerobic culture of bile. No control cats were reported to be receiving antibiotics at the time of euthanasia, but prior history of antibiotic administration was unknown.

Bile 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing

The 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing was performed on bile samples from 43 cats with suspected hepatobiliary disease, bile samples from 17 control cats, and 17 negative controls that did not contain bile (13 technical control samples, 3 reagent controls, and 1 negative library control). A total of 40/43 (93%) cats with suspected hepatobiliary disease, 17/17 (100%) control cats and 17/17 (100%) negative control samples yielded sequencing libraries of sufficient quality for further analysis (median per sample, 83,300 reads; minimum, 6,200 reads). This subset of samples included 8 samples from cats with positive culture results. To obtain the final table of ASVs (unique amplicon sequences after error‐correction), ASVs present in <5 samples and <100 reads were removed. This filtering step decreased the number of ASVs from 11,069 to 749 whereas retaining 91.7% of all reads.

A diverse set of microbial ASVs was obtained from all sample types: cats with suspected hepatobiliary disease, control cats, and negative control samples. We used the decontam prevalence method to test for ASVs that were unlikely to be explained as contaminants. 25 Briefly, the decontam prevalence method tested whether ASVs occurred in a significantly higher fraction of samples from cats than in negative control samples. Using a very permissive false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 0.67, we identified only 10 candidate non‐contaminant ASVs. 26 We BLAST‐ed these sequences against the National Center for Biotechnology Information nucleotide (nt) database: 8/10 were identified as off‐target amplification of cat genome or cat mitochondria. The remaining 2 were assigned as a Ruminococcus and a Tyzarella and considered of questionable true relevance because of the permissive FDR threshold.

Ordination of microbiome profiles based on the Bray‐curtis community dissimilarity metric identified 2 distinct clusters: One cluster containing all samples from control cats and negative controls and most samples from cats with suspected biliary disease, and a second cluster of 6 bile samples from cats with suspected biliary disease (Figure 2). Analysis of microbiome diversity in each sample using the Shannon diversity index indicated that the same set of 6 samples was distinguished from the rest by their low diversity (Figure 3). The composition of 5 of these 6 samples was dominated by ASVs consistent with E. coli accompanied by 0‐3 species of other taxa (Figure 4). The composition of the remaining sample was dominated by Bacillus and Rothia, but E. coli was detected at low (<0.1%) relative abundance. This same set of 6 samples was all culture‐positive for E. coli and had bactibilia identified by microscopy (Table 4). In summary, there was perfect concordance among microbiome profiles, E. coli culture, and bactibilia in identifying these 6 samples as different from the rest.

The concordance between microbiome sequencing and positive culture results for non‐E. coli taxa (ie, Clostridium, Enterococcus, Streptococcus and Staphylococcus) was overall unconvincing. With the exception of Clostridium, ASVs corresponding to non‐E. coli taxa were detected in the 16S rRNA sequencing data but were not present in higher than background proportions in the corresponding culture‐positive samples. No Peptostreptococcus spp. ASVs were detected in the study‐wide 16S rRNA gene sequencing reads. Because each of these non‐E. coli taxa was identified in only a single positive culture sample, generalizability of these taxon‐specific comparisons is low.

DISCUSSION

In our study, 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing was performed on bile collected from 60 cats and 17 negative control samples. The results do not support the existence of a core bile microbiome in apparently healthy cats or in cats that had bile sampled because of compelling clinical suspicion for hepatobiliary disease. Critical to correct interpretation of these results was inclusion of technical (simulated collection) and reagent‐only negative controls in the 16S rRNA gene sequencing. In biological samples having a low bacterial biomass, reagent and laboratory DNA contamination can outcompete low copy number sample DNA, swamping the amplification process, and resulting in spurious sequencing results. 31 , 32 This was evidenced by the diverse set of bacterial ASVs that was measured by sequencing across almost all samples in the study including those that did not contain bile. The sequenced negative controls allowed us to recognize that no difference existed between microbiome profiles in negative controls and most bile samples. This contrast also helped us recognize the markedly different microbiome profile obtained by 16S rRNA gene sequencing from bile samples that cultured positive for E. coli.

In cats with positive bile culture results for E. coli there was a strong concordance with higher abundance of E. coli in the sequence data and a low diversity of additional taxa that were present in higher proportions than would be expected from the contaminant background. Although some of these taxa are familiar pathogens in cat bile (eg, C. perfringens), others are not commonly associated with bile (Schaalia [Actinomyces] canis) or are generally unfamiliar (Rothia nasimurium). 16 How these taxa might contribute, directly or indirectly, to pathogenesis of E. coli biliary infection in cats is unknown. It might be worthwhile therefore to consider a role for bacterial co‐infection in cats with positive bile culture results for E. coli, particularly in those that fail to respond or relapse after antibiotic treatment.

Except for a single bile sample with positive culture and sequencing results for Clostridium sp., no concordance was found between positive bile culture results for non‐E. coli bacteria (as with Enterococcus sp., Streptococcus sp., Staphylococcus epidermis, and Peptostreptococcus sp.) and results of 16S rRNA gene sequencing performed on the same sample. Samples culturing positive for these bacteria did not contain ASV corresponding to these bacterial isolates or those ASVs were not higher than predicted by background contamination. Bile samples culturing positive for Staphylococcus epidermis and Peptostreptococcus sp. were unique among culture positive samples in having no bacteria or inflammatory cells observed on bile cytologic examination. These isolates likely represent skin contamination acquired during bile sample collection. The other non‐E. coli culture positive isolates also may represent non‐bile skin contaminants, but inadequate liberation of DNA during extraction (ie, gram positive bacteria with resilient cell walls), recent use of antibiotics, or failure to collect and sequence all bile from the gallbladder could have resulted in inaccurate conclusions that these bacteria were not present.

An important limitation of our study is that only a small subset of cats ultimately had positive bile culture results which limits the sample size of this subpopulation. Unique microbiome findings were observed in bile from cats culturing positive for E. coli, but other cultured bacteria (ie, Gram positive) only were observed in bile from single cats. Additional studies focusing on a larger number of culture positive cats are warranted to better characterize and understand the bacterial populations present in these cats and their possible role in disease pathogenesis. Moreover, the clinical patients included in our study did not have liver biopsies performed and therefore the presence or absence of pathology cannot be correlated with bile culture or 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing results. A subset of cats in the study had received or were receiving antibiotics and information about prior antibiotic use in control cats was unknown. These and other limitations of retrospective data collection may have impacted our findings.

Aside from the low‐diversity consortium of uncultured ASV identified concurrently in cats with positive bile culture results for E. coli, 16S rRNA gene sequencing did not identify any bile samples with high abundance ASV that were missed by culture (ie, false‐negative culture results) or unique ASV that would not be amenable to routine culture (ie, fastidious bacteria with atypical culture requirements or unculturable). These findings suggest, at least in this set of cats and bile samples, that standard aerobic and anaerobic culture was sensitive for detection of bacterial infection. Particularly in culture negative bile samples that lack evidence of bacteria or inflammation on bile cytology, additional diagnostic testing for non‐bacterial causes of hepatobiliary disease should be prioritized. Also, based on this sample population, there would be minimal added value to performing 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing to diagnose bacterial bile infection in cats and such results would require careful attention to the impact of contamination on interpretation of the sequence data obtained. That said, in cats with bile E. coli infection and failing to respond to treatment, 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing may have potential value in identifying the full range of co‐infecting bacteria present.
