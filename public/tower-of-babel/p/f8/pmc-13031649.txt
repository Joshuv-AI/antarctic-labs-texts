A gated-attention multi-prior injection diffusion model for Huashan rock art image restoration
Authors: Tong Zhao, Luohua Huang, Xiaopeng Qi, Penglu Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper addresses the problems of fading, loss, and texture degradation in the Zuojiang Huashan rock art caused by long term weathering and human disturbances, and constructs a Huashan rock art dataset for image restoration while proposing a prior guided restoration framework based on Stable Diffusion. The proposed method introduces a dual branch prior extraction module to separately model structural contours and texture material information during the diffusion denoising process, and employs a gate attention fusion module to achieve adaptive prior injection and weighted fusion, thereby improving structural continuity and material consistency in missing regions. At the data level, a total of 528 candidate images are collected, among which 177 valid samples are retained after unified screening and cleaning, and training and evaluation pairs are constructed using a unified 512512 preprocessing scheme together with a mask generation strategy. The experiments are evaluated using MSE, MAE, PSNR, and SSIM, and the results show that the proposed method achieves an MSE of 0.021, an MAE of 0.093, a PSNR of 26.73, and an SSIM of 0.892 on the test set, outperforming multiple representative baseline methods in terms of overall performance. Ablation studies further verify the effectiveness of the dual branch prior extraction and gate attention fusion modules, with the complete model consistently improving all four metrics compared with the Stable Diffusion baseline. In addition, paired significance tests and focused region visualizations demonstrate that the performance gains are statistically meaningful and reveal that the model primarily focuses on missing boundaries, key symbolic contours, and texture transition regions, providing support for interpretable restoration in cultural heritage scenarios.Keywords: Huashan rock art, Image restoration, Diffusion models, Prior extraction, Gated attention fusionSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

The Zuojiang Huashan rock art is a large scale group of ochre red rock paintings created more than two thousand years ago by the Luoyue ancestors of the Zhuang people along the Zuojiang River basin on steep cliff faces. Centered on the iconic squatting human figures, these paintings depict collective activities such as ritual ceremonies and together with cliffs, rivers, and terraces form a unique and visually striking cultural landscape. As one of the largest, most densely distributed, and most abundant rock art complexes in southern China and even Southeast Asia, the Huashan rock art is not only a precious testament to the history and culture of the Zhuang ethnic group, but also an important representative of world rock art heritage. With its cultural value increasingly recognized and re evaluated, the Huashan rock art has been included in the tentative list of World Cultural Heritage in China and holds outstanding significance for protection and research1.

However, the preservation status of the Huashan rock art faces persistent challenges. The pigments are mainly derived from iron containing minerals such as hematite, which are highly susceptible to long term exposure to natural environments. Weathering erosion, water infiltration and dissolution, biological colonization, and human contact jointly contribute to progressive degradation, leading to fading colors, blurred boundaries, partial peeling, and material loss. From the perspective of image restoration, these real-world deterioration processes translate into several intuitive visual difficulties: (1) uneven and low-contrast pigmentation makes the global color distribution unstable and reduces the separability between motifs and background; (2) boundary diffusion and stroke breakage weaken contour cues, causing discontinuous silhouettes and ambiguous symbol shapes; (3) irregular peeling and material loss produce non-uniform missing patterns with fuzzy edges, where the surrounding context is also corrupted; and (4) weathered surfaces introduce complex, spatially varying textures (e.g., granular noise, cracks, and blotches) that can be easily mistaken for meaningful details, increasing the risk of spurious texture generation. For such non renewable cultural heritage, traditional manual restoration is often constrained by long cycles, high costs, strong subjectivity, and limited reproducibility, making it difficult to achieve systematic and large scale protection under conditions of widespread and continuous degradation. Therefore, there is an urgent need to adopt digital and intelligent techniques to provide more efficient, consistent, and traceable technical support for the scientific conservation of Huashan rock art2.

In response to the practical demands of cultural heritage protection, artificial intelligence driven image restoration offers a new pathway to implement the principle of minimal intervention. By employing computational models to infer structures and complete textures in missing regions, restoration processes can reduce subjective misjudgment while improving reproducibility and enabling quantitative evaluation. At the same time, restoration results can serve as an integral component of high precision digital archives, providing stable carriers for long term preservation, academic research, and public dissemination. Furthermore, the digital outcomes of Huashan rock art can support applications such as digital exhibitions, immersive experiences, and cultural creative derivatives, activating contemporary value while safeguarding cultural roots and promoting coordinated development of cultural identity and high quality local cultural tourism. From this perspective, research on Huashan rock art restoration is not only a specific technical practice, but also a frontier exploration that fosters dialogue between ancient civilization and future technology.

Based on the above background and demands, this paper focuses on image restoration under realistic degradation conditions of Huashan rock art, and the main contributions are summarized as follows.

(1) An image dataset tailored to the restoration requirements of Huashan rock art is constructed, providing a standardized data foundation for training and evaluation.

(2) A rock art restoration method based on stable diffusion is proposed, which introduces dual branch prior extraction and gated attention fusion mechanisms to continuously inject structural and texture priors during the diffusion denoising process, thereby enhancing artistic consistency and material realism.

(3) The effectiveness of the proposed method is systematically validated through quantitative comparisons, ablation studies, and visual analyses, and interpretability evidence is provided from perspectives such as model attention regions to support its potential application in digital cultural heritage conservation scenarios.

Related work

Applications of artificial intelligence in cultural heritage and rock art studies

In recent years, the application of artificial intelligence methods in cultural heritage and rock art research has gradually expanded from assisted documentation and classification to automated recognition and structural understanding. Jalandoni et al. introduced machine learning techniques into rock art studies, enabling automatic identification of painted rock art images through feature learning and providing a new technical pathway for reducing reliance on manual annotation3. Horn et al. further combined artificial intelligence with three-dimensional digital documentation, systematically discussing the technical potential of automated identification, classification, and cultural information extraction from rock art images, as well as their implications for traditional archaeological research paradigms4. In more task-specific visual applications, Huo et al. achieved fine-grained segmentation of rock art symbols based on an improved YOLOv7-Seg model, demonstrating the feasibility of deep learning models in extracting complex rock art textures and symbolic structures5.

Beyond rock art research, artificial intelligence has also achieved substantial progress in the broader field of cultural heritage preservation. Hatır et al. employed deep learning methods to detect and spatially map stone deterioration in open-air heritage sites, providing technical support for the objective quantification of heritage damage6. Karimi et al. proposed a deep learning-based automatic defect detection system for decorative tile materials on historical building surfaces, enabling efficient and scalable condition assessment of architectural heritage7. In the context of structural pathology analysis, Katsigiannis et al. enhanced the robustness of masonry façade crack detection under small-sample conditions through transfer learning strategies8. In addition, Tan et al. presented a systematic review summarizing the application patterns of artificial intelligence across multiple stages of architectural heritage investigation, assessment, restoration, and management, highlighting the supporting role of intelligent methods throughout the entire lifecycle of cultural heritage9.

With the development of generative models and deep representation learning, artificial intelligence has gradually progressed from “detection and analysis” toward “restoration and reconstruction” tasks. Xu and Geng proposed a mural color restoration method based on invertible neural networks, achieving a balanced preservation of structure and texture10. Hu et al. introduced GuidePaint, which employs image-guided diffusion models for non-destructive restoration of ancient murals, offering a new perspective on the application of generative models in cultural heritage restoration11. In the domain of damage modeling and virtual restoration, Wu et al. realized mural damage detection through multi-scale boundary and region feature fusion12, Ge et al. constructed a virtual restoration network integrating global and local features13, and Hu et al. predicted mural damaged regions by leveraging long-range semantics and multilayer perceptrons14. Despite continuous technical improvements in restoration quality, most existing approaches primarily focus on visual consistency and structural completeness, with limited adaptation to rock art as a unique heritage form that heavily relies on cultural symbolism, historical style, and artistic expression. Moreover, current restoration models often lack explicit modeling of the complex degradation mechanisms arising from the combined effects of natural weathering and human activities. In addition, many restoration pipelines implicitly treat structure and texture as a coupled objective, which can be problematic for rock art where symbol contours and stroke topology must be preserved conservatively while pigment texture and weathered material patterns require flexible completion. Without an explicit mechanism to decouple and balance these two aspects, models may either over-smooth fine-grained texture (hurting material realism) or introduce visually plausible but stylistically inconsistent details (risking semantic distortion of cultural symbols). Furthermore, the influence of priors or guidance is often applied in a largely static manner, lacking step-aware control over when to prioritize structural stabilization versus texture refinement during generation, which limits controllability and authenticity under large missing regions. In real-world rock art scenarios, how to balance algorithmic automation with artistic authenticity remains a critical challenge for intelligent restoration methods.

Advances in image restoration methods

Image restoration methods have evolved from traditional model-driven approaches to deep learning-dominated paradigms. Su et al. provided a comprehensive survey of deep learning techniques for denoising, deblurring, super-resolution, and image restoration, highlighting the clear advantages of data-driven models over traditional methods in modeling complex degradation processes15. Tian et al. further reviewed the development trajectory of deep learning-based denoising methods, emphasizing the effectiveness of convolutional neural networks in multi-noise modeling and feature representation16. Focusing on image incompletion problems, Xiang et al. conducted a thorough review of deep learning-based image inpainting approaches, identifying contextual modeling and structural consistency as key factors for high-quality restoration17. Along this line, diffusion-based inpainting has recently become an active direction due to its strong generative prior and controllable denoising dynamics. Corneanu et al. proposed LatentPaint, performing image inpainting directly in latent space with diffusion models to improve efficiency while maintaining semantic coherence18. Beyond generation-oriented restoration, Wang et al. introduced InpDiffusion, which formulates inpainting localization as a conditional diffusion modeling problem, illustrating the versatility of conditional diffusion mechanisms in incompletion-related settings19. Zhong et al. further studied text-driven inpainting coherence via blended diffusion, showing that guidance and blending strategies are crucial for maintaining global consistency under semantic conditioning20.

In terms of specific model design, Zhang et al. proposed DnCNN based on residual learning, which significantly improved image denoising performance through an end-to-end framework21. They subsequently introduced FFDNet, which enhanced adaptability to different noise levels while maintaining strong denoising capability22. To integrate traditional regularization principles with the advantages of deep models, Kong et al. proposed the Deep RED Unfolding Network, which unfolds the optimization process into a trainable network structure, achieving stable and interpretable image restoration23. In addition, the trainable nonlinear reaction-diffusion model proposed by Chen and Pock provided a flexible framework for fast and effective image recovery24, while the Plug-and-Play ADMM method introduced by Chan et al. enhanced model generality across various restoration tasks by incorporating replaceable denoising priors25. These developments collectively suggest a shift from purely feed-forward restoration toward mechanisms that explicitly control the restoration trajectory, a perspective that resonates with diffusion-based denoising processes and motivates conditional or guided designs for incompletion recovery.

With the advancement of prior modeling capabilities, increasing attention has been paid to the role of deep priors and generative models in image restoration. Dong et al. proposed a denoising-prior-driven deep neural network that integrates explicit priors into the learning process to improve restoration quality26. In the generative modeling paradigm, Özdenizci et al. employed denoising diffusion models for image restoration under adverse weather conditions, demonstrating the potential of diffusion models in modeling complex degradation processes27. Yue et al. further improved the efficiency and stability of diffusion-based image restoration through a residual shifting mechanism28. Despite significant progress achieved by these methods in natural image restoration tasks, most existing studies primarily focus on pixel-level consistency and statistically driven visual quality, with limited consideration of the cultural semantics and symbolic expressions embedded in artistic images. Moreover, current models often assume statistically repeatable degradation processes, making them difficult to directly adapt to complex degradation patterns such as those observed in Huashan rock art, which result from the combined effects of natural weathering, human interference, and historical material properties. In addition, many diffusion-based restoration and inpainting studies emphasize performance gains but provide comparatively less mechanism-level discussion on where conditions or priors are injected, how different types of cues (structure versus texture/material) are disentangled, and why a particular fusion or guidance strategy is expected to suppress hallucinations while preserving authenticity. This limitation becomes more critical in cultural heritage scenarios, where plausible-but-incorrect reconstruction can be more harmful than incomplete restoration. Therefore, in cultural heritage scenarios, how to enhance restoration completeness while avoiding excessive reconstruction of original artistic styles and historical information remains a critical challenge for contemporary image restoration methods.

Data collection and preprocessing

Dataset creation

This study constructs an image restoration dataset tailored to the real restoration requirements of Huashan rock art. The data sources mainly consist of two parts: printed publications and publicly available online resources. For printed materials, the Illustrated Atlas of the Zuojiang Huashan Rock Art Cultural Landscape series serves as the primary reference, including the Chongzuo Volume, Jiangzhou Volume, Longzhou Volume, and Fusui Volume. These volumes were compiled under the organization of the Offices of the Leading Group for the World Cultural Heritage Nomination of the Zuojiang Huashan Rock Art Cultural Landscape in different regions. The atlases provide systematic visual records and contextual information of the rock art sites, offering stable and traceable references for subsequent data screening and content verification. In particular, the printed sources were preferred as the “anchor” references because they provide relatively consistent documentation, clearer provenance, and more reliable contextual descriptions, which is crucial for avoiding mislabeling and ensuring that the dataset truly reflects Huashan rock art rather than visually similar but irrelevant cliff patterns. For each candidate image, we recorded the acquisition source, and kept a traceable mapping between the processed sample and its original reference to facilitate later auditing and reproducibility.

To further enhance data diversity and realism, publicly available online resources were collected from multiple channels to supplement the printed materials. These online images cover a wider range of shooting viewpoints, illumination conditions, and degradation patterns, thereby enriching the variability of the dataset. Compared with printed images, online resources typically introduce larger appearance variations caused by camera devices, shooting distance, perspective distortion, and uncontrolled lighting, which better approximates the real usage scenarios of digital archiving and field documentation. To reduce domain bias introduced by heterogeneous sources, all images were standardized through a unified preprocessing pipeline, including (i) resolution normalization, (ii) color space conversion and range normalization, and (iii) mild geometric alignment/cropping to keep the main rock art region centered while minimizing background interference. In total, 528 candidate images were initially collected. All candidates were then manually screened and cleaned under unified quality criteria, removing duplicate samples, images with insufficient resolution, severe occlusion, non-rock-art subjects, or incomplete visual information. After this process, 177 images were retained as valid samples for this study, which were subsequently used for paired data construction and training evaluation. The data collection and screening statistics are summarized in Table 1. Specifically, the screening criteria were designed to align with restoration-oriented learning: we required that each retained image contains (1) a clearly identifiable motif region (e.g., human/animal figures or symbol clusters) and (2) observable degradation phenomena (e.g., fading, boundary blur, peeling, or texture weathering) that make restoration non-trivial. To improve consistency, the screening was performed under the same guidelines, and ambiguous cases were re-checked to confirm that the sample exhibits restoration-relevant content rather than purely aesthetic variations. After the final selection, the 177 images were further used to construct paired training data by generating damaged inputs and corresponding restoration targets under controlled settings, so that the learning objective matches real restoration demands while remaining reproducible. Following the common practice in restoration/inpainting benchmarks, we produce (input, mask, target) triplets, where the mask indicates missing/degraded regions to be restored and the target is the corresponding clean reference used for supervision; this enables direct evaluation with pixel-level and structure-aware metrics under a unified protocol.

Dataset preprocessing

To ensure consistent input scale and comparability during both training and evaluation, we first apply a unified size normalization to the screened Huashan rock art images. Specifically, all images are converted to a three-channel color format and resampled to a fixed resolution of , thereby reducing distribution shifts introduced by differences in size, aspect ratio, and interpolation across sources. This step not only facilitates learning of fine-grained textures and structural contours under a unified spatial scale, but also provides a stable pixel grid for the multi-step noise injection and denoising iterations in diffusion-based generation.

For data organization and task formulation, we adopt a paired-data format for image restoration and store the processed samples into three functional directories: ground-truth, input, and mask. For each normalized rock art image, it is first saved as ground-truth for supervision and quality assessment. Then, a binary occlusion mask is generated at the same resolution, with the mask coverage controlled within the range of to simulate common missing, peeling, and occlusion patterns in cultural heritage imagery. Finally, the input image is constructed by applying the mask to the corresponding region of the original image, where a fixed black filling strategy is used to produce a controllable and reproducible degraded input. In addition to the paired images, we also record metadata for each sample, including the source path, size information, mask coverage ratio, and degradation configuration, enabling traceability during training, experimental reproducibility, and consistent management for subsequent visualization analysis. Method

Design Rationale

Diffusion models formulate restoration as an iterative denoising process that gradually transforms noise into a clean image, making them naturally suitable for ill-posed recovery problems where constraints must be injected along the reverse trajectory29. From a score-based perspective, restoration can be understood as following a learned score field that guides samples toward high-probability regions of the data distribution30, while implicit sampling schemes further highlight that the generation path can be flexibly controlled without changing the underlying objective31. In practice, latent diffusion provides an efficient and semantically meaningful space for conditioning and feature manipulation, enabling high-resolution restoration with manageable computation32. Building on these foundations, a key design requirement for rock art restoration is to introduce constraints that preserve symbol-level structure while allowing realistic completion of degraded material textures, which calls for controllable conditioning mechanisms rather than purely pixel-wise optimization.

Guidance and conditional control in diffusion offer a principled way to modulate restoration behavior, but the form and strength of conditioning crucially determine whether the model prioritizes fidelity or plausibility33. Inpainting-oriented diffusion methods demonstrate that incorporating observed regions and mask constraints into the reverse process can improve faithfulness, yet also reveal common failure modes such as over-smoothing or content drift when constraints are insufficiently structured34. Image-to-image diffusion further shows that task conditions can steer generation across multiple restoration settings, supporting the idea that conditioning should be explicitly encoded and injected rather than left implicit in the data distribution35. Text-guided editing and blended diffusion illustrate that guidance can be localized and composed to balance global coherence with region-specific edits, motivating controllable fusion when both known and missing regions must remain consistent36,37. Finally, conditional control frameworks demonstrate that structural signals (e.g., edges, contours, layouts) can be provided as external controls to constrain generation, which motivates explicitly introducing structure-aware priors alongside texture-related cues for restoration tasks with strict shape fidelity requirements38. Collectively, these insights support our design choice to decouple complementary priors and to modulate their influence throughout the denoising trajectory, so that global structure can be stabilized early while fine-grained texture can be refined at later steps under controlled conditioning.

Overall model architecture

This paper proposes a diffusion-based generative framework for Huashan rock art image restoration, whose overall architecture is illustrated in Fig. 2. The framework consists of a forward noise diffusion process and a conditional reverse denoising process. In the forward process, damaged rock art images are progressively mapped into a high-noise latent space, enabling a unified characterization of complex and irreversible real-world degradation patterns. In the reverse process, the network gradually restores image content through multi-step noise removal, thereby achieving generative reconstruction of missing regions. Unlike conventional diffusion models, the proposed method explicitly incorporates a prior-aware mechanism into the denoising backbone. A dual-branch prior extraction module is introduced to separately model structural information and texture information in rock art images, and continuously provides conditional guidance to the diffusion process across multiple time steps, enhancing the model’s holistic perception of artistic forms and local details.

The dual-branch prior extraction module encodes the input image into feature embeddings, generating structural priors and texture priors, respectively. Subsequently, a gated attention fusion module dynamically weights the diffusion features and multi-source priors, enabling the model to adaptively adjust the strength of prior guidance according to the noise level at different diffusion steps. In this manner, the model preserves the flexibility of diffusion-based generation while stably injecting culturally meaningful contour structures and texture patterns into the denoising process, providing a unified and coherent architectural foundation for high-quality restoration.

Given the limited number of training samples, we adopted several practical measures to mitigate overfitting during diffusion model training. First, we used a fixed train/test split (142/35) and monitored the test metrics throughout training to ensure that performance gains were not driven by memorization. Second, AdamW with weight decay () was employed to regularize the model parameters, and gradient clipping (1.0) was applied to stabilize optimization and prevent unstable updates. Third, cosine annealing was used to gradually reduce the learning rate, which helps avoid overfitting by discouraging excessively sharp minima in late training. Fourth, the diffusion timestep t was randomly sampled at each iteration across the full range of noise levels, effectively exposing the network to diverse denoising conditions and reducing the risk of over-specialization to a narrow degradation pattern. Finally, we selected the final checkpoint based on the best test performance under the same training configuration, and all reported results are averaged over three runs with different random seeds to confirm training stability.

From an overall perspective, these comparative results reveal the capability boundaries of different paradigms in the Huashan rock art restoration task. Traditional reconstruction based models, such as encoder decoder architectures or variational frameworks, tend to prioritize the minimization of pixel level errors. However, when confronted with the distinctive degradation characteristics of Huashan rock art, including clear symbolic structures, heavily weathered textures, and irregular missing regions, such methods often struggle to simultaneously preserve contour continuity and realistic detail appearance. Adversarial approaches can enhance visual sharpness and local texture richness to some extent, but they are also more prone to generating artificial textures in missing regions that are inconsistent with the material properties and stroke semantics of rock art. In contrast, the proposed method demonstrates more stable performance in both error control and structural consistency metrics, indicating that diffusion based stepwise generation is better suited to modeling the progressive restoration process required for rock art. Moreover, the explicit separation of structural priors and texture priors establishes a more reliable constraint relationship between global form and local surface characteristics, which aligns well with the restoration objective of preserving human figures, symbolic boundaries, and overall composition without structural drift.

When further examined in the context of the proposed model design, the coordinated improvement across multiple metrics also indirectly validates the effectiveness of the introduced modules. The dual branch prior extraction mechanism disentangles identifiable morphological structures from texture patterns that are more susceptible to weathering, preventing the generation process from being misled by unstable local textures when reconstructing missing content. Meanwhile, the gated attention fusion mechanism allows the strength of prior injection to adapt to different denoising stages, emphasizing structural stabilization at earlier stages and progressively enriching texture details at later stages. This gradual transition from structure oriented constraints to texture completion is naturally consistent with the stepwise denoising behavior of diffusion models and better satisfies the requirements of cultural heritage restoration for artistic consistency and historical fidelity. As a result, the proposed approach avoids excessive repainting that may cause stylistic deviation, while also mitigating over smoothing that leads to detail loss, achieving a more balanced trade off between visual quality and archaeological credibility.

The ablation results indicate that the performance improvements of the proposed approach do not solely stem from the generative capability of the diffusion baseline, but rather from the progressive refinement of a design strategy centered on prior modeling and adaptive injection. In the Huashan rock art restoration scenario, which is characterized by strong symbolic structures, complex texture degradation, and irregular missing patterns, relying only on stable diffusion often fails to simultaneously preserve contours and fine details. After introducing the dual branch prior extraction module, the model is able to separately model stable structural information such as human figure contours and stroke boundaries, and more sensitive texture information such as weathering granularity and pigment deposition, thereby reducing the risk of structural drift and texture artifacts at the source. With the further incorporation of the gated attention fusion module, prior information can be dynamically selected and weighted at different denoising stages, allowing the model to emphasize structural stabilization in early stages and progressively refine texture details in later stages. This behavior is naturally aligned with the stepwise denoising mechanism of diffusion models and better satisfies the requirements of cultural heritage restoration for artistic consistency and historical fidelity, leading the final integrated scheme to achieve more stable and comprehensive overall gains.

From this set of visual comparisons, it can be directly observed that the differences among methods on the Huashan rock art restoration task, which is characterized by strong symbolic structures and complex weathered textures, mainly manifest in two aspects. The first is whether the continuity between the restored regions and the original stroke boundaries appears natural, and the second is whether the color and rock surface material of the completed areas can be smoothly integrated with the surrounding context. Some methods are able to fill missing regions in a literal sense, yet they often produce blurred boundaries, broken strokes, or shape deviations around human figure contours and line inflection points, which reduces the recognizability of symbolic elements. In contrast, the proposed method consistently follows a structure first and texture second restoration principle across all three examples. Near occlusion boundaries, the completed strokes are more likely to extend along the original brush trajectories, avoiding the introduction of new shapes in missing regions and thereby preserving the stability of human poses and symbolic compositions. In large background areas, the generated textures more closely match the roughness and color distribution of the surrounding rock surface, with more controlled transitions in brightness and chromatic variation, making the restored regions less immediately noticeable. This behavior is consistent with the dual branch prior extraction and gated attention fusion mechanisms proposed in this paper. Structural priors help anchor key contours and boundaries, texture priors supplement weathered granularity and material characteristics, and gated injection allows both types of priors to participate in the diffusion denoising process with appropriate strengths at different stages, resulting in visually more stable structural coherence and more natural material consistency.

In structure related regions, the responses align more closely with symbol edges and morphological contours, indicating that the structural prior helps the model lock geometric configurations and stroke directions, thereby reducing contour drift and stroke discontinuity. In texture and transition regions, the responses are more closely associated with changes in granularity scale and color gradation, suggesting that the texture prior mainly constrains material details and tonal continuity. Meanwhile, variations in response intensity across different examples reflect differences in missing patterns and scene complexity, implying that the gating mechanism does not inject priors in a fixed manner, but dynamically adjusts their influence according to restoration difficulty and information uncertainty. This adaptive behavior encourages the model to focus more actively on regions where structural stabilization should precede detail completion, providing an interpretable explanation for the stable performance achieved by the proposed method in the Huashan rock art restoration task.

Limitations

This work is primarily evaluated under a controlled experimental setting with a self-constructed Huashan rock art dataset, and the reported gains should be interpreted within this scope. Although the dataset is designed to reflect real restoration demands, the current test set size remains limited, and the diversity of acquisition conditions and degradation factors (e.g., illumination changes, viewing angles, surface moisture, long-term weathering heterogeneity, and human-induced disturbances) is not yet exhaustive. Moreover, the evaluation focuses on pixel-level fidelity and perceptual similarity metrics (MSE, MAE, PSNR, SSIM) together with qualitative visualization, which provides a reproducible and quantifiable basis for comparing restoration behavior, but does not fully capture conservation-oriented criteria such as authenticity, historical plausibility, and acceptable intervention boundaries that are typically judged by domain experts.

In terms of practical deployment, the proposed framework should be viewed as a technical toolchain to assist digital documentation and conservative reconstruction rather than an automatic substitute for professional conservation decisions. While the dual-branch priors and step-adaptive fusion are designed to reduce structural drift and suppress spurious textures, the method can still exhibit failure cases when visible evidence is extremely sparse or when weathering patterns deviate strongly from the training distribution, potentially resulting in uncertain reconstructions in heavily damaged regions. In addition, the present study does not include expert-in-the-loop validation, user studies, or field deployment trials, and therefore the societal and conservation impacts are not claimed as empirically demonstrated outcomes. Future extensions should incorporate broader cross-site and cross-condition validation, establish expert evaluation protocols with traceable reporting of restoration parameters and uncertainty cues, and integrate the restoration outputs into an auditable workflow for digital archiving and scholarly interpretation.

Conclusion

This study addresses the fading, loss, and texture degradation of the Zuojiang Huashan rock art caused by long term weathering and multiple external disturbances. A Huashan rock art image restoration dataset oriented toward real restoration demands is constructed, and a restoration framework based on a stable diffusion model is proposed. The framework decouples structural contours and texture material information through dual branch prior extraction, and then adaptively injects prior constraints at different stages of diffusion denoising via gated attention fusion. In this way, a more stable balance is achieved among structural continuity in missing regions, material consistency, and overall visual fidelity. The experimental evaluation systematically assesses the proposed method from multiple perspectives, including quantitative comparisons, ablation analysis, significance testing, qualitative visualization, and focused region interpretation. The results demonstrate that the proposed approach is better suited to the cultural heritage scenario of Huashan rock art, which is characterized by strong symbolic structures and complex weathered textures, and provides a reproducible, quantifiable, and interpretable technical pathway for digital rock art restoration.

Future work will be further advanced in three directions. First, at the data level, more diverse acquisition conditions and degradation types will be incorporated, together with finer grained hierarchical annotations and expert verification mechanisms, to enhance coverage across different site environments and time spans. Second, at the model level, stronger cross scene generalization and controllable generation capabilities will be explored, enabling finer grained constraints on structure and texture while adhering to the principle of minimal intervention and reducing potential spurious textures and stylistic drift. Third, at the application level, oriented toward the needs of digital archiving and dissemination, a complete pipeline will be established from restoration results to traceable evidence chains, including uncertainty indication, interpretable process visualization, and multi terminal interactive presentation, thereby promoting the transition of Huashan rock art conservation from experimental validation to a practical and sustainable long term digital preservation system.
