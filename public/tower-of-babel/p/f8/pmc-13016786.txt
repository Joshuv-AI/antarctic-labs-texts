Endothelial to mesenchymal transition in cardiovascular diseases: molecular insights and clinical perspectives
Authors: Ignacio Fernando Hall, Elena Aikawa, Judith Sluimer, Andrew H Baker, Jason C Kovacic
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndothelial to mesenchymal transition (EndMT) is a process whereby endothelial cells transition to adopt a mesenchymal-like fate, e.g. to become smooth muscle cells, osteoblasts, fibroblasts, or chondrocytes. In embryonic heart development, the importance of EndMT was established several decades ago, with ECs undergoing EndMT to give rise to the endocardial cushions that ultimately develop into the cardiac valves. More recently, EndMT has been observed in various adult cardiovascular diseases. This has been established through the application of state-of-the-art research tools, including cell lineage tracing in mice and single cell RNA sequencing, which have allowed in depth profiling of endothelial cells that have undergone transition to a mesenchymal-like state. As with any emerging field, certain challenges have arisen, such as the lack of a standardized definition of what constitutes EndMT at a molecular level and obtaining proof in humans that EndMT is mechanistically involved in the pathophysiology of cardiovascular diseases. The greatest evidence for the presence of cells undergoing EndMT in the adult exists for transplant vasculopathy, pulmonary arterial hypertension, vein graft remodeling, atherosclerosis, and valvular heart disease. The transforming growth factor beta pathway is the major driver, but this is not the exclusive signalling mechanism governing this complex process. Translational large animal studies have already been undertaken to inhibit EndMT in both valvular heart disease and vein graft remodeling, with positive results. These studies pave the way towards first-in-human clinical inhibition of EndMT as a therapeutic strategy. Here we review this exciting field, with particular emphasis on the functional role of EndMT in adult cardiovascular diseases using atherosclerosis and valvular disease as exemplars.Keywords: Endothelial to mesenchymal transition, EndMT, EndoMT, Endothelial, Smooth muscle cell, Fibroblast, Plasticity, Atherosclerosis, Valvular disease

Introduction

The function of cardiovascular cells, such as endothelial cells (ECs), vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), fibroblasts, immune cells, and cardiomyocytes, is a critical factor in health, and these often become dysfunctional in disease states. Accordingly, there is growing interest in understanding the mechanisms that govern cell function, including how cell function is impacted by cellular heterogeneity within and across cell populations and how cell plasticity (i.e. ability to alter cellular identity) and activation alter cell function and fate.1–4 In the adult, ECs are generally described as a heterogenous population, which is structurally and molecularly differentiated to meet local needs in an organ-specific and within-organ manner.5 The distinct requirements of the arterial, venous, capillary, and lymphatic vascular systems is an obvious example of where local cues and physiologic requirements are associated with marked differences in EC subtypes.1

While ECs are generally considered a quiescent population in the adult, the maintenance of homeostasis is a dynamic balance requiring constant energy expenditure and signalling inputs. Under differing conditions, ECs may become activated, which can be an important aspect of normal healthy biological responses. For example, a healthy activated endothelium can facilitate immune cell attraction for wound healing or an infection control response. However, in a disease, EC diversification and (mal)adaptive changes can arise or be further amplified, thus enhancing heterogeneity, with changes in the proportion or composition of EC subpopulations.6–8 In this situation, cells may respond through increased plasticity, with extensive phenotypic and functional rearrangements. Accordingly, ECs can, ultimately, undergo transient or permanent cell fate changes. Endothelial to mesenchymal transition (EndMT), a process whereby ECs undergo changes along a continuum of intermediate stages towards a mesenchymal phenotype, represents a prototypical example of this cellular transformation.9,10 This review examines the functional heterogeneity and plasticity of ECs. It emphasizes the role of context-specific EndMT in maintaining cardiovascular homeostasis and driving pathological remodeling, the relevance of these aspects to cardiovascular disease (CVD), and the potential therapeutic opportunities (Graphical Abstract).

The biology of endothelial to mesenchymal transition

Phenotypic changes that occur during endothelial to mesenchymal transition

Endothelial cells are flattened cells that line lymphatics and blood vessels (veins and arteries) and the cardiac chambers and valves. Morphologically, ECs are characterized by their ‘cobblestone-like’, elongated, polygonal shape and their attachment both to each other by cell junctions [including cadherin 5 (CDH5)] and to the underlying basement membrane. Endothelial cells perform various functions, including forming a barrier between the lumen and underling tissues and regulating blood flow, vascular contractility, immune responses, and blood coagulation.

Endothelial to mesenchymal transition is a complex process characterized by major morphological, molecular, and functional changes in ECs (Figure 1).2,10,11 Endothelial to mesenchymal transition is considered to be a specific example of the broader programme of epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT), with ECs being a specialized type of epithelial cell. Importantly, while EndMT has only been intensively studied over the last two decades, EMT is a critical biologic programme that has been under investigation since the 1960s and is involved in a diverse array of biological and pathological processes from embryonic development to epithelial tumour metastasis.12

Endothelial to mesenchymal transition is characterized by the progressive loss of endothelial markers (CD31, cadherin 5, and VWF, among others) and concurrent acquisition of mesenchymal markers (i.e. TAGLN, ACTA2, collagen 1A1, and FSP1). This involves hallmark changes in cell–cell junctions, barrier integrity, permeability, cytoskeletal organization, and extracellular matrix composition. Alterations in cell mechanotransduction and mitochondrial function further contribute to the acquisition of contractile, migratory, and invasive properties

At the transcript and protein level, EndMT is generically described as the progressive loss of endothelial lineage-specific markers [such as CD31, CDH5, endothelial nitric oxide synthase (NOS3), and von Willebrand factor (vWF)] and concomitant increased expression of mesenchymal markers [such as alpha-smooth muscle actin (αSMA), smooth muscle 22 alpha (SM22α, also known as TAGLN), calponin, smooth muscle myosin heavy chain 11 (MYH11), and collagen 1A1 (COL1A1) and COL3A1].2,10,11 These transcripts and their proteins vary in their involvement in EndMT and the level of difference depending on the specific EC subtype undergoing EndMT (e.g. venous, arterial, or lymphatic ECs) and the final mesenchymal phenotype that is observed. The ultimate cell fate is also heterogenous and can include VSMCs, chondrocytes, osteoblasts, and fibroblasts. Further, ECs can also give rise to haematopoietic lineages via EndMT (which is prominent during embryonic development), and there is additional evidence for endothelial to immune cell transition in the adult.2,6,9–11 Hence, the term EndMT is somewhat non-specific with respect to both the initiating type of EC and the ultimate mesenchymal cell fate. At present, there is no agreed universal definition of EndMT at the molecular level, which can create challenges in terms of data cross-comparison, experimental validation, and the degree of certainty of key findings.10 In the context of this review, we propose the following practical definition: EndMT is a dynamic, multi-stage reprogramming process in which ECs lose their characteristic markers and functions such as cell–cell adhesion and acquire mesenchymal traits including enhanced migratory capacity, invasiveness, and extracellular matrix (ECM) production. This phenotypic shift contributes to tissue development, repair, fibrosis, and the progression of certain disease states.

In terms of changes in cell phenotype and functionality, ECs undergoing EndMT exhibit a shift from their characteristic morphology to an elongated or ‘spindle-like’ shape. Additional changes include loss of cell–cell junctions, loss of cellular polarity, cytoskeletal remodelling, and ECM breakdown, accompanied by increased collagen deposition, as well as altered mechanotransduction, barrier function, and cellular integrity.2,10,11 Cell metabolism can also be affected, with EC fatty acid oxidation13 and mitochondrial calcium signalling14 being additional important regulators of EndMT. Altogether, these alterations confer EndMT-derived cells with contractile, migratory, and invasive properties. Specifically, in terms of contractile properties of EndMT-derived cells, this has not been studied in great detail, but is assumed to be related to the upregulation of contractile proteins such as αSMA, calponin, and MYH11. Although VSMCs are generally considered to be the archetypal contractile cell in this context, enhanced contractile properties may also be seen in other EndMT-derived cell types such as fibroblast-like cells and potentially other cell types.15 An overview of the hallmark features of EndMT is provided in Figure 1.

While not well studied, mesenchymal cells can undergo a reciprocal cellular programme defined as mesenchymal-to-endothelial transition (MEndT).16 Despite limited understanding of the relationship between EndMT and MEndT, it appears likely to be more complex than just a bidirectional lineage conversion process. Further investigation is required to determine whether both occur in the same disease scenarios, as well as their dynamics and end-stage effects on disease outcomes.

Endothelial cell fate in endothelial to mesenchymal transition

Endothelial to mesenchymal transition is a multi-step process whereby cell fate changes exist along a continuum rather than as a binary event.2,4 At the far end of this transition, cells acquire a mesenchymal fate where the EC molecular signature is no longer present. However, cells in intermediate stages retain features and transcriptional signatures from both or multiple lineages.17,23–25 Thus, transitioning ECs may shift between endothelial and mesenchymal lineages dynamically, with some subsets potentially contributing to homeostasis restoration (i.e. reverting to being ECs) or further differentiating into more committed mesenchymal cells.1 It is tempting to speculate that partial or complete EndMT may induce the de novo formation of specialized EC subpopulations or prime/reprogramme existing EC subsets, thereby influencing human disease trajectories and ultimately patients’ response to therapies. Further research is needed to better characterize the plasticity of EndMT-responsive EC subsets and determine whether a common ‘transitioning signature’ exists, which might be later shaped by local cues, or if cells are inherently primed for lineage-specific changes (i.e. differentiation into chondrocytes rather than fibroblasts). Conceivably, enhancing EndMT reversal or halting transition into disease-associated phenotypes can be of clinical significance.19 Furthermore, investigating specific local responses and cellular milieus is essential to assess their influence on EndMT-responsive EC subpopulations but also to understand how EndMT impacts neighbouring cells.

Major signalling pathways involved in endothelial to mesenchymal transition

The EndMT programme is orchestrated by biochemical, biomechanical, and environmental signals and then executed by intracellular signalling cascades (Figure 2). This is a multi-step process, fine-tuned via interconnected molecular pathways, with the transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) signalling pathway playing a central, but not exclusive, regulatory role.10,11,19,25 A number of transcription factors facilitate the EndMT programme including TWIST, SNAI1 (SNAI; Snail family transcriptional repressor), SLUG (also known as SNAI2), ZEB1/2 (ZEB; zinc-finger E-box-binding homeobox) and SMAD2/3 (SMAD; Mothers against decapentaplegic homologue), with most of these belonging to the TGF-β family. While EndMT is essential for embryonic development, in the adult, it is mostly initiated under pathophysiological states that lead to aberrant TGF-β pathway activation.2

Endothelial to mesenchymal transition is regulated by a complex interplay of biochemical, biomechanical, and environmental cues. These factors act via intracellular signalling cascades, with the transforming growth factor beta pathway playing a central role both independently and through synergistic/antagonistic interactions with other pathways, including WNT, NOTCH, HIPPO, and FGF. Additional regulation can involve epigenetic marks and non-coding RNA–mediated processes. In the latent form, transforming growth factor beta ligand homodimers are bound to the latency-activating protein (LAP), and the resulting complex can associate with the latent transforming growth factor beta-binding protein (LTBP) anchored to the extracellular matrix. Upon activation, transforming growth factor beta ligands bind to Type 1/Type 2 receptor (transforming growth factor beta receptor 1 and transforming growth factor beta receptor 2) heterodimers, which upon phosphorylation activate regulatory SMAD2 and SMAD3 and their intracellular cascades. Ultimately, the phosphorylated SMAD2/3 complex translocates into the nucleus where it induces endothelial to mesenchymal transition gene programme activation. In response to cues such as inflammation, altered shear stress, and/or reactive oxygen species, EndMT can be triggered by either TGF-β1 or TGF-β2 with these ligand isoforms exhibiting varying degrees of activation dependent on the pathophysiological context (Figure 2).2,17,25 Biologically inactive (latent) TGF-β ligand is processed with the support of matrix metalloproteases (MMPs), thus binding to Type 1 and Type 2 receptors (TGF-βR1 and TGF-βR2), which upon heterodimerization and phosphorylation, activate regulatory SMAD2 and SMAD3 and their intracellular cascades.10,19,25 Phosphorylated SMAD2/3 translocate into the nucleus, functioning as co-activators of transcriptional complexes and mediating targeted gene regulation. Ultimately, the activation of this intracellular cascade positively regulates EndMT by promoting a variety of processes including cell differentiation, adhesion, migration, and apoptosis.22 Intriguingly, ‘non-canonical’ SMAD2/3-independent activation of the TGF-β pathway is also associated with EndMT.26 The role of additional signalling cascades in EndMT, including the WNT and NOTCH pathways have been extensively reviewed elsewhere.2,10,11 A summary of key EndMT signalling pathways is provided in Table 2.

As well as signalling pathways, recent studies have also revealed the important governing role that epigenetic factors play in EndMT (see dedicated review37). Epigenetics can influence the development of mesenchymal phenotypes via mechanisms such as DNA methylation and histone modification, as well as via non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs). For example, histone deacetylase 9 (HDAC9) promotes EndMT in atherosclerosis, suggesting that HDAC9 inhibition might be a promising target in this disease context.18

Cardiovascular development and disease states in which endothelial to mesenchymal transition is implicated

Endothelial to mesenchymal transition plays an integral role in embryonic development12 and was first described in cardiogenesis and vasculogenesis.12,38,39 During heart formation, subsets of endocardial ECs detach from the endocardium, undergo EndMT, and migrate into the ECM between the endocardium and myocardium. This results in endocardial cushion formation in the atrioventricular canal and the outflow tract, ultimately giving rise to the fibrous components of the atrioventricular septal complex and the atrioventricular heart valves.10,12 The role of EndMT in embryonic and cardiac development has been reviewed extensively elsewhere.10,12

Unlike during development where ECs fully transition into a mesenchymal cell fate through (complete) EndMT, in adults EndMT is posited to be mostly partial, marked by incomplete activation and differentiation into mesenchymal-like states.23 Also unlike in development, EndMT is recognized as a pathological mechanism contributing to various cardiovascular conditions in the adult (Table 3).

Studying endothelial to mesenchymal transition at differing levels of evidence

It is important to consider the major evolving approaches and tools that have been used to study EndMT in the adult. One of the most widely used but least reliable methods is immunohistochemistry staining of cells for the simultaneous co-expression of endothelial and mesenchymal markers, such as CD31 (EC marker) and TAGLN (mesenchymal cell marker).18 There are various limitations of this approach: challenges with accurate co-registration of positive staining, inability to identify ECs that have fully transitioned to a mesenchymal state (with total loss of endothelial marker expression), inability to identify cells early in the EndMT process that might not yet express abundant mesenchymal proteins, and an inability to distinguish cells undergoing EndMT from those undergoing MEndT. Nevertheless, immunohistochemistry, particularly immunofluorescence, for detecting simultaneous co-expression of endothelial and mesenchymal markers remains a cost-effective and relatively simple approach that requires minimal tissue samples.

With the advent of Cre-lox technologies in mice, lineage tracing became possible using endothelial Cre strains. In brief, Cre-lox systems allow for the permanent ‘marking’ of specific cell populations, such as all cells that express the endothelial marker Cdh5, with a ‘reporter’ such as a yellow or red fluorescent protein. However, as an initial challenge, constitutively active endothelial Cre systems (i.e. active from conception and not able to be dynamically ‘turned on’ or ‘off’) mark not only cells that have undergone EndMT but also a significant proportion of haematopoietic cells. This is because during early development, a proportion of haematopoietic lineages derive from ECs.18 This initially led to confusion in the field, as many adult contexts in which EndMT is observed (Table 3) also involve inflammation, which can recruit hematopoietic-derived inflammatory cells. As a solution, inducible Cre systems were developed that can be activated at any time point, which for studying EndMT can be done a few weeks after birth and thus well after the developmental period of endothelial to haematopoietic transition has ended.17,18 The advent of inducible mouse endothelial Cre systems was a game-changer for this field, because it allowed investigators to track ECs as they underwent EndMT and adopted differing mesenchymal fates.17,18 Because these systems permanently ‘mark’ ECs, it allows for the tracing of endothelial-derived cells even when they have lost the expression of all endothelial markers.

Most recently, single cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) has been used to study EndMT, which offers the possibility of more comprehensive gene and pathway analysis.14 It is also possible to combine scRNAseq with inducible mouse endothelial Cre-lox models to optimally interrogate and understand the fate of permanently marked endothelial-derived cells that have undergone EndMT. While scRNAseq is not at genome-wide scale, the EC state can be queried with a very broad mesenchymal gene signature to reveal (sub)clusters of ECs that also co-express mesenchymal genes, indicative of EndMT. Gene ontology and pathway analysis can also be performed to interrogate the involvement of defined pathways of EndMT regulators, such as TGF-β signalling, VSMC contractile genes, or EMT. In terms of how these analyses are conducted, first, the genes differentially expressed by certain cell (sub)clusters vs other cell (sub)clusters are identified. Second, this gene list is analysed to determine if the genes that are enriched in a specific cluster arise more often than could occur by chance and whether or not they are connected to EndMT-related pathways. Notably, there is not a single, direct gene ontology term for EndMT, with EMT (GO: 0001837) considered to be one of the closest. However, recent work involving detailed characterization of differing EC populations such as by Liu et al.49 may provide the basis for updated gene ontology references that are more specific for EndMT. In addition, in silico predictions of cell differentiation or transition using ‘pseudo-time trajectory analysis’ can interrogate EC fate. Importantly, even if cells are captured at the same, single timepoint, cells will appear in different states of transition. This allows comparison of individual cell transcriptomes, and cells are arranged according to their similarity or ‘distance’ from the transcriptome of origin. This distance vector is called pseudo-time, reflecting a predicted time rather than actual time. One caveat of this approach is that the point of origin is often set by the bioinformatician, rather than via an unbiased assessment. In data derived from cells or tissues without Cre-lox lineage tracing, this opens up the possibility of false-positive assignment of ECs as the origin of mesenchymal cells.

On the basis of the above, and to enable a rigorous comparison of the levels of evidence (LOEs) for EndMT across differing studies, a proposed scheme comprising five differing LOE is provided in simplified form in Figure 4 and Table 1 and in greater detail as Supplementary data online, Table S1. While we believe this scheme is consistent with contemporary agreement and understanding of the strengths and limitations of each of the tools that can be used to study EndMT, the allocation of the different LOE is arbitrary and ultimately based on our own perceptions, and ideally multiple techniques should be applied in conjunction.

Endothelial to mesenchymal transition in atherosclerosis

Lineage tracing studies suggesting endothelial to mesenchymal transition in atherosclerosis

Initial reports suggesting that EndMT may be involved in atherosclerosis emerged circa 2015 using co-staining techniques applied to human atherosclerotic plaques (LOE3).50 Rigorous studies using Cre-lox lineage tracing in mice soon followed from two independent laboratories (LOE5).17,22 Both Cre-lox studies showed, also using human samples, that the extent of coronary atherosclerosis and an unstable plaque morphology are correlated with the degree of EndMT,17,22 which in turn appeared to be related to altered collagen and MMP production in EndMT-derived cells.17 In terms of signalling pathways, these studies suggested a role for oxidative stress,17 altered shear stress, and endothelial FGF signalling;22 all known to be important features of atherosclerosis. Although these studies predated the era of scRNAseq, they provided an important proof of the role of EndMT in atherosclerosis.

Single cell RNA sequencing studies supporting the role of endothelial to mesenchymal transition in atherosclerosis

More recent studies confirmed and refined these observations of EndMT in atherosclerosis. At least six studies with Cdh5-driven fluorescent reporter expression for endothelial lineage tracing have now shown co-expression of at least one mesenchymal gene in Cdh5 lineage-positive cells in atherosclerotic plaque sections from mice, i.e. reaching the highest quality of evidence (LOE5).51–56

In greater detail, multiple murine studies have used scRNAseq of aortas or carotid arteries with atherosclerosis and identified one to four subsets of ECs (from among two to nine total EC subsets) that co-express mesenchymal genes (LOE4). In general, these studies were designed comparing healthy with atherosclerotic arterial segments. However, the chosen marker genes and also the number of marker genes vary between studies. Additional evidence from gene ontology and pathway analysis predict the involvement of those cells in EMT, VSMC contraction, matrix homeostasis, and/or TGF-β signalling. In addition, pseudo-time trajectory analyses confirmed that ECs transition into a mesenchymal state.57,58 However, limitations included a lack of lineage tracing and other potential biases as described in the preceding section. Only three murine studies have so far combined scRNAseq with EC lineage tracing in atherosclerosis.27,51,55 However, while these studies also detected EndMT cells using immunohistochemistry, reporter gene expression on single cell graphs was not shown27 or was shown in clusters annotated as VSMCs and based on very few cells.51 Only one study conclusively showed two lineage-positive EC subsets that co-express multiple mesenchymal genes, corroborated by gene ontology and pseudo-time analysis (LOE5),55 although these results were based on only ∼600 EndMT-positive cells. Two separate human scRNAseq studies and one meta-analysis demonstrated one EC subset co-expressing mesenchymal markers (LOE4) in carotid and coronary plaques.4,59,60 Two of these studies also provided evidence on pathway levels, but none showed pseudo-time analysis.4,59,60 Collectively, these murine lineage tracing studies fully establish that EndMT is observed in the development of murine atherogenesis. Evidence from human plaques partially corroborates these findings, but currently, it is not possible to perform EC lineage tracing in human tissues.

Elucidation of the complex factors related to endothelial to mesenchymal transition in atherosclerosis

Numerous studies have offered new insights into factors driving EndMT in atherosclerosis, in addition to TGF-β signalling and a high cholesterol diet. These include hyperglycaemia/diabetes,61 high matrix stiffness,62 macrophage inflammatory NF-kβ signalling,63 inflammatory IL-1β signalling in ECs, the epigenetic regulator HDAC9,18 and the mechanosensitive transcription factor axis GATA4–TWIST1.36,52,58 Furthermore, a number of additional genes have been shown to play a role in EndMT in atherosclerosis models in vivo, including mesenchymal transcription factor KLF11,64 epigenic regulator HDAC3,65 FGF receptor-degrading molecules epsin 1 and 2,54 the circadian rhythm gene Clock,66 the serine/threonine-protein kinase PIM1,67 and pluripotency factor OCT4.55 While many of these molecules and genes converge on the TGF-β pathway and/or its transcription regulators SNAI1, SLUG, ZEB1, ZEB2, and SP1, the numerous triggers and molecules illustrate the major complexity of EndMT regulation in atherogenesis.

Much work also remains to be done to clarify the mechanistic effects of how EndMT, and EndMT-derived cells, influences atherosclerosis development and progression. Some aspects are likely detrimental, such as the contributions of EndMT to neoangiogenesis,47 which is associated with a high-risk plaque phenotype due to the tendency of neovessels to rupture and cause intra-plaque haemorrhage. Conversely, other aspects are likely beneficial, such as EndMT-derived mesenchymal cell contributions to the thickening of fibrous caps, a feature associated with lower-risk plaques.52,56

Endothelial to mesenchymal transition is implicated in gene regulatory networks that are active in atherosclerosis, where network effects may be more active in women

Recent studies leveraging the STARNET study datasets,68 a systems genetics study of atherosclerosis in patients with and without coronary artery disease (CAD), have suggested that EndMT is involved in several gene regulatory networks (GRNs) that are active in atherosclerosis. In a study of 160 women and 160 age-matched men with CAD, sex-specific GRNs were inferred.69 Of the 32 GRNs inferred in tissues from women, three GRNs were specifically involved in atherosclerosis. One of these, named the ‘blue module’, was found to be highly enriched for genes involved in EMT by gene ontology.69

A subsequent study from the same investigators corroborated this female sex bias for EndMT in atherosclerosis by applying scRNAseq to carotid plaques obtained from seven female and eight male patients at atherectomy, where EndMT was found to be prominent in a specific cluster of ECs (EC2) from plaques obtained from female but not male patients.70 These carotid scRNAseq data were then integrated with the 135 previously inferred tissue-specific GRNs from the STARNET study.71 Three arterial wall GRNs were identified that showed strong enrichment for EC genes identified in the carotid plaque scRNAseq analysis. Of these, GRN122 was particularly enriched for genes from the female EC2 cluster related to EndMT. Interestingly, GRN122 contributed to a remarkable 4.1% of CAD heritability and was also strongly enriched in genes associated with the angiographic burden of coronary disease.70

These data substantially expand our understanding of EndMT but equally raise new questions about these sex-specific GRN effects. A provocative consideration is that these findings may hold the key to unravelling the perplexing observation that plaque erosion as a mechanism of acute coronary syndrome is more common in females than males. Endothelial to mesenchymal transition has previously been postulated to be involved in plaque erosion,10 and it is tempting to speculate that sex-biased GRNs, where EndMT is more pronounced in females than males, may be related to this phenomenon. Another study, only available as a non-peer-reviewed pre-print at the time of writing, supports the concept that erosion-prone fibrous atherosclerotic plaques from females exhibit a higher degree of EndMT than similar plaques from males.72

Endothelial to mesenchymal transition in valvular heart disease

Endothelial to mesenchymal transition in heart valve homeostasis and disease

Endothelial to mesenchymal transition plays a pivotal role in heart valve development, maintenance, and disease progression. During embryonic valvulogenesis, EndMT is essential to transforming ECs into mesenchymal cells that participate in the formation of valve structures. In post-natal life, however, aberrant reactivation of embryonic developmental pathways such as EndMT can lead to pathological remodeling, fibrosis, and calcification contributing to the progression of valvular diseases such as calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD) (LOE3)20 and ischaemic mitral regurgitation (LOE3).73 Notably, EndMT is more abundant in foetal and diseased valves, whereas in healthy adult valves, it is observed in low numbers of valvular endothelial cells (VECs), yet this process is essential for maintaining valvular interstitial cell (VIC) repopulation and overall valve homeostasis.74 This indicates a critical physiological role of EndMT and the existence of tightly controlled regulatory mechanisms that prevent excessive mesenchymal transition in normal physiological conditions.

The interaction between VECs and VICs is crucial for valve health. Valvular interstitial cells exist along a spectrum of phenotypes, from quiescent fibroblast-like cells to activated myofibroblasts that remodel the ECM in response to physiological stress. Mechanical forces, such as shear stress75 and cyclic strain,76 as well as inflammatory stimuli77 and matrix stiffness78 can induce EndMT, leading to VEC proliferation and phenotypic shifts. Single cell RNA sequencing studies have identified endothelial subpopulations in the aortic valve that display transcriptional profiles indicative of EndMT (LOE4),21 suggesting that endothelial plasticity plays an ongoing role in valve maintenance.79 Of note, to the best of our knowledge, there have been no Cre-lox lineage tracing studies (i.e. LOE5 studies) of valvular disease yet published, and therefore the highest LOE for valvular disease is currently LOE4.

Pathological endothelial to mesenchymal transition in aortic and mitral valve disease

Calcific aortic valve disease is a progressive disorder characterized by fibrosis, calcification, and eventual stenosis of the aortic valve. Endothelial to mesenchymal transition is a contributing mechanism in the early stages of CAVD, where VECs lose endothelial markers while gaining mesenchymal characteristics, including the expression of α-SMA, RUNX2, and osteopontin, which drive fibrotic and osteogenic transformation (LOE3).20 In vitro models have demonstrated that mechanical stress induces EndMT through distinct pathways.77,80–82 Low-magnitude strain (induced by cyclically stretching engineered endothelium to varying lengths) primarily activates TGF-β signalling, whereas high-magnitude strain promotes Wnt/β-catenin signalling.76 VICs inhibit EndMT in this setting, as shown in co-culture studies where VICs inhibited the transition of VECs towards an osteogenic phenotype.20 Inflammation is another key factor in EndMT-driven valve calcification.77 In CAVD, activated VECs express vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) and secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, which enhance endothelial plasticity and promote VIC activation. These findings suggest that targeting inflammatory pathways could be a therapeutic strategy for mitigating EndMT in CAVD.83

Endothelial to mesenchymal transition is also a significant contributor to mitral valve pathology, including ischaemic mitral valve regurgitation and rheumatic valve disease (LOE1-3).31,73,84 Following myocardial infarction (MI), particularly in cases leading to mitral regurgitation, increased mechanical stress from left ventricular remodeling and papillary muscle displacement triggers EndMT in VECs. This leads to fibroblast-like cells that secrete excessive ECM, causing leaflet thickening, fibrosis, and reduced flexibility, which exacerbate regurgitation (LOE1-3).85–88 Additionally, inflammatory cytokines (e.g. TNFα and IL-6) and TGF-β signalling, both up-regulated in the post-MI environment, further amplify EndMT and matrix remodeling, highlighting a complex interplay between mechanical and molecular cues in mitral valve disease.89 A distinct subpopulation of CD45-positive ECs has been identified within fibrotic mitral valve leaflets, indicating a potential role for haematopoietic–endothelial interactions in promoting EndMT-mediated fibrosis.90,91 Pharmacological inhibition of TGF-β with agents such as losartan has been shown to mitigate EndMT, reducing mitral valve fibrosis while preserving adaptive leaflet growth.88

Similar signalling pathways and epigenetic modifications influence EndMT in valvular disease as has been already described. For instance, in differing valvular disease contexts, activated SNAI1 interacts with SMAD3/4 to inhibit endothelial gene expression, thereby promoting mesenchymal transformation.84,92 Additionally, the transcription factor Slug has been implicated in aortic VIC calcification through the p53/Slug signalling pathway. CREB-binding protein (CBP) acetylates Slug, enhancing its stability and transcriptional activation ability.37 ​However, while both CBP and Slug were implicated in valve disease, further research is needed to elucidate whether CBP-mediated acetylation of Slug specifically contributes to aortic valve pathology.

Translation of endothelial to mesenchymal transition to the clinic

Given that EMT has been studied for several decades longer than EndMT (see Phenotypic changes that occur during endothelial to mesenchymal transition), it is unsurprising that the development of therapeutics targeting EMT is more advanced than for EndMT, with several cancer therapeutics targeting EMT already in clinical use.93,94 For example, imatinib (marketed as Gleevec) is a tyrosine kinase inhibitor that can inhibit EMT via the Notch and TGF-β pathways and is an established treatment for chronic myeloid leukaemia. With inhibition of EMT serving as somewhat of a roadmap for clinical translation, the possibility of targeting EndMT for therapeutic benefit has been touted for over a decade. However, there are unique challenges that need to be addressed before this is a clinical reality. An important and somewhat conceptual challenge is the premise that ‘EndMT is fundamentally bad’, and that its inhibition will be clinically beneficial. As discussed earlier, EndMT is important for neovascularization, angiogenesis, normal valve function, and tissue repair and regeneration.44,47,79 Therefore, the inhibition of EndMT may cause undesirable effects. Additionally, if therapies to inhibit EndMT are administered systemically, there may be side effects in non-target tissues unless highly selective mechanisms or tissue-specific pathways are targeted.

Numerous technical and regulatory challenges must be addressed. First, evidence of causality in appropriate models of disease is essential but sometimes lacking or limited. Second, a therapeutic, e.g. a small molecule or advanced therapy (i.e. RNA therapeutic or gene therapy), would ideally need precise action on the EndMT causal process in the absence of off-target effects, e.g. ensuring no detrimental effects to ECs that are not involved in the EndMT process. Third, precision regarding when and where to target EndMT is of paramount importance due to the heterogenous nature of the process. Fourth, careful identification of appropriate patient populations and clinical study design to monitor the therapeutic effects is necessary.

In terms of potential approaches, because EndMT involves the loss of canonical EC markers and gain of mesenchymal markers, high-throughput screens of millions of small molecules would be realistic in order to identify specific targets of the EndMT process. Approaches might also leverage RNAseq and other high-throughput data to inform repurposing of already approved drugs, as has been successful in other areas.95 Depending on the disease context, systemic delivery will likely be required for conditions such as valvular disease or atherosclerosis. Thus, the parameters and considerations for success will be typical for any drug development, including timing of intervention, off-target effects, and so forth.

For the specific example of valvular disease, the targeting of EndMT and its key regulatory pathways presents a promising therapeutic avenue. Several strategies have been explored, including (i) TGF-β inhibition, pharmacological agents such as losartan and pirfenidone have shown efficacy in reducing EndMT-mediated fibrosis in preclinical models,88 with losartan known to block TGF-β-induced phosphorylation of ERK92 and pirfenidone known to inhibit TGF-β signalling via several mechanisms96; (ii) Wnt/β-catenin modulation, small-molecule inhibitors targeting the Wnt pathway have been proposed to prevent EndMT-induced osteogenesis in CAVD31; (iii) epigenetic regulation, hypermethylation of BMP7, an endogenous EndMT inhibitor, has been implicated in CVD, suggesting that DNA methylation-targeting therapies could offer novel treatment options97; and (iv) endothelial progenitor cell therapy, emerging research suggests that endothelial progenitor cells may have the potential to restore normal endothelial function and counteract EndMT-driven pathology.98 Furthermore, statins improve endothelial function by reducing chromatin accessibility at EndMT-regulating genes in a yes-associated protein (YAP)-dependent manner.34 Although the administration of statins has not been shown to be beneficial in the context of valvular heart disease, it is possible that a component of their ‘pleiotropic beneficial effects’ in atherosclerotic arterial disease may be due to their effect on inhibiting EndMT.34

A potentially unique therapeutic opportunity exists for addressing adverse vascular remodeling with neointimal hyperplasia that arises in the weeks following creation of an arteriovenous fistula (i.e. for dialysis) or arterial bypass grafts using veins, where the development of pathological neointima is the primary cause of the high rate of intermediate phase vein graft stenosis and failure.99 The opportunity for localized advanced therapies to be delivered is significant since the autologous vein, which is still used in the vast majority of bypass procedures, is available ex vivo for therapeutic intervention where RNA- or gene-based therapeutics can be administered during the surgical procedure directly to the vein. A critical feature of this is the need for effective uptake of the therapeutic into ECs within a short time (e.g. 30–60 min) and for the surgical team to dwell the therapeutic for this minimum time to achieve a reduction in EndMT and thus neointima formation. It has been shown that both siRNA100 and gene therapy19 can be delivered effectively, providing the groundwork for clinical trials. Furthermore, other approaches, such as the use of modified RNA, CRISPR technology, or even gene editing to activate or inhibit gene expression, would also be appropriate for this form of localized delivery. These approaches have the benefit of direct access to the target tissue, optimal safety to the patient since the therapeutic agent can be removed from the vein prior to grafting, and reduced manufacturing costs compared to approaches that administer such therapies systemically. Following on from a mouse study showing EndMT plays a major role in neointimal formation following vein graft surgery (LOE5),25 our group recently completed a large animal translational study of SMAD3 inhibition to block EndMT at the time of surgery for the creation of a femoral arteriovenous fistula using a lentiviral gene therapy approach.19 At 30 days post-arteriovenous fistula creation, compared to control lentivirus, the local administration of lentivirus targeting SMAD3 for 1 h to the autologous segment of vein was associated with a significant reduction in neointimal hyperplasia and the degree of graft stenosis, as well as increased EC coverage of the grafted segments.19 There were no systemic effects observed,19 and we are now working to develop a Phase I human clinical study of this approach. Given that the TGF-β pathway inhibitor imatinib is already in clinical use (see above), and because TGF-β signalling is a major regulator of EndMT, we believe that targeting of SMAD3 as an anti-TGF-β strategy is an appropriate initial therapeutic strategy to pursue. A substantial body of research supports the potential for translating endothelial to mesenchymal transition-targeted therapies into clinical applications. However, advancing towards first-in-human studies requires further preclinical validation and optimization and design of optimal therapeutic strategies and formulations, as well as selectively targeting patient populations most likely to benefit while minimizing off-target effects

Limitations of current endothelial to mesenchymal transition research

As discussed, there are numerous challenges and limitations of current EndMT research. Those already mentioned include the lack of a standardized definition of what constitutes EndMT at a molecular level, the current lack of a specific gene ontology term for EndMT, obtaining proof in humans that EndMT is mechanistically involved in the pathophysiology of CVDs (as opposed to EndMT being merely a bystander effect), and a wide range of experimental techniques that have been used to identify EndMT that are likely to be of varying accuracy—an issue we have addressed in this review by creating a LOE scale.

With respect to the potential casualty of EndMT in differing disease states, causality is typically accepted as having been demonstrated following rigorous investigation with multiple lines of evidence, including genetic association, experimental manipulation, and biological plausibility. Certainly, there are a number of studies where EndMT has been blocked or inhibited (i.e. experimental manipulation) in animal models where the outcome indicated a causal role for EndMT. For example, the studies described in Conclusions regarding SMAD3 inhibition to block EndMT during vein graft remodeling in mice and pigs, which led to a significant reduction of neointimal formation,19,25 is evidence for a causal role of EndMT in this scenario (i.e. neointimal formation). Similarly, murine atherosclerosis studies have inhibited specific pathways related to EndMT and demonstrated favourably altered plaque features suggestive of a causal role for EndMT in atherosclerosis.18,22,40 Further, there is biologic plausibility that EndMT plays a causative role in differing disease states, with these arguments being interwoven throughout this review. However, proving the causality of EndMT in humans via genetic association is significantly more challenging. This is mainly because, while genetic association studies can associate differing genes and genetic loci with a disease, they do not provide any information about the mechanistic pathway of that association. To again take SMAD3 as an example, its role in EndMT has been clearly demonstrated.19,25 In addition, in genome-wide association studies (GWAS), single nucleotide polymorphisms associated with the SMAD3 gene have been robustly associated with CAD.101 However, GWAS provide no information about the causal tissue(s) of candidate genes (i.e. the vessel wall, liver, adipose, and inflammatory cells), nor do they provide any information about mechanistic pathways. Therefore, current GWAS are unable to indicate if SMAD3 (or other genes) exert their effects via EndMT. Indeed, while it is possible that this signal for SMAD3 in CAD is related to EndMT, it is also plausible that SMAD3 may exert other effects in CAD, such as direct effects on resident VSMCs.102 As a result of these issues, although there is strong reason to believe EndMT is likely to be causal for the disease states discussed in this review on the basis of biologic plausibility and experimental manipulation in animal models, definitive human proof of causality is challenging to obtain and not yet demonstrated.

Conclusions

Where is the field of EndMT in adult cardiovascular diseases at overall? While a great deal still remains to be learned, for certain states such as atherosclerosis and valve disease, the evidence implicating the role of EndMT is incontrovertible, each with multiple LOE4–5 studies. New tools, particularly Cre-lox lineage tracing and scRNAseq, have been extensively deployed to better understand EndMT and its signalling pathways, which has dramatically accelerated our knowledge gains in this field. Future work should focus on unequivocal, stringent identification of EndMT-positive subsets in scRNAseq data of human disease and murine models. An important tool for the field would be identification of a set of robust EndMT signature genes and a corresponding gene ontology term for EndMT, which should be based on murine reporter datasets with higher cell numbers, and cross-species integration.

A number of challenges remain to be overcome before manipulation of EndMT might be deployed as a clinical therapy, not the least of which includes optimal selection of EndMT-related therapeutic targets, mechanism of EndMT inhibition (e.g. gene therapy), route and timing of administration, mitigation of systemic and off-target effects, durability of effect, and meeting regulatory hurdles. However, these challenges are similar to those faced by most new therapeutic fields in the current era. Given the fact that manipulation of EMT is already being undertaken in the clinic (see Translation of endothelial to mesenchymal transition to the clinic), and that inhibition of EndMT has been demonstrated to have beneficial effects in translational large animal studies,19,88 we expect this field to be the subject of very intense research and scientific progress in the coming years.
