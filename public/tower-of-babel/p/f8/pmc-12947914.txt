Don’t Lose Sleep Over It, Impact of Living With an Ostomy on Sleep Disruptions
Authors: George Skountrianos, Renee Malandrino, Colleen Drolshagen, Rachel Lovegrove
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSE:The purpose of this study was to describe the influence of living with an ostomy on sleep disruptions using data from a self-reported survey and multinational clinical registry of individuals living with an ostomy.DESIGN:Cross-sectional survey and clinical registry.SUBJECTS AND SETTING:Survey data were collected from 5648 respondents living in the US with an ostomy, using any manufacturer’s product; these data were collected from October 2021 to April 2022. In addition, clinical registry data were extracted from 206 respondents living in the UK, US, and Canada, using all data available as of December 2022.METHODS:An electronic survey focusing on sleep was distributed through an industry-sponsored ostomy patient support program, product supply distributor, and ostomy patient association. Participation was anonymous, voluntary, and without compensation. Participants in the multinational registry completed monthly or quarterly electronic questionnaires that included information on sleep quality and sleep disruptions. Inferential analyses were conducted using Fisher exact test and chi-square test (χ2).RESULTS:Nearly half of the Sleep Survey respondents (n = 2676 of 5648, 47%) reported their pouching system had disrupted their main sleep period over the past 30 days. Seventy-five percent (n = 1845) experienced disruptions weekly, and 31% (n = 760) reported nightly sleep disruptions. Common causes included the need to empty a pouch (64%, n = 1722), leakage of effluent (40%, n = 1068), and worry about pouching system leakage (25%, n = 677). Respondents reporting peristomal skin problems were 1.5 times more likely to experience sleep disruptions (P < .001), while individuals with ileostomies were 1.3 and 1.6 times more likely to report disruptions compared to individuals with colostomies and urostomies, respectively (P < .001). The proportion of registry respondents who reported sleep disruptions in the preceding 30 days was 39% (n = 79). A vast majority (96%, n = 76) reported being bothered by these sleep disruptions.CONCLUSIONS:The prevalence of sleep disruptions among individuals living with an ostomy is high, especially for those living with an ileostomy.

INTRODUCTION

Approximately 50 to 70 million persons residing in the US1 and 45 million in Europe2 live with chronic sleep disorders. Sleep is a vital part of our physical and psychological wellbeing, and lack of sleep exerts a negative effect on multiple behaviors, such as decision-making, and emotions.3 Experiencing a lack of sleep places people at a higher risk for a number of health issues, such as heart problems, high blood pressure, obesity, and diabetes mellitus.3

Isik and colleagues evaluated the quality of sleep in a group of 222 participants living with an intestinal ostomy.4 More than half (52%) reported sleep problems and 60% stated that their ostomy negatively influenced sleep quality. In addition, 64% reported frequent interruptions in sleep because of ostomy-related problems.4 A study comparing sleep disturbances between men (n = 168) and women (n = 118) living with an ostomy found that women had significantly more sleep disruptions and greater fatigue than men.5 Nevertheless, both female and male participants reported similar ostomy-related sleep disturbances, such as leakage during the night or changing their ostomy pouching system during their main sleep period.

Kara and Aslan conducted a qualitative study examining the experiences of patients with ostomies (n = 18) on their first day at home following hospital discharge.6 They consistently reported trouble sleeping because of worry about pouching system leaking. Furukawa and colleagues performed a cross-sectional descriptive study that evaluated health-related quality of life (HRQOL) and quality of sleep in 86 adults living with a urostomy in Japan.7 They found that respondents living with a urostomy had poorer sleep quality and lower HRQOL than the general population.

While these studies provide valuable insights into sleep disturbances among individuals living with an ostomy, evidence related to sleep quality in persons living with an ostomy remains limited. The aim of this research was to describe the prevalence of sleep disturbance and identify factors contributing to impaired sleep quality in persons living with an ostomy.

METHODS

Data for this descriptive, cross-sectional study were obtained from 2 sources. The first was a self-reported Sleep Survey designed to understand the prevalence and causes of sleep disruptions among individuals living with an ostomy.8 The second is the Observational Study of Ostomy Consumers (OSO) registry, an observational ostomy clinical registry aimed at collecting information to understand the multidimensional aspects of life with an ostomy, including influence on sleep quality.9

Inclusion criteria for data captured from the Sleep Survey were individuals older than 18 years, residing in the US, living with one or more ostomies (colostomy, ileostomy, or urostomy), and using ostomy products, regardless of the manufacturer. Individuals were identified via the following sources: industry-sponsored ostomy patients support programs, ostomy product supply distributors, and ostomy patients’ associations. Responses were anonymous and participation was voluntary without compensation. Data were collected through a post-market, self-reported survey which did not involve product evaluation or clinician care/participation. The survey protocol, questionnaire, and analysis plan were reviewed and approved by WCG IRB, #20254402, 11/10/2025.

The OSO registry is an ongoing prospective, noninterventional, observational, longitudinal registry collecting self-reported information from individuals living with an ostomy. The overall goal of the OSO registry is to collect longitudinal self-reported data that can provide a resource in better understanding aspects of life with an ostomy. Inclusion and exclusion criteria, recruitment, data analysis, and initial findings have been published previously.9 For purposes of this study, data from the OSO registry were used to explore associations between sleep disturbances, leakage, peristomal skin complications (PSC), and ostomy type (colostomy, ileostomy, urostomy). The OSO registry captures information on the prevalence of sleep disruption and how bothersome these disruptions are to the individual. Questions pertaining to peristomal skin condition and sleep were captured on a 30-day recall basis (ie, “Over the past 30 days, …”).

Registry participants reside in the US, Canada, and UK. They complete monthly or quarterly electronic questionnaires, which include validated HRQOL instruments, self-reported health assessments, and health care resource utilization.9 Study procedures were reviewed and approved by Institutional Review Boards or Human Ethics Committees in the USA and Canada. In addition, the complete study protocol was reviewed and approved by WCG IRB (approval # 20180937, 2/29/2022). Study procedures were reviewed and deemed exempt from individual informed consent in the UK. Finally, study procedures were posted on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03715197).

Sleep Survey Instrument

An 8-question electronic self-reported survey was created to evaluate the prevalence of sleep disruptions and associations between sleep disturbances, PSCs, and ostomy type. Three survey questions were ostomy related (ostomy type, ostomy duration, and peristomal skin condition) and 5 were sleep related (occurrence and frequency of sleep disruptions, ostomy- and product-related causes of sleep disruptions, degree of sleep disruptions, and actions taken to address sleep disruptions). The survey questionnaire did not undergo formal validation and took approximately 5 minutes to complete. Data from participants who had at least one ostomy were analyzed.

Study Procedures and Data Collection

Sleep Survey questionnaires were distributed to individuals with an ostomy living in the US between October 2021 and April 2022. Responses were captured via an electronic cloud-based survey platform (Qualtrics, Provo, UT). Qualtrics is compliant with General Data Protection Regulation10 and the California Consumer Privacy Act.11 Data from the OSO registry were extracted and analyzed from subjects who had provided responses as of December 2022. These responses were captured via an electronic patient reported outcomes system (Medrio ePro, San Francisco, CA). This company’s procedures are compliant with the 21CFR Part 11,12 General Data Protection Regulation,10 and the California Consumer Privacy Act.11

Data Analysis

Survey and registry data analyses were retrospective in nature. Data were summarized using standard descriptive statistics for categorical variables (ie, frequency counts and proportions). Bivariate categorical analyses between ostomy characteristics and sleep disruption variables were also performed. Statistical analyses were conducted using Fisher exact test (FET) or chi-square test (χ2), as appropriate. Relative risks (RR) were used to estimate the effect size between 2 categorical variables. P-values less than or equal to .05 were deemed statistically significant. As the survey and registry study was voluntary and self-reported, missing data occurred. As such, the sample size per question varied; no missing data imputation was performed. Quantitative and free-response data were collected; however, only quantitative results are reported here.

RESULTS

A total of 5648 individuals living with at least one ostomy completed the Sleep Survey. Seventy percent (n = 3931) had lived with an ostomy for 4 years or less and 29% (n = 1649) underwent ostomy surgery less than 1 year prior to data collection (Table 1). The most common ostomy type was colostomy (41%, n = 2312), followed by ileostomy (33%, n = 1883) and urostomy (21%, n = 1175). Five percent of participants (n = 278) were unsure of their ostomy type or whether they had multiple ostomies. Slightly less than half of respondents (42%, n = 2383) indicated their peristomal skin was intact with no signs of redness, rash, blisters, or open sores. The prevalence of sleep disruption that respondents attributed to their ostomy pouching system was 47% (n = 2676, Table 2). Of those individuals, 75% (n = 1845) indicated that sleep disruptions occurred at least once a week, with 31% (n = 760) reporting nightly disruptions. Furthermore, 90% (n = 2163) of the respondents reported that the sleep disruptions were bothersome. Nearly 1 in 5 participants (19%, n = 453) ranked the disruptions as quite or extremely bothersome. As the frequency of disruptions increased, the degree of bother also increased (χ2 test, P < .001). Individuals who reported sleep disruptions 4 to 7 times per week were 4.6 times more likely to state the disruptions were quite or extremely bothersome (RR = 4.6, 95% CI: 3.8 to 5.6, P < .001) relative to those experiencing less frequent disruptions. The top 3 reported events leading to sleep disruption were pouch ballooning requiring a pouch change (64%, n = 1722), pouching system leaks requiring a pouch change (40%, n = 1068), and worry/anxiety that the pouch system will come off or leak (25%, n = 677). DISCUSSION

The findings from this study offers real-world insights on how living with an ostomy affects sleep quality. Across both datasets, approximately 40% to 50% of individuals reported sleep disruptions due to their pouching system. For 90% to 97% of these individuals, the sleep disruptions were bothersome, with 19% to 26% reporting the disruptions are quite or extremely bothersome. Not surprisingly, the frequency of sleep disruptions contributed to the degree of bother. Individuals who reported sleep disruptions 4 to 7 times per week were 4.6 times more likely to state the disruptions were quite or extremely bothersome relative to those experiencing less frequent disruptions.

These results are consistent with prior studies which found that sleep disturbances are common among people living with an ostomy.4,6 The work of Isik and colleagues4 showed that more than half of the individuals with intestinal stomas reported poor sleep, with those living with an ileostomy or temporary ostomy reporting the worst sleep quality. Furthermore, our findings are consistent with those of Kara and Aslan,6 who observed that worry about pouch leakage contributed to sleep disruptions. In addition to reinforcing prior findings, our research expands the existing evidence base by identifying several ostomy-related factors that contribute to the likelihood of sleep disruption. This includes new insights into how factors such as ostomy type, PSC, and time since surgery influence sleep quality, factors which to our knowledge, have not been comprehensively examined in prior studies.

Individuals with ileostomies were significantly more likely to experience sleep disruptions compared to individuals with colostomies or urostomies. Sleep disruption was also more common among individuals with PSC. These outcomes are not unexpected: ileostomies typically produce higher-volume, liquid output, which increases the risk of leakage and, in turn, the likelihood of developing PSC, both of which can interfere with sleep. Peristomal skin complications themselves may contribute directly to poor sleep through pain, itching, or burning, and can also undermine the ability to maintain a secure barrier seal.

In the Sleep Survey, we found that individuals living with an ostomy less than one year were 1.2 times more likely to experience sleep disruptions compared to individuals with a stoma duration of greater than a year. Ostomy duration was not found to be a significant risk factor in the OSO registry, due to limited sample size, which required broader duration categories. Specifically, there were not enough individuals living with an ostomy for less than one year for adequate analysis, thus requiring us to compare participants with durations less than three years to those with durations greater than three years. Nevertheless, we observed a higher prevalent of sleep disruptions among participants who had lived with an ostomy for less than three years when compared to those living with an ostoy for more than three years (46% vs 35%).

In addition to ostomy-related risk factors, we found that the pouching system also plays a role in sleep disruptions. The most commonly reported pouching system-related events leading to sleep disruption were pouch ballooning requiring a pouch change (64%), pouching system leaks requiring a pouch change (40%), and worry/anxiety that the pouch system will come off or leak (25%). While pouch ballooning may be addressed through diet, addressing leakage is critical. Numerous publications report ostomy leakage has a profound psychological impact on individuals living with an ostomy, often leading to emotional distress and diminished self-esteem.13-15 Our findings strengthen this body of work by further expanding the impact of leakage to sleep disruption. The Sleep Survey revealed that both pouching system leaks and the psychological worry/anxiety of leakage occurring contribute to impaired sleep quality.

Despite the high prevalence and consequences of sleep disruption, 40% of participants indicated they did nothing to resolve the issue. Rather, only 10% sought help from a health care provider. We therefore recommend that WOC nurses and other clinicians routinely ask patients about their quality of sleep, including pointed conversations about pouching options, sleep patterns, habits, type and quantity of output, and challenges. Given that peristomal skin health directly influences HRQOL, including sleep, we strongly recommend that clinicians address skin health proactively and immediately. Other strategies can also be considered such as laughter yoga. Harputlu and colleagues16 who found that that laughter yoga significantly improved sleep among individuals with fecal ostomies, suggesting that supportive interventions beyond pouching modifications may have value.

Strengths and Limitations

The Sleep Survey included over 5700 participants residing in the US, allowing robust analysis of quality of sleep among individuals living with ostomies, regardless of ostomy product or manufacturer. The OSO registry further added strength by enabling exploration of sleep disturbances in relation to ostomy type and skin complications in an international population. These consistent findings across 2 independent sources strengthen the importance of ostomy-related sleep quality. While the Sleep Survey and OSO registry offer valuable insights into sleep issues among individuals with ostomies, some considerations remain. The Sleep Survey’s US-based sample may limit broader generalizability, and the OSO registry’s international reach, though promising, is still developing. Additionally, both studies relied on cross-sectional data, closed-ended questions. Finally, we did not use a validated quality of sleep assessment tool.

CONCLUSIONS

Research findings suggest that living with an ostomy may lead to sleep disruptions with individuals with an ileostomy being impacted the greatest. Skin health and the presence of PSC directly contribute to poor quality of sleep. Furthermore, individuals living with an ostomy for less than one year are more likely to experience sleep disruptions. Health care providers caring for individuals living with an ostomy are encouraged to recognize potential sleep disturbances and proactively address them with their patients.
