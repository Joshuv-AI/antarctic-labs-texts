Infection and resistance mechanisms of phage vB_VpM-PP270 in Vibrio parahaemolyticus via capsular polysaccharide
Authors: Zhu Xihui, Hong Junshan, Xu Jiayin, Tang Luqi, Li Pei, Jiang Wei, Zhang Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryVibrio parahaemolyticus is a major foodborne pathogen that threatens public health and aquaculture, necessitating alternative biocontrol strategies such as phage therapy. This study introduces phage vB_VpM-PP270, isolated from aquaculture wastewater, and explores its infection mechanism targeting V. parahaemolyticus VP270. The research highlights capsular polysaccharide (CPS) as the primary receptor for phage adsorption and identifies ORF22, a tail spike protein, as the receptor-binding protein. PP270 demonstrates stability across a wide pH range and temperatures, effectively inhibiting VP270 growth in culture and on chilled shrimp. Phage-resistant mutants show mutations in CPS biosynthesis genes, which reduce phage adsorption and promote biofilm formation. These findings provide insights into phage-host interactions and offer a framework for developing phage therapies resilient to resistance, with significant implications for food safety and sustainable aquaculture practices.

Introduction

V. parahaemolyticus is a major bacterial pathogen that causes widespread infections and fatalities in aquaculture species, leading to substantial economic losses in the industry.1 Certain strains of V. parahaemolyticus also cause human diseases, including diarrhea, fever, and vomiting.2 The overuse of antibiotics has led to increasing resistance in V. parahaemolyticus,3 highlighting the urgent need for green and safe alternatives. Phages are among the most abundant biological entities in nature,4 are considered promising antimicrobial agents due to their specific host recognition and lytic properties, and have been extensively researched and applied.

Phages can specifically recognize and rapidly lyse host bacteria; however, bacteria may develop resistance over time, significantly reducing the efficacy of phage therapy. Bacteria have evolved various defense mechanisms to block phage infections, which can occur at multiple stages of the infection process. The F plasmid in Escherichia coli encodes the outer membrane protein TraT, which can alter the conformation of OmpA, preventing phage infection that uses OmpA as a receptor.5 The Vibrio cholerae phage ICP2 uses OmpU as a receptor to initiate adsorption. When the ompU gene is mutated, altering the receptor structure, ICP2 can no longer infect V. cholerae.6 CPS on the surface of Staphylococcus strains prevent the adsorption of phage U16, while the cell wall and teichoic acid components inactivate it.7 Bacteria can produce microcin J25, which competes with phages for binding to the FhuA receptor on E. coli, thereby reducing phage adsorption.8 Additionally, bacteria can utilize systems such as Superinfection Exclusion (Sie) and CRISPR-Cas to defend against phage invasion.9

A key strategy among bacterial resistance mechanisms is the alteration of phage adsorption receptors on the bacterial surface. Phage attachment to the bacterial surface begins when the RBP at the tail tip binds to receptors on the host cell surface. The main types of adsorption receptors include pili, flagella, lipopolysaccharides (LPS), CPS, and outer membrane proteins (OMPs).10 CPS is an extracellular polysaccharide that provides bacterial protection and is associated with virulence.11 Several studies have identified CPS as a phage receptor. For example, the E. coli phage PNJ1809-36 uses the K1-type capsule as an adsorption receptor.12 The adsorption receptor for the Clostridium perfringens phage CPS1, a member of the Picovirinae, has been identified as CPS.13 Infection by Acinetobacter baumannii phage APK37.1 requires an intact K3-type capsular polysaccharide. A reduction in branched sugars leads to failure of APK37.1 infection.14 Despite growing interest in phage therapy for Vibrio pathogens, comprehensive studies integrating the discovery of new phages with receptor identification and detailed analysis of resistance mechanisms remain scarce. Therefore, we aim to isolate phages to control V. parahaemolyticus infections, study the molecular mechanisms of resistance, and identify the phage RBP and adsorption receptor, providing data for biological control strategies. These findings not only fill a key knowledge gap in Vibrio phage biology but also inform the rational design of phage-based strategies for food safety and sustainable aquaculture

Results

Results of phage isolation, identification, and biological characterization

Electron microscopy revealed that PP270 lacks elongated tail fibers, instead possessing a baseplate with a cell-puncturing protein for genome injection and a tail spike protein for host recognition (Figure 1A). The optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI) for PP270 was determined to be 0.1 (Figure 1B), under which condition PP270 significantly suppressed the growth of V. parahaemolyticus in culture medium within 140 min (Figure 1C). Thermal stability assays demonstrated that PP270 retained high activity across temperatures from 4°C to 50°C, with a marked reduction in activity following 1 h incubation at 60°C and complete inactivation at 70°C (Figure 1D). PP270 exhibited broad pH tolerance, maintaining activity from pH 3 to 11 (Figure 1E), with elevated titers in alkaline conditions (pH 8–11) relative to neutral pH, whereas titers declined in acidic environments (pH 3–6). Low-temperature bacteriostatic assays showed that PP270 substantially inhibited V. parahaemolyticus growth on refrigerated shrimp meat surfaces at 24 and 48 h (Figure 1F). Results of genome analysis

The genome of PP270 comprises 39,329 bp and encodes 58 open reading frames (ORFs), of which 30 have assigned functions, while the remaining 28 encode proteins of unknown function (Figure 2A). Among the 30 annotated proteins, 21 contribute to phage structural components, including the head, neck, and tail, whereas the other 9 are key enzymes and regulators, such as endonucleases, exonucleases, lysozymes, metallopeptidases, Lambda repressor proteins, and Rha family phage regulators. BLASTn alignment of the PP270 genome against phage sequences in the NCBI NR database identified the highest similarities to four Vibrio phages, with sequence identities ranging from 84.22% to 93.88% and coverage of 71%–74%; the closest relative was Vibrio phage XM1 (GenBank: MT720689.1), sharing 93.88% identity but only 71% coverage. Average nucleotide identity (ANI) values for these four phages ranged from 83.8 to 90.9, with the maximum observed for XM1 (Figure 2B). Genomic colinearity analysis also revealed that PP270 exhibits consistent or similar gene order and structure with multiple regions of four closely related phage genomes, showing the highest similarity to the XM1 genome (Figure 2C). All four phages are members of the class Caudoviricetes (tailed phages) with unclassified genera and species; accordingly, PP270 is assigned to Caudoviricetes. Screening and phenotypic characterization of phage-resistant bacteria

The co-cultivation of V. parahaemolyticus VP270 with PP270 on LB agar for 12 h yielded numerous small white colonies (Figure 3A). These colonies were serially passaged on TCBS agar to assess phage sensitivity, resulting in the isolation of 12 resistant mutants. The mutants were categorized into two groups (Figure 3B): six strains (KR1, KR2, KR6, KR8, KR10, KR12) formed dry, transparent colonies and exhibited complete phage resistance (KR type); the remaining six (KS1, KS5, KS7, KS13, KS19, KS20) produced moist, opaque colonies akin to the wild type and showed partial resistance (KS type), as PP270 formed diffuse plaques on these strains. Growth curve analyses indicated slower proliferation rates for both KR and KS type mutants compared to VP270 (Figure 3C), potentially reflecting fitness costs associated with resistance. Bacteriostatic assays confirmed that PP270 inhibited VP270 growth within 8 h during liquid co-incubation but had negligible effects on the mutants (Figure 3C), validating their phage-resistant status.Figure 3Screening results of phage-resistant bacteria(A) Colony morphology of resistant mutants on phage double-layer plates.(B) Phage susceptibility testing of resistant mutants.(C) Growth curve measurement of resistant mutants under PP270 lysis.Data are displayed as the means ± SD from three independent experiments.

Resistant mutants were cultured on Congo red and LB agar for 24–36 h to evaluate colony morphology. Notable differences were observed relative to the wild type (Figure 4A): wild-type colonies displayed an opaque (OP) phenotype, characterized by thick, moist, viscous morphology; KR type mutants exhibited a transparent (TR) phenotype with thin, dry colonies; and KS type mutants showed a semi-transparent phenotype intermediate between OP and TR. Such OP to TR shifts is frequently linked to CPS deficiency or reduction. Congo red staining revealed diminished red-staining areas in both KR and KS type colonies compared to the wild type, indicative of reduced polysaccharide production (Figure 4A). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) demonstrated irregular, rough surfaces on wild-type VP270 cells, contrasted by smooth surfaces in KR-type mutants and reduced, wrinkled roughness in KS-type mutants (Figure 4B), likely attributable to decreased surface polysaccharide secretion. Phage adsorption assays revealed significantly lower adsorption rates for KR and KS type mutants versus wild-type VP270 (Figures 4C and 4D), suggesting alterations in surface receptors that impair phage binding. The treatment of VP270 with 100 mM sodium periodate markedly reduced adsorption rates, implying carbohydrate components in the receptor, whereas proteinase K treatment had no effect, excluding protein involvement (Figure 4E). Analysis and validation of resistance-associated mutations

Whole-genome sequencing of the 12 resistant mutants, referenced against the VP270 genome, was conducted to identify causative mutations, prioritizing variants common to all KR or KS type strains. Mutations in the ugd gene (encoding UDP-glucose dehydrogenase) were universal in KR type mutants (Figure 5): single-nucleotide substitution in KR1, deletions in KR2, KR8, KR10, and KR12, and a 6-bp insertion in KR6 (resulting in the insertion of two amino acids). KS-type mutants harbored mutations in two glycosyltransferase genes: single-nucleotide deletions in gtr2 (KS1, KS5, KS7), a 10-bp deletion in gtr (KS13), and single-nucleotide substitutions in gtr (KS19, KS20). The CPS biosynthesis cluster in V. parahaemolyticus (K-antigen locus, GenBank: GCF_000196095.1 VP0215–VP0244) is well-characterized and unrelated to O-antigen synthesis. To elucidate the roles of ugd, gtr, and gtr2 in resistance, we generated deletion mutants (Δugd, Δgtr, Δgtr2) and complemented strains (Δugd: ugd, Δgtr: gtr, Δgtr2: gtr2). Spot assays showed no plaque formation on deletion mutants with PP270, indicating resistance, whereas complemented strains formed plaques indistinguishable from the wild type (Figure 6A), confirming these genes influence phage susceptibility. Relative to the moist, smooth OP phenotype of wild-type VP270, Δugd displayed a dry, rough TR phenotype, while Δgtr and Δgtr2 exhibited intermediate morphologies; complementation restored the wild-type OP phenotype (Figure 6B). Congo red assays indicated reduced red-staining areas in deletion mutant colonies, reflecting diminished polysaccharide production, with restoration in complemented strains (Figure 6C), suggesting these genes regulate extracellular carbohydrate synthesis. Adsorption assays demonstrated decreased rates in deletion mutants, recovered upon complementation (Figure 6D), linking gene function to receptor biosynthesis. Validation of phage adsorption receptor and receptor-binding protein

The co-incubation of PP270 with VP270-derived CPS significantly reduced phage adsorption rates, unlike VP255-derived CPS, which matched PBS controls (Figure 7A), indicating competitive, host-specific binding by VP270 CPS. Bioinformatic analysis annotated ORF22 as a tail spike protein with head-binding (InterPro: PF09008) and carbohydrate-binding (InterPro: IPR036573) domains, which was expressed and purified. Incubation with VP270 reduced subsequent phage adsorption compared to PBS controls, but not with an unrelated TF protein (Figure 7B), demonstrating ORF22 binding to surface receptors and implicating it as the receptor-binding protein (RBP) of PP270. To assess ORF22-CPS interaction, ORF22 was pre-incubated with VP270 CPS before application to VP270 cells. Adsorption rates increased relative to PBS and VP255 CPS controls (Figure 7C), confirming CPS blockade of ORF22 binding sites and direct CPS-ORF22 interaction. Collectively, these results establish ORF22 as the RBP of PP270, CPS as its key receptor on VP270, and phage adsorption as mediated by ORF22-CPS binding.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to isolate and characterize a vibrio phage targeting V. parahaemolyticus, while elucidating the molecular mechanisms of infection and host resistance. Key findings demonstrate that PP270 constitutes a new species in the class Caudoviricetes, infecting VP270 via specific recognition of CPS by its tail spike protein ORF22. Resistance arises from mutations in CPS biosynthesis genes (ugd, gtr, gtr2), which modify host surface features and augment biofilm formation.

Tail spike proteins commonly serve as RBP for polysaccharide receptors; the short-tailed morphology of PP270, coupled with the carbohydrate-binding module in ORF22, promotes efficient CPS adsorption and enables rapid lysis at an optimal MOI of 0.1. The absence of integrases or lysogeny-associated genes in the PP270 genome affirms its obligately lytic lifestyle. PP270 shares substantial sequence similarity with Vibrio phages such as XM115 but diverges in genomic coverage and ORF arrangement, particularly RBP sequences, reflecting evolutionary tailoring of tail structures (based on BLASTn analyses; data not shown). Its robust stability over a wide pH range (3–11) and temperatures (up to 60°C) supports adaptation to aquaculture settings, as illustrated by the effective inhibition of VP270 on frozen shrimp meat. Repeated pH stability assessments consistently revealed elevated titers in alkaline conditions, contrasting with typical phage profiles; this phenomenon may stem from unidentified proteins or mechanisms, warranting further exploration to uncover the basis of PP270’s alkaline affinity. CPS gene mutations underpin the resistance mechanism by impairing receptor accessibility, yielding reduced adsorption rates (from ∼90% in wild-type strains to <20% in mutants) and colony phenotype transitions from opaque to semi-transparent, attributable to diminished extracellular polysaccharide output. The concomitant biofilm enhancement (up to 2.5-fold) implies a fitness trade-off, bolstering survival in adverse conditions and aligning with phage-host evolutionary dynamics. Prior research indicates that CPS abundance suppresses biofilm formation,16 corroborating our data on CPS reduction and biofilm upregulation. Competitive binding experiments substantiated CPS as the principal receptor, with host-derived CPS inhibiting adsorption in a specific manner, unlike non-host CPS, underscoring the structural precision of ORF22-CPS interactions.

Colony transparency in Vibrio spp. has long been linked to CPS levels.17 The deletion of ugd in Vibrio alginolyticus yields thinner colonies on Congo red agar and CPS deficits,18 mirroring the morphology of our ugd deletion strains and implying a conserved role in CPS biosynthesis for V. parahaemolyticus. Mutations in CPS genes wcaJ or wbaZ confer phage resistance in carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae.19 In Acinetobacter baumannii XH198, the glycosyltransferase gtr9—a putative CPS synthesis protein in the type 3 locus—prevents infection by phage Phab24 upon mutation or deletion, concurrently diminishing adsorption rates, biofilm formation, and virulence.20 Similarly, gtr2 in Enterococcus faecalis EFa02 contributes to CPS assembly, with mutations causing CPS loss and abrogating adsorption by phage EFap02.21 These observations accord with our evidence that disruptions in the three CPS genes elicit CPS-dependent resistance. While CPS synthesis genes function as receptor determinants in diverse bacteria, investigations into their involvement in V. parahaemolyticus phage resistance remain scarce.

Phage-host interplay is frequently framed as an evolutionary arms race,22,23 wherein bacteria acquire anti-phage defenses24 and phages adapt via RBP modifications for reinfection.25 Such reciprocal evolution fosters biodiversity in both entities. Phage resistance poses a major barrier to therapeutic deployment; although cocktails can delay emergence,26 complete prevention is elusive,27 and broad-spectrum applications risk off-target impacts and unintended resistance.28 Thus, dissecting resistance mechanisms illuminates evolutionary drivers in phage-bacteria systems and furnishes insights for overcoming application hurdles.

In conclusion, this work positions PP270 as a lytic phage employing CPS-mediated infection and charts resistance trajectories in V. parahaemolyticus, addressing lacunae in phage-host molecular dialogues. The enhanced comprehension of resistance can inform phage cocktail formulations to curb adaptive responses, bolstering food safety strategies. Prospective integration into polyphage regimens holds potential to transform Vibrio management, alongside deeper scrutiny of CPS diversity across Vibrio taxa for refined phage utility.

Limitations of the study

This study provides valuable insights into the infection mechanisms and resistance of V. parahaemolyticus phage vB_VpM-PP270. However, there are several aspects that warrant further investigation. First, the study focuses on a single phage strain, and future research could explore a broader spectrum of phages to provide a more comprehensive understanding of phage-host interactions. Additionally, while key mutations associated with phage resistance have been identified, other potential genetic factors contributing to resistance remain unexplored. Lastly, although the experiments were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, the application of this phage in aquaculture environments will be further validated in future studies.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study utilized Vibrio parahaemolyticus VP270 and its phage PP270 as the core microbial chassis. Genetically engineered derivatives included three gene-deletion (VP270Δugd, VP270Δgtr, VP270Δgtr2) and three gene-complementation (VP270Δugd:ugd, VP270Δgtr:gtr, VP270Δgtr2:gtr2) strains. All bacterial strains are detailed in the key resources table.

Method details

Bacterial strains, plasmids, and growth conditions

The strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in key resources table. VP270 was cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB, Huankai, China) medium containing 3% NaCl. All LB media used in this study for culturing V. parahaemolyticus contain 3% NaCl, which will not be specifically mentioned thereafter. Thiosulfate Citrate Bile Salts Sucrose Agar (TCBS, Huankai, China) was used for selective culturing of Vibrio species. E. coli was cultured in standard LB medium with antibiotics added as required. The E. coli CC118 λpir strain carrying the suicide plasmid pYAK1 and the HB101 strain carrying the conjugative transfer helper plasmid pRK2013 were used for gene knockout. For gene complementation, the CC118 λpir strain carrying pMMB207 and the HB101 strain carrying pRK2013 were used. Protein expression was performed in BL21 (DE3) cells carrying the pCold-TF plasmid. Antibiotic concentrations were as follows: chloramphenicol, 10 μg/ml; kanamycin, 100 μg/ml; ampicillin, 100 μg/ml.

Isolation, identification, and biological characterization of phages

The phage isolation and purification method is based on the commonly used double-layer agar plate technique with slight modifications29,30,31 samples were centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 minutes to remove impurities, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove bacteria. The filtered supernatant was then mixed with 3 mL of 2×LB medium and incubated with V. parahaemolyticus at a 1:100 ratio. The mixture was cultured at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm for 6-8 hours. The culture was then centrifuged, filtered, and 3 μl of the filtrate was dropped onto LB solid medium pre-inoculated with V. parahaemolyticus. After incubation at 37°C for 6-8 hours, the appearance of plaques indicated the presence of a phage. The phage was subsequently purified using the double-layer plaque method until uniform plaque morphology was achieved. A 20 μl aliquot of purified phage liquid was applied to copper grids, dried, and negatively stained with 2% PTA for 30–60 seconds. Phage morphology was observed and recorded using an H_7650 transmission electron microscope.

The methods for determining the optimal MOI and stability assay for phage were based on the approach described by Li et al.32 PP270 was added to 6 mL of VP270 (5.0×10ˆ8 CFU/mL) at MOI values of 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001. After incubation at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm for 10 hours, the mixture was centrifuged at 4°C, 5000 g for 5 minutes, filtered, and the phage titer was measured. The MOI yielding the highest titer was considered optimal. To test the ability of PP270 to inhibit V. parahaemolyticus in culture, VP270 and PP270 were added to a 96-well plate containing 200 μl LB medium at an MOI of 0.1 and incubated at 37°C, 200 rpm. The control group contained only VP270. OD600 was measured every 10 minutes, and the variation curve was plotted. Temperature stability was tested by adding 100 μl of PP270 (1×10ˆ8 PFU/mL) to 900 μL SM buffer and incubating at 25°C, 37°C, 50°C, 60°C, and 70°C for 1 hour. Low-temperature stability was tested at 4°C, and the phage titer was measured using soft-agar overlay method (0.5% agar in the top layer). The pH stability test involved adding 100 μl of PP270 (1×10ˆ8 PFU/mL) to SM buffer at pH values ranging from 2 to 12, followed by incubation at room temperature for 1 hour and titer measurement. The biocontrol effects of phages on shrimp samples were assessed using the method outlined by Dalene et al.,33 with modifications. For antibacterial tests, several equal-sized Litopenaeus vannamei shrimp were de-shelled, cut into small pieces, soaked in 75% ethanol for 10 minutes, washed with PBS three times, and irradiated with UV for 20 minutes to evaporate the alcohol. The sterilized shrimp pieces were placed in sterile 24-well plates. 100 μl of VP270 was applied to the shrimp surface, followed by 100 μl of PP270 after a 10-minute incubation at 37°C. The control group received 100 μl of PBS. After 24 and 48 hours, the shrimp were washed with sterile PBS, and the eluted bacteria were counted.

Phage genomic sequencing analysis

Methods for phage genome extraction and sequencing are as described above.31 phage genomic DNA was extracted using the λ phage genome extraction kit. Fresh phage-enriched liquid (phage titer ≥1×10ˆ9 PFU/mL) was used for extraction. Genomic samples were sent to Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd. for sequencing. Phage genome sequencing was performed using the Illumina HiSeq platform, and sequencing reads were assembled using Spades 3.11.1.34 BLASTn was used to compare the PP270 genome with the NCBI NR database to identify homologous phages. DiGAlign35 was used to visualize the genome similarity between PP270 and highly homologous phages. ANI values between PP270 and related phages were calculated using ANIclustermap (https://github.com/moshi4/ANIclustermap). GeneMarkS36 was used to predict ORFs, and blastx was employed to retrieve homologous proteins from the NCBI NR database for functional annotation. The InterPro database37 was used to annotate functional domains of key proteins, particularly tail fiber proteins or tail spike proteins. The genome and annotations were uploaded to the Proksee website38 for genomic annotation mapping.

Screening and phenotypic detection of phage resistance mutants

The isolation of phage-resistant mutants was based on the method described by Li et al. with minor modifications,39 100 μl of PP270 and 100 μl of V. parahaemolyticus VP270 were mixed in 5 mL of LB medium (0.5% agar) at approximately 50°C. The mixture was poured onto sterile LB solid medium and incubated at 37°C for 12 hours. If single colonies appeared, they were passaged five times on TCBS medium to ensure genetic inheritance of the mutation. After passaging, resistant V. parahaemolyticus was streaked onto LB solid medium and dried. 3 μl of PP270 was applied to the surface, and the mixture was incubated at 37°C for 6 hours. If the plaques disappeared or became turbid, the strain was considered a phage-resistant mutant. To further confirm resistance, we tested the ability of PP270 to inhibit the growth of resistant mutants in LB liquid medium. 200 μl of LB was added to a 96-well plate, followed by VP270 and resistant mutants at an MOI of 0.1, and incubation at 37°C, 200 rpm for 8 hours. OD600 was measured every 30 minutes, and the OD600 variation curve was plotted. The control group contained wild-type V. parahaemolyticus VP270 and PP270. A bacterial growth control group without PP270 was also set up.

To observe colony morphology changes in resistant mutants, VP270 or resistant mutants were streaked onto LB solid medium and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. Photographs of the colonies were taken. Congo red binds to bacterial extracellular polysaccharides, giving colonies a red color that correlates with polysaccharide production.40 10 μl of wild-type or resistant mutant bacterial liquid was added to LB solid medium containing 40 μg/ml Congo red and incubated at 37°C for 48 hours. The intensity of red pigmentation and size of the colony center were observed. To further observe morphological changes, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed. After centrifuging the bacterial liquid and discarding the medium, the sample was washed with PBS, centrifuged again, and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at room temperature for 30 minutes. The sample was stored at 4°C and sent to Wuhan Kairino Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for SEM analysis.

To determine if resistance is related to changes in adsorption receptors, we measured the adsorption rate of resistant mutants. The measurement of phage adsorption rate was performed according to the method of Fan et al. with slight modifications.41 2 ml centrifuge tubes were prepared, and 100 μl of PP270 (1×10ˆ8 PFU/ml) and 900 μl of VP270 (1×10ˆ9 CFU/ml) were added. After mixing, the tubes were incubated at 37°C for 10 minutes, then the samples were immediately placed on ice following incubation to halt phage adsorption prior to centrifugation, followed by centrifugation at 7000 g for 4 minutes. 100 μl of the supernatant was used to calculate the phage titer. Non-adsorption controls were prepared by mixing 900 μl LB liquid medium with 100 μl PP270. The adsorption rate was calculated as(PFULB-PFUtreat)/PFULB, with three repetitions for each group. The detection method for phage receptor types was based on the approach described by Kiljunen et al. with minor modifications.42 Bacterial cultures were resuspended in 100 mM NaIO4 (dissolved in 50 mM sodium acetate) and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 2 hours. PBS was used as a control. The adsorption rate after treatment was measured. Additionally, to examine whether protease K treatment affects phage adsorption, protease K was added to bacterial cultures at a final concentration of 20 μg/ml, and incubation was performed at 37°C for 2 hours. After washing with PBS, the adsorption rate was tested, with PBS as a control.

Analysis of resistant mutants

The genome of the resistant mutant strain was sequenced using the Illumina platform. Total DNA was extracted from the mutant strain using a bacterial genome extraction kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). The genomic DNA was fragmented to 350 bp by sonication, and sequencing adapters for Illumina sequencing were ligated. The samples were PCR amplified, purified using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, USA), and the library quality was assessed on the Agilent 5400 system (Agilent, USA). Qualified libraries were sequenced on the Illumina platform with PE150 reads. Sequencing was conducted by Novogene Technology Co., Ltd. Fastp (version 0.23.1)43 was used to assess the quality of the raw reads and remove low-quality sequences, ensuring high-quality reads for further analysis. To obtain the complete reference genome, the wild-type V. parahaemolyticus VP270 genome was sequenced on both the Pacific Biosciences platform (PacBio, Menlo Park, CA) and the Illumina platform. The reads were assembled using Falcon software (version 0.3.0, https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/FALCON/), followed by error correction using Racon (version 1.4.13, https://github.com/isovic/racon) and Pilon (version 1.22).44 The genome sequencing and assembly were performed by Novogene Technology Co., Ltd.

To identify specific mutation loci in the resistant mutants, the sequencing reads from the resistant mutants were compared to the VP270 genome uploaded to the BV-BRC website.45 The variant analysis function of BV-BRC was used to identify mutation genes in the resistant mutants. The parameters used included uploading paired-end sequencing reads from the resistant mutants as the comparison library and using the wild-type V. parahaemolyticus VP270 genome as the reference genome. Sequence alignment was performed using BWA-mem,46 and mutation loci were identified using FreeBayes.47 Mutations appearing in multiple resistant mutant genomes were considered potential candidates related to phage sensitivity. blastx was used for functional annotation of the mutation genes.

Construction of gene-deletion strains and phenotypic assays

Gene deletions were generated by homologous recombination. Using the V. parahaemolyticus VP270 genome as template and primers (Table S1), the upstream and downstream homology arms of each target gene were amplified and cloned into the suicide vector pYAK1. The recombinant plasmid was transformed into E. coli CC118 λpir, and positive clones were selected on LB agar containing 10 μg/mL chloramphenicol. PCR-verified clones served as conjugation donors. Donor CC118 λpir(pYAK1) and helper HB101(pRK2013) were cultured in LB with chloramphenicol (10 μg/mL) or kanamycin (100 μg/mL), respectively, at 37°C and 200 rpm for 6–8 h; the recipient V. parahaemolyticus VP270 was cultured in LB at 37°C and 200 rpm for 4–5 h. 1 ml of each culture was pelleted, washed in LB to remove antibiotics, and resuspended in 200 μL LB. The suspensions were combined, washed, resuspended to 200 μL, and spotted onto a 0.22-μm membrane placed on BHI agar. After incubation at 37°C for 12 h, cells were eluted with 4 mL LB, washed, concentrated to 300 μL, and 200 μL was spread onto TCBS agar containing 10 μg/mL chloramphenicol. Following incubation at 37°C for 12 h, colonies were transferred to LB containing 20% sucrose and incubated at 37°C for 18–24 h. Colonies from sucrose plates were inoculated into LB broth (37°C, 200 rpm, 6 h), and PCR with primers was used to confirm deletion (amplicons smaller than wild type). Deletion strains were repeatedly passaged on 20% sucrose plates to promote loss of the suicide vector until the sacB gene was no longer detectable, yielding stable mutants.

For complementation, the corresponding gene fragment was cloned into pMMB207 and transformed into CC118 λpir. PCR-verified, sequence-confirmed clones served as donors. Donor, helper, and the deletion mutant were mated on BHI agar to introduce recombinant pMMB207 into the mutant, and complemented strains were selected on TCBS agar containing chloramphenicol.

Phage sensitivity was assessed by spot assay. Lawns of deletion and complemented strains on LB agar were air-dried, 3 μL PP270 was spotted, and plates were incubated at 37°C for 6–8 h to evaluate plaque loss or turbidity. To test whether the targeted genes affect receptor synthesis, phage adsorption was measured by mixing 900 μL of each strain with 100 μL PP270, incubating at 37°C for 10 min (LB-only control in parallel), washing, and determining the residual phage titer in the supernatant to calculate adsorption. Colony morphology was examined after streaking strains on LB agar and incubating at 37°C for 48 h. For Congo red assays, 10 μL of culture was spotted onto LB agar containing 40 μg/mL Congo red, incubated at 37°C for 48 h, and the intensity and diameter of the central red zone were recorded.

Because biofilm formation depends on carbohydrate synthesis and underpins colonization and infection, biofilm production by deletion strains was quantified. Measurement of biofilm formation following the method of Srdjan et al.48 In sterile 96-well plates, 200 μL LB per well was inoculated 1:100 and incubated statically at 37°C for 48 h. Wells were aspirated, washed three times with PBS, fixed with 100 μL methanol for 15 min, air-dried, stained with 1% crystal violet for 10 min, rinsed under running water, dried at 37°C, and destained with 100 μL 33% acetic acid for 30 min at 37°C. Absorbance at 595 nm (A595) was recorded. Each strain was tested in triplicate wells.

CPS competitive binding assay

CPS from host strain VP270 and non-host strain VP255 were extracted and quantified as described by Li et al. (2024). The CPS competitive binding assay method was performed according to the approach described by Ge et al.49 PP270 (100 μL, 10ˆ8 PFU/mL) was mixed with 900 μL CPS (100 mg/mL) from VP270 or VP255 and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. For the control, 900 μL PBS replaced CPS. Phage adsorption was measured after incubation.

Identification and validation of the phage receptor-binding protein (RBP)

Among 21 annotated structural proteins, ORF22 was annotated as a tail spike protein and considered a putative RBP because it contains a tail spike head-binding domain (PFAM09008) and a carbohydrate-binding module (IPR036573). Using the VP270 genome as template and primers, ORF22 with vector homology arms was amplified and cloned into the pCold-TF expression vector (Takara, China) to generate pCold-TF-ORF22, which was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3). Expression of ORF22–TF–His (bearing a Trigger Factor solubility tag and 6×His) was induced with 0.2 mM IPTG at 16°C for 12 h; the empty pCold-TF vector was expressed in parallel to produce TF–His. Cells were harvested, resuspended in bacterial lysis buffer (ACE Biotechnology, China), incubated at 4°C for 5–10 min, and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min. The supernatant was incubated with 1 mL His-tag magnetic beads (Yisheng, China) at 4°C for 1 h with gentle mixing. Beads were washed with buffer containing 20 mM imidazole, and bound proteins were eluted with buffer containing 250 mM imidazole. To remove imidazole, eluates were concentrated in 30-kDa MWCO ultrafiltration devices (Amicon, Germany) at 4,000 g for 20 min, buffer-exchanged into PBS, and washed three times. Protein purity was assessed by SDS–PAGE, and concentrations were determined using a BCA kit (Epizyme, China).

To test whether ORF22 functions as an RBP, following the method of Li et al., a protein competition assay was performed using purified ORF22 protein.39 500 μL ORF22–TF–His or TF–His (1 mg/mL) was mixed with 900 μL VP270 and incubated at 37°C for 15 min. Cells were washed three times with PBS, resuspended to 900 μL, and challenged with 100 μL PP270. After 10 min at 37°C, cultures were centrifuged and the supernatant titer was determined to calculate adsorption. To further evaluate the binding of ORF22 to the VP270 CPS, we followed the approach described by Ge et al. with minor modifications.49 CPS was preincubated with ORF22–TF–His and the mixture used to block receptor-binding sites on VP270 prior to adsorption measurement; the control used PBS mixed with ORF22. Specifically, 500 μL VP270 CPS (50 mg/mL) was combined with 500 μL ORF22–TF–His (1 mg/mL) and incubated at 37°C for 15 min, followed by addition of 900 μL VP270 and a further 15-min incubation at 37°C. Cells were washed, resuspended to 900 μL, and phage adsorption was quantified.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data are presented as means±the standard deviations(s.d.) from a minimum of three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was carried out using Student t tests or one-way ANOVA using GraphPad Prism v10 software. P ≤ 0.05 was considered significant. Significance is indicated in the figures by asterisks (∗: P ≤ 0.05; ∗∗: P ≤ 0.01; ∗∗∗: P ≤ 0.001;∗∗∗∗: P ≤ 0.0001).
