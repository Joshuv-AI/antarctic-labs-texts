Neoliberal Governance and Agribusiness Influence: Public Health Policymaking and the Risk to Meatpacking Workers During COVID‐19
Authors: India Luxton, Stephanie A Malin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn this article, we examine how neoliberal governance, corporate influence and public health policy intersected during the COVID‐19 pandemic in one meatpacking town we call Cattleford. We focus on two research questions: (1) In what ways did the political power of agribusiness shape local‐level public health policymaking around meatpacking during COVID‐19? and (2) how did neoliberalised governance mechanisms influence public health policies during the COVID‐19 pandemic? Utilising 39 in‐depth interviews, we found that Cattleford's public health approach was heavily influenced by pro‐business, laissez‐faire ideologies, which prioritised individual responsibility over collective health measures. Some local officials resisted state‐level guidance, enabling JBS Foods to operate with minimal restrictions, which heightened risks for meatpacking workers. We connect these local dynamics to broader trends in deregulation and individualised public health approaches. We examine how neoliberal health policies framed illness and risk as individual responsibilities, reinforcing unequal protection for essential workers and deepening their vulnerability. Our findings show how neoliberalism and the political influence of agribusiness have important implications for public health and worker safety during significant events such as COVID‐19.

Introduction

In the United States, the coronavirus pandemic (‘COVID‐19’) resulted in over 1.2 million deaths, with close to 650,000 deaths in the first year (Ruhm 2022). Local, state and federal governments implemented a range of public health measures to respond to COVID‐19. However, under the United States Defense Production Act of 1950, meatpacking plants were designated as ‘essential critical infrastructure’ and maintained operations throughout the pandemic. Dense working conditions and limited protections contributed to widespread outbreaks; by September 2021, over 59,000 workers had tested positive for COVID‐19 and nearly 300 had died (Herstein et al. 2021; Douglas 2021). Meatpacking workers were especially vulnerable due to working conditions, limited access to healthcare, and precarious employment and citizenship status (Gottlieb et al. 2025; Herstein et al. 2021; Krumel and Goodrich 2021). In this article, we assess the intersection between neoliberal governance, agribusiness power and COVID‐19 public health policymaking through an extended case study of one Midwestern meatpacking town.

Globally, policy responses to COVID‐19 in the meatpacking sector varied. In Germany, severe outbreaks led to the government banning subcontracted labour in the industry and the introduction of measures aimed at reducing overcrowded and unsafe working conditions, such as increasing the frequency of inspections and setting standards for housing (Ban et al. 2022; Gottlieb et al. 2025). After outbreaks in two meatpacking plants, the Netherlands empowered the heads of the region to close businesses and impose quarantines. However, these powers were used sparingly, with a deference to meat companies who voluntarily committed to improving workers' conditions (Gottlieb et al. 2025). As we describe throughout this article, the United States was constrained by neoliberal policies that limited industry‐specific safety mandates and broader public health responses.

In the United States, communities with large‐scale beef, pork and chicken processing facilities had dramatic increases in per capita infection rates, illustrating the detrimental impacts of limited state interference on both workers and their surrounding communities (Saitone et al. 2021). Although research has shown disproportionate impacts of COVID‐19 on marginalised communities (Clark et al. 2020; Waltenburg et al. 2020), research has yet to assess related processes of public health policymaking from perspectives of local, state and federal officials. Moreover, little research has examined dynamics involving essential industries, such as the meatpacking sector, in the development and enforcement of COVID‐19 public health guidelines (Dempsey et al. 2023; Luxton and Malin 2025).

In this article, we use 39 in‐depth interviews to examine (1) multiscalar outcomes related to federal‐, state‐ and local‐level decision making and (2) the role of neoliberal ideologies and corporate power in public health policymaking during COVID‐19. We illustrate how these dynamics shaped local‐level outcomes in a meatpacking town that we call Cattleford, home to a large JBS Foods facility, an agribusiness firm that is the world's largest meat producer (Gelles 2023). Globally, three firms (JBS of Brazil, WH Group of China and Tyson of the United States) control 83.5% of beef production, 66% of pork production and 58.5% of chicken production (Hendrickson 2015). We examine how neoliberal health policies framed illness and risk as individual responsibilities, reinforcing unequal protection for essential workers and deepening their vulnerability. Our findings show how neoliberalism and the political influence of agribusiness have important implications for public health and worker safety during significant events such as COVID‐19.

Neoliberalism, Labour and Public Health: Essential Work, Racialised Inequities and the U.S. Meatpacking Industry

Below, we review literature on neoliberalism's impact on labour precarity, public health, corporate regulation and worker protections. We highlight how individualised health logics align with decades of neoliberal deregulation and intersect with racial capitalism. We then focus on the meatpacking sector, examining consequences on workers and local communities.

The Implications of Neoliberalism for Public Health and Labour

Although its definition and applications are often contested, neoliberalism can best be understood as ‘a diverse and interlinked set of practices that reflects a heightened, evolved, and destructive form of capitalism’ (Malin 2015, 16). Neoliberalism is expressed through policy measures such as privatisation, marketisation, state deregulation, market‐friendly re‐regulation and the creation of self‐governing individuals (Malin 2015; Malin and Kallman 2022). Neoliberal ideals, including autonomy from state interference and regulation in favour of free markets and individualism, influence governance structures and cultural systems due to ‘ideational embeddedness’. In other words, broader societal rules, conventions and norms increasingly reflect commitment to neoliberalism's market‐centredness (Somers and Block 2005; Block and Somers 2014).

In neoliberalised systems, the state promotes and protects free markets and economic elites, producing legislation and regulatory frameworks that benefit industries and corporations rather than providing social safety nets or protections for all people (Harvey 2005). Neoliberal policies enact deregulation (cutting rules) and re‐regulation (changing rules or budgets for enforcement)—reducing government oversight across industries, including meatpacking (Luxton and Malin 2025). Devolution of governance also occurs—where the federal state relinquishes governance responsibilities to smaller units of government (such as individual states, counties and municipalities), having them create or enforce regulations amid ever‐tightening budgets and reduced staff. Devolution and deregulation/re‐regulation create conditions for corporate self‐regulation, where corporations self‐regulate their environmental, labour and other compliance in the absence of state enforcement, oversight and funded staff (Kreuziger 2005).

Beginning in the 1970s, neoliberal policies also helped diminish worker power and security and made work more precarious—by undermining and shrinking unions; workplace protections, oversights and worker safety; workers' legal protections; and making workplaces less subject to state oversight and more reliant on corporate self‐regulation (Quinlan and Rawling 2024; Underhill and Quinlan 2024; Quinlan 2021; O'Connor et al. 2020). Across industries and nations, a ‘new world of work’ (re)emerged, with marked tendencies towards privatisation, downsizing, mechanisation, outsourcing, underemployment, reduced access to workers' compensation and other forms of labour precarity (Lewchuk 2017; Kalleberg 2009; Quinlan and Rawling 2024; Quinlan 2021; Rosewarne 2010). Labour precarity has also been influenced by the reclassification of jobs as contract labour, which eliminates benefits and job protections typically provided under standard labour laws and regulations.

Further, neoliberal policies have important implications for public health—both in who bears responsibility for health and in the structure of public health systems. The general trends of neoliberal policies carried over to healthcare sectors and created scenarios where healthcare systems suffered from neglect and underinvestment from the state (Sparke and Williams 2022). By focusing on the market as the arbiter of everything (Harvey 2005) and by privileging wealth creation above all else, neoliberal policies encouraged deficient healthcare systems. Underinvestment and unequal investment in and access to equitable health systems and services, alongside deregulated and re‐regulated healthcare contexts, laid the groundwork for the sort of ineffective governance that helped make COVID‐19 a public health disaster in places such as the United States, Italy, Spain (Navarro 2020; Sparke and Williams 2022), Brazil (Ortega and Orsini 2020; Sott et al. 2022) and other neoliberalised spaces.

Neoliberalism's implications for public health during COVID‐19 were dire in an austere neoliberal setting like the United States. Here, devolution defines the landscape, where 51 states (including the District of Columbia), 2794 local health departments and 565 federally recognised American Indian and Alaska Native tribal public health agencies comprise the public health system (Hyde and Shortell 2012). Organisational models of state–local relationships vary and include decentralised, centralised and hybrid models (Salinsky 2010). Whether under the administration of local or state government, local public health agencies typically bear direct operational responsibilities for delivering most public health services available within specific jurisdictions. State health departments, on the other hand, are responsible for promoting public health and implementing disease prevention and preparedness plans, supporting local health departments and collecting data related to disease surveillance.

This devolution of governance to state and local public health systems and services—rather than a centralised federal public health model—exemplifies the impact of neoliberal policies, where healthcare has become increasingly privatised and government's capacity to lead public community‐level health interventions has been significantly reduced in favour of individual‐level approaches (Malin and Kallman 2022; Vera 2020). Further, the dominant biomedical model thrives under neoliberal systems, as it places health responsibilities on individuals rather than the state—evading accountability for health programmes and policies aimed at ensuring public health in favour of individualised approaches (Vera 2020). The biomedical model sees individual behaviours as the ‘locus of illness’, with health outcomes deriving from people's diet, lifestyle and genetics. Although these factors are important, health is shaped by intersecting, multiscalar factors (Link and Phelan 1995; Phelan and Link 2015; Williams et al. 2019). The biomedical model, though, ignores environmental and social determinants of health. In turn, this approach exacerbates existing social inequalities, including socioeconomic status, race, environmental exposures and access to healthcare—with those who are the most marginalised impacted disproportionately (Brown et al. 2011).

Neoliberalism, ‘Essential Workers’ and Racialised Health Outcomes During COVID‐19

Because neoliberal approaches prioritise personal responsibility and market‐based solutions, they fail to account for structural determinants of health (Link and Phelan 1995; Phelan and Link 2015; Williams et al. 2019). Typically, individually based health risk factors are prioritised and not contextualised within broader social conditions (Link and Phelan 1995). However, Phelan and Link (2015) argue that racism is a fundamental cause of health inequalities and race is interlocked with socioeconomic status. Likewise, Williams et al. (2019) argue that racism fundamentally causes adverse health outcomes for racial/ethnic minorities and broader health inequalities. Racially and economically minoritised communities are more likely to live in areas that expose them to pollution and experience environmental racism, to face barriers in receiving accessible healthcare and to have higher rates of illness and death (Ndugga and Artiga 2023; Roberts et al. 2018; Taylor 2014). Neoliberal austerity coupled with racial capitalism—a system that extracts value through racialised exploitation and devaluation—makes state neglect and environmental harm not only possible but politically acceptable (Pulido 2016, 2017). Together, both systematically shape health outcomes by concentrating risks such as environmental hazards, disinvestment and inadequate healthcare access in communities of colour (Pulido 2016, 2017).

Patterns of racism as a driver of public health inequalities were evident during COVID‐19 (Reid et al. 2021; Rogers et al. 2020). Racial and social differentiation embedded within systems of racial capitalism exposed workers to higher risks and deemed their labour, not their lives, to be essential. Once COVID‐19 emerged, workers in ‘essential’ industries, such as transport (Qureshi et al. 2024), food production (including meatpacking), seafaring (Kirkby 2020), retail and healthcare experienced amplified precarity and exposure risk (Underhill and Quinlan 2024; Quinlan and Rawling 2024; Quinlan 2021). Marginalised communities had higher case rates and death rates and were more likely to be employed in ‘essential’ industries where they faced increased risk of exposure (Hawkins 2020). In the United States, Black and Latino workers were over‐represented in frontline roles while simultaneously being more likely to live in multigenerational or crowded housing, further increasing transmission risks (Goldman et al. 2021). In the meatpacking sector, 87% of reported cases occurred among racial and ethnic minority workers (Waltenburg et al. 2020).

In many ways, neoliberal political‐economic outcomes set the stage for worker vulnerability and precarity during COVID‐19 (Quinlan 2021). Although a few exceptional examples exist of policy responses that countered neoliberal norms—such as the Australian state of Victoria's relatively robust worker protection and compensation programmes (Underhill and Quinlan 2024; Quinlan and Rawling 2024)—most workers did not experience similar, or any, safety nets. Neoliberal logics of austerity, privatisation and scant social safety nets affected the ways COVID‐19 was mitigated, which had dangerous impacts, especially for the most vulnerable workers and members of society (Decoteau and Garrett 2022; Decoteau 2024). The nations with the lowest investments in healthcare and heaviest reliance on privatised, uneven healthcare systems (like the United States) saw the highest mortality rates during the pandemic (Barrera‐Algarín et al. 2020). These general conditions and trends exacerbated COVID‐19's impacts on vulnerable populations and precarious workers, with direct implications for our study.

The U.S. Meatpacking Sector: Worker Impacts, Community Influence and Economic Power

In the United States, 25% of meatpacking workers are Black, 44% are Latino and 10% are Asian American Pacific Islander. 51.5% are foreign‐born (Fremstad et al. 2020). Nearly half (45.1%) of workers live in low‐income families, and 82% of workers have a high school education or less (Fremstad et al. 2020). These racial and economic demographics are not coincidental and, instead, are the product of industry recruitment efforts (Freshour 2017; Johnson‐Webb 2002; Krissman 2000; Stuesse and Helton 2013). In recent decades, as the U.S. border has become increasingly militarised, as Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) raids have increased, and with the development of identity verification systems such as E‐Verify, a reliance on undocumented workers has become more difficult (Genoways 2014; Freshour 2017). In response, the industry has shifted to recruiting refugee workers. Although similarly vulnerable to undocumented workers, they are legal employees (Frydenlund and Dunn 2022). The structural vulnerability of immigrants and refugees has created a precarious workforce that is less likely to contest poor working conditions due to fear of retaliation and limited alternative economic opportunities (Broadway 2007; Genoways 2014).

Today, meatpacking facilities are frequently located in rural areas in right‐to‐work states, where they are the dominant employer and are less likely to have unionised employees (Broadway 2007; USDA ERS 2021). Their economic impacts are substantial yet paradoxical, with both positive and negative effects on host regions, including job creation in rural areas and simultaneous economic vulnerability (Hendrickson et al. 2020). These companies are difficult to influence and regulate, and their host communities are reluctant to challenge their practices due to reliance on employment (Broadway and Stull 2010). Research overwhelmingly reports that industrialised farms and meatpacking plants are related to relatively worse conditions for socioeconomic well‐being, social fabrics, social disruption, class composition and environment (Broadway 2007; Lobao and Stofferahn 2008).

Despite negative impacts, meatpacking plants bring jobs and can considerably influence local political economies. When plants act as an area’s dominant employer, communities can be characterised as ‘modern company towns’ where residents’ economic livelihoods tie closely to meatpacking plants (Willingham and Ajilore 2019). In the United States, there are 56 counties where meatpacking accounts for more than 20 percent of employment (USDA ERS 2021), signalling a particular type of natural resource dependence. Concentrated animal feeding operations (CAFOs) and meatpacking plants are isolated in more rural, often poorer areas with little or no social capital and political power—also demonstrating patterns of environmental injustice and racism, where environmental and health risks and hazards concentrate among racially minoritised and poor communities (Wing et al. 2000; Taylor 2014). As a result, meatpacking and CAFO workers are exposed to multiple health risks, both in the workplace and in their broader community (Carrillo and Ipsen 2021; Wing et al. 2000).

Amid the concentrated labour market, these dominant employers can significantly influence local political‐economic decisions. Firms often give political contributions to local candidates and parties, and they may seek tax incentives or subsidies in exchange for maintaining or expanding their operations, all of which can result in decisions that prioritise corporate interests over other community concerns (Leonard 2014). Firms frequently make community donations—sponsoring local events, supporting food pantries and funding community infrastructure like new housing developments. Considered cumulatively, actions like these could be construed as environmental blackmail; where communities accept economic volatility, environmental harms and labour risks due to perceptions that local economies would fail to thrive without industry (Bullard 2019; Leonard 2014). In this article, we analyse the implications of agribusiness’ political power on public health and worker safety policymaking in one meatpacking town during COVID‐19.

Methods

In this article, we ask (1) in what ways did the political power of agribusiness shape local‐level public health policymaking during COVID‐19 and (2) how did neoliberalised governances mechanisms influence public health policymaking during the COVID‐19 pandemic? We utilised in‐depth interviews to inform our extended case study approach. The extended case method (ECM) helps provide an analytical frame for examining external social forces, power dynamics and connecting social patterns to theory (Buraway 1998). This article is part of a broader project that examined regulatory oversight and policymaking in the meatpacking sector during COVID‐19 through a multiscalar lens and mixed‐methods approach. Here, we focus primarily on the local and state levels and draw solely on in‐depth interview data.

Research Site Background

We focus our analysis on one JBS Foods plant (henceforth ‘JBS’) located in a Midwestern town we call Cattleford. We selected this site due to the extent of COVID‐19 outbreaks at the facility and the embedded nature of agribusiness in the local economy. Cattleford leads the state in beef production, grain farming and dairy operations. With most of the county’s land dedicated to farming, agricultural operations generate billions in annual revenue and, in 2000, agriculture and agribusiness accounted for over a third of the county’s economic activity. The facility itself employs thousands of workers, is the county’s largest employer, contributes significantly to local tax revenues and maintains partnerships with numerous regional producers who supply cattle for processing. Politically, Cattleford has been historically dominated by the Republican Party, with over half of residents voting for the Republican candidate in the 2021 municipal election.

Cattleford is also a ‘home rule’ county, which is a governance structure that enables local governments to act and legislate on local matters (Bunch 2014). Home rule counties are less constrained by state requirements, as they are not required to follow state statutes in matters of local and municipal concern and thus do not face state interference (Berman 2019). Counties with home rule powers can use their expanded authority to attract businesses and industries that align with their economic development objectives, offering incentives, creating business‐friendly environments and implementing policies to promote growth. Other advantages include flexibility in county procedures such as enacting local ordinances, finances and elections (Berman 2019). However, home rule also brings the potential for conflict, as differences in policies and regulations between local governments and the state can lead to legal disputes or challenges. This was particularly notable during COVID‐19, where home rule counties were able to resist statewide mandates, including mask orders and business closures.

In‐Depth Interviews

In‐depth interviews are useful for gathering information on individuals’ lived experiences, meaning‐making processes and perceptions of phenomena (McGrath et al. 2019). Participants were identified through a combination of manual internet searches, document analysis and snowball sampling, where we asked interviewees to recommend other individuals to participate (Noy 2008). We also leveraged local personal contacts to recruit a small sample of workers, a population that was difficult to access. In total, we conducted 39 semi‐structured, in‐depth interviews with 38 respondents, including local, state and federal representatives related to public health, agriculture and labour (n = 23); workers (n = 4); worker advocates (n = 6); and attorneys and reporters (n = 5). All participants provided informed consent to participate. Our study received IRB approval from our R1 university, with multiple revisions to ensure strong protection for meatpacking workers, bureaucrats and policymakers. This included using oral consent forms, meeting in private locations or virtually and de‐identifying all data. To protect confidentiality, all respondents have been given a pseudonym. Although we disclose the company’s name due to its global economic power, specific details regarding our research site’s geographic location have been deliberately obscured to prevent any potential identification through contextual or locational information.

The first author conducted interviews from August 2022 to March 2023. Interviews were conducted in person at a location selected by the participant, over the phone or over Zoom/Microsoft Teams. All interviews were recorded with participants’ permission, transcribed verbatim and coded by the first author using NVivo, a qualitative data analysis software. We used an inductive and iterative approach, focusing on techniques such as open coding, category development and abstraction to transition from specific findings to broader, more generalised insights (Saldana 2015). Initial codes were developed around emergent themes, including labour precarity, public health narratives (such as justifications for keeping plants operational), regulatory frameworks and disease mitigation measures. After the initial round of line‐by‐line coding (Saldana 2015), we revisited the coded interview transcripts and organised and collapsed codes into larger analytic themes. These themes inform the analysis we present below.

Findings and Analysis

Our findings illustrate (1) how public health responses amplified meatpacking worker vulnerability during COVID‐19 and (2) the role of neoliberal public health structures and policymaking in shaping these outcomes. We provide an overview of local‐level public health responses to COVID‐19, including the role of home rule governance, agribusiness interests and neoliberal ideologies. We argue that Cattleford embraced an individual responsibility approach to disease mitigation that enabled corporate self‐regulation and increased risks for meatpacking workers. Throughout, our data showcase how pre‐existing worker safety issues were amplified during the pandemic. We discuss these findings in greater detail below.

Public Health, Agribusiness Interests and Local Contexts

Although federal and international health authorities such as the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the Occupational Health and Safety Administration (OSHA) and the World Health Organization (WHO) provided guidance and issued safety recommendations to control the spread of COVID‐19, national guidelines were layered on top of state and local‐level regulations. The interface between local and state health departments is shaped by the state’s public health system and local governance structures, including home rule counties, further reflecting neoliberal devolution (Salinsky 2010). Although states issued statewide mandates—including public health orders, mandatory mask mandates, social distancing guidelines, business restrictions and capacity limits for gatherings—enforcement occurred at local levels.

In Cattleford, county commissioners have the ultimate authority over public health decisions, ordinances and guidance under the county’s governance structure. Across interviews with local political and public health officials, all participants highlighted how home rule conventions enable localised approaches to governance, which theoretically offered beneficial flexibility in policymaking during COVID‐19. However, this flexibility can lead to policy decisions being influenced more by vocal constituents than by expert knowledge. John, a local public health official who oversees communication, education and strategic planning, identified how commissioners’ unique positions fundamentally challenged their objective decision‐making:

The challenge is that they’re all elected officials, so they are politicians ….They’re going to have their finger on the pulse of their constituents who can be very vocal, whereas a Board of Health would have some insulation against that ….There’s pros and cons to it ….They’re going to be attuned to their constituents. And if one constituent calls up and complains, well, they obviously had them in their ear already and so, they’re going to be like, ‘Okay, you guys gotta adjust the messaging’. And so that’s what [the health department] did.

This challenge intensified during COVID‐19, particularly due to the pandemic’s politicised nature. A 2020 Pew report found that less than half of Republicans believed COVID‐19 was a major threat to the health of the US population, compared with 85% of Democrats (Tyson 2020). Political affiliation has been correlated with mask‐wearing, with belief in the legitimacy of the pandemic and with concerns about contracting and/or spreading the virus (Tyson 2020). Thus, the political affiliation of elected representatives and the dominant affiliations of their broader community in a heavily Republican context played important roles in COVID‐19 response efforts.

Although many counties adopted mask mandates, social distancing, gathering restrictions and business restrictions, Cattleford took a neoliberalised, laissez‐faire approach—where individuals were entrusted to make responsible decisions and businesses remained open. Frank, a local elected government official, characterised this approach as necessary, given the county’s ‘essential’ economic functions:

You people in [nearby cities], you’re members of the laptop class; you pack up your laptop and go home. I got somebody about a mile that way cutting meat so you can eat and so you can stay at home. We’re an ag processing manufacturing [county]. In my narrative with the media at that time, I said, ‘Well, Cattleford County can’t close because we’re essential. We need to stay open, so you can stay home’.

The county’s agriculturally dependent economy provided a key rationale for keeping businesses open, including JBS, the town’s primary employer. The notion of running the county as ‘a business’, as stated by one local elected official, illustrates neoliberal ideologies that promoted economic activity over public health considerations. Neoliberal ideologies view markets as governing social life, and, in turn, policies are aimed at ensuring economic activity persists, rather than ensuring social and environmental well‐being (Harvey 2005; Somers and Block 2005; Block and Somers 2014). Because the county is self‐governed, public health decisions reflected the pro‐market, pro‐individual ideology of elected county officials, an ideology that is deeply imbued with neoliberal norms and their embeddedness after 40 years of cultural dominance in places such as the United States.

Cattleford's strategy for combating COVID‐19 also reflected weak enforcement capacities of local and state health departments. Local public health officials commented on limited enforcement capacity for public health orders, due to funding constraints and understaffing, alongside an absence of legitimacy for state and local agencies to enforce guidance and mandates. Simon, a local clinical and community public health official, described discrepancies between state public health orders, their limited enforcement and county‐level power to decide their own courses of action:

Here in Cattleford County, the Board of Health is your county commissioners. They may have an advisory board, Board of Health, but that’s not … the ultimate governing board. Then, you have the state trying to issue public health orders that follow the entire state. And then the question becomes, okay, the state puts out these very broad public health orders. But they literally have no ability to enforce them. And then the county can adopt … kind of their own public health orders mimicking the state. And then they have the ability within the county to enforce them. But then, if you have a county that disagrees with that, they don't issue the public health orders, and there is no real enforcement of those public health orders in the county.

A lack of enforcement capacity was used to question not only the authority of the state but also the authority of their public health department. Limited enforcement, and relatedly, constrained government involvement in providing social safety nets such as public health infrastructure, highlights outcomes of neoliberalism, where the regulatory capacity of public agencies has historically been eroded in favour of devolved and corporate regulation, individualism and free market fundamentalism. This overlaps in important ways with public health outcomes as workers have experienced precarity and reduced political power for decades.

Rather than Cattleford implementing public safety and health regulations, their policies prioritised maintaining economic activities and, in doing so, their home rule governance structure benefitted firms such as JBS. Further, because the broader state public health model is decentralised, the state public health department has no authority over local public health governments—even in counties without home rule power. As the director of the state public health department explained:

We don't have authority over local public health departments. They are their own entities. They're either county level or regional, multi county. We do give them funds, and we do have contracts. We have a contractual relationship, but we don't have any legal authority over them.

This approach to public health creates inconsistencies in public health enforcement and responses, as state agencies can provide funding and guidance but lack the legal and cultural power to mandate local actions. This broader patchwork state approach to public health empowered places such as Cattleford to act independently from national recommendations. Instead, they embraced neoliberalised public health strategies premised on individual responsibility and privileging economic activity above public health.

Individualised Responsibility and Localised Lack of Enforcement

In the absence of county mandates, individuals were encouraged to take personal responsibility for adhering to health guidelines, such as mask‐wearing and social distancing. When asked about the county's disease mitigation strategy, John, who oversees communication, education and strategic planning in Cattleford's public health department, stated:

We never had any mandates that businesses close. We didn't mandate masks, it was all left up to individuals. And we certainly put the data out there. We were allowed to put the data out there, the information out there, but there were no mandates.

To share information about COVID‐19, the county launched a COVID‐19 dashboard in April 2020 that included data on the following: the number of cases reported in the last week, cumulative incidence rate per 100,000 residents, a 7‐day average positive test count, a 7‐day admission rate per 100,000 residents and the number of deaths in the last month. In the early months, the local newspaper also published weekly reports on COVID‐19, summarising changes in positive testing rates, hospitalisation rates, deaths and, importantly—as it was not published on the county dashboard—outbreak locations. Officials trusted the public to make informed decisions based on information publicly provided; reflecting a commitment to neoliberalised, individual‐level approaches to disease mitigation that assume equitable access to technologies such as computers and the internet.

By April 2020, the county had one of the highest case and death rates in the state. In comparison, a neighbouring county with a slightly higher population had 78% fewer cases and deaths. Notably, the neighbouring county's approach was much stricter than Cattleford's, implementing stay‐at‐home orders, business and school closures, and social distancing requirements. In contrast, Cattleford issued ‘safer‐at‐work’ guidelines that allowed all businesses within the county to reopen after the expiration of a statewide mandate.

Reliance on individual responsibility was a cornerstone of Cattleford's response strategy, in lieu of mandates and enforcement measures. Frank, a local elected official, explained:

If you believe in the way that we're set up, you must believe in individual … personal responsibility. Ultimately, I can provide you all the information, that's the proper role of government. For me to hold your hand and tell you to or not to do something, I'm not going to make a decision for you, [that’s] not the proper role of government. You as an individual in a society must make that decision for yourself …. It all boiled down to … whether you believe in the power of the individual, or whether you believe in the power of government.

Instead of using his policymaking power to issue local protective measures, he directly critiqued mandates as government interference—as authoritarian—rather than part of collective, preventative public health approaches. Rather than supporting state mandates or local‐level interventions, Frank used his institutional power as a local elected official to elevate individual responsibility as the preferred disease mitigation strategy. Yet, these individualised responses to disease mitigation neglected the social and economic contexts that undergird an individual's autonomy to make ‘responsible’ decisions.

Notably, there were other tensions associated with this approach to disease mitigation and the growing spread of COVID‐19 among workers at the plant and the broader community. The director of the state public health department described some of the challenges she experienced while navigating the plant's ultimate 2‐week voluntary closure.

There were conversations with the federal government about it. And the federal government at the time, their priority was to keep them open … state public health agencies were trying to prevent transmission. So, it was hard. We were at cross purposes at times …. The CDC provided guidance. They don't really have authority over us, per se. It was more about … this is a priority for us to keep these meatpacking plants open. And it was sort of insinuated that if we didn't work toward that in partnership, we would be less likely to get some of that federal technical assistance that we needed. It was kind of a fine line to walk. But ultimately, we did end up working together.

Likewise, one participant (who requested to be off the record) provided some details on the pushback the local health department received from both the federal government and from JBS.

[The director] wanted to shut JBS down. And they got pushback from JBS … there was communication challenges there, because as a public health official, he was seeing that there were high infection rates. And they needed to do a deep clean and put in some standards to protect the workers …. From the federal level, the White House got involved mostly just to say, ‘This is an essential business, you can't shut them down’. JBS then voluntarily closed for like a week or so.

In the month after, the director of the Cattleford health department retired—a retirement that some interviewees believed was linked to disagreeing with the county's public health response. Indeed, a National Association of County and City Health Officials survey found that extensive pressure from elected officials, subsequent interference with public health measures and efforts to diminish the authority of public health officials during the pandemic were a large factor in public health officials leaving their jobs—with over 500 public health officials having left by October 2021 (National Association of County and City Health Officials (NACCHO) 2021).

Tensions between public health officials, agribusiness and elected leaders highlight the limitations of decentralised public health systems, where devolved enforcement power is often fragmented and subject to political interference. The pushback from JBS, federal authorities and local elected officials illustrates competing interests in public health policymaking, particularly related to industries deemed essential to the national economy. Moreover, the consequences for public health officials and departments who attempted to enforce stricter protections reflect a broader trend of hostility towards public health authorities. These findings raise significant concerns regarding the balance of power in public health governance and outcomes for at‐risk workers, particularly the extent to which economic and political actors can shape or obstruct health policymaking.

Corporate Self‐Regulation, Intensified Risks and Worker Injustices

Instead of local or state interventions governing outcomes, meatpacking companies, including JBS, were largely tasked with implementing safety precautions for workers; precautions were voluntary, not mandatory. The meatpacking industry leveraged its existing power to become entrusted with managing the pandemic and controlling disease spread, promoting self‐regulation over state or local ‘interference’ (Dempsey et al. 2023; Luxton and Malin 2025). Robert, a local elected official, emphasised his confidence in JBS's self‐regulated safety precautions—despite precautions reducing, not eliminating, risk.

When they had configured the plan for protection for their employees … they had a tour for the elected officials here. We were invited and … we went through that tour … So, they [JBS] said ‘We were taking immediate action’. … [But] you had those citizens here that were advocating for the minority groups saying that ‘People need to stand up for them. You're not standing up for them. You're issuing their death sentences’ … The company, JBS, announced that they were doing everything they could. And I believe in my heart … they were reacting as quickly [as they could]. They were very proactive prior to COVID‐19 even being announced …. It's just a shame that so many things were so emotional.

Both Robert's faith in the company and JBS’s strategic tour for elected officials exemplified a continued partnership that was critical to maintaining JBS's self‐regulation over health and safety precautions. Robert believed that JBS had prepared for the pandemic to the best of their ability and questioned the legitimacy of claims made by worker advocates in the community, despite high case rates.

A confluence of factors—workplaces with limited social distancing, financial incentives to keep working, lack of access to sick pay or healthcare—created localised situations where ‘essential’ workers, many of whom were already vulnerable due to their immigrant and refugee status, faced particularly high risks of exposure to and transmission of the virus (Herstein et al. 2021). Pre‐existing safety and health issues also amplified difficulties in disease mitigation efforts. The structure of meatpacking work, with workers in close quarters, made following social distancing guidelines challenging. Thomas, a researcher who examined COVID‐19 spread at the facility, explained:

These people are standing shoulder‐to‐shoulder …. There's not too many places where people stand shoulder‐to‐shoulder and there's even fewer places where people stand … shoulder‐to‐shoulder along the line. And then three feet away on the other side of the conveyor, you have the same thing …. And they're shoulder‐to‐shoulder and face‐to‐face in a loud, cold environment. In order to communicate with somebody across from you, you have to speak loudly. And I think what we know now is that speaking loudly is a bad thing. Viral aerosol … I think those are really the risk factors. It's just the proximity. And then they're crowded no matter where they are … they're crowded in the lunchroom. They're crowded in the locker rooms; they're crowded on the lines.

To address the proximity of workers, upon the plant's reopening, JBS installed plastic dividers on the line and in the cafeteria, a tent outside to expand cafeteria space and staggered shifts to reduce crowding as employees entered the plant. These safety precautions did increase the ability of workers to socially distance at the plant. However, interviewed local, state and federal public health officials all identified a lack of available quarantine housing, issues with shared transportation and some identified barriers to accessing sick pay and testing.

Because CDC/OSHA federal guidance was largely voluntary (Dempsey et al. 2023; Luxton and Malin 2025), and enforcement was largely done locally, underlying issues and power dynamics within meatpacking plants were also not addressed in disease mitigation strategies. Carol, a federal labour and worker safety policy expert, reflected on sociopolitical conditions that heightened disease transmission among workers and how plant power dynamics hindered workers' agency:

This was an industry that just kept going. Everybody was working side by side, and … it ripped around these plants … you had the Trump administration, and governors saying, ‘Whatever, we don't care, you got to go to work. You're not going to get unemployment insurance if you quit your job because you think it's unsafe’. You had local public health agencies who don't usually ever deal with worker safety … and they don't quite get … the power dynamics in the workplace … the power imbalance in the workplace is employers, even in a union plant, decide what the workers should do.

As Carol recognised, firms continued production despite a pandemic, in part because their ability to do so was bolstered by federal directives, state policy and local‐level actions. Further, plant power dynamics kept workers from contesting working conditions and reporting issues to the local union and OSHA. Explicit pressure from supervisors and management to continue working—and a largely absent local, state and federal regulatory framework—constrained workers' ability to advocate for enhanced safety.

In other cases, fear was implicit rather than explicit and built on legacies of power dynamics. Kyle, a Cattleford reporter, discussed a culture of fear at the plant and linked this fear to the refugee and immigrant status of workers:

Multiple to thousands of them are new to this country … they don't know what worker’s rights mean or are. They don't know how to contact or what a worker’s rights attorney is. There's a lot of ways that you can take advantage of people like that, is I guess what I'm getting to. Where you don't know where you are … these people don't know what resources they have and maybe don't have access to these resources. And just kind of gently put on them, if you will, this idea of, if you say something, you'll be in trouble. And you don't have to actually threaten, you don't have to actually say ‘if you say something, we will fire you’, you just have to … kind of imply it.

Because many meatpacking workers are foreign‐born, firms can capitalise on a lack of knowledge about resources and recourse for issues related to worker safety. Rather than provide information to enhance workers' understanding of community resources, including who to report safety issues to and how to apply for compensation in cases of injury, firms profit from these procedural injustices and the social and economic precarity of workers.

Beyond power dynamics, participants identified challenges to effective disease mitigation, such as language barriers, faulty temperature‐testing equipment and insufficient access to PPE. For example, a public health worker, who was contracted by JBS, expressed concern about the reliability of the temperature checking, the screening process and the lack of language accessibility.

They [workers] knew that I couldn't let them go to work. So, they just would kind of nod [when I asked about symptoms]. And then just keep going. You know, nothing. They would say ‘nothing’, ‘nothing’ to let me know that they don't have symptoms. And then the stuffy nose. ‘Oh, allergies’. I can't argue with them … they live paycheck to paycheck. I mean, of course, they're gonna lie. I'm sure there was tons of people going through that line who had symptoms. And who should have been in quarantine.

Rather than management informing workers about next steps when they were symptomatic, including how and where to access testing, it was the responsibility of temperature screeners. In turn, workers often decided not to report symptoms to screeners. When JBS stopped paying for testing in the summer of 2020, it was also the temperature screeners' responsibility to tell workers that they needed to go through their insurance or pay a $100 test fee.

Further, to incentivise workers to continue working, JBS implemented a $600 attendance bonus in the pandemic's early months. These attendance bonuses capitalised on the economic vulnerability of workers while constructing the illusion of choice. Workers were ‘choosing’ to continue to work, even if this choice was influenced by firm policy and their precarious economic position. These accounts illustrate how neoliberal approaches to disease mitigation amplified workers' exposure to workplace risks and constrained workers' abilities to act ‘responsibly’ in high‐risk, under‐regulated environments.

Discussion and Conclusion

Using 39 in‐depth interviews, we examined (1) agribusiness' political power on local public health policymaking during COVID‐19 and (2) how neoliberal ideologies influenced public health policies and their enforcement during COVID‐19. Our findings contribute to research on the implications of neoliberalism on public health (Sparke and Williams 2022; Quinlan 2021; Underhill and Quinlan 2024; Quinlan and Rawling 2024), particularly during an unprecedented public health crisis. We show how pre‐existing devolution, deregulation/re‐regulation and worker precarity within neoliberal racial capitalism can be especially destabilising and unjust in public health emergencies such as pandemics.

This article expands research on how neoliberal political‐economic outcomes influenced worker vulnerability and public health risks during COVID‐19 (Quinlan 2021; Sott et al. 2022; Sparke and Williams 2022). Drawing on perspectives of local policymakers, we illustrate how the ‘ideational embeddedness’ of neoliberalism (Somers and Block 2005; Block and Somers 2014) shaped the county's pro‐business, individualised disease mitigation strategy. When the conventions of neoliberalism are embedded into regulatory frameworks, inadequate public health oversights and preferences for corporate self‐regulation shape inequitable health outcomes (Navarro 2020; Sparke and Williams 2022; Vera 2020). Cattleford's home rule status granted the county government the authority to enact and enforce certain public health measures and regulations independently of the state government. We argued that limited county‐level public health guidance reflected a neoliberalised approach which failed to account for structural, institutional and sociopolitical factors that constrained the ability of meatpacking workers to make proactive health decisions.

By placing the responsibility for health on individuals rather than institutions, public health strategies reinforced the biomedical model and overlooked structural determinants of health, intensifying risks experienced by meatpacking workers (Brown et al. 2011; Link and Phelan 1995; Phelan and Link 2015; Williams et al. 2019; Vera 2020). Intersectional vulnerabilities, such as citizenship status, language differences and economic inequalities, were not included in the county's disease mitigation response. The combination of economic and social vulnerability, a culture of fear and lack of protection amplified the disproportionate impact of COVID‐19 on essential workers and revealed the intersection of racial capitalism and public health (Pulido 2016, 2017). These findings illustrate how individualised public health logics obscure structural conditions that produce vulnerability, particularly for racialised and precarious workers (Link and Phelan 1995; Phelan and Link 2015; Williams et al. 2019).

Further, our findings suggest that JBS leveraged its existing economic and political power to influence governance decisions and public health policy in favour of corporate self‐regulation over state interference. These findings also illustrate how the context of a ‘modern company town’ influenced policymaking during COVID‐19 (Willingham and Ajilore 2019). Through political donations and their status as the town's number one employer, JBS has become embedded in Cattleford's economic sphere: shaping the county's political landscape to their favour. Although we focused on one location, the dominance of meatpacking firms in local political‐economic structures is a pattern across U.S. meatpacking towns (USDA ERS 2021). Our findings have important implications for deepening an understanding of the outcomes of a concentrated labour market during public health crises. Here, we enhance previous work (Decoteau and Garrett 2022; Decoteau 2024), establishing how neoliberal austerity, deregulation/re‐regulation and scant social safety nets affected mitigation of COVID‐19.

Although our research was robust, it was limited to investigating public health policymaking in one meatpacking town. However, as one third of counties in the United States are home rule (National Association of Counties (NACo) 2024), these findings can inform further research on the implications of this governance structure during public health crises. Additional research could explore how agribusiness actors shaped public health responses during COVID‐19 across geographic locations. Comparative studies could provide additional insights into how neoliberal ideologies influenced the balance between economic interests and public health during COVID‐19. For example, in 2020, Germany implemented a series of legislations to reduce worker exploitation in the meat industry, including removing the use of subcontracted workers (Ban et al. 2022; Gottlieb et al. 2025). A cross‐country comparison could provide additional insights related to policy differences and responses to COVID‐19 across the global meat industry.

Our findings further support the need to address neoliberalism's long‐standing structural influence and its marked impacts on public health policymaking and labour structures (Ortega and Orsini 2020; Sott et al. 2022; Sparke and Williams 2022; Underhill and Quinlan 2024; Quinlan 2021). Corporations benefit significantly from an individualised rhetoric where devolved governance is weak and where disease mitigation relies on already‐vulnerable, disempowered workers to individually avoid workplace hazards. Yet, structural conditions shape worker experiences and power, including decades of neoliberal precarity, weakened labour power and deregulation that undermines workplace and social safety. Further, racially, economically and otherwise minoritised workers face intersecting vulnerabilities that further limit their ability.

There is a clear need for more worker protections, including enhancing union power and equitable decision‐making structures, to help reduce dangerous and disproportionate risks. This is especially important for groups such as meatpacking workers, who already contend with neoliberalism's long‐term structural vulnerabilities and workplace environmental injustices (Broadway and Stull 2008; Carrillo and Ipsen 2021). Further, enhancing the regulatory capacity and power of local‐ and state‐level institutions is critical to addressing the weaknesses inherent in a neoliberal approach to governance. To better protect workers, especially during public health emergencies, local and state authorities must actively enforce regulations rather than relying on corporate self‐regulation.
