Comment regarding ‘gut microbiota, circadian rhythms and their interactions: implications for the pathogenesis and treatment of depression’
Authors: Hongyu Quan, Lihua Jin, Yongmei Li
Published: 2026

We carefully examined the review by Du et al. [1] concerning the interaction between the gut microbiome and circadian rhythms in individuals with depression. The article adeptly synthesizes a complex and rapidly evolving field, elucidating promising therapeutic avenues. Although the gut-brain-circadian rhythm axis signifies a substantial advancement, we wish to underscore a critical yet frequently neglected aspect: the significant influence of coexisting pain conditions, particularly neuropathic pain (NP), on gut microbiota and emotional states.

The review highlights that external factors, including diet, exercise, and light-dark cycles, can disrupt the gut microbiome and circadian rhythms, thereby contributing to mood alterations linked to depression. Nevertheless, it inadequately addresses the impact of internal pathological conditions, such as pain—particularly chronic pain—as significant disruptors of gut ecological balance [2]. Research utilizing animal models of NP indicates that NP itself can precipitate substantial dysbiosis of the gut microbiome, evidenced by diminished α-diversity, altered Bacteroidetes/Bifidobacterium ratios, and variations in the abundance of key genera [3]. These microbial alterations are closely correlated with pain-related behaviours. For example, an increase in key genera such as Bacteroides may enhance neuroinflammation through Toll-like receptor activation, promote the production of bacteria that generate short-chain fatty acids, and potentially impair anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective functions, thereby exacerbating depressive symptoms [4].

In particular, within models such as spinal cord injury, commonly linked to NP, both the pain and stress resulting from the injury can independently trigger dysbiosis of the gut microbiota. This dysbiosis may, in turn, be associated with or exacerbate disturbances in circadian rhythms. This creates a detrimental cycle: pain leads to gut microbiota dysbiosis, which then causes circadian rhythm disruption, ultimately intensifying depressive symptoms and pain sensitivity. The framework proposed by the authors, which emphasizes external temporal signals, could be substantially improved by incorporating endogenous stressors, specifically the role of chronic pain, which we consistently highlight. Specifically, some factors, such as sleep disturbances induced by pain, directly disrupt circadian rhythms, and stress-activated pathways can further modify gut permeability and microbial composition.

Although the authors express an optimistic view regarding interventions like probiotics and faecal microbiota transplantation, it is imperative to exercise caution when applying these methods to patients with comorbid pain and depression. Research on microbiota-based interventions indicates potential in alleviating neuropathic pain through modulation of the neuro-immune axis. This suggests that therapeutic strategies for patients with neuropathic pain and depressive symptoms should not only focus on the gut-circadian rhythm interface but also directly address dysbiosis induced by pain [5].

In conclusion, we acknowledge the substantial review contributed by the authors. To advance this field further, future research should consider situating the “gut-brain-circadian rhythm” therapeutic model within the context of patients exhibiting neuropathic pain and depressive symptoms. Such an approach would enhance our understanding of the underlying pathophysiology and foster the development of more personalized treatment strategies.
