Extracellular vesicles derived from clonal mesenchymal stromal cells preconditioned by indirect hypoxia modulate immune responses in diabetic mice more effectively than directly preconditioned vesicles
Authors: Mehdi Soleymani-Goloujeh, Mahnaz Babaahmadi, Faezeh Shekari, Mojgan Barati, Nasrin Fallah, Mahmoud Alipour Choshali, Nima Makvnd Gholipour, Maryam Hezavehei, Masoumeh Azimi, Saeed Yakhkeshi, Samira Vesali, Saeed Mohammadi, Hossein Baharvand, Seyedeh-Nafiseh Hassani, Ensiyeh Hajizadeh-Saffar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs) or MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) are considered potential modulators of the immune system in type 1 diabetes (T1D) therapy. On the other hand, preconditioning MSCs with inflammatory agents is a promising strategy to improve the therapeutic effects of these cells. Therefore, we performed various direct preconditioning regimens (IFN-γ, poly(I: C), LPS, and hypoxia) on clonal MSCs (cMSCs), and for indirect preconditioning regimens, the same regimens were applied to peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). PBMC-derived concentrated conditioned media (CCM) was transferred into cMSC cultures to mimic in vivo conditions to obtain cMSC-EVs with better immunomodulatory effects and then selected hypoxia-induced cMSC-EVs and their source cells to determine their influence on immune responses in multiple low-dose/Streptozotocin (MLD/STZ)-induced mouse model.MethodsDirect preconditioning regimens include four groups: IFN-γ (50 ng/ml), poly(I: C) (42.22 µg/ml), LPS (1 µg/ml), and hypoxia (1% oxygen). For indirect preconditioning, these regimens were applied to PBMCs, and PBMC-derived CCM was added to cMSC cultures. The resulting cMSC-CCM was assessed for the expression of anti-inflammatory, pro-inflammatory, and regenerative factors and its ability to inhibit lymphocyte proliferation. The isolated EVs from the most effective direct and indirect preconditioned cMSC-CCM groups and their source cells were transplanted in MLD/STZ-induced mouse models of T1D and followed for 73 days.ResultsIn vitro characterization revealed that direct and indirect hypoxia preconditioning were the most effective groups among the different preconditioning regimens. Therefore, four treatment groups, including direct hypoxia-induced cMSC-EVs (dH-MSC-EVs), indirect hypoxia-induced MSC-derived EVs (iH-MSC-EVs), and their cell sources (dH-MSCs and iH-MSCs), were selected for EV isolation and animal studies. The in vivo study revealed that the fasting blood glucose (FBG) trend decreased in the four treatment groups compared with the sham group. However, the effects of direct/indirect hypoxia on cMSC-EVs were associated with an increase in the levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4 and IL-10) in the serum of the MLD/STZ-induced mouse models compared with their parental cell counterparts. Indeed, iH-MSC-EVs attenuate immune responses in MLD/STZ-induced mouse models. Furthermore, compared with their source cells and dH-MSC-EVs, iH-MSC-EVs significantly reduced the levels of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6) in the serum of the MLD/STZ-induced mouse models.ConclusionOur results showed the effectiveness of direct/indirect hypoxia preconditioning for cMSCs to modulate immune responses in the MLD/STZ-induced mice model. This study demonstrated that the immunomodulatory potential of iH-MSC-EVs is significantly greater than that of dH-MSC-EVs and their source cells. Our results suggest iH-MSC-EVs, could be a potential option for modulating the immune response in T1D.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04568-z.

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is an autoimmune disease characterized by a strong inflammatory response. The pathophysiology of the disease appears to involve direct destruction of pancreatic β-cells by autoreactive immune cells that target insulin-producing cells. Despite promising therapeutic developments, there are currently no treatments that can effectively stop or reverse the progressive destruction of β-cells [1, 2]. Pancreatic islet transplantation has emerged as a potential therapeutic option for individuals with T1D. However, this approach has significant limitations, including a shortage of donor organs and the need for lifelong immunosuppression to prevent transplant rejection [3].

Numerous experimental studies have demonstrated that bone marrow mesenchymal stromal/stem cells (BM-MSCs) alleviate diabetes in animal models. Successful clinical results of the transplantation of BM-MSCs in diseases have been reported [4–7]. MSCs have shown promise in treating T1D-associated complications because of their ability to regulate immune responses and promote tissue regeneration [8, 9].

However, several major challenges hinder the effectiveness of these cells within damaged tissue. Oxidative stress, inflammation, hypoxia, and activation of apoptotic pathways impair the migratory and engraftment abilities of transplanted stem cells and result in significant cell death, thereby reducing their therapeutic efficacy. In addition, damaged tissues often have a poor vascular supply and insufficient trophic factors, which are crucial for the survival and function of transplanted stem cells [10].

To address these key challenges, researchers have focused on optimizing MSC treatments by considering the inflammatory environment and preconditioning methods to improve their effectiveness in treating T1D [11, 12]. Two primary approaches to optimize the therapeutic potential of MSCs are hypoxic stimulation and cytokine application. When MSCs are exposed to hypoxic conditions, their immunomodulatory abilities can be significantly enhanced. This preconditioning method mimics the physiological conditions found in damaged or inflamed tissue, where oxygen levels are typically reduced [13–15]. Another approach to improve the immunomodulatory properties of MSCs is the application of specific cytokines. Cytokines are signaling molecules that play crucial roles in regulating immune responses. The exposure of MSCs with carefully selected cytokines, primed them to exhibit enhanced immunomodulatory effects when they are administered as a cell therapy. This preconditioning strategy aims to optimize the interactions of MSCs with the cells of the adaptive immune system, leading to improved therapeutic outcomes [16, 17]. These approaches mimic physiological conditions in vivo and resemble the critical role played by peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) as one of the first barriers against pathogens. The interaction between PBMCs and inflammatory triggers initiates signaling cascades and the release of numerous cytokines that recruit various cells, including MSCs, to the site of inflammation. PBMCs can regulate the expression of cytokines such as IL-6, which are involved in inflammatory response. Furthermore, the presence of PBMCs can lead to the downregulation of proinflammatory cytokine gene expression, thereby promoting a more favorable environment for MSC-mediated tissue repair [15]. In recent years, direct coculturing of MSCs with PBMCs has been shown to be a common method for mimicking in vivo conditions. However, this approach has certain limitations, including challenges in regulating the ratio of PBMCs to MSCs, possible negative effects of PBMCs on MSCs, and variations in results due to differences in MSC source and heterogeneity [16, 18].

Given these limitations, the use of indirect methods may be beneficial. In this study, indirect preconditioning of MSCs is achieved by transferring the concentrated conditioned medium (CCM) of preconditioned PBMCs to an MSC culture. We hypothesized that using the CCM of preconditioned PBMCs might be more effective than directly coculturing PBMCs and MSCs.

We used a uniform population of human bone marrow-derived clonal MSCs (hBM-cMSCs) that were isolated and generated via an optimized method under good manufacturing practice (GMP) conditions [19].

In the present study, we preconditioned cells with a variety of preconditioning regimens, including (a) direct preconditioning with IFN-γ, poly(I: C), LPS, or hypoxia and (b) indirect preconditioning with CCM from PBMC cultures preconditioned with IFN-γ, poly(I: C), LPS, or hypoxia. Ultimately, the group with the greatest potential for immunomodulation and regeneration was selected for in vivo studies based on several criteria, including the expression levels of various anti-inflammatory and proinflammatory factors, effectiveness in inhibiting lymphocyte proliferation, and regenerative capacity. The aim of this selection process was to identify the most promising candidates for further investigations and possible therapeutic applications.

However, concerns such as entrapment in the pulmonary vasculature and potential side effects post-transplantation have raised questions about cell therapies [20, 21]. To address these challenges, researchers have shown that MSC-EVs, as a form of cell-free therapy, have therapeutic effects like those of their parental cells [22–25]. Various inflammatory compounds and hypoxia, induce MSCs to produce EVs containing anti-inflammatory and regenerative factors [24, 26, 27].

To assess the therapeutic effects of MSC-EVs and their parental cells, we isolated them from directly and indirectly preconditioned MSCs, characterized them, and introduced them into multiple low-dose Streptozotocin (MLD-STZ) mouse models of T1D.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

for this study was obtained from the Royan Institutional Review Board and the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Royan Institute (Title: Evaluation of Extracellular Vesicles derived from pre-treated clonal BM-MSCs on -cell Regeneration and Immunomodulation in Type 1 Diabetes Murine Model; Approval number: IR.ACECR.ROYAN.REC.1397.228; Date: December 04, 2018). All the animal experiments were performed in accordance with the NIH guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Isolation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells

The Iranian Blood Transfusion Organization (IBTO) received 400 ml of fresh whole blood from a healthy donor after providing informed consent and performing virological tests. PBMCs were isolated via the standard Ficoll®-Paque PLUS method (17-1440-02, GE Healthcare, Sweden). These PBMCs were then cultured in 150 cm2 flasks (90150, TPP TechnoPlastic Products, Switzerland) with 15–20 ml of complete culture medium containing alpha-minimum medium (α-MEM; 11,900,073, Gibco, UK) supplemented with 5% human platelet lysate (hPL; Cell Tech Pharmed Co., Iran), 1% GlutaMAX™ (35050–038, Gibco, UK), 1% MEM nonessential amino acids (NEAAs; 111400–35, Gibco, UK), heparin (5,000 U/ml) (heparin sodium, 21061, Alborz Daru Pharmaceutical Company, Iran), 2-mercaptoethanol (2-ME; M7522, Sigma‒Aldrich, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (5,000 U/ml) (Pen/Strep; 15070, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Viability was assessed via the trypan blue (111732, Sigma‒Aldrich, USA) dye exclusion method, and approximately 6 × 108 PBMCs were distributed into T150 flasks.

Culture of clonal mesenchymal stromal cells

MSCs used in this study were clonally expanded primary human bone marrow-derived MSCs (hBM-cMSCs). These cells were generated under GMP conditions and cryopreserved at the cell bank of the Royan Advanced Therapy Medicinal Product-Technology Development Center (Royan ATMP-TDC), as previously described [19]. A frozen vial containing 1 × 106 cMSCs was thawed and plated in 150 cm2 flasks (90150, TPP TechnoPlastic Products, Switzerland) with 20 ml of complete culture medium consisting of α-MEM. After counting, the cells were divided and distributed into 150 cm2 culture flasks (8 × 105 cells per flask). Preconditioning was performed when the cMSCs reached 60–70% confluencey.

Direct/indirect preconditioning of clonal mesenchymal stromal cells

Four groups were considered for direct and indirect (through PBMCs) preconditioning of cMSCs. The groups were as follows: (1) the FN group, which included 50 ng/ml IFN-γ (ab51240, Abcam, USA); (2) the PO group, which included 42.22 µg/ml polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (Poly(I: C); P1530-25MG, Sigma‒Aldrich, USA); (3) the LP group, which included 1 µg/ml lipopolysaccharide (LPS; L2630-10MG, Sigma‒Aldrich, USA); and (4) the hypoxia group (H), which was cultivated under 1% O2. For each treatment group and control group (NO: no treatment), three 150 cm2 flasks were seeded with cMSCs, which were primed for 48 h (hr) in a tri-gas incubator incubator (37°C, 85% humidity, 5% CO2 for all groups, and 1% O2/94% N2 for the H group). To verify the hypoxic response, HIF-1α protein levels were assessed by Western blotting after 48 h of exposure to 1% O2. Although the corresponding blots are not shown, our experiments confirmed an increase in HIF-1α expression under hypoxia, as expected. Interestingly, we also observed elevated HIF-1α levels in response to LPS treatment, consistent with the known role of inflammatory stimuli in inducing hypoxia-like signaling pathways.

For direct preconditioning, cMSCs in all groups (NO, FN, PO, LP, and H) were treated for 48 h, the culture media was then replaced with fresh media, and the cells were allowed to rest for 72 h under normal incubation conditions. This allowed the secretome of the cMSCs to be released. The collected CM from each group was concentrated 10-fold with Vivaflow® 200 (14-558-318, Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany), and directly preconditioned cMSC-derived concentrated CM (d-MSC-CCM) was cryopreserved at -80°C for further experiments. The directly preconditioned cMSCs were also cryopreserved with a freezing medium containing 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; D2650, Sigma‒Aldrich, USA) and 90% fetal bovine serum (FBS; 10270–106, Gibco, UK) and stored at -196°C in a liquid nitrogen storage tank (MVExc, 47/11–10, MVE Biological Solutions, USA).

For indirect preconditioning, PBMC-CCM derived from NO-treated and FN-, PO-, LP-, and H-preconditioned PBMC cultures were used to precondition cMSCs. PBMC-CCM from all the groups were obtained in the same manner as described for the cMSC-CCM preparation.

Indirect preconditioning of cMSCs was performed as follows: 48 h incubation with PBMCs-CCM, followed by replacement of the supernatant with fresh medium and incubation for another 72 h under standard incubator conditions. The indirectly preconditioned cMSC-derived concentrated CM (i-MSC-CCM) was then collected, along with the indirectly preconditioned cMSCs (i-MSCs), and frozen. After 48 h of direct or indirect preconditioning, cells were washed three times with sterile PBS to remove residual stimuli and CCM components prior to further processing. Endotoxin testing was not performed on the final conditioned medium used for in vivo studies, as none of the selected groups involved direct LPS or Poly(I: C) stimulation. These selections were based on in vitro assays and functional outcomes. If such groups had been selected, LAL-based endotoxin testing would have been performed. Isolation and characterization of clonal mesenchymal stromal Cell-Derived extracellular vesicles

At approximately 90% confluence, a complete α-MEM culture medium was collected, and EVs were isolated as previously described [28]. Briefly, the CM obtained from each preconditioned group (dH-MSCs and iH-MSCs) was concentrated with Vivaflow® 200. The CCM was subjected to centrifugation steps (300 × g for 10 min and 3,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C) to remove debris and dead cell bodies. The obtained CCM was subjected to ultracentrifugation steps at 20,000 × g for 30 min with an additional washing repeat and 110,000 × g for 120 min. The pellet was washed with DPBS and centrifuged at 110,000 × g for an additional 120 min. Ultracentrifugation was performed with a 45Ti rotor and an Optima L-100XP Beckman Coulter ultracentrifuge instrument.

The characterization was based on the MISEV2018 [29] and MISEV2023 [30] recommendations. The protein content was measured via a Pierce™ BCA protein assay kit (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). To determine particle size, materials diluted fivefold in PBS (refractive index 33.1, viscosity 1.08) were subjected to dynamic light scattering (DLS) size measurements via a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) was used to assess the morphology of the MSC-EVs.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and Western blot

To measure cytokines in both direct and indirect cMSC-CCM, the levels of IL-10, IL-6, IL-1β, TGF-β, IFN-γ, TNF-α, VEGF, and IGF-1 were measured via DuoSet® ELISA kits (DY217B, DY206, DY201, DY240, DY285B, DY210, DY293B, DY291, R&D Systems, USA), and the amounts of HGF and bFGF were measured via human Quantikine® ELISA kits (DHG00 and DFB50, R&D Systems, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For the in vivo functional assay, the concentrations of serum cytokines, including IL-4, IL-10, TNF-α, and IL-6, were measured via commercial Mouse Quantikine ELISA kits (M4000B, M1000B, MTA00B, and M6000B, respectively), following the manufacturer’s instructions (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA).

Western blots of MSCs subjected to different preconditioning regimens were performed according to previously published methods [31]. Additionally, EV surface markers were identified via WB by a previously described protocol [28] (all primary antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table S1).

Lymphocyte proliferation assay

For lymphocyte proliferation assay (LPA), PBMCs were first labeled with 5 mM CellTrace™ (CFSE Cell Proliferation Kit; C34554, Invitrogen, USA) for 5 min, and 15 × 104 PMBCs were added to each well of a 96-well round-bottom plate (Z707880, TPP TechnoPlastic Products, Switzerland). The proliferation of PBMCs was stimulated with 10 g/ml phytohemagglutinin (PHA-P; L1668-5MG; Sigma‒Aldrich, USA). A total of 250 µl from d-MSC-CCM and i-MSC-CCM samples was used for coculture with PBMCs. After four days of incubation, the proliferation of the PBMCs was measured via the CFSE dilution technique. PBMCs were controlled on a BD FACSCalibur™ (BD Biosciences, USA) flow cytometer for forward and side scattering. For the EV functional test, PBMCs were prepared, and two groups of dH-MSC-EVs and iH-MSC-EVs were used for coculture with PBMCs. Finally, 240 µl of prepared cell suspension plus 10 µl of dH-MSC-EVs or iH-MSC-EVs (containing 20 µg) were added to each well (triple) of a 96-well round-bottom plate. The 96-well plate was placed in a normoxic incubator. After four days, the proliferation of the PBMCs was measured via the CFSE dilution technique. All the results were evaluated via FlowJo™ software 7.6.1 (BD Biosciences, USA) and compared with those of the negative control.

Experimental multiple low-dose/streptozotocin-induced diabetic mouse model

Fifty-seven male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the Royan Institute animal farm (Halejerd, Karaj, Iran). The animals acclimated to their new surroundings within two weeks of their arrival. All animals were housed in polypropylene cages separately, with free access to standard food and water. They were maintained on a 12/12-hour light/dark cycle at a room temperature of 22–24°C and a humidity of 30–70%. Fifty-two C57BL/6J mice were rendered diabetic according to the Animal Models of Diabetic Complications Consortium (AMDCC) protocol [32, 33]. Four weeks after the last STZ injection, the blood glucose levels of the mice were measured via a glucometer (Blood Glucose Test Meter GLUCOCARD 01-mini, ärkray Global Business Inc., Shiga, Japan). Mice with blood glucose levels > 250 mg/dl on two consecutive days (34 and 35 days after day 0 of the MLD/STZ-induced diabetes procedure) were considered diabetic.

Transplantation of direct/indirectly hypoxic clonal mesenchymal stromal cells and their extracellular vesicles

The diabetic mice were randomly divided into five groups, including four treatment groups (dH-MSCs, iH-MSCs, dH-MSC-EVs, and iH-MSC-EVs; n = 8 per group) and an untreated sham group (vehicle recipients; n = 20). The treated animals were compared with the healthy control group (no diabetes induction and no treatment; n = 5). The vehicle was a combination of 95% normal saline (N.S., Pro-Fleks, Turkey) plus 5% human serum albumin (HSA, Octalbin 20%, Octapharma, Switzerland). The route of administration for the groups containing cells and their sham counterparts was intravenous (IV), whereas, for the groups containing EVs and their sham counterparts, it was intraperitoneal (IP). Mice were anesthetized via the intraperitoneal administration of 80 mg/kg ketamine (Narkamon 100 mg/ml; 475928 A, Bioveta, Czech Republic) and 10 mg/kg xylazine (Xylased 20 mg/ml; 385728 A, Bioveta, Czech Republic). Reflex testing was performed prior to injections to confirm adequate depth of anesthesia and ensure minimal discomfort. Administration was performed twice, on days 40 and 47, after the first STZ induction with 1.5 × 106 cells or 100 µg of EVs per injection in 150 µl of vehicle through 0.5 mL ultrafine (29G) insulin syringes (BD, USA).

Functional tests

Fasting blood glucose, glycosylated hemoglobin, and glucose tolerance tests

The fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels (mg/dl) of all the groups were measured by collecting blood from the tail veins of the mice via disposable test strips and a glucometer after 4–6 h of fasting. FBG levels were monitored regularly at one-day intervals for up to 110 days. The animals were considered hyperglycemic after two consecutive measurements of FBG > 250 mg/dl were taken on days 34 and 35. On day 110, we checked the serum hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) of the mice via an appropriate device (I Sens Inc., HbA1c cartridge: DHJaAD, I Sens Inc., South Korea) (data not shown). The animals were weighed monthly for up to 110 days.

The intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IP-GTT) was performed on day 110 at the end of the follow-up for all groups. After 4–6 h of fasting, 2 g/kg D-glucose (G7021, Sigma‒Aldrich, USA) was intraperitoneally injected into the mice (10 µl/gr). At 0, 15, 30, 60, and 120 min after injection, blood glucose levels were measured with a glucometer.

Histological analysis of pancreatic tissue

In accordance with AVMA standards [34] for animal euthanasia, the mice were euthanized via the intraperitoneal administration of 80 mg/kg ketamine (Narkamon 100 mg/ml; 475928 A, Bioveta, Czech Republic) and 10 mg/kg xylazine (Xylased 20 mg/ml; 385728 A, Bioveta, Czech Republic) and subsequently euthanized with carbon dioxide to remove the pancreatic tissue. The pancreatic tissue was removed to perform the necessary laboratory studies [34]. Each pancreas was fixed in 10% formalin for five days at 4°C, soaked in agar before being embedded in paraffin, and then divided into nine equal parts. The embedded pancreatic tissue blocks were sectioned with a microtome (5 μm sections) and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for general histological and morphological assessment.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed using an anti-insulin antibody to determine whether insulin-secreting islets were present [35]. All the antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table S1. A negative control was prepared with PBS in place of the main antibody in the section.

Stereological assessment and the volume of the pancreas

A stereological assessment of pancreatic tissue was performed as described previously [35, 36]. Nine paraffin blocks with a thickness of 0.1 cm were prepared from each pancreas. A 5-µm section was selected from each block (nine sections per pancreas). IHC was used to identify insulin-producing islets in the tissue sections. The Cavalieri method was used to estimate the volume of the pancreas via a stereomicroscope (Olympus, SZX 16, Tokyo, Japan) connected to a computer at a magnification of 0.7x. To estimate the volume of the pancreas, random points were superimposed on the images. The points were then counted, and the volume was calculated according to a previously published protocol [36].

Statistical analysis

The Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the data. The descriptive results for the quantitative variables are expressed as the means and standard deviations, and those for the qualitative variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. When the data were normally distributed, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s post hoc test were used to compare the means of the quantitative variables in more than two groups. If the data were not normally distributed, the Kruskal‒Wallis test was performed. The chi-square test was used to compare the frequency or percentage of qualitative variables. Statistical analysis was performed via SPSS software (version 22.0.0; Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the graphs were created via GraphPad Prism software (version 9.4.1; San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Influence of direct or indirect preconditioning on the immunomodulatory properties of cMSC

Direct preconditioning

To understand the effects of direct preconditioning on cMSCs, cMSC-CCM subjected to different treatments, including direct IFN-γ preconditioning (dFN-MSC-CCM), direct poly(I: C) preconditioning (dPO-MSC-CCM), direct LPS preconditioning (dLP-MSC-CCM), and direct hypoxia preconditioning (dH-MSC-CCM), and compared with the untreated NO group as a control to determine the level of cytokine secretion (Fig. 2).

(B-C) Western blot analysis revealed significant upregulation of COX2, VEGF, and HGF in dH-MSC-CCM (Full-length blots are presented in Supplementary Material 2). Indirect preconditioning

Indirectly preconditioned cMSC-CCM via PBMCs, referred to as indirect IFN-γ preconditioning (iFN-MSC CCM), indirect Poly(I) preconditioning (iPO-MSC-CCM), indirect LPS preconditioning (iLP-MSC-CCM), and indirect hypoxia preconditioning (iH-MSC-CCM), were evaluated by Western blot analysis (Fig. 3). The results revealed significantly greater expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines in the iLP-MSC-CCM group (p value < 0.000) and the regeneration-related cytokines FGF2 and HGF in the iH-CCM group than the control group (p values < 0.0001 and 0.017, respectively) (Fig. 3-A, and Supplementary Figure S2-B). Moreover, the expression of anti-inflammatory factor COX1 in the iNO-MSC-CCM group (p value < 0.05) and COX2 in the iH-MSC-CCM group (p value < 0.001) were higher than the control group. Furthermore, none of the proinflammatory factors were significantly downregulated in the preconditioned groups. However, NF-κB and TNF-α levels were significantly greater in the iLP-MSC-CCM group (p value < 0.01), and JNK was significantly greater in the iH-MSC-CCM group (p value < 0.001). Interestingly, the assessment of regeneration-related factors revealed greater expression of VEGF in iLP and iH-MSC-CCM (p value < 0.001) and Ang-1 and HGF in iH-MSC-CCM (p value < 0.001) than the control group (Fig. 3-B, C) (Full-length blots are presented in Supplementary Material 2). Furthermore, LPA analysis revealed that iLP-MSC-CCM and iH-MSC-CCM significantly inhibited lymphocyte proliferation. The ability of iLP-MSC-CCM and iH-MSC-CCM to inhibit PBMC proliferation was almost 90% and 60%, respectively (p value < 0.0001, Fig. 3-D, and Supplementary Figure S3-A, B, C).

The assessment of total volume of insulin-secreting islets

Histological examination of H&E-stained pancreatic tissue sections, especially islets of Langerhans from healthy control mice, revealed that acinar cells and islets of Langerhans cells of the pancreas were present in normal proportions. The islet cells were embedded within the acinar cells and surrounded by a fine capsule (Supplementary Figures S5-F and S7-A). In the sham group, the cell density significantly decreased, and the islets were disrupted. A photomicrograph of the diabetic pancreatic islets clearly revealed STZ-induced damage in the form of empty spaces (Supplementary Figures S5-F and S7-A). Uniform eosinophilic material and few atrophic cells largely occupied the islets. Eosinophilic material also surrounds blood vessels. There was decreased inflammatory cell infiltration in the mice in each of the four treatment groups. In some cases, partial regeneration of insulin-producing cells was observed compared with that in the sham mice (Supplementary Figure S7-A).

IHC analysis via an anti-insulin antibody revealed that the MLD/STZ injection protocol selectively destroyed the insulin-secreting islets. Insulin levels were high in the islets of the healthy control group (Supplementary Figure S7-A, right panels). The insulin-stained sections were used to assess the impact of combined treatment on β-cell regeneration. First, the total volume of each pancreas in all the experimental groups was estimated. The results revealed no significant differences between the total volumes of the pancreas in the experimental groups. Next, analysis of the total volume of the insulin-secreting islets revealed that we could not detect any significant regeneration of pancreatic β-cells. Moreover, it may not have been detected with this level of accuracy by our method. In addition, our stereological results revealed no significant differences between the total volumes of the pancreas and the insulin-secreting islets in the experimental groups (Supplementary Figure S7-B, C).

Discussion

Despite the potential benefits of MSC treatment for diabetic patients, its clinical application faces significant challenges because of the impact of the microenvironment on MSC activity and function [37, 38]. In diabetic patients, the pathological microenvironment of high glucose levels can impair MSC proliferation, increase senescence and programmed cell death [39, 40], reduce migration ability, and diminish the secretion of growth factors that are essential for regeneration [41, 42].

However, recent advances in preconditioned MSCs have shown promising results in improving their survival and migration to target tissues, as well as enhancing their immunoregulatory, differentiation, and proangiogenic properties post-transplantation. Preconditioning strategies involve pretreating MSCs with various factors or conditions, such as hypoxia, inflammatory stimuli, or other factors, before therapy. However, challenges remain in defining the optimal approaches for preconditioning in MSC-based therapies [18, 43, 44].

Herein, we introduce a novel indirect method that more closely mimics the complex in vivo environment. In this approach, cMSCs are preconditioned by adding the CCM from preconditioned PBMCs into the cMSC culture. The CCM from preconditioned PBMCs contains a variety of cytokines and growth factors that activate cMSCs and enhance their therapeutic potential. These factors include pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines, as well as growth factors that support cell survival and differentiation [15]. In the present study, we used IFN-γ, poly(I: C), LPS, and hypoxia for preconditioning and compared direct and indirect preconditioning.

We analyzed cytokine and growth factor expression patterns to assess a variety of proteins potentially linked to the immunosuppressive and regenerative functions of preconditioned cMSCs. Our findings revealed elevated levels of both anti-inflammatory and proinflammatory factors following indirect preconditioning of cMSCs. Both LPS and hypoxia stimulate the release of anti- and proinflammatory cytokines; however, LPS causes greater variation and elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, which is a significant drawback when selecting an immune-modulating preconditioning regimen. Compared with untreated group, the indirect LPS treatment and hypoxia groups demonstrated approximately 80% inhibition of lymphocyte proliferation. In contrast, no significant inhibition was observed in the direct preconditioning groups. These findings highlight the potential of hypoxia-treated cells to exhibit enhanced immunomodulatory and regenerative properties.

Similarly, Rhijn et al. [45] demonstrated that compared with control adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs) cultured under hypoxic conditions (1% O2) more potently suppressed the proliferation of mitogen-stimulated CD4 and CD8 T lymphocytes. They suggested that hypoxia-preconditioned ASCs have enhanced immunomodulatory effects, which could be harnessed for therapeutic applications.

Our results also showed a significant increase in the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 and regenerative factors VEGF and IGF-1, along with a decrease in the pro-inflammatory factors TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, and IL-1β in the direct hypoxia group. Although there was no significant increase in the expression of immunomodulatory factors in the indirect group compared with the normoxic group, there was a significant increase in the expression of the regenerative factors VEGF, HGF, and Ang1.

Similarly, Rosová et al. [46] reported that when MSCs were cultured under hypoxic conditions, the expression of HGF and its primary receptor, mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor (c-Met), increased, and the HGF/cMet signaling pathway was crucial for MSC migration.

The differences in cytokine release between direct and indirect preconditioning approaches seem to reflect both how strongly the cells are stimulated and the way that stimulation occurs. When cMSCs are directly exposed to potent stressors like hypoxia or LPS, they respond with broad internal stress signaling, including activation of NF-κB, which drives the release of both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines. This was particularly noticeable in the direct hypoxia group (dH-MSC-CCM), where TNF-α and IL-6 levels were nearly ten times higher than those in the indirectly preconditioned groups. In contrast, indirect preconditioning with PBMC-derived conditioned medium delivers a more nuanced paracrine signal, resulting in a more targeted and balanced immunoregulatory response. This difference was not just molecular but also functional—lymphocyte proliferation assays showed that the indirect groups, especially iH-MSC-CCM and iLP-MSC-CCM, were much more effective at suppressing immune cell growth. Altogether, these findings suggest that indirect preconditioning guides MSCs toward a more clinically useful, anti-inflammatory phenotype.

We also want to acknowledge a technical point that might come up. The conditioned media from the directly preconditioned groups were prepared earlier and stored at -80°C for several months, while those from the indirect groups were more recently collected and used. All samples were handled using the same freeze–thaw procedures, and − 80°C storage is widely accepted for preserving cytokines and soluble signals. Although long-term storage could introduce some variability, the strong and consistent differences we observed across both molecular and functional outcomes make it unlikely that storage timing alone explains the results [47].

Based on the in vitro findings, we hypothesized that the hypoxia groups presented the most promising immunomodulatory and regenerative properties. The greatest advantage of the hypoxic treatment groups is their ability to be produced on an industrial large-scale platform and their suitability for clinical applications without the concern of residual materials in the cell or the overlying culture medium.

Studies have also shown that reduced oxygen availability significantly increases EV release from MSCs, which essentially represents the functional components of their parent cells [48]. Therefore, EVs can be used as a substitute for MSCs. They offer several advantages over their cellular counterparts, including better stability, biocompatibility, lower immunogenicity, and easier tolerance by the host [22]. Additionally, EVs are more efficient at systemic delivery, as they are not entrapped in the vasculature as are whole cells [25]. Furthermore, EVs do not pose the risk of aneuploidy or other chromosomal abnormalities, as they do not divide, integrate, or dynamically respond to their microenvironment like their parental MSCs [23]. Our findings from the LPA test showed that both direct and indirect cMSC-EVs significantly decreased lymphocyte proliferation. However, confirmation of these effects requires further testing in animal models and in vivo condition.

For this purpose, we conducted a study using MLD/STZ-induced mouse model. This model offers several advantages over a single high-dose method in terms of better recapitulating the gradual, immune-mediated, and dynamic nature of β-cell destruction observed in human T1D. Even with significant β-cell loss, the MLD/STZ model can maintain some residual β-cell function for a prolonged period. This is more representative of the clinical presentation of T1D in humans, where β-cell function is often preserved, especially early in the disease course [49].

In this study, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of two regimens, direct/indirect hypoxia-preconditioned cMSC-EVs at a dose of 100 µg and direct/indirect hypoxia-preconditioned cMSCs at a dose of 1.5 × 106 cells, which were administered twice over a 7-day period to ameliorate the clinical manifestations of diabetes in four distinct mouse models of T1D. Histopathological analysis of the diabetic mouse models revealed a reduction in inflammatory cell infiltration within the pancreatic tissue sections of the group treated with indirect hypoxia-preconditioned cMSC-EVs compared with sham group. Previous studies have established the critical role of inflammatory cytokines in driving cellular infiltration, inflammatory cytokine production, and subsequent β-cell destruction in the pathogenesis of T1D [50]. Therefore, the attenuation of proinflammatory cytokines and the augmentation of anti-inflammatory cytokines may contribute to the alleviation of clinical symptoms. The pivotal roles of TNF-α and IL-6, which are produced and secreted by T helper-1 (Th1) lymphocytes, in the pathophysiology of T1D are well documented. TNF-α is one of the most critical cytokines that plays a crucial role in pancreatic inflammation and triggers T-cell-mediated β-cell destruction [51]. Our results revealed a significant decrease in the serum levels of TNF-α and IL-6 in the group treated with indirectly hypoxia-preconditioned cMSC-EVs compared with those in the sham group. Our findings revealed a substantial increase in the serum level of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 following the indirect administration of hypoxia-derived cMSC-EVs compared with that in the sham group, which may be attributed to the secretion of IL-10 by Tregs. We observed a lower mortality rate in the EV-treated groups than in the cell-based treatment groups, and this improved survival probability led to the maintenance of stable FBG levels in the treated mice. Thus, the MLD/STZ-induced diabetic mice demonstrated an amelioration of disease complications. This result strengthens our confidence in the potential of cell-free therapies as viable alternatives to cell-based approaches.

Our results align with the findings of Nojehdehi et al. [52], who reported that the intraperitoneal injection of exosomes from autologous adipose tissue-derived MSCs (AD-MSCs) from C57BL/6J mice had immunomodulatory effects on the activity of T cells and reduced the clinical symptoms of MLD/STZ-induced T1D in a mouse model. They also reported the upregulation of IL-4, L-10, and TGF-β and the downregulation of IL-17 and IFN-γ in T1D model mice, thereby slowing the progression of the disease. Compared with those in untreated T1D mice, pancreatic islets were regenerated, and the body weights and blood glucose levels of T1D model mice treated with exosomes derived from AD‐MSCs were maintained at stable levels. Both studies demonstrated the promising therapeutic potential of hypoxia-preconditioned MSC-derived EVs in T1D, with similarities in the observed immunomodulatory effects and cytokine regulation.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that the potency of EVs, especially those derived through both direct and indirect preconditioning methods, could surpass that of their parental cells. Among the various preconditioning regimens tested, hypoxia preconditioning stands out as particularly effective, providing a straightforward, cost-effective, and manageable approach for culturing cMSCs in bioreactors. Intriguingly, when cMSCs are introduced into inflamed conditions, they seem capable of producing EVs that have a more pronounced effect on the immune system than those produced under standard in vitro conditions. This insight opens the door to the use of PBMCs for indirect, high-efficiency preconditioning of cMSCs. Nonetheless, the allure of cell-free therapies, such as EVs, becomes increasingly apparent in the context of treating autoimmune diseases such as T1D compared with their source cells. The potential for EVs to significantly alleviate or modulate T1D is promising and merits further investigation. A broader range of studies is essential to fully understand the intricate mechanisms of MSC-EV therapy, including how these vesicles interact with immune cells and contribute to immunomodulation and β-cell regeneration. Moving forward, we must focus our efforts on translating these promising findings into practical applications and pave the way for potential T1D treatments.
