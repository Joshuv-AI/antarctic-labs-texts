Codanin-1, defective in congenital dyserythropoietic anemia I (CDA-I), regulates erythroid differentiation
Authors: Linette Bosques, Susree Modepalli, Arvindhan Nagarajan, Caroline Tang, Sandra Martínez-Morilla, Nur-Taz Rahman, Sameet S Mehta, Diane S Krause, Hannah Tamary, Patrick G Gallagher, Shilpa M Hattangadi, Gary M Kupfer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCongenital dyserythropoietic anemia type I (CDA-I) is an autosomal recessive disorder marked by ineffective erythropoiesis, abnormal morphology of bone marrow erythroblasts, and iron overload. Most cases of CDA-I are caused by mutations in the CDAN1 gene, which encodes a ubiquitous protein of unknown function, Codanin-1.MethodsTo investigate the role of Codanin-1 in the molecular pathways involved in CDA-I, we developed erythroid models using human K562 cells and primary human CD34 + cells from mobilized peripheral blood.ResultsHere we show that Codanin-1 expression is required for erythroid progenitor development and normal erythroid cell differentiation. Erythroid cells lacking Codanin-1 demonstrated morphologic changes similar to those observed in CDA-I. Global gene expression changes after Codanin-1 knockdown revealed alterations in a set of key erythroid genes. In particular, the AHSP gene, which showed reduced mRNA and protein expression levels after Codanin-1 knockdown, also demonstrated increased Codanin-1 occupancy at its gene regulatory region by chromatin immunoprecipitation coupled to high-throughput sequencing.ConclusionIn summary, using cell models recapitulating many features of CDA-I, we have studied and confirmed the importance of Codanin-1 during erythroid differentiation and provide mechanistic insight into how loss of Codanin-1 expression results in CDA-I.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06540-6.Keywords: Binuclearity, Benzidine, Codanin-1, Erythroid, Hemoglobin

Background

The autosomal recessive blood disorder congenital dyserythropoietic anemia type I (CDA-I) is characterized by anemia and abnormal morphology of erythroid precursors in the bone marrow, associated with ineffective erythropoiesis [1, 2]. Patients suffer from iron overload for several reasons, including ineffective erythropoiesis, destruction of peripheral erythrocytes, and blood transfusion therapy [3]. Bone marrow examination reveals marked erythroid hyperplasia and megaloblastic erythroblasts [4]. Light microscopy shows internuclear chromatin bridges and binuclearity of erythroblasts [5, 6]. Electron microscopy exhibits electron-dense heterochromatin in over 60% of erythroblasts, giving the nucleus a spongy “Swiss cheese” appearance [7, 8]. Most CDA-I cases are caused by presumed loss-of-function mutations in the CDAN1 gene [9]. CDAN1 encodes the highly conserved protein of unknown function Codanin-1, which contains no functional domain or intracellular localization signal. Codanin-1 has been implicated in the regulation of histone supply during DNA replication in non-erythroid cells; [10] however, the function of Codanin-1 in erythroid differentiation is still not fully understood. While CDAN1 is ubiquitously expressed, its mutations cause an erythroid-specific phenotype. Studies have shown that Codanin-1 is predominantly expressed during early erythroid maturation. Erythroid-specific knockout of Codanin-1 in mice recapitulates key features of CDA-I, including binucleated erythroblasts and defective terminal erythropoiesis, underscoring its critical role in erythroid lineage commitment [11].

Mutations in CDAN1 disrupt its interaction with Asf1, leading to impaired histone supply and defective chromatin assembly. Cryo-electron microscopy studies have revealed that Codanin-1 forms a complex with ASF1 and C15ORF1, a protein implicated in CDA-I. This complex is involved in regulating histone supply during DNA replication. Disruption of this complex, due to mutations in CDAN1, leads to defective chromatin assembly and the characteristic nuclear abnormalities observed in CDA-I erythroblasts [12, 13] We have developed erythroid models to study the pathophysiology of CDA-I. Here we show the constitutive expression of Codanin-1 in cells induced down the erythroid lineage, suggesting a role for Codanin-1 during erythropoiesis. Indeed, knockdown of Codanin-1 in human K562 cells and primary human CD34+ cells from mobilized peripheral blood demonstrate its expression is required for erythropoiesis. Consistent with the CDA-I phenotype, Codanin-1 knockdown cells display binuclearity and decreased hemoglobin synthesis and fail to complete erythroid maturation. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) reveals global gene expression changes in response to Codanin-1 knockdown under erythroid conditions, identifying potential Codanin-1 target genes involved in erythropoiesis. More specifically, Codanin-1 knockdown leads to diminished expression of AHSP (alpha-hemoglobin stabilizing protein), a molecular chaperone required during normal erythropoiesis for the stabilization of free alpha-hemoglobin [14]. Interestingly, many of these genes, including AHSP, demonstrate Codanin-1 occupancy as indicated by chromatin immunoprecipitation coupled to high-throughput sequencing (ChIP-seq) and validated by PCR-based ChIP analysis in K562 cells. Taken together, our results provide evidence that supports a mechanistic understanding of the role of Codanin-1 in erythropoiesis. Understanding the precise functions of Codanin-1 and the molecular consequences of its deficiency is not only essential for elucidating the pathogenesis of CDA-I but also has broader implications for understanding nuclear remodeling events that are fundamental to hematopoietic development.

Methods

Cell line culture

K562 cells were cultured in IMDM, 5% FBS, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (all purchased from Invitrogen). Erythroid differentiation of K562 cells was induced with 5-aminolevulinic acid (Sigma-Aldrich) at a final concentration of 0.5 mM. 293FT cells were cultured in DMEM, 10% FBS, 0.1 mM non-essential amino acids, 6 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 500 µg/ml geneticin (all purchased from Invitrogen).

SiRNA transfection

Short interfering RNA (siRNA) pool targeting CDAN1 (M-015478-01) and non-targeting siRNA (D-001210-02) were purchased from Dharmacon. K562 cells (1 × 106) were transfected with 70 nM (double transfection of 35 nM, 0 and 48 h) or 200 nM (single transfection) siRNA using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermofisher) and Amaxa Nucleofector Kit V (Lonza), respectively. Knockdown efficiency was assessed by quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) and immunoblot. The four individual CDAN1 siRNAs in the pool and an siRNA targeting the 3’UTR of CDAN1 were also purchased and knockdown efficiency was assessed (Supplemental Fig. 2A).

Primary human CD34+ cell culture

Mobilized adult peripheral blood CD34+ cells were purchased from the Yale Cooperative Center of Excellence in Hematology and Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center. CD34+ cells were cultured in a 4-phase culture system (expansion and erythroid differentiation I, II, and III) over 21 days at 37 °C in 5% CO2, as previously shown [15]. Expansion medium consisted of StemSpan SFEM (StemCell Technologies) supplemented with 100 ng/mL recombinant human-stem cell factor (rh-SCF), 100 ng/mL recombinant human-Flt-3 ligand, 20 ng/mL recombinant human-interleukin-3 (rh-IL-3), 20 ng/mL recombinant human-interleukin-6 (StemSpan CC100 Cytokine Cocktail), and 2% penicillin/streptomycin. Cell concentration was maintained at 0.1 × 106 cells/mL throughout expansion. After expansion, cells were switched to erythroid medium. Cell concentration was maintained at 0.1 × 106, 0.2 × 106, and 0.3 × 106 cells/mL in erythroid phases I (5 days), II (4 days), and III (8 days), respectively. The erythroid differentiation medium included IMDM with 15% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1% BSA, 2% penicillin/streptomycin, and supplemented with 500 µg/mL human holo-transferrin (T0665; Sigma-Aldrich), 10 ug/mL insulin (I0516; Sigma-Aldrich). More specifically, erythroid phase I included 2 µM dexamethasone (D2915; Sigma-Aldrich), 1 µM β-estradiol (E2758; Sigma-Aldrich), 5 ng/mL rh-IL-3 (200-03; Peprotech), 100 ng/mL rh-SCF (300-07; Peprotech), and 6 U/mL erythropoietin (Procrit). Phase II included 50 ng/mL rh-SCF and 6 U/mL erythropoietin. Lastly, phase III included 2 U/mL erythropoietin; cells were cultured in fibronectin (0855913; MP Biomedicals) coated plates.

Colony-forming assay

To determine the effect of Codanin-1 knockdown on the generation of erythroid progenitors, a colony-forming assay was performed with cells transduced with TagRFP-tagged control shRNA or shRNA targeting Codanin-1, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Prior to seeding, cells were sorted and cultured in erythroid medium for 3 days (phase I). A total of 1 × 106 cells was plated in methylcellulose-based medium (Methocult™ H4034, StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) in 35 mm Petri dishes (Falcon, Cockeysville, MD, USA) in triplicate. Dishes were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 14 days. Colonies were scored using an inverted microscope.

Flow cytometry

For cell surface markers, the following antibodies were used: CD71 APC bM-A712, 561940) and CD235a FITC (GA-R2, 561017) or CD235a BV421 (GA-R2, 562938; BD Biosciences). As isotype controls, the following antibodies were used (BD Biosciences): IgG2a APC (G155-178, 555576) and IgG2b FITC (27–35, 555742) or IgG2b BV421 (27–35, 562748). Propidium iodide (P4170; Sigma-Aldrich) was used to exclude dead cells. To measure enucleation rate by flow cytometry, cells were stained with terminal erythroid marker CD235a and 10 µg/ml Hoechst 33342 (Sigma-Aldrich). Sorting of TagRFP+ cells was performed on a FACS Aria II and analyzed on a FACS LSR II (BD Biosciences). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

ChIP-seq

ChIP was performed as described previously [16, 17]. Briefly, 30 million K562 cells per sample were cross-linked for 10 min at room temperature with 1% formaldehyde. Nuclei were isolated and sonicated in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100) on a Diagenode Standard Bioruptor for 18 cycles at 20 s with 60 s rest between cycles. Sonicated lysates were immunoprecipitated by overnight incubation at 4 °C with Dynabeads magnetic protein G beads (Thermofisher) bound with antibody against protein of choice. ChIP assays were performed using either anti-Codanin-1 antibodies (Cod1, Cod2), anti-GATA1 antibody (N6) (sc-265X, Santa Cruz), or rabbit IgG (sc-2027, Santa Cruz) as control. Cod1 corresponds to homemade antibody raised against the N-terminal of Codanin-1 (amino acids 123–142, ProteinTech). Cod2 corresponds to commercial antibody raised against the C-terminal of Codanin-1 from Santa Cruz (sc-68404, Santa Cruz). The sheared chromatin was then reverse-crosslinked from the beads and the resulting DNA purified using phenol-chlorophyll-isoamyl alcohol (Sigma-Aldrich), followed by ethanol precipitation. For PCR-based ChIP, PCR primers were designed near promoter-regulatory region of the genes demonstrating differential expression after Codanin-1 knockdown. Primer sequences are shown in Supplemental Table 1. Fold enrichment was determined by normalizing ChIPed DNA Ct values with rabbit IgG Ct values. Two replicates of each Codanin-1 and IgG ChIP were analyzed by qPCR. ChIP samples were then converted into libraries by Yale’s Center for Genome Analysis using the Illumina ChIP-seq library preparation kit and sequenced using the Illumina platform. Briefly, we trimmed the reads for quality and length using Trimmomatic [18] to maintain minimum base call quality of Q30, and minimum length of 45 bases. The trimmed reads were aligned against the UCSC reference genome version hg37 using the bwa mem aligner [19]. The alignment was further processed with MACS2 [20] to call peaks. The peaks are typically called if the normalized coverage for a given region is between 5- to 50-fold above the background. We used the rabbit IgG DNA as our control (background), and immunoprecipitated DNA to call the peaks.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated with TRIzol (Invitrogen) or the RNeasy kit (QIAGEN). DNA contamination was removed with DNase I treatment (Roche). Total RNA was recovered from the genomic DNA removal reaction using the RNeasy MinElute Cleanup Kit (Qiagen). RNA concentration and purity (A260/A280 above 1.8) were assessed using a Nanodrop ND-8000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies). DNA-free total RNA (0.5–1 µg) was used for cDNA synthesis using SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen) with random primers. An equal amount of cDNA was used for qPCR reactions, performed on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad) using KAPA SYBR Fast qPCR Mix (Kapa Biosystems), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to the expression of RPL27 and OAZ1 and quantitated by the delta-delta Ct method, as previously described [21]. Primer sequences are shown in Supplemental Table 1.

RNA-seq

RNA used for RT-qPCR analysis was also used to generate a cDNA library that was sequenced using the Illumina platform at Yale’s Center for Genome Analysis. Briefly, we trimmed the reads for quality and length using Trimmomatic [18] such that the minimum base call quality was at least Q30, and the length of the reads was at least 45 b. The trimmed reads were aligned against the UCSC reference genome version hg37. We used the TopHat2 [22]. The transcript abundance estimates were calculated using cufflinks, and differential gene expression analysis was carried out with cuffdiff [22]. The results were visualized using R and cummerbund [22]. We called the genes as “significantly” differentially expressed as long as the q-value (False Discovery Rate also known as adjusted p-value) was ≤ 0.05 (5% or less). Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (www.qiagen.com/ingenuity) was used for gene set enrichment analyses.

Statistics

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Two-tailed Student’s t test were used to determine significance. Statistical significance is denoted as *P ≤ 0.05; **P ≤ 0.01; ***P ≤ 0.001; ****P ≤ 0.0001. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism software (GraphPad Software).

Results

Codanin-1 is constitutively expressed during erythroid differentiation

To study Codanin-1 in an erythroid model, we induced K562 cells down the erythroid lineage with 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA), as described previously [23, 24]. Cells were assayed and harvested at the following time points: 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. Erythroid differentiation was assessed by quantitatively estimating the fraction of cells synthesizing hemoglobin using benzidine staining. Treatment with ALA resulted in a progressive increase in the percentage of benzidine-positive cells, as shown in representative images (Fig. 1A-B). At 0 h, only 2.2% were benzidine positive, while by 72 h of ALA treatment, 33.9% (P ≤ 0.001 versus 0 h) of the cells were positive for benzidine staining. Increased hemoglobin production was also evidenced by the pink/red cell pellet observed at 72 h of ALA treatment compared to 0 h (Supplemental Fig. 1). Erythroid differentiation was further validated by measuring the induction of alpha-globin gene expression in response to ALA treatment. The mRNA level of alpha-globin was measured before and after treatment using RT-qPCR. Increased expression of alpha-globin mRNA was detected upon ALA treatment relative to 0 h and normalized to housekeeping gene RPL27 (Fig. 1C). Enhanced alpha-globin expression was also detected at the protein level by immunoblot (Fig. 1D, top blot).

Upon validation of the erythroid differentiation model, we examined whether CDAN1 expression levels were similarly induced in response to ALA treatment. However, CDAN1 mRNA levels were constitutive during erythroid differentiation, as shown by RT-qPCR (Fig. 1C). Consistent with mRNA expression, Codanin-1 protein expression remained unchanged upon ALA treatment (Fig. 1D, bottom blot). Using publicly available data, mRNA expression data was obtained from mobilized human peripheral blood CD34+ progenitors induced down the erythroid lineage [25]. CDAN1 mRNA levels were normalized to day 1 of erythroid induction. Consistent with our results in K562 cells, CDAN1 was constitutively expressed during erythroid differentiation of primary cells (Fig. 1E).

Codanin-1 knockdown results in the Inhibition of erythroid differentiation in K562 cells

CDA-I is an autosomal recessive disease caused by presumed loss-of-function of Codanin-1. Thus, we investigated the effect of Codanin-1 knockdown on erythroid differentiation. K562 cells were transfected with siRNA targeting Codanin-1 (CDAN1 siRNA) or non-targeting siRNA (control siRNA). Erythroid differentiation was induced 24 h post-transfection, subjecting cells to ALA treatment for 72 h or left untreated. Knockdown efficiency was assessed by RT-qPCR and immunoblot. Transfection of CDAN1 siRNA resulted in 40% knockdown of mRNA expression (Fig. 2A). This level of mRNA knockdown led to almost complete absence of Codanin-1 protein expression (Fig. 2B). We next sought to determine whether Codanin-1 knockdown affected cell growth. Cells were counted by trypan blue exclusion at the indicated time points. No significant difference in proliferation was observed, and no apoptotic cells were noted (Supplemental Fig. 2). Cell cycle profile was also assessed in Codanin-1 knockdown cells, and no difference was observed compared to control cells (Supplemental Fig. 3).

We next investigated whether knockdown of Codanin-1 induced any morphological features of dyserythropoiesis, such as binuclearity, observed in reportedly 0.6–2.8% of CDA-I erythroblasts in patients [26]. Cytospin slides prepared from siRNA-transfected cells treated with ALA were stained with Wright-Giemsa, and binucleated cells were scored (Fig. 2C). Codanin-1 knockdown cells demonstrated increased binuclearity compared to control siRNA cells (8.3%, Fig. 2D).

To assess erythroid differentiation in cells deficient for Codanin-1 expression, hemoglobin synthesis was measured by benzidine staining. The increased hemoglobin production associated with ALA treatment was detected in cells transfected with control siRNA, in which 51.1% were benzidine-positive (Fig. 2E-F). However, analogous to the decreased hemoglobin levels observed in CDA-I patients, cells transfected with CDAN1 siRNA displayed a lower percentage (32.7%) of benzidine staining. Based on these results, we hypothesized that knockdown of Codanin-1 would also affect the induction of alpha-globin expression in response to ALA treatment. Thus, the mRNA and protein levels of alpha-globin were measured under Codanin-1 knockdown conditions. At the mRNA level, alpha-globin expression was unaffected, implying a lack of direct connection between Codanin-1 and globin gene transcription (Supplemental Fig. 4). However, immunoblot analysis showed markedly decreased alpha-globin protein levels in Codanin-1 knockdown cells (Fig. 2G). These findings suggest Codanin-1 may be regulating alpha-globin expression post-transcriptionally. We then wanted to determine if restoration of Codanin-1 expression would rescue the diminished level of alpha-globin observed when Codanin-1 is knocked down. Thus, K562 cells were transduced with the pCCL-C-MND-GFP vector carrying wild-type Codanin-1 or empty vector as control. Subsequently, GFP+ cells were sorted and transfected with siRNA targeting the 3’UTR of Codanin-1. This allowed us to only inhibit the expression of endogenous Codanin-1, while expression driven by exogenous wild-type Codanin-1 remained unaffected. At 24 h after siRNA transfection, cells were induced with ALA treatment for 48 h. Endogenous knockdown and exogenous expression of Codanin-1 was confirmed at the protein level with immunoblotting, as shown in Fig. 2H (blot 1, lanes 2 and 3, respectively). As similarly observed in Fig. 2G, knockdown of Codanin-1 resulted in decreased induction of alpha-globin expression upon ALA treatment (Fig. 2H, blot 5, lane 2). Interestingly, re-expression of Codanin-1 resulted in the restoration of alpha-globin expression upon ALA treatment (Fig. 2H, blot 5, lane 4), further suggesting a role for codain-1 in erythropoiesis.

Codanin-1 knockdown in primary human CD34+ cells impairs erythropoiesis

The effect of Codanin-1 knockdown was also studied in primary human CD34+ cells induced down the erythroid lineage. Cells were differentiated following a 4-phase culture system, including expansion and erythroid differentiation I, II, and III, that generates terminally differentiated erythroid cells, as previously demonstrated [15]. To knockdown Codanin-1 expression, cells were expanded for 24 h and then transduced with TagRFP-tagged shRNA targeting the 3’UTR (CDAN1-1) or the coding sequence (CDAN1-2). Non-targeting shRNA (Control) was used as control. TagRFP+ cells were sorted 72 h post-transduction, and knockdown efficiency was measured by RT-qPCR and immunoblot (Fig. 3A-B). Cells transduced with shCDAN1-1 and shCDAN1-2 demonstrated 40% and 24% knockdown of CDAN1 mRNA expression, respectively (Fig. 3A). At the protein level, transduction with shCDAN1 also resulted in a significantly decreased Codanin-1 expression (Fig. 3B).

Once knockdown was confirmed, cells were switched to the erythroid differentiation medium. Cell morphology was assessed to determine whether Codanin-1 knockdown resulted in features of dyserythropoiesis. On day 9 of the erythroid culture (phase I), cells were scored in cytospin preparations stained with Wright-Giemsa, as shown in representative images (Fig. 3C). Binuclearity was present in 8.9% of Codanin-1 knockdown cells and none of the control cells (Fig. 3D). The internuclear chromatin bridges found in CDA-1 erythroblasts were observed under Codanin-1 knockdown conditions but not in control cells. Thus, the effects of Codanin-1 knockdown observed in the primary human erythropoiesis system were similar to those observed in K562 cells and in patients.

During erythropoiesis, progenitor cells undergo sequential stages of differentiation until giving rise to mature red blood cells [27, 28]. Early- and late-stage erythroid precursors, termed burst-forming unit-erythroid (BFU-E) and colony-forming unit-erythroid (CFU-E), respectively, can be identified and quantified by CFU assays [29–31]. To assess the effect of Codanin-1 knockdown on the generation of committed erythroid progenitors, in vitro CFU assays were conducted with cells transduced with shRNA LUC (control) or shRNA targeting Codanin-1 (shRNA CDAN1-1 or shCDAN1-2). Prior to seeding, cells were FACS-sorted for TagRFP and cultured until day 3 of the erythroid differentiation phase I. Colonies were scored after 14 days. Codanin-1 knockdown cells gave rise to a similar number of CFU-granulocyte/macrophage (CFU-GM) colonies when compared to control cells (Table 1). However, significantly lower numbers of BFU-E and CFU-E colonies were detected under Codanin-1 knockdown conditions compared to control. For example, shRNA CDAN1-1 and CDAN1-2 gave rise to 5.7 ± 1.5 and 10.3 ± 1.5 BFU-E and 11.3 ± 1.2 and 16.3 ± 4.5 CFU-E, respectively, while shRNA control cells gave rise to 23.7 ± 7.2 BFU-E and 34.0 ± 8.7 CFU-E (*p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, two-tailed unpaired Student t-test). As compared to the percentage of shRNA control, these numbers represented 88% and 68% decrease, respectively, in the number of BFU-E colonies, whereas CFU-E colonies derived from shRNA CDAN1-1 and CDAN1-2 demonstrated a 77% and 55% decrease, respectively (Fig. 3E). These findings suggest a role for Codanin-1 during the erythroid commitment stage in hematopoiesis.

Based on these data, we hypothesized that knockdown of Codanin-1 would affect the progression of primary human CD34+ cells towards the erythroid lineage. To assess erythroid differentiation in Codanin-1 knockdown cells, flow cytometry was used to monitor the expression of major erythroid lineage markers CD71 and CD235a [32]. During expansion, cells were primarily CD71+/CD235a− but progressed towards CD71high/CD235adim after 5 days. On day 9 of culture (phase I), Codanin-1 knockdown cells demonstrated impaired maturation, as shown in representative flow cytometric contour plots of CD71 versus CD235a (Fig. 3F). More specifically, cells transduced with shCDAN1-1 and shCDAN1-2 demonstrated an 81% and 70% decrease, respectively, in the emerging CD235Adim population compared to control shRNA cells (Fig. 3G; *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, two-tailed unpaired Student t-test). Because the expression of CD235a increased with time, the cells acquired the CD71high/CD235ahigh phenotype after 3 days. Examination of the erythroid culture on day 12 (phase II) also showed a delay in progression under Codanin-1 knockdown conditions. When compared to shControl cells, shCDAN1-1 and shCDAN1-2 exhibited a decrease of 50% and 56%, respectively, in the CD71high/CD235ahigh population. As cells terminally differentiated towards the erythroid lineage (phase III; days 14–21), the expression of CD71 was gradually lost, while expression of CD235a was maintained. By day 21, Codanin-1 knockdown cells, shCDAN1-1 and shCDAN1-2, displayed a reduction of 37% and 54%, respectively, in the terminally differentiated CD71−/CD235a+ population. The results from 2 independent experiments using cells from 2 donors are shown in bar graphs, which show a statistically significant smaller population of CD71+/CD235a+ as well as CD235a+ in Codanin-1 knockdown cells compared to control cells (Fig. 3G; *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, two-tailed unpaired Student t-test). Taken together, these findings suggest that Codanin-1 knockdown impairs erythroid maturation, as shown by the reduced number of colonies derived from erythroid progenitors and decreased expression of erythroid lineage markers.

In this system, enucleated reticulocytes are generated during the final stages of differentiation. Thus, the rate of enucleation was quantified by flow cytometry analysis of terminal erythroid marker, CD235a, and Hoechst staining for DNA. As shown in representative flow cytometric contour plots, Codanin-1 knockdown did not affect enucleation rates (Supplemental Fig. 5; *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, two-tailed Student t-test).

Codanin-1 knockdown alters gene expression

It is not known how defective function of Codanin-1 results in CDA-I. Because Codanin-1 knockdown impaired erythroid differentiation, we predicted that identifying gene expression changes in response to knockdown could lend insight into the molecular mechanisms and pathways underlying CDA-I. Thus, using RNA-seq, we analyzed the effects of CDAN1 knockdown in primary CD34+ cells from day 12 of the erythroid culture, when Codanin-1 knockdown cells demonstrated a significant delay in CD71/CD235a progression (Fig. 3F-G).

Cuffdiff software [22] was used to identify changes in transcript levels in response to CDAN1 knockdown compared to those in control cells. Figure 4A shows the Fragments Per Kilobase Million (FPKM) expression levels of CDAN1 upon knockdown in the primary erythroid cell data sets. In primary CD34+ cells induced down the erythroid lineage, a total of 342 genes were differentially expressed (274 downregulated; 68 upregulated). Codanin-1 knockdown leads to diminished AHSP protein expression

RNA-seq studies allowed us to identify novel target genes involved in the pathophysiology of CDA-I. In particular, we focused on AHSP, which encodes for a molecular chaperone that plays a critical role in erythropoiesis by stabilizing alpha-hemoglobin and preventing its damaging precipitation [14, 34]. In fact, AHSP-deficient mice show abnormal erythroid morphology with intracellular inclusion bodies that decrease erythrocyte lifespan by damaging the cell membrane [14]. The deleterious effects of alpha-hemoglobin precipitation have also been shown to contribute to the pathophysiology of ineffective erythropoiesis in beta-thalassemia [35, 36].

To determine whether knockdown of Codanin-1 also affected AHSP expression at the protein level, we conducted transfections with siRNA targeting Codanin-1 or non-targeting in K562 cells induced down the erythroid lineage. Indeed, immunoblotting results confirmed that protein expression levels of AHSP were diminished under Codanin-1 knockdown conditions (Fig. 4E). Codanin-1 knockdown also resulted in decreased alpha-globin protein expression, suggesting its precipitation in association with decreased AHSP expression (Fig. 4E and Supplemental Fig. 8 A). To further confirm these findings, we instead conducted the extraction of proteins using a high salt lysis buffer (500 mM), which results in the solubilization of precipitated proteins. We speculated that under these conditions the expression of alpha-globin would be unaffected. As shown in Supplemental Fig. 8B, alpha-globin protein expression was unaffected by diminished AHSP expression in Codanin-1 knockdown cells. Additionally, we confirmed the significantly sustained decrease in globin production with Codanin-1 knockdown up to 120 h (Fig. 4D; p < 0.0001, 2-way ANOVA).

Codanin-1 binds to the regulatory regions of the genes it regulates

Codanin-1 has been reported to be a cytoplasmic protein as well as a chromatin-bound protein [10, 37, 38]. Because we observed that Codanin-1 knockdown affects gene expression, we wanted to determine whether Codanin-1 was exerting its function by binding to DNA regulatory regions. To determine the level of Codanin-1 binding at the regulatory region of the genes differentially expressed after Codanin-1 knockdown, ChIP-seq was performed in K562 cells. Two human anti-Codanin-1 antibodies (Cod1, Cod2) were tested and validated by IP assays (Supplemental Fig. 9 A). As a control, we used an antibody against erythroid master regulator GATA1. Interestingly, Codanin-1 immunoprecipitated an enriched amount of genomic DNA when compared to IgG control and was equivalent to the amount of DNA immunoprecipitated by GATA1. More specifically, 7105 Codanin-1 binding peaks were detected; 3266 for GATA1. The peak sizes showed a broad distribution, ranging from 110 to 366 base pairs for Codanin-1 and 96–256 base pairs for GATA1. We quantified the overlapping peaks between the Codanin-1 and GATA1 ChIP-seq sets, with a minimal overlap of one base pair. A total of 1080 overlapping peaks were detected; fragment length ranged in size from 95 to 324 base pairs. Binding sites were then filtered to include those found within 5 kilobases of the transcription start site (TSS). As shown in Fig. 5A, Codanin-1 bound to 1191 genes, with usually one binding site per gene. However, 118 genes had more than one Codanin-1 binding site. GATA1 bound to 456 genes, with 88 genes having more than one binding site. Overall, we detected 118 genes with binding sites for both Codanin-1 and GATA1 within 5 kilobases of the TSS.

Together with the expression data in Codanin-1 knockdown cells (Fig. 4), these findings suggest that Codanin-1 binds to target genes and regulates their expression.

Discussion

Codanin-1 is a ubiquitously expressed protein implicated in chromatin assembly, heterochromatin organization, and cell cycle progression [10, 37, 38]. Mutations in Codanin-1 result in the erythroid phenotype of CDA-I, marked by ineffective erythropoiesis, abnormal erythroid morphology, and iron overload [4, 26]. Nevertheless, the function of Codanin-1 during erythroid differentiation is not fully understood. Our findings using erythroid cell systems provide evidence suggesting that Codanin-1 is required for the regulation of genes implicated in mechanisms underlying the pathophysiology of CDA-I.

Here we study the effect of Codanin-1 knockdown on erythropoiesis and demonstrate that its constitutive expression is required for differentiation. As with CDA-I, an autosomal recessive disease with presumed loss of Codanin-1 function, knockdown of Codanin-1 in K562 and primary human CD34+ cells impaired erythropoiesis. Many features of the CDA-I phenotype were recapitulated upon Codanin-1 knockdown, including decreased hemoglobin synthesis and erythroid maturation and binuclearity. The presence of binucleation in siRNA-transfected cells while maintaining cell growth unlike in the erythroid model with knockdown is possibly due to the prolonged effect of the shRNA on the cells. Extended shRNA-mediated knockdown can induce more pronounced morphological changes in cells, likely due to the cumulative impact of sustained protein depletion on cellular pathways and structural integrity. The knockdown system represents a suitable tool to investigate how loss of Codanin-1 expression leads to CDA-I.

To identify the genes implicated in the inhibition of erythroid differentiation upon Codanin-1 knockdown, global expression changes were measured in Codanin-1 knockdown cells. RNA-seq studies identified novel erythroid target genes. Interestingly, we detected significant overlap between Codanin-1 target genes implicated by RNA-seq, demonstrating Codanin-1 occupancy as measured by ChIP-seq and confirmed by PCR-based ChIP analysis, suggesting a positive correlation between chromatin occupancy and gene expression. In addition to erythroid-specific target genes, Codanin-1 also binds the regulatory regions of genes involved in chromatin remodeling (CECR2), cell proliferation (LRPAP1), and transcriptional and translational regulation (EPAS1, HIPK2, 18 S rRNA) (Supplemental Fig. 10). This gives a preliminary insight into the previously speculated function for Codanin-1 and also brings forth a plausible mechanism for regulation of erythroid-specific gene expression by Codanin-1.

The expression of AHSP was diminished in Codanin-1 knockdown cells when compared to control cells (Fig. 4). Interestingly, Codanin-1 knockdown also resulted in lack of hemoglobin synthesis and decreased alpha-globin protein expression in K562 cells and an early erythroid differentiation defect in primary human CD34+ cells (Figs. 2C-E and 3F, respectively), suggesting that decreased AHSP contributes to the abnormal erythropoiesis observed in CDA-I. In fact, several of the defects observed in AHSP knockout mice have been reported in a severe case of CDA-I [40]. Studies have also shown that altered expression of AHSP worsens the phenotype of beta-thalassemia mouse models, another disease of ineffective erythropoiesis [36]. AHSP-deficient mice exhibited erythrocyte abnormalities, including the presence of hemoglobin precipitates, consistent with impaired stabilization of α-globin chains [41]. Alternatively, the post-transcriptional differences in alpha-globin expression, following Codanin-1 knockdown in K562 cells, may be attributed to yet another hallmark of CDA-1– hemochromatosis. Increased accumulation of iron and a simultaneous decrease in AHSP results in an excess leftover of the alpha-subunit of hemoglobin that eventually form precipitated aggregates in the erythrocyte cytoplasm. Glycophorin A (GYPA), which encodes for an erythroid-specific membrane protein, also displayed decreased expression following Codanin-1 knockdown. These findings correlate with flow cytometry data showing significant decrease in CD235A+ populations in primary human CD34+ cells undergoing erythropoiesis with knockdown of Codanin-1 (Fig. 3F-G). GYPA facilitates the movement of erythroid membrane protein band 3 to the cell surface [42]. Band 3 is encoded by SLC4A1, which was downregulated upon Codanin-1 knockdown. Interestingly, mutated-SLC4A1 zebrafish show dyserythropoiesis similar to CDA [43].

The idea that Codanin-1 may have direct links to transcription and chromatin is exemplified by other CDA subtypes. For example, CDA-IV can be caused by mutations in the erythroid transcription factor EKLF/KLF1 when mutated at amino acid E335 [44–46]. In addition, mutations in the predicted endonuclease C15ORF41 are found in CDA-I cases with no obvious pathogenic Codanin-1 mutation [47]. The physical interaction, either normal or abnormal, of these proteins with Codanin-1 remains to be determined [48].

Our data do not suggest a primary enucleation defect as a mechanism of increased nuclearity. Rather, our primary cell data demonstrate a defect in the emergence of a CD71+/CD235a+ population early in our 21-day erythroid culture. Coupled with Codanin-1 genomic occupancy at target genes, Codanin-1 appears to be important for expression at the early stages of differentiation. We also did not observe any differences in cell proliferation and cell cycle progression after Codanin-1 knockdown in erythroid cells. Another study reported otherwise, showing increased proliferation in cells with Codanin-1 knockdown. However, these differences were demonstrated in non-erythroid cells [10]. It appears Codanin-1 may have a different role in erythroid cells that explains the almost exclusive erythroid phenotype of CDA-I. Recent reports have characterized the interactions between CDAN1 and C15ORF41 (also implicated in CDA-1) [49–51]. Nucleolar enrichment of CDAN1 and C15ORF41 in erythroblasts in addition to other cell types is also shown [51]. Whether the spatio-temporal localization of CDAN1 in the nucleolus during erythropoietic differentiation affects global gene expression is an avenue we are currently exploring.

We show a comprehensive analysis of chromatin occupancy and gene expression profiling by endogenous expression and knockdown of Codanin-1, respectively, providing insight into the mechanisms underlying CDA-I. Results suggest not only that Codanin-1 is required for erythropoiesis but also that Codanin-1 may be acting as a transcriptional co-activator under erythroid conditions. Non-erythroid genes are also perturbed by alterations in Codanin-1 expression. Animal models recapitulating the pathophysiological characteristics, esp. hematological characteristics, of CDA-1 are challenging to generate as homozygous null mice are usually embryonically lethal, much in accordance with the absence of any Codanin-1 null CDA-1 patients [51]. While this reinforces the ubiquity of Codanin-1, the tissue-specific deletion of Codanin-1 can aid in better understanding the erythroid-specific pathophysiology of CDA-1.

Conclusions

CDA-1 is a rare autosomal recessive inherited blood disorder caused to due to mutations in Codanin-1. Functions of the protein encoded by Codanin-1, CDAN1, are yet to be investigated in detail. In this study, we effectively show alterations in erythroid differentiation following CDAN1 knockdown and recapitulate the block in erythropoiesis in primary human CD34 + cells as well following CDAN1 inhibition. Morphological characteristics similar to those observed in CDA-I were demonstrated by erythroid cells lacking Codanin-1. Changes in global gene expression after Codanin-1 knockdown revealed alterations in a set of key erythroid genes, including AHSP. In addition to reduced mRNA and protein expression levels after Codanin-1 knockdown, AHSP also demonstrated increased Codanin-1 occupancy at its gene regulatory region by chromatin immunoprecipitation coupled to high-throughput sequencing. In conclusion, we provide here a basic understanding of how CDAN1 affects erythropoiesis in general as well as under the pathological condition of CDA-1.
