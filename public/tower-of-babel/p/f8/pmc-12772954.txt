The role and competencies of an oncology care coordinator: an essential link in patient-centered cancer care
Authors: Mateusz Grajek, Eliza Działach, Jolanta Meller, Bartosz Urbański, Ewa Malchrowicz-Mośko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOncology care is a complex, multidisciplinary process requiring coordinated collaboration among various healthcare professionals. The role of the Oncology Care Coordinator (OCC) has gained prominence as a key component in enhancing the quality and efficiency of cancer care. Coordinators assist patients in navigating the healthcare system, provide emotional support, and help minimize delays in diagnosis and treatment. The aim of this study was to analyze the level of professional competencies among oncology care coordinators working within the Polish healthcare system. The study assessed self-perceived proficiency across ten key domains and examined the relationships between these competencies and sociodemographic factors such as education level and work experience.Materials and methodsThis was a cross-sectional, quantitative study using a custom-designed self-assessment questionnaire (OCCCSAQ). Participants included a total of 300 coordinators from various healthcare institutions across Poland. The questionnaire measured competencies in communication, work organization, patient education, psychological support, knowledge of procedures and law, stress management, digital literacy, and conflict resolution. Data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics (t-tests, ANOVA, Pearson correlations).ResultsThe highest-rated competencies were interpersonal communication (M = 8.7), psychological support (M = 8.6), and both work organization and patient education (M = 8.5). The lowest-rated were legal knowledge (M = 7.2) and stress management (M = 7.5). Education level significantly influenced perceived competence, with higher-educated coordinators scoring better in procedural knowledge, digital systems, and conflict resolution. Experience positively affected knowledge of procedures and law, but not stress management.ConclusionsOncology care coordinators demonstrated high competence in key areas of patient interaction and care coordination. However, there are gaps in legal knowledge and stress management that should be addressed through targeted training. Specialized roles — for example, focusing on patient education or psychological support — may also enhance service quality and job performance.

Introduction

Cancer care is a complex and multidisciplinary field that requires seamless coordination among various healthcare professionals to ensure optimal patient outcomes. In recent years, the role of the oncology care coordinator (OCC) has gained significant recognition as a key element in improving the quality and efficiency of oncological treatment pathways [1]. As cancer diagnoses continue to rise globally, so does the necessity for well-structured care coordination that enhances patient navigation through the healthcare system, minimizes delays in diagnosis and treatment, and provides continuous psychosocial support [2, 3].

The oncology care coordinator serves as a bridge between patients, oncologists, surgeons, radiologists, pathologists, nurses, and other healthcare providers, facilitating communication and care continuity. The role is particularly crucial in addressing the challenges associated with fragmented healthcare systems, where patients often struggle with complex medical information, multiple appointments, and various treatment modalities [4]. Studies indicate that effective care coordination reduces stress and uncertainty for patients while also leading to better adherence to treatment protocols and improved overall survival rates (Fig. 1) [5].

Beyond logistical support, the competencies of an oncology care coordinator extend to providing emotional and psychological guidance. Cancer diagnosis and treatment can be overwhelming for patients and their families, making it essential for coordinators to possess strong interpersonal and counseling skills [6, 7]. Additionally, they must be adept at health education, ensuring that patients understand their diagnosis, available treatment options, potential side effects, and supportive care measures [8]. Research suggests that when patients are well-informed, they are more likely to actively participate in their care decisions, leading to improved treatment experiences and satisfaction [9].

Moreover, oncology care coordinators play a vital role in addressing disparities in cancer care. Socioeconomic factors, language barriers, and geographical limitations can all contribute to differences in access to timely diagnosis and treatment. Coordinators help bridge these gaps by facilitating referrals, advocating for patient needs, and connecting individuals with financial and social support resources [10–12]. The growing emphasis on personalized medicine further highlights the necessity for care coordination, as treatment plans are increasingly tailored to individual genetic, molecular, and clinical characteristics [13, 14].

As the demand for oncology care coordination rises, there is a growing need to standardize the required competencies and professional training for OCCs. While many healthcare systems have implemented structured care coordination programs, disparities still exist in the level of training, certification, and recognition of this profession across different countries and institutions [15]. The integration of digital health technologies, such as electronic medical records and telemedicine, presents new opportunities for enhancing the efficiency of oncology care coordination, further underscoring the importance of this evolving role [16].

Objective

The primary aim of the present study was to conduct an in-depth analysis of the level of mastery in a selected set of professional competencies among individuals employed as oncology care coordinators within the Polish healthcare system. In the context of the growing complexity of oncological treatment processes and the increasingly multifaceted organizational demands placed on healthcare systems, the role of the care coordinator has emerged as a critical component in ensuring the continuity, quality, and efficiency of patient care. Accordingly, the identification and assessment of the competencies required to effectively fulfill this role is of fundamental importance for informing workforce development strategies, shaping targeted training programs, preventing professional burnout, and designing systemic interventions aimed at enhancing care coordination across oncological pathways.

The study aimed not only to evaluate the overall level of self-perceived competence across ten key domains — including interpersonal communication, work organization, patient education, psychological support, knowledge of medical procedures, legal literacy, information system proficiency, conflict resolution, multidisciplinary collaboration, and stress management—but also to explore the internal relationships among these competencies and their associations with selected socio-demographic variables, such as level of education and length of professional experience. An additional objective was to determine whether statistically significant differences in self-assessed competencies existed depending on contextual factors, including the type of healthcare institution, educational background, years in the role, and patient caseload, in order to identify specific factors that may contribute to either the development or the deficiency of particular skillsets.

Materials and methods

A quantitative research approach was adopted, grounded in the diagnostic survey method using a structured self-assessment questionnaire designed to evaluate key professional competencies. The research was cross-sectional and exploratory in nature, aiming not only to assess the perceived level of specific competencies among oncology care coordinators but also to identify potential areas for professional development and to explore the influence of socio-demographic variables on self-assessed proficiency levels. The data collection was carried out over a four-month period and encompassed a total of 300 participants (N = 300), all of whom were actively employed as oncology care coordinators within various healthcare institutions across Poland. Due to the absence of a centralized national registry for individuals in this role, a purposive sampling strategy was employed, supplemented by elements of stratified sampling. This approach allowed for the diversification of the sample in terms of geographical distribution, type of healthcare facility (public hospital, private clinic, or ambulatory center), level of institutional referentiality, and the average patient load managed by coordinators.

Participants were recruited through professional networks, industry associations, and direct outreach via hospital administration and department heads responsible for oncology services. Participation in the study was strictly voluntary, and all respondents were fully informed of the purpose, scope, and confidentiality of the study, including their right to withdraw at any stage without consequence. All procedures adhered to the principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki, and the research project received formal approval from the Ethics Committee for Research Involving Human Subjects at the coordinating academic institution. Data were anonymized at the point of collection, and access to raw data was restricted to the core research team operating under signed confidentiality agreements.

The study population consisted of 300 individuals, of whom 84.7% identified as female, a demographic distribution that reflects broader trends toward the feminization of care-related and coordination professions in healthcare. The average age of participants was 42.8 years [standard deviation (SD) = 9.6], with the majority falling within the 35 to 50 age bracket. Regarding educational background, 68% of respondents reported holding higher education degrees (bachelor’s or master’s level), 25% had completed secondary medical education, and the remaining 7% indicated other forms of professional development, including specialist certification programs and postgraduate training. The mean duration of employment as a care coordinator was 6.4 years (SD = 4.1), with 22% having worked in the role for less than two years and 17% reporting over a decade of experience. In terms of employment setting, 58% worked in public hospitals, 24% in private healthcare facilities, and 18% in outpatient oncology centers. The mean number of patients under the direct care of a single coordinator was 78.5 (SD = 21.3), underscoring the high workload and organizational demands inherent in this role. Although the sampling was not probabilistic, the inclusion of participants from diverse institutional contexts and demographic profiles enhances the external validity of the findings and permits a cautious generalization to the broader population of oncology care coordinators within the Polish healthcare system.

The research instrument employed was the Oncology Care Coordinator Competency Self-Assessment Questionnaire (OCCCSAQ), which was developed specifically for the purposes of this study. It consisted of two main sections. The first section collected demographic and professional background data, including age, gender, level of education, years of work experience, type of employing institution, average number of patients managed, and regional location. The second section comprised ten items designed to measure perceived proficiency in ten key competency areas identified through a review of relevant literature and expert consultation. These included interpersonal communication, work organization, patient education, psychological support for patients, knowledge of medical procedures, collaboration with multidisciplinary teams, legal knowledge in healthcare, stress management, digital literacy with clinical information systems, and conflict resolution. Each competency was rated by participants on a 10-point Likert-type scale, where 1 indicated a complete lack of competence and 10 indicated full mastery.

The content validity of the questionnaire was assessed through expert review involving professionals from oncology, nursing, health psychology, and healthcare management. The inter-rater agreement among experts was high, confirming the conceptual clarity and relevance of the items. Prior to full deployment, a pilot test was conducted with a group of 30 coordinators to ensure linguistic clarity and to refine ambiguous items. The final version of the questionnaire demonstrated strong psychometric properties. The overall internal consistency, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.88, and subscale reliabilities ranged from 0.76 to 0.91, indicating excellent internal coherence and scale reliability.

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 27.0). The analysis included descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, ranges, and distribution checks), inferential statistics for group comparisons (independent samples t-tests, paired samples t-tests, and one-way ANOVA), as well as correlational analyses using Pearson’s r coefficient. A significance level of α = 0.05 was adopted for all statistical tests. The analyses enabled not only a comprehensive profiling of the coordinators’ perceived competence levels but also the identification of statistically significant relationships between self-assessed skills and key demographic and professional variables. As such, the findings provide empirically grounded insights with direct implications for both theoretical models of professional development and practical strategies for workforce planning and continuing education within oncology care services.

Results

Descriptive statistical analysis revealed relatively high self-assessments across all ten competencies, with mean scores ranging from 7.2 to 8.7. The highest-rated competency was interpersonal communication, which received a mean score of 8.7 with a standard deviation of 1.01. This result is consistent with the nature of the care coordinator’s role, which necessitates frequent, sensitive, and effective communication with both patients and multidisciplinary care teams. Psychological support for patients was rated similarly high (M = 8.6; SD = 1.04), reflecting an acute awareness among coordinators of the emotional toll of cancer diagnoses and treatments, and the critical importance of empathy and emotional intelligence in the therapeutic process.

Equally high were the ratings for work organization (M = 8.5; SD = 1.10) and patient education (M = 8.5; SD = 1.07), which together highlight the dual logistical and educational responsibilities intrinsic to the coordinator’s function. The competency related to collaboration within multidisciplinary teams was also rated favorably (M = 8.3; SD = 1.03), suggesting a broadly shared perception of competence and confidence in team-based decision- making and coordinated care pathways.

Slightly lower, yet still positive evaluations were observed in areas involving conflict resolution (M = 7.9; SD = 1.14), which may suggest a degree of variability in respondents’ experience and confidence in managing interpersonal tensions and disputes — inevitable in emotionally charged, high-stakes environments such as oncology care units. Proficiency in using health information systems received a mean score of 7.8 (SD = 1.30), pointing to generally adequate digital literacy among respondents, albeit with a wider range of variation possibly attributable to generational factors or differences in the technological infrastructure of their respective workplaces.

Familiarity with medical procedures was assessed at a moderate level (M = 7.6; SD = 1.17), which, while not critically low, may nonetheless indicate a need for periodic training and updates to ensure procedural compliance, especially in light of the complex and rapidly evolving nature of oncological treatment protocols. The two lowest-rated competencies were legal knowledge (M = 7.2; SD = 1.29) and stress management (M = 7.5; SD = 1.22). These results are particularly noteworthy given the legal exposure and emotional strain inherent in oncology care. The relative lack of confidence in legal competence may reflect insufficient formal training in medical law and ethics, while the modest self-assessment in stress management raises concerns about long-term risks of burnout and reduced job satisfaction.

To further explore perceived differences between core competencies, a paired-sample t-test was conducted comparing interpersonal communication and work organization. The result was not statistically significant (t = −1.13, p = 0.259), indicating that participants perceive these two competencies to be comparably developed and equally essential to their professional role. Additionally, a Pearson correlation analysis investigating the relationship between psychological support and patient education yielded a non-significant result (r = −0.040, p = 0.491), suggesting that these skills, though both patient-centered, may operate independently. While patient education often draws upon cognitive and didactic strategies, psychological support relies more heavily on affective and interpersonal capacities.

A significant and moderately strong positive correlation was found between knowledge of medical procedures and proficiency in information systems use (r = 0.41, p < 0.01), indicating that individuals who are more confident in navigating clinical protocols are also more likely to possess digital competencies — likely a reflection of the increasing integration of procedural and digital workflows in clinical practice.

Beyond inter-competency relationships, the study also investigated the impact of demographic variables on competency assessments. An analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed that professional experience had a statistically significant effect on certain competencies. Participants with more than ten years of experience rated their knowledge of medical procedures significantly higher (F(2,297) = 5.01, p = 0.008), as well as their understanding of medical law (F(2,297) = 4.62, p = 0.011). Interestingly, no significant difference was observed in stress management across levels of professional experience (p = 0.184), implying that longevity in the role does not necessarily translate into improved coping strategies, and that more structured psychosocial support may be needed irrespective of experience.

Equally revealing were the findings regarding the role of educational attainment. Participants were divided into two groups based on whether they held a secondary or a higher education degree (bachelor’s or master’s level). T-tests for independent samples demonstrated significant differences across multiple domains. Those with higher education scored significantly better in their understanding of medical procedures (M = 7.9 vs. M = 7.1; t = 3.88; p < 0.001), proficiency in digital systems (M = 8.1 vs. M = 7.2; t = 3.45; p = 0.001), and patient education skills (M = 8.7 vs. M = 7.9; t = 3.91; p < 0.001). Conflict resolution was also rated significantly higher among university-educated respondents (M = 8.1 vs. M = 7.3; t = 3.22; p = 0.002). Notably, competencies in interpersonal communication and psychological support did not show statistically significant differences between the two educational groups, suggesting that these interpersonal skills may be cultivated outside formal academic contexts — through professional practice, mentorship, or intrinsic personality traits (Tab. 1).

Discussion

In modern oncology care, the organizational landscape is marked by increasing complexity, multi-stage diagnostic and therapeutic pathways, and the necessity for coordination among numerous stakeholders, including physicians, nurses, psychologists, dietitians, physiotherapists and physical activity professionals, administrative staff, and funding bodies. Within this context, the role of the oncology care coordinator emerges not merely as beneficial but as essential to ensuring coherence, continuity, and patient-centeredness throughout the care process. The coordinator functions as an interface between the patient and the healthcare system, facilitating the timely flow of information, ensuring procedural adherence, and providing comprehensive support across both clinical and psychosocial dimensions [1–3].

The responsibilities of the oncology care coordinator extend well beyond traditional administrative tasks. They encompass a spectrum of clinically and organizationally embedded activities that demand advanced interpersonal skills, high levels of organizational competence, procedural and legal knowledge, and emotional resilience. From a systems perspective, the coordinator serves as a guardian of therapeutic quality and continuity, effectively minimizing the risk of care disruption, informational breakdowns, and procedural delays. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the implementation of care coordination roles contributes to reduced time to diagnosis and treatment initiation, improved patient experience, and lower psychological distress associated with the therapeutic journey [4–6].

Given this broad scope of responsibility, the competencies identified in the present study gain particular relevance. Skills such as interpersonal communication, work organization, patient education, psychological support, knowledge of medical procedures, proficiency with information systems, and conflict resolution are not merely desirable—they form the cornerstone of effective coordination practice [9]. High levels of communicative competence enable accurate information delivery to both patients and medical teams, reduce the likelihood of misunderstandings, and foster the development of trust — an indispensable element in the context of life-threatening or chronic illness. Similarly, organizational skills and procedural knowledge equip the coordinator to manage patient pathways efficiently, optimize resource utilization, and enhance systemic effectiveness [8].

Importantly, the role of the coordinator is not limited to neutral process management; it also encompasses significant emotional and social dimensions of care. Oncology patients frequently report feelings of confusion, fear, and insufficient support—areas in which the coordinator acts as a guide, an advocate, and a source of continuity. The ability to offer psychological support, demonstrate empathy, and respond to patients’ emotional needs plays a critical role in shaping their quality of life and treatment adherence [3].

The findings of this study provide valuable insights into the self-assessed level of professional competencies among oncology care coordinators and contribute meaningfully to the growing — though still limited — body of literature addressing this occupational group. The high mean scores observed across most assessed domains — particularly in interpersonal communication, psychological support, work organization, and patient education — suggest that respondents perceive themselves as well-equipped to perform key responsibilities involving direct patient interaction and coordination with medical teams. These results are consistent with prior research that emphasizes the foundational role of soft skills in interdisciplinary healthcare environments and their influence on the effectiveness and quality of coordinated care [1–5].

At the same time, relatively lower ratings were observed in areas such as legal knowledge and stress management, which may indicate critical competency gaps with potentially serious implications. A lack of confidence in legal knowledge could lead to unintended regulatory violations, exposing both individuals and institutions to legal risk. While coordinators with longer tenure tended to rate their legal knowledge slightly higher, the differences, although statistically significant, did not close the overall deficit. This supports existing calls for the systematic inclusion of legal literacy modules in continuing education programs for healthcare professionals, particularly those in coordinating roles [17].

Similarly, the findings regarding stress management are notable. The absence of a significant relationship between years of experience and confidence in managing stress suggests that on-the-job adaptation may be insufficient without additional institutional support. This aligns with prior studies highlighting the high emotional demands faced by healthcare workers in oncology and palliative care and the corresponding risk of burnout in the absence of structured psychosocial interventions [18, 19]. Researchers have emphasized the importance of providing access to supervision, mental health resources, and organizational cultures that promote psychological safety and open communication [19–21].

An important dimension of this study was the relationship between educational attainment and perceived competency levels. Participants with higher education credentials reported significantly greater proficiency in procedural, technical, informational, and interpersonal competencies such as conflict resolution. These findings corroborate earlier research suggesting that higher education not only facilitates access to theoretical knowledge but also fosters the development of initiative, accountability, and decision-making skills in complex clinical environments [10, 21]. Interestingly, there were no significant differences between educational groups in interpersonal communication and psychological support, indicating that these competencies may be shaped not only through formal education but also via professional experience, personality traits, and organizational culture [5–8].

Of particular interest is the moderate positive correlation observed between knowledge of medical procedures and proficiency in information systems. This relationship may reflect a competency synergy, whereby individuals fluent in clinical workflows are also better equipped to utilize digital systems supporting care coordination. The need for integrated training that links clinical protocols with digital literacy is increasingly recognized in the literature as a response to the digitization of healthcare delivery [22–24].

Recent studies emphasize the pivotal role of oncology patient navigators—often referred to as medical care coordinators—in minimizing healthcare disparities and enhancing treatment pathways, especially within underserved communities. A retrospective analysis at a U.S. Comprehensive Cancer Center revealed that only 22.2% of African‑American breast cancer patients received navigator support during diagnosis and treatment, highlighting a significant gap in equitable care access [25]. Building on this, the Navigator‑Assisted Hypofractionation (NAVAH) initiative demonstrated how embedding navigators within hypofractionated radiotherapy workflows substantially eased logistical burdens and improved treatment adherence among the same demographic [26]. Additionally, a study conducted at the Greater Poland Cancer Centre showed that using predictive anatomical criteria — specifically, an anterolateral heart-to-chest-wall distance of ≥ 1.5 cm on free‑breathing CT — can reliably forecast a ≥ 30% reduction in cardiac and LAD doses when applying DIBH via Active Breathing Coordinator [27].

An important strength of the present study lies in its comprehensive and methodologically rigorous approach to examining self-assessed professional competencies among oncology care coordinators — a professional group that remains underrepresented in empirical research despite its growing significance in contemporary healthcare systems. The study employed a purpose-built measurement tool specifically designed to capture key dimensions of competence relevant to the role, including both interpersonal and procedural domains. The instrument demonstrated high internal consistency, as evidenced by a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.88, and underwent both expert content validation and pilot testing, which collectively ensure its psychometric robustness. Moreover, the sample itself — although obtained through purposive and stratified recruitment — was diverse and well-distributed in terms of geographical representation, type of healthcare institution, educational background, and professional experience. This heterogeneity enhances the external validity of the findings and allows for cautiously generalized conclusions regarding the broader population of oncology care coordinators in Poland.

Despite these strengths, the study is not without limitations. The use of a self-assessment methodology introduces the possibility of response bias, particularly in the direction of social desirability. Respondents may have overestimated their actual levels of competence, especially in domains perceived as socially or professionally valued. While the anonymity of the survey may have mitigated this effect to some extent, the potential for subjective distortion remains. It is also worth noting that self-reported data, by nature, reflect perceived rather than objectively demonstrated competence, which may diverge from actual performance in clinical practice. Nevertheless, despite the subjective nature of self-assessment methodology, the internal consistency of the measurement tool and the diversity of the sample — in terms of healthcare setting, geographic distribution, and experience — support the credibility and applicability of the findings. Nevertheless, future research should consider incorporating triangulated methods, including supervisor evaluations, direct observation, and objective performance data, to further validate the results and reduce the risk of social desirability bias.

In addition, the absence of longitudinal data precludes an assessment of how competencies evolve over time or in response to targeted professional development interventions. The cross-sectional nature of the study captures a static snapshot rather than dynamic change, which limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Another limitation is the lack of qualitative data, which might have enriched the interpretation of numerical results and provided insight into the contextual factors shaping perceived competence — such as workplace culture, management style, or access to training resources. Finally, while the study addressed a wide array of competencies, some potentially relevant domains — such as ethical decision-making or leadership — were beyond its scope, suggesting avenues for expansion in future research.

Conclusions

In the face of growing demand for individualized, holistic, and coordinated oncology care, the competencies of care coordinators must be regarded not as peripheral assets but as central components of a modern healthcare delivery model. Their development, standardization, and integration into national quality frameworks represent key priorities for health policy, professional education, and service delivery. Research such as the present study underscores the notion that investing in the competencies of oncology care coordinators may directly contribute to better treatment outcomes, improved patient experiences, and enhanced operational efficiency across the healthcare system.

The highest rated competencies were interpersonal communication, work organization and psychological support of patients — key skills for effective coordination of oncology care.

Knowledge of medical law was rated lowest, indicating the need for additional training in this area.

There were no significant differences in ratings between interpersonal communication and work organization, suggesting that both are equally important.

No significant correlation was found between psychological support and patient education, which may mean that these are independent skills that require separate development.

Recommendations

It is worth investing in medical law training to improve coordinators’ competence in this area.

Stress management should become an area for development, as it is one of the least evaluated aspects of the job.

Specialization of coordinators in different areas could be considered - for example, some could focus on patient education and others on psychological support.

The results of the survey indicate that oncology care coordinators are highly competent in key areas of their work, but there are some gaps that could be filled with appropriate training. In particular, it would be worth focusing on legal aspects and stress management strategies to further enhance the effectiveness of their work and improve the patient experience.
