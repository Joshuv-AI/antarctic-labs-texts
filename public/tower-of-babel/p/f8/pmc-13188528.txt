Association between persistent symptoms and health-related quality of life in the post-COVID-19 condition: a cross-sectional survey in a nationally representative sample of French adults
Authors: Olivier Steichen, Joel Coste, Cyrille Delpierre, Olivier Robineau, Anne Gallay, Tatjana T Makovski, Cedric Lemogne, Sarah Tebeka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUp to 30% of individuals infected with COVID-19 experience persistent symptoms, known as post-COVID-19 condition (PCC), which can impair health-related quality of life (HRQoL). While post-COVID-19 condition (PCC) is known to affect health-related quality of life (HRQoL), the specific contribution of individual symptoms remains underexplored. This study examined how specific symptoms affect HRQoL in individuals with PCC compared to those who had COVID-19 without PCC and those never infected.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among a nationally representative sample of 1,813 French adults through telephone and online interviews. Participants were grouped according to WHO PCC criteria, based on infection status and 31 persistent symptoms. HRQoL was measured using the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS-29), and impairment was defined as having at least two affected domains. Logistic regressions adjusted for confounders were used to assess associations.ResultsAmong 1,813 participants (365 with PCC, 1,270 previously infected without PCC, 178 never infected), impaired HRQoL was significantly more frequent in with PCC (36%) than in those previously infected without PCC or never infected (adjusted odds ratio: 2.02; 95% CI 1.19–3.42]). The symptoms most strongly associated with HRQoL impairment in PCC were anxiety (OR 3.75; 95% CI: 1.80–7.80), joint pain (OR 2.31; 95% CI: 1.15–4.65), and sleep disturbances (OR 2.14; 95% CI 1.08–4.25), whereas fatigue, shortness of breath, and memory issues had a weaker association with impaired HRQoL in PCC than in other groups.ConclusionPCC, and particularly joint pain, anxiety and sleep disturbance, are associated with impaired HRQoL. Targeted interventions for these symptoms may improve well-being in affected individuals.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-026-02533-9.Keywords: Post-acute COVID-19 syndrome, COVID-19, Quality of life, Signs and symptoms, Cross-sectional studies

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic has affected millions worldwide. Up to 30% of patients with a history of COVID-19 continue to experience symptoms beyond the acute phase, including fatigue, memory and attention impairment, shortness of breath, and muscle pain [1–3]. These symptoms often disrupt daily living and well-being, leading to the notion of long COVID [4, 5]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines Post-COVID-19 Condition (PCC) as symptoms that arise within three months of the acute infection, persist for at least two months, and cannot be explained by alternative diagnoses [6]. These symptoms may persist from the acute illness or follow initial recovery, they may fluctuate or relapse over time, and generally have an impact on everyday functioning.

While previous studies have confirmed that long COVID significantly reduces health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [7], they are often limited by small sample sizes, hospital-based populations, and inconsistent definitions of long COVID, making it challenging to identify symptoms most strongly associated with HRQoL impairment [8]. Moreover, it remains unclear how specific persistent symptoms influence HRQoL in patients with long COVID, compared to other individuals with similar persistent symptoms.

Understanding the symptom-specific associations with HRQoL could help refine targeted interventions for individuals with long COVID. Indeed, specific symptoms influence HRQoL through distinct mechanisms, including direct physiological effects, psychological distress, or functional limitations, each potentially requiring specific therapeutic approaches.

Methods

The report adheres to the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) [9] and to the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology statement [10].

Aim, design and settings

This study aims to: (i) compare HRQoL between individuals with PCC, those who have recovered from COVID-19 without PCC, and those never infected, (ii) identify which persistent symptoms are most strongly associated with HRQoL impairment in PCC, and (iii) explore whether the associations between these persistent symptoms and HRQoL differ between individual with PCC and the other groups.

It relies on a cross-sectional survey evaluating the prevalence of persistent symptoms in individuals with PCC and their specific impact on HRQoL among metropolitan French adults [11–13]. Participants were categorized into three groups: (i) those meeting the WHO definition of PCC (PCC group), (ii) those previously infected but not meeting the PCC definition (infected non-PCC group) at the time of the interview, and (iii) those without a history of SARS-CoV-2 infection (never infected group).

Study population and eligibility criteria

Adults aged 18 or older, residing in metropolitan France, and providing informed consent qualified for the study. Participants were randomly sampled, via mobile and landline phone numbers, by the Ipsos polling institute. For mobile numbers, the owner was directly selected. The landline sample used a two-stage design: one individual per household was selected, using the Kish method.

The target sample size of at least 10,000 participants was chosen to ensure sufficient precision for subgroup estimates, based on the results of a previous study [14].

Questionnaire design

The survey comprised two phases:

Computer-assisted telephone interview (CATI): This phase collected sociodemographic and economic data (age, sex, place of residence, household composition, education, household income), SARS-CoV-2 infection details (date of infections, diagnostic method, hospitalization, intensive care unit admission), and current symptoms (date of onset, and impact on daily activities).

Computer-assisted telephone interview (CATI): This phase collected sociodemographic and economic data (age, sex, place of residence, household composition, education, household income), SARS-CoV-2 infection details (date of infections, diagnostic method, hospitalization, intensive care unit admission), and current symptoms (date of onset, and impact on daily activities).

Computer-assisted web interview (CAWI): This phase collected detailed data on healthcare use in the past 12 months, work leaves due to COVID-19, vaccination, pre-existing conditions from a predefined list of 21 (Additional file, Table A1), mental health using anxiety and depression questionnaires (GAD-7 and PHQ-9), HRQoL, social support, and the pandemic’s impact on income, professional and personal life.

Computer-assisted web interview (CAWI): This phase collected detailed data on healthcare use in the past 12 months, work leaves due to COVID-19, vaccination, pre-existing conditions from a predefined list of 21 (Additional file, Table A1), mental health using anxiety and depression questionnaires (GAD-7 and PHQ-9), HRQoL, social support, and the pandemic’s impact on income, professional and personal life.

Key variables and instruments

Operationalisation of the WHO definition of post-COVID-19 condition

The WHO definition was translated into operational criteria applied to identify PCC cases, using a checklist of 31 predefined symptoms. Participants with confirmed or probable SARS-CoV-2 infection at least three months before the interview were classified as having PCC if they reported experiencing at least one listed symptom that was present at the time of the interview or within the past few days, appeared within three months of an infection, lasted for at least two months, had an impact (even low) on daily functioning, and could not be attributed to an alternative diagnosis according to a physician. In the WHO definition, symptoms may fluctuate or relapse, and functional impact is not required although generally expected. In this survey, however, PCC classification required current symptom reporting and at least a minimal functional impact to enhance specificity.

Health-related quality of life

The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System-29 (PROMIS-29) was used to assess HRQoL. This validated and widely used tool comprises 29 questions that measure eight dimensions of HRQoL: physical capacity, anxiety, depression, fatigue, sleep, social functioning, pain intensity, and pain interference with daily activities [15]. Each dimension is scored depending on the answers to the corresponding questions.

The PROMIS-29 has been used to investigate the HRQoL of the general population during the early phases of the pandemic [16] and of patients after severe COVID-19 [17]. The French translation has been validated and used to construct population reference values in 2016 [18, 19]. PROMIS-29 was selected rather than shorter global instruments (e.g., PROMIS-10) because it provides a multidimensional profile of HRQoL, allowing examination of symptom-specific associations across physical, mental, and social domains.

However, several PROMIS dimensions overlap with common persistent symptoms in PCC (fatigue, pain, sleep disturbances, depression, anxiety). To ensure that our criterion for impaired HRQoL did not simply reflect the presence of these symptoms, we adopted stringent thresholds for impairment in each PROMIS dimension, specifically the 5th or 95th percentile based on gender- and age-specific distributions from the general population, as in the psychometric validation study of the French version of the PROMIS-29 [18] and in previous work from our group [20]. Moreover, because one of our outcomes was the association between specific symptoms and impaired HRQoL, the latter was defined as impairment in at least two PROMIS dimensions.

Adjustment variables

Previous studies have shown that HRQoL, assessed with the PROMIS in the general population during the early phase of the pandemic, varied depending on age, sex, and education level [17]. A previous analysis of our study population identified the number of chronic conditions, mental disorders, and household financial satisfaction as the study variables associated with the risk of PCC [13]. As they are also associated with HRQoL [20–23], we included them as adjustment variables.

The results of anxiety and depression questionnaires were not included as adjustment variables due to the risk of overadjustment, since these mental issues are components and consequences of both persistent symptoms and impaired HRQoL. The association between PCC and depressive symptoms in this survey has been analysed in detail in a previous paper [12].

SARS-CoV-2 infection

Infection status was determined based on participant self-reporting. Cases were classified as confirmed if validated by a positive diagnostic test (e.g., PCR, antigen) or a physician diagnosis. Probable cases included participants who believed they had been infected but lacked formal confirmation, a common situation in the early stage of the pandemic. Multiple infections could be declared and were counted separately if they were at least 2 months apart.

Survey administration

A professional market research provider conducted the survey (https://www.ipsos.com/en). The CATI and CAWI questionnaires were pretested during a pilot study involving eight investigators and 101 respondents, from 3 to 12 August 2022. This pilot assessed the flow of the questionnaire, the consistency of participant classification into the three study groups, the willingness to complete the CAWI, and the duration of each questionnaire.

Full-scale interviews were conducted from September 2 to December 31, 2022. CATIs were conducted by trained professionals following a standardized script displayed on a personal computer screen. The 55 CATI interviewers underwent a six-hour training. They knew that one goal of the study was to detect participants with long COVID. To prevent information bias, they had to follow the algorithms implemented in the CATI system, where SARS-CoV-2 infections and symptoms were recorded in separate parts of the questionnaire. The CATI lasted 15 min on average.

Participants who met the WHO definition of PCC and agreed to participate then completed the CAWI phase online. To manage respondent burden and resources, only a sample of never-infected participants (sampling ratio 1/5.7) and participants infected 3 months or more before the interview but not meeting the WHO PCC definition (sampling ratio 1/2.0) were invited to complete the CAWI. The PROMIS was recorded in the CAWI stage of the study, without human intervention. The CAWI lasted about 20 minutes.

Answers to all questions of the CATI and CAWI were mandatory to progress to the next question. No data was therefore missing.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarize demographic information, symptom prevalence, and HRQoL across three groups: participants with PCC, those previously infected without PCC, and those never infected.

Data are presented as raw numbers and weighted percentages, using the raking ratio. Weights account for both the selection probability (landline versus mobile telephone, within the household for landline phone) and the structure of the French population [11, 13]. Weights accounting for population structure were based on the following covariates: sex crossed with age in 10-year groups, region of residence, size of urban unit, size of household and education level, using data available from the French National Institute for Statistics and Economic Studies (Insee). Re-weighting was performed for the CAWI data, to further correct for differential nonresponse based on age, sex, employment, education, and chronic conditions.

PROMIS-29 results are reported as proportions of participants with impaired HRQoL (at least two affected domains), and proportions of affected participants for each domain. Standardized effect sizes, reported as Cohen’s d, were derived from odds ratios using Chinn’s approximation (d = ln(OR)/1.81), assuming an underlying continuous latent distribution [24]. For HRQoL, differences d > 0.5 are generally considered clinically meaningful [25].

Odds ratios (OR) and third 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed on weighted numbers to assess associations between PCC and impaired quality-of-life, with the never infected group as the reference. A logistic regression model was used to adjust the association between PCC and HRQoL for prespecified confounders (age, sex, education level, household financial satisfaction, number of chronic conditions, mental disorders, Additional file, Fig. A1).

The main analysis assessed the associations between persistent symptoms and HRQoL among participants with PCC. Assuming that 1/3 of participants with PCC had at least two affected PROMIS domains, 40 participants with a given symptom were needed to detect a significant association with an OR > 2 between this symptom and at least two affected PROMIS domains with a power of 0.80. Only symptoms reported by ≥ 40 participants with PCC were thus included in the analysis to ensure statistical power. Collinearity between persistent symptoms was assessed using Pearson’s phi coefficient, calculated from survey-weighted 2 × 2 contingency tables. Observed associations were weak to modest (phi < 0.40), indicating no problematic multicollinearity (Additional File, Table A2). To account for the impact of clustered symptoms, which appears to be a major driver of HRQoL in patients with PCC [26, 27], a sensitivity analysis adjusted for the number of symptoms was also performed. Secondary analyses were conducted modeling individual PROMIS domain scores as outcomes to assess domain-specific associations. These analyses were run to confirm positive associations between persistent symptoms such as fatigue, pain, sleep disturbances and anxiety and impairment beyond their corresponding PROMIS domain, despite limited power possibly precluding statistical significance.

Lastly, interaction analyses were used to test if the associations between the main persistent symptoms and HRQoL differed in participants with PCC compared to other groups. Logistic regression models included terms for the persistent symptom of interest, PCC status, and a multiplicative interaction between the two. Exponentiated interaction coefficients are presented as ratios of odds ratios (RORs) with 95% confidence intervals and represent the relative strength of the association between a given symptom and impaired HRQoL in participants with PCC compared with the reference group. A ROR equal to 1 indicates no effect modification (the symptom has a similar association with HRQoL regardless of PCC status), whereas a ROR greater than 1 indicates a stronger association in PCC participants, and an ROR less than 1 indicates a weaker association. Infected non-PCC participants and never-infected participants were pooled as the reference group to increase statistical power, as previous studies have shown that HRQoL returns to its baseline level three months after acute COVID-19 when it is not followed by PCC [28]. Although the similar PROMIS scores in the two control groups of this study also support combining them, a sensitivity analysis was performed with infected participants without PCC as controls (excluding never-infected participants).

Data management and statistical analyses were conducted using SAS, version 9.2 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Respondent characteristics

A total of 94,627 phone numbers were called: 45,300 were incorrect numbers, 24,754 did not respond, 4,808 were ineligible, 1,111 were either unavailable or abandoned the call before the end of the questionnaire, and 8,039 refused to participate. Finally, 10,615 people aged 18 and older and residing in metropolitan France participated in the study. The mean age was 50.3 years (range: 18–97 years) and 5,556 (52%) were women.

At least one SARS-CoV-2 infection, whether confirmed or probable, was reported by 5,781 (54.5%) of respondents, among whom 5,131 (48.3%) reported at least one infection three months or more before the survey, and 430 (8.4%) of them met the WHO definition of PCC. Among participants with PCC, 18 (4.2%) had been hospitalized for COVID-19, including 5 (1.2%) admitted to an intensive care unit.

The CAWI was proposed to 4,227 participants: all 430 meeting the PCC definition, 2,397 infected participants without PCC, and 848 with no previous known SARS-CoV-2 infection. This study involves the 1813 respondents who completed the CAWI: 178 had PCC, 1270 had a history of SARS-CoV-2 infection without PCC, and 365 had no known history of SARS-CoV-2 infection (Fig. 1). Younger and less educated participants were slightly underrepresented among CAWI responders compared to non-responders (Additional File, Table A3). Symptom-specific associations

Among participants with PCC, the main persistent symptoms associated with impaired HRQoL were anxiety (OR: 3.75; 95% CI: 1.80–7.80), joint pain (OR: 2.31; 95% CI: 1.15–4.65), and sleep disturbances (OR: 2.14; 95% CI 1.08–4.25). After adjustment for the number of persistent symptoms, only anxiety remained associated with impaired HRQoL in participants with PCC (adjusted OR: 2.56; 95% CI: 1.07–4.25) (Table 4). Anxiety was also the persistent symptom with the broadest associations across PROMIS-29 domains (Additional file, Table A6 and Table A7).

Discussion

Key findings

This study analysed the impact of PCC on HRQoL among French adults. Compared to individuals who recovered from COVID-19 without persistent symptoms or who were never infected, participants with PCC experienced significant impairments across all domains of HRQoL. Anxiety, joint pain, and sleep disturbances emerged as the persistent symptoms most strongly associated with HRQoL in PCC participants, the majority of whom experienced an initial mild acute COVID-19 episode not requiring hospitalization. Domain-level analyses confirmed that they were positively associated with impairments extending beyond their corresponding PROMIS domains, supporting that findings were not solely driven by measurement overlap. Anxiety was the symptom with the broadest associations across domains. Moreover, anxiety remained significantly associated with HRQoL impairment even when adjusting for the total number of symptoms. By contrast, persistent fatigue, impaired memory, and shortness of breath, while common in participants with PCC, had less impact on HRQoL in these individuals compared to those experiencing these symptoms outside PCC.

Interpretation

These findings are consistent with previous work analyzing the broad effects of persistent symptoms on HRQoL, although variations exist across studies regarding their relative impact.

Burden of post-COVID-19 condition on individuals’ quality of life

Our findings confirm the burden of PCC on affected individuals, particularly concerning domains such as mental health, sleep, and physical functioning. This is consistent with previous studies that have shown, up to two years after COVID-19, that PCC is associated with significant functional limitations [7, 29] and reduced HRQoL [30]. However, there is a large variability in quality-of-life impairment among individuals with PCC.

Association between specific symptoms and reduced quality of life

As in previous studies, the main persistent symptoms experienced by individuals with PCC in our study were fatigue, shortness of breath, joint pain, anxiety, impaired memory, and sleep disturbances [3, 5, 7, 31]. Our study highlights anxiety, joint pain, and sleep disturbances as the most strongly associated with HRQoL impairment, consistent with prior research on PCC [32]. Anxiety appears to be a prominent symptom, significantly associated with impaired HRQoL even after accounting for the number of persistent symptoms. This strong association could be partly explained by a mediating role of anxiety between other persistent symptoms and HRQoL impairment. Should it be the case, our results would nevertheless still suggest that targeting anxiety may be a useful strategy to mitigate the burden of PCC. The hypothesis that anxiety is closely intertwined with HRQoL impairment indeed aligns with studies on other chronic conditions, such as chronic kidney disease [33] or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [34], suggesting shared mechanisms that warrant further investigations.

Although fatigue, shortness of breath, and cognitive impairment have been previously associated with reduced HRQoL in PCC [7, 28, 29], these associations were unclear in our study. The relatively lower association with these symptoms in our study could reflect differences in measurement tools, population characteristics, or healthcare systems. These symptoms were also less associated with HRQoL in participants with PCC compared to other participants with these persistent symptoms. These findings do not contradict their central role in PCC, but rather highlight that their contribution to impaired HRQoL may be indirect, cumulative, or changed by adaptation mechanisms. Previous evidence shows that the number of concurrent symptoms is a major determinant of HRQoL in PCC [26, 27]. Fatigue and dyspnea are among the most prevalent symptoms and frequently co-occur with other manifestations in PCC, suggesting that their impact on quality of life may be mediated by overall symptom burden rather than by a strong independent effect detectable in multivariable models. Another possibility is that individuals with PCC may partially adapt to persistent symptoms such as fatigue, dyspnea, or cognitive difficulties over time, leading to a shift in how they impact HRQoL. Patients may recalibrate expectations or develop coping strategies, resulting in smaller observed associations with HRQoL despite persistent symptoms.

Generalizability

The surveyed sample may not fully represent the broader French population, especially regarding geographic diversity and socioeconomic status, despite random selection. Participation in the CAWI was higher among middle-aged and higher-educated CATI respondents. Analyses were re-weighted to mitigate this imbalance, although some residual selection bias may remain. As lower age and education categories tended to show worse HRQoL, any remaining bias would likely result in a modest underestimation of the prevalence of impaired HRQoL. Moreover, due to variability in socioeconomic backgrounds, healthcare systems, and pandemic policies across different regions, the findings may not directly apply to other countries. Our sample did not include enough patients with severe COVID-19 (requiring hospitalization) to draw any conclusion regarding this subgroup. This is important because hospitalized patients experience greater reductions in quality of life compared to the general infected population [8]. The prevalence and impact of specific symptoms, such as impaired memory or shortness of breath, may also differ after severe COVID-19. Lastly, the causes or circumstances of persistent symptoms outside PCC were not recorded and are likely heterogeneous. Therefore, analyses of their differential impact on HRQoL across subgroups should be considered exploratory and may not generalize to all causes or circumstances.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include its large, representative sample, the use of a standard definition of long COVID, and a validated tool (PROMIS-29) to assess HRQoL. Additionally, the inclusion of multiple comparison groups enhances the robustness of the findings.

However, this study has limitations. First, several domains of the PROMIS-29 (anxiety, depression, fatigue, sleep disturbances, and pain) overlap with common persistent symptoms of PCC. To minimize the risk of circular findings, we used a stringent definition of impaired HRQoL, requiring at least two PROMIS-29 domains to be affected at or beyond the 5th or 95th percentile thresholds. Each PROMIS-29 domain was affected in about 5% of participants without PCC, supporting the generalisability of these percentile thresholds. Only 36% of individuals with PCC had two or more affected PROMIS-29 domains, highlighting the ability of our definition to capture meaningful and specific impairments, thereby ensuring informative analyses. Moreover, because the overlap between persistent symptoms and PROMIS-29 domains equally affects analyses in participants both with or without PCC, it cannot fully explain the observed difference in the impact of persistent fatigue on HRQoL between these groups.

Second, the reliance on self-reported infection history and symptomatology may have introduced recall bias, potentially leading to misclassification of PCC status, particularly among individuals with otherwise unexplained persistent symptoms. Such misclassification could have biased associations in either direction; however, if individuals with poorer HRQoL were more likely to recall or attribute symptoms to prior SARS-CoV-2 infection, the observed associations may be overestimated. Moreover, although the list of qualifying symptoms was large (31 symptoms), over 200 have been reported in long COVID and all could not be included [35]. Because PCC classification required at least one symptom at the time of the interview with self-reported functional impact, individuals with fluctuating symptoms not present at the time of interview and those with symptoms without functional impact were not classified as having PCC. This conservative approach may underestimate the true prevalence of PCC and possibly increased associations with impaired HRQoL since only individuals with current symptoms and an impact on everyday functioning were included. Third, given the cross-sectional design, associations cannot establish causality, and reverse causation or residual confounding cannot be excluded. Although the study adjusted for some confounders, unmeasured variables associated with the risk of PCC and with HRQoL, such as stress or loneliness [36], could influence the findings. Fourth, the sample size allowed for robust inferences regarding the six most prevalent persistent symptoms only. Nonetheless, due to multiple tests, our results should still be regarded as exploratory. Fifth, the study did not fully explore the clustering or severity of symptoms, potentially overlooking nuanced patterns of their impact on HRQoL. However, previous studies suggest that the number of symptoms, rather than their nature, is the main driver of HRQoL [26, 27] and we adjusted for this number.

Implications for practice and research

The findings have implications for public health and clinical practice. First, interventions focusing on mental health support, pain management, and sleep hygiene could substantially improve HRQoL. More generally, integrating psychological, physical, and social interventions may be particularly effective for PCC management [37]. Second, the relatively lower association of fatigue and memory impairment with HRQoL in our study suggests that these symptoms, while common, may not uniformly translate into severe functional impairment. This emphasizes personalized care based on symptom profiles and functional assessment at the individual level.

Future studies should explore why persistent symptoms affect HRQoL differently in PCC after severe COVID-19 and in PCC compared to other causes. Longitudinal studies are needed to track the progression of symptoms and their effects on HRQoL over time. Additionally, the clustering of symptoms and their combined impact warrants further investigation, as this could refine treatment strategies. International comparisons could also elucidate the role of cultural, healthcare, and socioeconomic factors in PCC outcomes.

Conclusions

PCC imposes a substantial burden on HRQoL, with anxiety, joint pain, and sleep disturbances being the symptoms most strongly associated with impaired HRQoL in our study. These findings suggest the potential value of personalized care and targeted interventions to improve quality of life in PCC.
