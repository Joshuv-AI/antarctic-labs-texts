Carefully Tailored: Doctrinal Methods and Empirical Contributions
Authors: Stefan Theil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDoctrinal research is the distinct methodological approach of lawyers and a core contribution of legal studies to human knowledge. Yet, lawyers do not often explain their doctrinal research methods and by implication struggle to articulate where empirical research can make helpful contributions. The articles suggests that doctrinal research is characterised by three core features that make specific assumptions about law: (i) legal sources are the exclusive avenue for altering rules and principles of law; (ii) legal sources are intelligible, coherent and consistent; and (iii) the success of any doctrinal account is contingent on legal sources alone. On this basis, we can appreciate that empirical contributions are essential: (i) to an accurate understanding of the law; (ii) to critiques, because the law lacks frameworks to evaluate its own operation; and (iii) to surveying legal sources. However, this insight comes with a word of caution: not all empirical methods are well suited to providing doctrinally relevant insights and there are some sophisticated pitfalls to avoid along the way.Keywords: methodology, doctrinal research, empirical research, jurisprudence, artificial intelligence

1. Introduction

Doctrinal research is the distinct methodological approach of lawyers and a core contribution of legal studies to human knowledge. Lawyers can uniquely contribute a deep familiarity with legal sources and the internal framework of the law which overall allows them to describe, infer and revise the doctrinal rules and principles of an ever-evolving legal system.

Yet, legal scholarship is generally more known to implicitly apply, rather than expressly articulate and explain, this doctrinal approach. The relatively under-theorised state of a core method can in some ways lead to a beneficial flexibility, an openness to drawing on different methods and insights to answer a wider array of questions.1 The downside is a risk of losing the methodological distinctiveness of doctrinal research: a confusion of general openness for ‘anything goes’ that encourages prolific and unreflective borrowing of methods whose parameters and limitations lawyers may not understand particularly well. As a result, legal scholarship struggles to explain both what it does when it engages in doctrinal research and, by implication, whether empirical methods can make helpful contributions.

The overall argument in this article is that once we adequately define doctrinal methods, it becomes clear that empirical research is essential to understanding the law, critiquing its operation and surveying legal sources. However, this insight comes with a word of caution: empirical research is a broad church, and not all methods are well suited to providing doctrinally relevant insights. Indeed, the richness of empirical methods masks sophisticated methodological pitfalls, some of which are presented in this article as cautionary tales. The path to these pitfalls starts with a lack of clear separation between doctrinal, empirical and normative claims, the methodological approaches that can justify them and their respective limitations. I begin with a rough outline of what I consider to be the three features making up the irreducible core of an ideal-type doctrinal research.2

First, doctrinal research is concerned with the study of legal sources as identified by the rule of recognition of a given legal system: typically, legislation (primary and secondary) and court decisions. It accepts unreservedly that legal sources are the exclusive avenue for altering rules and principles of law. Therefore, doctrinal research seeks to specify and develop the legal rules and principles that legal sources articulate: taken in isolation as individual provisions, collectively in a particular source or body of law and across the legal system as a whole.

Second, scholarship is distinctly doctrinal (as opposed to normative, empirical, comparative, historical, economic or political) because it assumes the intelligibility, coherence and consistency of legal sources. This point requires more unpacking and justification later, but for now, let me just note that it does not follow that doctrinal research also (implausibly) presumes that the rules and principles we derive from legal sources share these features.

Third, doctrinal research characteristically adopts an internal perspective on the law, viewing it as a self-contained system of rules and principles established by legal sources.3 Where inconsistencies and gaps are identified, these are filled by refining accounts of the rules and principles in a manner that best fits with the ‘inner logic’4 of the legal system.5 Thus, doctrinal accounts evaluate the attractiveness of articulated rules and principles exclusively on the basis of legal sources. Such accounts are ‘normatively inert’ in the sense that the answers they furnish are not impacted by the desirability (moral or otherwise) of the rules and principles posited by legal sources.6

In elaborating these essential features of doctrinal research, I assume and adopt, rather than defend, a positivist account of law.7 The argument thus proceeds on the basis that ‘[i]nany legal system, whether a given norm is legally valid, and hence whether it forms part of the law of that system, depends on its sources, not its merits’.8 To a legal positivist, what ultimately matters is ‘social pedigree’, as discerned through the relevant social practices of a particular legal community.9 That does not, of course, mean that legal positivism rejects any and all relevant interactions between law and morality. Indeed, many positivists recognise that there are various important connections.10 The decisive point is that materials beyond legal sources are only conditionally relevant to a doctrinal account: they matter only and exclusively if legal sources require us to consult them.11 By contrast, empirical and normative research are not confined to examining, nor limited by legal sources in this way. This is because they characteristically adopt an external point of view and thereby necessarily reject, in whole or in part, the core features of doctrinal research I have just identified. Instead, empirical and normative research respectively defend truth claims that are contingent on direct and indirect observation and experience, or an external normative framework (for instance political liberalism) that evaluates the desirability of a given doctrinal position. For the purposes of this article, normative research is therefore different to making normative arguments in the context of a doctrinal account. The former evaluates the law against some external standard: the latter is ‘normative’ in a general sense, but remains doctrinal, for instance by arguing that a certain rule would successfully close a gap in the law.

To clarify the distinction between these types of research, suppose that an insurance dispute hinges on the height of a particular apartment building. Doctrinal research helps us ascertain the legal rules and principles applicable to the dispute laid down in legislation and the decisions of courts. This may reveal a legal rule that hazardous cladding (which falls short of certain fire safety requirements) on residential buildings above 11 meters in height must be remediated by the original developer.12 Notwithstanding that legal argument is likely to ensue over precisely how the building height ought to be measured, at some point somebody needs to survey the building, and this is where empirical research becomes relevant. Determining whether a building is above 11 meters high is only possible from a viewpoint external to the law: namely, direct observation using the standards provided by the metric system.13 Once we have our answer, normative research can interrogate whether the rules and principles we identified ensure a just distribution of building remediation costs in practice, evaluated against an external normative framework such as distributional justice. Normative research thus takes the legal rules and principles as its starting point and evaluates whether they are ‘good’ by the standards of a particular external framework.

As the example suggests, there is no hermetic seal between questions internal and external to the law. Indeed, much legal scholarship is in practice a combination of several methodological approaches: it is rarely exclusively doctrinal, normative or empirical. With the ground cleared, we are able to appreciate the contributions that external empirical evidence can make to doctrinal accounts. The article suggests three broad areas of application.

First, external empirical facts are necessary for an accurate understanding of the law because the law is often conditioned on such facts. For example, legislation can only come into being if a bill successfully clears all relevant procedural steps to become an Act of Parliament.14 When a Bill becomes an Act, the exercise of public law powers is often conditioned on some empirical fact, such as the age15 or annual income of an individual.16 The crucial difference to pure empirical research (conducted, for instance, in socio-legal studies) is that external facts matter to a doctrinal account only to the extent that the law itself renders them relevant. As I explore in this article, this is something we can only determine through a prior doctrinal analysis, which necessarily restricts the scope of meaningful empirical contributions to doctrine.

Second, external empirical facts allow us to evaluate the practical impact of the law on the world and inspire normative arguments for change. They allow us to imagine a world where different doctrinal rules and principles prevail, ones that might better fit and fulfil our normative preferences. For instance, the Independent Review of Administrative Law (IRAL) argued that Cart17 judicial review proceedings ought to be abolished in part due to their low empirical ‘success’ rate and correspondingly disproportionate allocation of judicial resources.18 There is an empirical dependency inherent in this argument, but it is different to the first area of application I identified: legal sources do not rely on these empirical facts for their operation. The test for whether an individual can proceed with a Cart challenge is not contingent on them demonstrating that such challenges are statistically likely to succeed or are a good investment of judicial resources overall. As I discuss later, the IRAL is also a cautionary tale, illustrating the need for rigorous standards in gathering and analysing empirical evidence.

Third, external empirical facts can improve the quality and rigour of doctrinal research because they allow researchers to effectively deal with what would otherwise be an overwhelming volume of legal sources. Researchers may, for instance, extract information from a large database of employment tribunal decisions, or from European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) decisions pertaining to environmental protection to articulate more doctrinally refined rules and principles.19

The article begins by expanding on this rough sketch, distinguishing doctrinal research from other methods and defending its core features (section 2). I then return to the specific contributions that empirical research can make to doctrinal research (section 3), leaving contributions to critical, socio-legal and normative scholarship to one side.20 The article concludes with some sceptical thoughts on the promise of artificial intelligence (AI) and emphasises the important functions served by (human-led) doctrinal research.

2. Defining Doctrinal Research

At this stage, I should clearly distinguish legal sources from legal rules and principles. For the purposes of this article, rules and principles are derived from legal sources, and are the product of a doctrinal analysis. A doctrinal evaluation of legal sources can often support various interpretations and hence a range of (competing) rules and principles. For instance, the case of LA (Albania) might lead us to conclude that as a matter of constitutional law it is permissible to (partially) oust the jurisdiction of the High Court, notwithstanding the Supreme Court decision in Privacy International.21 In LA (Albania), the Supreme Court denied permission to appeal, commenting favourably on the effectiveness of the ouster clause contained in section 11A of the Tribunals, Court and Enforcement Act 2007. This is a partial ouster clause because while it excludes recourse to the High Court on some grounds, it leaves other grounds of review intact. Partial ouster clauses thereby seemingly avoid the constitutional question of principle considered in Privacy International. In that case, some judges had suggested in obiter dicta that a complete ouster clause might not be upheld by courts if enacted by Parliament.

Conversely, we might question whether LA (Albania) meaningfully qualified the decision in Privacy International, given the different factual circumstances, we might disagree generally about the legal relevance of obiter statements or, indeed, about how best to understand the institutional role and constitutional limits of judicial review. It is for this reason that doctrinal accounts more commonly deal with a spectrum of arguable and unarguable interpretations of legal sources, rather than in unequivocally right and wrong answers.

At a basic level, doctrinal research is therefore in the business of articulating rules and principles of law that are plausibly expressed by legal sources. As such, it can serve a range of different purposes, including as the basis for a critical account of law. When conducted for its own sake, doctrinal research has at least three identifiable goals with overlapping but distinct approaches: description, prescription and justification. It is important to reiterate that most real-world legal scholarship is not so easily pigeonholed: it commonly pursues multiple doctrinal objectives and often weaves these into a critical analysis. The following overview is therefore necessarily a heuristic for the purposes of a general categorisation, synergising the insightful accounts offered by Neil MacCormick, Jan Smits and Jason Varuhas.22

First, descriptive doctrinal research primarily attempts to analyse, categorise and systematically present what legal sources have to say on a given issue, including any identifiable gaps.23 The overarching focus is on describing the content of legal sources and deriving the rules and principles that follow from them on their own terms, morally neutral and without any justificatory aims, nor in direct pursuit of any specific material outcomes.24 The quintessential descriptive doctrinal research is commonly found in case notes and practitioner handbooks, which prioritise descriptive accuracy and attention to detail.25 That is not to suggest that there is a sort of automatism to this process that yields a clearly ‘correct’ doctrinal account of any given source. There is a broad range of reasonable views on what is descriptively important about a source, whether a given rule or principle follows from it and whether gaps and inconsistencies are correctly identified.

Secondly, prescriptive doctrinal research has many elements of what Varuhas describes as systematisation, the organising of rules and principles into broader categories within the legal system (for instance public law, private law or criminal law), determining what they collectively require, as well as closing any gaps and eliminating inconsistencies.26 Prescriptive research thus builds upon a descriptive doctrinal account to articulate, refine and defend rules and principles as providing a good fit with the internal logic and overall framework of the legal system. Again, there will be a range of reasonable views on whether a doctrinal account is successful in its prescriptive endeavour. Journal articles often take on this task, especially in terms of articulating and defending rules and principles as a remedy to gaps and inconsistencies. Textbooks perform a similar task when they systematise and explain the law for the uninitiated. They invariably go beyond a descriptive account because they curate key themes and issues for exhibition, simplify complexities and gloss over inconsistencies in order to fulfil their educational mission.27

Thirdly and finally, there is justificatory doctrinal research. This approach derives justifications for certain rules and principles from legal sources and, in turn, uses these justifications to refine accounts of rules and principles.28 These justifications can be values that the researcher suggests are manifested in legal sources or they can arise from more expansive and abstract ‘grand theory’29 accounts, which suggest an underlining, unifying theory underpinning the rules and principles posited by legal sources in a particular area of law. Justificatory accounts thus build on descriptive and prescriptive accounts to offer explanations and justifications for preferred rules and principles, as well as point to desirable future developments of the law. An example is the theory that parliamentary sovereignty is the foundational principle underpinning the UK constitution and specifically the law of judicial review. This ultra vires theory allows us to identify rules and principles that determine whether a decision maker acted unlawfully, beyond the powers conferred by Parliament.30 It also explains how the theory holds even if a specific parliamentary intention on restricting executive powers (for instance on grounds of procedural fairness) is not apparent from statutory language and justifies why we ought to reject rival rules and principles based on other justifications. This could be a rival theory which views the rule of law as the foundational principle and therefore treats the common law as a source of restrictions on legislative power that persist despite contrary parliamentary intentions (common law constitutionalism).31 Unlike normative accounts, which are measured against some yardstick external to the law, ‘grand theories’ remain responsive to changes in legal sources. This is because they become untenable on their own terms if they lack sufficient explanatory capacity for cases decided by courts or legislation passed by parliament.32

What all forms of doctrinal research share, regardless of their objectives, is a common commitment to three core features that render the research distinctly doctrinal: an exclusive focus on legal sources; a foundational assumption of intelligibility, coherence and consistency; and an ultimate contingency on legal sources alone.33

A. Exclusive Focus on Legal Sources

First, doctrinal research accepts that legal sources are the exclusive avenue for altering rules and principles of law and hence the exclusive focus of its analysis. Doctrinal research thus necessarily adopts what HLA Hart called the internal point of view.34 Let me begin by specifying what I mean by legal sources.

Legal sources are the materials that require examination to answer a given doctrinal question. In keeping with the legal positivist outlook, which sources we ought to consult is exclusively a question of social pedigree within a given legal system. Generally, in a domestic context, the relevant materials identified by the rule of recognition include: (i) all forms of legislation, whether primary or delegated; (ii) all court decisions, regardless of where they fall in the court structure; and (iii) subject to constitutional requirements, international law and the decisions of international courts and tribunals.

The focus on legal sources is exclusive in the sense that any doctrinal account can only engage in the close study of legal sources to derive relevant rules and principles. Doctrinal work therefore rejects as constitutively relevant pronouncements on the law that often occur in close proximity to (but do not themselves constitute) legal sources: legal opinions and commentary offered by law makers and judges in interviews,35 dissenting opinions in judgments, all manner of soft law instruments, political declarations, guidelines drafted by think-tanks, non-governmental organisations, civil society groups and international organisations, as well as all legal scholarship.36 We may consult such law-adjacent pronouncements to aid our understanding of legal sources,37 but whatever explanation they offer is at best a contestable account of the rules and principles established by a legal source. This leads to the next important feature of doctrinal research, the foundational assumptions of intelligibility, coherence and consistency of legal sources that underpin any account of rules and principles.

B. Foundational Assumptions of Intelligibility, Coherence and Consistency

The second feature of doctrinal research is that it labours under foundational assumptions that legal sources are intelligible, coherent and consistent.38 In line with the work of Raz, I suggest that if legal sources do not exhibit these features, the legal system they (purport to) create would not be a legal system at all: it would fall short of elementary demands of the rule of law and hence fail in its central coordinating function for society.39

Legal sources must be intelligible and coherent because otherwise they are unable to make provisions that the intended audience can understand and observe in practice. A simple example would be a piece of legislation drafted in a fantasy language that is understood only by its creator or a string of words that do not form a meaningful sentence. For the same reasons, a given legal source must be internally consistent because individuals and society cannot adhere to contradictory demands, and contradictions cannot be successfully integrated into the wider legal system. For instance, the same legal source cannot require an administrative body to perform two mutually exclusive actions, such as that doing the former necessarily implies not doing the latter. Of course, that assumption of internal consistency does not extend to the implausible assumption that two separate legal sources cannot make contradictory demands. Sometimes this happens inadvertently, when fresh legislation is passed in ignorance of an earlier statute, or deliberately, because the aim is to abolish some disfavoured provisions. In those circumstances, a legal system must furnish a resolution mechanism. This might be the rule that Acts of Parliament repeal (implicitly or expressly) earlier Acts,40 or that more specialised provisions take precedence in application over more general ones when they both apply to the same set of facts (lex specialis derogat legi generali).

Without these assumptions and conflict resolution rules, every legal source would stand on its own, unintelligible to other sources, unable to avoid contradictions without an obvious resolution mechanism and incapable of articulating coherent rules and principles worthy of systematic study. Law would be incapable of coordinating behaviour across society, and there would be little point in running competitive democratic elections, debating legislation in Parliament or adjudicating disputes on the basis of legal sources.41 That, of course, does not mean that the rules and principles we derive from legal sources must necessarily share these features, nor that intelligibility, coherence and consistency hold, in practice, across all areas of the law and all legal sources at all times. It is in the end a necessary assumption that enables meaningful doctrinal research, not an empirical or philosophical claim established by evidence or sufficient justification.

A helpful analogy might be found in cherished family jigsaw puzzle sets. Looking at the jigsaw pieces of more complex sets laid out on a table, it is not clear that they should all fit together: the number of pieces can be overwhelming, how one piece attaches to another is not immediately obvious, much less how they will ultimately combine to form the picture printed on the carboard box. And yet, we would never begin, much less complete, the puzzle if we allowed this complexity to overwhelm us. Instead, we assume that somehow the pieces do fit together and cohere into an overall picture: we may start by sorting them according to notable features (colour, texture, pattern, type) before finding the edge pieces to assemble the outer frame. Note that we can engage in this practice even though we know well that over the years important pieces may have been lost (gaps), and that rogue pieces from other sets may have made their way into the collection (inconsistencies). Doctrinal research is similar in the sense that it puzzles over legal sources.

Admittedly, the analogy has its limitations. The legal system is unlike a jigsaw puzzle because there is no grand design ensuring the outcome of a pleasing picture or even the existence of clear boundaries: human societies and their law making are messy and are not delineated by clear edge pieces. There is also generally no one ‘correct’ way to organise and assemble legal sources into legal rules and principles because legal sources are flexible in shape, can fit together in numerous ways and can be assembled to create different pictures. Finally, while a puzzle set is at some point completed, the evolution of the law is continuous and open ended: we keep adding pieces to the legal puzzle.42 However, much like a puzzle set, if legal sources are not presumed to fit together and make sense of each other to form an overall picture, then doctrinal research is pointless.

While a doctrinal researcher must therefore subscribe to the foundational assumptions for the sake of meaningful research, this does not mean that they (implausibly) believe that there are no inconsistencies or gaps in the law,43 nor that there is no room for good-faith disagreement on the rules and principles derived from legal sources.44 Contradictions, gaps and conflicts abound, rules and principles are contestable and not meaningfully separable in any case from a particular jurisdiction at a particular point in time. Our understanding always lags behind the dynamic evolution of legal sources and is subject to a constant process of refinement and evolution.45 How we fit the pieces together, fill the gaps left by missing pieces and determine whether a piece does not in fact ‘belong’ in a set are the subject of fruitful debates.

C. Contingency on Legal Sources

Third and finally, the attractiveness of proposed rules and principles is contingent on the internal logic and framework of the legal system. The process of deriving rules and principles from legal sources is thus characterised by a general absence of any need for external input: the law furnishes its own normative framework, where external empirical facts and morality only matter to the extent that the law itself deems them relevant.46 Doctrinal research evaluates changes in legal sources from an internal perspective to determine how they change rules and principles: either by demonstrating consistency with previously articulated rules and principles or by adjusting them accordingly.47

In this respect, the article takes a different view to Alan Bogg and Neil MacCormick, who both view normative (moral) argument as constitutive of doctrinal research.48 Bogg claims that morality is ‘inescapable’ and ‘integral’49 to legal reasoning. However, as this section will show, that is not the same as a licence to introduce moral arguments into doctrinal accounts at any point, nor does it alter the ultimate benchmark of doctrinal accounts. Moral argument is only compatible with doctrinal accounts if such reasoning is required by legal sources. For instance, a piece of legislation may ask a decision maker to determine whether a certain external empirical condition has been satisfied or instruct a judge to look towards moral principles to resolve a case where posited legal norms do not provide a complete answer.50 In these cases, legal sources may play, in the words of John Gardner, a ‘nonredundant but also nondecisive role’ in answering a legal question.51 Nonredundant because only the legal source can authorise the doctrinal researcher to turn to empirical input or moral argument, and nondecisive because the legal source alone does not answer the question without recourse to said empirical input or moral argument. This also explains why a doctrinal account is the necessary starting point for so much legal scholarship: if one cannot give an account of what the law requires on its own terms and hence whether empirical or moral input is needed, then other methods cannot sensibly be applied to its study.52

As previously suggested, this process is emphatically not reducible to a purely descriptive and deductive exercise, at the end of which one is left with an unequivocally correct answer.53 Legal sources do not contain a set of clear, comprehensive and self-executing instructions, and doctrinal research always involves an element of rational reconstruction based on values.54 Even the seemingly straightforward provision that no vehicles are permitted in the park is open to a range of plausible interpretations and may produce outcomes that are difficult to predict.55 This is because law is ultimately the product of a shared social practice between legal officials,56 and the fact that legal sources do not exist in a vacuum: their scope and content are determined by and evolve with the legal system around them.57 This also explains why the rules and principles that a doctrinal account derives from legal sources may be different to what its authors (originally) intended.58

All of this leaves the legal formalism at the heart of many enthusiastic accounts of AI59 on shaky conceptual ground.60 Language is simply not as clear, and legal sources not as conclusively determinative of rules and principles at any given moment, as would be required for primary reliance on machine learning and natural language processing. There is a complicated interdependence between legal sources over time which leaves an irreducible subjective element in any doctrinal account, especially in the application of rules and principles to specific scenarios.

Even a descriptive doctrinal account is therefore never entirely detached from prior assumptions about the nature of law, conceptions of the legal system and its underlining values, as well as views on what is descriptively important about legal sources—whether consciously articulated or subconsciously pursued.61 While the existence of this subjective element clearly matters, it does not mean that doctrinal research necessarily collapses into the subjective views of the researcher. As we shall see, doctrinal research remains eminently feasible precisely because any doctrinal account can only draw upon and must justify itself against the benchmark provided by legal sources: no matter how normatively attractive an account, it is untenable if it cannot be reconciled with legal sources.

I will now turn to consider some of the implications of this contingency on legal sources, namely the difficulties that a subjective element introduces for the separation of doctrinal work from normative scholarship, and what this all means for seemingly paradoxical critiques of legal sources on a doctrinal basis.

(i) Separating doctrinal from normative research

Admittedly, the subjective element inherent in doctrinal research can make it difficult to draw a clear line between internal doctrinal work and external normative research. As I mentioned, the doctrinalist has a subjective element with respect to the values they take the legal system to manifest and what they find descriptively important about legal sources. Meanwhile, a normative account has a subjective element with respect to the chosen (moral) framework that grounds their appraisal of the law.

The crucial difference is that the doctrinalist proceeds from an internal perspective and therefore accepts legal sources as the ultimate benchmark for the attractiveness of articulated rules and principles. As we saw, external evidence does not add anything to a doctrinal account unless the law itself requires such inputs. The reason for this is—contrary to the musings of Christopher McCrudden62—that justice, morality, utility, policy and social science evidence do not constitute sources of law, nor do they necessarily offer us an account of the rules and principles that follow from them. It is therefore of no consequence to a doctrinal account whether the law provides an economically sensible approach to societal issues63 or whether it is attractive when appraised against a liberal-egalitarian framework.64 The internal perspective of the doctrinalist restricts what type of account can be plausibly developed and justified.65 By contrast, normative accounts may well work extensively with legal sources, but the ultimate benchmark is their chosen external framework, unencumbered by limitations imposed by the internal point of view.

None of this is to say that truth claims contingent on external normative frameworks are less worthwhile. They are a crucial tool for critiquing the law, for instance Victorian conceptions of the rule of law and its role in the governance of the British Empire.66 External empirical data can assist normative accounts, for instance those that critically evaluate whether constitutionalising human rights improves respect for rights in practice.67 Such accounts are essential for the evolution of law because they imagine possible changes to legal sources and at times decisively drive such change, especially through policy debate in government and Parliament. Consider, for instance, the impact that external normative frameworks like economic liberalism had on shaping domestic and international legal systems and how these in turn perpetuated economic liberalism.68 The law is, in this sense, shaped by and a key shaper of human society.69

(ii) Wrongly decided cases

What should we make, therefore, of claims that a court wrongly decided a case? From a doctrinal perspective, such claims may simply be empty because the statement has no consequences for the doctrinal position.70 The ‘wrong’ decision contributes to the body of legal sources on which we rely for our articulation of legal rules and principles: the ground on which any doctrinal account must stand has shifted.71 For instance, the decision in Miller/Cherry presented a change in legal sources that at least on some accounts altered the rules and principles surrounding prorogation, parliamentary sovereignty and proceedings in Parliament.72 There is a lively debate about the exact nature and extent of these changes, but dismissing all impact on legal rules and principles appears untenable. That said, there are three ways in which critiques of a change to legal sources are doctrinally intelligible, but only the first two are doctrinal claims following the categorisation of the article.

The first is to understand such claims as historical. In other words, authors would be claiming that at the relevant time when the decision in Miller/Cherry was taken, before the ground shifted, legal sources required a different outcome. The decision was wrong because it erred in its interpretation and application of the law as it stood at the time.73 Such claims are historical because they plainly do not impact on the relevance of Miller/Cherry for the current doctrinal position, although they may well illuminate how that position came about.

Second, critiquing a court decision as wrong can be a doctrinal claim if the author denies that the change in legal sources brought about any material change to the rules and principles we derive from them. Doctrinalists certainly must take changes to legal sources seriously, but it does not follow that they have to agree that underlining rules and principles have changed, nor necessarily for the reasons offered by a particular court.74 There are more and less convincing justifications for court decisions, and over time alternative explanations may better account for their outcome and trajectory. The argument would therefore not so much dismiss the decision in Miller/Cherry as ‘wrong’ than argue for its limited significance, whether on the basis of its peculiar factual background or because it is an aberration in an otherwise coherent doctrinal picture (a rogue puzzle piece). A misguided decision can be overturned or revisited, and a poorly drafted piece of legislation can be repealed.

However, as the weight of the legal sources shifts, and grows to accept and incorporate changes, it becomes increasingly difficult to present them as aberrations.75 Jane Stapleton rightly critiques the tendency to ignore court decisions that undermine a preferred ‘grand theory’.76 She suggests that a reactionary dismissal of bothersome cases is often rooted in a prior normative agenda that is not responsive to the doctrinal reality. For instance, an account may suggest that tort law can be explained through one unifying principle of economic efficiency and any cases that do not fit with this explanation are either not really relevant to tort law or simply wrong and should therefore be discarded.77 As discussed, a limited capacity to explain court decisions suggests that the implicit benchmark is not legal sources but instead an unarticulated normative preference, for instance in economic efficiency as an organising principle of tort law.78 At this point, such theories cross over from making a doctrinal argument into the realm of the normative, our third and final category.

The third way of understanding claims that a court decision is wrong is to take them as normative statements. Theories that no longer treat legal sources as the ultimate benchmark for the attractiveness of their account are best understood in this way. They are normative because of their (implicit or express) suggestion that a change to legal sources is at odds with an otherwise preferable approach required under a framework external to legal sources. For instance, one might argue that the UK Supreme Court used parliamentary sovereignty and parliamentary accountability in a novel way that usurped a piece of the political constitution in Miller/Cherry. Prorogation was previously governed by constitutional convention, and this development represents an unpalatable (and hence ‘wrong’) shift towards legal constitutionalism. To be clear: there is nothing at all wrong with making such an argument in legal scholarship. However, it cannot be defended doctrinally, and instead requires a different method, one that normatively justifies why we ought to prefer political constitutionalism over rival organising principles in evaluating the law.

3. Taxonomy: Empirical Contributions

Now that we have a clear understanding of the core features of doctrinal research, we can appreciate where empirical research is helpful to doctrinal work, especially in mixed methods settings: empirical dependency, critical perspectives and preparation are considered below, along with strategies to avoid common methodological pitfalls, which I introduce through cautionary tales.

A. Empirical Dependency of the Law

The first area of application arises where the law is dependent on establishing an external empirical fact. Complications are introduced into the analysis chiefly through the rules of evidence, burdens of proof and legal fictions through which the law connects with the outside world.

(i) Easy cases

Easy cases arise where a legal regime may only come into force, or a legal power may only be exercised by an official, on the condition of some external empirical facts being satisfied. This can be quite straightforward, for instance requiring that the Secretary of State ‘appoint’ a day for a statutory compensation scheme to come into force,79 or more complex, involving layers of external empirical and legal requirements. In the Evans case, an accountable person had a statutory power under section 53(2) of the Freedom of Information Act 2000, triggered once they formed an opinion ‘on reasonable grounds’ that relevant departments were entitled to refuse disclosure, for instance of the advocacy letters written by Prince Charles (as he then was) to government departments, the so-called ‘black spider memos’.80 Even seemingly straightforward legal provisions that are impacted by the age81 or annual income of an individual may require complex empirical analysis.82 In these areas, lawyers and courts rely on tailored expert evidence to determine whether a legal test, contingent on external empirical facts, has been met. The results are then mediated through legal sources, legal fictions, rules of evidence and burdens of proof. Therein lies a considerable limitation for any successful contribution of empirical research to doctrinal work, which I now turn to explore through two examples.

(ii) Empirical analysis missing the mark

Even where an external empirical dependency has been doctrinally established, it is crucial to recognise that evidence requires translation before it is serviceable for doctrinal accounts. This is because many areas of law have a complicated set of rules that manage the complexity of the outside world. The law may assign the burden of proof to one party and specify a standard, or turn to legal fictions, falsehoods that are knowingly relied upon to facilitate a smooth operation of the law.83 In this way, the law makes ‘a judgment about the probability and moral tolerability of false positives vis-à-vis false negatives’, but does not insist upon complete empirical certainty.84 EU consumer protection law provides a straightforward example. The burden is on the manufacturer to demonstrate that a sold product was not defective from the start within the first 12 months of a fault manifesting.85 Statistical fault rates from the factory that produced the product may be helpful but on their own inconclusive. The burden of proof requires demonstrating that the specific product, bought by the individual bringing a claim, was not defective. Given that detailed information on the quality of a specific item at the moment of sale is often impossible to furnish, or only obtainable at an economically disproportionate cost relative to the value of the item, the burden of proof often eliminates the practical need for empirical input: if a defect manifests itself within the first 12 months, the consumer is typically refunded or offered a replacement.

Even where empirical inputs are still practically useful to a case, they might miss the mark if they are conducted with an insufficient attentiveness to doctrinal requirements. Examples are attempts in the field of attribution science to contribute to lawsuits surrounding climate change. In some ways, both law and attribution science grapple with the same fundamental problem: namely, that absolute certainty on the link between climate change and specific extreme weather events is unattainable. However, that does not mean that their respective responses are straightforwardly compatible.

Scientific modelling of climate change draws inferences from robust data that can be reliably analysed and modelled to allow for greater or lesser confidence in establishing a statistically significant correlation.86 The standard legal test, by contrast, asks whether ‘but for’ the existence of x, an outcome y would have occurred. Even the most advanced attribution science cannot establish whether, but for the CO2 emissions of a specific German energy conglomerate, a glacier in Peru would not have melted and thus threatened the home and livelihood of the applicant.87 In all likelihood, attribution science is likely to conclude that the emissions of a specific energy conglomerate were not causal because the harm would have occurred anyway due to the overwhelming impact of other emission sources. In such circumstances, the law typically avails itself of workarounds to avoid empirical uncertainties or, indeed, outcomes that cut against the logic and underlining values of the legal system.88 It is precisely these workarounds that can make it difficult for attribution science to contribute meaningfully. Attribution science cannot by itself satisfy conventional legal causality requirements (‘but for’ causality) and would be unhelpful for two typical modifications that the law adopts at such evidential impasses.

To appreciate why, consider the related example of asbestos exposure cases. In the same way as asbestos exposure litigation refashioned the standard legal test for causality, we can imagine that the law may eventually provide that proof of a material contribution to the general risk of climate change events is sufficient proof of a material contribution to the damage caused by a specific climate change event.89 Under such a doctrinal approach, it would no longer matter whether the emissions of a specific energy conglomerate were causal for the melting of a glacier and ensuing damage to property: it would suffice to show that the emissions contributed to global greenhouse gas emissions, thus heightening the underlying risk of climate change events. Alternatively, legislation may create a statutory climate change damage insurance that compensates individuals for certain types of damages that are legally deemed to be caused by climate change. This insurance would pay out to individuals regardless of whether a particular damage event can be causally linked to greenhouse gas emissions and climate change.90

It is not clear that either of these typical legal responses call for scientific modelling, nor that they would benefit decisively from establishing statistical correlation. The first approach is only interested in material contributions to a general risk of climate change events (not specific attribution), which, in principle, may be empirically no more complicated than assessing annual CO2 emissions. Meanwhile, the second approach only asks whether a recognised damage has occurred: it does not matter whether it can be linked to climate change.

(iii) Answering doctrinal questions with empirical analysis

Adam Perry provides an example of an empirical approach to doctrinal questions with a far more ambitious objective.91 His goal is a probabilistic analysis of parliamentary intention given certain doctrinal evidence: the words Parliament used as well as the context and supporting material on a given statutory provision.92 To accomplish this, Perry relies on Bayes’s theorem: a method for evaluating the probability of an event, based on prior knowledge of conditions relevant to that event. Depending on whether a judicial interpretation of a provision matches the thus determined probable parliamentary intention, Perry suggests we can discern whether the judicial interpretation of statutory language is ‘natural’ or ‘strained’.93 While this may at first blush appear as a useful empirical (probabilistic) contribution to doctrinal analysis, on closer inspection there are some significant problems. It is doubtful whether we can apply Bayes’s theorem to statutory interpretation in this way and whether it can yield meaningful insights.

A slightly simplified example may assist at this stage to better understand Bayes’s theorem. Assume that a hiker in a forest sees smoke in the distance and is trying to decide whether this indicates a dangerous fire. The hiker knows that dangerous fires are overall a rare occurrence in the forest (1% probability), but almost all dangerous fires cause smoke (90%). On the other hand, it is also possible for smoke to result from controlled fires at campsites in this area (10%). Applying Bayes’s theorem, we arrive at a 9% probability that the smoke observed by the hiker is caused by a dangerous fire.94

Perry applies this rationale to the legal world against the backdrop of the ouster clause found in section 4 (4) of the Foreign Compensation Act 1950, the subject of the House of Lords decision in Anisminic.95 The background to the case is the Suez Crisis and the ensuing seizure by the Egyptian government of property owned by the appellant company. The tribunal held that the company was not eligible for compensation because their successor in title was not of British nationality as required under its interpretation of the Act.

Perry posits that the inclusion of the ouster clause in the Act lends itself to two competing interpretations of parliamentary intention: either (i) Parliament intended to oust the jurisdiction of the High Court in all circumstances; or (ii) it intended to do so only for legally ‘valid determinations’. Perry then assigns numerical values to the evidence, depending on the perceived level of support for either possibility, and ultimately concludes that Parliament was more likely to have intended option (i). Those familiar with Anisminic will have noticed that the House of Lords ultimately concluded that option (ii) was the correct interpretation. The ouster clause was held ineffective because the tribunal’s interpretation of ‘successors in title’ was flawed and hence beyond its legal powers, meaning there was no valid determination. From Perry’s perspective, this is a ‘strained’ interpretation because it is at odds with the ‘probable’ intention of Parliament. While this approach gives the impression of a probabilistic analysis, it may ultimately do little more than express a prior doctrinal assessment in numerical terms.

The reason a Bayesian analysis is superficially attractive is because it relies on an oversimplification that shoehorns an inherently doctrinal analysis into probabilistic constraints. Note that a foundational assumption of Bayes’s theorem is a limited set of possible events that respond to known evidence. In our hiker example, the two possible events are either a dangerous or a non-dangerous fire, which respond to our evidence on smoke. Perry assumes, but does not justify, that there are only two possible parliamentary intentions manifested in the ouster clause: either ousting the jurisdiction of the High Court or ousting it only for legally valid determinations.96 Whether this assessment is correct is far from clear. Parliament might just as well have intended to allow further exceptions that were simply not at issue in Anisminic. There are also some difficulties in transferring this binary presumption of parliamentary intention to any case where an administrative body is entitled to interpret the jurisdictional boundaries of its own powers within the margins drawn by irrationality.97 In these cases, courts have consistently held that Parliament properly (legally) intended a wide and indeterminate range of things when enacting words into statute.

As a starting point, we therefore need to determine which parliamentary intentions are plausibly reflected in the ouster clause before applying Bayes’s theorem. Parliament may well have a range of policy intentions, including ousting the jurisdiction of the High Court entirely, or something short of this outcome. However, much like broadcast interviews of former judges, policy intentions are not legal sources. Only those intentions that Parliament successfully enacts into legislation are legally relevant. We therefore require a preliminary doctrinal analysis, one that interprets and weighs the evidence to ascertain which legal intentions are plausibly reflected in the ouster clause. An analysis that posits only two possible intentions as its starting point and then weighs the evidence only in relation to those intentions is putting the cart before the horse.

This leads us to a further issue. While it is entirely fair to compare only those parliamentary intentions ultimately considered by the court in Anisminic, we can only proceed with a Bayesian analysis if we have an awareness of all possible parliamentary intentions. Otherwise, we cannot be sure that we are weighing the evidence fairly. This is because the support that a piece of evidence lends to one intention over another is impacted by the overall number of possible parliamentary intentions it supports and to what degree it does so.

Taking our hiker example, even if we are only interested in determining the probability of a dangerous fire, it matters to our analysis whether there are other possible explanations for the smoke other than a non-dangerous fire. For instance, suppose that the smoke might also be the result of solid carbon dioxide (dry ice) used during occassional horror movie shoots in the forest (5% probability). If there are more than two possible sources of smoke (dangerous fire, non-dangerous fire and dry ice), then we are left with an overall lower likelihood of a dangerous fire (6%).98 In our legal scenario, the decision in Anisminic is of no assistance in determining the number of plausible legal intentions. Anisminic cannot be the object of our Bayesian analysis and simultaneously furnish sufficient evidence for a foundational premise of the analysis without risking circular reasoning: the doctrinal evidence on possible parliamentary intentions must come from the Act itself.

But even if we granted, for the sake of argument, that the Act reflects only two possible parliamentary intentions, we would still struggle to apply Bayes’s theorem. This is because we need to determine the relative weight of the evidence in support of all possible intentions. In other words, a doctrinal analysis must evaluate (and translate into numerical values) evidence (text, context and supporting material) that has a wide range of possible interpretations: both when considered in isolation and certainly once the evidence makes contact with other rules and principles, as well as values of the legal system. Parliament cannot freeze the meaning of legislation in time because it legislates in the context of an ever-changing legal system,99 and it cannot in any case conclusively determine the application of legal rules to particular cases before a court.100

If support for this claim is needed, I would refer readers to any judgment that contains multiple opinions arriving at different interpretations of the same statutory provision.101 In the context of ouster clauses, Privacy International is a useful example for the range of interpretations and conclusions legislation can support on parliamentary intention even if judges happen to agree on the ultimate outcome.102 It is notable that Perry offers no doctrinal justification for his assumptions on the probabilistic weight of the evidence he considers: the numerical values seem to be entirely arbitrary.103

A rejoinder might be that this line of critique misses the mark: it focuses unduly on the contested interpretation and weighing of the evidence over the raw (and often binary) judicial outcomes. One could argue that it is irrelevant that the judges in Privacy International disagreed as to the relative weight of the evidence: what matters is a majority agreement on the outcome. There are echoes of this argument in Perry’s suggestion that the notion of statutory interpretation may be a fiction, even though it provides a ‘plausible enough’ approach for legal analysis.104 That point is helpful as far as it goes, but it does not address the fundamental problem.

If we adopt such an outcome-orientated outlook, we are veering close to a legal realist position: the idea that the law has no (or only limited) normative force, especially in the decision-making process of the judiciary.105 However, that legal realist approach, whatever its merits, is not open to us when conducting a Bayesian analysis of statutory provisions. Such an analysis can only proceed from a premise that legal realists are urging us to reject: we are committed to statutory language having normative force and thus probabilistic relevance for the underlining parliamentary intention. It is hard to see how the text, context and supporting material could be of use for discerning parliamentary intention otherwise. In the terms of our hiker example, if a ‘fire realist’ denies that the presence of smoke and campsites has any probabilistic relevance for dangerous fires, then they have no basis for a Bayesian analysis.

Taking on board these concerns, we can start to appreciate that the seemingly straightforward weighing of the evidence to determine parliamentary intention in the Foreign Compensation Act 1950 is quite complex. The research cannot be meaningfully reduced to a Bayesian probability analysis because the number of possible intentions and the weight assigned to the evidence are decisive and only plausibly justifiable on the basis of a prior doctrinal analysis.

Assume, however, that we overcome these problems and successfully complete the necessary prior doctrinal analysis. We both determine the number of possible parliamentary intentions and quantify the weight of the evidence in a consistent and principled manner. We would end up inadvertently undermining much of the usefulness of the Bayesian analysis. Our extensive preliminary analysis essentially determines what Parliament probably intended and in turn answers precisely the question that the Bayesian analysis was supposed to illuminate. What more would a probabilistic approach add beyond expressing the results of our doctrinal conclusions in numerical terms?

B. Critical Perspectives from Outside the Box

The second area of application for empirical research is where we desire a perspective on the law from outside the box of legal doctrine. For instance, we might explore the political, cultural or societal forces that influence the law, and how its practical application impacts on individuals and society, or we might seek to evaluate a contemplated reform that relies on empirical claims.106 All of this can provide the fruitful ground for critiques and advocating for change on the basis of an external normative framework.

(i) Informing policy options

Empirical work can open the law to a universe of valuable insights that are productively integrated into a wide range of legal scholarship. Socio-legal studies is an entire interdisciplinary field that focuses on exploring law as a social phenomenon. It aims to understand how legal ideas, practices and institutions function and are influenced by cultural, economic, historical, political and social forces. These are perspectives that a doctrinal approach simply cannot provide. For instance, Joe Tomlinson, Lewis Graham and Alexandra Sinclair used such analysis to argue that judicial review of delegated legislation does not unduly interfere with executive law making.107 Such work can then be a helpful guideline to reforms or provide an avenue for critiquing doctrinal accounts. When empirical work is introduced in such a supporting role to weigh up policy options, it is crucial that it follows rigorous standards in terms of study design, analysis and supported conclusions. The IRAL undermined its argument because it missed the vast majority of Cart proceedings in its dataset and therefore significantly underestimated the ‘success’ rate in its final report.108 There were also considerable methodological problems with the adopted definition of ‘success’. While the IRAL accepted that success in judicial review generally does not exclusively come in the form of a favourable (and published) judgment, it seemingly omitted accounting for this fact in its evaluation of the ‘success’ rate of Cart judicial review proceedings.109 Doctrinal, empirical and normative research can work together successfully to produce richer legal scholarship, but recent attempts more often underline the need to be clear about the respective areas of application and the claims that each analysis can helpfully illuminate.110

(ii) Methodological confusion

An example of methodological confusion in a mixed methods setting can be found in an article by Laurence Helfer and Erik Voeten.111 From an initially sound starting point of coding Grand Chamber judgments of the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) as to the existence of so-called ‘walking back dissents’, they draw empirically and doctrinally questionable conclusions.

The authors define ‘walking back’ dissenting opinions as those that assert the majority have overturned ‘prior rulings or settled doctrine in a way that favours the respondent government’.112 On this basis, they conclude that: (i) an increase in the number of walking back dissents is observable in the examined time periods; (ii) the frequency of walking back dissents is a suitable way of determining whether majority decisions have given rights a regressive interpretation favouring the state; and (iii) walking back dissents indicate that majority decisions are more deferential to states as a response to political pressure. The first proposition is methodologically sound (albeit with some lingering concerns over coding reliability), while the latter two contain some degree of methodological confusion. I examine these propositions in turn.

First, it is debatable whether there has been an increase in the number of walking back dissents in the examined period. A review article published by Alec Stone, Wayne Sandholtz and Mads Andenas claims there are deficiencies in the coding operation and a failure to find clear evidence of ‘walking back’ allegations in a representative sample.113 A rejoinder published by Helfer and Voeten suggests that this critique is mistaken and down to the use of significantly narrower coding criteria than employed by their original study.114

A third possibility is that the discrepancies are attributable to ambiguities in the dissenting opinions analysed. As we observed, language is simply often not as clear, and legal sources not as conclusively determinative of rules and principles so as to avoid good-faith disagreements. For instance, the study examines the case of A, B and C, where the dissent alleges that the ECtHR unduly broadened the margin of appreciation afforded to Ireland on abortion rights by introducing a novel criterion, thereby ostensibly offsetting a strong European consensus that would council a narrow margin of appreciation.115 Is it material to the categorisation that the dissenters themselves acknowledge that a strong European consensus by itself ‘usually’, but not invariably, leads to a narrower margin of appreciation for the state?116 The coding criteria do not provide a definitive answer. They state that references to the margin of appreciation may constitute an overturning allegation only if the dissenting opinion ‘asserts that the doctrine was applied in a different way (eg more broadly or narrowly) in prior case law, whether or not a particular case is cited in the separate opinion’.117

The difficulty is that it is not clear whether the dissenters in A, B and C make such an assertion. On their own terms, they seem to be at most suggesting that the introduction of the novel criterion of ‘moral views’ offset the impact of a strong European consensus. However, they are notably not suggesting that but for the novel criterion, the margin of appreciation would have been narrow (and thus beneficial to the applicants) because even a strong European consensus is acknowledged to only ‘usually’ have this effect. Stone, Sandholtz and Andenas may have a point because that is arguably sufficiently different to the envisioned scenario and hence should not count as a walking back dissent. If the dissent acknowledges that prior case law is inconsistent on the relevance of a strong European consensus for the margin of appreciation, then how can the dissenters plausibly assert a departure from prior case law?

The objective here is not to settle the debate between the authors and their critics, but to illustrate the pitfall: if empirical work is to provide a basis for critical analysis, then it must employ rigorous methods that achieve a high level of coding consistency and reasonably accommodate the complexity of language. This is a challenging and time-consuming task: legal language is open-textured and responsive to subtle shifts that can be easily missed or open to competing interpretations.

Second, while it is certainly possible to count the number of ‘walking back’ allegations following a preliminary doctrinal analysis that reliably identifies them, such data cannot by itself furnish doctrinal conclusions. Helfer and Voeten acknowledge this limitation at various points in their article,118 but perplexingly nonetheless draw a direct doctrinal inference when they conclude that ‘[f]irst hesitantly and then more frequently after 2012, the Grand Chamber started to narrow its interpretation of the Convention, especially in cases against consolidated democracies’.119 It is not clear from the context whether they base this particular claim on their dataset, but it is worth noting that neither their data nor the preliminary doctrinal analysis supports this conclusion. The frequency of walking back dissents can only offer clues as to where compelling doctrinal evidence may be located. Anything more requires a doctrinal evaluation of the veracity of the walking back dissents, including the doctrinal landscape at the time, the ratio and dicta of the majority opinion and the arguments put forward in the dissent. Given the absence of such an analysis, it is unclear how the authors can be sure that ‘tacit overturning’ of rights protections is occurring, let alone becoming more frequent over time against consolidated democracies.120

Third and finally, empirical evidence based on coding decisions does not allow for inferences as to the underlining reasons and motivations. Even if we accept that a robust doctrinal analysis would show a more restrictive interpretation of the ECHR over time, the data cannot establish a link to state pressure. Again, Helfer and Voeten acknowledge this limitation elsewhere but nonetheless slip into drawing unsupported inferences in their conclusion. They offer two explanations for their findings: either the ECtHR is overtly responding to political pressure or it is tacitly restricting its interpretation of rights in light of a more restrictive rights environment in Member States.121 However, there is at least one further possibility: there is no overturning (tacit or otherwise) of prior case law in favour of governments. Simply put, the walking back dissents may be doctrinally mistaken. Neither the dataset nor the preliminary doctrinal analysis establishes that these are plausible explanations, nor can we tell which one is more convincing. A different type of empirical research—namely, interviews with sitting and former judges—could shed light on whether pressure from states was a factor during deliberations, but that was not part of their study.

C. Preparation and the Promise of Legal AI

Finally, there is space for empirical analysis when surveying legal sources before conducting a doctrinal analysis: simply put, when a lawyer decides what material is relevant and should inform their research into a legal question. The key challenge here is the vast number of potentially relevant legal sources. For instance, in 2023–24, His Majesty’s Courts & Tribunals Service (HMCTS) recorded 376,000 receipts and disposed of 345,000 cases, with an outstanding caseload of 653,000.122 The ECtHR issues tens of thousands of decisions annually, and at the end of 2023 had a backlog of approximately 68,450 pending applications.123

In many ways, conventional doctrinal analysis has failed to reckon with this wealth of legal sources and not kept pace with modern developments. In the age of hard copy law reports and journals, it was often simply not feasible to obtain a complete overview of all decisions on a given legal question. Whenever a case was published widely, it almost inevitably gained greater attention and prominence: not necessarily due to a robust analysis of its doctrinal significance, but simply because of the limited sample of available cases.124 In this sense, empirical analysis can play a crucial role as a preparatory tool for better doctrinal work. This exercise may well confirm widely held views on the significance of particular cases, or it may chip away at a dominant view that perhaps crystallised prematurely. In this sense, doctrinal and empirical analyses complement one another, and an approach relying exclusively on either one is necessarily deficient.

Some promising advancements have been made in recent years in better incorporating empirical analysis. Lewis Graham used empirical analysis in his analysis of UK Supreme Court decisions in public law cases.125 He examined whether there is any evidence to suggest that the Supreme Court has adopted a more restrained approach following some notable changes in its composition.126 The cutting edge of this approach combines legal databases with large language model (LLM) analysis to assist with a wide range of core legal tasks, including searching and summarising court decisions and drafting documents.127 The hope of some is that AI will eventually uncover and articulate legal rules and principles contained in legal sources quicker and with a higher degree of accuracy than is currently possible through manual (human) examination. In time, some enthusiastic supporters even claim this will lead to a ‘legal singularity’, profoundly altering the relationship of legal professionals to the law and allowing the broader public to ‘have an enhanced sense of their legal rights and obligations, with minimum uncertainty’.128 That, however, seems unlikely given that even the rather basic usage of LLMs in supporting roles comes with significant caveats.

The first concern relates to a structural problem faced by all current iterations of LLMs. As Varun Magesh and others show in their paper, hallucinations, where models make false statements or falsely attribute a statement to a particular source, as well as failures to address the query or provide proper citations for factual claims remain a concern.129 Accordingly, there is a possibility, even with the best of training data, that an LLM will draw indefensible conclusions and misrepresent their content. Meanwhile, there is some evidence of LLM quality irrevocably degrading when trained on lower-quality material, especially those created through LLMs, suggesting a persistent need for high-quality, human-generated writing.130

Secondly, more advanced use cases for LLMs are premised on the idea that court decisions are ‘essentially deterministic or probabilistic outputs, produced on the basis of static or unambiguous legal rules, in a societal vacuum’.131 As I have shown, this misses the contestability of any account of the rules and principles that legal sources establish as well as the possibility of good-faith disagreements over their implications. Language is simply not as clear, and legal sources not as conclusively determinative of rules and principles, as would be required for meaningful doctrinal statements derived from machine learning and natural language processing alone.

Consider again the example of ouster clauses. Assume for the sake of argument that courts uphold ouster clauses in 90% of cases but the majority of these cases originate in the 20th century and generally pertain to ‘partial’ ouster clauses. Meanwhile, decisions hostile to ouster clauses are grouped around the early 21st century and generally pertain to seemingly ‘complete’ ouster clauses. Simply training an LLM on all court decisions pertaining to ouster clauses might lead it to conclude that there is a high (90%) probability that Parliament can successfully exclude the jurisdiction of the High Court.

From an external point of view, that is a reasonable inference, but this perspective prevents us from accounting for the outsized influence of some cases (eg Privacy International), the overall legal context, and factual nuances. As I have shown, only doctrinal research conducted from an internal point of view can establish the relevant facts of a case, distinguish important from unimportant features of legal sources, derive rules and principles from them accordingly and incorporate insights from the wider the legal system and its underlining values. Only then do we get a sense of the constitutional status of ouster clauses and their permissibility. An LLM cannot autonomously perform this evaluation from an external point of view: indeed, nobody can.

In order to gain competent doctrinal insights, we need a way to give LLMs access to the internal perspective on the law. The problem is that any potential solutions to this issue are likely to undermine the benefits that LLMs were intended to provide in the first place: the promise of greater certainty, owing to a superior ability to digest information and thus ‘uncover’ rules and principles more efficiently from a relatively unbiased perspective.

Suppose that we ‘teach’ an LLM a series of general ‘meta’ rules about the law, placing our thumb on the scale of the external perspective just enough to afford AI a glimpse into the doctrinal box. These rules could account for the outsized influence of some cases by suggesting that Supreme Court decisions from the 21st century are more ‘important’ than High Court decisions from the 19th century. However, we would only succeed in positing rules and principles for the interpretation of legal sources that are themselves in need of interpretation, contextualisation and application. A further set of higher-order rules and principles that elaborate how the lower-order rules and principles should be applied would not fix the problem: we would just replicate our issue at a higher level of abstraction, ending up with an infinite nesting doll of ‘meta’ rules and principles.

We could conceivably avoid the nesting doll problem by instead frontloading the LLM training data with a detailed, bespoke doctrinal analysis on the issue of ouster clauses. This would provide a nuanced account of the rules and principles from within the doctrinal box. However, it would negate any efficiency gains, because every legal question would require its own prior analysis, and in any case would only imprint our own contestable interpretation of legal sources on the LLM. We would find ourselves back at the familiar impasse we encountered when discussing the feasibility of a Bayesian analysis in doctrinal research. The LLM would reflect back to us the conclusions of our extensive, prior doctrinal analysis and be no more efficient than humans, nor any closer to arriving at an authoritative interpretation of the law.

Finally, we could attempt to avoid this efficiency and imprinting problem by inputting as wide a range of legal sources as possible into the training data, along with relevant (human) doctrinal analysis. The hope would be that the LLM ‘learns’ the ‘meta’ rules and principles independently from specific human intervention and arrives at a more balanced account than we could provide, while avoiding the need for a bespoke doctrinal analysis. However, this is unlikely to be sufficient. Greater doctrinal clarity is not necessarily achieved by weighing up all legal sources and doctrinal accounts equally: selection of salient features, interpretation, contextualisation, reconciliation with the wider legal system and its underlining values are crucial to meaningful doctrinal research. To borrow an analogy from colour theory, if we mix all the colours on the spectrum together in equal measure, we end up with a unified, messy blob, one that has averaged out the distinctiveness and useful insights of its original sources. These concerns are only amplified if the training data relies on sampling or a limited selection of material as opposed to a truly comprehensive dataset of legal sources and doctrinal accounts. It entails the risk that assessments are skewed by incomplete data, as was the case with the IRAL, or predetermined by a normative assessment, as is the case with some ‘grand theory’ accounts.

Therefore, it seems that the potential for an empirical analysis is limited and unlikely to go much beyond pointing doctrinal researchers towards general statistical trends and promising decisions for further analysis. Both the nature of law as a social practice and structural problems render the prospect of LLMs autonomously engaging in original doctrinal research, much less competently identifying the ‘correct’ rules and principles applicable to complicated scenarios implausible.

4. Conclusion

The emphasis on the distinctiveness of doctrinal research in the article is not to suggest that other research methods are not valuable. Indeed, pure doctrinal accounts would in many ways be impoverished, unimaginative and needlessly deferential to the status quo. However, without a clear doctrinal account at its base, legal scholarship is at risk of losing both its ability to bridge normative divides and its distinct contribution to human knowledge.

One of the key advantages of doctrinal research is that it provides a shared methodological approach that enables discussions about legal rules and principles in a manner susceptible to falsification on the basis of legal sources. Lawyers that may well be deeply at odds on normative questions can debate doctrine without being at cross purposes or engaging in a discussion that is reducible to differences in outlook. This not only improves the quality of legal debate, but also hints at the wider role that law plays in society. Law is valuable and useful precisely because it allows all kinds of ideological positions to be translated into legal sources that govern society. Law thus furnishes a shared vocabulary and toolset for the implementation of desired policies that ultimately provide a key rationale for the democratic contest of ideas.

The absence of rigorous doctrinal research would also severely limit the viable contributions that lawyers can make to the advancement of human knowledge.132 To be blunt, there are simply better philosophers, historians, anthropologists, sociologists, economists, scientists and politicians in the world than the ones that tend to be educated in law schools. What lawyers can uniquely contribute is a deep familiarity with legal sources and the internal framework of the law which allows them to derive and revise the doctrinal rules and principles of an ever evolving legal system. This doctrinal expertise forms the basis for all manner of legal advice and prevents an innumerable number of disputes from ever seeing the inside of a courtroom. This is a valuable contribution because it sustains the ability of the law to guide individual behaviour, whether in a criminal, private or public law setting.

At the same time, doctrinal research must increasingly reckon with a large volume of legal sources and the opportunities presented by technological advances. As with the introduction of any new technology and ensuing adjustments of long-practiced methods, this inevitably leads to teething problems—in part, because new methods lead to critical reflection on old practices and because the technology is not yet fully understood and appreciated, in terms of both its potential and its limitations. The most promising applications are currently the use of LLMs in a supportive empirical role, a preparatory tool for the doctrinal researcher. Attempts to draw direct doctrinal conclusions from external empirical evidence or, indeed, engage in empirical research of doctrinal concepts seem significantly less promising. Legal sources do not present us with equations for rules and principles of law, and doctrinal research is not an attempt to definitively solve them. Perhaps unsurprisingly, lawyers deal far more commonly in degrees of arguable interpretations of legal sources than in unequivocally correct answers.

In that spirit, I have sought to contribute to our collective understanding of doctrinal methods and the potential for empirical contributions. Much like an initial attempt at a jigsaw puzzle, the article hopefully provides a robust foundation that allows future puzzlers to expand on its omissions and improve on its shortcomings.
