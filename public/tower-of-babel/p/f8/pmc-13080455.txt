Time-Driven Activity-Based Costing Methodology for Cost Savings in the EMOTE-TNK Study
Authors: Saptarshi Ghosh, Isabelle Delos Reyes, Carlos Perez Vega, C Joseph Yelvington, Greg M Worsowicz, Olivia Boykin, Josephine F Huang, Lynda Christel, Tiffany M Halstead, Lesia H Mooney, Robert S Kaplan, Pablo Moreno Franco, William D Freeman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo calculate financial savings resulting from early mobilization of patients with acute ischemic stroke receiving tenecteplase (TNK) in the Early Mobilization Outcomes with Tenecteplase Treatment in Acute Ischemic Stroke (EMOTE-TNK) safety and tolerability study.Patients and MethodsWe used the Time-Driven Activity-Based Costing methodology to compare costs of 180 patients who had early mobilization, defined as occurring between 13 and 24 hours after thrombolysis, with those of patients who had conventional mobilization, defined as waiting 24 hours after thrombolysis. Patients were seen from February 13, 2021, through March 18, 2024.ResultsWe estimated that, by starting rehabilitation service evaluations earlier, the average duration of hospitalization was reduced by 0.5 days. Costs per patient in the conventional model were estimated to be $5000, whereas in the new model, it was $3750 (savings per patient, $1250). For our cohort of 180 patients, the total amount saved was $225,000 and 90 hospital days.ConclusionThe current study showed that, compared with conventional mobilization, early mobilization was associated with up to a 25% reduction in costs for patients with acute ischemic stroke receiving TNK. Future work should explore the downstream effects of early mobilization on discharge planning and other aspects of quality care.

Strokes, previously the second-leading cause of deaths in the United States, are now the fifth leading cause because outcomes have been improved by thrombolysis with tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) or tenecteplase (TNK), a genetically engineered tPA. The half-life of tPA is short, and if bleeding or other complications occur, then they typically are clinically evident within the first 6 hours after initiating thrombolysis. Thus, rehabilitation after stroke has conventionally started 24 hours1,2 after administering intravenous (IV) tPA.

Earlier mobilization (<24 hours after thrombolysis) has been reported to be relatively safe and feasible for patients with neurologic disorders when the patient’s vital signs and neurologic symptoms are used to gauge tolerance of treatment.3,4 Further, early mobilization is important for patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) because it can prevent debility and immobility complications.1 Research is essential for early mobilization after thrombolysis for patients with ischemic stroke, and in present times, conclusive data are required.5 As part of a Mayo Clinic Comprehensive Stroke Center administrative review, we adapted our existing early mobilization protocol for patients receiving tPA to include those receiving IV TNK.6 Our group assessed the safety of mobilization between 13 and 24 hours after thrombolysis for 180 patients receiving TNK in the Early Mobilization Outcomes with Tenecteplase Treatment in Acute Ischemic Stroke (EMOTE-TNK) study, and our findings confirmed its safety and feasibility (I. Delos Reyes, unpublished data, 2025). Of the 180 patients in the cohort, 159 (88.3%) were able to engage in out-of-bed mobilization without any complications. The remaining 21 patients faced minor adverse events, most commonly orthostasis (affecting 7 patients [3.9%]). Among the patients with adverse events, the early mobilization protocol could still be continued in about half of them (10 of 21 patients).

The purpose of this study was to analyze data from the completed EMOTE-TNK safety and tolerability study and determine the cost savings associated with early mobilization using the Time-Driven Activity-Based Costing (TDABC) methodology.

Patients and Methods

The EMOTE-TNK study was a retrospective, single-center study conducted at the Mayo Clinic. This study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board. The need for informed consent was waived by the institutional review board. Eligible patients were 18 years or older, received treatment with TNK after AIS, and were able to actively engage in out-of-bed mobilization within 24 hours after TNK administration. Patients were seen from February 13, 2021, through March 18, 2024.

Overview of the Early Mobilization Protocol

Early mobilization services by a physical therapist (PT) or occupational therapist (OT) could be safely initiated if patients were hemodynamically stable (not receiving vasopressors or IV antihypertensive medication, not requiring multiple IV boluses of either medication). Contraindications to initiating services included an existing or recently removed femoral sheath, hemodynamic instability, angioedema, heart rate exceeding 120/min, systolic blood pressure greater than 180 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure greater than 110 mm Hg, marked diaphoresis, facial pallor, intensely anxious or painful facial expression (especially in aphasic patients), and active bleeding from lines, catheters, intervention sites, or wounds.

We took precautions to safely initiate PT and OT services for patients with femoral arterial lines. We could proceed with services for patients with radial arterial lines and upper extremity peripherally inserted central catheters if they had been flushed with normal saline (not heparin) by nursing staff. Some patients with other types of lines or orders for bed rest also could start services. When appropriate, we sought approval to begin services from the on-call physician in the neurology intensive care unit. The plan of care was discussed with a registered nurse.

The PT or OT reviewed each patient’s record and verified the time elapsed since thrombolytic administration (≥13 hours since initiation of thrombolysis). Therapists were directed to monitor for worsening signs or symptoms during screening (eg, marked diaphoresis, facial pallor, intensely anxious, or painful facial expression), a decrease in systolic blood pressure or mean arterial pressure of 30 mm Hg or more while standing, increased light-headedness (or faint feeling), and worsening numbness or weakness. If any of these signs or symptoms were present, then patients were returned to a supine position and nursing staff were notified. The patient’s ability to independently complete activities of daily living (Barthel index) was also recorded. If no abnormal signs or symptoms were present and blood pressure and mean arterial pressure remained stable, then the therapist progressed with the evaluation and mobilization of the patient. Details of the protocol followed for early mobilization are shown in the Supplemental Appendix 1, available online at http://www.mcpiqojournal.org.

TDABC Methodology

We followed the step-by-step approach proposed by Kaplan and Porter7 to estimate health care costs.

Step 1: Define the Process Map

We outlined the activities and resources involved in the conventional and new models of stroke care. In the conventional model, the steps were (1) stroke onset, (2) hospital admission, (3) initial evaluation and treatment (0-12 hours after thrombolysis), (4) regular mobility evaluation (24-36 hours after thrombolysis), and (5) hospital discharge (after regular evaluation). In the new (EMOTE-TNK) model, the steps were (1) stroke onset, (2) hospital admission, (3) initial evaluation and treatment (0-12 hours after thrombolysis), (4) early mobility evaluation (13-24 hours after thrombolysis), and (5) hospital discharge (after early evaluation).

Step 2: Identify Resource Costs

We assumed that the primary cost driver was the hospital length of stay (LOS), which costs an estimated average of $2500 per day.8,9 The LOS was used as the cost driver because the medical resources (including human personnel) and equipment required by both protocols were similar and were consumed at similar rates over time; hence, the capacity cost rate7 of the resources were approximately similar.

Step 3: Calculate Time Estimates

In the conventional model, patients were hospitalized for 2 days (48 hours). In the EMOTE-TNK model, patients were hospitalized for 1.5 days (36 hours).

Step 4: Determine Cost Savings

TDABC modeling was applied using the Harvard Business School methodology7 to understand the downstream economic benefits from earlier discharge. Economic modeling was based on multiplying the average cost per day (CPD) of hospitalization ($2500) by the number of days saved with the early mobilization protocol.

Discussion

Medical errors can occur during the administration of conventional tPA, which requires a bolus dose followed by an infusion. Specifically, the nurse must use a dedicated IV line, calculate dosage, and follow a 2-step process in which 10% of the 0.9 mg/kg dose is given as an IV bolus over 1 minute, followed by a 1-hour infusion (maximum dose, 90 mg).10, 11, 12 Thus, TNK is increasingly favored for its simplicity and ease of administration as a single IV bolus injection (0.25 mg/kg; maximum dose, 25 mg).10, 11, 12 This single injection also takes less time than a tPA infusion.

Although many comprehensive stroke centers are switching to TNK for thrombolysis, little data have been published about the safety and tolerability of early mobilization after TNK treatment or about the downstream financial and outcome benefits to patients. Some studies have shown the safety and importance of early mobilization for patients with neurologic disorders. A prospective observational study of 18 patients who received IV recombinant tPA showed that early mobilization (within 24 hours of AIS) appeared to be relatively safe and feasible for most patients.3 Similarly, a retrospective review of 153 patients showed that early progressive mobilization appeared to be safe and feasible for patients with external ventricular drains in the neurosurgical intensive care unit.4 A systematic review suggested that the optimal time for starting mobilization was more than 24 hours after stroke, while considering hemodynamic stability and safety criteria.1 Our study goes a step further by highlighting the downstream benefits (eg, reduced LOS and fewer costs) of early mobilization for patients receiving TNK. The TDABC approach applied here has been used in a similar manner in previous studies,8,9 and it was well-suited for comparing costs between the protocols.

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design and a limited sample size of 180 patients from a single institution, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Notably, we used the hospital LOS as the primary cost driver in our model, with the total cost of the hospital stay acting as a surrogate for the expenses incurred by hospital treatment, because the only difference between conventional treatment and the EMOTE-TNK protocol was the early mobility evaluation that led to early discharge. Thus, in our case, the reduction in costs, calculated from the LOS, was a reasonable estimate of savings. We assumed that the entire cost of the hospital stay was used to procure resources for the patient, but this approach also may limit the generalizability of how our TDABC model can be applied to other medical scenarios. However, future studies may include a detailed financial analysis of the various components of care, including human personnel, medications administered, and medical equipment used, to further elucidate the effectiveness of early mobilization. We note that generalizability to other settings also may be affected by variation in availability of resources, such as availability of TNK or adequate PT staff to assess patients per the protocol.

Although this study has numerous limitations, it also has considerable strengths. Even though the data were retrospectively obtained, the EMOTE-TNK study remains one of the largest TNK mobilization studies to date for stroke. In addition, to our knowledge, this study is the first to use the TDABC model to explore downstream aspects of care, such as LOS and cost savings, for an early rehabilitation model.

Again, it is to be noted that the aggregate savings of 0.5 days is represented for the sake of mathematical modeling, and such a decrease would definitely not apply to every individual patient. Our study findings suggest that if PT is actively implemented to mobilize a patient earlier, a potential 2-day stay could be converted to a 1.5-day stay, or a potential 5-day stay could be converted to a 3.75-day stay. The 0.5 day of savings is basically a representation of “mean proportion of days saved” per patient and practically, some patients might have more days saved and some fewer. However, we believe that considerable net savings will be seen overall.

Furthermore, an overall decrease in the LOS means that more hospital beds would be available for new stroke patients. Because even a few moments saved is invaluable in stroke treatment, fewer treatment delays would have great downstream cost benefits in maintaining a patient’s quality of life and functional status. We acknowledge that a majority of patients in the study cohort (>80%) had a low to moderate National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score. The results of this study thus might be more applicable to patients with AIS and low to moderate stroke severity.

Conclusion

The EMOTE-TNK protocol considerably reduced the hospital LOS, resulting in substantial cost savings (approximately $225,000 for a cohort of 180 patients) without compromising patient care. The EMOTE-TNK study validated the safety and feasibility of early mobilization after thrombolysis with TNK, and the current findings also demonstrate the economic benefits of this approach. The implications of this research extend beyond immediate clinical practice. It paves the way for future studies in optimizing stroke care and rehabilitation, which may offer valuable insights for policymaking.
