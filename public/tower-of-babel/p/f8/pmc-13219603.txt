REPLY TO: “Daylight Saving Time and Mortality—Proceed with Caution “in response to “Daylight saving time affects European mortality patterns” by Levy et al
Authors: François R Herrmann, Laurent Lévy, Jean-Marie Robine, Grégoire Rey, Raúl Fernando Méndez Turrubiates, Marcos Quijal-Zamorano, Hicham Achebak, Joan Ballester, Xavier Rodó
Published: 2026

replying to E. B. Klerman et al. Nature Communications 10.1038/s41467-024-45837-4 (2024)

In their comments, Klerman et al. cast a doubt on the quality of the regression model we used and its interpretation. Nevertheless, we are thankful for their comments, as they help us provide more detailed explanations. Here, we discuss the reasons they put forward to explain the apparent discrepancy between Fig. 1 and the model’s results.

Unadjusted average daily death rate/100,000 observed in 16 European countries (1998–2012) around spring DST change, by years.

We acknowledge an apparent difference between the results presented in the original Fig. 1b, c—described in the first paragraph under “DST and mortality” in the original paper—and those derived from the regression model. This difference can be attributed to several factors: the figures illustrate the total number of deaths per day (adjusted for day length) based on the average daily population over a limited time window centered around the DST transitions. As such, they offer a simplified snapshot of a much more complex reality.

In contrast, the regression model encompasses the full 15-year observation period, thereby accounting for year-to-year variability, as illustrated in Fig. 1 below. Moreover, the model integrates a broader range of factors influencing mortality rates, including sex and age group (as shown in the original Figures 2a to 2r), country-specific effects (original Supplementary Fig. S2), and other covariates adjusted for in the analysis.

“Severe collinearity/concurvity in the statistical model?”

The concept of concurvity was not addressed in previous studies on DST transition. It was first introduced by Buja et al (1989) in the context of generalized additive models, where it describes the nonlinear analogue of multicollinearity, when one smooth term can be approximated by a combination of others, potentially destabilizing model estimates1. While concurvity may not necessarily bias estimates, it can inflate their variance; however, this is less concerning in our case, as the dataset represents the entire population.

We computed three concurvity indices using the concurvity function in R, which quantify the extent to which a variable can be explained by others, with values ranging from 0 (no issue) to 1 (complete identifiability failure). As shown in Table 1, most indices are near zero, except for moderate to high values between “Month” and “Mean Temperature” (0.08–0.64) and between “Month” and “Season” (0.55–0.81). To address this, we re-estimated the model three times, each time retaining only one of the correlated variables (“Month,” “Season,” or “Daily Mean Temperature”) and a fourth time excluding the “sincos” (a sine-cosine function that considers daylight seasonality) term due to potential collinearity with geographic coordinates. Across all specifications, the results remained consistent, particularly regarding the weekly effect of DST transitions (Supplementary Data 1).

In many papers, the DST transition effect is assessed simply by comparing mortality before and after the DST transition. In our model, we considered the yearly, seasonal, and monthly trends, which are known to influence mortality, and assessed whether the week effect still occurs after the DST transitions while adjusting for these trends and other confounders. When adding the following interaction terms (age* “Week number Spring DST » or sex*“Week number Spring DST”, which correspond to the variable name used in the original Table 1) in the models (see point 5. below) none are significant.

“…the dataset should only include weeks of data before and after those transitions,…”

We deliberately choose to use the entire time series (all days of the 15 years of observation) to consider all the information at our disposal, and thus to evaluate both DST transitions with the same model, while adjusting simultaneously for the seasonal and monthly trends along with all the other adjusting variables.

As proposed, we restricted the analysis to three time-windows: 8 weeks, 2 weeks, and 1 week before and after each DST transition—conducted separately by sex. The models included 10-year age groups and interaction terms between each age group and a binary variable coded as 0 before and 1 after the DST transition.

Following the spring transition, we observed a significant increase in mortality among women when the analysis was limited to the 2-week and 8-week windows. This effect was particularly evident in the 20.0–29.9, 40.0–49.9, and 60+ age groups, using the 0.0–9.9 age group as the reference. The incidence rate ratios (IRRs) for these groups ranged from 1.113 to 1.414.

In contrast, after the fall transition, we found no significant changes in most analyses. The only exception was among men in the 1-week window, where a decrease in mortality was observed for the 20.0–29.9 and 50.0–79.9 age groups, with IRRs ranging from 0.748 to 0.84.

These results are detailed in the supplemental materials (see Supplementary Data 2, 3).

“Although the paper’s Fig. 1shows data from 2 months before and after the DST changes, the statistical model’s comparison for DST results is based on a single week before the changes …….”

All the weeks from the entire year are included in the model; the week before the changes (-1) is just the reference week of a given DST transition, as done by many authors. For instance, in a systematic review of the impact of DST transition on road traffic collision risk, 6 out of 24 papers used the week before as reference, three papers used the two weeks before, three used the Monday before, and the remaining used various numbers of days, weeks or months2. As there is no consensus on a unique reference period, we used the most frequent one: the week before. The longer-term seasonal trends in the data are dealt with by the corresponding adjustment over the full year of both the seasonal and the monthly effects.

“These seasonal trends alone cause the mortality to be higher a few weeks in the fall and lower in the spring after the comparison week– as is found using this statistical model. This method does not unveil the independent effect of DST transitions. When a comparison is made vs. this trend (as in Fig. 1b/c), the opposite change in mortality rates is seen.

Therefore, incorporating seasonal trends explicitly is critical – e.g., through an Interrupted Time Series design using a carefully defined pre-post interval that does not include the entire year to test a change in slope.”

We did not use an Interrupted Time Series (ITS) design due to several methodological limitations. ITS relies on assumptions of linearity through segmented regression, which may not accurately capture non-linear or complex trends. The results are sensitive to the chosen functional form (e.g., linear vs. polynomial), and issues such as autocorrelation, recurring patterns, and time-varying confounding can further distort estimates.

Additionally, the reliability of ITS results is highly sensitive to the number of data points available before and after the intervention, which can significantly influence the findings. Furthermore, satisfying stationarity assumptions remains challenging unless a segmented regression approach is applied. The absence of a control group further complicates the analysis, making it considerably more difficult to isolate the specific effects of the intervention.

“The authors have a mixed design with 16 countries observed from 1998 to 2012. Whether or not this is adequately accounted for in the pooled regression model is uncertain. The authors do not test interactions between countries and the variables of interest. Ignoring the existence of more than one dimension, as in this case, leads to biased estimators and wrong variances of the estimators.”

We extended the binomial negative regression model by including several interaction terms, such as sex × age group (10-year bands), sex × month, age × month, sex × country code, age × country code, age × day of the week, sex × day of the week, age × week number (Spring DST), age × week number (Fall DST), sex × week number (Spring DST), and sex × week number (Fall DST). However, caution is warranted when incorporating numerous interactions, as they may complicate interpretation and obscure main effects. In the original manuscript we already mentioned and added interactions between sex and age and the following variables: month, day of the week, Spring DST, Fall DST, and country.

Other considerations C1.1 “The incidence rate ratio (IRR) reported as statistically significant for the spring is 0.97 (weeks 1 and 2) and for the fall is 1.02 (weeks 1, 2) - and, contrary to their conclusions, 0.99 in week 0) and 0.97 in week 4.”

Table 2 shows the incidence rate ratio (IRR) from the original Table 1 presented along with the expected IRR obtained from the original Fig. 1b, c envelopes. The IRR associated with “Week number Spring DST” provided in the original Table 1 is a direct measure of the slope between the current week versus the week before DST (week “-1”). Weeks are centered on Wednesday. For instance, the IRR of the first week (0.965) is the ratio of mortality between week “+1” and week “-1” (the week before DST) which shows a significant decrease as observed in the original Fig. 1b, lower than the expected IRR (the upper 95% confidence interval boundary is below). The IRR of the second week (0.972) is the ratio of mortality between week “+2” and week “-1”, its decline remains significant. The IRR of the third week (0.988) is the ratio of mortality between week “+3” and week “-1”, this decline is no longer significant. Three weeks after the Spring DST transition, the adjusted model shows no significant impact of the transition on mortality. Thus, our original model does not contradict the figure.

The IRR significantly increases during the first two weeks after the Fall transition, but in the same magnitude as expected and observed. The IRR values on weeks 4, 6, 7, and 8 show significant decreases larger than both the expected and observed values. These weeks were not discussed in the original paper. OtherC1.2 “The IRR for other metrics are similar or much higher. These other statistically significant factors do not receive the same emphasis by authors….”

The effects of these other adjusting variables on mortality are well known and were already discussed in previous papers using the same data set: age and sex3, temperature and humidity4,5, countries and GDP6. Due to space limitations, we did not focus on them again.

OtherC1.3 “In addition, more commonly used adjustments for multiple comparisons would render the primary finding as mixed or non-significant (e.g., Bonferroni adjustment would have p-value = 0.0006), despite the robust sample size.”

All 40 parameters were analyzed at once in the same regression model, so adjustments are usually not required. A classical Bonferroni threshold is equal to a p-value of 0.00125 ( = 0.05/40, cf. Table 1 of the original publication), not the p-value = 0.0006 proposed by Klerman et al., and thus most of our results hold. The Benjamini–Hochberg method we used controls the False Discovery Rate in a more efficient way than Bonferoni does7. Some authors think like Perneger that “Bonferroni adjustments are, at best, unnecessary and, at worst, deleterious to sound statistical inference”8. The dataset is not a large sample, but the whole population of the studied areas.

OtherC2. “Since the reason for deaths is not reported, no distinction can be made …”

We agree and had already identified this as a limitation in our original discussion. We tried to access this data, but many countries are reluctant to share it because of the potential risk of identifying the cause of death of a given individual on a given day. In any case, even without this information, the structure of the results is still robust and the conclusions hold.

OtherC3. “Even without the potential statistical issues mentioned above, the over-extension of these results … to claim that DST … affects mortality is not supported and should not be included in the title or in the discussion.”

We do not agree with this comment. We stated in our abstract that “mortality decreases in spring and increases in fall during the first two weeks following each DST transition” and we detect a significant association, even if its effect size is moderate. This observed association does not mean causation, and as shown in Table 3, the results obtained from the original model are very similar to those expected from the unadjusted graph values. Additionally, all IRR are below 1 after the Spring DST transition and above 1 after the Fall DST transition. Thus, the original model does not diverge from the observed graph values. As explained above effects are present. Moreover, many papers with “DST” in their title, present and discuss, like ours, the effect of DST transitions, not DST per se. This shift in language did not strike the first three reviewers of our paper2,9–12.

Having all these considerations in mind, we must acknowledge that the discussion section on DST by Klerman et al. is sound and constructive. We understand and indeed share most of the points in their discussion section. We also agree that chronic effects of living in DST are not addressed by either the statistical model or the paper.
