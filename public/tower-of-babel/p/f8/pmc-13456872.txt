Wunderlich syndrome in a hypertensive male with multiple renal arteries: a rare case report

Introduction

Wunderlich syndrome (WS) is a rare clinical entity characterized by the acute onset of spontaneous renal hemorrhage into the subcapsular, perirenal, and/or pararenal spaces without a history of antecedent trauma. Carl Wunderlich defined it as “spontaneous renal capsule apoplexy” in 1856, from which it derives its name[1].

Neoplasms are the most common underlying pathology in up to 60% of cases and include renal angiomyolipoma (AML) and renal cell carcinoma (RCC). Vascular disorders, ruptured kidney cysts, infections, kidney stones, and blood clotting disorders contribute to other causes. Multiple renal arteries (MRA), though a common variant, underlying hypertensive arteriosclerosis or micro-anomalies can lead to spontaneous bleeding. Five percent of WS cases are referred to as idiopathic. Flank or abdominal pain is the most common manifestation of WS and is seen in 67% of patients, but hematuria, a flank mass, and hypovolemic shock may occur in isolation or in concert, i.e., Lenk's triad[2,3].

Cross-sectional imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging, and ultrasonography (US; including contrast-enhanced US) and angiography have been used to assess patients who present with symptoms of WS; they are pivotal in detecting perirenal hemorrhage and identifying the underlying cause[4].

Management options range from conservative management, embolization, and surgical intervention. In cases of active bleeding, embolization of the renal or segmental artery is the preferred course of action for the majority of patients, followed by surgical intervention[3,5].

Here, we present a case of WS in a middle-aged hypertensive male with MRA. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria[6].

Case presentation

A 51-year-old male, a known case of hypertension under regular medication, presented to the Emergency with left flank pain and fullness for 4 days. He also complained of bluish discoloration over the flank for 1 day. He denied fever, hematuria, burning micturition, constipation, or trauma. On examination, vital signs were stable with a heart rate of 86/min, blood pressure of 132/74 mmHg, and pulse oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. No pallor was noted. Per abdominal examination revealed diffuse ecchymosis over the left lumbar region with flank fullness and mild tenderness. Laboratory investigations showed a total leukocyte count of 11 600/mm³, hemoglobin of 9.3 g/dL, and platelets of 156 000/mm³. Renal function tests, coagulation profile, and urine routine microscopic examination were within normal limits.

Ultrasonography revealed a lobulated, septate collection of approximately 690 ml in the left intraparietal region, compressing the left kidney. Contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) of the abdomen and pelvis was performed before and after intravenous contrast and oral neutral contrast. It revealed a large left retroperitoneal hematoma of approximately 790 ml in size, along with a wedge-shaped hypodensity 13 mm deep in the interpolar cortex of the left kidney, all suggestive of AAST Grade III renal injury (Figs 1, 2, and 3). Retrospectively, there was no history of any known trivial trauma, persistent cough, constipation, or known coagulopathy. He was managed conservatively in the high care unit under regular monitoring of vitals, urine output, abdominal girth, and hemoglobin, with absolute bed rest for 1 week. Intravenous fluid, two pints over 24 hours, along with a normal diet and Injection Tranexamic 500 mg TDS, was continued for 3 days and then stopped. Vitals and hemoglobin remained stable, not warranting any transfusion. A repeat CECT with an abdominal aortic angiogram showed findings consistent with the previous scan (AAST Grade III renal injury) but additionally revealed three left renal arteries with no aneurysm or contrast extravasation. The abdominal aorta and its branches were of normal caliber.

The patient continued to improve with conservative management and was discharged on day 8 with advice for regular follow-up. He was instructed to avoid strenuous activity for 3 weeks and contact sports for 2 months. On follow-up, there was a gradual resolution of the swelling and hematoma on imaging, accompanied by symptomatic improvement.

Discussion

Spontaneous renal hematoma was first documented by Théophile Bonet in “Sepulchretum, sive anatomia practica ex cadaveribus morbo denatis” (1679) and later recognized as a distinct clinical syndrome by Carl Reinhold August Wunderlich[7]. The classical triad, known as Lenk’s triad, consists of a gradual onset of flank pain, a palpable tender mass, and hypovolemic shock. Additional manifestations may include nausea, vomiting, hematuria, and hemodynamic instability, making WS a potentially life-threatening condition. The non-specific presentation of WS often poses a diagnostic challenge for emergency physicians. Hence, prompt recognition and diagnosis are essential to prevent severe complications[3,7].

Renal neoplasms represent the predominant cause of WS, accounting for nearly 60% of cases – most commonly renal AML and RCC. Vascular pathologies, such as polyarteritis nodosa, renal artery aneurysms, and pseudoaneurysms, contribute to approximately 20–30% of cases. Less frequent causes include hydronephrosis, cyst rupture (5–10%), and infections. Pre-existing risk factors predisposing to WS include hemodialysis, hypertension, end-stage renal disease, recurrent urinary tract infections (particularly pyelonephritis), and polycystic renal disease[8,9].

MRA are a common anatomical variant seen in 20–30% of individuals (two in 22%, three in 1–2%, four in 0.1%). The presence of an accessory or MRA may predispose individuals to focal vascular instability – particularly via branch aneurysm or pseudoaneurysm formation – that can rupture and give rise to spontaneous perirenal hemorrhage[10,11]. Case reports in the literature document instances of accessory renal artery aneurysm rupture causing retroperitoneal hemorrhage. However, there is no robust epidemiologic evidence that MRA alone is an independent risk factor for WS. In our patient, the finding of three renal arteries on the left side, in the absence of trauma or tumor, raises the possibility that variant vascular supply may have contributed to the hemorrhage event. It remains speculative to attribute causality to the variant anatomy alone; other unrecognized micro-aneurysms or vessel wall pathology (hypertension and arteriosclerosis) may have contributed[10,12].

Point-of-care US (POCUS) serves as a valuable first-line imaging modality, especially in the emergency setting; however, confirmation with contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) is essential, as it remains the gold standard in suspected retroperitoneal hemorrhage. In hemodynamically unstable patients, an urgent abdominal CT along with an angiography scan is mandatory to localize the bleeding source and assess the extent of hemorrhage, with reported sensitivity ranging between 92% and 100% for detecting perirenal hematoma[4,13].

The management of WS depends primarily on the presence of active bleeding and hemodynamic stability. In stable cases, conservative management is appropriate, consisting of fluid resuscitation, blood transfusion when indicated, prompt reversal of anticoagulation, analgesia, and antibiotic therapy, which along with serial imaging can be reasonable as reported in our case[5,14,15]. When active extravasation is evident, angiography with selective embolization is preferred. Partial or radical nephrectomy is reserved for neoplastic or refractory bleeding cases, or for patients with persistent instability[3,16].

Conclusion

WS remains a rare but potentially life-threatening emergency. While typically associated with neoplasms or vascular pathologies, this case highlights that anatomical variations, such as MRA, may serve as a predisposing factor for vascular instability in hypertensive patients.
