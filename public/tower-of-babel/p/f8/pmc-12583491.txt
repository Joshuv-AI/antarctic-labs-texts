Assessing electronic medical record usage in private healthcare facilities of Amhara region, Ethiopia
Authors: Sossina Wondwossen, Mulusew Andualem Asemahagn, Habtamu Alganeh Guadie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite advances in information technology, the adoption of electronic medical records (EMRs) in healthcare remains limited, and usage rates are still low. Understanding the current utilization of EMR systems in private health facilities is crucial for guiding future digital health integration, enhancing interoperability, and building the capacity of the health workforce. However, the use of EMR systems in private hospitals in the Amhara region has not been well documented. This study therefore aimed to assess the utilization of EMR systems and identify associated factors among health professionals working in private hospitals in Bahir Dar city, Ethiopia. An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted from February 13 to March 20, 2023, among 367 health professionals. Participants were selected through simple random sampling using computer-generated numbers from hospital-specific sampling frames via Open Epi software. Bivariate logistic regression was first performed, and variables with p < 0.25 were included in the multivariable analysis. Associations were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. A total of 351 respondents participated, yielding a response rate of 95.6%. Overall, 45.3% (95% CI: 41–50%) of health professionals reported utilizing the EMR system. Factors significantly associated with EMR utilization included access to basic EMR training (AOR = 2.22, 95% CI: 1.04–4.72), overall satisfaction of health care providers (AOR = 2.64, 95% CI: 1.39–6.09), and a user-friendly EMR interface (AOR = 2.14, 95% CI: 1.12–4.13). The utilization of EMR systems by healthcare professionals in private health facilities was found to be suboptimal. Key factors influencing EMR use included lack of access to training, user satisfaction, and the system’s interface usability. EMRs have the potential to improve health service performance if supported by continuous technical assistance and commitment to optimize their use. Developing EMR software tailored to local needs and conditions, along with sustained efforts to address barriers, is essential to ensure widespread and effective adoption and to fully realize the benefits of digital health records. Introduction

Health is universally recognized as a fundamental determinant of human well-being. In recent decades, the healthcare sector has experienced profound transformations, largely driven by rapid technological advancements. Within this evolving landscape, technology has become an indispensable component of healthcare delivery. Among the numerous innovations designed to enhance clinical practice, the electronic medical record (EMR) is particularly noteworthy1. EMRs, defined as computerized medical information systems, are progressively replacing traditional paper-based records and reshaping medical practice. They allow the creation of structured, legible, and standardized patient records, thereby facilitating efficient data collection, interoperability, and timely access to health information2.

In Ethiopia, the Health Information Systems Strategic Plan (2020/21–2024/25) was developed as a sub-strategy aligned with the second Health Sector Transformation Plan (HSTP II) to guide digital health initiatives3. Despite the growing importance of EMRs, research on their implementation and associated factors in private hospitals remains limited, even though these facilities contribute significantly to healthcare delivery. Private hospitals in Ethiopia play a vital role in providing timely access to essential services for both urban and rural populations. Moreover, the expansion of the private health sector reduces the burden on government facilities while enhancing consumer choice and fostering competition4.

Since 2007/2008, Ethiopia, with support from Tulane University, has pursued EMR development. However, acceptance and utilization have declined over time. Many healthcare institutions continue to struggle with sustaining the system, focusing predominantly on material and technical challenges while underestimating the importance of professional engagement and attitudes. A study at Ayder Referral Hospital reported considerable negative perceptions toward EMR utilization5. Implementing EMR systems is complex even in resource-rich settings, as it requires aligning local needs with available technology and resources6. These challenges are magnified in low- and middle-income countries, where goals and constraints are less clearly defined, more heterogeneous, and compounded by limited experience in sustaining EMR systems7,8.

Despite these barriers, EMRs hold the potential to enhance workflow efficiency, generate reliable data, improve patient satisfaction, and contribute to better clinical outcomes. However, physician distrust, limited communication, and systemic challenges hinder their successful integration into healthcare systems across Africa9–12.

In Ethiopia, underutilization of EMRs continues to limit data management and evidence-based decision-making. Heavy reliance on paper-based records reduces accessibility and reusability of information, negatively impacting patient care, service quality, and overall system performance. Incomplete medical records may further compromise patient management while creating administrative, financial, and legal complications13–15. A study in southern Ethiopia emphasized that the absence of EMRs undermines medical documentation quality, often leading to incomplete and illegible records that adversely affect patient monitoring and care16,17.

Medication errors ranked as the 14th leading cause of death in healthcare—are another critical concern. EMRs have been shown to significantly reduce such errors, particularly in critical care settings, by targeting specific error categories and improving documentation practices18–20. Experiences from Kenya, where EMR systems have been in use for nearly two decades, suggest potential improvements in care quality, though outside trial settings it is estimated that 50%–80% of EMR initiatives fail to achieve intended outcomes21. To date, only a limited number of studies have examined electronic medical record (EMR) utilization within private healthcare facilities, particularly in the selected study area. Therefore, the present study aims to assess the level of EMR utilization and identify associated factors among healthcare professionals working in private healthcare facilities in Bahir Dar City, Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted from February 13 to March 20, 2023, in Bahir Dar City, the capital of the Amhara National Regional State, located approximately 520 km from Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia. The city has 10 public health centers, 3 governmental hospitals, and 4 private hospitals. For this study, private hospitals that had initiated the use of electronic medical record (EMR) systems were included. These institutions and the number of healthcare professionals employed in each are as follows: Adinas Hospital (145), Afilas Hospital (127), Dream Care Hospital (116), and Gamby Hospital (159). In total, 547 healthcare professionals were working in the selected institutions.

Sample size determination, study participants, and sampling procedures

The sample size for this study was calculated using a single population proportion formula, assuming a population proportion of 31.7%, a 95% confidence interval, and a 5% margin of error. After accounting for a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was determined to be 367 healthcare professionals. The sample was drawn from a total of 547 professionals working at GAMBY Teaching Hospital, Dream Care Hospital, Adinas Hospital, and Afilas Hospital using a simple random sampling technique. A complete list of professionals served as the sampling frame, with serial numbers assigned from 1 to N. Random selection was then performed using computer-generated numbers in OpenEpi version 3 (Fig. 1).

Schematic presentation of stratified sampling techniques and proportional allocation of participants in Bahir Dar private health facilities, North West, Ethiopia, 2023.

Operational definition

Electronic medical record utilization: Healthcare professionals were classified as EMR users if they utilized at least 6 out of the 12 core functionalities of the system. These functionalities included: documenting patient clinical profiles, ordering and displaying laboratory and radiologic investigations, e-prescription, clinical decision support, patient scheduling, interdepartmental referrals, financial management, reporting, and communication of feedback between staff and administrators. Utilization was assessed using 12 yes/no questions. A score of fewer than 6 indicated poor EMR utilization, whereas a score of 6 or more was categorized as good utilization22.

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected from four health institutions in Bahir Dar City using a semi-structured, pre-tested, self-administered questionnaire translated into Amharic. Two health informatics graduates were recruited for data collection and supervision, respectively. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all study participants prior to administering the questionnaire. The questionnaire was numerically coded and organized into the following sections: socio-demographic characteristics, EMR system functionalities, EMR utilization, user attitudes toward the EMR system, perceived ease of use, and EMR-related training.

Data management, analysis and quality assurance

Data were entered into EpiData version 4.6 and subsequently exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including mean and standard deviation, were used to summarize the data in terms of frequencies and proportions. Bi-variable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine associations between the dependent and independent variables. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bi-variable analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression. Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05, and associations were reported using adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Quality assurance was maintained through rigorous implementation of the data collection procedures. All data collection instruments were reviewed and corrected for completeness and printing errors prior to the main study. A pretest was conducted at Gondar in one of the private hospital using 5% of the total sample size to validate the research instruments. Ambiguous questions were revised, and translation issues from English to Amharic were corrected. The reliability of the instrument was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding a coefficient of 0.80.

Data collectors received training on the importance of data quality, proper administration of the questionnaire, personal conduct, checking for completeness, and their rights and responsibilities during the process. Continuous supervision was provided to address any issues promptly. Data were cleaned, edited, and checked for consistency, completeness, and missing values by the principal investigator prior to and during analysis.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Bahir Dar university institutional review board (IRB) before starting the actual work. Letter of support was obtained from the concerned bodies of health facilities. Informed verbal consent from each study participants was taken after a clear explanation about the purpose, duration, required samples, and data collection methods of the study to them. The study subjects were assured of the confidentiality of their responses, beneficial from the study was secured from anything regarding the study, no special payment due to participating.

Result

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 351 health professionals participated with a response rate of 96%. The majority 177 (50.4%) of the respondents were females. Nearly a third 122 (34.8%) of the respondents were within the age category of 29–34 years. 242 (68.9%) of the participants were degree holders followed by masters and above 73 (20.8%). In terms of profession, the majority 194(55.3%) of participants were nurses followed by laboratory technicians 37(10.5%) and 27(7.7%) specialists. Regarding the work experience 31% of respondents had 6–10 years of experience (Table 1).

Factors associated with the use of EMR

From all 18 variables that were tested in binary regression, 10 variables were candidate for a multivariable regression those are age, experience, satisfaction, type of documentation, preference, user-friendly EMR interface, EMR training, computer training, profession and working unit were candidates for a multivariable regression with p-value < 0.25 while variables such as sex. Attitude, perceived easiness didn’t have a significant association.

From a multivariable logistic regression model, Participants who said that EMR has a user-friendly interface are 2(AOR = 2, 95% CI = 1.115–4.130) times more likely to use EMR than those who said the EMR interface is not user-friendly. Individuals who are satisfied with EMR were 3(AOR = 3, 95% CI = 1.39–5.004) times more likely to use EMR than those who are not satisfied. Regarding preference those who prefer EMR were 3 (AOR = 3, 95% CI = 1.324–6.094) times more likely to use EMR. Related to EMR training, participants who take EMR training were 2 times (AOR = 2, 95% CI = 1.041–4.716) more likely to use EMR than those who didn’t take EMR training. Discussion

This study was carried out to assess the use of EMR and identify factors associated with its use among health professionals currently engaged in private healthcare facilities. This study found that 45.3% of healthcare professionals used EMRs. Comparing the EMR utilization rate in this study to findings from previous research, it was similar to a study from the West Oromia region (42%)23, but lower than the rate reported for Dire Dawa Hospital (67.7%)15 and higher than the rate found in the Gondar study (31.7%)24.

A study conducted in North Gondar showed that the overall health professionals’ utilization of the electronic medical record (EMR) system was 46.5%23. This result is in line with the findings from the current study.

The EMR utilization rate reported in this study was lower than the 87.6% observed at Ayder Referral Hospital25. The higher utilization rate at the latter hospital may be attributed to its status as one of the pilot sites for EMR deployment, which allowed for a relatively longer interval between system implementation and utilization assessment in that context. Another plausible explanation is that, in the setting of the present study, some health facilities lacked dedicated working spaces, potentially resulting in incomplete implementation of all EMR functionalities. Furthermore, power fluctuations in certain areas, as reported in the previous study, may also have contributed to the lower EMR utilization observed in this study.

A study conducted in Harar reported that 42.3% of respondents were using the electronic medical record system26, which is consistent with the findings of the present study. Similarly, a study from the United Kingdom indicated that 45.3% of professionals were currently using an EMR. Although this utilization rate appears comparable to our findings, the contexts differ. Specifically, our study assessed EMR use across all departments, whereas the UK study was limited to a single healthcare facility unit. Thus, the relatively lower utilization rate in our setting highlights the need for further improvement in EMR adoption across departments27.

In the current study, the most commonly utilized EMR components by healthcare practitioners were patient scheduling, the billing system, and laboratory order and result display. These findings are consistent with studies conducted in the western region of Oromia and in Tigray7,9. In contrast, the least utilized features were feedback exchange and the clinical decision support system, with only 24.8% of participants reporting use of the latter.

EMR training was significantly associated with system utilization; however, only 45.3% of healthcare professionals in this study had received basic training, compared to 64% at Gondar University Referral Hospital24. A user-friendly interface was also strongly associated with EMR use, with 65.2% of providers rating the system as user-friendly, consistent with findings from Jeddah, Saudi Arabia27. Similar associations between EMR utilization, training, and interface design have been reported in studies from Malawi, Saudi Arabia, and Tehran12,28,29.

Most participants (82.3%) preferred EMR systems over paper-based records, aligning with findings from Northern Ethiopia (76.4%)5 and Saudi Arabia (83%)29, but contrasting with results from Ayder Hospital, where 67.9% of providers favored paper records24.

Global studies have shown that EMR adoption varies widely due to multiple factors. In the present study, preference, overall satisfaction, EMR training, and user interface were significantly associated with utilization (p < 0.05). Respondents who preferred EMR over paper records were three times more likely to use the system (OR = 3, 95% CI: 1.324–6.094). This may reflect the link between preference and knowledge, as those favoring EMR are more likely to understand the system.

EMR utilization was also associated with user satisfaction. Notably, 43.0% of healthcare providers expressed dissatisfaction, primarily due to incomplete system implementation, unstable connectivity, inadequate training, and limited technical support. Similar associations between satisfaction and EMR use have been reported at Ayder Referral Hospital9 and in under-resourced hospitals in Ethiopia24.

In this study, the EMR user interface was significantly associated with utilization. Providers who perceived the interface as user-friendly were twice as likely to use EMR compared with those who did not (OR = 2.0, 95% CI: 1.115–4.130). This may reflect the overlap between training and usability perception, as many participants who rated the interface positively had also received EMR training. Similar associations between system design and EMR use have been reported in Kenya30.

Overall satisfaction was another predictor of EMR use. Satisfied participants were three times more likely to use EMR than those who were dissatisfied (OR = 3.0, 95% CI: 1.390–5.004). This may be explained by the link between perceived ease of use and satisfaction.

EMR training was also positively associated with utilization. Providers who had received basic EMR training were three times more likely to use the system than those without training (OR = 3.0, 95% CI: 1.324–6.094), consistent with findings from Ayder Hospital31. Training thus appears to play a key role in improving EMR adoption among healthcare professionals.

In Rwanda, 80% of participants reported satisfaction with EMR use32,33, a higher rate than observed in the present study. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in EMR software. Further research is warranted to clarify the factors underlying this variation.

Conclusion

EMR utilization in the healthcare facilities studied was unsatisfactory and fell short of national strategic targets. Although EMR systems have been introduced in private facilities, implementation remains fragmented, with patient documentation still heavily dependent on paper records. This dual system undermines efficiency, continuity of care, and the intended benefits of digital health transformation.

Moreover, adoption of core EMR functionalities was minimal, concentrated primarily on patient scheduling and laboratory order/result display. The limited use of clinical and decision-support features highlights a failure to leverage the system’s full potential. This underutilization points to systemic challenges including inadequate training, poor system design, and weak institutional commitments that must be addressed if EMR implementation is to meaningfully improve healthcare delivery.

Limitation and strength of the study

This study has the following limitations. It excluded private clinics, limiting generalizability, and did not incorporate qualitative methods, which could have provided deeper insights into user experiences. The cross-sectional design also restricts findings to associations rather than causal inferences. Nonetheless, by including all private hospitals in the study area, the research underscores the need for ongoing EMR training and improvements in system user-friendliness to enhance adoption and effective utilization.
