Tip-activated single-atom catalysis: CO oxidation on Au adatom on oxidized rutile TiO2 surface
Authors: Yuuki Adachi, Ján Brndiar, Martin Konôpka, Robert Turanský, Qiang Zhu, Huan Fei Wen, Yasuhiro Sugawara, Lev Kantorovich, Ivan Štich, Yan Jun Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Single-atom catalysis of carbon monoxide oxidation on metal-oxide surfaces is crucial for greenhouse recycling, automotive catalysis, and beyond, but reports of the atomic-scale mechanism are still scarce. Here, using scanning probe microscopy, we show that charging single gold atoms on oxidized rutile titanium dioxide surface, both positively and negatively, considerably promotes adsorption of carbon monoxide. No carbon monoxide adsorption is observed on neutral gold atoms. Two different carbon monoxide adsorption geometries on gold atoms are identified. We demonstrate full control over the redox state of adsorbed gold single atoms, carbon monoxide adsorption geometry, and carbon monoxide adsorption/desorption by the atomic force microscopy tip. On charged gold atoms, we activate Eley-Rideal oxidation reaction between carbon monoxide and a neighboring oxygen adatom by the tip. Our results provide unprecedented insights into carbon monoxide adsorption and suggest that the gold dual activity for carbon monoxide oxidation after electron or hole attachment is also the key ingredient in photocatalysis under realistic conditions.

INTRODUCTION

The research on exceptional catalytic properties of single-atom catalysis (SAC) on metal-oxide surfaces is becoming a new frontier in heterogeneous catalysis (1–4). The well-defined and uniform SAC offers great potential for fundamental understanding of atomic-scale catalysis by achieving high activity and selectivity due to the large coverage and high aspect ratio. The concept of SAC has also been used to disentangle the origins of various complex catalytic reactions, such as the effect of local charging and geometric effect at the ultimate spatial limit (1–4). Single adsorbed Au atoms (Au1) may play crucial role in conventional Au-based catalysts for various catalytic reactions (1–8). Several studies have shown that individual Au atoms on metal-oxide surfaces such as Pt1/FeOx (5), Au1/CeO2 (6), Au1/CuO (7), and Ni1/CuO (8) provide maximum utilization and demonstrate high activity in the CO oxidation reaction. Up to now, a general consensus of the origin of this high catalytic activity started to emerge as being a combination of the effects of reactant proximity and charge state of the noble atom (1–8). However, atomic-scale physical origins of SAC (9–14) are still under intense debate. Notably, catalytic performance in terms of Au nanoparticles may differ from the single-atom Au catalyst (10, 14). The key missing link is the ability to directly probe and understand the local environment and the charge state of the active site with atomic resolution. To this end, we study an Au-based SAC of CO oxidation on the oxidized rutile TiO2 (110)-(1 × 1) surface using atomic force microscopy (AFM). This is a prototypical metal-oxide surface suitable for various applications, catalysis included (9–24), and the AFM provides a single-atom resolution with a precise charge state control (25–27). To identify and manipulate the charge state of Au1, we used kelvin probe force spectroscopy (KPFS) as transitions of the Au1 charge state can directly be initiated and observed as voltage dependent frequency shift (Δf) jumps between two different parabolas. Each jump in KPFS corresponds to an increase (decrease) in the Au1 charge state by attaching an electron (hole) upon ramping the bias voltage U in the positive (negative) direction.

RESULTS

CO adsorption on Au1/O-rutile TiO2 (110)-(1 × 1) surface

An intriguing summary of our results is shown in Fig. 1. Rutile TiO2 (110)-(1 × 1) surface contains twofold coordinated protruding O atoms (O2c) and fivefold coordinated Ti atoms (Ti5c) that are alternatively aligned (10–18, 20–24). When the TiO2 surface is exposed to oxygen at room temperature, oxygen will dissociate and adsorb as adatoms (Oad) (see also fig. S2 for a large area AFM image) (21, 22). The subsequent deposition of Au atoms at low temperature leads to dispersed single Au atoms adsorbed in the immediate vicinity of the Oad (Fig. 1, A and D). Au adsorption on multiple Oad is also possible (see figs. S3 and S4). The stable asymmetric bright spot corresponds to a Au atom, that is initially charge neutral (Au10), with Oad (or multiple Oad) adsorbed on Ti5c in the nearby trench formed by the oxygen rows (O2c) (17). An electron or hole can be attached to Au10 by ramping the bias voltage with the AFM tip placed on top of it, leading to formation of a negative (Au1−; Fig. 1B) or a positive (Au1+; Fig. 1C) Au adatoms (25–27). We find that the redox reaction of Au markedly alters its chemical reactivity: After adsorbing CO, the molecules preferably adsorb on top of Au1− or Au1+. These results indicate that Au10 has lower reactivity to CO than Au1− and Au1+, which feature high reactivity (Fig. 1, F to I). Furthermore, qualitative analysis of many AFM images suggests that the adsorbed CO molecules are seen in AFM images in two adsorption states, either a protrusion/bean or a depression/donut, interpreted as Au1+-OC (C-up) or Au1+-CO (O-up) adsorption configurations (Fig. 1J) on both Au1− (Fig. 1, F and H) and Au1+ (Fig. 1, G and I). The line profiles corresponding to CO/Au1+ are in Fig. 1E for both bean and donut adsorption states. We speculate that the bean image shape (green/blue lines in Fig. 1E) reflects one of the two π2p molecular orbitals of CO possibly with a different alignment of the CO molecule (see fig. S17) and that the deep image depression corresponding to the donut image is due to repulsive tip-sample interaction over the CO molecule, which gives way and bends, resulting in ≈1-Å ditch over the CO molecule (orange line in Fig. 1E). A similar AFM image of the depression/donut adsorption shape is also found for the CO adsorbed onto the Ti row, with a depression in the middle, which is supposed to be a C-down atom adsorption geometry (28).

A range of manipulations of the CO/Au1 complex is available: (i) The redox state of Au1 can be altered by KPFS between cationic and anionic, (ii) the two CO adsorption geometries incurring CO rotation can reversibly be interchanged during scanning by lateral AFM manipulation suggesting a weak Au1─CO bond, and (iii) the CO molecule can be desorbed by KPFS (see Fig. 2 and fig. S9). Adsorption behavior with a weak (<1 eV) Au1─CO bond was also observed on related metal surfaces (4). Unexpectedly, customary density functional theory (DFT) modeling is not capable to describe such a weak Au1─CO interaction. In a nutshell, the strongly correlated and relativistic Au1 atom, in comparison with the work function of rutile O─TiO2 (≈6 eV), has a huge ionization potential in excess of 9 eV. As a result, a single-reference scalar relativistic DFT modeling aligns the Fermi energy with that of both subsystems, the 6s1 orbital is depopulated, and a cationic Au1+ species forms spontaneously strongly bonding to both the rutile TiO2 and the CO molecule. In doing so, the gold atom loses its atomic nature and behaves as a strongly surface bound Au1+(O2−)2 complex (see fig. S16). Identical conclusions were also reached in other related scalar relativistic simulations [see, for instance, (11, 13)]. Results of DFT modeling indirectly strongly suggest that a weaker Au1─CO interaction is only possible with the 6s electron(s) present on the Au1 atom, either in a singlet [Xe]4f145d106s2 (Au1−)or magnetic triplet [Xe]4f145d96s1 (Au1+) electronic configurations (see fig. S17 for more details). The magnetic triplet states may be true ground states in a relativistic many-body theory or may be local minima resulting from the KPFS manipulation.

Charge manipulation of Au1

In Fig. 2 (A to C), we demonstrate an ability to consecutively charge manipulate the Au1 by an electron transfer between Au1 and the AFM tip (21, 25–27, 31). The bright spot in Fig. 2A is attributed to Au10. Next, by moving the tip on top of the Au10 and ramping the bias voltage from 0 V in the positive direction and then back to 0 V (Fig. 2D), a bigger bright spot was formed at the position of Au10, which now corresponds to Au1− (Fig. 2B). An oxidation process can be induced by applying a negative bias (Fig. 2E), leading to formation of Au1+ (Fig. 2C). Characterization of the different charge states of Au1 can be performed by measuring local contact potential differences and Δf (z) spectra on top of Au10, Au1−, and Au1+ (Fig. 2G). Strong differences among the three Δf curves measured at small tip-sample distances are attributable to differences in the short-range chemical interaction between the tip and Au1. These effects are further corroborated by the different topographic heights measured on top of Au10, Au1−, and Au1+ (Fig. 2F). The apparent topographic height of Au1− is larger than of those of Au10 and Au1+, reflecting a distinct electrostatic interaction between the tip and the Au atom. We attribute the similarity of the AFM images of Au10 and Au1+ to the presumed magnetic nature of the Au1+ that will leave the outer shells of Au10 and Au1+ essentially identical and dissimilar to Au1− (6s1 versus 6s2; see also fig. S6). Contrary, the spectroscopy, which will probe much larger distances, will reveal distinct differences due to the very different nature of the electrostatic interactions. Furthermore, we find that the Au1 switches only between Au1− and Au1+, once an electron or hole has been attached to Au10 (26) .We propose that the reorganization energy (27), essentially the width of the KPFS hysteresis loop, for neutralizing Au from both positive and negative charge states (Au1+→Au10 and Au1−→Au10), is larger than that for transitions between positive and negative charge states

(Au1+→Au1− and Au1−→Au1+; see figs. S3, S6, and S7). The analysis (fig. S17) also strongly suggests that the Au1+↔Au1−charge manipulation corresponds to magnetic (triplet) ↔ nonmagnetic (singlet) tip-induced switching.

CO adsorption on Au atom in different redox states

We next show that CO adsorption can be activated directly by modifying the Au10 charge state with the AFM tip. Figure 3 shows AFM images of Au10, Au1+, and Au1−sequentially obtained under exposure to CO. In Fig. 3A, the circular bright and triangular dark spots (28) corresponding toAu10 and CO, respectively, are observed in the AFM image. The sequences of dark spots are formed by a lateral tip manipulation of the CO molecule on the surface during the scan (28, 29). No CO molecule is found adsorbed on top of Au10. These observations are further supported by large area AFM image of Au10 on oxidized rutile TiO2 (110) surface exposed to CO (Fig. 3D). Around 10 Au10 were observed in Fig. 3D, yet no CO was found to adsorb on top of Au10. Therefore, we conclude that CO adsorption probability on top of Au10 is low. Figure 3B shows the AFM image of the same scan area immediately after applying negative and positive bias voltages on top of the two Au10 of Fig. 3A. Upon the attachment of a hole and an electron to Au10, Fig. 3B shows two bright spots of different sizes that correspond to Au1+ and Au1−. Figure 3C shows the AFM (donut) image of the same scan area immediately after obtaining Fig. 3B, with fuzzy dark spots that correspond to CO adsorbed on top of the Au1+ and (CO/Au1+ and CO/Au1−) (15, 16). Full characterization of Au1−, Au1+, CO/Au1−, and CO/Au1+ by Δf(z) spectra is shown in Fig. 3E. The tip in the hole mode (see the Supplementary Materials) shows weaker interaction with Au1+ than Au1− (green and black curves in Fig. 3E), which is in line with our observations in Fig. 2G. We found that Δf values measured both on top of CO/Au1− and CO/Au1+ (blue and red curves in Fig. 3E) show qualitatively the same ordering, but their behavior is substantially different from that on top of Au1− and Au1+ (30). The observed spike feature at short tip-sample distances in the CO/Au1− and CO/Au1+ spectroscopies suggests that CO in donut adsorption geometry bends appreciably, which leads to the formation of the ditches in the AFM image over the CO molecules (Fig. 1). Direct comparison between bean- and donut-shaped images for the same charge state (Au1−) in Fig. 3F indicates that the donut-shaped related features appear at a small tip-sample distance (z < 0.13 nm). The spectroscopy over CO/Au1− and CO/Au1+ in the bean and donut adsorption geometries suggests that no temporal CO manipulation occurs (see also fig. S9W).

Tip-induced single-atom catalysis

We now use the AFM tip to accomplish a CO oxidation reaction. First, we select a target Au10 located in the vicinity of an oxygen adatom, Oad. At large tip-sample distance, Oad appears as a halo over the Ti trench. Next, a negative bias voltage is applied to Au10, leading first to formation of Au1+ and subsequently to CO adsorption on top of it. Figure 4A shows the AFM image of the CO/Au1+ complex, corresponding to CO adsorbed in geometry corresponding to the bean shape akin to that in Fig. 1G. In this case, the bean image has a pronounced asymmetry (see Fig. 1C), most likely due to the presence of an additional Oad in the vicinity. Figure 4B shows the AFM image of Au1+ immediately after applying a positive bias of +2.5 V with the tip positioned between CO/Au1+ and Oad, as indicated by the pink circle in Fig. 4A, suggesting that CO and Oad both disappear as evidenced by disappearance of the bean shaped AFM image of the CO molecule and removal of Oad from the ridge between the oxygen rows (Fig. 4, B, G, and H). The Δf(z) spectra measured on top of Au1+, and CO/Au1+ demonstrate the different attractive forces exerted by Au1+ and CO/Au1+ (Fig. 4F), which, in combination with the disappearance of the ditch in the line profile (Fig. 4E) and in the three-dimensional AFM image (Fig. 4, G and H), lend a further support for the CO desorption. Notably, although in Fig. 4 (A and B), the AFM images of Au+ with and without CO adsorption seem identical, Δf(z) in Fig. 4F, show different characteristics, thereby indicating that the Au atoms in each case are in different charge states resulting from the inherently different electronic structure. Since CO and Oad simultaneously disappear immediately after the positive bias voltage was applied, Fig. 4 (A and B) suggests the CO oxidation reaction: CO + O → CO2. We believe that CO2 desorbs from the surface either due to the large electric field or too weak a bond formed with Au1+. The same reaction has also been observed with Au1− (fig. S10). We have confirmed reproducibility of the experiment in Fig. 4, albeit only in the same bean shape CO adsorption geometry, suggesting that orientation of the CO molecule (O-down) is crucial for probability of the oxidation reaction to proceed. DISCUSSION

On the basis of our results, we portray the mechanism for a real photocatalytic CO reaction at the SAC cycle on the O-rutile TiO2 (110) surface. First, Au1− or Au1+ is formed by attachment of an electron or hole, and then CO preferably adsorbs on top of Au1− or Au1+. A weak bond between CO and Au1+, the geometric proximity between Oad and CO, and the applied field lead to the reduction of the activation barrier, facilitating the formation of CO2. These experiments give unique insights into the basic ingredients of the CO oxidation reaction that we activated by a voltage pulse. Under realistic reaction conditions, the CO oxidation will have a different activation source, but we expect the main features of the reaction revealed here to remain.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental

All AFM experiments were performed on a home-built low-temperature ultrahigh vacuum AFM system. Following procedures previously described in (21–24, 32–34), a Nanonis OC4 system with electronics and software was used for control and data acquisition. The optical beam deflection method was used to measure the deflection of the cantilever. The base pressure of the observation chamber was lower than 5.0 × 10−11 torr. The AFM measurements were performed in the frequency modulation detection mode with the temperature of the AFM unit kept at liquid nitrogen temperature (78 K). Iridium (Ir)–coated Si cantilever (Nano sensors SD-T10L100, f0 = 1000 kHz, A = 500 pm) has oscillated at resonance frequency keeping the oscillation amplitude constant. Metal Ir tips provide stable AFM imaging compared to the bare Si tip. The tip was initially annealed to 600 K and then cleaned by Ar+ sputtering to remove the contamination before experiments. The atom tracking method was used to compensate for the thermal drift between the tip and the surface during the measurements. The dc bias voltage was applied to the sample. Kelvin probe force microscopy was performed in frequency modulation mode based on the AFM previously described in (22, 35). The AFM imaging was performed using constant Δf mode at Vbias = 0 V to avoid the tunneling current to flow. Analysis of images of OH defects on the O2c rows reported in the Supplementary Materials confirms that they are imaged as depressions (21, 22). Therefore, we conclude that the images in Figs. 2 (A to C), 3 (A to D), and 4 (A and B) and figs. S2A, S4 (A to C), S5 (A to F), S6 to S8, and S9 (A to W) were taken in the hole mode, and figs. S3, S10, and S12 were taken either in the hole or neutral mode. The rutile TiO2 (110)-(1 × 1) sample (Furuuchi Chemical Corporation) was prepared by sputtering and annealing to 900 K in several cycles. The sample was exposed to oxygen at room temperature for ~0.5 liters and transferred to the measurement chamber precooled to 78 K. Then Au atoms (Nilaco purity, 99.99%) were deposited on oxidized TiO2 (110) at ~100 K by heating the gold evaporant in a crucible (Omicron EFM) under ultra-high vacuum (<1.0 × 10−11 torr) condition. Last, the sample was exposed to CO at 78 K in the measurement chamber. Customary scalar-relativistic DFT modeling techniques have attempted to simulate the experiments. Detail description of DFT calculation has shown in the Supplementary Materials.
