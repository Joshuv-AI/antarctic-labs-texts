Fournier's Gangrene and Purulent Peritonitis as a Result of Progressing Ischiorectal Abscess: A Case Report From Muhimbili National Hospital, Tanzania
Authors: Hervé Tshikomba Mbuyamba, Rajabu Athumani Bakari, Charles Oswald Ruboha, Alfred Chibwae, Kelvin Richard Martin, Boniface Kahindi Kazungu, Ahmed Abubakar Ahmed, Daniel William Kitua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIschiorectal abscess can rarely progress beyond the anorectal region to cause Fournier's gangrene, supralevator extension, and purulent peritonitis. Early recognition of disease progression, prompt imaging, aggressive surgical source control, and multidisciplinary management are critical to reducing morbidity and mortality in this life‐threatening complication.

Introduction

Ischiorectal abscesses are localized infections in the ischiorectal fossa, typically resulting from infection of the anal crypt glands [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. These abscesses are often caused by obstruction of the anal gland ducts, leading to accumulation of secretions, bacterial overgrowth, and abscess formation [6, 7]. While they may initially present as a simple collection of pus, complications can arise if the infection spreads to adjacent tissues. Notably, Fournier's gangrene, a rapidly progressing necrotizing fasciitis of the perineum and scrotum, can develop. This life‐threatening condition is associated with high morbidity and mortality if not managed promptly [8, 9]. Additionally, infection may ascend to involve the abdominal cavity, leading to purulent peritonitis, a severe and potentially fatal complication [10, 11].

The fascial planes play a significant role in the pathogenesis and spread of Fournier's gangrene. The infection often follows the Colles' fascia, Dartos' fascia, and Scarpa's fascia, facilitating rapid extension of the necrotizing process to the abdominal wall, scrotum, and perineum [9, 11, 12]. This case report highlights a case of a 41‐year‐old male who presented with a severe form of ischiorectal abscess that progressed to Fournier's gangrene and purulent peritonitis.

Case History and Examination

A 41‐year‐old male with no known chronic illness presented with a 5‐day history of acute scrotal swelling, which progressively increased in size and pain. Scrotal blisters, foul‐smelling discharge, fever, and generalized malaise later accompanied this. The patient denied symptoms suggestive of lower urinary tract infection or perineal trauma. Over the 5‐day course of the illness, he gradually developed bilious, non‐projectile vomiting, increasing in frequency from 3 to 7 episodes per day. This was associated with colicky abdominal pain, diarrhea, generalized abdominal distension, and loss of appetite. The patient's medical history revealed a perineal abscess that was reported to have last been drained three [3] years ago and it has been recurring. He denied a history of diabetes mellitus, HIV infection, or prior pelvic trauma.

On examination, the patient was cachexic, ill‐looking, conscious, pale, jaundiced with a pulse rate of 105 beats per minute, while the rest of the vital signs were within normal limits. A nasogastric tube was in place, draining bilious contents. The abdomen was globally distended with a tympanic note on percussion and reduced bowel sounds; however, no signs of peritonism were noted. The genital examination revealed a circumcised phallus with normal penile skin and a urethral catheter draining clear urine. The scrotum was swollen, dusky, and discolored, with multiple vesicles, crepitations, tenderness on palpation, and a brown, foul‐smelling discharge. Additionally, a left ischiorectal abscess was identified during the evaluation.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Laboratory investigations showed markedly elevated C‐reactive protein (227 mg/L) and white blood cell count (35 × 109/L) with neutrophilia, alongside severe anemia (hemoglobin 5.8 g/dL). Liver and renal function tests were abnormal, with blood urea nitrogen at 8.9 mmol/L, creatinine at 89 μmol/L, aspartate aminotransferase at 135 IU/L, alanine aminotransferase at 108 IU/L, direct bilirubin at 28.2 μmol/L, and indirect bilirubin at 18.7 μmol/L. Moreover, his albumin levels were 2.1 g/L. Random blood glucose and international normalized ratio values were within normal limits, and the patient tested negative for HIV. Pus culture identified Staphylococcus aureus as sensitive to cephalosporins and meropenem. Scrotal ultrasound revealed subcutaneous emphysema consistent with Fournier's gangrene, while an abdominal X‐ray showed multiple air‐fluid levels in the erect position, indicative of bowel obstruction. The differential diagnoses that were reached before laparotomy included Fournier's gangrene secondary to ischiorectal abscess and peritonitis secondary to complicated appendicitis, perforated peptic ulcer or perforated bowel.

The patient underwent surgical treatment, which included debridement of Fournier's gangrene (Figure 1), incision and drainage of a left ischiorectal abscess (Figure 2) that had a communicating fistula into the anal canal, and an exploratory laparotomy. Intraoperative findings revealed the extension of the left ischiorectal abscess through the retroperitoneal planes, reaching the root of the small bowel mesentery, along with peritoneal pus contamination (Figure 3). The peritoneal side of the anterior abdominal wall was also involved Figure 4. A colostomy was performed to divert stool away from the perineum. Postoperatively, the patient was managed with intravenous meropenem 1 g every 8 h for 3 weeks and received sitz baths twice daily with an antiseptic solution. Antibiotics were discontinued after pus cultures from the abdominal drain that was placed intraoperatively had no growth. The abdominal drain was removed on the 4th postoperative week once drainage of pus had ceased. The patient showed improvement and was eventually discharged for follow‐up through the urology clinic.

Discussion

Fournier's gangrene is frequently associated with identifiable sources of infection, including a colorectal nidus (30%–50% of cases), a urogenital nidus (20%–40% of cases), cutaneous infections (20% of cases), and local trauma [13, 14, 15]. Colorectal sources, in particular, encompass a wide range of conditions such as local infections, abscesses—especially in the perianal, perirectal, and ischiorectal regions—anal fissures, colonic perforations, diverticulitis, hemorrhoidectomy, and rectal carcinoma [8, 9, 13, 14, 15].

Fournier's gangrene secondary to an ischiorectal abscess is a rare but serious complication, typically requiring a combination of predisposing factors that compromise host immunity. The most commonly reported risk factors include diabetes mellitus (20%–70% of cases) and excessive alcohol consumption (20%–50% of cases) [15, 16, 17, 18]. Other contributing factors include extremes of age, malignancy, chronic steroid use, cytotoxic drugs, lymphoproliferative diseases, malnutrition, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection [14, 19, 20]. In our case, the majority of these predisposing factors were absent. However, the patient exhibited low albumin levels, indicative of malnutrition. This malnutrition could also predate the infection or result from the overwhelming inflammatory response. Elevated liver function test results further suggested systemic involvement, augmenting the possibility of overwhelming infection [15, 21, 22].

Ischiorectal abscesses typically arise from the obstruction of anal crypts, leading to infection. While they may be drained successfully, complications like Fournier's gangrene can develop if untreated. This life‐threatening necrotizing fasciitis begins in the perineum and spreads along fascial planes, including Colles' fascia, Dartos' fascia, and Scarpa's fascia. Bacterial toxins promote vascular thrombosis and necrosis. This infection's rapid spread is facilitated by these fascial planes, leading to extensive tissue loss and requiring immediate debridement [9, 11, 12]. The infection may spread to the abdominal cavity via hematogenous or direct extension, resulting in peritonitis [23, 24]. The elevated transaminases (AST, ALT) and bilirubin levels indicate possible hepatic dysfunction, secondary to sepsis or ascending infection from the perineum [25, 26].

The polymicrobial nature of Fournier's gangrene, involving both aerobic and anaerobic bacteria, is critical for the production of various exotoxins and enzymes—such as collagenase, heparinase, hyaluronidase, streptokinase, and streptodornase—that facilitate rapid bacterial proliferation and tissue destruction [9, 22]. Among the aerobic microorganisms, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae , and Staphylococcus aureus are the most commonly isolated, while Bacteroides fragilis is the predominant anaerobic organism [9, 22]. Other implicated organisms include Streptococcus, Enterococcus, Clostridium, Pseudomonas, and Proteus species. In our case, Staphylococcus aureus was identified in the pus culture. However, Group A streptococcus has been reported as the most common causative agent in cases of monomicrobial necrotizing fasciitis.

The diagnosis of Fournier's gangrene with peritonitis primarily relies on clinical assessment and requires a high index of suspicion. Laboratory findings are often nonspecific, underscoring the importance of imaging investigations. Imaging not only aids in diagnosing peritonitis but may also reveal characteristic findings of Fournier's gangrene, such as subcutaneous emphysema. The management of this condition demands prompt and decisive interventions based on three fundamental principles: rapid and aggressive surgical debridement of necrotic tissue, hemodynamic stabilization through urgent fluid resuscitation, and the administration of broad‐spectrum parenteral antibiotics [9, 27]. Timely execution of these measures is critical to improving patient outcomes.

Conclusion and Results

Fournier's gangrene is a life‐threatening condition requiring a high index of suspicion for early diagnosis and effective management. While it is often associated with predisposing factors such as diabetes and immunosuppression, cases without these factors, like ours, highlight the unpredictable nature of the disease. The polymicrobial etiology, driven by aerobic and anaerobic organisms, underscores the need for broad‐spectrum antibiotic therapy alongside rapid surgical debridement and hemodynamic stabilization. Imaging is crucial in identifying complications such as peritonitis and guiding timely intervention. This case emphasizes the importance of vigilance and prompt action to improve outcomes in patients with this severe condition. The patient improved after being in surgical ward for 3 weeks, and the plastic surgery team took over for scrotal reconstruction that was done successfully.
