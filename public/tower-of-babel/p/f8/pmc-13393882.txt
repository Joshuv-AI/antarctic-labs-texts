Designing advanced nanocomposite materials for efficient elimination of Victoria Blue B dye from aqueous media
Authors: Nada S. Al-Kadhi, Saad A. Aljlil, Ehab A. Abdelrahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The growing discharge of persistent cationic dyes necessitates adsorbents that combine high efficiency, facile production, and reliable regeneration. Herein, nickel oxide/magnesium chromate/carbon novel nanocomposites were successfully synthesized by a Pechini sol-gel route at 600 and 800 °C to obtain NMC600 and NMC800, respectively, for the elimination of Victoria Blue B from aqueous media. XRD, FE-SEM, HR-TEM, EDX, and BET analyses confirmed the formation of well-integrated nanostructures whose physicochemical properties were strongly governed by calcination temperature. NMC600 displayed smaller crystallites, finer polyhedral particles, higher residual carbon content, larger surface area (56.27 m2/g), and greater pore volume (0.2517 cm3/g), resulting in marked superior adsorption performance compared with NMC800. Under optimized conditions, NMC600 achieved a maximum Langmuir capacity of 371.74 mg/g, whereas NMC800 reached 194.55 mg/g. Kinetic evaluation revealed that dye uptake followed the pseudo-first-order model, while thermodynamic parameters confirmed a spontaneous, exothermic, and predominantly physical adsorption process. Mechanistic interpretation indicated that electrostatic attraction under alkaline conditions, hydrogen bonding, complexation, and π-π interaction collectively controlled dye removal. The nanocomposites also exhibited efficient desorption using 2 M HCl, stable recyclability over five cycles, resistance to common interfering ions, and promising performance in real wastewater.

Introduction

Water contamination by synthetic dyes has emerged as one of the most critical environmental threats of the 21st century, driven primarily by the global expansion of the textile, paper, cosmetic, and plastic industries [1–3]. These industries are responsible for releasing large volumes of colored effluents into natural water systems, often without adequate treatment. Once discharged, synthetic dyes remain chemically stable and resistant to microbial degradation, leading to long-term persistence in aquatic environments. These pollutants disrupt the photosynthetic activity of aquatic plants, reduce sunlight penetration, and endanger aquatic biodiversity [4–6]. More importantly, many synthetic dyes exhibit carcinogenic and mutagenic properties, which raise serious public health concerns [7, 8]. Among the wide variety of synthetic dyes, Victoria Blue B stands out as a cationic dye with widespread applications in the textile and ink industries. Its molecular structure consists of complex aromatic and heterocyclic components, making it highly resistant to conventional treatment methods. Victoria Blue B is toxic, non-biodegradable, and can accumulate in living organisms, where it may induce genotoxic and cytotoxic effects [9, 10]. Its removal from wastewater is therefore an urgent priority for both environmental protection and public health preservation. Conventional dye removal techniques include biological treatment [11, 12], membrane filtration [13], advanced oxidation processes [14], and chemical precipitation [15]. However, many of these approaches are hindered by limitations such as incomplete dye degradation, formation of harmful byproducts, high operational costs, and poor energy efficiency. Among them, adsorption is increasingly recognized as the most promising technique as well as practical method for dye elimination, offering advantages such as high removal efficiency, process simplicity, low cost, and suitability for large-scale implementation [16]. Nevertheless, the success of adsorption processes is heavily reliant on the properties of the adsorbent materials. Conventional adsorbents such as activated carbon [17], silica [18], and clays [19] suffer from drawbacks including low adsorption capacities, poor regeneration efficiency, and limited surface reactivity. This has led to the exploration of advanced nanomaterials and nanocomposites as next-generation adsorbents. Nanostructured materials exhibit superior adsorption behavior due to their high surface area, tunable pore size, and functionalizable surface groups [20–22]. Specifically, mixed metal oxides and spinel-type nanocomposites offer remarkable chemical and thermal stability, multiple active domains, and strong association with dye molecules [23–28]. The Pechini sol–gel method is a versatile and cost-effective route for synthesizing homogenous, multi-component oxide materials with controlled composition, particle size, and morphology [29–32]. It enables the formation of fine nanostructures at relatively low temperatures through the chelation and polyesterification of metal ions using organic acids and polyols. This technique is particularly suitable for preparing advanced nanocomposites with intimate mixing of metal precursors at the molecular level, leading to uniform phase distribution and high thermal stability [33, 34]. Compared with conventional solid-state reactions, co-precipitation, hydrothermal synthesis, and combustion-based routes, the Pechini sol–gel method offers several important advantages for preparing multi-component oxide/carbon nanocomposites. Solid-state reactions usually require high calcination temperatures and prolonged heating, which may promote particle coarsening, incomplete mixing, and phase segregation [35]. Co-precipitation is relatively simple, but it can suffer from non-uniform precipitation of different metal ions due to their different hydrolysis and solubility behaviors, which may reduce compositional homogeneity [36]. Hydrothermal methods can provide crystalline nanostructures but often require sealed autoclaves, longer reaction times, and pressure-controlled conditions, which may limit scalability [37]. Combustion synthesis is rapid and energy-efficient; however, the fast exothermic reaction can make particle growth and morphology difficult to control [38]. In contrast, the Pechini sol–gel route relies on chelation of metal ions followed by polyesterification with a polyol, producing a polymeric resin in which the metal cations are distributed at the molecular level. This homogeneous precursor network minimizes local compositional fluctuations, facilitates the formation of well-integrated mixed oxide phases at relatively moderate calcination temperatures, and allows better control over particle size, morphology, and residual carbon content [39]. These features make the Pechini route particularly suitable for the present NiO/MgCr2O4/C system, where uniform integration of NiO, MgCr2O4, and carbon domains is essential for achieving high surface accessibility, structural stability, and efficient adsorption performance. Recent efforts to remove Victoria Blue B using several materials have yielded mixed results. For instance, polypyrrole-encapsulated zirconium oxide achieved a maximum capacity of 238.09 mg/g [40], while mesoporous MCM-41 silica and ZnO nanoparticles exhibited 192.30 mg/g and 163.00 mg/g, respectively [41, 42]. Other systems, such as Fe2O3-based composites [43, 44] and alginate-supported activated carbon, showed relatively poor performance with capacities below 60 mg/g. Despite these advancements, challenges such as insufficient removal rates, low recyclability, and high cost underscore the continued demand for novel adsorbents capable of achieving high removal efficiency, ease of regeneration, and chemical robustness. Despite the progress achieved in Victoria Blue B removal, several important research gaps remain insufficiently addressed. Many reported adsorbents still suffer from moderate adsorption capacities, complex preparation procedures, limited regeneration assessment, insufficient real wastewater validation, efficient recyclability, or incomplete discussion of the adsorption pathways. Therefore, there remains a need for simply prepared multifunctional adsorbents that can combine high adsorption efficiency, structural stability, reusability, and multiple dye-removal interactions. In this context, NiO/MgCr2O4/C novel nanocomposite was facilely synthesized using Pechini sol–gel route at 600 and 800 °C. These integrated domains of nanocomposite can provide multiple adsorption sites and promote Victoria Blue B removal through four main pathways: electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, surface complexation, and π–π interaction. Thus, the novelty and significance of the present work lie in the first-time design of a simply prepared Pechini-derived NiO/MgCr2O4/C nanocomposite for Victoria Blue B sequestration, with the aim of achieving high adsorption performance through cooperative oxide/spinel/carbon interactions. Structural, morphological, and compositional characterizations were performed using XRD, FE-SEM, HR-TEM, and EDX analyses. The adsorption behavior of these materials toward Victoria Blue B dye was investigated under various conditions, including solution pH, initial dye concentration, contact time, and temperature. Furthermore, the isothermal, kinetic, and thermodynamic aspects of the adsorption process were evaluated. Finally, desorption and regeneration experiments were carried out to assess the reusability of the nanocomposites and their potential for sustainable wastewater treatment. Although chromium-containing materials require careful environmental consideration, the chromium used in the present study is incorporated as Cr(III) within the MgCr2O4 spinel framework rather than being present as free or hexavalent chromium species. The toxicity and mobility of chromium are strongly dependent on its oxidation state, with Cr(VI) being far more toxic, soluble, and environmentally mobile than Cr(III). In contrast, Cr(III)-based spinel chromites such as MgCr2O4 possess a compact and chemically stable oxide lattice, in which chromium is structurally bound within octahedral sites, thereby reducing its tendency to exist as readily mobile chromium species. Therefore, Cr was selected because it enables the formation of a robust MgCr2O4 spinel phase that contributes to structural stability, chemical resistance, and the creation of additional oxide surface domains beneficial for adsorption. This design aims to exploit the stability of the chromite spinel structure while avoiding the use of soluble or hexavalent chromium compounds. In addition, the present NiO/MgCr2O4/C material was intentionally designed as an in-situ integrated nanocomposite rather than as a physical mixture of separately prepared oxide phases. In the Pechini sol–gel route, Ni, Mg, and Cr ions are chelated and distributed within an organic polymeric network at the molecular level, and subsequent calcination promotes simultaneous phase formation, interfacial integration, and partial carbon retention. Attempts to obtain the individual constituent phases under the same preparation and calcination conditions did not provide sufficiently pure and directly comparable materials; therefore, comparison with such non-equivalent controls would not fairly represent the designed composite architecture. Based on the literature on Pechini-derived multiphase oxide/carbon adsorbents, the composite architecture is expected to provide synergistic adsorption advantages through the complementary roles of NiO active oxide sites, the structurally stable MgCr2O4 spinel framework, and carbon-assisted surface accessibility and dye–surface interactions [45–47]. Thus, Cr was selected to construct a robust MgCr2O4 spinel phase within an integrated NiO/MgCr2O4/C framework, aiming to exploit the stability of chromite spinel while avoiding soluble or hexavalent chromium species and enhancing the multifunctional adsorption character of the composite.

Experimental

Materials and reagents

All materials utilized in the study were of certified analytical grade and were procured from Sigma-Aldrich without any extended purification procedures. magnesium nitrate hexahydrate (Mg(NO3)2.6H2O, MW = 256.41 g/mol), tartaric acid (C4H6O6, MW = 150.09 g/mol), hydrochloric acid (HCl, MW = 36.46 g/mol), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, MW = 40.00 g/mol), potassium chloride (KCl, MW = 74.55 g/mol), polyethylene glycol 400 (H(OCH2CH2)nOH, average MW ≈ 400 g/mol), chromium nitrate nonahydrate (Cr(NO3)3.9H2O, MW = 400.15 g/mol), nickel nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2.6H2O, MW = 290.79 g/mol), and Victoria Blue B dye (C33H32ClN3, MW = 506.09 g/mol) were used as received for the synthesis of nanocomposites and adsorption evaluation procedures described in the following sections.

Synthesis of NiO/MgCr2O4/C nanoproducts

NiO/MgCr2O4/C nanoproducts were derived via the Pechini sol–gel procedure, as indicated in schematic Fig. 1. An amount of 35 g of chelating agent (i.e. tartaric acid) was initially solubilized using 100 mL of double-distilled water. In addition, in a separate beaker, 20 g of magnesium nitrate hexahydrate, 20 g of chromium nitrate nonahydrate, as well as 20 g of nickel nitrate hexahydrate were solubilized using 200 mL of double-distilled water. Also, the previously dissolved tartaric acid was afterward incorporated gradually to the metal nitrate mixture with uninterrupted mixing for a duration of 30 min. Thereafter, 15 mL of crosslinker (polyethylene glycol 400) was transferred to the blend, and stirring was maintained at 200 °C on a temperature-controlled hot plate equipped with magnetic stirring until total volatilization of the solvents was achieved, resulting in the production of a solid residue. The yielded solid was collected and calcinated at 600 and 800 °C for 3 h to produce NiO/MgCr2O4/C nanocomposites, which were marked as NMC600 and NMC800, respectively.

The calcination temperatures were selected based on reported Pechini/sol–gel-derived oxide systems, in which thermal treatment within the 600–800 °C range is commonly sufficient to decompose the organic/nitrate precursor network and induce the formation of crystalline metal oxide or spinel oxide phases [48, 49]. Previous studies have shown that Pechini-derived oxide precursors can begin to form crystalline oxide phases at approximately 600 °C, while higher calcination temperatures promote increased crystallinity and crystallite growth [48, 49]. In addition, sol–gel-derived MgCr2O4 spinel nanostructures have been reported to form as impurity-free phases within the 500–750 °C treatment range, confirming that the selected temperatures are appropriate for the development of MgCr2O4-containing oxide systems [50]. Accordingly, 600 °C was selected as a moderate calcination temperature to allow decomposition of the Pechini organic/nitrate precursor network and formation of the oxide/spinel nanocomposite while preserving part of the carbonaceous residue. Meanwhile, 800 °C was selected as a higher calcination temperature to promote stronger crystallization, further removal of organic/carbonaceous residues, grain growth, and textural evolution. Thus, the selected calcination temperatures enabled the preparation of two thermally distinct nanocomposites, allowing evaluation of the influence of calcination temperature on phase development, carbon content, surface area, porosity, and adsorption performance.

Characterization

The crystalline structure of the produced nanocomposites was examined using an X-ray diffraction (XRD) system (PANalytical X’Pert Pro Multipurpose Diffractometer) equipped with a Philips PW3040/60 X-ray source and an X’Celerator detector. The measurements were performed using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) operated at an accelerating voltage of 40 kV and a tube current of 40 mA. The external morphology and elemental constituents of the synthesized products were explored operating field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) associated with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The analyses were conducted using a Quanta 250 FEG scanning electron microscope to obtain detailed microstructural and compositional information. Furthermore, ultra-high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (JEM-2100Plus HR-TEM, JEOL) was employed to explore the internal structure, crystallinity, and nanoscale features of the synthesized products. BET surface area measurements were performed using a NOVA 2000 series analyzer (Quantachrome, USA). The surface functional groups of the synthesized nanocomposites before and after Victoria Blue B adsorption were identified using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR; Nicolet iS10, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). CHN elemental analysis was performed using an elemental analyzer (Thermo Scientific, Flash 2000 Organic, USA) to quantitatively determine the carbon content in the synthesized nanocomposites. The concentration of Victoria Blue B dye in the solution before and after adsorption was determined using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800) at the maximum absorption wavelength of 599 nm.

Results and discussion

The formation of NiO/MgCr2O4/C nanoproducts through the Pechini sol–gel route involves several successive chemical transformations. Initially, nickel nitrate, magnesium nitrate, and chromium nitrate dissolve in water to provide Ni2+, Mg2+, and Cr3+ ions. Tartaric acid then acts as a chelating agent, coordinating these metal cations through its carboxylate and hydroxyl groups and forming metal–tartrate complexes [57]. This chelation step is important because it prevents premature precipitation of individual metal species and promotes homogeneous distribution of the metal ions in the precursor solution. After the addition of polyethylene glycol 400 and heating at 200 °C, esterification and polyesterification reactions occur between tartaric acid and the polyhydroxy alcohol, producing a crosslinked polymeric resin in which Ni, Mg, and Cr ions are immobilized at the molecular level [58]. During calcination, the nitrate groups and organic polymeric network undergo thermal decomposition, releasing volatile gaseous products and generating oxide nuclei. Nickel species are converted into NiO domains, while magnesium and chromium species react to form the MgCr2O4 spinel phase. At the same time, incomplete decomposition of the Pechini organic matrix leaves a residual carbonaceous fraction, especially at the lower calcination temperature [57, 59, 60]. Therefore, the final product is formed as an in-situ integrated NiO/MgCr2O4/C nanocomposite rather than as a simple physical mixture of separately prepared phases.

Structural and morphological features

Figure 2 displays the crystalline phase patterns revealed by XRD for the NMC600 (Fig. 2A) and NMC800 (Fig. 2B) nanocomposites. The diffraction reflections observed at 2θ values of 37.36, 43.34, 62.85, 75.33, and 79.43 correspond to the (110), (-111), (020), (021), and (220) planes of nickel oxide (NiO), which is confirmed by the cubic phase as per JCPDS card number 00-047-1049. In addition, the peaks centered at 2θ values of 18.46, 30.42, 35.77, 47.53, 53.94, 57.49, and 66.32 are indexed to the (111), (220), (311), (331), (422), (511), and (531) planes of magnesium chromate (MgCr2O4), which crystallizes in the cubic system in accordance with JCPDS card number 01-075-3298.

In addition to phase identification, XRD-based relative phase-fraction estimation was performed to estimate the relative fractions of the crystalline oxide phases. In this approach, the relative amounts of NiO and MgCr2O4 were estimated from the XRD patterns by comparing the integrated areas of all distinguishable diffraction peaks assigned to each phase. The areas of all NiO-related peaks were summed, and the areas of all MgCr2O4-related peaks were also summed. The percentage contribution of each crystalline phase was then calculated relative to the total integrated area of all identified crystalline peaks in the sample. The results indicated that NMC600 consists of approximately 55% NiO and 45% MgCr2O4, whereas NMC800 consists of approximately 75% NiO and 25% MgCr2O4. This change suggests that increasing the calcination temperature from 600 to 800 °C promoted a higher relative contribution of the NiO crystalline phase. It should be noted that these values represent semi-quantitative estimates of the crystalline NiO and MgCr2O4 phases only. The carbonaceous component was not included in the XRD phase-ratio calculation because it exists in an amorphous form and therefore does not produce distinct crystalline diffraction peaks that can be reliably quantified by conventional XRD analysis. To complement the XRD analysis and quantitatively verify the amorphous carbon fraction, CHN elemental analysis was performed, revealing carbon contents of 3.6% and 2.8% for NMC600 and NMC800, respectively. These results confirm that NMC600 retained a higher amount of amorphous carbon than NMC800, consistent with the lower calcination temperature and the incomplete decomposition of the organic residues derived from the Pechini sol-gel precursors.

The XRD results obtained in the present study are in good agreement with our previously reported works [57, 61], where a similar increase in crystallite size and peak sharpening was observed with elevating calcination temperature, confirming the enhanced crystallinity and grain growth behavior. This consistent trend across different nanocomposite systems verifies the reliability of the applied synthesis route and supports the structural evolution evidenced in the current samples.

Figure 3 presents the energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) patterns of the NMC600 (Fig. 3A) and NMC800 (Fig. 3B) nanocomposites, while the corresponding atomic compositions are detailed in Table 2. The EDS analysis confirms the successful incorporation of carbon, oxygen, magnesium, chromium, and nickel in both nanoproducts. The measured carbon content in the NMC600 sample is 10.0%, which is notably higher than the 7.2% observed in the NMC800 sample. This reduction in carbon content at elevated calcination temperatures is associated with the exhaustive decomposition and volatilization of carbon-based precursors such as tartaric acid as well as polyethylene glycol 400 utilized during the Pechini sol-gel synthesis process. These organic compounds serve as crosslinkers and complexing agents, and their thermal degradation is more extensive at 800 °C compared to 600 °C, resulting in lower residual carbon. Moreover, the oxygen content increased from 39.6% in NMC600 to 48.7% in NMC800, likely due to improved oxidation of the metal precursors and more complete formation of the metal oxide phases at higher temperatures. The atomic percentage of magnesium increased from 15.3% to 18.9% with increasing calcination temperature, possibly due to enhanced crystallization of magnesium-containing phases. In contrast, chromium and nickel percentages decreased in NMC800 compared to NMC600, with chromium dropping from 16.5% to 12.9% and nickel from 18.6% to 12.3%. These variations in atomic percentages may result from differences in the thermal behavior and diffusion rates of the individual metal species during the high-temperature treatment, as well as potential surface segregation effects. Overall, the elemental distribution confirms that temperature significantly influences the chemical composition and purity of the synthesized nanocomposites. These variations in atomic percentages are interpreted as possible indications of differences in thermal stability and the redistribution of species, while acknowledging the inherent limitations of EDS as a semi-quantitative technique.

The elemental mapping images presented in Fig. 4 confirm the successful distribution of the constituent elements within the synthesized nanoproducts. Both the NMC600 (Fig. 4A) and NMC800 (Fig. 4B) samples exhibit a uniform spatial dispersion of elements throughout the examined regions, indicating effective incorporation during the preparation process. These observations demonstrate that the adopted synthesis route enables the formation of chemically stable and well-integrated nanostructures suitable for further functional applications.

Figure 5 demonstrates the FE-SEM images of the NMC600 and NMC800 products, where the surface morphologies as well as particle arrangements of both nanoproducts are clearly observed. The NMC600 sample, shown in Fig. 5A, exhibits densely packed nanoparticles with irregular polyhedral shapes that are uniformly distributed across the surface. In contrast, the NMC800 sample, presented in Fig. 5B, reveals larger and more coalesced agglomerates composed predominantly of spherical and semi-spherical grains. The average grain size was assessed to be 53.44 nm for NMC600 and 111.29 nm for NMC800. This noticeable increase in grain size with higher calcination temperature may result from the intensified thermal energy at 800 °C, which stimulates particle maturation through mechanisms such as grain boundary diffusion as well as coalescence of smaller crystalline units into larger structures. Removal of Victoria Blue B dye from aqueous media

Effect of pH

As shown in Fig. 9A, the % R of Victoria Blue B dye increased significantly with rising pH for both NMC600 and NMC800 nanocomposites, with the most pronounced differences observed at pH 2 and pH 10 due to electrostatic repulsion and electrostatic attraction, respectively. The adsorption behavior at different pH values in the current study is in strong agreement with previously reported findings, where the removal efficiency was significantly enhanced under alkaline conditions above the pHPZC due to the development of a negatively charged surface that favors electrostatic attraction with cationic dye species [57, 61].

Figure 9B demonstrates that the adsorption capacity of both materials increases progressively with the rise in solution pH as the surface charge becomes more favorable for the uptake of the cationic Victoria Blue B molecules. At highly acidic conditions, the capacities are very limited, reaching only 9.84 mg/g for NMC600 and 6.48 mg/g for NMC800 at pH 2 due to strong electrostatic repulsion and competition with excess protons. The highest capacities are achieved at pH 10, where NMC600 reaches 365.24 mg/g and NMC800 attains 190.65 mg/g, reflecting the dominant electrostatic attraction between the anionic adsorbent surface and cationic dye molecules.

Although electrostatic attraction becomes favorable at pH 10, adsorption is not expected to be quantitative at the applied loading (300 mg/L) because the process is governed by a finite number of accessible surface sites and an adsorption equilibrium. At this high concentration, site saturation and diffusion/aggregation constraints limit full utilization of internal mesoporous domains, leaving a non-zero residual dye concentration even under favorable surface charge conditions.

The incomplete adsorption at pH 10 can therefore be attributed to factors beyond electrostatic attraction. Victoria Blue B is a relatively bulky cationic dye with an extended aromatic structure, and steric hindrance may prevent dense molecular packing on the adsorbent surface or restrict access to some adsorption sites. In addition, although NMC600 and NMC800 possess mesoporous structures, diffusion of large dye molecules into internal or partially blocked pores can be limited, especially at high initial dye concentration. Dye–dye interactions may also contribute to aggregation or stacking of Victoria Blue B molecules in solution, decreasing the fraction of freely diffusible monomeric dye species and slowing their migration toward the internal adsorption domains. Thus, even under strongly favorable electrostatic conditions, the overall adsorption efficiency is governed by the combined effects of electrostatic attraction, finite active-site availability, steric accessibility, pore diffusion resistance, and possible dye aggregation.

From an application viewpoint, it should be noted that pH 10 represents the optimized condition for maximizing Victoria Blue B adsorption rather than a strict requirement for all treatment scenarios. The enhanced performance under alkaline conditions is attributed to surface deprotonation above pHPZC, which promotes electrostatic attraction between the negatively charged adsorbent surface and the cationic dye molecules. Nevertheless, industrial wastewater streams may be near neutral pH, and therefore pH adjustment would represent an additional operational step during large-scale implementation. Such adjustment can be integrated into conventional wastewater treatment processes, especially for dye-rich effluents where pH correction is commonly applied before discharge or advanced treatment. Therefore, the practical use of NMC600 and NMC800 should be considered most suitable for alkaline dye-containing effluents or for treatment systems where moderate pH adjustment is technically and economically acceptable.

Mechanism of dye adsorption

The pHPZC, which was obtained using batch method, of the NMC600 and NMC800 samples is 6.59 and 7.19, respectively, as shown in Fig. 10A. Also, the pHPZC, which was obtained using zeta potential method, of the NMC600 and NMC800 samples is 6.66 and 7.17, respectively, as shown in Fig. 10B. At pH 2, both samples exhibited minimal % R, where NMC600 reached only 2.19% and NMC800 showed a slightly lower value of 1.44%, which can be attributed to the electrostatic repulsion occurring under acidic conditions as illustrated in Fig. 11 when pH is lower than the point of zero charge. This condition leads to surface protonation of both nanocomposites, resulting in charge-based repulsive interactions between the protonated surface sites and Victoria Blue B cations. Conversely, at pH 10, where the pH exceeds the pHPZC values of both NMC600 and NMC800, surface deprotonation occurs and the surfaces become enriched with negative charges, thereby promoting robust electrostatic interaction with the cationic Victoria Blue B dye, as depicted in Fig. 11. This shift in surface charge leads to a substantial improvement in dye adsorption, as supported by the data and the morphological insights presented in Fig. 9, where NMC600 achieved a markedly higher % R of 81.16% compared to only 42.37% for NMC800, indicating that NMC600 exhibits superior adsorption performance under basic conditions due to its more favorable surface characteristics and enhanced affinity toward the dye molecules.

The slight differences between the pHPZC values obtained from the batch and zeta potential methods can be attributed to the different principles of the two approaches. The batch pH-drift method reflects the overall acid–base equilibrium of surface functional groups through the final change in solution pH, whereas the zeta potential method measures the electrokinetic potential near the particle slipping plane and is therefore more sensitive to particle dispersion, ionic atmosphere, and electrical double-layer effects. The very close pHPZC values obtained by the two methods confirm the reliability of the surface-charge assessment. For interpreting Victoria Blue B adsorption, the zeta potential method is considered more directly representative of the surface charge experienced by dye molecules in suspension; however, the batch method provides useful complementary equilibrium confirmation. Therefore, both methods consistently support the conclusion that at pH 10, which is higher than the pHPZC of both adsorbents, the NMC600 and NMC800 surfaces become negatively charged and favor electrostatic attractions with cationic Victoria Blue B molecules.

To clarify the adsorption mechanism, the NMC600 sample was selected as a representative example. Figure 12A shows the FTIR spectrum of NMC600 before Victoria Blue B adsorption and reflects the original surface functional groups and lattice vibrations of the adsorbent. The band at 405 cm⁻¹ is assigned to general metal–oxygen lattice vibrations within the mixed oxide framework, including Ni–O, Mg–O, and Cr–O bonds. The band at 467 cm⁻¹ is attributed mainly to Ni–O stretching vibrations, confirming the presence of Ni-containing active sites on the NMC600 surface. The band at 626 cm⁻¹ corresponds to Cr–O stretching vibrations in the MgCr2O4 spinel phase, confirming the contribution of the chromite structure to the adsorbent framework. The bands at 757 and 902 cm⁻¹ are related to aromatic C–H out-of-plane bending vibrations, which may originate from residual carbonaceous domains formed during the Pechini sol–gel synthesis. The band at 1447 cm⁻¹ is assigned to CH2/CH3 bending vibrations, indicating the presence of residual organic or carbon-containing groups on the surface. The band at 1647 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the H–O–H bending vibration of adsorbed water molecules and/or hydroxyl-associated surface water, confirming the presence of hydrated surface sites that can participate in hydrogen bonding. Figure 12B shows the FTIR spectrum of NMC600 after Victoria Blue B adsorption and confirms the successful attachment of dye molecules onto the adsorbent surface. The retained bands at 405 and 626 cm⁻¹ indicate that the mixed oxide framework remained stable after adsorption. However, the Ni–O band shifted from 467 to 474 cm⁻¹, demonstrating the direct involvement of Ni-containing surface sites in dye binding, most probably through surface complexation with the nitrogen atoms present in Victoria Blue B. The aromatic C–H bands shifted from 757 to 771 cm⁻¹ and from 902 to 882 cm⁻¹, indicating disturbance of the aromatic environment after adsorption and confirming π–π interactions between the aromatic rings of Victoria Blue B and the carbonaceous domains of NMC600. The new bands at 1047, 1151, 1240, and 1337 cm⁻¹ are assigned to C–N stretching vibrations of Victoria Blue B, proving the immobilization of the nitrogen-containing dye molecules on the adsorbent surface. The band at 1406 cm⁻¹, together with the shifted CH2/CH3 bending band from 1447 to 1441 cm⁻¹, confirms the contribution of the ethyl and diethylamino groups of Victoria Blue B after adsorption. The aromatic ring vibration bands at 1482 and 1600 cm⁻¹ further verify the presence of the aromatic dye framework and support π–π interaction as an important adsorption pathway. The shift of the O–H/H–O–H-associated band from 1647 to 1661 cm⁻¹ indicates a change in the hydrogen-bonding environment after dye uptake. In addition, the weak bands at 1985 and 2192 cm⁻¹ are attributed to overtone or combination modes of the conjugated aromatic system of Victoria Blue B, further confirming the involvement of the dye’s π-conjugated structure [62]. The band at 2930 cm⁻¹ is assigned to aliphatic C–H stretching vibrations of the ethyl substituents in Victoria Blue B. The broad band at 3400 cm⁻¹ corresponds to hydrogen-bonded O–H stretching vibrations, while the bands at 3711 and 3779 cm⁻¹ are assigned to isolated or free surface –OH stretching vibrations. These O–H-related bands confirm the participation of surface hydroxyl groups in hydrogen bonding with the nitrogen-containing groups of Victoria Blue B [47, 57]. Therefore, the complete FTIR band assignment before and after adsorption confirms that Victoria Blue B removal by NMC600 proceeds through a cooperative multistep mechanism involving electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, surface complexation, and π–π interaction, as shown in Fig. 11.

It should be emphasized that the pseudo-first-order model describes the overall adsorption rate behavior and should not be considered, by itself, as conclusive evidence for a purely physical adsorption mechanism. In the present system, the excellent pseudo-first-order fitting indicates that the macroscopic uptake rate is mainly governed by rapid occupation of accessible surface sites and low-energy adsorption contributions. However, this kinetic behavior can coexist with localized specific interactions between Victoria Blue B molecules and active surface sites of nanocomposites. Also, the low negative ΔHo values indicate that weak-to-moderate interactions dominate the overall energetic profile of adsorption, but they do not exclude the contribution of localized surface interactions. Consequently, the term “predominantly physical adsorption” is used here to describe the dominant thermodynamic character of the process rather than to imply the complete absence of specific dye–surface interactions. Therefore, the FTIR changes observed after adsorption should be interpreted as evidence for specific local interactions rather than as a contradiction to the pseudo-first-order kinetic behavior. The shift of the Ni–O band from 467 to 474 cm⁻¹ indicates involvement of surface metal–oxygen sites, while the changes in aromatic C–H and O–H/H–O–H-related bands, together with the newly appeared C–N and aromatic ring vibrations of Victoria Blue B, confirm the participation of electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, π–π interaction, and possible surface complexation. Thus, Victoria Blue B adsorption is better described as a cooperative multistep process in which low-energy physical interactions dominate the overall adsorption behavior, while stronger localized surface interactions stabilize the adsorbed dye molecules.

Although NMC600 and NMC800 exhibit moderate BET surface areas of 56.27 and 42.35 m2/g, respectively, the high adsorption capacities cannot be explained by surface area alone. In the present system, Victoria Blue B uptake is governed by the combined effect of accessible mesoporosity, surface chemistry, and strong dye–surface affinity. NMC600 possesses a higher pore volume of 0.2517 cm3/g compared with 0.1785 cm3/g for NMC800, together with a mesoporous structure that facilitates the diffusion and accommodation of Victoria Blue B molecules within accessible adsorption domains. More importantly, under alkaline conditions, the negatively charged surface of the adsorbent strongly attracts the cationic Victoria Blue B molecules through electrostatic interaction. The FTIR results further confirm that adsorption is not controlled only by geometric surface area, but also by specific interactions, including hydrogen bonding, π–π interaction between the aromatic dye structure and amorphous carbon domains, and possible surface complexation with metal–oxygen sites. Therefore, the high adsorption capacity achieved despite the moderate BET surface area can be attributed to the high effectiveness, accessibility, and chemical affinity of the available active sites rather than to the total BET surface area alone.

Effect of time

Figure 16A clarifies the retention performance of Victoria Blue B dye onto NMC600 as well as NMC800 over time and reveals distinct % R values for each sample. At 10 min, the % R of NMC600 reached 44.04%, while NMC800 achieved only 17.32%, indicating a significantly faster initial adsorption rate for NMC600. As time progressed, NMC600 continued to outperform NMC800, and at their respective equilibrium times of 50 min regarding NMC600 as well as 70 min regarding NMC800, the % R values were 81.04% and 42.19%, respectively. No additional enhancement in dye removal was detected beyond the established equilibrium time due to the full coverage of the available adsorption sites on both nanoproducts. The sustained higher performance of NMC600 as compared with NMC800 across all time points highlights its significant adsorption competence and kinetics, which is considered a result of its physicochemical properties that favor more rapid and extensive dye uptake.

Figure 16B shows a rapid increase in adsorption capacity during the initial stage followed by a gradual approach to equilibrium for both materials, which is typical of a process controlled by fast surface uptake at the beginning and slower intraparticle diffusion at later times. For NMC600, the capacity rises sharply from 198.18 mg/g at 10 min to 329.81 mg/g at 30 min and then increases more slowly, reaching 364.69 mg/g at 50 min before stabilizing near equilibrium at 365.24–365.28 mg/g between 80 and 100 min. A similar trend is observed for NMC800, where the capacity increases from 77.94 mg/g at 10 min to 150.35 mg/g at 30 min and continues to rise gradually to 189.84 mg/g at 70 min, followed by a near-constant value of about 189.92–190.08 mg/g at 80–100 min. The consistently higher capacities of NMC600 across the entire contact time confirm its greater affinity and higher density of accessible active sites compared with NMC800.

The contact-time profile can be interpreted as a multi-stage adsorption process. The rapid uptake observed during the initial period is attributed to the abundance of vacant active sites and the strong concentration-gradient-driven transfer of Victoria Blue B molecules from the solution to the adsorbent surface. As contact time increases, the adsorption rate gradually decreases because the easily accessible sites become progressively occupied and dye diffusion toward less accessible interparticle/mesoporous regions becomes slower. The equilibrium attained at 50 min for NMC600 and 70 min for NMC800 indicates that NMC600 provides faster mass transfer and more accessible adsorption domains, consistent with its higher surface area, larger pore volume, and smaller particle size. Similar contact-time-dependent adsorption behavior has been reported for porous carbon-based and hydrochar adsorbents, where rapid initial uptake is followed by a slower approach to equilibrium due to site saturation and diffusion limitations [57, 61, 63, 64].

Effect of temperature

As shown in Fig. 20A, the % R of Victoria Blue B dye decreases progressively with increasing temperature from 298 K to 328 K, indicating the exothermic nature of the decontamination process. At 298 K, NMC600 exhibits a high % R of 81.04%, while NMC800 shows a significantly lower value of 42.19%. This trend continues as the temperature rises, where at 308 K the values decline to 79.34% for NMC600 and 40.49% for NMC800. Further increases in temperature to 318 K result in reduced % R values of 76.72% for NMC600 and 38.07% for NMC800. At the highest examined temperature of 328 K, NMC600 still maintains superior adsorption with a % R of 73.31% in contrast to 34.81% for NMC800. These results confirm that NMC600 consistently outperforms NMC800 across the entire temperature range, which may be ascribed to its improved surface characteristics as well as stronger association with dye entities.

Figure 20B indicates that the uptake capacity of both materials decreases gradually with increasing temperature, which reflects the exothermic nature of the interaction between Victoria Blue B molecules and the adsorbent surface. For NMC600, the capacity declines from 364.69 mg/g at 298 K to 357.03 mg/g at 308 K and further to 345.24 mg/g at 318 K, reaching 329.89 mg/g at 328 K. A similar behavior is observed for NMC800, where the capacity decreases from 189.84 mg/g at 298 K to 182.19 mg/g at 308 K, followed by 171.30 mg/g at 318 K and 156.63 mg/g at 328 K. The consistent reduction in uptake with temperature suggests that physical interactions dominate the process, and that higher thermal energy weakens the affinity of the dye for both adsorbents.

This temperature-dependent decline can be explained by the exothermic nature of Victoria Blue B adsorption onto the nanocomposite surface. At higher temperatures, the increased thermal motion of dye molecules may weaken low-energy dye–surface interactions, enhance the tendency of adsorbed molecules to detach from active sites, and reduce the overall stability of the adsorption layer. Therefore, the decrease in both removal percentage and adsorption capacity with increasing temperature indicates that lower temperature is more favorable for Victoria Blue B uptake. The temperature-dependent results obtained in the present work are consistent with previous findings, where the dye elimination efficacy gradually decreased as the temperature increased, demonstrating the exothermic character of the adsorption [57, 61, 70].

Effect of adsorbent amount

Figure 22A displays that increasing the adsorbent dose initially enhances the removal efficiency for both materials because a larger mass provides more accessible active sites and surface area for dye uptake, and the improvement is consistently more pronounced for NMC600 than for NMC800. At 0.025 g, the removal is 23.80% for NMC600 and 14.56% for NMC800, and raising the dose to 0.1 g increases the removal to 81.04% and 42.19%, respectively, which indicates a strong gain in available sites at moderate loading. When the dose is further increased to 0.4 g, the removal decreases to 73.82% for NMC600 and 37.46% for NMC800, which stems from particle aggregation as well as site overlaps at higher solid loadings that reduce the effective surface utilization and lower the apparent removal per added mass.

Figure 22B shows that the adsorption capacity increases strongly as the adsorbent dose rises from a very low level to an intermediate level because more accessible active sites become available while the concentration driving force remains sufficient to utilize them effectively. At 0.025 g, the capacities are 107.12 mg/g for NMC600 and 65.51 mg/g for NMC800, and increasing the dose to 0.1 g raises the capacities to 364.69 mg/g and 189.84 mg/g, respectively, confirming the consistently higher uptake ability of NMC600. When the dose is further increased to 0.4 g, the capacity declines to 332.19 mg/g concerning NMC600 and 168.59 mg/g regarding NMC800, which stems from particle aggregation as well as site overlap at higher solid loadings that reduce effective surface utilization and leave more adsorption sites underused.

The dosage-dependent behavior also reflects the balance between the total number of adsorption sites and their effective utilization [71]. At very low adsorbent dosage, the available surface sites are insufficient for the fixed dye loading, resulting in limited Victoria Blue B removal. Increasing the dose to 0.1 g improves both removal efficiency and adsorption capacity because more active sites and mesoporous domains become accessible while the dye concentration remains high enough to effectively occupy these sites. However, at excessive dosage, the added particles may aggregate or overlap, causing partial shielding of active sites and reducing the effective exposed surface area. Consequently, a fraction of the available sites remains underutilized, and the apparent uptake per unit mass decreases. Therefore, 0.1 g can be considered the most efficient dosage under the applied conditions because it provides the best compromise between site availability and active-site utilization efficiency.

Effect of concentration

As illustrated in Fig. 23A, the % R of Victoria Blue B dye decreases as the initial dye concentration increases from 100 to 400 mg/L for both NMC600 and NMC800 nanocomposites. At 100 mg/L concentration, NMC600 exhibits nearly complete dye removal with a % R of 99.05%, while NMC800 achieves a slightly lower value of 94.76%. However, at 400 mg/L, the % R significantly declines to 61.39% for NMC600 and 32.16% for NMC800. This behavior reflects the full occupation of available binding domains on the adsorbent surface at higher concentrations, which constrains the maximum adsorption capability. NMC600 consistently demonstrates superior performance, indicating a greater number of effective adsorption centers and stronger association with dye molecules compared to NMC800.

Figure 23B demonstrates that the adsorption capacity increases with rising initial Victoria Blue B concentration because the higher concentration creates a stronger mass transfer driving force toward the adsorbent surface. For NMC600, the capacity grows from 148.58 mg/g with a preliminary concentration of 100 mg/L to 364.69 mg/g at 300 mg/L and then approaches saturation, reaching 368.31 mg/g upon increasing the concentration to 400 mg/L. NMC800 exhibits a similar trend, rising from 142.14 mg/g with 100 mg/L to 189.84 mg/g at 300 mg/L, followed by a near plateau of 192.95 mg/g at 400 mg/L, confirming the lower ultimate uptake of NMC800 compared with NMC600.

The concentration-dependent behavior can be explained by the competition between the increasing mass-transfer driving force and the finite number of adsorption sites. At low initial dye concentration, the available active sites are sufficient relative to the number of Victoria Blue B molecules, resulting in high removal efficiency. As the initial concentration increases, more dye molecules compete for the same number of adsorption domains, causing partial site saturation and a decrease in removal percentage. Meanwhile, the adsorption capacity increases with concentration because the higher concentration gradient enhances the transfer of dye molecules from the solution to the adsorbent surface until the available sites approach saturation. The near-plateau in capacity at higher concentrations indicates that the accessible adsorption domains become almost fully occupied [71].

The influence of initial dye concentration observed in the current study agrees with previous reports in which the removal efficiency decreased progressively with increasing concentration due to the limited number of available active adsorption sites on the adsorbent surface [57, 61].

Desorption efficiency and recyclability assessment

As shown in Fig. 27, the % D of Victoria Blue B dye from NMC600 and NMC800 nanohybrids increases with the concentration of HCl, signifying that acidic conditions enhance the release of dye particles from the adsorbent surface. At 1.0 M HCl, the % D reaches 82.38% for NMC600 and 84.58% for NMC800, demonstrating effective initial desorption. As the concentration increases to 1.5 M, the % D improves further to 92.73% for NMC600 and 93.94% for NMC800. Upon exposure to 2.0 M HCl, both nanoproducts achieve substantially full recovery, with % D values of 99.79% for NMC600 and 99.86% for NMC800. The excellent % D in the presence of HCl can be attributed to the high concentration of H⁺ ions, which compete effectively with dye molecules for adsorption sites, thereby weakening the interaction between dye particles and the surface of the adsorbent and promoting efficient dye release. These results approve that HCl is a highly successful desorbing agent and support the potential reusability of both NMC600 and NMC800 in multiple adsorption/desorption cycles.

The desorption experiments reported in previous studies confirmed that the dye-loaded adsorbents could be efficiently regenerated using hydrochloric acid as an eluting agent, demonstrating the reversible nature of the adsorption process and the stability of the active surface sites [57, 61].

To further confirm the chemical and structural stability of the adsorbent after regeneration, NMC600 was selected as an illustrative example and regenerated using 2 M HCl. The filtrate collected after the regeneration process was analyzed by inductively coupled plasma, and none of the main constituent metal elements of NMC600, including Ni, Mg, and Cr, were detected, confirming that the material did not undergo measurable metal leaching under the acidic regeneration conditions. Moreover, the XRD pattern of regenerated NMC600 shown in Fig. 28 retains the characteristic diffraction features of the NiO/MgCr2O4 crystalline framework and shows no additional impurity reflections. When compared with the original crystalline patterns presented in Fig. 2B, the preserved diffraction profile confirms that the regeneration process did not destroy the oxide framework or alter the principal crystalline structure. These findings verify the high chemical durability and structural robustness of NMC600 during regeneration with 2 M HCl.

As illustrated in Fig. 29, % R of Victoria Blue B dye gradually decreases with increasing cycle number for both NMC600 and NMC800 nanocomposites, revealing a modest reduction in uptake efficiency under repeated usage conditions. In the first cycle, NMC600 achieves a % R of 81.04%, while NMC800 records a lower value of 42.19%. After five successive cycles, NMC600 maintains a relatively high % R of 75.16%, whereas NMC800 drops to 34.77%. This trend demonstrates that both materials remain reusable, but NMC600 retains a significantly higher dye removal efficiency throughout all cycles, suggesting greater structural stability, surface functionality, and regeneration potential compared to NMC800. These results confirm the suitability of NMC600 for repeated retention–elution processes. The slight decline in removal efficiency after successive cycles can be attributed to possible surface fouling by residual dye molecules and partial deactivation of active adsorption sites, as well as minor pore blockage that may limit accessibility to internal mesoporous domains.

The selectivity of NMC600 toward Victoria Blue B in the presence of competing species can be interpreted by considering ionic strength, electrostatic screening, and competitive adsorption. The limited reduction caused by Na⁺ and K⁺ indicates that monovalent ions mainly screen the electrostatic attraction between the negatively charged adsorbent surface and cationic Victoria Blue B molecules without strongly occupying the adsorption domains. In contrast, Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ caused greater decreases in adsorption capacity because divalent cations possess higher charge density and can more effectively compete for negatively charged surface sites or compress the electrical double layer. The weak influence of Cl⁻, NO₃⁻, and SO₄²⁻ suggests that these anions do not directly compete with cationic Victoria Blue B for the main adsorption sites. The much stronger inhibition observed in the presence of crystal violet confirms that competitive adsorption is more significant when the interferent has a similar cationic and aromatic structure, allowing it to compete for electrostatic attraction sites and π-related interaction domains. Conversely, acid blue 25 showed minimal interference because its anionic nature makes it less competitive with Victoria Blue B under alkaline conditions. Therefore, NMC600 exhibits clear preferential adsorption toward Victoria Blue B in complex matrices, although its performance can be partially reduced by ionic strength effects and structurally similar cationic dyes.

Real wastewater treatment

To further assess the practical applicability of the fabricated nanohybrids, separation tests were performed using real wastewater effluent collected from students’ chemistry laboratories. The effluent was spiked with Victoria Blue B to obtain an initial dye concentration of 300 mg/L, thereby matching the conditions of the single-solute tests while maintaining the original composition of the wastewater matrix. Chemical analysis of the spiked sample confirmed the presence of common inorganic ions associated with routine laboratory activities, including K⁺ (12.7 mg/L), Na⁺ (58.4 mg/L), Ca²⁺ (24.3 mg/L), Mg²⁺ (7.9 mg/L), Cl⁻ (71.5 mg/L), NO₃⁻ (21.6 mg/L), SO₄²⁻ (46.2 mg/L), HCO₃⁻ (118.0 mg/L), and PO₄³⁻ (4.6 mg/L), along with trace levels of Cu (0.06 mg/L), Fe (0.23 mg/L), and Zn (0.11 mg/L), while no organic dyes other than the added Victoria Blue B were detected within the analytical limits. In addition to its ionic composition, the wastewater matrix was characterized by an approximate chemical oxygen demand (COD) of 192 mg/L, total organic carbon (TOC) content of 39 mg/L, and electrical conductivity of 910 µS/cm, indicating the presence of moderate organic loading and dissolved ionic species. Under the previously optimized operating conditions, the adsorption capacity reached 350.34 mg/g for NMC600 and 166.52 mg/g for NMC800, which are considerably lower than the corresponding capacities measured in distilled water at an identical initial concentration (371.74 mg/g for NMC600 and 194.55 mg/g for NMC800). This reduction is reasonably attributed to matrix effects such as ionic competition and partial screening or blockage of adsorption locations by dissolved constituents in the actual effluent. However, both nanoproducts retained strong performance, and NMC600 consistently outperformed NMC800, supporting their suitability for treating student laboratory wastewater polluted with Victoria Blue B.

Cost analysis

A preliminary cost consideration was carried out to provide an initial indication of the economic feasibility of the Pechini sol–gel route. The calculation was based on the actual precursor quantities used in one synthesis batch, including 20 g magnesium nitrate hexahydrate, 20 g chromium nitrate nonahydrate, 20 g nickel nitrate hexahydrate, 35 g tartaric acid, and 15 mL polyethylene glycol 400. After complete evaporation, the obtained precursor residue was divided equally into two portions and calcined separately at 600 and 800 °C, yielding 6.5 g of NMC600 and 5.0 g of NMC800, respectively. Based on representative laboratory/commercial reagent prices, the total raw-material cost of the precursor batch was estimated to be approximately 6.98 USD, corresponding to about 3.49 USD for each calcination portion. Therefore, the estimated raw-material costs were approximately 0.54 USD/g for NMC600 and 0.70 USD/g for NMC800. This estimate represents only the raw-material cost and does not include energy consumption, labor, equipment depreciation, washing/drying, or waste-management expenses. From a scale-up perspective, NMC600 is more attractive than NMC800 because it is produced at a lower calcination temperature, provides a higher yield, and exhibits markedly higher adsorption capacity, resulting in a better performance-to-cost balance. To further relate the preliminary cost estimation to adsorption performance, the raw-material cost was normalized to the maximum Victoria Blue B uptake capacity. Based on the estimated raw-material costs of 0.54 USD/g for NMC600 and 0.70 USD/g for NMC800, together with the Langmuir maximum adsorption capacities of 371.74 and 194.55 mg/g, respectively, the estimated raw-material cost per gram of dye adsorption capacity was approximately 1.45 USD/g dye for NMC600 and 3.60 USD/g dye for NMC800. This comparison clearly demonstrates that NMC600 provides a more favorable performance-to-cost balance, since it combines lower calcination temperature, higher product yield, lower estimated cost per gram of adsorbent, and substantially higher Victoria Blue B uptake capacity. Therefore, NMC600 represents the more economically promising candidate for future scale-up and practical wastewater treatment applications.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrated the successful fabrication of a novel nickel oxide/magnesium chromate/carbon nanocomposite through the Pechini sol–gel approach and its effective application for the disposal of Victoria Blue B dye from aqueous media. Structural and morphological analyses confirmed that the calcination temperature strongly governed the physicochemical features of the materials. The sample prepared at 600 °C (NMC600) exhibited a more favorable architecture characterized by higher surface area, richer pore structure, smaller crystallite size, and increased carbon content compared with the sample calcined at 800 °C (NMC800). These characteristics directly contributed to its superior adsorption performance. Adsorption investigations revealed that NMC600 achieved an outstanding maximum capacity of 371.74 mg/g, which markedly exceeded that of NMC800 (194.55 mg/g) and most previously reported adsorbents. The reliability of the kinetic interpretation was supported by the excellent agreement between the experimental equilibrium capacities (Qe, exp) and the values predicted by the pseudo-first-order model (Qe, cal), together with the extremely high correlation coefficients (R2 = 0.9999). This consistency confirms that the model adequately describes the overall rate behavior under the investigated conditions. The uptake process obeyed the Langmuir isotherm and pseudo-first-order kinetics, indicating a dominant monolayer physisorption mechanism governed by surface interactions. Thermodynamic analysis confirmed that the process was spontaneous and exothermic in nature. The regeneration study proved the excellent stability of the developed composite, where nearly complete desorption was achieved using 2 M HCl and more than three-quarters of the initial efficiency was retained after five consecutive cycles. Application to real wastewater further verified the practical reliability of the material under complex matrices with only minor performance reduction. Overall, the results establish NMC600 as a competitive, recyclable, and scalable adsorbent for the treatment of dye-contaminated effluents. Future efforts should be directed toward evaluating the material in continuous flow systems and exploring surface modification strategies to enhance selectivity toward mixed organic pollutants.
