Fourier-transform infrared fingerprinting with chemometrics characterizes the anti-inflammatory activity of Rhizophora mucronata leaf fractions and profiles the active-fraction metabolites using liquid chromatography–high-resolution mass spectrometry analysis
Authors: Lina Permatasari, Annisa Rizka Nirmala, Handa Muliasari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe extract of Rhizophora mucronata leaves has demonstrated potential anti-inflammatory activity. Its fractions may exhibit enhanced activity due to the presence of more specific metabolites. This research was performed to determine the anti-inflammatory activity and characterize the R. mucronata leaf fractions using Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy integrated with chemometric analysis. This was an experimental in vitro study evaluating the anti-inflammatory activity of R. mucronata leaf fractions and characterizing their chemical profiles using FTIR and liquid chromatography–high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS). The ethanol extract was fractionated using vacuum liquid chromatography. The anti-inflammatory activity was evaluated by measuring the inhibition of egg albumin denaturation in the sample. The fractions were characterized using FTIR spectroscopy combined with chemometrics. The secondary metabolite profile of the fraction with the highest anti-inflammatory activity was examined using LC-HRMS. The FTIR profile of R. mucronata leaves was analyzed using principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical cluster analysis. Fraction N has the highest percentage of egg albumin denaturation inhibition activity. Naringin and ferulic acid were the most abundant flavonoid and phenolic compounds detected in fraction N. FTIR spectra indicated variations in chemical composition among fractions. In the PCA biplot, fraction N was closely aligned with the variable at 1376 cm−1, indicating strong C-H bending of methyl groups. Fraction N demonstrated the ability to inhibit egg albumin denaturation. Nevertheless, further investigation is necessary to confirm these results.

INTRODUCTION

Rhizophora mucronata was the dominant mangrove species found on Lombok Island.[12] The extracts of R. mucronata leaves revealed the anti-inflammatory activity.[3] However, this biological activity from R. mucronata leaf fractions obtained through vacuum liquid chromatography (VLC), as assessed using the egg albumin denaturation inhibition method, has not been previously reported. The separation of compounds using VLC based on polarity results in more specific components in each fraction.[4] Furthermore, compounds that contributed to anti-inflammatory activity were expected to accumulate in a specific fraction. The compounds present in R. mucronata leaves may play a role in their anti-inflammatory activity.[5]

Functional groups in plant secondary metabolites may be involved in anti-inflammatory activity and can be identified using Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrophotometry.[6] This research aimed to examine the anti-inflammatory activities of R. mucronata leaf fractions obtained via VLC using the egg albumin denaturation inhibition method. The metabolite profiles of the active fractions were identified using liquid chromatography–high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS). Each fraction was characterized by FTIR and analyzed by principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Material

Ethanol 96%, methanol, ethyl acetate, chloroform, and n-hexane with technical grade were used. Silica gel 60 PF254 for preparative thin-layer chromatography was obtained from Merck (Germany). Methanol, acetonitrile, and water with grade pro-liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Fair Lawn, NJ, USA). Fresh chicken eggs were collected from a local farm in East Lombok, Indonesia. The egg albumin was obtained from the egg white of the hen. Diclofenac sodium was purchased from Total Equipment Pharmacy (Indonesia). R. mucronata leaves (7.8 kg) were obtained from Buwun Mas Village, West Sekotong District, West Lombok Regency, West Nusa Tenggara Province, Indonesia (geographical coordinates: 8.850112, 116.065392) in July 2022. Plant samples were collected in compliance with local and national regulations. Permission to collect the samples was obtained from the mangrove tourism caretaker in Buwun Mas Village.

Plant identification was performed at Mataram University, Mataram, Indonesia, using samples of twigs, leaves, and fruit, and was validated by I Gde Mertha (expert in plant taxonomy). A specimen of R. mucronata was deposited at the Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences, Mataram University, Indonesia, with voucher number LP01.RM.

Extraction and fractionation

Dried R. mucronata powder (300 g) was extracted with 1.5 L of 96% ethanol through ultrasonic-assisted extraction at 35°C for 30 min, repeated three times.[7] The collected filtrates were evaporated with a vacuum rotary evaporator at 45°C. The extract was then fractionated by VLC using 300 g of silica gel 60 PF254 as the stationary phase and eluted sequentially with n-hexane, chloroform, ethyl acetate, and methanol. The exact ratios and volumes of all the eluting systems are available in Supplementary Table 1. A total of 87 fractions were obtained.

Thin-layer chromatography of the fraction

All fractions were investigated by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) on silica gel F254 using various mobile-phase compositions of n-hexane, ethyl acetate, chloroform, and methanol and visualized under UV light at 366 nm [Supplementary Figure 1]. Fractions with similar TLC profiles were combined into 17 groups (A-Q), and fractions L-Q were further evaluated for anti-inflammatory activity using the egg albumin protein denaturation assay. The fractionation process is shown in Supplementary Figure 2.

Anti-inflammatory activity assay

Separation of the egg albumin solution follows the modified methods.[8] Ten grams of egg white, separated from the yolk, were agitated with a magnetic stirrer for 1 min. The distilled water (50 mL) was added, and the mixture was stirred again for 5 min. Finally, the mixture was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was obtained and referred to as the egg albumin solution.

The diclofenac sodium, extract, and fractions were diluted with distilled water to obtain a concentration series of 100–500 μg/mL.[9] Each test solution (2 mL), phosphate-buffered saline solution (2.5 mL), and 0.5 mL of albumin solution were mixed and incubated at 37°C ± 2°C for 15 min.[10] The temperature was elevated to 70°C ± 2°C for 5 min and subsequently cooled to room temperature.[11] The absorbance of each test solution was measured using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Analytik Jena Specord 200) at 660 nm. Each measurement was repeated three times. The inhibition percentage was calculated for each concentration using the following calculation formula:

Absorbance of control (Ac); Absorbance of sample (As).

Metabolite profiling using liquid chromatography–high-resolution mass spectrometry

Identification of metabolite profiling of R. mucronata leaf fractions was carried out based on Nugraheni et al.[12] Metabolite screening of R. mucronata leaf extract was performed in negative ionization mode.

Analysis using Fourier-transform infrared

The extracts and fractions of R. mucronata leaves were characterized using FTIR-attenuated total reflectance spectroscopy (Perkin Elmer) to detect their functional groups. The fractions were pressed firmly on a ZnSe crystal (15 force gauge). All fractions and extracts were scanned over 4000–400 cm−1 with 16 scans at a resolution of 4 cm−1.

Statistical analysis

The inhibition percentage values for each extract and the fractions were determined using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because the data were not normally distributed (P = 0.46), nonparametric tests were used. Differences among multiple groups were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons using the Mann–Whitney U-test at a 95% confidence level (P < 0.05). The multivariate analysis was performed using Minitab® Statistical Software, Version 17 (Minitab Inc., State College, PA, USA). FTIR spectrum fractions were characterized using the PCA and HCA methods.

DISCUSSION

The extract yield was >10%, indicating an effective extraction process. A higher yield indicates a more effective and efficient extraction process, suggesting a greater concentration of bioactive compounds.[13] Fraction O was obtained with ethyl acetate–methanol (4:6 and 2.5:7.5), indicating that the sample predominantly contains polar compounds. However, a higher yield does not necessarily correlate with stronger biological activity, as this depends on the specific active constituents present.[14]

Only the fraction L-Q of R. mucronata leaves was examined for their anti-inflammatory activity. Fractions A-K were not evaluated for anti-inflammatory activity due to poor solubility in aqueous media, which may limit the applicability of this method.[15] However, the egg albumin protein denaturation inhibition method effectively explained the anti-inflammatory activity of the samples, as one of the inflammatory responses involved protein denaturation in tissues.[16]

The positive control, diclofenac sodium, had a lower inhibition percentage than the sample. The percentage of inhibition by diclofenac sodium (312.5 μg/mL) was 25%. Meanwhile, other studies found that the percentage inhibition of diclofenac sodium at 500 μg/mL exceeded 90%.[17] The discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the initial incubation temperature and variability in the quality of eggs of hens.[19]

The higher anti-inflammatory activity observed in fraction N may be associated with the existence of flavonoid and phenolic compounds detected in the LC-HRMS analysis.[18] Fractions M, N, P, and O also demonstrated dose-dependent anti-inflammatory activity [Figure 1]. In contrast, fractions Q and L, as well as the extract, did not exhibit dose-dependent inhibition. This nondose-dependent behavior may be caused by the complex mixture of compounds in the sample, which can interact with proteins in different ways and produce a nonlinear inhibition response.[19]

LC-HRMS analysis detected 49 compounds. Naringin was the most abundant flavonoid compound at fraction N. The mass spectrum of naringin in negative-ion mode showed an ion fragment at m/z 459 [Supplementary Figure 3]. Naringin was present in “pain powder,” a traditional Chinese medicine combined with four other ingredients. This combination exhibited the most significant anti-inflammatory effect in an acute inflammation model.[20] The previous study reported inhibition of protein denaturation due to interactions between naringin-containing molecules and protein residues.[21]

Ferulic acid is among the most prevalent phenolic compounds in the fraction N of R. mucronata leaves. Ferulic acid exhibited considerable potential across various applications, including antioxidant and anti-inflammatory. The hydroxyl groups in ferulic acid can bind to amino acid residues in proteins, stabilizing the protein structure and preventing denaturation upon heating.[22]

Identifying active compounds in complex matrices remains an analytical challenge. LC-HRMS is widely used for screening bioactive compounds in plant extracts because it can analyze complex matrices effectively. However, this method still has limitations, including low concentrations of target compounds, limited fragmentation data, and difficulties in interpreting MS2 spectra.[23]

The spectrum FTIR of fraction Q showed the absence of phenolic or alcohol groups. Meanwhile, the vibration of fraction Q at frequencies 2916 cm−1 and 2849 cm−1 was higher than in the other samples. The frequency at 2849 cm−1 was attributed to the C-H aldehyde, which was supported by the vibration of C = O at 1700 cm−1.[6] Fraction P displayed a markedly different metabolite composition compared to the other samples because of the absence of vibration at 2916 cm−1.

The total value of PC1 and PC2 was 82.4%, indicating that these two principal components effectively represented all variables. The biplot showed that fractions P and Q were located far from the PCA loading plot for each wavenumber. The loading plot represents the influence of functional group frequency variables on the development of principal components. The biplot showed that fraction O contributed the most at 3372 cm−1 (O-H group).[24] In addition, fraction N was closely aligned with the variable at 1376 cm−1, indicating a strong association with this variable. It was indicated that the C-H bending of the methyl group in fraction N was high.[25]

Fractions L, M, and N demonstrated the highest level of similarity (85.09%) [Figure 5b]. Fractions L, M, and N have similar secondary metabolite content based on the FTIR spectra, but all of them have significantly different anti-inflammatory activity [Figure 1]. Despite the high spectral similarity among fractions L, M, and N, their differing anti-inflammatory activities suggest that minor constituents or differences in compound concentration may significantly influence bioactivity, highlighting the limitation of FTIR in distinguishing bioactive compounds at the molecular level. In addition, fractions P and Q showed low similarity with other samples (51.41% and 24.49%). Fraction Q exhibited C-H sp3 stretching, while fraction P showed O-H vibration. Their anti-inflammatory activities were also significantly different, suggesting variations in their chemical constituents.

CONCLUSION

Fraction N of R. mucronata leaf exhibited the highest anti-inflammatory activity. Meanwhile, further investigation of its anti-inflammatory activity using additional assays is required. LC-HRMS analysis revealed ferulic acid, and naringin was the most abundant phenolic and flavonoid compound in fraction N. The FTIR spectra, combined with chemometrics, indicated that the C-H functional group makes the greatest contribution to the anti-inflammatory activity of fraction N. The dendogram showed that the FTIR spectra of fraction N had characteristics similar to those of fractions M and L.
