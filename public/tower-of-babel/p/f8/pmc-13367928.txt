Treatment of Pediatric Fingertip Amputations by Chest and Palm Subcutaneous Pocket Techniques
Authors: Koichiro Yanai, Yuta Izawa, Takafumi Suzuki, Kentaro Futamura, Tsuyoshi Tajika, Hirotaka Chikuda, Masahiro Nishida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Although chest and palm subcutaneous pocket techniques are effective for pediatric fingertip amputations, particularly in pediatric patients younger than 2 years of age, few studies exist due to the rarity of these injuries. We report the treatment outcomes at our institution and consider factors associated with graft survival.Methods:We reviewed pediatric patients younger than 2 years of age with fingertip amputations who underwent the chest and palm subcutaneous pocket techniques between 2013 and 2025. Collected data included age, completeness of amputation, Ishikawa classification, donor site and pocketing period, postoperative management after separation (wet or gauze dressing), and graft survival. Graft survival was classified as complete recovery or partial necrosis.Results:Six patients (7 digits) were included in the study. The mean age was 17.4 months. Five digits had complete amputations, and 2 had incomplete amputations. According to the Ishikawa classification, 1 digit was classified as subzone I, and 3 digits each were classified as subzones II and III. Donor sites included 5 digits on the chest and 2 on the palm. The mean pocketing period was 11.7 days. All digits survived; however, partial necrosis occurred in 3 digits (43%). The pocketing period was longer in the complete recovery group (13.5 versus 9.3 d), and the wet dressing method tended to be used more frequently.Conclusions:Chest and palm subcutaneous pocket techniques are useful for pediatric fingertip amputations; however, maintaining an adequate pocketing period and appropriate postoperative wound management may be beneficial for improving graft survival.

Takeaways

Question: What factors influence treatment outcomes for the chest and palm subcutaneous pocket techniques used in pediatric finger amputation?

Findings: Among 7 digits, all survived, but 3 developed partial necrosis. There were differences between the complete recovery group and the partial necrosis group in the duration of the pocketing period and the treatment methods after separation.

Meaning: Appropriate selection of the pocketing period and treatment methods may improve the survival rate.

INTRODUCTION

Replantation for fingertip amputations has become increasingly feasible with advances in microsurgical techniques. However, in pediatric patients, the extremely small vessel diameter continues to make vascular anastomosis technically challenging.1,2

Chest and palm subcutaneous pocket techniques involve embedding the composite-grafted fingertip into a subcutaneous pocket to promote revascularization.3,4 These techniques have been reported to be effective even in children for whom microvascular repair is difficult.3,4

However, because fingertip amputations in pediatric patients younger than 2 years are rare, only a limited number of studies have reported the outcomes of chest and palm subcutaneous pocket techniques in this age group.4 Consequently, factors influencing treatment outcomes, such as the level of amputation, appropriate duration of pocketing, or treatment method after separation, remain insufficiently investigated.

This study reports the clinical outcomes of the chest and palm subcutaneous pocket techniques performed at our institution for pediatric fingertip amputations and considers factors associated with graft survival and appropriate perioperative management.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was approved by the institutional review board of Shonan Kamakura General Hospital (approval no. SKEC-26-10). Written informed consent to participate in this study was obtained from the guardians of all patients.

We retrospectively reviewed cases of fingertip amputation in pediatric patients younger than 2 years of age treated using chest and palm subcutaneous pocket techniques at our institution from 2013 to 2025. Collected variables included age, sex, follow-up period, injured digit, mechanism of injury, completeness of amputation, Ishikawa classification, presence of fracture, pocketing site and pocketing period, postoperative management after separation, complications (infection, joint contracture, etc.), and graft survival.5 The graft outcomes were categorized as complete recovery (CR) or partial necrosis (PN).

The details of the surgical procedure and treatment are described herein. The fractured fragments were stabilized with Kirschner wires, and the amputated segment was reattached to the amputation stump without performing vascular anastomosis (Fig. 1). After reattachment, the skin of the amputated segment was de-epithelialized to the mid-dermal layer using a scalpel (Fig. 1). In the chest subcutaneous pocket technique, the reattached portion was inserted subcutaneously into the anterior chest or contralateral infraclavicular region (Fig. 2). In contrast, in the palm subcutaneous pocket technique, a transverse incision was made in the ipsilateral palm, and the subcutaneous layer was undermined bluntly to create a pocket. The reattached part was inserted into the pocket and sutured to the palmar skin to prevent the inserted digit from withdrawing. For immobilization of the surgical site, the chest subcutaneous pocket technique used tapes, bandages, or bust bands to secure the hands and prevent withdrawal. In the palm subcutaneous pocket technique, the hands were immobilized in a grip position using bandages to prevent withdrawal. In both methods, patients were managed as inpatients during the pocketing period with continuous supervision provided by parents or nursing staff.

Postseparation management was categorized into 2 groups: the gauze dressing method, which involved applying gauze with ointment, and the wet dressing method, in which ointment was applied to the fingertip and covered with a film dressing to maintain a moist environment (Fig. 3). The choice between chest and palm subcutaneous pocket techniques, as well as the pocketing period and postseparation management protocol, was left to the discretion of the primary surgeon. PN was defined as the development of PN of the distal stump after separation that required additional procedures, such as local debridement (Figs. 4, 5).

RESULTS

Six patients with 7 affected digits (including 1 patient with multiple-digit injuries) were included in the study. The results are presented in Table 1. The mean age at injury was 17.4 ± 2.8 months (range, 12–23 mo). All patients were younger than 2 years of age. The cohort consisted of 5 male patients and 1 female patient. The average follow-up period was 24.6 (±23.2) months. The shortest duration was 6 months, and the longest was 60 months (5 y). Five digits had complete amputations, whereas 2 had incomplete amputations. The mechanisms of injury included 1 clean-cut injury and 6 blunt injuries. The clean-cut injury occurred when a finger was caught in a pipe chair. Regarding blunt injuries, there was 1 case each in which a finger was caught in a car window and a drawer, and 3 cases in which a finger was caught in the wheel of a moving bicycle. The injured digits included 1 index finger, 4 middle fingers, 1 ring finger, and 1 small finger. According to the Ishikawa classification, 1 digit was classified as subzone I, 3 as subzone II, and 3 as subzone III. Fractures were present in 5 digits, consisting of 4 distal phalanx fractures and 1 physeal injury. Regarding the pocketing site, 5 digits were pocketed in the chest and 2 in the palm. The mean duration of subcutaneous pocketing was 11.7 ± 2.3 days. Postseparation wound management consisted of the gauze dressing method in 3 digits and the wet dressing method in 4 digits. All digits survived; however, PN occurred in 3 digits (43%). The average pocketing period was 9.3 days in the PN group and 13.5 days in the CR group, with a slightly shorter duration in the PN group. Furthermore, postseparation management consisted of the gauze dressing method in the PN group and the wet dressing method in the CR group, indicating differences in treatment between the groups. Regarding complications, no cases developed infection, limitation of finger range of motion, or restricted range of motion in adjacent joints such as the shoulder or elbow. At the pocketing sites in the chest and palm, no severe scar formation requiring revision surgery was observed. In particular, no scar formation at the palmar site resulted in limitation of finger joint motion.

DISCUSSION

Treatment options for pediatric fingertip amputations include replantation, composite grafting, amputation stump plasty, and wet dressing techniques.6 Replantation offers the best functional and aesthetic outcomes when successful; however, in pediatric patients, the extremely small vessel diameter often makes microsurgical anastomosis technically challenging. Composite grafts have been proposed as an alternative to replantation because of their technical simplicity; however, the survival rate in pediatric cases is not always satisfactory. Moiemen and Elliot7 reported a 19% survival rate for subzone II injuries in pediatric cases treated with a composite graft. Therefore, even in cases of fingertip amputation, this procedure should not be chosen lightly for amputations more central than subzone II of the Ishikawa classification, which involves abundant tissue volume.4,7 The chest subcutaneous pocket technique was originally developed to improve composite graft survival in fingertip amputations where vascular anastomosis is difficult, with graft pocketing in the anterior chest.3 Arata et al4 later reported the palm subcutaneous pocket technique in which the graft is pocketed in the palm to reduce complications, such as joint contracture and accidental pullout, with subsequent reports supporting its effectiveness.

Factors reported to influence outcomes of chest and palm subcutaneous pocket techniques include age, mechanism of injury, amputation level, and pocketing period.1,6,8–10 However, only a limited number of studies have focused exclusively on pediatric patients, and many previous reports have included adult cases. In our study, the CR group tended to have a longer pocketing period and more frequent use of the wet dressing method after separation, whereas the mechanism of injury and the amputation level could not be adequately evaluated due to imbalances in patient characteristics. Based on prior research and the findings of this study, we examined factors influencing the treatment outcomes of chest and palm subcutaneous pocket techniques for pediatric fingertip amputations.

Younger pediatric patients, particularly those younger than 4–5 years of age, have been reported to show higher composite graft survival, including with chest and palm subcutaneous pocket techniques.6,8 Jung et al11 treated 10 digits in 10 patients (mean age 36.2 y) using the palm subcutaneous pocket technique and reported CR in 4 digits and PN in 6. In contrast, Arata et al4 treated 10 digits in 10 pediatric patients (mean age 4.5 y), with CR achieved in all cases. Although pediatric case series are rare, these reports suggest a higher survival rate. In our study, the mean age was 17.4 months, which is considerably younger than that reported in previous studies; nevertheless, PN occurred in 43% (3 of 7) of the digits, indicating that factors other than age may influence graft survival.

The amputation level and mechanism of injury were not clearly associated with the outcomes. Subzones I and II, with limited tissue, are generally considered to be more favorable for graft survival.6 However, Arata et al4 reported a CR in 1 digit classified as subzone IV, suggesting that the viable graft range may be larger in pediatric patients than in adults. Clean-cut injuries are generally considered more favorable than crush or avulsion injuries; however, our sample size was small, and injury patterns were unevenly distributed, limiting the conclusions.10,12

The pocketing period tended to be longer in the CR group. Previous reports recommend approximately 20 days for adults and 2 weeks for pediatric patients, suggesting that the latter may require a shorter pocketing period.4,10,11 In our study, separation occurred at a mean of 11.7 days, which was slightly shorter than that reported in previous pediatric studies. The mean pocketing period in the CR and PN groups was 13.5 versus 9.3 days, respectively, with the CR group generally aligning with the conventionally recommended period. Although no direct clinical studies have compared the donor sites between chest and palm subcutaneous pocket techniques, the latter technique is considered advantageous in terms of joint contracture prevention, ease of postoperative care, and aesthetic outcomes.4,10 In our series, 5 digits were treated using the chest subcutaneous pocket technique, and 2 digits were treated using the palm subcutaneous pocket technique; selection was at the surgeon’s discretion, and no significant differences in survival or joint contracture were observed. One patient treated with the chest subcutaneous pocket technique experienced PN due to accidental pullout, highlighting the need for strict immobilization in pediatric patients. In pediatric patients who have difficulty understanding the need for rest, strict management using tape, bandages, or bust bands is necessary to prevent accidental withdrawal. Therefore, except for cases in which pocketing into the palm is difficult, such as multiple finger amputations, the palm subcutaneous pocket technique may be relatively easier to manage.

Maintaining a moist environment with ointments, including those containing antibiotics, has been reported to be an important factor for improving composite graft survival.13 Moist conditions promote cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and growth factor activity; accelerate wound healing; and reduce necrosis and scar formation.13,14 However, no reports mention the treatment method after separation in chest and palm subcutaneous pocket techniques, and the effect of treatment on graft survival rates remains unknown. In our study, the wet dressing method was more common in the CR group after separation. As demonstrated in the case presented in Figure 4, even when circulation to the fingertip was confirmed at the time of separation, subsequent drying led to PN. This finding suggests that maintaining a strictly moist environment may be beneficial for graft survival. The choice of ointment (plain petroleum jelly, antibiotic-containing ointments, or prostaglandin ointments) was not standardized and warrants further study.13

This study has several limitations. First, regarding treatment methods, the timing of procedures, such as film or gauze changes, was not standardized. Particularly, in the gauze dressing group, the frequency of changes could directly affect factors such as wound dryness. Second, the follow-up period varied significantly between cases, leaving long-term outcomes unclear. For both CR and PN cases, complications such as nail deformity or growth impairment that may arise in the long term could not be evaluated. Furthermore, sensory recovery following pediatric fingertip amputation has been reported to be favorable regardless of whether nerve repair is performed.15,16 However, in the present study, which focused particularly on young pediatric patients, accurate assessment of sensory disturbance was not feasible. In addition, the follow-up period was relatively short, precluding sufficient evaluation of the extent of sensory recovery. Finally, this study included a small number of cases, and no statistical analyses were performed. In addition, treatment strategies were selected at the discretion of the operating surgeon in each case, which limits the generalizability of the findings and warrants cautious interpretation. Further studies with larger sample sizes, including multicenter investigations, are needed to provide more robust evidence.

CONCLUSIONS

Chest and palm subcutaneous pocket techniques are useful options for pediatric fingertip amputations; however, maintaining an adequate pocketing period and appropriate postoperative wound management may be beneficial for improving graft survival.
