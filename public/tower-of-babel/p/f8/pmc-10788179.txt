MUC5AC and MUC5B expression in canine gallbladder mucocele epithelial cells
Authors: Itsuma NAGAO, Kanako TSUJI, Yuko GOTO-KOSHINO, Masaya TSUBOI, James K. CHAMBERS, Kazuyuki UCHIDA, Satoshi KAMBAYASHI, Hirotaka TOMIYASU, Kenji BABA, Masaru OKUDA
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Gallbladder mucocele (GBM) is one of the most common gallbladder diseases in dogs. Its pathogenesis has not yet been clarified, but excessive accumulation of a secretory gel–forming mucin, MUC5AC in the gallbladder has been reported. This study aimed to ascertain if MUC5AC overproduction resulted in mucus accumulation in the gallbladder during GBM development. Eleven dogs undergoing cholecystectomy who were pathologically diagnosed with GBM were included, and the expression level of mucins, particularly MUC5AC and MUC5B, in their gallbladder epithelial cells was compared with those in normal gallbladder epithelial cells. On reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction screening, there was a significant difference (P<0.05) in the mRNA expression level of MUC1, but not of other mucins including MUC5AC and MUC5B, between mucocele and normal gallbladder epithelial cells. Protein expression levels were also evaluated for MUC5AC and MUC5B using immunohistochemistry. There was little immunoreactivity for MUC5AC, whereas MUC5B showed definitive staining in gallbladder epithelial cells. There was no difference in MUC5AC and MUC5B protein expression levels between mucocele and normal gallbladder epithelial cells. These data suggest that excessive production of mucin, especially MUC5AC and MUC5B, does not occur in canine GBM, and that abnormal mucus excretion, rather than excessive mucus production, may be the cause of GBM development.

Gallbladder mucocele (GBM) is one of the most common gallbladder diseases in dogs [8]. It is characterized by excessive mucus accumulation, composed mainly of mucin, in the gallbladder that can cause bile duct obstruction and gallbladder rupture [6, 24]. The only treatment for GBM is cholecystectomy; however, the perioperative mortality rate can approach 20% [11, 14, 26]. Several epidemiological studies have suggested various predisposing factors including breeds such as Shetland Sheepdogs, American Cocker Spaniels, Chihuahuas, Pomeranians, Miniature Schnauzers, and Border Terriers [1, 3, 15, 18], and endocrinopathies such as hyperadrenocorticism and hypothyroidism, and hyperlipidemia [18, 23]. However, the pathogenesis of GBM has not yet been clarified.

In addition to storing bile secreted from the liver, the gallbladder also regulates ion concentrations, water content, and aids with the reabsorption of lipids in bile. Secretion of mucins is also an important role of the gallbladder which protects epithelial cells from the high surfactant activity of bile acids [16, 20]. It has been reported that the membrane-bound mucins MUC1 and MUC3 and the secretory gel–forming mucins MUC2, MUC5AC, MUC5B, and MUC6 are expressed in human and mouse gallbladders [5, 10, 25, 30]. Mass spectrometry analysis revealed that MUC5AC and MUC5B were the predominant mucins in the canine gallbladder [16]. Furthermore, MUC5AC content in mucocele gallbladder was significantly higher than that in normal gallbladder [16]. Although MUC5B is primarily expressed in the trachea and forms highly transportable fluid gels, MUC5AC is expressed in the colon and forms firm gels [17]. It can be assumed that increased MUC5AC concentration in the gallbladder could cause increased biliary viscosity which could be associated with the onset of GBM. However, the cause of increased MUC5AC concentration has not yet been determined. Two hypotheses are possible for the pathogenesis of GBM: an increase in production or a decrease in excretion of these mucins. Increased production of MUC5AC can come from overexpression in gallbladder epithelial cells. Whereas several factors such as decreased gallbladder motility and altered biliary properties can cause decreased excretion and excessive accumulation of MUC5AC in the gallbladder. In this study, to determine the cause of increased MUC5AC in mucocele gallbladder, we evaluated the amount of mucin production in gallbladder epithelial cells from dogs with GBM.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Dogs

Dogs undergoing cholecystectomy at the Veterinary Medical Center of the University of Tokyo or Animal Medical Center of Yamaguchi University, who were histopathologically diagnosed with GBM at the Laboratory of Veterinary Pathology at the University of Tokyo or IDEXX Laboratories, Inc. (Tokyo, Japan) were included in this study. Written informed consent for the use of gallbladder tissues was obtained from all owners. Dogs from which normal gallbladders were obtained were research animals maintained at Yamaguchi University and were euthanized for other experimental purposes that were approved by the Yamaguchi University Ethics Committee (Permission No. 209). Experimental and animal care procedures were performed in accordance with the Yamaguchi University Animal Care and Use guidelines.

Gallbladder sample collection

Gallbladder mucocele tissues were obtained during surgical cholecystectomy from each dog with the disease. Normal gallbladder tissues were obtained from research dogs immediately following euthanasia. The gallbladder was opened longitudinally, and the tissue was fixed using 10% neutral buffered formalin to obtain a full-thickness section for histopathological evaluation. The remaining gallbladder tissues were placed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution until epithelial cell isolation.

Isolation of gallbladder epithelial cells

Gallbladder tissue was washed with ice-cold PBS at least five times. Any remaining mucus gel was removed using forceps. To isolate epithelial cells, gallbladder samples were digested with 0.25% w/v trypsin (Fujifilm Wako, Osaka, Japan) or 2,000 U/mL of dispase (Goudou Shusei, Tokyo, Japan) at 37°C for 45 min. After inactivation of trypsin or dispase by adding 10% fetal bovine serum, cells were collected by centrifugation at 1,500 rpm for 5 min. After another wash with PBS, the pelleted cells were stored at −80°C until utilized. Cell viability was confirmed to be greater than 95% as assessed by the trypan blue exclusion test. Additionally, the epithelial cells percentages assessed by immunocytochemistry (anti-EpCAM, ab71916, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) in six of twenty samples ranged from 92% to 98%.

Quantification of mRNA expression

Total RNA was extracted from gallbladder epithelial cells using RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) or NucleoSpin RNA XS kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany), and reverse transcribed using SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was conducted using THUNDERBIRD Next SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) and a CFX96 Touch Real-time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with a reaction volume of 20 µL. To normalize the amount of cDNA, the ratio of the relative quantity of cDNA for each target gene to that of the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) gene was adopted. Immunohistochemistry

Together with the tissues obtained in the present study, six stock, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded, gallbladder tissues were also subjected to immunohistochemistry. These were collected from the laboratory dogs that had been euthanized for experimental purposes and were all confirmed to be histopathologically normal. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned with 4 µm thickness. Antigen retrieval was performed by autoclaving the samples at 121°C for 20 min in a sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Inactivation of endogenous peroxidase was performed using 3% H2O2 in methanol for 3 min at room temperature. Then, the samples were blocked with 8% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline at 37°C for 30 min and incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibodies. Mouse anti-MUC5AC monoclonal antibody (Clone number: 45M1, 1:100, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) [7] and rabbit anti-MUC5B polyclonal antibody (1:50, Eurofins Genomics, Ebersberg, Germany), which was generated against a synthetic peptide (CQWTKWIDVDY) [16], were used as primary antibodies. After washing with PBS, samples were incubated for 30 min at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase–labeled anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (EnVision + SYStem HRP Labeled Polymer Anti-Mouse, Dako, Glostrup, Denmark) or horseradish peroxidase–labeled anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (EnVision + SYStem HRP Labeled Polymer Anti-Rabbit, Dako), and the color was developed using 3,3-diaminobenzidine (DAB). For MUC5AC, a healthy dog’s stomach section was used as a positive control because stainability of dog gallbladder has not been reported.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Five randomly selected fields of view for each slide were photographed with a 10× objective lens, and the area of positive DAB coloration in epithelial cells was calculated using open-source image analysis software (FIJI, http://fiji.sc/Fiji). Briefly, the DAB color was extracted using the Color Deconvolution function in FIJI, and the area was calculated by defining a threshold value of 0–160 as positive.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R Studio v1.4.1717 (RStudio, Boston, MA, USA). The Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test was used for gene expression and immunohistochemical analysis. A P-value of <0.05 was considered to be significant.

RESULTS

Animals

Mucocele gallbladder tissues were obtained from 11 dogs with GBM, including 4 neutered males, 3 spayed females, 3 unneutered males, and 1 unspayed female. There were 4 Toy Poodles, 3 Shetland Sheepdogs, 1 Chihuahua, 1 Pomeranian, 1 Shih Tzu, and 1 Papillon. None of the cases had ruptured gallbladders or evidence of gallbladder mucosal necrosis on pathological evaluation. Normal gallbladder tissues were obtained from 6 research dogs who were unspayed female beagles.

qPCR analysis

In addition to MUC5AC and MUC5B gene expression levels, as the predominantly expressed canine gallbladder mucins [16], the mRNA expression level of four other mucin genes, MUC1, MUC2, MUC4, and MUC6 were evaluated by RT-qPCR using gallbladder epithelial cells from 11 mucocele and 6 normal gallbladders. MUC3, which is known to be expressed in the gallbladder of humans and mice, was excluded from the analysis due to a lack of available sequencing information in dogs.

DISCUSSION

To assess the production of MUC5AC and MUC5B in gallbladder epithelial cells of dogs with GBM, the mRNA and protein expression levels of the genes were analyzed. Additionally, gene expression analysis of other mucins in the canine gallbladder were conducted and demonstrated that MUC1, MUC2, MUC4, MUC5AC, MUC5B, and MUC6 were expressed in both mucocele and normal gallbladders at the mRNA level. Because MUC4 is not expressed in human and mouse mucosal epithelium except for those with adenocarcinoma [5, 10, 25, 30], MUC4 mRNA expression in canine gallbladder epithelial cells was surprising. Assessing MUC4 protein expression in canine tissue in the future would be helpful in evaluating this finding. In comparison, there was a significant difference in MUC1 mRNA expression between the mucocele and normal gallbladder epithelial cells. However, no significant differences were observed in MUC5AC and MUC5B mRNA which was inconsistent with previous studies on mucin accumulation in GBM [16].

In a previous study using mass spectrometry to analyze mucus accumulated in the gallbladder of animals with GBM, no accumulation of MUC1 was observed [16]. On the other hand, in the present study, MUC1 was found to be expressed at the gene level as well as MUC5AC and MUC5B. Though the protein expression was not evaluated in the present study, MUC1 may not be secreted in the mucus because of its membrane-bound characteristics. In mice, increased MUC1 expression has been reported to cause decreased gallbladder motility, biliary congestion, enlarged gallbladder size, and increased mucin concentration in the gallbladder [29]. Similarly, MUC1 expression has also been reported to regulate the thickness of the intestinal mucus layer [21, 27]. These findings suggest that increased MUC1 expression could be related to the accumulation of mucus in the gallbladder with GBM. In a previous study assessing the changes in the shape of mucin molecules in accumulated mucus gel of GBM cases, highly cross-linked mucin with a condensed molecular shape was often found in the mucus of GBM cases [16]. It is conceivable that MUC1 might have a catalytic role in promoting the polymerization of MUC5AC and MUC5B. Furthermore, since reduced gallbladder motility has been reported in cases of GBM [28], it is possible that increased MUC1 expression may function to reduce gallbladder motility in dogs, as previously reported in mice [29]. Thus, although elevated MUC1 expression may not be a direct factor in mucus accumulation, it may have an indirect effect on the pathophysiology of GBM. To investigate the relationship between the severity of GBM and the expression level of MUC1, we examined the correlation between MUC1 gene expression and blood test markers related to the outcome of GBM cases such as total bilirubin, C-reactive protein, alkaline phosphatase, and alanine aminotransferase [4, 13, 15], but none of the correlations between these markers and MUC1 were significant (data not shown). Further studies to examine the protein expression level of MUC1 in gallbladder epithelial cells of animals with GBM and the influence of increased expression of MUC1 on GBM are needed.

Next, MUC5AC and MUC5B protein expression was evaluated. They were shown to be abundant in canine gallbladder mucus in previous studies [16]. MUC5AC showed little staining in the gallbladder of all dogs used in this study. Since the antibody used in this study demonstrated positive staining in canine Barrett’s esophagus tissue [7] and the normal canine stomach tissue in this study, the reactivity of this antibody against canine MUC5AC is highly likely. The expression level of MUC5AC in the gallbladder was significantly lower than that in the stomach, and no difference in protein expression levels between the two groups was observed within the detectable range. MUC5B staining was clearly observed in all dogs, and there was no difference in protein expression levels between the two groups. Combined with the lack of increase in mRNA expression of MUC5AC and MUC5B, this suggests that the amount of mucin secretion was not different in dogs with GBM.

The cause of GBM is primarily attributed to an excess accumulation of mucin in the gallbladder. From the present study, it is conceivable that this is caused by a decrease in mucin excretion rather than an increase in mucin production by gallbladder epithelial cells. There are several possibilities for impaired mucus excretion. Decreased gallbladder motility has been reported in canine GBM [28] and is probably related to impaired mucus excretion, although it is not clear whether it is the cause or the result of GBM development. A genetic variant of ABCB4 is known to be associated with familial biliary congestion in humans; however, the association with the development of canine GBM is still controversial [9, 22]. Increased mucin gel viscosity due to an alteration in its molecular structure might also induce impaired mucus excretion. Indeed, inter- and intramolecular cross-linking of mucin molecules resulting in polymerization of the molecules has been observed in mucin-filled gallbladders, which increases viscous properties [16]. These changes in the cross-linking of mucin molecules have similarities to cystic fibrosis (CF), a genetic disease in humans, that causes mucus accumulation in multiple organs due to abnormal ion channel function [2, 12, 16, 31]. Excessive accumulation of mucus, primarily composed of MUC5AC and MUC5B, is also observed in the gallbladder of patients with CF; however, epithelial cell mucin secretion is not increased [19, 31]. Since the present results share common features with CF, an investigation into biliary ion concentration and increased viscosity would be warranted.

In conclusion, the expression levels of MUC5AC and MUC5B were not increased in the mucocele gallbladder epithelial cells when compared with the normal gallbladder at mRNA and protein levels. It is conceivable that excessive mucin secretion in the gallbladder epithelial cells is not responsible for the development of gallbladder mucous cysts. More detailed studies on factors causing decreased excretion of mucins from the gallbladder are needed in the future.
