The Alteplase Dose Assessment for Pleural Infection Therapy (ADAPT) Study-3: Efficacy and Safety of 1 mg Alteplase as a Starting Intrapleural Dose
Authors: Estee P.M. Lau, Michaela Donaghy, Sam Faber, Sophie Krivinskas, Francesco Piccolo, Calvin Sidhu, Tommaso D’Orsogna, Sona Vekaria, Edward Fysh, Y.C. Gary Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIntrapleural tissue plasminogen activator/deoxyribonuclease (tPA/DNase) therapy has revolutionised management of pleural infection. However, the optimal tPA dose is still unknown. The Alteplase Dose Assessment for Pleural infection Therapy (ADAPT) study-3 aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a lower starting intrapleural dose of 1 mg tPA (with 5 mg DNase) for pleural infection.MethodsConsecutive patients with pleural infection who required intrapleural fibrinolytic therapy were treated with 1 mg tPA/5 mg DNase. Escalation of tPA doses were permitted at any time at the discretion of treating physicians.ResultsA total of 100 patients (22% female, mean age = 60.2) received a starting dose of intrapleural 1 mg tPA/5 mg DNase. Most (94%) patients were successfully treated and were discharged without needing surgery. Patients received a median of 5 [IQR = 3–6] doses of tPA/DNase and 6 mg of tPA [median, IQR = 3–8] in total. Treatment success was supported by improved pleural fluid drainage (from 238 mL pre-treatment to 2013 mL [IQR = 1,269–2,863] at 72 h, p < 0.0001), reduction in serum C-reactive protein levels (by 47.5% on day 5, p < 0.0001), and clearance in radiographic pleural opacities (from median 24% [IQR = 15–34] of hemithorax before tPA/DNase therapy to 10% [IQR = 7–15] 72 h after the first dose of tPA/DNase, p < 0.0001). In 28 (28%) patients, tPA dose was escalated due to minimal drainage and/or persistent sepsis; 26 were successfully treated and 2 required surgery. One (1%) patient experienced pleural bleed. Four patients with metastatic cancers were palliated during treatment for pleural infection and died.ConclusionA starting intrapleural regimen of 1 mg tPA/5 mg DNase, with dose escalation as necessary, appears to be safe and effective for pleural infection.

Introduction

Pleural infection was reported to affect more than 80,000 patients in the United Kingdom and USA combined over a decade ago, and this figure is likely much higher today, as recent reports indicate a global rise in incidence [1–3]. It is associated with high morbidity and a mortality rate approaching 30% in the elderly population [4], often reflecting their vulnerability due to significant underlying comorbidities. The median duration of hospital stay ranged from 14 to 19 days [2, 5], contributing to an average cost per case of USD 38,591–the highest among pleural diseases [6].

Conventional treatment of pleural infection with antibiotic therapy and chest tube drainage fails in approximately 16–34% of patients [7–9], often due to the presence of fibrinous septations and fluid loculations [10]. This necessitates further interventions with intrapleural therapy and/or surgery [11], the latter has recognised procedural and anaesthetic risks [12–14] and is unsuitable for frail patients. The use of combination intrapleural tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and deoxyribonuclease (DNase) therapy has demonstrated significant benefits in improving fluid drainage, reducing surgical referrals and length of hospital stay compared to placebo in the Multicentre Intrapleural Sepsis Trial (MIST)-2 [9].

The dose of tPA (10 mg) used in the MIST-2 trial was chosen empirically based on anecdotal experience and small case reports, without standard dose-finding studies [9]. Our recent survey of international experts showed a wide variation of tPA/DNase dosage regimen being used worldwide [15]. In the survey, 72.7% of respondents (n = 32/44) have reportedly used tPA doses lower than 10 mg for managing pleural infection, along with different dosing regimens compared to the MIST-2 trial, reflecting an ongoing attempt to mitigate bleeding risks and reduce costs given that the optimal dosage of tPA is still unknown.

Our Alteplase Dose Assessment for Pleural infection Therapy (ADAPT) series consist of dose de-escalation studies using lower starting doses of intrapleural tPA (with DNase) for the management of pleural infection. We hypothesise that starting with lower doses of tPA, with up-titration if needed, may achieve similar efficacy to those obtained with higher doses and potentially reduce amount of total fibrinolytics exposure and thus their side effects and costs. ADAPT-1 and -2 studies investigated starting intrapleural doses of 5 mg and 2.5 mg of tPA, respectively, and demonstrated comparable efficacies to studies using 10 mg of tPA [16, 17]. The present third ADAPT study (ADAPT-3) aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of 1 mg tPA with 5 mg DNase as a starting intrapleural dose for the treatment of pleural infection.

Methods

This study is an observational cohort study conducted at a tertiary/quaternary pleural centre in Australia. Consecutive patients at Sir Charles Gairdner Hospital (SCGH), Western Australia who required intrapleural fibrinolytic/DNase therapy for pleural infection were treated with tPA (1 mg) and DNase (5 mg) and results of the first 100 patients were included. Ethics approval was obtained from SCGH Human Research Ethics Committee (RGS0000006042).

Pleural infection was defined as in our previous ADAPT studies [16, 17]. In brief, all patients had a clinical presentation compatible with pleural infection and pleural fluid that fulfilled at least one of the following criteria: (i) purulent fluid; (ii) pleural fluid pH ≤7.20; (iii) glucose ≤3.0 mmol/L; and/or (iv) presence of bacteria by Gram stain or microbial culture. Patients with missing pleural fluid biochemistry or whose results did not meet above criteria were included if they had supportive radiological evidence of infection and were treated as pleural infection by the treating physician and received tPA/DNase therapy. All patients received standard care (antibiotics and chest tube drainage) and management for pleural infection according to local practice/guidelines and assessed daily for response to treatment. All clinical and management decisions were determined by the patients’ attending physician. Dose escalation of tPA, additional drainage procedures and surgical referral was permitted at any time based on patients’ treatment response.

Patients received a starting dose of 1 mg tPA with 5 mg DNase (each in 50 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride) administered intrapleurally twice daily. tPA and DNase were administered separately: tPA was instilled into the pleural cavity and the chest tube was clamped for 45–60 min before being opened for free drainage for 45–60 min. This process was then repeated with 5 mg DNase. Patients’ demographics and relevant clinical data, pleural fluid biochemistry and microbiology, antibiotic therapy and treatment outcome (and complications) were recorded.

Treatment success was defined largely in keeping with definitions used in the ADAPT-1 and -2 studies [16, 17]. Treatment success was defined as survival to hospital discharge and without the need for surgery within 90 days (end of follow-up) after initiation of tPA/DNase therapy.

Escalation of tPA doses and/or additional pleural drainage interventions were captured. Changes in the (i) volume of pleural fluid drained, (ii) serum C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, and (iii) percentage of pleural opacity on chest X-rays (CXR) at the end of tPA/DNase therapy were compared to baseline. Areas of pleural opacity on CXRs were assessed by 2 independent scorers as per prior studies [16, 17]. Incidences of systemic and/or pleural bleeding and pain were also captured. Pleural bleed was defined as the presence of blood in the pleural cavity associated with an acute drop in haemoglobin levels necessitating blood transfusion.

Data were analysed (GraphPad Prism; La Jolla, CA, USA) and presented as mean (SD) or median [interquartile range, IQR]. Paired t test or Wilcoxon signed rank test were used to compare inter-group differences based on normality of the data. Multiple group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test (if parametric) or Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test (if nonparametric). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Demographics and Clinical Data

One hundred consecutive patients (22.0% female, mean age 60.2 years) received intrapleural fibrinolytic/DNase treatment with 1 mg tPA and 5 mg DNase twice daily between September 2020 and February 2025 (Table 1). Comorbidities were present in 83.0% of patients.

Demographic and clinical characteristics of patients

The most common cause of pleural infection was community-acquired pneumonia (58.0%, n = 58), with the median duration from symptom onset to pleural infection diagnosis of 7.0 [3.5–13.0] days. Seven patients (7.0%) developed pleural infection associated with the use of an indwelling pleural catheter (IPC) for the management of malignant pleural effusion (MPE).

Majority (91.8%) of patients had ultrasonographic evidence of loculation or septation, and the effusions were most commonly moderate in size and occupied 2–3 rib spaces (Table 2). Pleural fluid biochemistry levels were typical of those with pleural infection (Table 2). The median serum CRP level 24 h prior to the intrapleural tPA/DNase therapy was 238 [140–328] mg/L. Microorganisms were cultured from the pleural fluid of 44.9% of patients, of which 11.4% were polymicrobial (Table 2). Frank pus was found in 10.0% of patients and was described as turbid in another 21.0%. Fluid appearance was not documented in 6 patients. All patients received empirical systemic broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, most commonly piperacillin/tazobactam (n = 46, 46.0%) or ceftriaxone (n = 35, 35.0%).

Characteristics of pleural infection and microbiology

The median time from hospital admission to pleural infection diagnosis and chest drain insertion was 2 [0–5] days and 2 [1–6] days, respectively. Chest tube size was recorded in 96 patients; most (79.2%) were treated with 18-French (45.8%) and 12-French (33.3%) tubes. Eight (8%) patients had an IPC; one was inserted during the hospital admission for empyema in the context of an MPE. Twenty-five (25.0%) patients had additional chest tubes inserted for drainage; in 20 cases after the initiation of tPA/DNase therapy.

The median time from chest tube insertion to first dose of tPA/DNase was 1 [1–2] day, with 41.0% of patients receiving the first dose within 24 h and 71.0% by 48 h. For patients (n = 8) with an IPC in-situ, tPA/DNase was initiated (via the IPC) after 0.5 (median, IQR 0.0–2.0) days of pleural infection diagnosis.

Treatment Outcomes

Treatment success was defined (as in prior studies) as survival to hospital discharge without requiring surgery up to 90 days (end of follow-up) after the first dose of tPA/DNase. Ninety-four (94.0%) patients were successfully treated with a starting intrapleural dose of 1 mg tPA with 5 mg DNase. Treatment success was supported by radiological and biochemical improvements, see below.

Minimal Total Amount of tPA Required despite Need for Dose-Escalation

Patients received a median of 5 [3–6] doses of tPA/DNase and a median total amount of 6 mg [3–8] of tPA. Twenty-eight (28.0%) patients had tPA doses escalated to 2 mg (n = 2), 2.5 mg (n = 15), 5 mg (n = 14), or 10 mg (n = 1) by the attending physician after receiving 3.0 (SD 1.6) doses of 1 mg tPA with 5 mg DNase. Some patients received varying escalated doses of tPA. Decisions for escalating tPA doses were commonly due to minimal drainage, residual loculation and/or persistent sepsis. Most of these patients (n = 26/28, 92.9%) were successfully treated.

Increased Pleural Fluid Drainage

The median volume of pleural fluid drained increased significantly from 238 mL [115–551] in the 24 h before treatment to a cumulative of 1,025 mL [725–1,481] 24 h following the initial dose of tPA/DNase (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1). At 72 h, the median cumulative total volume of pleural fluid drained increased to 2,013 mL [1,269–2,863] (p < 0.0001). There was no significant difference in the median total volume of pleural fluid drained at 72 h between patients who required dose escalation (1,950 mL [1,325–2,790]) and those who did not (2,005 mL [1,219–2,731]), p = 0.95.

Reduction in Serum CRP Levels

The median CRP level at baseline (24 h prior to the first dose of tPA/DNase) was 238 mg/L [140–328]. Following the initiation of tPA/DNase therapy, significant reductions in CRP levels were observed (Fig. 2) and by day 5, there was a 47.5% [31.4–62.7] reduction in CRP levels from baseline.

Radiological Improvements on Chest Radiographs

Chest radiographs pre- and post-tPA/DNase treatment were available in 97 patients. The median percentage of hemithorax occupied by pleural opacity reduced from 24% [15–34] before tPA/DNase therapy to 10% [7-15] (p < 0.0001) at 72 h after the first dose of tPA/DNase (Fig. 3).

Hospital Length of Stay

The median length of stay from the first dose of tPA/DNase for the whole cohort was 8 [5–15] days. Length of stay was shorter in those successfully treated with 1 mg tPA/5 mg DNase without needing dose escalation (n = 72): median 6 [5–12] days versus 11 [7–18] days in those who failed and required dose escalation (n = 28), p = 0.002.

Treatment Failure

Six patients were considered to have failed tPA/DNase therapy. Two of them had ongoing infection/sepsis despite additional chest tube insertion for separate loculations and escalation of tPA doses to 5 mg. Both underwent successful video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) 3 and 7 days after starting tPA/DNase therapy. Four other patients died although pleural infection was not the main cause of death. Nonetheless, they did not meet our predefined criteria for success and were included as “treatment failure.” All 4 patients had advanced metastatic (mesothelioma, rectal and lung) cancers and proven MPEs but developed concurrent (either confirmed or suspected) pleural infection. All had good clinical response after 3 (n = 2), 6, and 7 (n = 1 each) doses of 1 mg tPA/DNase, but active treatment was withdrawn and changed to palliative intent because of cancer progression (e.g., new malignant pericardial tamponade) or general decline and died 5, 15, 18, and 36 days after the first dose of tPA/DNase.

Another 4 patients died from other co-morbidities (e.g., mesothelioma, metastatic breast cancer and cerebrovascular accident) within 90 days from the first dose of tPA/DNase. All achieved clinical resolution of infection prior to discharge and were not considered treatment failures.

Adverse Events

One patient (1%) fulfilled our criteria of pleural bleed attributable to tPA/DNase therapy. She received 1 dose of 1 mg tPA and 2 of 5 mg tPA (with DNase), followed by an insertion of a second chest drain due to poor fluid drainage. Pleural bleed occurred the following day with a drop in haemoglobin from 103 to 65 g/L. She was haemodynamically stable, received 2 units of packed red blood cells (PRBCs) and underwent successful video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery for evacuation of both infection and haematoma.

Two patients had self-limiting pleural bleed post-tPA but no drop in haemoglobin (a criterion for pleural bleeding). The 21-year-old man with left empyema received antibiotics and 3 doses of 1 mg tPA/DNase but had residual loculated fluid. Dose escalation to 5 mg of tPA/DNase triggered drainage of 500 mL of blood (fluid haematocrit 0.52) which was self-limiting. His vital signs and haemoglobin were stable but received a unit of PRBC prophylactically. Computed tomography scan showed contrast extravasation into the loculated pleural fluid at the 7th intercostal space where the chest drain entered the thorax, suggestive of intercostal vessel injury from drain insertion and aggravated by tPA. He proceeded to intercostal artery embolisation by which time there were no evidence of ongoing bleed. He recovered fully and was discharged 4 days later. Another patient with multiloculated pleural infection received 4 doses of 1 mg tPA/DNase and 2 doses of 5 mg tPA/DNase before insertion of a second chest tube directed at a residual posterior locule (through which 5 doses of 1 mg tPA/DNase were delivered). He drained a total of 1.3L of haemorrhagic fluid: haematocrit was 0.24 in pleural fluid (vs. 0.29 in the blood). He was haemodynamically stable and did not require blood transfusion.

A 74-year-old man with end stage renal failure and resultant chronic anaemia previously required blood transfusion. He developed a pleural infection and received 7 doses of 1 mg tPA/DNase with good response. His haemoglobin level drifted (from 91 to 75 g/L) over the treatment period which was considered a result of his chronic renal failure and sepsis. He was well and haemodynamically stable, received 1 unit of PRBC and made an uneventful recovery. One patient suffered a significant upper GI bleed from a large gastric ulcer 3 days after her last dose of tPA/DNase and was deemed not directly related. Pain after tPA/DNase was reported by 14 patients (14.0%) and was effectively managed with analgesics, without discontinuation of therapy.

Discussion

This study provides important efficacy and safety data of adopting a starting intrapleural dosing regimen of 1 mg tPA with 5 mg DNase in a large cohort of unselected patients with pleural infection. Using our composite endpoint, 94% of patients survived to hospital discharge without requiring surgery within 90 days of intrapleural tPA/DNase therapy. Patients also demonstrated improvements in radiological findings, pleural fluid drainage and inflammatory markers.

Our approach of starting with a low dose 1 mg tPA, with escalation as needed, achieved comparable efficacy rates to those reported in previous studies employing higher starting doses of tPA [16, 17], using similar outcome measures and definitions of treatment success. More importantly, patients only required a total median dose of 6 mg of tPA, an amount 10 times lower than that given in MIST-2 trial [9]. This finding is promising and is worth exploring, especially in high-risk patients. Clinicians are generally cautious when initiating intrapleural tPA/DNase therapy and this strategy could allow the inclusion of patients who might otherwise be excluded.

Cumulative evidence supports that intrapleural doses of 10 mg tPA (used in the MIST-2 protocol) may be excessive to clinical need. Recent laboratory studies found extremely high active tPA concentrations (median 8,150 IU/mL) in pleural fluid after a first intrapleural dose of 10 mg tPA [18]. This level was well in excess of peak plasma tPA activity after systemic thrombolysis for myocardial infarction or stroke (1,000–1,900 IU/mL). In a veterinary study, a median dose of 3.75 mg of tPA was adequate in treating parapneumonic effusions in 25 horses [19].

International experts in our prior survey [15] considered a starting regimen as suitable if ≥80% of patients can be successfully treated without dose adjustment. Starting with a low dose and escalating as needed allows for a personalised treatment regimen tailored to the individual patient’s clinical condition, treatment response and co-morbidities. Pleural infection is associated with significant heterogeneity and a “one dose fits all” protocol may be inappropriate. It is likely that the starting dose and the number of doses required would differ among patients. Given our approach successfully treated 94% of patients, it seems a suitable starting regimen until accurate patient stratification tools are available to identify those who require higher starting doses.

The bleeding rate observed in this study is slightly lower than that reported in previous ADAPT studies [16, 17]. However, we caution against over-interpretation of any direct comparison due to the low event rates and small sample sizes in our observational series. Definitive randomised comparative studies are warranted to determine if lower starting tPA doses provide a better safety profile. In our study, it is possible that clinicians’ confidence in using a very low starting dose of 1 mg tPA may have led to the inclusion of sicker patients with higher bleeding risks who might have been considered unsuitable for intrapleural fibrinolytic treatment if higher tPA doses were used.

The median length of hospitalisation in this study is comparable to previous ADAPT studies [16, 17, 20]. However, patients who required dose escalation in our cohort stayed significantly longer compared to those who did not (+5 days). This may be explained by the prolonged hospitalisation in a few sicker patients due to other conditions/co-morbidities (e.g., surgery-liver transplant, awaiting transfer to rural hospital) even after the successful treatment for pleural infection. This difference becomes insignificant (p = 0.07) when these patients are omitted from analysis.

Using lower doses of tPA without compromising on efficacy is crucial especially during worldwide shortages of tPA and can contribute to significant cost savings. Although, this largely depends on the availability of vial sizes in different countries, given that reconstituted tPA has a shelf-life of 24 h when refrigerated (manufacturer’s advice). In Australia, a 10 mg vial costs approximately AUD 624, and once reconstituted, allows a maximum of 2–3 doses when reduced doses of tPA are used. A 2 mg vial is also available and is considerably less expensive. Some hospitals also have their own manufacturing guidelines for reconstituted smaller doses of tPA that is deep-frozen (−20°C) and can be kept for up to 6 months.

This study has limitations. Firstly, the descriptive nature of this study precludes any firm conclusions to be drawn without a direct comparison with 10 mg tPA. However, our findings are generalisable as our cohort comprised of the typical patient population with pleural infection. Consecutive patients who required intrapleural tPA/DNase were included and the standard starting dose of 1 mg tPA was adopted in our clinical practice protocol; hence selection bias is kept minimal. Secondly, there were no set criteria used to determine which patients required dose escalation, additional chest tubes insertion or referral for surgery. This is a pragmatic real-life cohort study, where all clinical management decisions were left to the discretion of the attending clinician, which we consider appropriate given the heterogeneity associated with pleural infection. Thirdly, several patients with underlying advanced malignancy had active treatment withdrawn and were palliated. Whether tPA/DNase treatment had resolved the infections were not clear and we conservatively included them as “treatment failure.” This reflects the patient complexity commonly encountered in everyday clinical practice. Lastly, this was a single-centre study at an elite pleural centre highly experienced in the use of intrapleural tPA/DNase therapy. Further studies are needed to assess reproducibility of the outcomes in a wider setting.

In conclusion, a starting intrapleural dose of 1 mg tPA with 5 mg DNase, with dose escalation as needed, appears to be a safe and effective treatment strategy for the management of pleural infection. Future prospective comparison trial of this dosing strategy against 10 mg tPA is warranted.
