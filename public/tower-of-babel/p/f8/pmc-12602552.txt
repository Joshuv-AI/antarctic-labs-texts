Cesarean delivery modifies the association between prenatal exogenous progesterone and cryptorchidism: a case–control study
Authors: Junfeng Zhao, Xiaoqiang Cheng, Zhan Shi, Defeng Chen, Yuanchao Shen, Hongji Zhong, Jianming Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the association between prenatal exogenous progesterone exposure and cryptorchidism, and whether delivery mode modifies this association. We conducted a retrospective case–control study of 610 cryptorchidism cases and 610 controls from pediatric surgical admissions between April 2016 and October 2021. Data on prenatal exogenous progesterone exposure, delivery mode, and relevant covariates were extracted from medical records. Multivariable logistic regression assessed associations and interactions. Prenatal exogenous progesterone exposure was associated with increased odds of cryptorchidism (aOR = 1.85; 95% CI = 1.42–2.40; P < .001). This association was significantly modified by delivery mode (interaction P = .001). In the cesarean delivery subgroup, progesterone exposure was linked to a higher incidence of cryptorchidism (65.6% vs. 41.9%; P < .001) and substantially increased odds (aOR = 3.27; 95% CI = 2.15–4.98; P < .001), whereas the association was attenuated and not statistically significant following vaginal delivery (aOR = 1.28; 95% CI = 0.90–1.81). Within the cesarean subgroup, progesterone-exposed infants also had higher rates of preterm birth (13.8% vs. 7.3%; P = .018) and low birth weight (17.5% vs. 9.9%; P = .014), whereas maternal age was comparable between groups (P = .409). Conclusion: Our findings support that prenatal exogenous progesterone exposure was associated with increased odds of cryptorchidism, with a stronger association among cesarean deliveries. The higher prevalence of preterm birth and low birth weight in progesterone-exposed cesarean deliveries is consistent with case-mix differences and may reflect them. Clinicians should remain cautious, and further confirmation from prospective studies is warranted. What is Known:• Cryptorchidism is common; etiology is multifactorial and incompletely understood. • Perinatal factors (e.g., prematurity, LBW) and endocrine disruption have been implicated. What is New:• Prenatal exogenous progesterone exposure is associated with higher odds of cryptorchidism. • Cesarean delivery modifies this association, resulting in a stronger association in exposed infants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00431-025-06599-3.

Introduction

Cryptorchidism, defined as the failure of one or both testes to descend into the scrotum, is the most common congenital anomaly in male infants, affecting approximately 1–9% of full-term newborn males [1–3]. Despite its clinical implications—including impaired fertility and an increased risk of testicular malignancy—its etiology remains incompletely understood. Known risk factors include genetic predisposition [1, 4], perinatal determinants such as low birth weight (LBW) and prematurity [2, 5], maternal conditions like gestational diabetes and preeclampsia [6], and environmental exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemicals during critical windows of testicular descent [1, 7]. However, the role of maternal hormone exposure, specifically exogenous progesterone administered during pregnancy, in cryptorchidism has not been thoroughly investigated.

Progesterone is frequently prescribed during early pregnancy to manage threatened miscarriage, first-trimester bleeding, or to support assisted reproductive technology (ART) pregnancies [8–10]. While clinically beneficial, concerns remain regarding potential endocrine-disrupting effects on fetal gonadal development. Epidemiological studies have linked prenatal progesterone exposure to hypospadias, suggesting possible anti-androgenic effects [11, 12]. Although direct mechanistic evidence is limited, progesterone has been hypothesized to influence fetal androgen availability and testicular descent through endocrine modulation, providing a biologically plausible rationale for its potential role in cryptorchidism [13].

In addition, prior studies examining delivery mode as a risk factor for cryptorchidism have produced inconsistent findings [5, 14], and its potential interaction with prenatal exogenous progesterone exposure has not been explored. Clarifying whether delivery mode modifies progesterone-related odds could offer critical insights for clinical management.

Therefore, we aimed to investigate the association between prenatal exogenous progesterone exposure and the odds of cryptorchidism, specifically evaluating delivery mode as a potential effect modifier.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This hospital-based retrospective case–control study was conducted at Ningbo University Affiliated Women and Children’s Hospital April 2016 and October 2021. The study adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for observational research. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Ningbo University Affiliated Women and Children’s Hospital (No. NBFE-2025-KY-072), with waived informed consent due to the retrospective use of de-identified data.

Study population

Initial cohort

Participants were initially identified from an initial cohort of 8538 pediatric surgical admissions within the Pediatric Surgery Department. This initial cohort included all male infants and children aged ≤ 18 years admitted specifically for surgical procedures with a hospital stay duration of at least 2 days.

Exclusion criteria

Participants were excluded from the initial cohort if they had repeated admissions (≥ 2 hospitalizations) during the study period or genetic anomalies (including the disorders of sex development (DSD), chromosomal anomalies, or other syndromic disorders), leaving 8148 participants eligible for further evaluation.

Case and control definitions

Cases were defined as children undergoing orchiopexy for clinically confirmed cryptorchidism (persistent unilateral or bilateral undescended testis beyond 6 months of age). Patients were subsequently excluded if diagnosed with testicular dysgenesis syndrome (TDS), vanishing testes, secondary cryptorchidism, retractile testes, or if essential clinical data were missing. Ultimately, 610 cryptorchidism cases were included.

Controls were selected from pediatric patients undergoing unrelated surgical procedures, such as appendectomy, circumcision, or hernia/hydrocele repairs. Patients were excluded if they had endocrine-related urogenital anomalies (e.g., hypospadias, congenital penile curvature, persistent Müllerian duct syndrome), congenital heart diseases, or missing essential clinical data. From eligible participants (n = 2417), 610 controls were randomly selected. Subsequent statistical analyses confirmed no significant difference in maternal age distribution between cases and controls.

Data collection, quality assurance, and data verification

Data were extracted from electronic medical records using a standardized, piloted form with explicit variable definitions. Three pediatric clinicians received protocol training prior to abstraction. Blinding to case–control status was not feasible due to visible admission indications. Therefore, we restricted extraction to objective, source-documented variables and applied predefined coding rules. For data integrity, we implemented automated range/logic checks and cross-field consistency rules, de-duplicated records using a composite key (medical record number, date of birth, admission date), and manually reviewed any flagged collisions. In addition, a random 10% of records underwent independent double-extraction, with discrepancies adjudicated by a senior investigator via source-document review. Missing data handling

For all study variables (including exposure, outcome, and covariates), we excluded cases with any missing data, performing a complete-case analysis. This approach ensured all analyzed observations had complete data across all specified variables.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test (normal distribution) or Mann–Whitney U test (non-normal), and χ2 or Fisher’s exact test for binary categorical variables.

Univariable logistic regression was used to identify factors potentially associated with cryptorchidism (P < 0.1). However, given clinical relevance and previous literature, all collected variables were included in subsequent multivariable logistic regression models. Model 1 adjusted for prematurity, smoking, alcohol, hypertension, diabetes, (maternal) age, delivery mode and blood type. Mode 2 further adjusted for LBW, abortion, ART, and family history. To evaluate potential collinearity among covariates, we calculated variance inflation factors (VIFs) for binary/continuous variables and generalized VIFs (GVIFs) for categorical variables; all VIFs and adjusted GVIF^(1/(2 × df)) values were < 1.3, indicating negligible multicollinearity. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and discrimination was quantified by the area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals.

Interaction analyses explicitly tested the modifying effect of delivery mode (vaginal vs. cesarean) on the association between exogenous progesterone exposure and cryptorchidism, with P for interaction values reported in both the text and tables. Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness and consistency of the association across clinically relevant strata. To test the robustness of our findings, we performed sensitivity analyses excluding controls with hernia/hydrocele or ART cases.

All statistical tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to interaction and subgroup analysis P values to account for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software 4.2.2 (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and Free Statistics Analysis Platform (Version 2.1.1, Beijing, China, http://www.clinicalscientists.cn/freestatistics). Given the retrospective design, no a priori sample-size calculation was performed; post hoc, the study had > 90% power to detect the observed effect size (OR = 1.85) at α = 0.05.

Results

From an initial cohort of 8538 pediatric surgical admissions, 1220 pediatric patients met inclusion criteria (610 cryptorchidism cases and 610 controls; Fig. 1). No differences were observed for maternal smoking, alcohol, or hypertension (P > 0.05 for all). Association between prenatal exogenous progesterone and cryptorchidism

Prenatal exogenous progesterone exposure was associated with a 72% increased odds of cryptorchidism in unadjusted analysis (OR = 1.72; 95% CI, 1.34–2.22; P < 0.001; Supplementary Table 1). In multivariable models, the association persisted (Model 1: aOR 1.76, 1.37–2.28; P < 0.001) and remained after additional adjustment for LBW, abortion, ART, and family history (Model 2: aOR 1.85, 1.42–2.40; P < 0.001; Table 2). Other univariable associations are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The fully adjusted model showed adequate calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow χ2 = 7.83, P = 0.451) and modest discrimination (AUC = 0.62, 95% CI, 0.59–0.65; Supplementary Fig. 2). The complete output of the final multivariable model, including all parameter estimates, is provided in Supplementary Table 8. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

Discussion

This study adds epidemiological evidence that prenatal exogenous progesterone exposure is associated with an increased odds of cryptorchidism, and this association was robust across multiple sensitivity analyses. Critically, we identified cesarean delivery as a significant modifier of this effect, with the association substantially stronger in infants delivered by cesarean than by vaginal delivery. The convergence of higher rates of preterm birth and low birth weight among progesterone-exposed cesarean deliveries, though not formal modifiers themselves, may underscore the potential interplay of perinatal factors in this relationship.

Previous research on cryptorchidism risk has focused on estrogens, androgens, and insulin-like 3 (INSL3) with inconsistent results [7, 15, 16], whereas direct human evidence regarding maternal progesterone exposure is scarce. Animal studies show that disruptions in testosterone, INSL3, or luteinizing hormone (LH) signaling pathways can impair testicular descent [17, 18], but comparable human epidemiology specific to progesterone is lacking. Our findings help address this gap by linking prenatal exogenous progesterone exposure with cryptorchidism and highlighting delivery mode as a potential effect modifier.

Although direct clinical evidence is limited, progesterone may disrupt fetal endocrine homeostasis. Prior study suggests possible anti-androgenic effects of prenatal progestins, including associations with hypospadias [19], and use in ART protocols suggests suppression of maternal pituitary LH, theoretically reducing fetal testosterone availability [20]. These pathways represent biologically plausible and may help contextualize the observed association, but do not imply causation.

Cesarean delivery is unlikely to act as a direct cause of cryptorchidism; rather, it may function as a modifier of progesterone-related susceptibility. Evidence indicates lower neonatal stress-axis activation in cesarean-delivered infants (lower basal and post-stressor cortisol) compared with vaginal delivery, suggesting altered neuroendocrine adaptation [21, 22]. While direct evidence on HPG-axis activation remains limited, it is plausible that a blunted stress response could attenuate the early postnatal surge in gonadotropins and testosterone relevant to testicular descent. Taken together, we propose a hypothesis-generating “two-hit” framework: prenatal exogenous progesterone may confer latent vulnerability via endocrine disruption, and cesarean delivery may provide perinatal compensatory stimuli-hormonal (labor-related surges in fetal LH/testosterone, stress-axis activation) and mechanical (birth-canal compression). This framework is explicitly hypothetical and intended to guide future prospective and mechanistic studies.

Existing studies on cesarean-cryptorchidism link are inconsistent. Increased risk has been reported in some studies [5, 23], while others observed no adjusted association [14]. One possible explanation for these inconsistencies is that delivery mode itself may not directly cause cryptorchidism but rather serve as an indirect indicator reflecting other underlying prenatal or perinatal risk factors. Consistent with this interpretation, our univariable analyses initially demonstrated that cesarean delivery was not associated with an increased odds of cryptorchidism compared to vaginal delivery. However, subgroup and interaction analyses demonstrated that cesarean delivery significantly modified cryptorchidism odds among infants exposed to prenatal exogenous progesterone, suggesting delivery mode may indeed modify cryptorchidism susceptibility indirectly by interacting with hormonal exposures.

Beyond direct causal interpretations, it is also important to consider that prenatal exogenous progesterone exposure, cesarean delivery, and cryptorchidism may covary due to shared maternal–fetal vulnerabilities (e.g., endocrine or placental dysfunction) that shape clinical decisions and neonatal outcomes. This “common etiology” framework is consistent with recent evidence highlighting cryptorchidism as part of a broader spectrum of developmental and long-term sequelae, including testicular cancer [24]. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as associations that may reflect shared pathways and effect modification, rather than direct causal effects.

A notable strength of our study includes a large sample focusing specifically on exogenous progesterone and formal interaction testing with delivery mode, alongside comprehensive covariate adjustment. Model performance was acceptable with modest discriminatory, supporting the adequacy of the specifications for describing the observed data. Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective case–control design restricts causal inference, highlighting the need for prospective cohort studies. Second, reliance on medical records may introduce information bias, including incomplete documentation of exposure parameters and clinical indications, preventing assessment of confounding by indication. Third, as a single-center, hospital-based study, generalizability may be limited. Fourth, although measured confounders were comprehensively adjusted for, individual matching (e.g., by birth year or gestational age) was not used, so the possibility of residual confounding cannot be excluded. Finally, the absence of hormonal biomarkers (e.g., amniotic fluid or cord blood) precludes mechanistic validation, warranting further prospective studies incorporating such data.

Given the widespread clinical use of progesterone and the high global rates of cesarean delivery, these results have practice relevance. The observed association between prenatal exogenous progesterone exposure and cryptorchidism was stronger among cesarean deliveries, and may inform antenatal counseling and targeted postnatal surveillance. While progesterone remains appropriate for indicated high-risk pregnancies, clinicians may adopt heightened vigilance for testicular descent in male infants with this combined prenatal history. These data support indication-based use and shared decision-making, and may, pending further confirmatory studies, inform future guidance.

Conclusions

This study provides epidemiological evidence supporting an association between prenatal exogenous progesterone exposure and an increased odds of cryptorchidism, notably modified by cesarean delivery. These findings underscore the need for further prospective research with detailed exposure assessments to better understand the underlying biological mechanisms.
