Revealing the genetic and immunological crosstalk between SLE and ccRCC: An integrative genomic analysis approach
Authors: Feng Lin, Kewei Yang, Dan Li, Tianqi Chen, Tianbo Luo, Feifei Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Although systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) are clinically distinct, emerging evidence suggests they may share underlying genetic and immunoregulatory mechanisms. However, the specific molecular basis of their potential comorbidity has not been clearly defined, and no prior study has comprehensively investigated their shared genomic architecture. To fill this critical knowledge gap, we conducted the first integrative genome-wide analysis to identify common susceptibility loci, pleiotropic genes, and convergent biological pathways between SLE and ccRCC. Utilizing large-scale genome-wide association studies summary data from European populations, we employed a combination of methods including LDSC, PLACO, MAGMA, Summary data–based Mendelian randomization , and HyPrColoc, alongside functional annotation and pathway enrichment analyses via FUMA and Metascape. We identified a significant positive genetic correlation between SLE and ccRCC (Rg = 0.287, P = 1.5 × 10−4), along with 11 pleiotropic loci, notably including colocalization signals at 3q26.2 and 11q22.3. We further identified 63 pleiotropic genes, with AKT1, ERBB2, and STAT1 functioning as key network hubs associated with the PI3K/Akt, NF-κB, and JAK-STAT pathways. Immunological colocalization analysis indicated that CD24⁺IgD⁺CD38⁻ B cells and unswitched memory B cells may constitute potential comorbid subpopulations. Summary data–based Mendelian randomization analysis further revealed CASP9 as a promising therapeutic target for comorbidity intervention. Overall, this study identified pleiotropic loci and key hub genes enriched in immune-oncogenic pathways, including PI3K/Akt, NF-κB, and JAK-STAT, and proposed potential therapeutic targets, thereby providing a concrete basis for understanding the comorbidity mechanisms of SLE and ccRCC and informing future precision interventions.

1. Introduction

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), the most common subtype of kidney cancer, is strongly linked to genetic mutations, epigenetic changes, and immune dysregulation.[1] Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a multifactorial autoimmune disorder marked by chronic inflammation and immune system dysregulation.[2] Despite their distinct clinical features and etiologies, emerging evidence suggests overlapping genetic and molecular pathways between SLE and ccRCC, indicating potential comorbidity mechanisms. In ccRCC, loss-of-function mutations in the VHL gene result in aberrant activation of the HIF pathway, promoting angiogenesis and uncontrolled cell proliferation.[3] Additional mutations in genes such as PBRM1, SETD2, and BAP1 are frequently observed and are implicated in chromatin remodeling and cell cycle control.[4] In SLE, GWAS have identified risk genes such as HLA, IRF5, STAT4, and TNFAIP3, which are primarily involved in immune and inflammatory regulation.[2] Notably, several interferon pathway–related genes exhibit aberrant expression in both SLE and ccRCC, suggesting a shared genetic susceptibility.[5,6]

In recent years, genomic and epigenomic research has played a pivotal role in clarifying the mechanisms of complex diseases. For instance, Roudbar et al integrated single nucleotide variants with DNA methylation profiles to reveal genetic-epigenetic interactions in rheumatoid arthritis. Their findings demonstrated that genetic variation alone is insufficient to fully account for disease risk, highlighting the need to incorporate multilayered regulatory information.[7] Extending this evidence, Alavi et al showed that genomic and epigenomic approaches not only help elucidate pathogenic pathways but also provide a theoretical foundation for drug development and precision medicine.[8] Building on these insights, applying such approaches to investigate the comorbidity between SLE and ccRCC may uncover shared pathogenic mechanisms, enable drug repurposing, and support the advancement of personalized therapeutic strategies.

Epidemiological studies have suggested that patients with SLE may face an increased risk of developing certain malignancies. Several studies have reported a consistently elevated incidence of hematologic malignancies in SLE patients, with non-Hodgkin lymphoma being particularly prominent.[9] Elevated risks of lung, thyroid, and vaginal/vulvar cancers have also been observed.[10] However, the association between SLE and ccRCC remains unclear. Some studies suggest that SLE patients may be at higher risk of developing renal cancer,[9,11] especially those who have undergone kidney transplantation and received prolonged immunosuppressive therapy.[12] In contrast, other studies have reported conflicting findings. For instance, a Mendelian randomization study investigating causal links between autoimmune diseases and renal cancer suggested that SLE may act as a protective factor against kidney cancer.[13]

Whether the comorbidity between ccRCC and SLE is driven by a few specific loci or by a broader genome-wide correlation remains unclear. To date, systematic investigations into the genetic overlap and shared susceptibility architecture of the 2 diseases remain limited. Therefore, the core problem this study addresses is whether SLE and ccRCC share statistically significant genetic loci, functional genes, and immune mechanisms that can explain their potential comorbidity. Cross-trait analysis has increasingly been used in large-scale GWAS to identify pleiotropic variants and shared genetic architectures across diseases.[14] In this study, we present the first systematic investigation of the comorbidity mechanisms between ccRCC and SLE, employing an integrative framework that combines multiple genetic tools – including LDSC, PLACO, MAGMA, and HyPrColoc (Fig. 1). Specifically, LDSC estimates genome-wide genetic correlation from linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs),[15] while PLACO identifies shared disease-associated loci that may reveal common pathogenic mechanisms.[16] Compared to prior studies that typically focus on single diseases or isolated loci, our multi-level integrative framework offers both mechanistic depth and translational relevance, providing a solid foundation for understanding comorbidity and guiding precision therapeutic strategies.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Sources of GWAS summary statistics for SLE and ccRCC

This study utilized large-scale GWAS analyses to systematically explore the shared genetic architecture of SLE and ccRCC. Due to substantial population-level differences in genetic background and variant patterns, and given the high quality and completeness of publicly available GWAS data for European cohorts, the analysis was limited to individuals of European ancestry. The GWAS summary data for SLE (including 5201 cases and 9066 controls) were obtained from a previously published GWAS meta-analysis.[17] The data for ccRCC (including 16,321 cases and 759,800 controls) were derived from a separate GWAS meta-analysis.[18] All datasets used in this study were publicly available, and no additional ethical approval was required.

2.2. Standardization of GWAS summary statistics

To ensure the consistency and comparability of GWAS summary data, several standardized processing steps were applied. First, all variant genomic coordinates were mapped to the human reference genome GRCh37 (Genome Reference Consortium Human Build 37), and effect allele orientations were harmonized to prevent strand-related bias. Second, the major histocompatibility complex region (chr6: 24–35 Mb, GRCh37) was excluded because its complex LD structure may generate false-positive associations.[19] Third, variants with a minor allele frequency below 1% were filtered out to reduce statistical noise. Finally, potential sample overlap was assessed before data integration to minimize the risk of biased estimates.

2.3. Genome-wide genetic correlation analysis

To evaluate genome-wide genetic correlation between SLE and ccRCC, we used publicly available GWAS summary statistics and LD score regression (LDSC). LDSC distinguishes confounding effects from true polygenic signals by evaluating the relationship between SNP association statistics and their LD scores, and it further estimates the genetic correlation between traits.[14,15] After standardizing the GWAS datasets, we used the official LDSC software (https://github.com/bulik/ldsc) to estimate the genetic correlation between the 2 diseases. LD scores for SNPs were derived from European reference data in Phase 3 of the 1000 Genomes Project.[20]

2.4. Identification of pleiotropic risk loci and colocalization analysis

A pleiotropic analysis under composite null hypothesis (PLACO) is a robust tool for identifying true pleiotropic signals, offering better control of false discovery rates (FDRs) than traditional methods.[21,22] Its principle is based on the composite null hypothesis framework: under the null hypothesis (H0), a SNP has no effect on either phenotype or affects only one; under the alternative hypothesis (H1), the SNP has significant effects on both phenotypes. In practice, PLACO uses GWAS summary statistics for 2 phenotypes as input and calculates Z-scores for each SNP across both traits. It then conducts a joint test of the bivariate effects within the composite null framework to generate a P-value. SNPs reaching genome-wide significance (5 × 10⁻⁸) are considered potential pleiotropic loci. By integrating information from 2 traits, PLACO increases the power to detect pleiotropic variants while reducing false positives that may arise in single-trait analyses.[21] Identified pleiotropic loci were then functionally annotated using the functional mapping and gene annotation (FUMA) of GWAS.[23] The procedure included: identifying significant SNPs using a genome-wide significance threshold of 5 × 10⁻⁸; grouping SNPs in strong LD (R2 >0.6) into LD blocks using 1000 Genomes reference data to define risk loci; and applying Bayesian colocalization analysis to assess whether SLE and ccRCC share causal variants at the same loci.[24] A posterior probability (PP.H4) ≥ 0.75 was considered strong evidence for colocalization between the 2 traits.

2.5. Identification of pleiotropic genes and functional enrichment

Based on PLACO results, pleiotropic genes were identified using the multi-marker analysis of genomic annotation (MAGMA) method. MAGMA defines gene locations using the ENSEMBL database and integrates LD information to aggregate pleiotropic SNPs within each gene, thereby enabling systematic detection of multi-marker effects.[25] Multiple testing was corrected using the FDR method, with a significance threshold of PFDR <.05 to ensure robust results. To further investigate the biological functions of pleiotropic genes, Metascape was used for functional annotation and pathway enrichment analysis, integrating multiple databases such as gene ontology, KEGG, WikiPathways, and protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks.[26] Additionally, genome-wide tissue-specific enrichment analysis was conducted using data from 53 tissue types in the GTEx project.[27]

2.6. Identification of drug targets

Potential drug targets among pleiotropic genes were identified using the Summary data–based Mendelian randomization (SMR) approach.[28] Specifically, SMR integrates GWAS and eQTL data to assess potential causal relationships between gene expression and disease phenotypes. To reduce false-positive results due to LD, the Heterogeneity In dependent instruments (HEIDI) test was used to assess heterogeneity. Several genes with significant causal associations at the expression level were identified, providing a theoretical basis for subsequent functional validation and targeted drug development.

2.7. Multi-trait colocalization

A multi-trait colocalization method, HyPrColoc, was applied to identify immune cell signals at known pleiotropic risk loci. HyPrColoc is a Bayesian-based multi-trait colocalization tool that identifies clusters of traits potentially influenced by shared causal variants across GWAS summary statistics from multiple phenotypes.[29] First, GWAS summary statistics for multiple traits were integrated with the LD structure from a reference panel to identify candidate regions at the LD block level in a stepwise manner. Next, hierarchical clustering and iterative aggregation were employed to determine whether different traits share the same causal variants. Following this, HyPrColoc calculates the PP of full colocalization for the shared causal variants, providing a quantitative measure of the credibility of colocalization signals. Finally, the method outputs candidate regions, shared causal SNPs, and their associated traits, facilitating efficient and interpretable multi-trait colocalization analysis. GWAS summary data for immune cell traits were retrieved from the public GWAS Catalog (dataset IDs: GCST0001391 to GCST0002121).[30] Colocalization results were visualized using the GENI plots R package (https://github.com/jrs95/geni.plots).

3. Results

3.1. Genome-wide genetic correlation between SLE and ccRCC

Using the LDSC method, we systematically evaluated the genome-wide genetic correlation between SLE and ccRCC. The analysis revealed a significant positive genetic correlation between the 2 diseases (Rg = 0.287, P = 1.5 × 10−4). Genetic covariance analysis further estimated an intercept of 0.01 (standard error = 0.0055), indicating minimal influence from population stratification or systematic bias, thereby reinforcing the robustness of the observed correlation.

3.2. Identification of pleiotropic genetic loci

To identify potential pleiotropic loci shared between SLE and ccRCC, we applied the PLACO method in conjunction with the FUMA platform. A genome-wide joint analysis was first conducted using PLACO, and a Q-Q plot was generated (Fig. S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q646). The close alignment between observed and expected P-values suggested negligible impact from population stratification or technical artifacts. Subsequent PLACO analysis identified 232 significant pleiotropic SNPs (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q645). These SNPs were functionally annotated via FUMA, resulting in the identification of 11 pleiotropic loci shared between the 2 diseases (Table 1). Notably, 2 of these loci (18.2%) demonstrated colocalization with a shared causal variant in both conditions (PP.H4 ≥ 0.75), supporting the existence of a common pathogenic mechanism in the corresponding genomic regions (Fig. 2).

3.3. Identification of pleiotropic genes and functional enrichment

In this study, significant SNP signals were mapped to their nearest gene loci using the FUMA platform, and gene-level pleiotropy was assessed through the MAGMA method, leading to the identification of 63 pleiotropic genes (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q645). To explore the biological relevance of these genes, functional enrichment analysis was conducted using the Metascape platform (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q645). The results demonstrated that candidate genes were significantly enriched in several key biological processes, including cell proliferation and apoptosis, autophagy and lipid metabolism, inflammation, immune regulation, and multiple cancer-associated signaling pathways (Fig. 3A, Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q645), indicating a potential shared molecular mechanism underlying the pathogenesis of both diseases.

Transcription factor target enrichment analysis (Fig. 3B, Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q645) revealed significant overrepresentation of downstream targets regulated by transcription factors such as HNF4, MEF2C, and ICSBP. These findings highlight the central involvement of metabolic regulation, cell cycle progression, cellular differentiation, and interferon-mediated immune pathways in the comorbid pathophysiology of SLE and ccRCC.

PPI network analysis further revealed that AKT1, ERBB2, and STAT1 occupy hub positions among pleiotropic genes (Fig. 3C), exhibiting high connectivity and functional centrality. Tissue-specific expression profiles indicated that these core genes are highly expressed in EBV-transformed B cells, spleen, and peripheral blood (Fig. 3D), suggesting their key regulatory roles in immune-relevant tissues.

Additionally, eQTL analysis showed significant expression of 6 pleiotropic genes – including ACTRT3, CASP9, and DOT1L – in whole blood, spleen, and EBV-transformed lymphocytes (Fig. S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q646). Among them, both SMR and HEIDI analyses supported CASP9 as a candidate therapeutic target for SLE–ccRCC comorbidity (Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q645), underscoring its potential translational significance.

3.4. Immune cell colocalization

Preliminary analyses indicated that immune pathways may play a pivotal role in the comorbid mechanisms underlying SLE and ccRCC. To further evaluate this hypothesis, we applied the HyPrColoc method to conduct multi-trait colocalization analysis at SLE–ccRCC shared risk loci exhibiting high colocalization probabilities (PPFC >0.70), with the aim of identifying putative immune cell signals. The analysis revealed that genetic variants significantly colocalized within the 3q26.2 region were associated with 2 B-cell subpopulations: CD24⁺IgD⁺CD38⁻ B cells and unswitched memory B cells (Table S7, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q645, Fig. 4). These findings underscore the potential functional relevance of these immune cell subsets in the shared pathogenic mechanisms of SLE and ccRCC.

4. Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the genetic comorbidity architecture between SLE and ccRCC. LDSC analysis revealed a significant positive genetic correlation between SLE and ccRCC. This finding is consistent with Li et al., who identified 45 cancer–autoimmune disease risk genes, some of which are shared between SLE and renal cancer. Shared T/B-cell signaling, interferon pathways, and drug targets underscore the immunological network links between the 2 diseases.[31] These findings suggest potential cross-disease effects involving genetic susceptibility, immune regulation, and apoptotic pathways between SLE and ccRCC.

Through cross-trait PLACO analysis integrated with FUMA annotation, we identified 11 pleiotropic loci shared by SLE and ccRCC, with strong colocalization signals at 3q26.2 and 11q22.3. The rs7629500 variant in the 3′ untranslated region of the VHL gene has been shown to increase ccRCC risk nearly threefold. Additionally, 7 ccRCC-specific susceptibility loci have been identified, including 1p36.33, 1q21.3, 7q21.2, 9q31.3, 10q22.1, 11p11.2, and 22q13.31.[18] Notably, IRF5 (7q32.1) serves as a shared risk factor for both SLE and ccRCC.[32,33] Another key variant, rs2304256 in TYK2, causes a Val362Phe substitution, with the A allele significantly associated with reduced SLE risk in multiple populations.[34] We also observed considerable overlap between previously reported risk genes and the loci identified in this study. For example, the ATM gene at 11q22.3 plays a critical role in DNA repair, and its mutations are strongly associated with ccRCC, breast cancer, melanoma, and other malignancies.[18] This region also harbors several inflammasome-related genes (CASP1, CASP4, CASP5), whose dysregulation may aggravate immune imbalance in SLE via upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-18.[35] At 3q26.2, the TERC gene contributes to the pathogenesis of both SLE and kidney cancer by regulating telomere stability.[36] These colocalized loci may represent shared causal variants and provide a foundation for constructing a genetic network of SLE–ccRCC comorbidity.

MAGMA analysis identified 63 pleiotropic genes, including well-known SLE-associated genes such as IRF5, BANK1, and TNIP1. Previous GWAS confirmed IRF5, BLK, ITGAM, BANK1, IRF7, RAK1/MECP2, and HLA as key SLE susceptibility genes.[33] TYK2 and IRF5 exhibit synergistic effects, with gene–gene interactions enhancing SLE susceptibility.[37] In ccRCC, the IRF family and type I interferon pathway, including TYK2, constitute a central anticancer regulatory network.[38] IRF5 and TYK2 are critical regulators of the interferon and JAK-STAT signaling pathways, respectively, and are well recognized as core pathogenic genes in SLE. These discoveries have facilitated the development of targeted therapies. For example, the interferon pathway inhibitor Anifrolumab significantly reduced disease activity in phase II/III clinical trials. Likewise, the TYK2 inhibitor Deucravacitinib demonstrated strong efficacy and good tolerability in phase II studies, establishing it as a promising therapeutic candidate for SLE and a potential complement to Anifrolumab.[39] Beyond autoimmune disease, synthetic lethality analyses have identified functional interactions between IRF5, TYK2, and VHL mutations. In VHL-mutant ccRCC, inhibition of TYK2 or IRF5 triggers tumor cell death, suggesting opportunities to expand therapeutic applications to this patient group.[40] The NF-κB pathway, regulated by TNIP1/ABIN1, suppresses inflammation in SLE,[41] but its aberrant activation promotes tumorigenesis in ccRCC.[42] BANK1 promotes B-cell activation via the TLR7–STAT1 pathway and serves as a key susceptibility gene for SLE.[43] Novel candidate genes including LRRC34, LRRIQ4, C11orf65, and KDELC2 were identified at 3q26.2 and 11q22.3, potentially expanding the comorbidity genetic landscape. These findings suggest that comorbidity may arise from a set of pleiotropic and shared regulatory genes.

Importantly, PPI analysis revealed that key genes – AKT1, ERBB2, and STAT1 – occupy central positions within the pleiotropic network. Previous studies show that AKT1, a central component of the PI3K/Akt pathway, plays a broad role in immune regulation, supporting B-cell survival and T cell function, and is hyperactivated in both SLE and multiple cancers.[44] ERBB2 (HER2), a receptor tyrosine kinase, activates the NF-κB pathway and may contribute to pathogenesis in the chronic inflammatory environment of SLE.[45] It also acts as a key upstream receptor in the PI3K/Akt pathway, promoting cancer cell survival and proliferation.[46] STAT1 acts as a signal transducer and transcription activator, playing a pivotal role in the JAK-STAT pathway. This pathway is widely implicated in various autoimmune diseases and cancers.[47] STAT1 is a major downstream effector of interferon (IFN) signaling, which is central to the pathogenesis of SLE.[48] Moreover, chromatin proximity and regulatory synergy between STAT1 and STAT4 drive sustained expression of IFN-γ and pro-inflammatory chemokines, contributing to systemic inflammation.[49] Interestingly, Th cell–mediated antitumor immunity depends on the transcription factor T-bet, which is activated through STAT1 and STAT4 signaling.[50] In summary, AKT1, ERBB2, and STAT1 represent the PI3K/Akt, NF-κB, and JAK-STAT pathways, 3 essential and intersecting inflammatory signaling cascades. Compared with previous studies, this work not only reaffirms the significance of key signaling pathways but also identifies potential co-expression and synergistic amplification among AKT1, ERBB2, and STAT1. These molecular interactions may reflect a shared signaling network between SLE and ccRCC, suggesting a shift from studying isolated pathway abnormalities to examining broader, network-level mechanisms that drive disease comorbidity.

Tissue-specific expression analysis confirmed elevated expression of these key genes in immune-related tissues, including EBV-transformed B cells, spleen, and peripheral blood, supporting their involvement in the comorbid mechanisms of SLE and ccRCC.

At the drug target level, SMR and HEIDI analyses identified several genes, including CASP9, that may causally influence disease development by regulating gene expression in tissues such as blood and spleen. CASP9 is a key initiator caspase in the mitochondria-dependent apoptotic pathway. It contributes to cell death, immune clearance, and tumor regulation by activating downstream caspase-3.[51] Dysregulation or aberrant activation of caspase-9 has been linked to autoimmune diseases, including SLE and rheumatoid arthritis.[52] Therefore, CASP9 may serve as a promising therapeutic target for both SLE and ccRCC.

Multi-trait colocalization analysis revealed that, within the 3q26.2 genomic region, SLE and ccRCC share CD24⁺IgD⁺CD38⁻ B cells and unswitched memory B cells as potential pathogenic subsets, likely contributing to disease pathogenesis. This finding aligns with Guo et al., who proposed that aberrant B cell activation may act as a mechanistic link between SLE and ccRCC, potentially promoting ccRCC development via modulation of the inflammatory microenvironment.[53]

Previous studies have mainly examined susceptibility genes of individual diseases or epidemiological links. This study applies cross-trait genetic analysis to provide the first molecular-level evidence of a positive genetic correlation between SLE and ccRCC. It reinforces the roles of IRF5 and TYK2, while identifying AKT1, ERBB2, and STAT1 as novel hub genes within a pleiotropic network. Their potential co-expression and synergistic regulation suggest comorbidity may arise from coordinated immune-inflammatory signaling. This shifts the research focus from isolated pathways to an integrated network-level view of disease mechanisms. Immunologically, distinct comorbidity-associated B cell subsets were identified, offering refined insight into shared pathogenesis. Future work should prioritize functional validation of core genes, development of targeted interventions, and integration of multi-ethnic GWAS data with longitudinal cohorts to assess clinical applicability and biomarker potential.

This study identifies potential genetic mechanisms linking SLE and ccRCC, but several limitations should be noted. The reliance on GWAS summary data from European populations may introduce bias due to population-specific effects and sample size disparities, requiring validation in multi-ethnic cohorts. Summary-level data also limit analysis of gene-environment interactions. Moreover, the absence of dual-disease functional models hampers confirmation of shared gene effects. Translating these findings into clinical practice is challenging, especially given the opposing needs of immunosuppression for autoimmune disease and immune activation for cancer therapy. Future research should focus on incorporating diverse population datasets, conducting functional validation in relevant models, and designing targeted clinical trials to bridge the gap between genetic discovery and precision treatment.

5. Conclusion

This study reveals a significant shared genetic architecture between SLE and ccRCC based on large-scale European GWAS data. Eleven pleiotropic loci and sixty-three shared genes were identified, with AKT1, ERBB2, and STAT1 as central hubs enriched in immune-oncogenic pathways. Immune cell colocalization highlights the specific B-cell subsets as key contributors to comorbid mechanisms. The results offer novel insights into the genetic basis of SLE–ccRCC comorbidity and inform future therapeutic strategies.
