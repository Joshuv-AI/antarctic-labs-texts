Gorilla knuckle-walking mechanics suggest independent evolution in African great apes
Authors: Akimasa Ito, Masayuki Tanaka, Nobuaki Yoshida, Naomichi Ogihara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gorillas and chimpanzees both use knuckle-walking (KW). Whether this behavior is a homologous trait inherited from their last common ancestor or the result of convergent evolution remains debated. This study investigated the biomechanics of gorilla KW by measuring ground reaction forces (GRFs) and comparing them with previously reported chimpanzee data. GRFs were recorded using two in-series force plates embedded in a horizontal beam in the outdoor enclosure of Kyoto City Zoo. Three adult gorillas voluntarily performed KW during normal activity, and 90 steady-state trials were analyzed. The results revealed interspecific differences: chimpanzees exhibited greater peak vertical GRFs in the hindlimbs, approximately 1.5 times those in the forelimbs, suggesting greater hindlimb weight support. In contrast, gorillas showed comparable peak GRFs in both limbs, with slightly higher values in the hindlimbs, indicating a more balanced weight distribution. Moreover, two individuals showed out-of-phase fluctuations in gravitational and kinetic energy of the center of mass, indicating an inverted pendulum mechanism and relatively efficient energy recovery. The distinct GRF patterns and apparent mechanical efficiency suggest that gorilla KW differs biomechanically from that of chimpanzees. These findings appear to be consistent with the hypothesis that KW in gorillas and chimpanzees evolved independently, which may suggest convergent evolution rather than shared ancestry.

Introduction

The acquisition of habitual upright bipedalism is the defining trait that distinguishes the hominin lineage from African great apes. Understanding the ecological and evolutionary contexts in which this unique locomotor mode and its associated anatomical adaptations arose remains a central question in biological anthropology. A key aspect of this inquiry is reconstructing the locomotor behavior of the last common ancestor (LCA) of humans and African apes, which is critical for understanding the evolutionary trajectory of human bipedalism1,2.

Interpreting the functional significance of morphological variation in fossil specimens is fundamental to reconstructing locomotor evolution. However, the fossil record from the late Miocene, the period when the human lineage diverged from African great apes, is fragmentary. Fossil discoveries from this timeframe are rare, and most consist of dental and cranial remains, with postcranial fossils essential for inferring locomotor behaviors largely absent3–5. Consequently, the locomotor mode of the LCA remains unknown, and the evolutionary pathways that led to bipedalism in hominins and knuckle-walking (KW) in chimpanzees and gorillas remain unresolved.

Identifying the selective pressures that led to the evolution of KW in chimpanzees and gorillas is crucial for understanding why bipedalism emerged in the human lineage6–9. If KW and bipedalism represent divergent solutions to environmental and ecological challenges faced by the LCA, then determining the factors that favored KW can provide insights into the selective pressures that drove the evolution of habitual bipedalism in hominins. To infer these evolutionary patterns, it is essential to first establish a comprehensive understanding of the biomechanical mechanisms and functional advantages of KW.

Locomotion fundamentally involves mechanical interactions between the body and the environment, mediated through reaction forces exerted by the ground10. Ground reaction force (GRF) analysis provides critical insights into the biomechanics of movement, revealing differences in force generation that may reflect underlying anatomical and functional adaptations. By examining how forces are generated and distributed during locomotion, we can better understand the mechanical demands imposed by different gait strategies and their evolutionary significance. Despite extensive research on human bipedal locomotion, locomotion in African apes, particularly KW, remains relatively understudied. Both chimpanzees and gorillas employ KW as their primary form of terrestrial locomotion11. However, although GRF data for chimpanzee KW are available, albeit limited12–18, virtually no comparable data exist for gorillas. This lack of data hampers our ability to assess whether KW in these species reflects a retained ancestral trait or an instance of parallel evolution.

In this study, we present the first quantitative GRF dataset for KW in gorillas. By comparing these data to existing findings on chimpanzee KW, we evaluate whether KW mechanics are conserved across species or exhibit species-specific characteristics. This comparison has significant implications for reconstructing the locomotor behavior of the LCA of humans and African great apes, as well as for assessing whether KW was inherited from this ancestor6,19–23 or evolved independently in chimpanzees and gorillas7,24–26. Our findings contribute to a deeper understanding of primate locomotor diversity and the evolutionary pathways leading to human bipedalism. By elucidating the biomechanical characteristics of gorilla KW, we aim to refine models of hominin locomotor evolution and clarify the role of KW in the ancestry of African apes and humans.

Material and methods

Three healthy adult western lowland gorillas (Gorilla gorilla) at Kyoto City Zoo (Kyoto, Japan) participated in the experiment (Table 1). The three gorillas consist of a father (MT), a mother (GK), and their firstborn son (GT). They lived together as a family group of four, including their second son born in 2018. No restrictions were placed on their daily life or behavior for the purposes of this study. In the zoo enclosure where they live, 15-cm-wide steel beams are arranged throughout the space (Fig. 1A). The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee at Kyoto City Zoo (2023-KCZ-008, 2024-KCZ-008) and the Animal Care Committee at the University of Tokyo (A2023S007). In this study, we installed two custom-made force plates in series on one of the horizontal beams located approximately two meters above the ground and measured the ground reaction forces as the gorillas voluntarily walked across them in both directions at a self-selected speed. The walking surface is discontinuous on the right side of the beam (Fig. 1A). The continuous surface extended 2.5 m to the left of the two force plates and 1 m to the right. For analysis, we only included gait cycles in which the entire body was on this continuous surface, excluding trials where the animals ascended laterally onto the branched beam or stepped onto the lower right-hand section. Although this setup does not represent terrestrial locomotion, it captures arboreal quadrupedalism, which accounts for approximately 30% of arboreal locomotor modes in gorillas27, and thus represents a common and ecologically relevant form of locomotion for this species. It should be noted, however, that although the laterally narrow beam mimics a branch, its flat upper surface differs from a natural cylindrical support. Accordingly, this setup represents an experimental approximation of arboreal conditions rather than a direct replication of natural substrates. Each custom-made force plate was constructed using a six-axis force sensor (SFS100YS302R6, Leptrino, Saku, Japan) and measured 150 mm × 200 mm (Fig. 1B). Signals from each force sensor were recorded at 100 Hz and transmitted to a computer on a local network via Ethernet converters (CNVE001, Leptrino, Saku, Japan). The GRFs were normalized by body weight for comparisons. The GRFs signals were not low-pass filtered. The accuracies of the force measurements and the calculated center-of-pressure positions were confirmed to be within 10 N and 2 mm, respectively, based on the method described by Ito et al.28.

In this study, walking sequences in which gorillas walked quasi-steadily on the beam and stepped on the force plates were selected for the analysis. It must be noted, however, that the gorillas walked on the beam only a few times per day at most, and among those, instances where they stepped precisely on the force plates without overstepping were even more limited. To increase the number of usable trials in which they walked without stepping off the force plates, we treated the two adjacent force plates as a single unit in this study, thereby maximizing the number of usable trials.

In the diagonal sequence gait of gorillas, forelimb touchdown is followed by that of the ipsilateral hindlimb, and the contact positions of those ipsilateral fore- and hindlimbs are adjacent to each other (Fig. 2). Therefore, in addition to measuring the ground reaction force profiles for each independent contact of the hand and foot, we also recorded the combined force of the ipsilateral hand–foot pair, as they often contacted the same force plate. For the trials in which the GRF of the fore- and hindlimb are independently measured, the time was expressed as a percentage of the contact duration. For the combined force of the ipsilateral hand-foot pair, the time was expressed as a percentage of the period between forelimb contact and foot-off of the ipsilateral hindlimb, which is in the present study longer than the corresponding gait cycle as shown in the footfall pattern in Fig. 3. Anteroposterior GRFs are negative for breaking and positive for propulsive components. Mediolateral GRFs are positive for lateral components. When combining data from the left and right limbs, the mediolateral components of the right limb were inverted so that positive values consistently represented the same lateral direction across both sides. GRF data were time-normalized to the stride cycle before applying values from one side to the contralateral limb. Specifically, each stride was resampled to 101 points (0–100% of the stride), ensuring comparability between left and right limb data. The gorillas were simultaneously filmed at 60 Hz using two cameras (IB9367-EHT-v2, VIVOTEK, New Taipei City, Taiwan). Cycle duration, duty factor (= stance phase duration/cycle duration) and footfall patterns (i.e., limb phase) for each walking sequence were determined from the video recordings. Limb phase was defined as the percentage of the cycle duration at which each limb made contact with or lifted off from the substrate relative to the forelimb. Diagonality and symmetry were also calculated from the video recordings. Diagonality was defined as the percentage of the cycle duration between touchdown of a hindlimb and the subsequent touchdown of the ipsilateral forelimb, with higher values indicating more diagonal-sequence gaits. Symmetry was defined as the phase shift between left and right forelimb footfalls, expressed as a fraction of the stride cycle, with a value of 0.5 indicating perfectly symmetry. The forward velocity of each walking sequence was calculated by digitizing the position of the ear using the three-dimensional motion analysis software Argus, an open-source motion analysis software29. The Froude number (Fr), defined as Fr = v2/gL, where v is velocity, g is gravitational acceleration, and L is leg length, was then computed. The mean distance between the hip and the metatarsal head during the stance phase was used as the effective leg length for calculating Fr30.

To characterize gait mechanics, we calculated the temporal changes in potential energy (PE) and kinetic energy (KE) of the center of mass (COM) over a gait cycle using GRF data31. The PE and KE were defined as PE = mgh and KE = m(vx2 + vz2)/2, respectively, where m is the body mass, g is the gravitational acceleration, h is the vertical displacement of the center of mass (COM) from its position at foot contact, and vx and vz are the forward and vertical velocities of the COM, respectively. The percentage of energy recovery (%Recovery) due to pendular exchange between PE and KE (i.e., the inverted pendulum mechanism) during quadrupedal walking was then computed based on the instantaneous PE and KE of the COM32,33. Normalized GRF profiles were averaged, and the GRF of the contralateral hand and foot was reconstructed based on the averaged footfall pattern. The net GRF acting on the COM was estimated by summing the measured GRFs of the ipsilateral hand and foot with the reconstructed GRFs of the contralateral side34. Translational acceleration of the COM was computed by dividing the net GRF by body mass and subtracting gravitational acceleration. The vertical displacement as well as forward velocities of the COM were then obtained by numerical integration of the acceleration, yielding the temporal changes in PE and KE35,36. For steady-state locomotion, the integrated vertical GRF over a stride should equal the product of body mass and stride duration. In practice, deviations from this condition occur due to the assumptions of bilateral symmetry and imperfectly steady motion. Therefore, a small offset value was introduced to satisfy this physical constraint. The initial vertical velocity was adjusted so that COM height at the beginning and end of each stride cycle remained equal, assuming steady cyclic locomotion.

Results

A total of 90 KW sequences from three gorillas were collected and analyzed in the present study (Table 1). In this study, ground reaction force profiles for both the right and left sides were collected. The number of sequences obtained from GT was considerably smaller than those from GK and MT, as GT was less likely to walk across the designated horizontal beam. GK and MT consistently adopted a KW hand posture when walking on the beam, and palmigrade placement was not observed at any time. Although extremely rare, GT was occasionally observed grasping the beam during locomotion. However, all data reported here concern gaits adopting the ordinary KW hand posture.

Table 2 summarizes the means and standard deviations of gait parameters for each subject. The stride length was approximately 1 m and consistent across all three subjects. However, the cycle duration tended to be longer in the larger individuals. As a result, the mean gait velocity of GK (approximately 1 m/s) was higher than that of the two heavier subjects, with the heaviest individual, MT, exhibiting the lowest gait velocity (approximately 0.6–0.7 m/s). The mean Froude numbers for the collected walking sequences were < 0.25, 0.17, 0.07, and 0.05 for GK, GT, and MT, respectively, indicating that the locomotion was relatively slow even for GK, and very slow for the other two gorillas. Discussion

The present study demonstrated that the peak vertical GRF in the hindlimb is greater than that in the forelimb and that the hindlimb primarily contributes to propulsion in KW in the gorilla (Fig. 4). This finding is consistent with a well-known characteristic of quadrupedal walking in primates, in which the vertical GRF of the forelimbs is comparatively lower than that of the hindlimbs, and the hindlimbs play a major role in forward propulsion, the pattern contrasting with that of non-primate quadruped where the forelimbs typically bear more weight12,14,41. However, the present study demonstrated that the gait mechanics of KW in gorillas differ from those in chimpanzees. In this study, the ratio of normalized peak vertical GRFs between the forelimb and hindlimb was approximately 0.5:0.65 for GK and GT, and 0.5:0.5 for MT, indicating that the distribution was relatively close to 1:1. In contrast, previously reported ratios for chimpanzees are 0.49:0.84 (single subject, 8 years old, 32 kg; Kimura13), 0.76:1.16 (4 subjects, 4–7 years old, 19–21 kg; Demes et al.14), and 0.5:1.0 (3 subjects, 6–19 years old, 34–62 kg; Pontzer et al.17), indicating that the hindlimb peak vertical GRF in gorillas is considerably lower than in chimpanzees. This difference may be partly attributed to the slower walking speed and consequently higher duty factor observed in gorilla KW, which results in all four limbs contacting the ground simultaneously (Fig. 3). When contact time is prolonged, the required vertical GRF per limb decreases because the upward impulse must still counterbalance the downward impulse due to body weight. Given their large body size, gorillas likely adopt longer stance durations to reduce the load on their hindlimbs during weight support. This results in a gait pattern characterized by a longer duty factor and simultaneous four-limb ground contact, features that are typically absent in chimpanzee KW. Another possible explanation is that interspecific differences reflect variation in body morphology and biomechanics that has adapted to different locomotor ecologies. During arboreal quadrupedalism, primates typically shift a greater proportion of vertical loading to the hindlimbs compared with terrestrial locomotion42,43. Therefore, the differences observed between gorillas and chimpanzees may be interpreted as morphological and biomechanical strategies shaped by terrestrial adaptation in gorillas and arboreal adaptation in chimpanzees. However, while these factors may partially explain the lower hindlimb peak GRFs observed in gorillas, they may not fully account for the difference. Further investigation is needed to determine the underlying causes. Fundamental differences may exist in the locomotor mechanics or gait strategies between the two species.

The present study also demonstrated that the %Recovery during KW in gorillas can reach levels comparable to those observed in human bipedal walking (60–70%). This finding was unexpected, as it is generally assumed that apes do not utilize pendulum-like walking mechanics. In fact, the %Recovery for chimpanzee KW has not been directly reported. However, given that percentage recovery during bipedal walking in chimpanzees is relatively low (24% in adults; Kimura and Yaguramaki44; 15% in subadults; Demes et al.14), that chimpanzee KW is characterized by hindlimb-dominant vertical GRFs that approximate body weight, similar to what is seen in bipedal walking13,14,17, and that the metabolic cost of transport for bipedal and quadrupedal walking in chimpanzees is nearly equivalent16, it is generally inferred that chimpanzees do not employ an inverted-pendulum mechanism during quadrupedal locomotion. A similar inference has been made for bonobos45. Given these previous findings, the high %Recovery observed in gorilla KW is surprising. Although the value was low in MT, the fact that potential and kinetic energy fluctuated out-of-phase suggests that recovery could increase at higher walking speeds. These results possibly imply that KW in gorillas may be more mechanically efficient than previously assumed.

Gorillas, which support relatively greater loads on the forelimbs, maintain a more neutral knuckle posture without pronounced wrist hyperextension7. This posture is mechanically advantageous under high loading because ground reaction forces pass close to the wrist joint axis, reducing destabilizing torques, and their robust carpal morphology is consistent with this supportive function. By contrast, chimpanzees, which experience relatively lighter forelimb loading, often allow substantial wrist hyperextension and instead rely on the passive properties of the digital flexor tendons to bear compressive loads during stance46, rather than on carpal robustness. As Kivell and Schmitt argue, these biomechanical and anatomical differences suggest that KW in gorillas and chimpanzees reflects fundamentally different solutions to forelimb loading, rather than a single inherited pattern7. In addition, gorillas appear to walk not only with relatively columnar wrists but also with more extended elbows40, effectively using the forelimbs as stiff, columnar supports. This posture may possibly account for the higher %Recovery observed in gorillas. Because %Recovery fundamentally reflects inverted-pendulum-like oscillations of the center of mass, it is plausible that gorillas achieve higher values, at least in the forelimbs, by employing this columnar support strategy.

The observed differences in GRF and COM mechanics between gorilla and chimpanzee KW are consistent with the hypothesis that this locomotor strategy evolved independently in the two lineages of African great apes. Previous studies have proposed that African great apes and humans shared a common KW ancestor, based on wrist morphology observed in Australopithecus that limits wrist extension and resembles adaptations for KW6,20–23. However, other studies argue against this hypothesis, noting the absence of KW -related anatomical features in modern humans and Ardipithecus24,25. Developmental and morphological data further support the idea that KW evolved independently in chimpanzees and gorillas. Ontogenetic patterns differ between the two species26,47,48, and notable differences exist in carpal bone structure. The relatively high energy efficiency observed in gorilla KW may reflect a unique adaptation to their large body size and slow, weight-bearing locomotion, whereas chimpanzees appear to rely on a more hindlimb-driven, less energy-efficient gait16. This could also be the reason why the gait mechanics of vertical climbing is different between the two species49,50. Clarifying this distinction provides a more coherent framework for understanding why superficially similar KW postures may have evolved independently in the two lineages, rather than being inherited from the LCA. However, this interpretation should be treated with caution, because differences in speed and substrate conditions between the gorilla locomotion in this study and chimpanzee locomotion in Pontzer et al.17 limit the strength of a direct comparison.

One limitation of the present study is that we analyzed KW locomotion on a horizontal beam instead of flat ground. Although we believe this setup did not substantially alter the gorillas’ gait patterns, it may have imposed some spatial constraints on limb placement, particularly limiting variability in hand and foot positions. These constraints, along with the narrow surface, may have contributed to the reduced walking speed observed. Nevertheless, all locomotor behaviors analyzed were entirely voluntary and self-selected, which we believe better reflect natural walking of gorilla. From visual observations, the gait patterns did not appear qualitatively different from those observed during flat-ground walking. Future studies should aim to analyze GRFs during overground locomotion across a range of walking speeds to verify the present findings. It is also a limitation that comparable data for chimpanzee KW have not yet been obtained by us. Because chimpanzee data were derived from previous literature and not measured in the same experimental context, direct comparisons remain limited. Comparable experiments on chimpanzees and actual quantification of the %Recovery in chimpanzee’s KW will be necessary to confirm our interpretations. In the future, though it may not be feasible in practice, we hope to take advantage of opportunities during zoo enclosure renovations to embed force plates into walking substrates, which would allow for the collection of GRF data from chimpanzees.
