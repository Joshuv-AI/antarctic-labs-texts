Successful management of uncommon embedded foreign body ingestion: a case report
Authors: Wenwen Su, Yuqi Xia, Feng Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIncidents of pharyngeal foreign bodies are common in otolaryngological emergencies. In most cases, foreign bodies are easily identified and removed with or without the aid of flexible laryngoscopy. However, fully embedded foreign bodies in the tonsil are extremely rare and present a unique challenge for otolaryngologists.Case summaryA 53-year-old Han male had a toothbrush head embedded in the palatine tonsil for 1 month, resulting in a prolonged discomfort and delayed diagnosis. The final diagnosis was a foreign body (toothbrush head) in left tonsil, established through computed tomography, medical history, and surgery. The foreign body was detected and removed from the oropharynx under general anesthesia. The patient recovered uneventfully during the 2-week follow-up.ConclusionsAccurate diagnosis of pharyngeal foreign body is the key to successful treatment. For patients suspected of chronic foreign body ingestion, it is imperative to comprehensively assess the history, physical examination, and imaging. Surgery is recommended for complicated cases of embedded pharyngeal foreign bodies.

Introduction

Pharyngeal foreign bodies are commonly encountered in clinical practice for otolaryngologists. They are usually present in the palatine tonsil, tongue base and pyriform sinus [1]. Foreign bodies such as fish bones and other animal bones are common types and can often be easily diagnosed with ingestion history and inspection with or without the help of instruments [2]. However, in some cases, foreign bodies are embedded in the pharynx and cannot be identified, increasing the difficulty of diagnosis and treatment [3]. In these circumstances, complications can occur and some patients need surgery. We report a case of a toothbrush head embedded in the palatine tonsil that resulted in prolonged inflammation for 1 month and delayed diagnosis. To date, such an occurrence has been scarcely documented in the medical literature. The foreign body was detected and removed from the oropharynx under general anesthesia. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Peking University First Hospital and informed consent was obtained from this patient for the publication of this case report.

Case presentation

A 53-year-old Han male presented to a local hospital with the complaint of foreign body sensation of throat for 1 month, which worsened during swallowing without dysphagia. He denied sore throat, fever, reflux, heartburn, dyspnea and other discomfort. The symptoms gradually worsened; consequently, the patient presented to the Department of Otolaryngology for further evaluation and management. The patient was in good health without any history of chronic disease. The clinicians noticed that the left tonsil was displaced to the midline (Fig. 1A) and suspected peritonsillitis or tonsil tumor. Routine laboratory examination revealed a normal blood count and white blood cell count. Computed tomography (CT) demonstrated an enlarged left tonsil with a high-density shadow in the center (Fig. 1B, C). They asked for the consultation of our department. We suspected a foreign body embedded in the left palatine tonsil, instead of peritonsillitis or tonsil tumor. The patient recalled carefully and claimed he broke his toothbrush when brushing his teeth 1 month ago. The patient was referred to our department for exploratory surgery. Radiofrequency ablation assisted partial tonsillectomy was performed to identify and remove the foreign body under general anesthesia (Fig. 2A, B). The foreign body turned out to be a toothbrush head and was removed, measuring 4.3 cm in length (Fig. 2C). Purulent secretion and granulation tissue could be found around the toothbrush head. The wound was thoroughly irrigated with normal saline and sutured. The patient made an uneventful recovery and was discharged 4 days later. Discussion

Pharyngeal foreign body impaction represents a prevalent clinical entity within the field of otolaryngology. Most cases with foreign body ingestion occur in children, elderly people or adults with mental disorders and other physical impairments [4]. Sharp and irregular foreign bodies often get stuck in the tonsils, tongue base, vallecula and pyriform sinus. Most patients with pharyngeal foreign bodies come to the hospital within 24 h due to unpleasant experiences with a definite history of foreign bodies and symptoms, including sore throat, odynophagia, dysphagia and even choking [5]. Most foreign bodies can spontaneously pass through the digestive tract and less than 1% cases require surgery [6]. Unlike with acute patients, the diagnosis of foreign body ingestion is often challenging, because patients are frequently unaware of ingesting foreign bodies, and the clinical manifestations exhibit a broad spectrum, ranging from complete asymptomaticity to nonspecific symptoms, such as localized pain, nausea, or fever. Consequently, the absence of obvious symptoms can delay diagnosis and even cause serious complications [7].

Clinicians can easily identify most pharyngeal foreign bodies with careful examination. Foreign bodies chronically embedded within the palatine tonsils or pharyngeal walls present a significant diagnostic challenge; consequently, cases involving a treatment delay exceeding 1 month are exceptionally rare [7]. Unilateral enlarged tonsil in this patient may result from peritonsillitis, peritonsillar abscess, parapharyngeal space tumor, or benign/malignant tonsil tumor; pharyngeal foreign bodies are not a main concern. Under this condition, CT contributes to the identification and localization of foreign bodies which is effective in diagnosis with high sensitivity and specificity, especially in the cases of embedded pharyngeal foreign bodies [5]. Accurate diagnosis is the key to targeted treatment. An ingested foreign body may pierce tissues, and migrate to peritonsillar space and other deeper spaces, leading to an increased risk of complications [8]. Foreign bodies should be removed timely upon diagnosis to avoid complications [9].

In the present case, the patient who scored 28 on the mini-mental status exam was unaware of the foreign body intake at the initial visit to the local hospital. Meanwhile, the enlarged tonsil also complicated the diagnosis, so doctors initially suspected a tumor. We reviewed the patient’s CT images during a consultation and suspected an oropharyngeal foreign body. We confirmed this finding after checking the patient’s ingestion history and performing surgical exploration. The patient’s condition had persisted for 1 month, so we recommended surgical treatment following the diagnosis. We identified and removed the 4.3 cm toothbrush head after partial tonsil resection under general anesthesia without severe complications. Such a large embedded pharyngeal foreign body is extremely rare. Sometimes, when the foreign body is small, it can be more difficult and challenging to remove the pharyngeal foreign body because of the difficulty of localization.

Based on our experience, some suggestions should be taken into consideration for foreign body investigation. First, the chief complaint, food consumption and other possible contributory factors should be comprehensively investigated. Second, the clinician need proficient CT reading skills, as accurate diagnosis underpins successful treatment. For patients suspected of chronic foreign body ingestion, detailed medical history and thorough CT review are vital, and treatment results confirm diagnosis accuracy. Surgery is the primary choice for complicated embedded pharyngeal foreign body cases.

Conclusion

It is difficult to make the correct diagnosis for asymptomatic embedded pharyngeal foreign bodies, especially those that have been forgotten for a long time. It is imperative to comprehensively assess the patient’s history and imaging. Surgery is recommended as the first option for complicated cases of embedded pharyngeal foreign bodies.
