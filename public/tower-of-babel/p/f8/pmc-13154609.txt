Web-based education enhances self-care agency, self-efficacy, and quality of life in patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Esra Nur Boztaş, Serap Sayar, Ferhat Sayar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTotal knee arthroplasty plays a pivotal role in restoring patients’ quality of life; however, challenges related to postoperative adaptation and self-care remain significant. Integrating technology into patient education through web-based and mobile application-supported interventions has emerged as an innovative approach to strengthen recovery and enhance patient outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of a web-based education (WBE) program on self-care power, self-efficacy, and quality of life in patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty.MethodsThis prospective, two-group clinical trial was conducted between January and October 2024 at the Orthopedics and Traumatology Clinic of a city hospital. A total of 67 patients were assigned to either the WBE group (n = 33) or the control group (n = 34). The control group received standard unstructured discharge education, whereas the WBE group received the same education plus a one-month web-based education program using a mobile application starting on the 15th day post-discharge. Data were collected via the Self-Care Agency Scale (SCAS), the Self-Efficacy Scale (SES), and the Short Form-36 Health Survey (SF-36) at 15 days and one-month post-discharge.ResultsNo significant baseline differences were observed between the WBE and control groups (p > 0.05). After the intervention, the WBE group showed significantly greater improvements in self-care agency, self-efficacy, and quality of life compared with the control group (p < 0.05). Mixed-design ANOVA demonstrated significant group×time interaction effects for all outcomes, with large effects for self-care agency (ηp²=0.703–0.827) and moderate to large effects for self-efficacy (ηp² = 0.435–0.719) and SF-36 quality-of-life subdimensions (ηp²=0.451–0.713).ConclusionWeb-designed education via mobile applications was shown to be effective in improving the self-care power and self-efficacy and increasing the quality of life of patients undergoing TKA.Trial registrationThis study was registered in the ClinicalTrials.gov database as (NCT06306027) Protocol Registration and Results System.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06779-3.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a prevalent and effective surgical intervention for advanced knee osteoarthritis that significantly improves patient mobility and quality of life [1]. Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) average for knee replacement surgery is 119 per 100,000 people [2]. Although TKA is a widely adopted and highly successful surgical procedure, it may still lead to a variety of postoperative complications [3]. Postoperatively, patients may develop complications that necessitate hospital readmission, including venous thromboembolism, bleeding, wound problems, vascular or nerve injury, instability, periprosthetic joint infection, periprosthetic fracture, dislocation, joint stiffness, implant fracture, and implant loosening [3–5]. However, following knee arthroplasty, patients are most affected in terms of mobility, and regaining their previous level of independence may take time. Consequently, patients often require support and guidance to readjust to daily life [6, 7]. A study conducted by Aamir Mahdi et al. [8] reported that following TKA, patients experienced pain, kinesiophobia, swelling, and a reduced range of motion, which in turn negatively affected their self-care abilities and led to diminished self-care capacity. Patients who experience difficulties in performing activities of daily living exhibit reduced self-efficacy as a consequence of diminished self-care capacity [9]. Tanuguchi et al. [10] reported that patients with higher levels of self-efficacy were able to perform mobility activities more comfortably and effectively during the rehabilitation process.

Severe pain, swelling, and restricted mobility can significantly impair patients’ quality of life [11–13]. Although TKA has been shown to significantly improve health-related quality of life in the long term [14, 15], patients may experience a temporary decline in self-care capacity, self-efficacy, and quality of life during the early postoperative period due to pain, functional limitations, and increased dependence on assistance. This transitional phase highlights the importance of supportive interventions aimed at facilitating recovery and adaptation in the immediate post-discharge period.

The postoperative recovery period necessitates comprehensive patient education to optimize functional outcomes and ensure successful rehabilitation [16]. The role of healthcare professionals in patient education and counseling is critical for optimizing postoperative outcomes and facilitating patient adherence to the recovery process [9]. With advances in information technology, patient education has increasingly transitioned to web-based platforms accessible via smartphones, tablets, and computers. Web-based education (WBE) has been shown to be effective in enhancing patient knowledge, promoting behavior change, and facilitating the acquisition of new skills [17, 18]. WBE programs have been shown to be effective in patients undergoing orthopedic procedures such as meniscectomy, knee arthroscopy, and anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, as well as in other surgical populations [19–23]. However, research on patients undergoing TKA remains limited. Despite the use of web-based education programs in the care of patients undergoing TKA, existing studies have predominantly focused on the effects of such programs on outcomes such as knowledge enhancement, pain management, changes in joint range of motion, patient satisfaction, postoperative complications, and knee functional performance [24–26]. However, particularly during the critical postoperative recovery period in which patients are required to actively manage their own care at home following discharge, concepts such as self-care agency and perceived self-efficacy have been insufficiently addressed. Furthermore, to date, no study has been identified that simultaneously examines self-care agency, self-efficacy, and quality of life within the context of a structured, nurse-led web-based education program. Nevertheless, effective self-care behaviors and self-efficacy play a central role in successful rehabilitation and long-term recovery following total knee arthroplasty. Enhancing self-care ability, supporting self-efficacy, and improving quality of life are critical for successful rehabilitation after major orthopedic surgery, making it essential to investigate the impact of WBE in this population. The increasing volume of TKA procedures further highlights the need for scalable and engaging patient education platforms to manage the perioperative period effectively [27]. In addition, WBE allows healthcare professionals and nurses to monitor patients post discharge, contributing to both the sustainability of care and innovation in healthcare services.

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the effects of a WBE program on self-care agency, perceived self-efficacy, and quality of life in patients undergoing TKA. It was hypothesized that patients receiving the WBE program would demonstrate greater self-care agency (H1a), higher self-efficacy (H1b), and improved quality of life (H1c) compared with patients receiving standard postoperative care.

Methods

Study design

The study employed a two-group (WBE and control), two-time-point (pretest and posttest) design. This prospective, two-arm, randomized controlled clinical trial was conducted between January and October 2024 at the orthopedics and traumatology outpatient clinic of a city hospital in the Central Anatolia region of Turkey, and it has been registered at ClinicalTrials.gov under the protocol identifier NCT06306027.

Study population and sample size

Participants were eligible if they were 40 years and older; literate; underwent TKA for the first time, had internet access via a smartphone, tablet, or computer, possessed basic smartphone skills, and had no cognitive or neurological impairments. The exclusion criteria included unwillingness to participate, undergoing bilateral TKA, or the development of complications or deterioration in the general condition during the study.

The sample size was calculated via G*Power 3.1.9.2 on the basis of Cohen’s (1988) standardized effect size. Assuming α = 0.05 and β = 0.20 (80% power) and using repeated-measures ANOVA with a medium effect size (0.25), 28 patients per group were needed. The sample size calculation was designed to detect an overall intervention effect across the main outcome measures rather than being based on a single predefined primary outcome. Considering a potential 20% drop-out rate, the target sample size was adjusted to 35 patients per group. Randomization and Blinding

The participants were assigned to the WBE or control group via block randomization with four-patient permutations generated via a lottery. Group A was designated WBE, and group B was the control. Numbers 1–6 were randomly sequenced 17 times via https://www.randomizer.org. Eligible patients who provided informed consent were allocated by a researcher independent of the one implementing the intervention (F.S.), ensuring allocation concealment. The researcher (E.N.B.) implementing the intervention could not be blinded because of active involvement in installation, introduction, and data collection, but blinding was maintained for the statistician during analysis.

Instruments

Personal information form

This form was developed by the researchers and included a total of 11 questions covering sociodemographic and descriptive characteristics.

Self-care agency scale (SCAS)

The Self-Care Agency Scale (SCAS) assesses an individual’s ability to care for themselves. The Turkish version [28] includes 35 items on a five-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 0 to 140; higher scores indicate greater self-care ability. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.92 in the validation study, 0.95 in the pretest and 0.98 in the posttest in the current study.

Self-efficacy scale (SES)

The self-efficacy scale (SES) assesses perceived self-efficacy and behavioral change. The Turkish version [29] includes 23 items on a Likert scale, with scores ranging from 23 to 115; higher scores indicate greater self-efficacy. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.81 in the validation study, 0.91 in the pretest and 0.95 in the posttest in the current study.

Short form-36 health survey (sf-36)

The SF-36 Health Survey developed by Ware [30] assesses quality of life across eight domains: physical functioning, role-physical, role-emotional, bodily pain, mental health, vitality, social functioning, and general health perception. The Turkish version [31] reported Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.73–0.76. Scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better quality of life. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha ranged from 0.63 to 0.99 at the pretest and 0.83–0.93 at the posttest.

Intervention

Development of the WBE program

The WBE program, named “My Knee Health”, was developed by researchers on the basis of the literature [9, 12, 26, 32–34]. The literature review was conducted to determine the scope and components of the educational content, while the web-based platform was selected as the delivery method. Content validity was evaluated by seven experts, yielding a Content Validity Index of 0.98, indicating excellent agreement [35]. The material was further assessed with the DISCERN (Quality Criteria for Consumer Health Information) instrument, scoring 75/80, which reflects high quality. The validated content was subsequently integrated into a mobile application by a software developer.

The “My Knee Health” application consists of seven main sections: user information, general information (knee structure, joint function, and prosthesis-related basics), potential complications, daily tips, educational content (e.g., pain management, nutrition, exercise, and home modifications), surveys and scales, and communication with researchers (Fig. 2). Implementation of the study

Participants randomized to the WBE group received the “My Knee Health” mobile application with guided educational content and audio support, were instructed to complete it within one month, and their engagement and completion were monitored via an administrative panel with reminders and phone follow-ups, whereas control group patients received routine care, treatment, and unstructured discharge education as part of the clinical routine.

Measurements

Baseline (pretest) assessments were conducted 15 days after discharge using the Personal Information Form, Self-Care Agency Scale (SCAS), Self-Efficacy Scale (SES), and Short Form Health Survey (SF-36). Posttest (follow-up) assessments were conducted four weeks after the pretest using the SCAS, SES, and SF-36. No additional follow-up measurements were performed.

Data analyses

The data were analyzed via IBM SPSS Statistics Standard Concurrent User version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Skewness and kurtosis values were examined to assess the normality of the variables, confirming that the data were normally distributed. Independent sample t tests were used to compare continuous descriptive characteristics between groups, whereas categorical variables were compared via chi-square tests. To evaluate changes over time between the two groups (WBE and control), a 2 (group) × 2 (time: pretest and posttest) mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. When a significant interaction was observed, between-group comparisons at each time point were conducted using independent-samples t tests, and within-group changes from pretest to posttest were examined using paired-samples t tests. A p value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Minimal Clinically İmportant Difference (MCID) values were calculated specifically for the present study using a distribution-based approach based on the variability observed in the study sample. This approach was adopted because universally established MCID thresholds were not available for several of the outcome measures. Study-specific MCID values are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

No significant differences were found when the WBE and control groups were compared on the basis of sociodemographic characteristics (Table 1).

There was no statistically significant difference in SCAS scores between the WBE and control groups prior to the intervention (p > 0.05). In the WBE group, substantial increases were observed in all SCAS subdimensions and in the total SCAS score following the intervention (within-group t = − 13.69 to − 23.34, p < 0.001), while scores in the control group remained largely unchanged. At posttest, participants in the WBE group achieved markedly higher SCAS scores than those in the control group (t = 10.29–11.83, p < 0.001), accompanied by large effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 2.50–2.87). Results of the mixed-design ANOVA further confirmed a pronounced group × time interaction across all self-care agency outcomes (F = 156.55–314.96, p < 0.001). The strength of these effects was considerable, as reflected by large partial eta squared values (ηp² = 0.703–0.827), underscoring the clinically substantial impact of the web-based education program on patients’ self-care agency (Table 2).

There were no significant differences in SES scores between the WBE and control groups at baseline (p > 0.05). Following the intervention, the WBE group showed significant improvements across all SES subdimensions (within-group t = − 11.83 to − 14.69, p < 0.001), whereas no significant changes were observed in the control group. At posttest, SES scores were significantly higher in the WBE group compared with the control group (t = 6.22–8.62, p < 0.001), with large effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 1.51–2.09). Mixed-design ANOVA demonstrated significant group × time interaction effects for all outcomes (F = 50.91–168.81, p < 0.001). The XXXagnitüde of these interaction effects was moderate to large (ηp² = 0.435–0.719), indicating that the web-based education intervention produced clinically meaningful and robust improvements in self-efficacy (Table 3).

Discussion

Effective patient education is a critical component of perioperative care for individuals undergoing TKA, as it can influence recovery, self-management, and overall well-being. This study investigated the effects of a WBE program delivered via a mobile application on self-care agency, self-efficacy, and quality of life in TKA patients. The findings demonstrate that participation in the WBE program positively impacted patients’ self-care agency, enhanced their self-efficacy, and improved their overall quality of life. These results underscore the potential of digital educational interventions to support patients in acquiring essential knowledge, facilitating behavioral change, and making informed health-related decisions during the perioperative period. In this context, the age profile of the study population provides important insight into the interpretation of these findings. An important finding of this study is that the majority of participants were older than 50 years, an age group that generally has less lifelong exposure to digital technologies. Despite this, patients demonstrated high engagement with and adherence to the app-based web education program, suggesting that age alone should not be considered a barrier to digital health interventions. This may be attributed to the user-friendly design of the application, structured educational content, audio support, and continuous guidance provided during the intervention period. These findings demonstrate that, when appropriate support and design principles are applied, older adults are capable of effectively using digital health technologies. Therefore, web-based educational interventions may be both feasible and effective even in older orthopedic patient populations.

In this study, no significant differences were observed between the WBE and control groups in the pretest self-care agency scores (p > 0.05); however, at the posttest, patients who participated in the WBE program demonstrated a significant increase in both the total and subscale scores compared with those in the control group (p < 0.05). This finding aligns with the literature indicating that mobile- and web-based educational interventions can enhance patients’ self-care abilities. Timmers et al. [36] reported that a mobile-based education program following TKA improved patients’ ability to perform self-care activities after discharge. Similarly, Umapathy et al. [37] reported that a 12-month web-based program significantly enhanced self-care, self-management, and lifestyle behaviors in patients with knee or hip osteoarthritis. Li et al. [38] also demonstrated that a continuous web-based education program positively affected knee function, sleep quality, negative emotions, and self-care agency. In addition to these literature findings, our study provides a novel perspective on the clinical meaningfulness of improvement in self-care agency. Although no universal Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) threshold exists for the Self-Care Agency Scale in the literature, our data revealed a striking contrast between the groups (Supplementary Table S1). The control group showed a mean change of only 1.13 points (95% CI: −0.79 to 3.06), indicating no clinically or statistically significant improvement with standard care alone. In contrast, the WBE group achieved a mean change of 26.22 points (95% CI: 23.60–28.84). This substantial increase, well beyond the margins of measurement error observed in the control group, suggests that the WBE intervention generates a robust and clinically meaningful enhancement in patients’ capacity to care for themselves. From a clinical perspective, these results suggest that maintaining continuity of patient education through web-based platforms after discharge may play a critical role in supporting patients’ adaptation to the postoperative period and promoting effective self-care at home. In contrast, patients receiving only standard care may experience difficulties with exercise adherence, pain management, and daily activities, potentially due to fear of prosthesis damage or falls. The scalability and accessibility of web-based education programs support their integration into routine postoperative care, particularly in settings with limited clinical resources. Future research should explore the long-term sustainability of these effects, assess cost-effectiveness, and examine the applicability of web-based education across different surgical populations and healthcare contexts. Patients’ self-efficacy is critical for initiating and maintaining positive health behaviors after TKA [39]. Continuity of education after discharge enhances self-efficacy, facilitating adaptation to the postoperative routine and improving knee functional outcomes [40]. In this study, patients receiving WBE demonstrated significant improvements in both total and subscale self-efficacy scores compared with those in the control group (p < 0.05), indicating that WBE effectively enhances confidence in managing rehabilitation. Baxter et al. [41] reported that, compared with face-to-face education, tele-based education programs improved self-efficacy in high-risk TKA patients. Similarly, Wang et al. [42] and Zhao et al. [43] reported that mobile- or internet-based rehabilitation programs were effective at increasing patients’ self-efficacy and promoting active engagement in the rehabilitation process. It is crucial to highlight the clinical meaningfulness of these findings by examining the magnitude of change, particularly given the lack of established MCID values for the Self-Efficacy Scale in this population. Our data revealed a critical disparity (Supplementary Table S1): the control group exhibited a mean change of −0.52 (95% CI: −2.26 to 1.22), indicating that without structured support, patients’ confidence in their ability to manage care did not improve and even showed a tendency toward regression. In stark contrast, the WBE group demonstrated a substantial mean increase of 17.44 points (95% CI: 15.26–19.61). This distinct separation between the groups confirms that the improvement in self-efficacy is not a natural outcome of the postoperative period but is directly attributable to the WBE intervention, representing a clinically significant gain in patient empowerment. These findings support that WBE is an effective and reliable tool for improving self-efficacy and can be integrated into clinical patient education strategies. Clinically, improved self-efficacy may contribute to greater adherence to exercise programs, more effective pain coping strategies, and enhanced patient autonomy during recovery, underscoring the value of WBE as a complementary component of postoperative care.

At baseline, no significant differences were observed between the control and WBE groups across the SF-36 subscales (p > 0.05). Postintervention, the WBE group demonstrated significantly higher scores, reflecting meaningful improvements in quality of life compared with the control group (p < 0.05). These findings are consistent with those of previous studies [44–47] and support the effectiveness of video-, web-, and mobile-based educational interventions in providing patients with comprehensive postoperative information, pain management strategies, exercise guidance, and care instructions, thereby increasing their quality of life. Continuous access to educational content may facilitate stress and anxiety management, promote early mobilization, and support independence in daily activities. From a clinical perspective, these improvements in quality of life are particularly relevant, as they reflect patients’ overall well-being and functional recovery during the postoperative period. Integrating web-based education into routine postoperative care may therefore contribute to a more patient-centered rehabilitation process and improved recovery experiences for individuals undergoing TKA. To further contextualize these clinically relevant improvements, the findings were evaluated against established MCID thresholds reported in the literature. The improvements observed in quality-of-life outcomes in the present study were interpreted in relation to previously reported findings and MCID thresholds (Supplementary Table S1). Overall, the magnitude of change exceeded the MCID values reported by Clement et al. [48] and was consistent with the higher change ranges described by Keurentjes et al. [49], indicating clinically meaningful improvements. Consistent with prior reports, high scores and ceiling effects in the Role Emotional and Social Functioning subdimensions of the SF-36 aligned with the findings of Clement et al. [48], who noted that ceiling effects may occur in these domains during the postoperative period, whereas such effects were not observed in the Physical Functioning subdimension. When evaluated against the MCID thresholds defined by Clement et al. [48], the control group also exceeded MCID thresholds in several physical subdimensions as a natural consequence of surgery, including Physical Functioning (change: 21.01 vs. MCID: 3.9), Pain (6.76 vs. 6.4), and Role Physical (5.48 vs. 4.0); however, these gains remained close to the respective cutoffs. In contrast, the WBE group demonstrated substantially greater improvements, exceeding MCID thresholds by wide margins in Physical Functioning (36.09) and Pain (22.85), with similarly pronounced effects across other physical subdimensions. Moreover, while the control group failed to reach MCID thresholds in several psychosocial and general health subdimensions General Health (0.91 vs. 5.3), Mental Health (2.86 vs. 4.4), and Vitality (2.28 vs. 2.9) the WBE group exceeded MCID values across all subdimensions (e.g., General Health: 13.67). Collectively, these findings suggest that although surgery alone may yield clinically meaningful physical improvements, structured and continuous educational interventions such as web-based education are essential for achieving clinically relevant gains in overall well-being, energy levels, and mental health beyond standard care alone.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that a WBE program significantly improved self-care agency, self-efficacy, and quality of life in patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty. Patients in the WBE group achieved higher scores across all outcomes than did those in the control group did, indicating that the “My Knee Health” mobile application is an effective intervention. The findings suggest that integrating technology-assisted education into postoperative care may provide patients with continuous and comprehensive guidance, thereby facilitating pain management, promoting early mobilization, and enhancing independence in daily activities. Further studies should investigate the long-term effects of WBE interventions across diverse clinical settings and age groups and develop strategies to improve engagement among elderly patients or those with limited technological literacy to further optimize rehabilitation outcomes.

Limitations

The limitations of this study include its single-center design, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings and demographic groups, and the assessment of outcomes only during the short-term postoperative period, without evaluating long-term effects. However, this time frame was intentionally selected to focus on the early post-discharge period, when patients experience rapid recovery, increased care needs, and a critical adaptation process following TKA. Because the first 6 months after TKA represent a phase of rapid recovery and frequent patient needs, it remains unclear whether the effects of the web-based education program are sustained over time. Future studies should therefore include longer follow-up assessments, such as evaluations at 6 months or later. In addition, multicenter studies with larger and more diverse samples are warranted to improve the external validity of the findings. Furthermore, the use of mobile applications requires smartphone and internet access, limiting the inclusion of economically disadvantaged participants. Given the advanced age of some patients, there is also a need to provide training to caregivers or family members to ensure proper use of the application. Future research may also consider hybrid or alternative educational formats to improve accessibility among patients with limited digital resources.
