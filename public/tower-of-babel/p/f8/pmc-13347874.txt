The world’s longest running civil war and chronic childhood undernutrition in Myanmar: a propensity score matched analysis
Authors: Dhiman Bhadra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildhood undernutrition remains a major public health challenge in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in settings affected by armed conflict and fragility. Prolonged civil conflict disrupts livelihoods, food systems and service delivery, yet its nutritional consequences remain poorly quantified. This study assesses the association between regional exposure to armed conflict and chronic childhood undernutrition in Myanmar.MethodsWe linked nationally representative data obtained from the 2015–16 Myanmar Demographic and Health Survey with conflict event records from the Uppsala Conflict Data Program (2010–2015). Administrative regions were classified as conflict-affected or conflict-insulated based on the spatio-temporal frequency of conflict-related fatalities. Using a propensity score matching (PSM) approach, we estimated the association between conflict exposure and stunting (height-for-age Z-score < -2 SD) among a sample of 3,792 children matched on the basis of various demographic, socioeconomic and environmental attributes using the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT). Robustness checks across alternative matching algorithms and covariate exposure classification were carried out as well as sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding.ResultsThe estimated ATT was 0.045 (95% bootstrap CI: 0.005–0.083), corresponding to a 4.5 percentage point higher prevalence of stunting among children residing in conflict-affected regions compared with matched children from conflict-insulated regions. As a secondary analysis, post-matching multivariable logistic regression yielded a 28% higher odds of stunting for the former group (95% CI 1.10-1.49). Exploratory subgroup analysis suggested more pronounced associations among children of mothers with no education or employment, from poorer households and without access to improved sanitation. Results were robust to alternative propensity score modeling frameworks and moderate levels of unmeasured confounding.ConclusionsProtracted armed conflict is strongly associated with child undernutrition in Myanmar, compounding existing inequalities and undermining progress toward national and global nutrition targets. Thus, strengthening conflict-sensitive nutrition programming, community-based service delivery and health system resilience is essential to protect vulnerable children in fragile and conflict-affected contexts as in Myanmar.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01341-7.

Introduction

Childhood undernutrition remains a persistent public health challenge in low and middle-income countries (LMICs), where progress towards Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) has been uneven. Among the various manifestations of undernutrition, childhood stunting - a key marker of chronic nutritional deprivation during key growth periods - continues to impede survival, cognitive and physical development, school attainment and long-term productivity of an individual [1–3]. Despite global declines, approximately 148 million children under five remain stunted, with the highest burdens in sub-Saharan Africa and South and Southeast Asia [4]. Some of the highest incidences of childhood stunting are witnessed in fragile and conflict-affected settings where nearly one in three children suffer from it [5, 6].

A growing body of evidence shows that armed conflict functions as a structural determinant of health and nutrition [7–9]. Conflicts undermine food systems, constrain agricultural production, impede health, sanitation and nutrition services and result in large-scale displacement of vulnerable populations [10–12]. The resulting instability perpetuates poverty, food insecurity and psychosocial stress, contributing to poor feeding practices and nutritional deficits for the mother and child [13, 14]. While the aforementioned pathways provide a conceptual framework for understanding the relationship between armed conflict and child undernutrition, the present study focuses on estimating overall associations and adjusts for observed demographic, socioeconomic and environmental characteristics that partially proxy these mechanisms, rather than directly estimating specific causal pathways.

Empirical quantification of the association between armed conflict and childhood undernutrition is often overlooked in the context of Southeast Asia. Myanmar offers an important case for such analysis. It is the second largest country in Southeast Asia as well as one of the poorest. It is administratively divided into seven regions, seven states and one union territory which houses the national capital, Nay Pyi Taw. Myanmar has experienced one of the world’s longest-running civil conflicts since its independence in 1948, with hostilities largely concentrated in ethnic minority-dominated regions [15–18]. Decades of protracted civil conflict have affected large segments of the minority population, with recurring episodes of violence and displacement. Despite intermittent periods of stability and socioeconomic development, food insecurity and childhood undernutrition have remained persistently high and have worsened post-military takeover in 2021 [19]. The 2015–16 Myanmar Demographic and Health Survey (MDHS) estimated that 29% of children under five were stunted, one of the highest rates in the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) [20]. As of 2025, the cumulative impact of this conflict has pushed a staggering 16.7 million people, amounting to nearly one-third of its population, into the brink of acute food insecurity, hunger and starvation. Although Myanmar has experienced a substantial escalation in conflict and humanitarian need following the military takeover in 2021, the present study focuses on conflict exposure during the period preceding the 2015–16 Myanmar Demographic and Health Survey. This temporal distinction is important, as the analysis reflects patterns of conflict and undernutrition prior to the recent escalation.

Stunting is a core indicator used by governments and international agencies to track chronic undernutrition, benchmark progress against global and national nutrition targets, and guide programmatic prioritization. While a growing body of research has examined the relationship between armed conflict and child undernutrition in other conflict-affected settings, including parts of sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia [21–28], comparable population-level evidence for Myanmar remains limited. The current study attempts to address this gap by investigating the association between regional exposure to armed conflict and chronic childhood undernutrition in Myanmar using nationally representative data obtained from the 2015-16 Myanmar Demographic Health Survey. To facilitate this comparison, regions were categorized as conflict-affected or conflict-insulated based on patterns of conflict exposure during the study period. A propensity score-matching (PSM) approach [29–34] was used to estimate the association between living in a conflict-affected region and childhood stunting among comparable groups of children while adjusting for demographic, maternal and environmental covariates. This approach provides population-level insights into the conflict–undernutrition relationship in a setting characterized by protracted and geographically diffuse violence.

This study contributes to global and regional evidence on the health and nutrition consequences of fragility and conflict primarily in four ways. First, it quantifies the nutritional cost of prolonged conflict using rigorous counterfactual methods applied to large-scale survey data. Second, it identifies social and environmental factors that amplify children’s vulnerability to adverse health outcomes of conflict exposure, such as maternal education, employment and sanitation access. Third, the study demonstrates a transparent and adaptable analytical framework for integrating conflict analytics into population-health surveillance using widely accessible data sources in low- and middle-income countries. Finally, this study contributes to the broader discourse on social determinants of health by empirically examining armed conflict as a structural determinant of childhood undernutrition in a protracted conflict setting. We hope that the findings from this study will be relevant for national policymakers, humanitarian organizations and global actors seeking to safeguard maternal and child health in fragile and conflict-affected contexts.

Methods

This study uses nationally representative data from the 2015–16 Myanmar Demographic and Health Survey [20] linked with geocoded conflict event data from the Uppsala Conflict Data Program (UCDP-GED)[35, 36]. The conflict data covers the period 2010-2015 corresponding to the five-year age range of children included in the survey. Given the cross-sectional nature of the MDHS and the protracted, geographically diffuse character of armed conflict in Myanmar, exposure was operationalized at the first administrative level (states and regions) to capture sustained conflict environments rather than precise timing of individual exposure. A propensity score matching (PSM) approach was employed to construct comparable groups of children residing in conflict-affected and conflict-insulated regions based on observed demographic, socioeconomic, and environmental characteristics. The Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT), defined as the difference in stunting prevalence between children residing in conflict-affected regions and their matched counterparts in conflict-insulated regions, was estimated as the primary measure of association, followed by post-matching regression analyses. We assess the stability of the estimated ATT through complementary diagnostic analyses. First, robustness checks evaluate how the estimates change under alternative propensity score specifications and exposure thresholds. Second, a formal sensitivity analysis using E-values quantifies the strength of unmeasured confounding that would be required to fully explain the observed association.

Myanmar demographic health survey

Data on child anthropometry and household characteristics were obtained from the 2015–16 Myanmar Demographic and Health Survey (MDHS), a nationally representative household survey conducted by the Ministry of Health and Sports in collaboration with ICF International [20]. The 2015-16 MDHS is the only nationally representative Demographic and Health Survey conducted in Myanmar to date and therefore represents the most recent available population-level dataset for examining childhood undernutrition in this context. The MDHS employed a stratified, two-stage cluster sampling design. The children’s recode file contained information on 4,815 children born within five years preceding the survey. Of these, 621 observations were excluded due to missing or implausible values on key variables, including height-for-age Z-scores and covariates used in the matching procedure. The final analysis-ready sample therefore consisted of 4,194 children aged 0–59 months with complete information on the relevant variables. Standard DHS data cleaning procedures were followed, including exclusion of biologically implausible anthropometric values based on World Health Organization child growth standards [37, 38] and DHS data quality guidelines [20]. Height-for-age Z-scores were calculated using the WHO child growth standards [37] and stunting was defined as a Z-score below −2 standard deviations from the reference median.

Survey design and weighting

The Myanmar Demographic and Health Survey employs a complex sampling design involving stratification, clustering and unequal selection probabilities. To account for this design, survey sampling weights were incorporated at all stages of the analysis. Specifically, DHS sampling weights were used in the estimation of propensity scores, in weighted t-tests for assessing covariate balance before and after matching, in evaluating ATT and in post-matching regression analyses. Incorporating survey weights helps account for differential sampling probabilities and ensures that comparisons reflect the underlying survey design. 95% confidence intervals for the primary ATT estimates were obtained using bootstrap resampling of the matched, weighted sample.

The conflict data used in this study is sourced from the Uppsala Conflict Data Program (UCDP) which contains data on more than 100,000 conflict events around the world since 1989 [35, 36]. It provides granular information on each conflict, including dates of occurrence, opposing parties, number of fatalities and geo-spatial coordinates. This enables us to examine the spatio-temporal distribution and magnitude of conflict events and fatalities across regions. Covariates of interest

Conflict exposure was defined as a binary indicator at the regional level of Myanmar, indicating whether a child hails from a conflict-affected region or not. Based on tabulated information on conflict events and fatalities from the UCDP during 2010–2015 (Table 1), regions were classified as conflict-affected if cumulative conflict-related fatalities exceeded 20 during the 2010–2015 period. This cutoff was selected to distinguish regions experiencing sustained and recurrent conflict from those with negligible or sporadic violence while maintaining sufficient contrast between exposure groups. The choice is informed by the empirical distribution of fatalities across regions (Table 1), where a small subset of regions account for substantially higher fatality counts and are widely recognized as epicenters of protracted conflict. The 20-fatality threshold represents the lowest value that meaningfully separates sustained conflict environments from minimal-violence regions while preserving statistical power. This approach therefore ensures consistency with established geographic understandings of Myanmar’s conflict landscape while providing a transparent quantitative rule for exposure classification.

Covariates included in the propensity score model were selected based on established conceptual frameworks and empirical evidence linking child, maternal, and household determinants to childhood undernutrition [1–3, 39, 40], as well as literature documenting the pathways through which armed conflict may affect both socioeconomic conditions and child health outcomes [9, 21, 24]. Specifically, households located in conflict-affected and conflict-insulated regions were matched based on the following baseline covariates: child-level characteristics (age, sex, birth order and recent diarrheal illness, defined as an episode of diarrhea in the two weeks preceding the survey interview), maternal characteristics (age at first birth, educational attainment, employment status, body mass index and short stature (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 145$$\end{document}cm)) and household characteristics (number of children under five, place of residence, sex of household head, wealth quintile, sanitation facility and access to on-premise drinking water). Recent diarrheal illness was included as a proxy for short-term infectious disease burden, which is a well-established determinant of child nutritional status and growth outcomes in the literature [41–43]. However, we acknowledge that this measure captures illness within a limited recall period and does not reflect cumulative exposure to diarrheal disease over the child’s life course. Detailed definitions and categorizations of all variables are provided in Table S2 (Supplementary Materials). These variables were considered potential confounders of the association between conflict exposure and childhood stunting because they are established determinants of child growth and may be systematically distributed across regions with differing levels of conflict exposure. Adjusting for these characteristics helps account for observable socioeconomic and demographic differences between conflict-affected and conflict-insulated regions. In the Myanmar context, this adjustment is particularly important because longstanding ethnic tensions and conflict dynamics have been closely intertwined with patterns of economic marginalization and uneven social development across minority-dominated regions [17, 18]. Regions that later became conflict-affected have historically exhibited lower levels of household wealth, maternal education, and access to sanitation and water, as well as poorer maternal and child health indicators. These structural differences may predate or coexist with conflict exposure and are also strongly associated with childhood nutritional outcomes. Accounting for these characteristics therefore helps reduce bias arising from pre-existing regional disparities. Unless otherwise stated, all variable definitions and categorizations follow standard Myanmar Demographic and Health Survey (MDHS) conventions [20].

Outcome and estimand

The outcome variable is a binary stunting indicator with categories “stunted” and “not stunted” respectively. These categories are based on anthropometric measures of a child’s height with respect to age, as quantified by the height-for-age Z score (HAZ). Specifically, children whose standardized HAZ score is below –2 standard deviations from the World Health Organization child growth standards median [44] are labelled as chronically undernourished or stunted. While continuous HAZ captures variation across the full growth distribution, this study focuses on binary stunting because it is the primary indicator used for population-level monitoring and policy evaluation of chronic undernutrition including child health research and international nutrition surveillance efforts [45, 46]. In addition to its policy relevance, the binary stunting outcome facilitates estimation of interpretable prevalence differences within the matching-based analytical framework employed in this study. The primary estimand of interest is the ATT, defined as the difference in stunting prevalence between children residing in conflict-affected regions and their matched counterparts in conflict-insulated regions.

Propensity score matching

Our choice of the propensity score estimation framework is motivated by the intent to quantify the association between conflict exposure and chronic childhood undernutrition in the presence of selection bias. The matching framework mitigates selection bias by improving balance between treatment and control groups with respect to observed covariates. We believe that the child, maternal and household-specific covariates considered in this study adequately capture the observables necessary to make this empirical strategy viable. Below, we briefly mention the methodological details of the matching framework.

Matching criteria

Nearest-neighbour PSM was implemented with replacement using a 1:3 matching ratio for generating matched samples of children belonging to conflict-affected and conflict-insulated zones. In this procedure, a child belonging to a conflict-affected zone is matched with three children belonging to conflict-insulated zones based on the proximity of their propensity scores. No explicit caliper was imposed on the propensity score distance. Instead, match quality was assessed using balance diagnostics, including standardized mean differences (SMD) and weighted t-tests before and after matching. This approach allows assessment of match quality directly through covariate balance rather than imposing an arbitrary distance threshold. Balance for continuous covariates was assessed using variance ratios (VR), defined as the ratio of the variance of a covariate in the treated group to that in the matched control group. Variance ratios between 0.5 and 2 are commonly considered indicative of acceptable balance in matched samples [47]. However, variance ratios were used as a supplementary diagnostic to corroborate balance achieved through matching, while standardized mean differences and weighted t-tests served as the primary balance criterion. Some control observations were not selected into the matched sample due to limited overlap in propensity scores or lack of suitability as close matches under the nearest-neighbour algorithm. Matching with replacement was chosen to improve match quality and reduce bias when overlap between treated and control units is imperfect. The choice of a 1:3 matching ratio reflects a common bias-variance trade-off in matching designs, allowing improved precision through the use of multiple control observations while maintaining acceptable match quality. Effect estimation

Following PSM, the ATT was estimated on the risk difference scale as the weighted difference in mean stunting prevalence between treated children (residing in conflict-affected regions) and their matched controls (residing in conflict-insulated regions), using the matching weights generated by the nearest-neighbour matching procedure. As matching was conducted with replacement, matching weights were used when computing group means. Statistical uncertainty was quantified using nonparametric bootstrap resampling of the matched sample. Specifically, 1000 replications of the matched data were carried out with replacement and the ATT was re-estimated for each replicate. Percentile-based 95% confidence interval of the ATT was obtained from the empirical distribution of the bootstrap estimates.

Post-matching regression adjustment

For completeness and comparability with prior studies, we additionally estimated multivariable logistic regression models on the matched sample, adjusting for baseline covariates. These post-matching regression models were used to improve statistical precision and to assess the robustness of the estimated association to residual imbalance. They do not redefine the estimand identified by the matching design but provide adjusted summaries of the association between conflict exposure and childhood stunting conditional on the matched design. However, given the relatively high prevalence of stunting, these should not be interpreted as causal risk ratios. Estimates obtained from matching alone and from matching followed by regression adjustment were qualitatively similar, indicating limited sensitivity to the additional adjustment.

Identification assumptions

The validity of the estimated ATT relies on several standard assumptions. First, conditional exchangeability is assumed, such that conditional on the observed covariates used in the propensity score model, conflict exposure is independent of potential childhood stunting outcomes. Second, the overlap (or common support) assumption requires that children residing in conflict-affected and conflict-insulated regions have sufficiently similar distributions of observed characteristics, which we assessed using balance diagnostics after matching. Finally, the analysis assumes no interference between units (stable unit treatment value assumption), such that conflict exposure status in one region does not directly affect the nutritional outcomes of children in other regions. While these assumptions cannot be directly tested, we include a rich set of child, maternal, household and environmental covariates to enhance plausibility of conditional exchangeability in the Myanmar context. Although the covariates selected are grounded in established conceptual frameworks and prior empirical evidence, unmeasured confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Robustness checks further assess the stability of the estimated associations to alternative propensity score estimation models and conflict exposure thresholds.

In addition to the primary analysis, we conducted exploratory subgroup analyses to examine whether the association between conflict exposure and childhood stunting varies across selected child, maternal and household characteristics. Subgroup-specific associations were estimated using post-matching multivariable logistic regression models analogous to those fitted in the full sample, while adjusting for the same set of baseline covariates included in the primary analysis and are presented for hypothesis generation. These analyses are intended to explore potential effect modification within the matched sample and should be interpreted as exploratory. As with the primary analysis, the estimated associations reflect adjusted comparisons under the stated identifying assumptions and are not interpreted as definitive causal effects. Since these analyses involve stratification of the matched sample, statistical power to detect modest differences may be reduced relative to the primary analysis.

Sensitivity analysis to unmeasured confounding

The PSM technique mitigates biases stemming from observed confounders i.e variables which are associated with both the treatment and outcome, by improving balance between exposure groups. However, it fails to correct for biases that originate from unobserved or latent confounders. The estimation of treatment effects with such matching methods relies on the crucial assumption that treatment and control groups do not differ on such unobservable confounders that may influence selection into treatment and outcome variables. If both the groups differ on such unobserved confounders, there may be a hidden bias to which matching estimators are not robust [48]. Since direct estimation of the quantum of selection bias is nearly impossible with observational data, we employ an indirect method based on E-values to assess the robustness of the estimated conflict–stunting association to potential unmeasured confounding [49].

The E-value is designed to assess “evidence of causality” in observational studies in the presence of unobserved confounders. It is defined as the minimum strength of association on the risk ratio scale that an unobserved confounder need to have with both the exposure (conflict status) and the outcome (child stunting), conditional on measured covariates, to fully explain away the observed association. Larger E-values indicate greater robustness to unmeasured confounding. Following recommended practice, we report E-values for both the point estimate and for the limit of the 95% confidence interval closest to the null. The latter reflects the minimum strength of unmeasured confounding required to move the confidence interval to include the null value, and thus provides a conservative assessment of robustness. Because the outcome is common, E-values were computed using the appropriate approximation for odds ratios as described by VanderWeele and Ding (2017).

All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.2) [50]. Propensity score estimation and matching were implemented using the MatchIt package [51], covariate balance diagnostics were assessed using the MatchIt, cobalt [52] and weights packages [53], survey-weighted analyses were conducted using the survey package [54] while E-value calculations were performed using the EValue package [55].

Matching and covariate balance

The matching procedure retained all 1,966 treated children and matched them to 1,826 unique control children. Because matching was performed with replacement, some controls were reused, resulting in an effective control sample size of 1,077. There was adequate overlap in the propensity score distributions between the conflict-affected and conflict-insulated groups. All treated observations had comparable control observations within the observed propensity score range, while control units without suitable matches were excluded from the matched sample.

Table 3 reports descriptive statistics and standardized mean differences (SMD) for baseline covariates after PSM. Post-matching, all covariates achieved absolute SMDs below 0.1, reflecting substantial improvement in balance relative to the unmatched sample, where several covariates exhibited larger imbalances (Table 2). Weighted t-tests further indicated no statistically significant differences in baseline covariates between exposure groups. Variance ratios for continuous covariates were within the acceptable thresholds (0.5 to 2), suggesting comparable dispersion across matched groups. Taken together, these diagnostics indicate that the matching procedure substantially reduced observed covariate imbalances between children from conflict-affected and conflict-insulated regions. Effect estimate

Children residing in conflict-affected regions exhibited a higher prevalence of stunting compared to those in conflict-insulated regions. Specifically, the estimated ATT indicated an absolute difference of 0.045 with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval (0.005, 0.083) corresponding to a 4.5 percentage-point higher prevalence among children in conflict-affected regions. As a secondary analysis, we fitted a weighted logistic regression model on the matched data with conflict zone as the independent variable and baseline covariates as controls. The model yielded an adjusted odds ratio of 1.28 (95% CI: 1.10 - 1.49) corresponding to 28% higher odds of stunting among children residing in conflict-affected regions. This estimate is consistent in direction and magnitude with the ATT results. These odds ratios are reported as associative summaries derived from the matched data. As with the ATT estimates, their interpretation depends on the stated identification assumptions and should not be construed as establishing definitive causality.

To assess potential multicollinearity among covariates included in the post-matching logistic regression model, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were computed (Table S3 in supplementary document). All VIF values were well below 2 which is suggestive of negligible multicollinearity among predictors. Therefore, the estimated odds ratios are unlikely to be distorted by collinearity.

Robustness checks

We checked the robustness of the results to alternative specification of the propensity score estimation frameworks namely i) generalised additive model [58], ii) LASSO [59], iii) ridge regression [60], iv) elastic net [61], v) random forest [62], vi) covariate balancing propensity score model [63] and vii) Bayesian additive regression trees [64]. These alternative frameworks were selected to represent a broad and complementary set of modeling strategies commonly used in applied observational studies, spanning parametric (logistic regression with regularization), semi-parametric (generalized additive models) and machine-learning–based approaches (random forests and Bayesian additive regression trees). This diversity allows us to assess whether the estimated treatment effects are sensitive to assumptions about functional form, variable selection and model flexibility underlying propensity score estimation. Importantly, all models target the same binary exposure and are used solely for propensity score estimation, with outcome analysis conducted in a consistent manner across specifications.

For each of these frameworks, we re-estimated the ATT and the corresponding 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, as depicted in Table 5. Across all alternative specifications, the estimated ATT remains positive and broadly similar in magnitude. However, statistical significance varies across modeling approaches, with confidence intervals for some specifications including the null value. While the direction and approximate size of the association are generally consistent, these results indicate that inference regarding statistical significance depends on the choice of propensity score specification. Sensitivity assessment (E-value analysis)

We assessed sensitivity to potential unmeasured confounding using E-values. This analysis does not constitute a separate estimation framework but rather evaluates how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be to explain away the observed association. The approximate relative risk ratio corresponding to the estimated treatment effect on stunting was 1.13 (95% CI: 1.05–1.22), yielding an E-value of 1.52 for the point estimate and 1.28 for the confidence interval limit closest to the null. These values indicate that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both conflict exposure and childhood stunting by a risk ratio of at least 1.52-fold (or 1.28-fold to shift the confidence interval to include the null), above and beyond the measured covariates, to fully explain away the observed association. While this suggests that the estimated effect is sensitive to unmeasured confounding of moderate strength, it also indicates that such confounding would need to be meaningfully associated with both exposure and outcome. Accordingly, the E-value analysis provides a transparent quantitative benchmark for interpreting the robustness of the findings rather than establishing causal certainty.

Discussion

This study provides robust, nationally representative evidence that armed conflict is associated with chronic childhood undernutrition in Myanmar. Using a PSM framework, the primary analysis estimated an ATT of 0.045 (95% bootstrap CI: 0.005–0.083), indicating a 4.5 percentage point higher prevalence of stunting among children residing in conflict-affected regions compared to comparable children in conflict-insulated regions. A secondary post-matching multivariable logistic regression yielded an adjusted odds ratio of 1.28 (95% CI: 1.10–1.49), consistent in direction and magnitude with the ATT estimate. These findings should be interpreted in light of the identifying assumptions underlying the matching framework and the variability in precision observed across alternative model specifications. A key strength of this study is the use of nationally representative Demographic and Health Survey data linked with geocoded conflict records, enabling a population-level assessment of the conflict–nutrition association in Myanmar.

These findings align with a growing body of research linking violence and instability to poor nutritional outcomes [22, 25, 65] and reinforce that conflict acts as a structural driver of health inequity particularly among socioeconomically vulnerable populations [66, 67]. Similar associations have been documented in fragile settings such as Gaza [68], Syria [69], Nigeria [70], and the Democratic Republic of Congo [25], where conflict-related displacement, food insecurity, and infrastructure disruption have been linked to impaired child growth and elevated stunting prevalence. The magnitude of association observed here is comparable to estimates from these contexts, underscoring the vulnerability of child nutrition systems in protracted conflict settings. The results align with UNICEF’s Conceptual Framework on the Determinants of Malnutrition (2020), which positions political instability among the underlying causes of chronic undernutrition.

While the matching-based design aims to reduce confounding and approximate a counterfactual comparison, the observational nature of the data, region-level exposure definition, and reliance on identifying assumptions preclude strong causal claims. Although odds ratios are reported for comparability with existing literature, substantive interpretation in this study is anchored on absolute effect measures (ATT), which more directly reflect differences in stunting prevalence across conflict environments. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as population-level associations rather than definitive causal effects.

Exploratory subgroup analyses suggest that the association between conflict exposure and childhood stunting may be more pronounced among socioeconomically vulnerable groups, including children of mothers with lower educational attainment, those from poorer households, and those lacking access to improved sanitation. Although these differences should be interpreted cautiously given overlapping confidence intervals and the exploratory nature of the analysis, they are consistent with prior evidence indicating that armed conflict often amplifies existing socioeconomic inequalities and disproportionately affects marginalized populations [22, 71]. They also resonate with the literature on intersectional determinants of nutrition, where poverty, gender inequality and limited access to WASH services intersect to produce higher risk [1, 72]. These patterns underscore the importance of ensuring that nutrition and humanitarian interventions in conflict-affected settings are equity-focused and prioritize vulnerable populations. Subgroup-specific associations and E-value–based sensitivity analyses should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory evidence.

Policy and programmatic relevance

The findings of this study do not describe specific nutrition programs currently in place in Myanmar; rather, they offer evidence-based insights that may inform the design and prioritization of future nutrition and humanitarian interventions in conflict-affected settings. Having said that, the results support the integration of conflict-sensitive nutrition programming within national and humanitarian systems. In fragile settings, scaling up community-based management of acute malnutrition (CMAM) and infant and young child feeding (IYCF) services through local health volunteers can ensure continuity of care despite insecurity [73]. Strengthening the supply chains for micronutrient supplements, fortifying community outreach, and coordinating across sectors (nutrition, water and sanitation, maternal health) are essential for building resilience. From a systems perspective, these findings reinforce the need to strengthen health-system resilience - the capacity to maintain essential preventive and nutritional services under crisis conditions. Recent global nutrition reports stress the importance of resilient food systems and integrated health and nutrition programs in crisis-affected settings, reinforcing the need for targeted policies in regions with sustained instability [5, 74]. Myanmar’s experience offers lessons for other LMICs on the importance of embedding nutrition interventions within broader humanitarian and primary health strategies. While the analytical framework employed here may be applicable to other conflict-affected settings, the findings themselves should be interpreted in light of Myanmar’s unique political history, ethnic geography, and pattern of protracted, geographically diffuse conflict. Extrapolation to other low- and middle-income countries should therefore be undertaken with caution. Moreover, these findings reflect conflict exposure patterns preceding the 2015–16 MDHS and should not be interpreted as describing nutritional conditions following the post-2021 escalation.

Mechanisms and pathways

The observed association between conflict exposure and higher stunting prevalence is consistent with established pathways through which armed conflict can undermine child nutrition. Prior research suggests that conflict-related economic disruption, reduced food security, displacement, and weakened healthcare access may impair child growth [7, 10, 75]. Although the present study does not directly test these mechanisms, the stronger associations observed among socioeconomically vulnerable subgroups (e.g., poorer households and children lacking improved sanitation) are consistent with such pathways. These findings underscore the importance of strengthening nutrition and health systems in conflict-affected regions.

Limitations

All major methodological and contextual limitations of this study are consolidated here for clarity. Each of these point to important directions for future research as well. These include constraints related to causal interpretation, the regional definition of conflict exposure, sample size and statistical power limitations (particularly in exploratory subgroup analyses), measurement choices and data availability constraints. Subgroup-specific findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. First, while the analysis establishes a robust association between conflict exposure and childhood stunting, it does not permit a detailed examination of the specific mechanisms through which armed conflict affects childhood nutritional outcomes. Prior research has identified multiple plausible pathways, including loss of livelihoods and economic stress, food insecurity, displacement, war-related victimization, disruptions to maternal healthcare utilization, and maternal stress during pregnancy [9, 25, 27, 76]. It is likely that a subset of these mechanisms operates in the Myanmar context as well. However, as noted in Akresh et al. (2012, 2022), rigorous identification of these channels requires granular household-level data on assets, crop production and consumption, displacement histories, disease exposure, water and sanitation conditions, as well as fine-grained conflict information. Such data are not currently available for Myanmar, limiting our ability to empirically disentangle these pathways. Second, conflict exposure is measured at the regional level and does not capture individual-level proximity to or intensity of conflict events. In a setting such as Myanmar, where armed conflict is geographically diffuse and population movement is common, it is inherently difficult to precisely identify whether, when, or through which channels individual children are affected by conflict. Consequently, some degree of exposure misclassification is unavoidable. Importantly, because conflict exposure is defined independently of childhood nutritional outcomes, any resulting misclassification is likely to be non-differential and would tend to attenuate the estimated associations toward the null, suggesting that the reported effects may be conservative. Third, the analysis does not distinguish between specific timing of conflict exposure, such as in utero versus postnatal periods. Childhood stunting reflects cumulative nutritional deprivation over early life, and conflict-related disruptions may plausibly operate across multiple biologically relevant windows, including both prenatal and early childhood periods. While more precise temporal alignment of exposure could enhance understanding of underlying mechanisms, the available data do not permit such disaggregation. Instead, the exposure definition is intended to capture sustained conflict environments relevant to chronic undernutrition rather than short-term or acute shocks. An additional limitation relates to the dichotomization of the continuous height-for-age Z-score into a binary stunting indicator. While this choice aligns with policy-relevant definitions and global nutrition targets, it entails a loss of information and may obscure more subtle gradients in nutritional status. Future work using continuous outcomes or distributional approaches could provide a more nuanced characterization of the conflict–nutrition relationship. Fourth, the availability of additional rounds of nationally representative survey data would enable the application of alternative inferential approaches, such as difference-in-differences designs, which could further strengthen causal interpretation of the conflict–health relationship in Myanmar [22, 24, 27]. Moreover, the analysis relies on 2015–16 survey data and therefore does not capture the substantial escalation in conflict, displacement, and humanitarian deterioration following the 2021 military takeover. The findings should thus be interpreted as reflecting the pre-coup conflict environment, and the magnitude and mechanisms of the association may differ under the current geopolitical context.

Conclusion

This study documents robust associations between regional exposure to armed conflict and higher prevalence of childhood stunting in Myanmar rather than definitive causal effects. Specifically, it highlights that child nutrition interventions in fragile and conflict-affected contexts must go beyond food provision to address disrupted systems of care and inequitable access. In Myanmar, spatially targeted nutrition and maternal health interventions such as fortified food distribution, mobile IYCF services, and sanitation improvement should be prioritized in high-conflict states. Global experience shows that community-based, decentralized approaches are the most effective in maintaining service continuity during insecurity. More broadly, these findings demonstrate that monitoring subnational conflict exposure can help identify emerging pockets of nutritional vulnerability across LMICs. At the same time, while the present findings provide empirical evidence on conflict–nutrition associations, arguments for targeted nutrition and humanitarian interventions remain normative and should be guided by broader ethical, political, and contextual considerations beyond the scope of this study. These conclusions should also be interpreted in light of key methodological limitations discussed above, including regional-level exposure misclassification, dichotomization of nutritional outcomes and the possibility of residual unmeasured confounding. By integrating conflict analytics into national nutrition surveillance systems, governments and humanitarian partners can anticipate and mitigate the indirect health consequences of violence. Building such resilience into health and nutrition programming is critical for sustaining progress toward global nutrition targets amid growing instability.
