Maternal vitamin D intake correlates with serum 25-Hydroxyvitamin D, Interleukin-6, and ferritin in maternal and umbilical cord
Authors: Yusrawati Yusrawati, Efrida Efrida, Nasrum Massi, Bahrul Fikri, Naoki Shimojo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background Vitamin D is associated with fetal growth and development. It also plays an essential role in the body’s immune system and placental regulation of the mother. This study investigated the correlation between vitamin D intake and 25-hydroxyvitamin D, interleukin-6, and ferritin levels in the maternal and umbilical cord. Materials and methods A cross-sectional study was conducted from June to December 2022 in Hermina Hospital, General Hospital Bunda Padang, and Army Hospital Dr. Reksodiwiryo. A total of 40 pregnant persons were observed in this study. The participants approved and signed the informed consent. The data were collected using a food frequency questionnaire. Maternal and umbilical cord serum were analyzed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. All data were normally distributed, and the Pearson correlation test was performed. Results Mean vitamin D intake was 16,54 ± 2,92 mcg, maternal and umbilical cord serum 25(OH)D levels were 14,74 ± 4,93 ng/mL and 17,02 ± 5,94 ng/mL, maternal and umbilical cord serum IL-6 levels were 81,04 ± 12,23 and 98,75 ± 16,58 ng/L, maternal and umbilical cord serum ferritin levels were 41 ± 50,97 and 122,97 ± 71,23 ng/mL. There was a moderate correlation between vitamin D intake and serum 25(OH)D maternal (r = 0,379 and p = 0,016) and umbilical cord (r = 0,490 and p = 0,001). Vitamin D intake showed weak, non-significant correlations with maternal IL-6 (r = 0,292, p = 0,068) and umbilical cord IL-6 (r=-0,243, p = 0,131), and negligible correlation with maternal (r = 0,006, p = 0,971) and umbilical cord ferritin (r=-0,205, p = 0,204). Conclusions This study found a moderate correlation between maternal vitamin D intake and serum 25(OH)D, but weak correlations with IL-6 and ferritin.

Introduction

Vitamin D is associated with fetal growth and development by increasing the calcium requirement for bone development during the third-trimester pregnancy1. It also plays an essential role in the body’s immune system and regulates the placental, testes, endometrial, pituitary gland, and ovaries2. In pregnancy, the active form 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 [1,25(OH)D] will affect innate immune responses3. The escalation of vitamin D levels modulates and reduces TLR-2, TLR-4, and TLR-9 levels, reducing IL-6 levels4.

As a protein found within cells that store iron, ferritin, as the serum marker, has been considered to be associated with vitamin D metabolism and plays a role in red cell development. As 1,25(OH)D is involved in the proliferation and maturation of erythroid progenitor cells, the deficiency of 1,25(OH)D may be associated with the impairment of erythropoiesis5.

Sun exposure, diet, culture, and lifestyle are the determining factors affecting vitamin D availability6. As the high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in the mother can influence other micronutrient deficiencies in the newborn and affect growth and development, we aimed to investigate the correlation between vitamin D intake and 25-hydroxyvitamin D, interleukin-6, and ferritin levels in the maternal and umbilical cord.

Materials and methods

This analytical observational study used a cross-sectional design to investigate the correlation between maternal vitamin D intake and serum levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and ferritin in maternal and umbilical cord blood. The study was conducted from June to December 2022 at three hospitals in Padang, Indonesia: Hermina Hospital, Bunda BMC General Hospital, and Army Hospital Dr. Reksodiwiryo. Laboratory analyses were performed at the Biomedical Laboratory, Faculty of Medicine, Andalas University. Participants were recruited through consecutive sampling. All pregnant mothers willing to withdraw blood were included in this study. The exclusion criteria included chronic maternal conditions (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, liver or renal diseases), acute infections, or any inflammatory disorders. A total of 40 participants were enrolled.

This study has been approved by the Health Research Ethics Committee of Andalas University (approval number: 2937/UN.16.02.D/PP/2022), and the research was performed according to relevant guidelines/regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians.

Patients’ data collection

Subjective and objective data on pregnant women were collected, including name, age, education, occupation, weight before and after pregnancy, height, LILA (circumference of the upper arm), LMP (last menstrual period), parity, history of pregnancy, previous mother illnesses, blood pressure, and routine blood counts. Maternal vitamin D intake data were collected using the FFQ questionnaire developed and validated by Lipoeto et al.7.

Definition of vitamin D deficiency

Vitamin D level was categorized as a sufficient (25(OH)D ≥ 30 ng/mL), insufficient (25(OH)D = 20–29 ng/mL), or deficient (25(OH)D < 20 ng/mL). Serum 25(OH)D levels of < 10 ng/mL are considered severe vitamin D deficiency8.

Sample collection

Maternal blood samples were taken from the median cubital vein as much as ± 2 cc, and samples of umbilical cord blood were taken from the umbilical vein as much as ± 2 cc immediately after the umbilical cord was dissected. The collected mother’s blood was contained in the mother’s vacutainer, and the cord blood was put into the baby’s vacutainer. Centrifugation of maternal and umbilical cord blood was carried out at a speed of 2000–3000 rotations per minute (rpm) for 10–20 min. Centrifuged serum was taken using a micropipette and then put into a microtube that had been coded according to the identity of the participants.

Maternal serum and umbilical cord were stored in the Biomedical Laboratory (temperature − 80 C). The 25-hydroxy vitamin D (Diagnostic Biochem Canada), interleukin-6 (Bioassay Technology Laboratory), and ferritin (Diagnostic Biochem Canada) in maternal and umbilical cord serum were measured after all samples were collected using Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assays (ELISA). All assays were performed in duplicate according to standardized manufacturer protocols. The 25(OH)D assay had a detection limit of 2 ng/mL with an inter-assay coefficient of variation (CV) of < 10%. The IL-6 assay had a sensitivity of 1.5 ng/L and a linear range of 5–1500 ng/L. Ferritin measurements followed the 25–283 ng/mL diagnostic reference range.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25. The distribution of each variable was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive statistics were reported as means ± standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed data and medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for non-normally distributed data.

Correlation analyses were conducted to examine the relationships between maternal vitamin D intake and serum levels of 25(OH)D, IL-6, and ferritin in maternal and umbilical cord samples. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used for normally distributed variables, while Spearman’s rank correlation was used for variables that were not normally distributed. A two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study showed a correlation between vitamin D and umbilical cord blood and maternal serum 25(OH)D levels. Our result contrasts with Asyari et al.‘s study, which reported an insignificant correlation between vitamin D intake and maternal serum 25(OH)D level9. 25(OH)D levels during pregnancy mainly depend on the maternal supply. The 25(OH)D can be instantly passed through the placenta and activated into fetal renal 1,25(OH). The 1,25(OH)D can also be synthesized in the placenta to regulate metabolism10. Hollick et al.’s study of 40 mother-infant pairs reported that 76% and 81% of mother and umbilical cord blood had lower 25(OH)D levels. They showed that vitamin D deficiency was associated with a low vitamin D level during pregnancy8. This finding is linear with a longitudinal study by Shakeri and Jafarirad, which found that 7,33%, 76,6%, and 15,9% of mothers had a deficiency, insufficiency, and normal vitamin D, respectively11.

A strong correlation between the mother and the umbilical cord during pregnancy is reported in this study. Various observational studies reported that the elevation of vitamin D binding protein (DBP) during pregnancy was 40–50% higher compared to a non-pregnant woman in the early third trimester and lowered at term. Generally, the liver is the leading production site of DBP. However, since the human placenta also expressed DPB to the surface, we speculate that the higher levels of DPB might be due to a high turnover of trophoblast12.

Our study found a weak positive correlation between vitamin D intake and maternal IL-6 levels (r = 0,292) and a weak negative correlation with umbilical cord IL-6 levels (r = −0,243), though neither reached statistical significance. This suggests that the relationship is not strong while higher maternal vitamin D intake might be slightly associated with lower fetal inflammation (as indicated by umbilical cord IL-6 levels). These findings align with Xu et al.‘s report of non-significant associations between vitamin D deficiency and IL-6 elevation13. The observed trend may relate to Mazidi et al.‘s findings that umbilical cord vitamin D levels influence antimicrobial production and TLR-mediated inflammatory responses4. However, our results contrast Lisnawati et al.‘s report on vitamin D’s inhibitory effect on IL-6, possibly due to differences in population characteristics or vitamin D status14. The weak negative correlation we observed (r = −0,243) suggests that while increased vitamin D intake might modestly reduce fetal inflammation, other factors likely play more substantial roles in regulating IL-6 levels. This partial agreement with Liu and Hewison’s findings regarding vitamin D’s anti-inflammatory effects may reflect our study population’s sufficient vitamin D status (mean intake > 15 mcg), potentially attenuating stronger inverse relationships in deficient populations2.

Interleukin-6 is a pleiotropic cytokine that mediates embryo implantation and development15,16. The serum IL-6 during pregnancy is significantly increased17. A significant increase in IL-6 levels was reported during the third trimester compared to early pregnancy18. Based on our result, we assumed that the mother with an adequate vitamin D diet would have maternal and umbilical cord IL-6 levels of 81,04 ± 12,23 ng/L, within the normal levels. This shows that sufficient levels based on government recommendation (15 mcg) can affect better maternal vitamin D outcomes and prevent maternal IL-6 during pregnancy and umbilical cord IL-6 in the placenta.

Our study found no correlation between vitamin D and maternal ferritin levels. Additionally, a negative correlation was found between vitamin D and umbilical cord ferritin levels. Furthermore, no statistically significant correlation of vitamin D levels to the maternal and umbilical cord IL-6 or ferritin levels was reported. A positive correlation between serum 25(OH) D and ferritin has been reported in the female gender19. It shows that vitamin D plays a vital role in preventing anemia. It also helps reduce oxidative stress, insulin resistance, and inflammation. Vitamin D can also reduce ferritin levels; both processes can occur together. A study by Fadilah et al., reported the association between an increase in serum ferritin and a decrease of serum 25(OH)D in primary thalassemia children aged 2–14 years old20. Furthermore, a study by Judistiani et al., in first-trimester pregnancy, showed that mothers with normal or insufficient vitamin D had low ferritin levels and a high risk of anemia21.

This study provides clinical data on maternal and umbilical cord 25-hydroxyvitamin D, interleukin-6, and ferritin levels in pregnancy, but it has several limitations. The small sample size (n = 40) may limit our ability to detect weak correlations, especially for variables such as IL-6 and ferritin. However, previous research demonstrates that meaningful and statistically significant findings can still be obtained from modest sample sizes. For example, Rodda et al. conducted a study involving 78 pregnant women. They found a strong correlation between maternal and umbilical cord serum 25(OH)D concentrations (r = 0.88, p < 0.0001), suggesting that medium-sized studies can provide reliable results22. Similarly, Jutell et al.. and Aggarwal et al., with sample sizes of 298 and 300, respectively, confirmed strong maternal-fetal vitamin D correlations23,24. These studies collectively illustrate that both medium and large samples yield consistent findings regarding maternal and neonatal vitamin D dynamics. While our sample is smaller, it remains within the acceptable range for exploratory studies and contributes valuable data to the growing evidence base, especially in underrepresented populations. Nonetheless, we recommend that future research involve larger, multi-center cohorts to confirm these associations and explore clinical outcomes more robustly.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional study identified a moderate positive correlation between maternal vitamin D intake and serum 25(OH)D levels in maternal and umbilical cord blood. These findings suggest that adequate dietary vitamin D intake was associated with higher maternal and umbilical cord serum 25(OH)D levels, which could have implications for immune and iron-related parameters during pregnancy.
