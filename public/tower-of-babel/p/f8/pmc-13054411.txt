Combined treatment of deep and superficial venous reflux accelerates ulcer healing and improves symptoms: A real-world retrospective study
Authors: Wei Zheng, Zongheng Gu, Hui Zhao, Changbao Yan, Jie Zhang, Liang Zhao, Yingfeng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal surgical strategy for treating chronic venous disease with concomitant deep and superficial reflux but without iliac/proximal obstruction remains unclear. This study aimed to compare clinical symptoms, health-related quality-of-life, and ulcer outcomes between superficial venous surgery alone and combined deep-superficial surgery (performed concurrently or in stages).MethodsIn this single-center retrospective cohort, 229 patients were grouped by treatment and received superficial-only (n = 138), concurrent deep + superficial (n = 49), or staged deep + superficial (n = 42) interventions. Outcomes were Venous Clinical Severity Score (VCSS), Chronic Venous Insufficiency Questionnaire-20 (CIVIQ-20) at baseline and follow-up (1, 3, and 6 months, and last follow-up), ulcer outcomes (n = 59) assessed by healing rates and Kaplan-Meier time-to-healing analysis, duplex valve competence after deep intervention, and complications. Analyses used Welch one-way analysis of variance with Games-Howell post hoc and log-rank testing.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable (baseline VCSS 8.6±3.3, 9.6±4.0, and 9.0±3.9; P = .32). Between-group differences in VCSS were significant at 1 month (P = .012), 6 months (P < .001), and last follow-up (P = .017). Staged treatment reduced VCSS vs superficial-only at 1 month (mean difference [MD], −1.47; Padj = .008); both concurrent and staged strategies showed lower VCSS vs superficial-only at 6 months (MD, −0.98 and −1.01; Padj = .003 and .001) and last follow-up (MD, −0.76 and −0.68; Padj = .025 and .033). The CIVIQ-20 at the last-follow-up was lower with concurrent and staged strategies vs superficial-only (MD, −2.83 [Padj < .001]; MD, −2.40 [Padj = .010]). Internal valvuloplasty (n = 77) demonstrated lower VCSS at 1 to 6 months and last follow-up vs superficial-only (all Padj ≤ .026) and a lower CIVIQ-20 score. Among 59 active ulcers, 89.8% healed overall; healing proportions did not differ (P = .310), but time to healing differed by Kaplan-Meier analysis (log-rank P = .038). Duplex after deep intervention showed reflux normalization (<0.5 seconds) in 94.5% to 98.9% at 1 to 6 months; complications were minor, with no symptomatic deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism or major bleeding.ConclusionsIn patients with chronic venous disease with concomitant deep and superficial reflux but no iliac obstruction, adding deep reflux correction (concurrent or staged) was associated with greater and more durable symptom relief and better quality of life than superficial-only surgery, with high duplex-confirmed competence and low complication rates; ulcer healing time may be accelerated.

Chronic venous disease (CVD) represents one of the most common vascular disorders of the lower extremities, ranging from uncomplicated varicose veins to skin changes and venous ulceration, with a substantial cumulative impact on quality of life.1, 2, 3 The Clinical, Etiological, Anatomical, and Pathophysiological (CEAP) classification and Venous Clinical Severity Score (VCSS) facilitate standardized staging and assessment of disease severity4, 5, 6; however, optimal management still hinges on identifying the dominant source of venous hypertension. Superficial venous insufficiency is the most prevalent and readily treatable etiology, and thermal ablation, stripping, or foam sclerotherapy provide symptom relief for most C2 to C4 patients.1,4,7, 8, 9 Proximal outflow obstruction/stenosis, particularly iliac vein compression syndrome, represents a distinct mechanism requiring restoration of outflow; iliac stenting combined with superficial treatment improves ulcer healing.10, 11, 12, 13, 14

In practice, a clinically important subgroup lacks significant iliofemoral obstruction yet demonstrates concomitant deep venous reflux (eg, common femoral or femoral valve incompetence), superficial reflux (great/small saphenous systems), and variable perforator reflux—that is, multisource reflux-driven venous hypertension.15, 16, 17, 18 For such patients, high-quality evidence is lacking regarding whether treatment of superficial veins alone is sufficient or whether concurrent or sequential management of deep venous reflux is necessary. The existing literature primarily consists of small-sample empirical reports or analyses confounded by other types of CVD.18, 19, 20 Mechanistically, persistent proximal deep reflux can transmit distally during ambulation, and when superficial and perforator reflux coexist, deep-superficial pressure communication may sustain microcirculatory hypertension and inflammation, contributing to advanced CEAP C5 to C6 disease and refractory ulceration.1,3 Thus, superficial ablation alone may interrupt peripheral overflow without correcting the proximal reflux source, potentially limiting symptom improvement and delaying ulcer healing.18, 19, 20, 21

With increasing standardization of deep venous valve reconstruction, transplantation, and transposition, recent studies have explored combined correction of concurrent deep and superficial reflux,19,21, 22, 23, 24 reporting improved symptom scores and ulcer outcomes compared with superficial-only treatment.18, 19, 20, 21,25,26 However, these reports are constrained by small samples and limited homogeneous superficial-only comparators,18,19,21, 22, 23 and key practical questions remain unresolved, including whether concurrent intervention increases treatment burden and whether staged strategies yield comparable outcomes. Accordingly, outcomes should be evaluated specifically in patients with combined deep and superficial reflux after excluding iliac obstruction, a fundamentally different outflow-obstructive etiology.1,14

We retrospectively reviewed 229 patients with reflux-associated CVD without iliac/proximal deep venous obstruction but with concomitant deep and superficial reflux. Based on the operative strategy received, patients were categorized into three groups and compared for symptom severity (VCSS), quality of life (Chronic Venous Insufficiency Questionnaire-20 [CIVIQ-20]), and venous ulcer outcomes (healing rate and time to healing).5,6,27, 28, 29 We hypothesized that combined management of deep and superficial reflux may be associated with more complete and durable relief of venous hypertension than superficial treatment alone, with corresponding improvements in the VCSS and CIVIQ-20 and more favorable ulcer healing outcomes. The findings of this study may help to inform clinical decision-making between superficial-only and deep + superficial surgical strategies and provide descriptive baseline cohort characteristics to support future prospective or multicenter investigations.

Methods

Study design and population

This single-center retrospective cohort included 229 adults undergoing lower-extremity venous surgery within a standardized venous care pathway. Eligible patients had concomitant deep and superficial venous reflux with no organic iliac/proximal deep venous obstruction (ie, no iliofemoral stenosis or occlusion) confirmed by duplex ultrasonography and, when required, venography. Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years; pathological deep venous reflux (retrograde flow >1.0 second) in the common femoral, femoral, and/or popliteal vein, coexisting with superficial venous reflux (retrograde flow >0.5 second) in the great or small saphenous vein and/or major tributaries; imaging excluding significant iliac/inferior vena cava/femoroiliac junction stenosis or obstruction; complete baseline CEAP class, VCSS, and CIVIQ-20; and one or more postoperative follow-up visits, allowing assessment of VCSS/CIVIQ-20 and ulcer status.

Exclusion criteria were iliac vein lesions with a clear indication for stent reconstruction, active deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or acute DVT within 6 months, severe arterial ischemia (ankle-brachial index of <0.8) or need for concomitant arterial reconstruction, and insufficient data for primary outcome assessment.

Grouping and treatment strategies

Patients were categorized by treatment received: group 1, superficial surgery only (n = 138); group 2, concurrent deep + superficial surgery (n = 49), defined as deep reflux correction performed in the same session or within 7 days; and group 3, staged deep + superficial surgery (n = 42), defined as an interval of >7 days. We defined a 7-day interval to approximate a single hospitalization episode; sensitivity analyses using alternative interval definitions were performed (Supplementary Table I, online only), and the results demonstrated consistent findings with the primary analysis.

Preoperative imaging and reflux assessment

All patients underwent standardized duplex ultrasound mapping in the vascular laboratory. Examinations were preferentially performed in the standing/dependent position (or reverse Trendelenburg if needed). Reflux was provoked by Valsalva for proximal segments when appropriate and by distal augmentation for femoral/popliteal segments. Spectral Doppler was acquired in the longitudinal plane (insonation angle 45°-60°, ∼2-mm sample volume), and reflux duration was measured from onset of retrograde flow to return to baseline. Deep reflux was defined as >1.0 second and superficial reflux as >0.5 second. Antegrade and retrograde venography was performed to further characterize venous pathology and was routinely obtained for all patients scheduled for deep venous procedures, as well as for some undergoing superficial venous surgery.

Surgical procedure

Superficial interventions were selected according to duplex-defined reflux distribution and included high ligation with stripping, endovenous thermal ablation (radiofrequency/laser) with or without phlebectomy, and adjunct foam sclerotherapy as indicated. Incompetent perforators and periulcer/subulcer refluxing veins were treated whenever feasible (primarily via selective phlebectomy and/or adjunct sclerotherapy).

In deep venous surgery, a detailed operative plan outlining the target segment(s) and strategic approach was formulated preoperatively. The execution of the final technique remained contingent upon intraoperative findings, governed specifically by valve morphology, tissue quality, and assessment via strip test. Valvuloplasty was preferred when leaflet geometry and tissue were suitable for durable coaptation; transplantation/transposition or other reconstructive approaches were reserved for nonreconstructable valves. All deep venous reconstructions were performed by the same lead surgeon. Postoperative management

All patients were prescribed compression therapy. After deep venous procedures, unfractionated heparin infusion was initiated on the day of surgery with activated partial thromboplastin time-guided titration, then transitioned to rivaroxaban on postoperative day 3 for ≥3 months. High-risk patients undergoing superficial-only surgery received prophylactic anticoagulation (low-molecular-weight heparin or rivaroxaban).

Data collection and follow-up

Baseline variables included demographics, laterality, CEAP class, VCSS, CIVIQ-20, comorbidities, DVT history, deep reflux segment(s), reflux duration (maximum reflux time), and perforator reflux. Follow-up was scheduled at 1 month (30 ± 14 days), 3 months (90 ± 30 days), and 6 months (180 ± 45 days); the visit closest to the target timepoint within each window was used. Superficial-only patients underwent duplex ultrasound examination at 1 month to confirm technical success; subsequent duplex ultrasound examinations were performed only if clinically indicated. Patients undergoing deep procedures underwent duplex ultrasound examination at 1, 3, and 6 months to assess valve competence and patency. Missed clinic visits were supplemented by telephone/WeChat follow-up.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the preoperative to postoperative change in the VCSS. Changes in the CIVIQ-20 (standard format; lower scores indicate better quality of life) and ulcer healing status were also assessed. Among patients with active venous ulcers, healing was defined as complete epithelialization without exudate or the need for dressings; nonhealing was defined as a persistently open wound or only partial reduction at the last follow-up. Time to healing was calculated from the index (ie, last) surgery date to the date of documented complete healing. In patients who underwent deep venous procedures, duplex-defined reflux status was categorized as normalization (≤0.5 second), near-normalization (0.5 to <1.0 second), or persistent reflux (≥1.0 second).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.0). Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as number (%). Baseline variables were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal-Wallis tests (continuous) and χ2 or Fisher's exact tests (categorical). VCSS and CIVIQ-20 were compared across groups at baseline and each follow-up timepoint using Welch's one-way ANOVA; when significant, pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Games-Howell procedure with multiplicity-adjusted P values, and results were reported as mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Ulcer healing proportions were compared using χ2/Fisher's exact tests, and time to complete healing was analyzed with Kaplan-Meier methods and compared with log-rank tests; patients without healing were censored at the last follow-up. Duplex valve function outcomes were summarized descriptively. A two-sided P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Luhe Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No. 2024-LHKY-068-02) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective study design. All patient data were anonymized before analysis.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 229 patients were enrolled and categorized into group 1 (n = 138), group 2 (n = 49), and group 3 (n = 42) according to the surgical treatment strategy. Baseline demographic characteristics and disease severity were generally comparable across the three groups, with no significant between-group differences at baseline (Table I). The mean patient age was 60.4 ± 11.4, 62.0 ± 13.2, and 61.2 ± 11.5 years in groups 1, 2, and 3, respectively (P = .68), and the mean body mass index was 26.3 ± 3.8, 26.1 ± 3.9, and 26.5 ± 3.9 kg/m2 (P = .91). Baseline clinical severity was similar, with a VCSS of 8.6 ± 3.3, 9.6 ± 4.0, and 9.0 ± 3.9 (P = .32). The prevalence of venous ulcers did not differ significantly among groups. To minimize the influence of baseline imbalance and potential confounding, we performed multivariable-adjusted change score regression analyses (Supplementary Table II, online only). After adjustment for age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities (hypertension/diabetes), smoking, alcohol use, CEAP class, prior DVT, the segmental distribution of deep venous reflux, and perforator reflux and its management, both groups 2 and 3 were associated with greater improvement in VCSS compared with group 1, with a greater magnitude of improvement observed in group 2. With respect to health-related quality of life (CIVIQ-20), improvement was also more pronounced in group 2 than in group 1.

To evaluate whether differences in clinical severity persisted at the end of follow-up and to address potential etiologic heterogeneity, pairwise comparisons of last follow-up VCSS were performed stratified by disease etiology (Supplementary Table III, online only). Post-thrombotic etiology was operationally defined as a documented history of prior DVT. Patients without a history of DVT were classified as having primary reflux. In patients with primary reflux, group 2 was associated with a significantly lower adjusted last follow-up VCSS compared with group 1 (ΔVCSS, −0.92; 95% CI, −1.60 to −0.25; P = .007), whereas group 3 showed a similar but nonsignificant trend; no significant difference was observed between groups 2 and 3. In the post-thrombotic subgroup, both groups 2 and 3 were associated with a lower adjusted last follow-up VCSS compared with group 1 (group 2 vs group 1: ΔVCSS, −1.83; 95% CI, −2.97 to −0.69; P = .002; group 3 vs group 1: ΔVCSS, −1.19; 95% CI, −2.08 to −0.31; P = .008), whereas the difference between groups 2 and 3 was not statistically significant. These post-thrombotic findings should be interpreted with caution given the limited sample size.

Patients were further stratified by deep venous procedure (Supplementary Table IV, online only): superficial only (n = 138), internal valvuloplasty (IVP; n = 77), and IVP plus segmental valve transplantation (SVT) (n = 14). Welch one-way ANOVA showed between-group differences in VCSS at all postoperative time points and at the last follow-up (all P ≤ .042). On Games-Howell testing, the IVP group had a lower VCSS than the superficial-only group at 1, 3, and 6 months and at the last follow-up (MD, −1.12, −0.77, −1.03, and −0.78; all Padj ≤ .026). At the last follow-up, CIVIQ-20 also differed among groups (P < .001), with lower scores in the IVP group than in the superficial-only group (MD, −2.93; 95% CI, −4.57 to −1.29; Padj < .001). The IVP + SVT subgroup was small (n = 14), limiting power for between-group comparisons.

VCSS decreased over follow-up in all groups (Supplementary Table V, online only). Group 3 improved more rapidly than group 1 early after surgery (1 month: β, −1.30; 95% CI, −2.04 to −0.56; P < .001) and remained lower at 6 months (β, −0.82; 95% CI, −1.57 to −0.08; P = .030), with no significant differences at the last follow-up. By technique, IVP was associated with a lower VCSS than superficial-only management at 1 month (β, −1.12; 95% CI, −1.78 to −0.46; P < .001) and persisted through the last follow-up (β, −0.78; 95% CI, −1.44 to −0.12; P = .021), whereas IVP + SVT did not differ significantly. These patterns were visually corroborated by trajectory plots of mean VCSS (95% CI) by group (Supplementary Fig 1, online only) and by technique (Supplementary Fig 2, online only).

Overall, 53 of 59 ulcers healed (89.8%), with healing proportions of 84.8% (28/33), 93.3% (14/15), and 100.0% (11/11) in groups 1, 2, and 3, respectively (P = .310). The median time to healing did not differ significantly (overall, 2.76 months; group 1, 3.30 months; group 2, 2.68 months; group 3, 2.37 months; P = .223). Time to ulcer healing was assessed with multivariable Cox regression, censoring nonhealed patients at the last follow-up, and healing at the last follow-up was evaluated using Firth penalized logistic regression to reduce small-sample bias (Supplementary Table VI, online only). Covariates included age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, alcohol use, prior DVT, perforator reflux, perforator management, duration of postoperative compression therapy, baseline ulcer area, ulcer location, and ulcer duration.

In the multivariable Cox model, the hazard ratio for healing was 3.20 for group 2 vs group 1 (95% CI, 0.73-14.09; P = .124) and 0.88 for group 3 vs group 1 (95% CI, 0.21-3.76; P = .862). In the Firth logistic regression model, the odds ratio for healing at last follow-up was 3.04 for group 2 vs group 1 (95% CI, 0.08-113.04; P = .547) and 0.97 for group 3 vs group 1 (95% CI, 0.05-17.87; P = .981). Given the small number of nonhealing events, these estimates were imprecise, with wide CIs.

When stratified by deep venous procedure (Supplementary Table VII, online only), ulcer healing was common across groups. IVP was associated with a shorter time to healing and a significantly higher hazard of healing compared with superficial-only treatment in both unadjusted and adjusted Cox models. In contrast, IVP + SVT showed a similar direction of effect, but did not attain statistical significance, likely owing to limited power from the small sample size.

Duplex ultrasound valve function after deep venous intervention

Postoperative duplex ultrasound examination after deep venous intervention showed a high rate of reflux normalization: 96.7% (88/91) at 1 month, 98.9% (90/91) at 3 months, and 94.5% (86/91) at 6 months (reflux time <0.5 second). Residual reflux, when present, was mild (<1.0 second).

Complications

All procedures were completed as planned. Early (≤30-day) adverse events were limited to minor bleeding/hematoma and wound-related complications. In group 1, puncture site bruising occurred in one patient and local skin changes were noted (induration, n = 4; pigmentation, n = 4). In group 2, early wound events included one hematoma, one incisional fat liquefaction, and one delayed wound healing. In group 3, wound complications included two hematomas, one incisional infection, and one incisional fat liquefaction. No major bleeding requiring reoperation and no symptomatic DVT or pulmonary embolism occurred. No late failures or reinterventions were recorded during follow-up.

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective cohort of 229 patients with concomitant deep and superficial venous reflux but without significant iliac or proximal deep venous obstruction, treatment strategies incorporating correction of deep venous reflux—performed either concomitantly with superficial surgery or in a staged fashion—were associated with lower postoperative VCSS and CIVIQ-20 compared with superficial surgery alone. This study sought to describe real-world outcomes for reflux-dominant CVD characterized by multisource reflux and to inform clinical decision-making in this setting.

Recent authoritative guidelines emphasize multidimensional decision-making that integrates anatomical compartment (superficial, deep, and perforator systems), etiology (primary vs post-thrombotic), proximal obstruction, and clinical severity.30,31 Comparative evidence remains limited for superficial-only intervention vs combined strategies addressing deep venous reflux, and for whether a concomitant or staged approach is preferable. Prior work also indicates that superficial and deep reflux commonly coexist in symptomatic chronic venous insufficiency, and that measures of deep reflux severity may be associated with clinical outcomes after superficial treatment.32 Deep venous valve-focused procedures (eg, valvuloplasty and other reconstructive options) have been reported for refractory disease, although available evidence remains heterogeneous and dependent on center experience.23,24

The observed between-strategy differences are compatible with the hypothesis that persistent proximal deep reflux may contribute to sustained distal venous hypertension during ambulation and orthostasis; accordingly, superficial-only intervention may not fully address the proximal reflux component in selected patients. Improvements in CIVIQ-20 paralleled reductions in VCSS, suggesting concordance between clinical severity and patient-reported quality of life. The absence of a statistically significant difference at 3 months may reflect early postoperative convergence related to compression, wound recovery, and short-term effects of superficial procedures, with separation emerging at later intervals.

In procedure-stratified analyses, IVP was associated with a lower VCSS at multiple time points and a lower CIVIQ-20 at the last follow-up compared with superficial-only treatment; inferences for IVP + SVT were limited by the small subgroup size. Ulcer-related analyses suggested broadly favorable healing across groups, with point estimates directionally favoring combined strategies in some comparisons; however, the ulcer sample size and the number of nonhealing events were limited, and adjusted estimates were imprecise. Postoperative duplex ultrasound examination demonstrated high rates of reflux normalization after deep venous intervention, supporting that valve-related reflux parameters improved in most treated patients.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, treatment selection was clinician driven, introducing potential selection bias and residual confounding. Second, technique-stratified inference—particularly for IVP + SVT—should be considered exploratory. Third, duplex surveillance differed by strategy, limiting direct comparability of hemodynamic follow-up across groups. Finally, because patients with iliac outflow obstruction were excluded, these findings apply primarily to reflux-dominant disease and should not be extrapolated to obstruction-dominant or mixed phenotypes.

Among patients with multisource reflux without iliac obstruction, strategies incorporating deep reflux correction were associated with more favorable postoperative VCSS and CIVIQ-20 compared with superficial treatment alone, and staged intervention appeared to be a clinically viable alternative to concomitant surgery. Future prospective or multicenter studies with standardized superficial/perforator management, predefined staging criteria, and longer follow-up are warranted to evaluate durability and ulcer recurrence.

Conclusions

In reflux-associated CVD without iliac/proximal deep venous obstruction, combined correction of deep and superficial reflux was associated with lower VCSS, lower CIVIQ-20, and a trend toward more favorable ulcer healing metrics.
