Economic Benefits of Surgical Aortic Valve Replacement on Patients With Symptomatic Aortic Valve Regurgitation
Authors: Sasha Anne Still, Michael Ryan, Candace Gunnarsson, Natalie Heidrich, James E. Davies
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAortic regurgitation is distinguished by the backflow of blood from the aorta into the left ventricle. American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines recommend surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) for patients with symptomatic aortic regurgitation (sAR). This study estimates the difference in mortality, health care use, and costs between patients with sAR who receive SAVR within 12 months of diagnosis versus those who do not.Methods and ResultsWe used the Optum United Healthcare database to identify 132 317 patients diagnosed with sAR from 2016 to 2021 who had at least 6 months of enrollment before sAR and 12 months of enrollment after. Criteria were no history of aortic stenosis or transcatheter aortic valve replacement and ≥2 visits for heart failure, angina, dyspnea, or syncope. Outcomes were all‐cause mortality, health care use, and annualized cost. Baseline differences in demographics and comorbidities were adjusted with inverse propensity score weighting. We modeled survival and estimated health care use and costs using Cox proportional hazards and general linear models, respectively. Of the 132 317 patients, 400 underwent SAVR within 12 months of diagnosis. They were on average younger, more often men, and with a slightly higher Elixhauser Comorbidity Index score. After inverse propensity score weighting, patients with sAR who had SAVR had lower mortality, fewer inpatient and emergency department visits, fewer hospital days, and lower annualized cost.ConclusionsSAVR performed within 12 months of an sAR diagnosis is associated with improved mortality and lower annualized health care use and costs. These clinical and economic benefits should be considered when managing patients with sAR.

Aortic regurgitation (AR) is due to malcoaptation of aortic valve leaflets leading to diastolic reflux of blood from the aorta into the left ventricle. In the acute setting, severe AR leads to acute volume overload with subsequent elevation in left ventricular end diastolic pressure and decreased stroke volume. Such cases frequently call for urgent surgical correction to prevent hemodynamic collapse. Chronic AR, on the other hand, may remain clinically quiescent for years, because progressive volume overload and left ventricular enlargement blunt the physiologic impact of worsening valvular regurgitation; yet, this too will eventually lead to heart failure. For patients with asymptomatic AR and normal cardiac function, the timing of operative intervention is controversial. Symptoms are an important indicator for operative intervention in chronic severe AR, because they herald increasing volume overload and exacerbation of heart failure. Of patients with chronic severe AR, those with severe symptoms have a yearly mortality rate of ~25%, whereas the rates for those with mild or no symptoms are 6.3% and 5.8%, respectively. 1

Current American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association guidelines recommend surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) in patients with severe AR who are symptomatic or those who are asymptomatic with depressed ejection fraction (EF) (class I indication). 2 They also recommend aortic valve replacement for patients with severe AR who are asymptomatic with preserved EF and a significantly dilated left ventricle (class IIa indication). On the other hand, asymptomatic patients with severe AR and preserved yet declining EF or a progressively dilating left ventricle should undergo periodic monitoring. Multiple published reports have shown excellent outcomes with early SAVR for patients with chronic, asymptomatic AR. 3 , 4 , 5

The purpose of this study was to analyze the real‐world effectiveness of SAVR in patients with symptomatic AR (sAR). In doing so, we compared differences in mortality, health care use, and costs between patients with sAR who undergo SAVR within 12 months of their sAR diagnosis versus those who undergo medical management alone.

Methods

Data Source and Study Inclusion

The authors declare that all supporting data are available within the article. This study used Optum data files containing data from United Healthcare claims for patients across the United States from 2016 to 2021. Optum data provide a continuum of treatment and cost, such as medications by therapeutic area, provider notes with treatment rationale, and cost by procedure and condition. All data used to perform this analysis were deidentified and accessed in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. As a retrospective analysis of a deidentified database, the research was exempt from institutional review board review under 45 CFR 46.101(b)(4).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

To be included in this retrospective database analysis, patients had to have a claim for International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) for AR. The AR must be considered symptomatic, defined as ≥2 visits (inpatient or outpatient) for either heart failure, angina, dyspnea, or syncope. Time 0 was the last date all conditions were met.

Patients had to have a minimum of 6 months of continuous enrollment before their time 0 (diagnosis of sAR) to capture baseline comorbid conditions and 12 months of continuous enrollment following time 0 as a landmark period to look for patients receiving SAVR (Figure 1). Patients were excluded from the study if they had any record of aortic stenosis at any time in the data set or transcatheter aortic valve replacement any time after index. Follow‐up time was consistent for both cohorts, with an average±SD of 1.7±1.3 years (P=0.9863).

Variables of Interest

There are 2 main cohorts for this study: (1) treatment group: patients with sAR must receive a SAVR during the 12‐month landmark period and (2) control group: patients with sAR cannot have a record of receiving a SAVR during the 12‐month landmark period. Outcomes of interest for this study included mortality, all‐cause hospitalizations, cardiovascular‐related hospitalizations, total hospital days, emergency department (ED) visits, outpatient visits, office visits, and costs. All outcomes were annualized to reflect the total outcome in the postperiod divided by the number of years of follow‐up. All count outcomes were rounded to the nearest whole number to use Poisson and negative binomial count‐regression methodologies. Covariates included age, sex, race, region, index year, insurance type (commercial or Medicare Advantage), and the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, which identifies 31 categories of comorbidities associated with mortality.

Statistical Analysis

For this analysis we used inverse propensity score weighting due to differences in sample size between treatment and control. The propensity score was calculated from a logistic regression model as the probability that a patient received a SAVR given the patient's characteristics and other important factors. These characteristics are the covariates in the logistic regression model and include patient demographics: age (at index), sex (men, women), race and ethnicity (Asian, Black, White, and Hispanic), region (Midwest, Northeast, South, and West), index year (2016–2020), insurance type (commercial, Medicare), and patient comorbidities measured by the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index.

The Cox proportional hazards model was used to estimate survival, and general linear models were generated for annualized health care use (all‐cause hospitalizations, cardiovascular‐related hospitalizations, ED visits, hospital days, outpatient hospital visits, office/clinic visits) and annualized total costs. All outcomes were modeled separately.

Results

The total number of patients identified with sAR was 132 317. Four hundred (0.30%) had a record of SAVR within the first 12 months of their sAR diagnosis (Figure 1). For the 131 917 patients who did not receive a SAVR during the 12‐month landmark period, these patients could go on to receive a SAVR during the outcome period; however, few did, so only 97 (0.07%) patients had a record of a SAVR anytime following the landmark period.

Compared with the control group, patients with sAR who underwent SAVR were younger on average (65 versus 75 years old), more often men (71% versus 48%), less Hispanic (6% versus 10%), exhibited a slighter higher Elixhauser Comorbidity Index score (5.1 versus 4.8), and had a higher rate of commercial insurance (36% versus 11%). Regional distributions were similar, with the South dominant in both cohorts. Distribution of index year was also similar between the 2 groups (Table 1).

Patients with sAR who underwent SAVR exhibited a mortality benefit at 1 year (4% versus 10%), 3 years (9% versus 26%), and 5 years (15% versus 39%) in patients who underwent SAVR compared with those who did not (Figure 2). After inverse propensity score weighting, the mortality benefit was statistically significant (hazard ratio [HR], 0.384 [95% CI, 0.375–0.394]; P<0.0001). Additionally, patients demonstrated a statistically significant decrease in estimated annualized inpatient visits (0.33 versus 0.43; P=0.0039), annualized ED visits (0.54 versus 0.70; P=0.0002), and annualized hospital days (2.70 versus 3.14; P<0.0001; Table 2) after inverse propensity score weighting. Annualized costs were also significantly lower, with a difference of $7655 ($33 744 versus $41 399; Figure 3). For the 400 patients in the cohort receiving SAVR, their annualized costs did not include the cost of the SAVR procedure, because this occurred during the landmark period. No differences were found in cardiovascular inpatient visits or office visits. Outpatient visits were higher in patients who underwent SAVR.

Discussion

After adjusting for differences in patient demographics and comorbidity, patients with sAR who received a SAVR within 12 months of their incident sAR diagnosis experienced improved early and intermediate survival. Additionally, those who underwent SAVR experienced significantly lower estimated annualized inpatient and ED visits, average hospital days, and estimated annualized costs (18% reduction).

To improve survival and prevent the hemodynamic consequences of chronic left ventricular volume overload and clinical repercussions, symptomatic aortic regurgitation was made a class I indication for SAVR. 2 However, even after surgical valve replacement, this patient population incurs poorer survival and increased readmission for heart failure compared with patients with asymptomatic AR. 6 , 7 Demeester et al demonstrated decreased 10‐year overall and cardiovascular survival as well as increased readmissions for heart failure among patients who underwent SAVR for class I indication compared with patients with class II indication. They also reported increased long‐term mortality in patients with class II indications after SAVR, especially if EF <55% and left ventricular end‐systolic dimension >20 to 22 mm/m2. 6

Studies continue to demonstrate improved outcomes following early SAVR in patients with asymptomatic severe aortic regurgitation. In a retrospective study of 230 patients with asymptomatic severe AR, EF ≥50%, and left ventricular end‐diastolic dimension of >70 mm, those who underwent early SAVR compared with conservative management demonstrated improved 3‐year, 5‐year, and 10‐year cardiovascular survival. 4 Furthermore, Turk et al showed significantly improved mortality at 1, 5, and 10 years with early SAVR compared with medical management alone among patients with asymptomatic severe AR. This benefit increased substantially within a subgroup of EF >50%, left ventricular end‐diastolic dimension <70 mm, and left ventricular end‐systolic diameter <50 mm (5‐year mortality 5% versus 43%). 8 Additional studies reiterate a survival benefit with early valve replacement in patients with asymptomatic, severe AR. 3 , 9

Aortic valve replacement for AR assists in preservation of postoperative left ventricular function, reiterating the importance of treating AR expeditiously, even in the context of asymptomatic disease. The current study supports these findings, because 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year mortality rates were significantly lower in patients receiving SAVR compared with medical management alone (Kaplan Meier survival at 5 years: 15% versus 39%). Chronic aortic AR leads to left ventricular volume and pressure overload, which begets a series of compensatory and deleterious myocardial changes leading to congestive heart failure. Progression of heart failure and associated clinical symptoms inevitably lead to increased frequency of outpatient physician appointments, ED visits, and inpatient hospitalizations. This higher level of care temporizes symptoms until definitive surgical treatment is considered. Aortic valve disease accounts for a significant incremental effect on annual patient health care expenditure. 10 Implementing surgical cure for severe valvular disease not only improves survival but also has proven to be a cost‐effective strategy in the lifetime management of disease. 11 , 12 Our study demonstrates that early intervention with SAVR can effectively reduce health care use and expenditures while also improving survival. Moreover, our data reflect real‐world patient characteristics and outcomes across the country as compared with evidence from controlled clinical trials.

Limitations of this study include the fact that sAR was ascertained through sources of automated data that rely on medical coding; therefore, a reporting bias exists. We could only control for known confounders, largely of generalized illness, and lacked the ability to control for unknown confounders, ideally by randomization, stratification, or matching. Statistical modeling with inverse propensity score weighting was used to control for the potential confounding effect of known variables with between‐group differences. Although statistical models controlled for several factors, models could not control for some variables that are not included in an administrative database, such as echocardiography results, New York Heart Association class, or volume for the centers who performed early aortic valve replacement, because increasing hospital/practice volumes are associated with better outcomes. Furthermore, there is the potential for a residual confounding effect due to the fact that we could not control for things that make a person more likely to receive an intervention. Finally, the severity of AR could not be abstracted in the Optum database. As a requisite for severity, the presence of symptoms was used and defined as ≥2 visits (inpatient or outpatient) for either heart failure, angina, dyspnea, or syncope.

Conclusions

In our claims analysis from 2016 to 2021, we identified 132 317 patients with sAR and found that only 400 (0.30%) underwent SAVR within the first 12 months of their diagnosis. Those who underwent SAVR experienced both a mortality benefit and demonstrated significantly lower estimated annualized inpatient and ED visits, average hospital days, and estimated annualized costs. Consideration should be given to aggressive surgical management of sAR in appropriately selected patients.
