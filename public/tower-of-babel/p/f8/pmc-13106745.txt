Contribution of tissue clearing and 3D image analysis to in vitro modeling of human cortical development
Authors: Antoine Retho, Annousha Devi Govindan, Marie-Laure Bonnet, Sébastien Brot, Afsaneh Gaillard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThree-dimensional (3D) brain-like models such as neurospheres and organoids recapitulate key aspects of human cortical development, providing valuable platforms for studying neurogenesis, disease mechanisms, and translational applications. However, their characterization has traditionally relied on two-dimensional (2D) histology, which fails to capture spatially clustered populations and complex cytoarchitecture. Tissue-clearing methods combined with advanced imaging now enable volumetric analyses that preserve the native 3D organization of neural cells. We applied the iDISCO+ clearing protocol to human iPSC-derived neurospheres and performed whole-mount immunolabeling followed by light-sheet fluorescence microscopy. Quantitative analyses of cell-type composition were benchmarked against conventional 2D cryosectioning. Temporal studies were performed from day 25 to day 60 of differentiation using layer-specific cortical markers (BRN2, CTIP2, and FOXP2) to assess the dynamics of superficial and deep cortical neuron generation. Finally, the impact of 3D analysis on marker co-localization was evaluated using spot-based spatial quantification of CTIP2 and COUP-TF1 expressions. Benchmarking volumetric quantification against conventional histology revealed that both methods produced comparable estimates for broadly distributed markers such as Ki67 and CTIP2. In contrast, 2D analysis substantially underestimated clustered populations, with SATB2+ and especially FOXP2+ neurons detected at significantly higher proportions in 3D. Building on this, temporal analysis of 3D-cleared neurospheres demonstrated a marked expansion of BRN2+ superficial neurons between day 25 and day 60, a robust late-stage increase in CTIP2+ deep-layer neurons, and a maturation-dependent rise of FOXP2+ layer VI neurons. In addition, 3D analysis enabled robust quantification of CTIP2+/COUP-TF1+ co-expressing neurons, revealing significantly higher estimates than 2D sectional analysis by integrating spatial information across the entire neurosphere volume. These dynamics underscore the capacity of 3D volumetric imaging to capture both homogeneous and spatially restricted neuronal populations, providing a faithful readout of cortical layer specification during neurosphere differentiation. Our study demonstrates that iDISCO+ combined with light-sheet microscopy provides a powerful and reliable approach for quantitative, multiscale characterization of neurospheres. By preserving spatial integrity and enabling volumetric assessment of both single-marker expression and co-localization, this approach reduces biases inherent to 2D sectioning and improves the accuracy of temporal and spatial analyses in 3D neural models. Introduction

Advances in stem cell technologies have led to the generation of three-dimensional (3D) brain-like structures, including brain organoids and neurospheres, which recapitulate key features of human neurodevelopment in vitro. Cortical organoids and neurospheres are 3D multicellular aggregates composed of neural progenitors, differentiated neurons, and glial cells. These in vitro models recapitulate key aspects of early human cortical development, including cell-type diversity, spatial patterning, and dynamic developmental trajectories1–4. In addition, these 3D culture models are employed to model neurodegenerative diseases5–8, and serve as a potential source of cells for transplantation9,10.

Therefore, rigorous characterization of these 3D culture models remains essential to ensure their reliability and relevance for translational applications. The characterization of generated cortical 3D models has traditionally relied primarily on 2D histological sectioning, which can limit the accuracy of data interpretation and hinder a comprehensive understanding of neuronal development. By contrast, emerging 3D imaging methods enable volumetric quantification of intact structures, capturing multilayered architectures and spatially clustered cell populations that may otherwise be underestimated.

Tissue-clearing technologies have greatly expanded the potential of 3D analyses by enabling high-resolution visualization of intact biological specimens. Among them, solvent-based approaches such as iDISCO have proven particularly effective for neuronal models, combining immunolabeling with optical clearing to preserve cytoarchitecture and permit deep antibody penetration11. The improved iDISCO+ protocol further minimizes tissue shrinkage, providing robust volumetric immunostaining and light-sheet imaging of brain organoids and neurospheres2,12. Despite these advances, the quantitative exploitation of large 3D datasets remains challenging, underscoring the need for standardized benchmarking against conventional methods.

Robust molecular markers are indispensable for such analyses. The mammalian cerebral cortex is organized into six distinct layers. Neurons within each cortical layer express distinct molecular markers that reflect their identity, position, and functional role. For example, CTIP2 is predominantly expressed in layer V projection neurons, while FOXP2 marks layer VI neurons. In contrast, upper-layer neurons express markers such as SATB2, and BRN2, which are associated with callosal and intracortical connectivity. These markers not only allow precise identification of neuronal subtypes but also provide a molecular framework for tracking their temporal emergence and spatial distribution in vitro.

In this study, we assessed the temporal generation of cortical neurons in 3D neurospheres derived from Human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSC) by comparing classical 2D analysis methods with advanced 3D approaches.

Materials and methods

Generation of three dimensional (3D) cortical neurospheres

Human dermal fibroblasts, derived from donor skin biopsies, were commercially obtained from CliniSciences (France), and subsequently reprogrammed into hiPSCs in our laboratory using the StemMACS mRNA Reprogramming kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-104-460) as described previously13. The genomic stability of resulting hiPSC lines was assessed using the iCS-digital PSC 24-probe kit (Stemgenomics, Montpellier, France). hiPSCs were cultured on 6-well plates coated with vitronectin (10 µg/ml, Life Technologies, A14700) in StemMACS iPS-Brew XF (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-104-368) until reaching a confluence of 80%.

For the induction of 3D cortical neurospheres, a modified protocol of Motono et al.14 was applied (Fig. 1A). Briefly, hiPSC were dissociated into single cells using Accutase Pro (Gibco life tech, 11105-01) and counted with Trypan blue. To generate 3D neurospheres, 9 000 cells were seeded per well in ultra-low adhesion 96-well plate (S-BIO, MS-9096 MZ) and cultured for 18 days in embryoid body (EB) medium, composed of DMEM-Ham’s F12 + Glutamax (Gibco, 31331), Knockout Serum Replacement (15%, Life Technologies, 10828-028), non-essential amino acids (0.1mM, Gibco, 11140-50), and 2-mercaptoethanol (0.1mM, Gibco, 21985-023). Y27632 (10 µM, Miltenyi Biotec, 130-103-922) was added for the first 3 days to enhance cell survival. To promote ectodermal fate, SB431542 (10 µM, OZYM, STE04-0010) and bFGF (10 ng/mL, Gibco, PHG0026) were added during the first 6 days. To specify anterior cortical identity, LDN193189 (0.1 µM, OZYM, STE04-0074) and C59-WNT (10 nM, Tocris Bioscience, 5148) were included from day 0 to day 18. Medium was replaced every 3 days.

After 18 days, neurospheres were transferred to 24-well culture plates coated with poly-L-ornithine (50 µg/mL, Sigma, 03655), Laminin (5 µg/mL, Invitrogen, 23017-015) and fibronectin (5 µg/mL, Sigma, F2006) and cultured for 28 days in Neurobasal Medium (Gibco, 21103-049), B-27 without vitamin A (2%, Gibco, 12587010), L-Glutamine (2mM, Gibco,25030-032) and Penicillin/Streptomycin (10 units/mL, Gibco, 15140122), with half-medium changes every 3 days.

Sample preparation and immunostaining

Neurospheres were harvested at different time points: day 25, day 31, day 39, day 46, day 53 and day 60. On the day of fixation, neurospheres were washed in Dulbecco’s Phosphate Buffer Saline (DPBS1X, Gibco, 14190-094) and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 15710) for 30 min at room temperature (RT) (Fig. 1A). After fixation, neurospheres were rinsed in DPBS 1X and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose/DPBS1X at 4 °C until further use.

Immunostaining on cryosections

For 2D analysis, samples were cryosectioned into 20 μm-thick slices and mounted on slides. Sections were rinsed with PBS 0.1 M, permeabilized and blocked in a solution containing 0,3% Triton X-100, 3% BSA and PBS 1X for 1h30 at RT. The samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the appropriate primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution. The following day, sections were washed in PBS 1X and incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 1h30 at RT. Nuclear staining was performed with DAPI (1:10 000, Sigma-Aldrich, D9542). Finally, sections were rinsed in PBS 1X and coverslipped using DePex mountant (Sigma-Aldrich, 06522). All primary and secondary antibodies used in this study are listed in Table 1.

Whole-mount immunostaining

Whole-mount immunolabelling of intact neurospheres was performed using an adapted iDISCO protocol from Renier’s Lab11. Briefly, neurospheres were washed thrice in DPBS 1X and then incubated for three days at RT in a blocking/permeabilization solution composed of DPBS 1X, 3% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, A7906), and 0.3% Triton X-100 (Merck, 28817.295). Neurospheres were incubated with primary antibodies for four days at 4 °C under gentle agitation. After washing in DPBS 1X for 24 h, they were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for another four days at 4 °C. Primary and secondary antibody were diluted in the blocking solution. Nuclear staining was performed using TO-PRO-3 (1:5,000, Invitrogen, T30605) for 2 h at RT, followed by six washes in DPBS 1X. As for cryosections, the list of antibodies and dilutions is provided in Table 1.

Tissue clearing process: iDISCO method

Following immunostaining, neurospheres were embedded in 1.2% low-melting point agarose (Invitrogen, 16520050) cast in Lab-Tek® Permanox® 8-well chambers (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 177445) as molds. After 3 h of polymerization at room temperature, agarose-embedded neurospheres were carefully unmolded and transferred to 5 mL amber tubes for the clearing process. Tissue clearing was performed using an adapted iDISCO protocol11, consisting of sequential dehydration in graded methanol series (20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, 100%; Thermo Scientific, 031721.K2) under continuous slow rotation, followed by overnight incubation in 100% methanol. Delipidation was then achieved by 3 h incubation in 33% methanol / 66% dichloromethane (DCM, Sigma, 270997), followed by 2 h in 100% DCM. Finally, refractive index (RI) matching was performed by immersing the samples in ethyl cinnamate (ECi, Sigma-Aldrich, 112372) until optical clearing was obtained. Cleared neurospheres were stored in amber glass tubes at room temperature until imaging.

Imaging and quantification

2D imaging and quantification

Immunostained cryosections were imaged using a Zeiss Axio Imager.M2 microscope equipped with Apotome.2 structured illumination and either a 10x or 20x objective (Carl Zeiss, Germany). For each neurosphere, 8–10 sections were analysed per marker. Quantification of marker-positive cells was performed manually or automatically using Qupath (version 0.4.4), applying predefined thresholds and segmentation criteria. Results were expressed as the percentage of DAPI+ nuclei positive for the marker of interest.

3D imaging and quantification

Cleared neurospheres were imaged with an UltraMicroscope II light-sheet microscope (Miltenyi Biotec, LaVision) equipped with a sCMOS camera (Andor Neo) and a 2x/0,5 objective lens. Image acquisition was performed with a 3 μm-thick light-sheet illumination, and an optical zoom of 2× was applied, resulting in a final effective magnification of 4×. Stacks were acquired with a z-step of 3 μm to ensure optimal axial resolution and complete coverage of the neurospheres. Acquired image stacks were reconstructed in 3D using Imaris software (Bitplane, version 10, Fig. 1B). Nuclear markers were quantified with the “spot” function, using an estimated nuclear diameter of 7 μm. The intensity threshold was empirically adjusted to discriminate true signals from background noise, and each detected nucleus was segmented as an individual spot (Fig. 1C). Quantitative data, corresponding to the number of spots per marker and per neurosphere, were exported as tables. The proportion of marker-positive nuclei was calculated relative to the TO-PRO-3 + population, providing an unbiased quantification of the cellular composition within 3D neurospheres. For co-localization analysis, spot detection was first performed independently for each channel as described above. Co-localization between CTIP2 and COUP-TF1 spots was then assessed using the “Colocalize Spots” module in Imaris. Spots were considered co-localized when the distance between their centers was ≤ 3 μm, allowing partial overlap of nuclear spots and accounting for minor spatial offsets between channels. This threshold was defined based on the estimated nuclear diameter and the axial resolution of the light-sheet imaging system.

Statistics

Quantitative analysis was performed on 3D neurospheres obtained from at least 3 independent cultures. Unless otherwise indicated, data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses and graph generation were carried out using GraphPad Prism (version 9, San Diego CA, USA). Normality of the datasets was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk test. Data having a Gaussian distribution were analyzed using ordinary one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparisons test. When normality was not met, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test. For pairwise comparisons between 2D and 3D analyses, either an unpaired t-test (parametric) or a Mann–Whitney test (non-parametric) was used.

Results

A comparative analysis of 2D sectional and 3D volumetric quantification in neurospheres

To assess the reliability of volumetric quantification, we compared immunofluorescence labelling on cryosections with whole-mount light-sheet imaging of cleared neurospheres at day 46 of differentiation (Fig. 2; see Supplementary Video). At this stage, neurospheres are sufficiently mature and express both proliferative and layer-specific cortical markers, allowing a robust comparison between 2D and 3D quantification approaches. For this comparison, we selected markers with distinct spatial distribution patterns: Ki67, a proliferation marker, and CTIP2, a deep-layer neuronal marker, both of which are broadly and homogeneously expressed; and SATB2 and FOXP2, which are typically organized in discrete clusters. In cryosections (Fig. 2A–D), all four markers were readily detected. The same markers imaged in optically cleared whole neurospheres (Fig. 2A’–D’) showed distribution patterns for Ki67 and CTIP2 that were largely consistent with those observed in cryosections, with both markers appearing evenly distributed throughout the tissue. In contrast, SATB2 and FOXP2 labelled cells were restricted to discrete regions, which became more apparent when the three-dimensional cytoarchitecture of the neurosphere was preserved.

Quantitative comparison further confirmed the observed differences in the spatial distribution of markers (Fig. 2E–H). Quantification of Ki67+ cells revealed 13.92 ± 1.37% of total cells in cryosections, compared to 16.62 ± 1.60% in whole-mount neurospheres, with no statistically significant difference between methods (P = 0.2286; Fig. 2E). The proportion of CTIP2+ cells was 48.99 ± 1.98% in 2D sections and 53.24 ± 1.60% in 3D whole-mount neurospheres, with no significant difference observed (P = 0.1387; Fig. 2F). This consistency indicates that both approaches yield comparable quantification for markers exhibiting broad and homogeneous spatial distribution. In contrast, SATB2+ cells accounted for 10.0 ± 1.52% in 2D sections versus 16.5 ± 1.85% in 3D whole-mount neurospheres (P = 0.014; Fig. 2G), while FOXP2+ cells represented 2.1 ± 0.45% in 2D compared to 18.6 ± 1.63% in 3D (P = 0.0357; Fig. 2H). These striking discrepancies indicate that two-dimensional sampling substantially underestimates the abundance of cell populations that adopt clustered or focal arrangements within neurospheres.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that while sectional analysis remains adequate for markers with homogeneous spatial distribution, accurate quantification of more complex, clustered cellular populations require preservation of the intact three-dimensional architecture. Having established the added value of volumetric imaging for accurate quantification, we next applied this approach to investigate the temporal dynamics of cortical neuron specification in human neurospheres.

A temporal analysis of superficial and deep layer progenitor generation

To illustrate the biological relevance of volumetric quantification beyond methodological validation, we next investigated the temporal dynamics of cortical neuron generation in 3D-cleared neurospheres. Because neurospheres undergo substantial growth and changes in three-dimensional organization over time, temporal analyses were performed exclusively using volumetric quantification to avoid sectioning-related sampling biases inherent to 2D approaches. We therefore performed immunohistochemical analysis on 3D-cleared neurospheres collected between day 25 (D25) and day 60 (D60) of differentiation, using a panel of layer-specific markers: BRN2 (layers II/III), CTIP2 (layer V), and FOXP2 (layer VI).

Immunofluorescence analysis of representative neurospheres revealed dynamic temporal changes in marker expression (Fig. 3). At D25, BRN2+ (Fig. 3A), CTIP2+ (Fig. 3B), and FOXP2+ (Fig. 3C) cells were already detectable in small, nascent spheroids. By D46, the same markers imaged in cleared neurospheres (Fig. 3A’–C’) showed an increase in signal intensity and spheroid size, indicating progressive neuronal differentiation and expansion. At D60, BRN2+, CTIP2+, and FOXP2+ cell populations (Fig. 3A’’–C’’) occupied larger domains within the markedly enlarged spheroids, consistent with ongoing proliferation and maturation of cortical neurons.

Quantitative analysis further highlighted these temporal dynamics (Fig. 3D–G). Analysis of neurosphere growth revealed a significant increase in total cell number across differentiation stages (one-way ANOVA, F = 28.63, p < 0.0001; Fig. 3D). At day 25, neurospheres contained 85,210 ± 6,060 cells, whereas a marked increase was observed at later stages, reaching 300,240 ± 20,577 cells by day 53. A similar number of cells was detected at day 60 (300,558 ± 18,201 cells), with no statistically significant difference between day 53 and day 60. This limited variation over the D53–D60 interval is consistent with the slow kinetics of human cortical development, which extends well beyond the differentiation window examined here.

Next, we analyzed the temporal dynamics of layer-specific cortical markers across the same differentiation stages (Fig. 3E–G). Superficial-layer BRN2+ neurons exhibited a significant increase over time, as revealed by the Kruskal–Wallis test (H = 14.52, p = 0.0009; Fig. 3E). BRN2+ cells increased from 29,468 ± 2,804 at D25 to 101,591 ± 18,466 at D53 and 104,306 ± 18,466 at D60, indicating a pronounced late-stage expansion of upper-layer neurons. For deep-layer V neurons, one-way ANOVA revealed a significant temporal effect on CTIP2 expression (F = 24.72, p < 0.0001; Fig. 3F). The total number of CTIP2+ cells increased from 45,346 ± 3,832 at D46 to 159,497 ± 11,028 at D60, reflecting robust expansion of the layer V neuronal population during this maturation window. Similarly, deep-layer VI neurons marked by FOXP2 increased significantly over time (Kruskal–Wallis test, H = 22.14, p = 0.0005; Fig. 3G). FOXP2+ cells rose from 15,767 ± 1,497 at D25 to 44,500 ± 2,333 at D53 and 43,240 ± 5,711 at D60, consistent with maturation-dependent expansion of this neuronal subtype.

Importantly, when expressed as a proportion of total cells, the relative contribution of cortical neuronal subtypes remained largely stable over time. BRN2+ neurons accounted for 35.2 ± 0.73% of total cells at day 25 and 34.75 ± 1.89% at day 60. Similarly, CTIP2+ neurons represented 50.2 ± 1.11% of the population at day 25 and 50.0 ± 0.44% at day 60. FOXP2+ neurons showed a modest decrease from 17.6 ± 0.68% at day 25 to 13.6 ± 1.17% at day 60. However, none of these changes reached statistical significance when analyzed as proportions, indicating that the observed temporal dynamics primarily reflect overall neurosphere growth rather than major shifts in cortical neuron subtype composition over the differentiation window examined.

These findings collectively show a progressive generation of both superficial and deep cortical neurons during neurosphere differentiation. This temporal evolution is accompanied by a marked increase in spheroid size, highlighting coordinated growth and maturation of neuronal populations over the developmental window examined.

Three-dimensional analysis enables accurate quantification of marker co-localization in neurospheres

After characterizing the temporal dynamics of cortical neuron generation at the level of whole neurospheres, we next assessed whether three-dimensional imaging could also improve the quantification of marker co-localization, representing a more stringent test of spatial accuracy. We compared 2D and 3D approaches for the quantification of marker co-localization within neurospheres by analyzing the co-expression of CTIP2 and COUP-TF1 (NR2F1) (Fig. 4). CTIP2+ neurons lacking COUP-TF1 expression are more closely associated with motor cortical projections. By contrast, CTIP2+/COUP-TF1+ cells likely correspond to posteriorly specified subcortical projection neurons, including populations that, during cortical development, project from sensory areas to targets such as the superior colliculus. Because neurospheres were anteriorly patterned via WNT inhibition, CTIP2+/COUP-TF1+ cells were expected to constitute only a minor population, rendering this analysis a stringent test of co-localization accuracy.

In 2D cryosections, CTIP2 and COUP-TF1 signals were readily detected, and co-localization could be identified within individual sections (Fig. 4A). Optical sections acquired by light-sheet microscopy from cleared neurospheres showed comparable patterns of marker expression at the level of a single optical plane (Fig. 4A′). Thus, when restricted to individual sections, both physical and optical slicing provide similar local information regarding marker overlap.

However, assessment of co-localization based on isolated sections does not capture the full three-dimensional spatial relationships between cells and may lead to biased estimates when extrapolated to the entire spheroid. Although local overlap can be detected in 2D sections, the limited sampling of the spheroid volume leads to an underestimation of co-expressing cells at the global level. By contrast, volumetric acquisition combined with spot-based analysis enabled accurate assessment of spatial co-localization by integrating three-dimensional positional information across the entire neurosphere volume (Fig. 4B). This approach allows reliable discrimination between true co-expressing CTIP2+/COUP-TF1+ cells and apparent overlap arising from partial sampling in individual sections.

Quantitative comparison revealed a significant difference between 2D and 3D analyses (Fig. 4C). Two-dimensional sectional analysis estimated that CTIP2+/COUP-TF1+ cells represented 0.90 ± 0.17% of the total cell population. In contrast, three-dimensional volumetric quantification revealed a significantly higher proportion of co-localized cells, reaching 2.48 ± 0.41% (unpaired t-test, p = 0.0065).

Together, these results indicate that although marker co-localization can be identified at the level of individual physical or optical sections, three-dimensional analysis provides a more accurate and representative quantification by resolving spatial relationships across the entire neurosphere volume.

Discussion

To evaluate the reliability of classical 2D histological quantification methods, we compared them with 3D tissue-clearing-based analysis in assessing the cellular composition of cortical neurospheres at maturation stage D46. This direct comparison allowed us to determine whether 2D sectioning provides an accurate representation of cell populations with varying spatial distribution patterns. We selected four markers for this analysis: Ki67 and CTIP2, which are broadly and homogeneously distributed throughout the neurosphere, and SATB2 and FOXP2, which are more sparsely expressed and organized in discrete clusters.

Our results demonstrated that for homogeneously distributed markers such as Ki67 and CTIP2, 2D and 3D quantification methods yielded comparable results. This suggests that, in the case of uniformly distributed cell populations, 2D histological methods can reliably estimate cell proportions14. However, the situation differed markedly for SATB2+ and FOXP2+ cells, which showed significant discrepancies between 2D and 3D analyses. Specifically, 2D quantification underestimated the proportion of these cells compared to the 3D method, likely due to the clustered and spatially restricted organization of these populations within the neurosphere. Similar limitations of thin-section sampling have been highlighted in previous studies of organoids and neurospheres11,15.

The underrepresentation observed in 2D analysis can be attributed to the inherent limitations of thin tissue sections, which may fail to capture entire clusters. Such partial sampling introduces bias and misrepresents the distribution and abundance of spatially localized cell types. In contrast, the 3D clearing and imaging approach preserves the structural integrity of the neurospheres, allowing for full volumetric assessment and accurate quantification of cell populations in their native spatial context.

Beyond quantitative accuracy, volumetric imaging provides clear advantages for assessing cellular localization and spatial organization within neurospheres. Three-dimensional analysis confirmed that neurons expressing markers of different cortical layers do not display the laminar organization typical of cortical neurons but are instead distributed throughout the spheroid volume in dispersed or clustered patterns, consistent with previous reports2,16. Such organizational features are difficult to robustly assess using 2D sections alone, where apparent stratification may arise from sectioning orientation, partial volume representation, or local clustering.

These limitations become even more pronounced when assessing marker co-localization. Although overlap between markers can be detected within individual sections, two-dimensional analysis fails to capture full three-dimensional spatial relationships and yields biased estimates when extrapolated to the entire spheroid. Thus, co-expressing cell populations are prone to underestimation when analyses rely on partial sampling of the spheroid volume. This issue is particularly relevant for sparsely represented populations such as CTIP2+/COUP-TF1+ neurons. The scarcity of CTIP2+/COUP-TF1+ neurons observed here is consistent with deliberate anterior patterning of cortical neurons via WNT inhibition, which restricts posterior cortical identities17. As a numerically limited yet biologically meaningful population, these cells are especially vulnerable to underestimation due to sampling bias. In this context, CTIP2+/COUP-TF1+ neurons constitute a biologically defined but numerically limited population, making their accurate quantification especially sensitive to sampling bias.

In this context, 3D volumetric analysis integrates spatial information across the entire neurosphere, enabling more accurate and representative quantification. Importantly, the discrepancies observed between 2D and 3D approaches were consistent across neurospheres and independent differentiation experiments, indicating systematic methodological biases rather than batch-specific variability. The magnitude of these discrepancies depended on marker distribution, with broadly expressed populations yielding comparable estimates, whereas spatially restricted or clustered populations were reproducibly underestimated by sectional analysis.

While volumetric quantification overcomes these limitations by analyzing intact whole neurospheres, it also imposes practical constraints, as each sample can typically be labelled with only a limited number of antibodies. Accordingly, 2D and 3D approaches should be viewed as complementary: sectional analysis remains suitable for high-throughput screening or extensive marker panels, whereas 3D volumetric imaging provides a robust advantage for accurate quantification of spatially complex or heterogeneous cell populations.

Beyond these methodological considerations, our findings have important implications for the interpretation and application of 3D neural models. While 2D methods remain accessible and valuable for preliminary analyses or markers with widespread expression, our data underscore the necessity of 3D imaging techniques for accurately assessing spatially complex or heterogeneous populations. This is particularly relevant in developmental neuroscience models, where cell positioning and clustering carry functional significance. Additionally, the potential misrepresentation of cellular composition is particularly critical in regenerative medicine, where the therapeutic efficacy of grafted neurospheres is highly dependent on their cellular composition. Previous research has highlighted that variability in transplantation outcomes often stems from the heterogeneity of the neurosphere populations used. Specifically, lineage-restricted progenitor cells tend to exhibit superior survival, migration, and differentiation capabilities in postnatal or adult brains compared to more pluripotent counterparts18. Temporal analysis using the iDISCO technique could therefore serve as a powerful tool to determine the optimal maturation stage of neurospheres for transplantation. By correlating cellular composition with developmental timepoints, we may better define the ideal transplantation window that maximizes therapeutic potential and minimizes variability in regenerative outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our comparative analysis demonstrates that 3D quantification provides a more accurate and spatially faithful representation of the cellular architecture in cortical neurosphere models. Future studies relying on 3D culture systems, such as brain organoids, should consider integrating volumetric imaging approaches to avoid misinterpretation of cellular composition and organization.
