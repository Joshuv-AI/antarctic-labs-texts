Decoding the link between ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction: a pathophysiological and clinical perspective
Authors: Ling Chen, Senrong Luo, Mingzhu Feng, Ayush Chandra, José Fidel Baizabal-Carvallo, Mohammad Mofatteh, Jiale Wu, Yuankang He, Zhikai Chen, Xiaoru Zeng, Junrong Tu, Xuejun Wang, Xuxing Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIschemic stroke and myocardial infarction (MI) are among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality globally. Recent research has increasingly highlighted the significant interrelation between these two vascular events, emphasizing shared pathophysiological mechanisms, overlapping risk factors, and complex clinical outcomes. This review explores the multifaceted relationship between ischemic stroke and MI, focusing on their common underlying mechanisms, including atherosclerosis, thromboembolism, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction. Both conditions often co-occur due to their shared risk factors, such as hypertension, diabetes, and smoking, leading to compounded health risks for affected individuals. This review aims to synthesize current evidence on the epidemiological, pathophysiological, and clinical interrelationships between ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction, highlighting practical clinical implications and areas for future research. Although ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction primarily affect distinct organ systems, atypical presentations and shared systemic effects can occasionally complicate diagnosis. Cardiac complications are common in acute ischemic stroke, while stroke-like symptoms may arise in patients with acute MI, especially when silent infarctions occur. This diagnostic challenge underscores the need for advanced neuroimaging and cardiac imaging modalities, alongside biomarkers like D-dimer, BNP, and troponins, to enhance early detection and guide therapeutic decisions. Therapeutic strategies for patients with both conditions must be comprehensive, combining acute management with long-term secondary prevention. While antiplatelet therapy, thrombolysis, and anticoagulation are essential in the acute phase, long-term management involves a focus on controlling cardiovascular risk factors, such as blood pressure and cholesterol, and promoting lifestyle modifications. The role of interdisciplinary care—particularly collaboration between cardiology and neurology—is essential for improving outcomes and minimizing complications. The intersection of ischemic stroke and MI presents significant clinical challenges but also opportunities for enhanced patient management. Integrated diagnostic approaches, personalized treatments, and further research into molecular mechanisms are vital for improving the prognosis of individuals affected by both conditions.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Myocardial infarction, Pathophysiology, Perspectives, Link

Introduction

Background and significance

Ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction (MI) remain among the most devastating cardiovascular events globally, often leading to significant disability, diminished quality of life, and death. Each year, millions of people are affected by these two conditions. According to the World Health Organization, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of death globally, accounting for an estimated 17.9 million deaths annually, with ischemic heart disease and stroke comprising the majority of these cases [1]. Over the years, increasing clinical evidence has pointed to a deeper connection between cardiac and cerebral vascular events, moving beyond their isolated presentations. Despite their distinct clinical manifestations, MI primarily affecting the myocardium and ischemic stroke targeting the cerebral circulation both share a common vascular origin and frequently coexist simultaneously or may occur sequentially in the same patient, a phenomenon that continues to intrigue clinicians and researchers alike. Myocardial infarction is defined by necrosis of cardiac myocytes due to prolonged ischemia, usually resulting from occlusion of coronary arteries by a thrombus formed on a ruptured atherosclerotic plaque [2]. Ischemic stroke, on the other hand, is characterized by the sudden loss of cerebral blood flow, typically resulting from embolic or thrombotic occlusion of cerebral arteries [3]. Although these diseases affect different organ systems, they share overlapping risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, smoking, obesity, sedentary lifestyle, and advanced age [4, 5]. These commonalities point to the systemic nature of vascular pathology underlying both conditions.

Emerging data suggest that patients who experience an MI are at increased risk for subsequent ischemic stroke, particularly during the early weeks following the cardiac event [6]. Conversely, those who suffer a stroke are more susceptible to myocardial injury due to autonomic dysfunction, inflammation, and shared pathophysiologic mechanisms [7]. The notion of a “cardio-cerebral” continuum, reflecting the deep interconnection between the heart and brain, is thus gaining recognition in modern medicine. Understanding the intricate relationship between ischemic stroke and MI is vital for several reasons. First, timely recognition of coexistent or impending complications can significantly improve clinical outcomes. For instance, an undetected left ventricular thrombus following an MI can detach, embolizing to the cerebral circulation and causing a devastating stroke [8]. Secondly, many therapeutic strategies, such as antithrombotic agents, statins, or lifestyle interventions, have dual benefits in preventing both cardiac and cerebral ischemic events, underscoring the need for integrated management approaches [9].

Despite the growing awareness of the link between these two entities, the exact biological pathways, clinical correlations, and prognostic implications remain under-investigated. As healthcare systems shift toward more personalized and holistic models of care, recognizing patients who are at the intersection of cardiac and cerebral ischemia becomes crucial. A comprehensive understanding of this dual pathology can lead to more effective prevention strategies, earlier diagnoses, and better long-term management for affected individuals [10].

Objective of the review

The primary objective of this literature review is to decode the complex and multifaceted relationship between ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction, focusing on both pathophysiological and clinical perspectives. By delving into shared mechanisms such as atherosclerosis, inflammation, thromboembolism, and neuro-cardiac interactions, this review aims to synthesize current knowledge that highlights the heart–brain connection in ischemic vascular events.

This endeavor is particularly relevant in the context of increasing global burdens of non-communicable diseases, wherein cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases often manifest in clusters rather than in isolation. Recent literature has underscored how inflammation and endothelial dysfunction serve as common denominators in both MI and ischemic stroke [11], and how systemic atherothrombosis does not discriminate between cerebral and coronary vessels [12]. Therefore, drawing these connections is not merely academic but central to enhancing patient-centered care in modern clinical practice.

By synthesizing current evidence and integrating both basic science and clinical viewpoints, this review contributes to the growing body of work emphasizing the interdependence of organ systems and the systemic nature of vascular disease. Ultimately, the goal is to support a more proactive, unified, and preventative approach to care—one that anticipates and addresses the interconnected threats posed by ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction.

Epidemiology of ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction

Global and regional burden of each condition

Ischemic stroke and MI rank among the most prevalent and deadly diseases worldwide, placing a substantial burden on individuals, families, and healthcare systems. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2019, ischemic heart disease remains the leading cause of death globally, accounting for approximately 120 deaths per 100,000 person-years, while stroke follows closely, responsible for over 85 deaths per 100,000 person-years, with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) comprising nearly 85% of all stroke types[13]. These figures not only reflect a major health crisis, but also highlight the urgent need for coordinated prevention strategies.

The burden is not evenly distributed. High-income countries have witnessed a gradual decline in mortality rates due to advances in healthcare, primary prevention, and acute management [14]. In contrast, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are experiencing a sharp rise in cases, driven by urbanization, sedentary lifestyles, poor dietary habits, and limited access to medical care [15]. For instance, South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa are seeing a growing prevalence of non-communicable diseases, including stroke and MI, as infectious diseases decline [16].

In Nepal, for example, recent studies report an incidence of approximately 149 stroke cases per 100,000 person-years, with acute ischemic strokes (AIS) accounting for over 70% of total stroke admissions[17]. Similarly, MI admissions are increasing in younger populations, often linked to undiagnosed hypertension and diabetes. These regional disparities highlight not only socioeconomic inequalities, but also variations in healthcare infrastructure and public awareness.

Co-occurrence and temporal relationship

The relationship between MI and ischemic stroke is both epidemiologically and clinically significant. It is now well-recognized that these two conditions can co-occur or follow each other in a bidirectional manner. Epidemiological studies have shown that stroke frequently follows acute MI, particularly during the first few weeks, when the risk is significantly elevated due to left ventricular thrombus formation, atrial fibrillation, and systemic inflammation [18]. One large-scale population study demonstrated that the incidence of ischemic stroke was highest within 30 days following an MI, peaking at about 1.5% during this period, which is nearly 44 times higher than the baseline risk [6].

Conversely, acute ischemic stroke can also precipitate cardiac complications, including myocardial injury or even infarction, through mechanisms such as sympathetic overactivity, autonomic dysregulation, and neurogenic stunned myocardium [19]. The phenomenon is often described under the umbrella term “stroke–heart syndrome”, where stroke-induced changes in brain regions such as the insular cortex impact cardiac rhythm and function [20].

Additionally, the presence of atrial fibrillation, which is often either chronic or newly diagnosed after stroke, serves as a crucial link between the two conditions, increasing the likelihood of embolic events in both the heart and brain [21]. Common risk factors for both conditions

The shared risk factors between ischemic stroke and MI reflect a common pathophysiological thread—primarily atherosclerosis and thrombosis affecting vascular territories throughout the body. Hypertension is one of the most significant risk factors for both conditions. It damages the endothelial lining of blood vessels, accelerates atherosclerotic plaque formation, and increases the risk of thromboembolic events [22]. The INTERSTROKE and INTERHEART studies both confirmed that uncontrolled high blood pressure is among the strongest modifiable risk factors for stroke and MI worldwide [9, 23].

Diabetes mellitus is another powerful contributor to both cerebral and coronary artery disease. It promotes chronic inflammation, dyslipidemia, and hypercoagulability, all of which compromise vascular health. Patients with diabetes are two to four times more likely to experience both MI and ischemic stroke compared to non-diabetics [24]. Notably, the presence of diabetes often masks early symptoms, leading to delayed diagnoses and poorer outcomes Fig. 2.

Smoking, both active and passive, is well-established as a potent vascular toxin. It accelerates the oxidation of low-density lipoprotein (LDL), promotes platelet aggregation, and reduces nitric oxide availability, contributing to endothelial dysfunction [25]. Smokers are at nearly twice the risk of ischemic stroke and three times the risk of MI compared to non-smokers [26]. Importantly, the benefits of smoking cessation are substantial; risk levels approach those of non-smokers within five years of quitting [27].

Dyslipidemia, particularly elevated LDL cholesterol and reduced high-density lipoprotein (HDL), plays a central role in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis. Elevated LDL promotes atherogenesis by infiltrating the endothelium, becoming oxidized, and triggering an inflammatory response that leads to foam cell formation and plaque buildup. Conversely, HDL facilitates reverse cholesterol transport and has anti-inflammatory properties, thus reducing atherosclerosis risk. Trials using statin medications have consistently demonstrated reduced incidence of both stroke and MI, indicating that lipid-lowering strategies benefit both vascular territories [28]. Obesity and physical inactivity are key contributors to metabolic syndrome, characterized by insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and hypertension, all of which promote atherosclerosis. Visceral fat secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines, exacerbating endothelial dysfunction, while a sedentary lifestyle reduces vascular nitric oxide production, enhancing thrombogenic potential. Both factors significantly increase the risk of ischemic stroke and MI [29].

Atrial fibrillation is a significant cardioembolic source of ischemic stroke and also increases risk of thrombotic complications post-MI [30]. Chronic systemic inflammation, marked by elevated CRP and cytokines, promotes plaque instability, making it a crucial shared risk factor.

Age and sex also influence disease susceptibility. Older adults are at higher risk for both ischemic stroke and MI due to cumulative vascular damage. Interestingly, while men are more prone to MI in earlier decades, women tend to have worse outcomes when they do experience either event, often due to delayed presentation and atypical symptoms [31]. Men have higher early MI risk; women have higher stroke mortality and atypical presentations.

Socioeconomic factors such as poverty, limited health literacy, and lack of access to preventive care contribute significantly, particularly in LMICs. Addressing these social determinants is as critical as managing clinical risk factors in reducing the global burden of both diseases [32].

Pathophysiological links between ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction

Understanding the intricate biological pathways that link ischemic stroke and MI is crucial for unraveling their shared origin and developing comprehensive treatment and prevention strategies. Although these two entities affect different organs, like the brain and the heart, they are often connected by a common vascular pathology, systemic inflammation, and neural–cardiac interplay.

Atherosclerosis and thromboembolism

At the heart of both ischemic stroke and MI lies atherosclerosis, a progressive inflammatory process that leads to the formation of plaques within the arterial walls. In coronary arteries, the rupture of these plaques can abruptly obstruct blood flow, leading to myocardial infarction. Similarly, when atherosclerotic plaques rupture in the carotid or vertebral arteries, they can generate emboli that travel to the cerebral circulation, causing ischemic stroke [4].

Plaque vulnerability plays a significant role in both conditions. Vulnerable plaques characterized by a large lipid core, thin fibrous cap, and high inflammatory cell content are more prone to rupture and subsequent thrombus formation [33]. Studies have demonstrated that patients with acute coronary syndrome often have evidence of concurrent asymptomatic cerebral atherosclerosis and vice versa, suggesting a systemic vascular vulnerability [34]. The process of thrombosis following plaque rupture mediated by platelet activation, fibrin deposition, and coagulation cascade activation is remarkably similar in both the heart and brain [35].

Moreover, advanced imaging techniques such as intravascular ultrasound and high-resolution MRI have shown overlapping patterns of atherosclerotic burden in coronary and cerebral arteries, further underscoring their shared pathogenesis [36].

Inflammation and endothelial dysfunction

Systemic inflammation is increasingly recognized as a pivotal player in both ischemic stroke and MI. Inflammatory mediators not only contribute to the formation and destabilization of atherosclerotic plaques, but also influence endothelial dysfunction, thrombogenicity, and vascular reactivity [37].

C-reactive protein (CRP), an acute-phase reactant produced by the liver in response to interleukin-6 (IL-6), has been found to be elevated in both acute ischemic stroke and MI patients. Elevated CRP levels are associated with worse outcomes in both conditions and may predict future vascular events [38]. Similarly, cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) play a crucial role in propagating vascular inflammation and increasing plaque vulnerability [39].

Endothelial dysfunction, marked by reduced nitric oxide bioavailability and increased oxidative stress, impairs the protective barrier function of the endothelium. This dysfunction sets the stage for leukocyte adhesion, vascular smooth muscle proliferation, and eventual thrombosis [40]. Given that both stroke and MI often arise from vascular events triggered by systemic endothelial impairment, therapeutic interventions targeting inflammation and endothelial repair are promising areas of research [41].

The cardio-cerebral syndrome

The concept of cardio-cerebral syndrome (CCS) attempts to explain the dynamic interaction between the brain and the heart during acute events. The CCS is classified into two types. Type 1 refers to cerebral complications following myocardial infarction typically due to embolism or hypoperfusion. Type 2 involves cardiac dysfunction following acute brain injury, such as ischemic stroke [42].

In type 1 CCS, the ischemic injury to the myocardium creates a pro-thrombotic environment that can lead to left ventricular dysfunction or mural thrombus formation, which may embolize to the brain. Atrial fibrillation leads to ineffective atrial contractions, resulting in blood stasis within the left atrial appendage, fostering thrombus formation. These thrombi can embolize to cerebral vessels, causing ischemic stroke [43]. On the other hand, type 2 CCS arises when acute brain injury, particularly in the insular cortex, leads to heightened sympathetic output, causing myocardial injury, arrhythmias, and even heart failure [44].

The pathophysiological mechanisms of CCS include autonomic imbalance, systemic inflammation, neurohormonal activation, and endothelial injury. The recognition of this bidirectional communication has practical implications for monitoring and treating patients after either type of vascular event [7].

In clinical practice, determining the primary event involves evaluating symptom chronology, cardiac biomarkers (elevated troponin suggests primary MI), neuroimaging for acute ischemic changes, and echocardiography for cardiac thrombus. Treatment prioritization should address life-threatening instability first if large vessel stroke dominates; early reperfusion takes priority; if cardiogenic shock ensues, cardiac intervention is prioritized while preventing secondary embolic events.

Autonomic dysfunction and the neuro-cardiac axis

The autonomic nervous system, especially its sympathetic branch, plays a central role in the development of cardiac complications following stroke. When the brain, particularly regions like the hypothalamus, medulla oblongata, and insular cortex is damaged, it disrupts the balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic output [45]. This imbalance can result in increased catecholamine release, vasoconstriction, arrhythmias, and even myocardial injury.

The term “neurogenic stunned myocardium” has been used to describe transient myocardial dysfunction in the absence of coronary artery occlusion, often observed after subarachnoid hemorrhage or large ischemic strokes [46]. This condition is attributed to excessive catecholamine surge and direct myocardial toxicity. Studies using ECG and echocardiography have revealed wall motion abnormalities and repolarization instability in stroke patients, further highlighting the link between brain injury and heart dysfunction [47].

The neuro-cardiac axis is also influenced by chronic stress, depression, and sleep disturbances, all common in patients with cerebrovascular or cardiovascular disease. Therapeutic strategies targeting autonomic dysregulation through beta-blockers, anti-inflammatory strategies, or even neuromodulation techniques could offer potential benefits in reducing cardiac complications after stroke [48].

Embolic sources

Thromboembolism is a key mechanism that bridges ischemic stroke and MI, especially when embolic material originates from the heart itself. Atrial fibrillation (AF), whether chronic or newly diagnosed, is one of the most common sources of cardioembolic stroke and is also linked to increased risk of MI due to impaired myocardial perfusion [49].

Left ventricular thrombus, especially after large anterior MI, poses a significant embolic risk. These thrombi can dislodge and travel to the cerebral arteries, causing stroke. The use of anticoagulation in post-MI patients with reduced ejection fraction or apical akinesia is often warranted to prevent this complication [50].

In some cases, paradoxical embolism may occur via a patent foramen ovale (PFO), where emboli from the venous system bypass the pulmonary filter and directly enter the systemic circulation. This mechanism, although less common, can simultaneously threaten both cerebral and coronary circulation, particularly in the presence of coagulopathies [51].

Therefore, cardiac evaluation using echocardiography and continuous rhythm monitoring is essential in stroke patients, particularly those without an obvious vascular cause. Similarly, stroke risk should be carefully assessed in MI patients, especially those with AF, left ventricular dysfunction, or mural thrombi.

Clinical presentations and overlap

The intersection of ischemic stroke and MI is not just pathophysiological but also clinical. The simultaneous or sequential occurrence of these two events can present a formidable diagnostic and management challenge. Their overlapping symptoms, entangled risk factors, and tendency for atypical or silent presentations often blur the clinical lines, making prompt recognition and appropriate treatment critical. A comprehensive understanding of the shared and distinct clinical manifestations of stroke and MI is essential to avoid missed diagnoses and to provide timely interventions.

Symptom overlap and diagnostic challenges

While they affect different organ systems, the initial presenting symptoms can sometimes be misleading, especially in elderly or patients with multiple comorbidities. For instance, chest pain, a hallmark of MI, may be absent in nearly one-third of patients, particularly women, older adults, and those with diabetes. Instead, these patients may present with fatigue, dizziness, or confusion symptoms that can easily overlap with stroke presentations [52].

Conversely, stroke patients may exhibit subtle or non-focal neurological signs, particularly in posterior circulation strokes, which might mimic or co-occur with cardiovascular symptoms like hypotension, syncope, or arrhythmias. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion in patients presenting with non-specific symptoms, especially when traditional cardiovascular or neurological signs are absent [53].

Diagnostic delays often occur when one event overshadows the other. For example, in a patient presenting with hemiparesis and aphasia, signs of cardiac ischemia such as chest discomfort or ECG changes might be overlooked, potentially missing an underlying MI. Similarly, neurological evaluation might be under-prioritized in a patient brought in for acute coronary syndrome, despite the presence of subtle focal deficits or altered consciousness [54].

Cardiac complications in acute ischemic stroke

Acute ischemic stroke can trigger a cascade of systemic responses that extend beyond the brain, notably affecting the heart. One of the most well-documented phenomena is neurogenic myocardial injury, often mediated by excessive catecholamine release following stroke-induced autonomic dysfunction. This can result in transient left ventricular dysfunction, arrhythmias, or even Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, commonly referred to as “stress cardiomyopathy” [55].

Stroke-induced cardiac injury is most often associated with large hemispheric infarcts and insular cortex involvement. These patients may develop ECG abnormalities such as ST-T changes, QT prolongation, and elevated cardiac biomarkers, which can mimic or coexist with true myocardial infarction. Studies have found that nearly 20–30% of acute ischemic stroke patients exhibit elevated troponin levels without obstructive coronary disease, a condition referred to as “stroke–heart syndrome” [19, 56].

Furthermore, atrial fibrillation, either pre-existing or newly diagnosed after a stroke, contributes significantly to secondary cardiovascular events. Continuous cardiac monitoring post-stroke is essential to identify such arrhythmias and to prevent potential recurrent strokes or cardiac complications [57].

Stroke presentations in patients with acute MI

On the flip side, patients presenting with acute MI are also at risk of developing acute ischemic stroke, particularly within the first 30 days following infarction. The underlying mechanisms include left ventricular thrombus formation, atrial fibrillation triggered by myocardial injury, and systemic embolization in cases of mural thrombi or prosthetic valves [44].

In clinical settings, stroke in the context of MI may present acutely during hospitalization or shortly after discharge. These strokes can often be missed or underappreciated, especially when masked by sedation, delirium, or coexisting cardiogenic shock. Some patients may show non-specific signs like decreased consciousness or confusion, which are easily attributed to low cardiac output or medication effects [34].

Stroke following MI is associated with a higher risk of in-hospital mortality and prolonged disability. Therefore, early neuroimaging and clinical vigilance are imperative when patients with MI develop neurological changes, however subtle they may appear [58].

Silent infarctions and subclinical presentations

An especially challenging aspect of the stroke–MI spectrum is the prevalence of silent or subclinical events. Silent cerebral infarctions, detected incidentally on MRI, are significantly more common in patients with a history of cardiovascular disease, particularly those with atrial fibrillation, hypertension, and diabetes [59]. These infarcts, while asymptomatic at onset, are associated with cognitive decline, recurrent strokes, and increased mortality over time.

Similarly, silent myocardial infarctions detected through ECG or cardiac imaging are prevalent in patients with cerebrovascular disease. A study revealed that up to 45% of MIs are unrecognized at the time they occur, especially in stroke patients who may be unable to communicate symptoms due to dysarthria, aphasia, or altered mental status [60].

The clinical implications of these silent infarctions are profound. They not only complicate the diagnostic picture, but also signal a need for aggressive secondary prevention strategies. Screening with brain MRI or echocardiography, especially in high-risk individuals, may help uncover these occult events and inform targeted management [61].

Moreover, stroke and MI frequently occur in a milieu of chronic conditions such as chronic kidney disease, peripheral artery disease, and cognitive impairment, which may further mask classic symptoms and necessitate a broader diagnostic approach. Incorporating biomarkers, continuous ECG monitoring, and neuroimaging into routine assessments for high-risk patients could improve detection rates of subclinical disease [62].

Diagnostic approaches

Diagnosing and evaluating the coexistence of ischemic stroke and MI demands a thorough and multidimensional strategy. Advances in neuroimaging, cardiac diagnostics, and the use of predictive tools have significantly enhanced clinicians’ ability to identify and manage both conditions effectively. A coordinated approach using imaging modalities, biomarkers, and validated scoring systems ensures comprehensive care for patients presenting with either or both of these vascular events.

Neuroimaging and cardiac imaging

Neuroimaging stands as the cornerstone for the evaluation of suspected stroke. A non-contrast computed tomography (CT) scan of the brain remains the initial and most accessible diagnostic modality, especially in emergency settings, due to its ability to rapidly rule out hemorrhage and identify large infarcts. However, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain, particularly diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), offers superior sensitivity in detecting acute ischemic changes, especially during the hyperacute phase or infarcts located in the posterior fossa and cortical regions [63]. MRI can also reveal silent infarctions, microbleeds, and white matter changes, all of which have prognostic implications. MRI (DWI) demonstrates a sensitivity of approximately 92–100% for acute AIS, compared to 64–80% sensitivity of CT within the first 6 h [64].

On the cardiac side, a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) is indispensable for patients with suspected or known stroke, as it can reveal ischemic changes, arrhythmias, or conduction abnormalities that may suggest a concurrent MI or a cardioembolic source [65]. Since atrial fibrillation is a major risk factor for embolic stroke and can be paroxysmal, prolonged ECG monitoring using Holter or implantable loop recorders is often recommended to detect occult arrhythmias [57].

Echocardiography, both transthoracic (TTE) and transesophageal (TEE), plays a vital role in identifying cardiac sources of emboli. TTE is often the first step and can evaluate cardiac function, ventricular thrombi, or wall motion abnormalities suggestive of MI. TEE, with its higher sensitivity for detecting left atrial appendage thrombi, valvular vegetations, and aortic plaques, is preferred in cases where embolic stroke is suspected without a clear source [66].

Cardiac imaging may also include coronary angiography in patients with suspected MI, especially if they have ongoing chest pain, elevated troponins, or ECG changes. In stroke patients with suspected acute coronary syndrome, timely coronary imaging may be life-saving. Cardiac MRI, due to time and resource constraints, is typically reserved for subacute or chronic evaluations of myocardial viability. In the acute setting, cardiac CT, with its faster acquisition time, serves as an effective modality to assess coronary arteries, particularly in patients with inconclusive ECG findings or contraindications to MRI [67].

Biomarkers and laboratory investigations

Biochemical markers have revolutionized the diagnosis and risk stratification of both stroke and MI. Among the most crucial biomarkers are cardiac troponins (I and T), which are highly specific for myocardial injury. Elevated troponin levels are frequently observed in acute stroke patients, even in the absence of MI, which may reflect neurogenic myocardial injury or concurrent coronary disease [68]. Thus, the interpretation of troponin levels in stroke patients must be contextualized with clinical, ECG, and imaging findings.

Brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) and its precursor NT-proBNP have emerged as useful tools for identifying cardioembolic stroke sources, particularly in patients with suspected heart failure or atrial fibrillation. Elevated BNP levels correlate with left atrial enlargement, ventricular dysfunction, and may also help to predict stroke outcomes [69]. D-dimer, a marker of fibrin degradation, is commonly elevated in thrombotic conditions and may support the diagnosis of stroke, particularly in cryptogenic or embolic cases. High levels of D-dimer have also been associated with increased mortality and poor outcomes in both stroke and MI [70].

Routine laboratory workup, including lipid profile, HbA1c, renal function, and inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein (CRP), helps evaluate the underlying vascular risk factors and systemic inflammation contributing to both pathologies. These tests not only support diagnosis, but also assist in guiding long-term management strategies [71].

Emerging biomarkers such as copeptin, growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15), and soluble suppression of tumorigenicity-2 (sST2) have demonstrated significant prognostic value in acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and myocardial infarction (MI) [72]. Copeptin is associated with infarct size, severity, and 1-year mortality in both AIS and STEMI patients. GDF-15 independently predicts cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, including stroke incidence, in acute coronary syndromes. sST2 has emerged as a powerful predictor of adverse outcomes across ACS, MI, and heart failure populations, with prognostic value additive to troponin and BNP [73]. Inflammatory markers, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, have emerged as potential predictors of adverse in-hospital outcomes among patients admitted with acute decompensation of atrial fibrillation [74].

Risk scores and prediction tools

In addition to imaging and laboratory markers, clinical risk scores are vital in estimating a patient’s likelihood of having recurrent events, predicting complications, and determining the need for intervention.

The CHA₂DS₂-VASc score is widely used in patients with atrial fibrillation to assess stroke risk and guide anticoagulation decisions. It incorporates factors such as congestive heart failure, hypertension, age, diabetes, stroke history, vascular disease, and sex [75]. A score of 2 or higher generally warrants anticoagulation therapy to reduce stroke risk.

For patients presenting with transient ischemic attacks (TIAs), the ABCD2 score helps estimate the short-term risk of stroke. This scoring system includes parameters such as age, blood pressure, clinical features (like weakness or speech impairment), duration of TIA, and presence of diabetes. A higher ABCD2 score indicates an elevated risk of stroke within days, prompting urgent evaluation and intervention [76].

In the context of myocardial infarction, the GRACE (Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events) score is a validated tool for predicting in-hospital and long-term mortality. It factors in age, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, renal function, cardiac arrest on admission, ST-segment deviation, and troponin elevation. This score is especially useful when evaluating stroke patients who may have experienced silent or coexisting MI, aiding decisions regarding invasive strategies or intensive care monitoring [77].

Additionally, integrated tools such as the ESUS (Embolic Stroke of Undetermined Source) risk stratification model are being explored to better identify hidden cardiac sources and guide tailored therapy in stroke patients with unclear etiology [78].

Therapeutic intersections

The dual burden of ischemic stroke and MI presents not only a diagnostic dilemma, but also a therapeutic challenge. These vascular catastrophes often share overlapping treatment pathways, yet each comes with its unique nuances. Addressing both conditions requires a fine balance—one that considers the benefits of intervention against the risks, especially when managing acute presentations and long-term care. Coordinated interdisciplinary care involving neurologists, cardiologists, and primary care providers is essential to ensure safe and effective management across the care continuum. According to the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) 2023 guidelines, dual antithrombotic therapy is recommended in selected high-risk patients with polyvascular disease to reduce combined cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risks [79].

Acute management

The acute phase of ischemic stroke and MI often demands rapid decisions regarding reperfusion therapy, antiplatelet agents, and anticoagulation. In ischemic stroke, time is brain; prompt administration of intravenous thrombolysis using tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) within the recommended window (generally within 4.5 h from symptom onset) can significantly improve outcomes [80]. However, thrombolysis carries a risk of bleeding, particularly in patients with recent MI, where myocardial rupture or pericardial hemorrhage is a concern [81]. This makes risk–benefit assessment crucial in patients with dual pathology.

Conversely, in MI, reperfusion strategies like percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and thrombolysis are also time-sensitive and life-saving. In patients presenting with MI who develop neurological symptoms suggestive of stroke, clinicians face the dilemma of balancing antithrombotic needs with the risk of hemorrhagic transformation of cerebral infarcts [82].

Antiplatelet therapy forms the cornerstone of acute management in both MI and non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke. Aspirin is universally recommended and is often combined with a second antiplatelet agent like clopidogrel for dual therapy, particularly in acute coronary syndrome or following stenting [83]. For patients with minor ischemic strokes or high-risk transient ischemic attacks (TIAs), short-term dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) has shown benefit in reducing recurrent events [84]. However, prolonged use increases bleeding risks, necessitating individualized treatment duration.

Anticoagulation, particularly in patients with atrial fibrillation or left ventricular thrombus, is essential for preventing embolic strokes. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have largely replaced warfarin in many cases due to their predictable pharmacokinetics and lower bleeding risks [85]. Nevertheless, their use in the acute setting of a recent stroke or MI requires careful timing—initiating too early may increase hemorrhagic risk, while delaying may predispose to recurrent embolic events [86].

Long-term secondary prevention

Beyond acute care, long-term management focuses on reducing the risk of recurrence through aggressive risk factor control. Statins are pivotal in secondary prevention, not just for lipid lowering but also for their pleiotropic effects such as improving endothelial function and reducing vascular inflammation. Studies like the SPARCL trial have demonstrated that high-dose atorvastatin reduces the risk of recurrent stroke in patients with recent ischemic stroke or TIA, even in the absence of known coronary artery disease [87].

Blood pressure control is equally critical. Hypertension is a major modifiable risk factor for both stroke and MI. Evidence suggests that lowering systolic blood pressure below 140 mmHg, and even further in some cases, reduces the incidence of both vascular events [88]. Choice of antihypertensives can be tailored based on comorbid conditions; angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors and angiotensin-receptor blockers (ARBs) are often preferred for their cardiovascular and renal protective benefits.

Glycemic control is another essential aspect, particularly in patients with diabetes. Poorly controlled blood glucose contributes to endothelial dysfunction, atherosclerosis, and prothrombotic states. Long-term trials have shown that tight glucose control, while not drastically reducing stroke rates, does decrease microvascular complications and may benefit cardiovascular health overall [89].

Lifestyle modification cannot be overstated. Smoking cessation, regular physical activity, a heart-healthy diet (such as the Mediterranean diet), weight management, and alcohol moderation are all proven strategies to reduce vascular risk [90]. Structured rehabilitation and counseling programs post-MI and stroke can play a significant role in reinforcing these behaviors and improving adherence to medication regimens.

Interdisciplinary care strategies

Given the overlapping pathologies and complex management, an interdisciplinary approach becomes essential. Neurology–cardiology collaboration enhances decision-making, especially in patients presenting with overlapping or sequential vascular events. For example, cardiologists can provide insight into the cardiac risk of anticoagulation, while neurologists guide the timing of therapy post-stroke to minimize the risk of hemorrhagic complications.

Stroke units with integrated cardiac monitoring or cardio-neuro units have been proposed as models to optimize care. These units allow simultaneous evaluation and treatment of cerebrovascular and cardiovascular conditions, ensuring streamlined diagnostics and timely interventions [91]. Furthermore, incorporating pharmacists, physiotherapists, and dietitians into the care team ensures a holistic approach that addresses medication adherence, physical rehabilitation, and dietary needs.

Telemedicine has also expanded the scope of interdisciplinary collaboration, especially in underserved or rural areas. Remote consultations allow real-time exchange between specialists, enabling quick decisions regarding thrombolysis, anticoagulation, or transfer for PCI/stroke intervention [92].

In the post-discharge phase, continuity of care is vital. Primary care physicians play a central role in coordinating follow-up, reinforcing risk factor control, and ensuring compliance with therapy. Electronic health records and shared care models improve communication between providers and reduce duplication or oversight in management plans.

Managing patients with ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction is an intricate dance that requires precision, timing, and teamwork. Acute therapies ranging from thrombolysis to dual antiplatelet use must be weighed carefully against bleeding risks. Long-term prevention demands a disciplined approach to medication, lifestyle, and chronic disease control. Above all, successful outcomes depend on interdisciplinary collaboration that transcends traditional boundaries. As medicine continues to evolve toward integrative care, the treatment of these vascular emergencies will increasingly rely on partnerships that put the patient at the center of every decision.

Special populations and considerations

When ischemic stroke and MI intersect, the impact varies significantly across different population groups. Age, sex, and underlying health status shape the clinical presentation, diagnostic challenges, and outcomes in profound ways. Special populations such as the elderly, young adults, and individuals of different sexes require nuanced consideration, as the pathophysiological underpinnings and treatment responses can diverge from general population trends. Understanding these distinctions not only enhances personalized care, but also contributes to more equitable health outcomes.

Elderly patients and polyvascular disease

Elderly individuals represent a growing segment of the global population, and they are disproportionately affected by both ischemic stroke and MI. Advancing age is a non-modifiable risk factor for both conditions, with age-related changes in vascular structure, increased atherosclerotic burden, and a higher prevalence of comorbidities such as hypertension, atrial fibrillation, and diabetes mellitus [93].

Polyvascular disease, defined as the presence of atherosclerosis in more than one vascular bed (e.g., coronary, cerebral, peripheral arteries), is particularly common in older adults. These patients face a significantly increased risk of recurrent ischemic events and mortality. In the REACH Registry, polyvascular disease was associated with a 2.5-fold increase in cardiovascular death and major adverse cardiac events compared to single-territory atherosclerosis [94]. Managing these patients is especially challenging because they often have complex medication regimens, frailty, and impaired physiological reserves.

Antithrombotic therapy, while central to secondary prevention, carries higher bleeding risks in the elderly. Balancing efficacy and safety becomes a fine art. Likewise, aggressive blood pressure or glycemic control may need adjustment to avoid iatrogenic complications in this population, such as falls or hypoglycemia, respectively [95]. Comprehensive geriatric assessment and individualized risk stratification are crucial in guiding therapeutic decisions.

Stroke and MI in young adults: uncommon etiologies

While less common, stroke and MI in young adults (typically defined as under 45 years) are clinically significant due to their long-term socioeconomic impact and emotional toll. These events in the young are often associated with non-traditional or rare causes compared to older adults. For instance, in young stroke patients, causes such as arterial dissection, patent foramen ovale (PFO), prothrombotic states (e.g., antiphospholipid syndrome), vasculitis, and drug abuse (e.g., cocaine, amphetamines) are frequently implicated [96]. Similarly, young adults with MI may present with spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD), congenital anomalies of coronary arteries, or hypercoagulable states [97].

Importantly, the role of genetics is more prominent in young-onset vascular disease. Familial hypercholesterolemia, for example, significantly raises the risk of premature coronary artery disease and, by extension, ischemic stroke [98]. Lifestyle factors, such as smoking, sedentary behavior, and poor dietary habits, also contribute significantly to early vascular pathology.

Diagnosing stroke or MI in this population can be delayed due to lower clinical suspicion, especially when symptoms are atypical or mild. Moreover, psychological implications such as anxiety, depression, and concerns about life expectancy are more pronounced in this group and require integrated psychosocial support as part of long-term care [99].

From a treatment perspective, young patients often tolerate interventions better and have a more robust recovery potential. However, they require tailored rehabilitation and secondary prevention strategies that address their long-term risks, reproductive health (in women), and occupational goals.

Sex-based differences in pathophysiology and outcomes

Sex differences in the pathophysiology, clinical presentation, and outcomes of stroke and MI are well documented, yet often underappreciated in practice. Women generally develop these conditions at an older age compared to men, but when they do, the outcomes tend to be poorer [100]. Part of this discrepancy stems from biological differences such as hormonal influences, smaller vessel caliber, and greater platelet reactivity as well as societal factors like disparities in healthcare access and symptom recognition.

For instance, women are more likely to present with non-traditional symptoms during an acute MI such as fatigue, nausea, or shortness of breath rather than the classic oppressive chest pain. This often leads to misdiagnosis or delayed treatment, which adversely impacts prognosis [101]. Similarly, stroke symptoms in women may include generalized weakness, mental status changes, or confusion, which can be mistaken for psychiatric or metabolic issues, particularly in the elderly [102].

Pathophysiologically, estrogen has been shown to exert a protective effect on the endothelium and lipid profile, potentially delaying the onset of atherosclerosis in premenopausal women. However, postmenopausal women lose this benefit, and the incidence of stroke and MI sharply rises. Pregnancy, oral contraceptives, and hormone replacement therapy also alter thrombotic risk, particularly in women with underlying prothrombotic tendencies [103].

Treatment disparities further widen the outcome gap. Women are less likely to receive reperfusion therapy, undergo coronary interventions, or be enrolled in clinical trials [104]. These gaps highlight the urgent need for sex-specific guidelines and increased awareness among healthcare providers.

On the positive side, recent awareness campaigns and sex-stratified research have begun to address these disparities. Including women in cardiovascular and neurological research, educating clinicians on sex-specific presentations, and promoting equity in care delivery are crucial steps forward.

Understanding the nuances of ischemic stroke and MI in special populations (elderly, young adults, or individuals across the sex spectrum) enhances the precision and efficacy of medical care. These groups present unique challenges that require tailored diagnostic strategies, therapeutic approaches, and follow-up plans. By acknowledging these differences and integrating them into practice, clinicians can provide more equitable, compassionate, and effective care, ultimately improving outcomes and quality of life for all affected individuals.

Recent advances and future directions

In the evolving landscape of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular medicine, scientific and technological advances are reshaping how we understand, diagnose, and manage ischemic stroke and MI. Precision medicine approaches, including polygenic risk scores and multi-omics integration, hold potential in predicting individual AIS-MI risks. Meanwhile, significant disparities persist globally, especially in LMICs, where access to integrated stroke-cardiac care is limited. Targeted public health interventions are needed to bridge this gap.

Molecular and genetic research

Molecular insights have illuminated the common threads between stroke and MI, particularly in the domains of atherosclerosis, inflammation, and thrombosis. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified several loci that contribute to the susceptibility of both ischemic stroke and coronary artery disease. One such locus is in chromosome 9p21, which has consistently been associated with increased risk of atherothrombotic events across diverse populations [105].

Inflammatory mediators like C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) are now recognized not merely as markers, but as active participants in the pathogenesis of both stroke and MI [106]. These discoveries have paved the way for trials exploring the therapeutic modulation of inflammation. For instance, the CANTOS trial demonstrated that targeting IL-1β with canakinumab significantly reduced the risk of recurrent cardiovascular events in patients with prior MI, independent of lipid levels [11]. Although primarily cardiac-focused, such findings are generating interest regarding their potential for stroke prevention.

Moreover, advances in epigenetics and transcriptomics are uncovering gene–environment interactions that predispose individuals to early vascular events. The identification of non-coding RNAs (e.g., microRNAs) that regulate endothelial function, lipid metabolism, and thrombosis is opening new therapeutic targets [107]. For example, miR-21 and miR-126 have been implicated in endothelial injury repair and angiogenesis in both cerebrovascular and cardiovascular settings [108].

Precision medicine, still in its infancy in stroke care, is expected to grow through integration with molecular profiling. In the future, clinicians may stratify patients not just by clinical scores but also by their genetic and epigenetic risk profiles, tailoring antithrombotic therapy, statin use, and even rehabilitation accordingly.

Artificial intelligence and machine learning in risk prediction

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are making significant inroads into risk prediction, diagnostics, and decision-making in vascular medicine. With the capacity to process vast, multidimensional datasets ranging from imaging to genomics, AI models are helping clinicians identify subtle patterns that may elude traditional statistical approaches.

For instance, deep learning algorithms applied to brain imaging can now detect early ischemic changes that are not visible on standard CT scans, thereby improving the speed and accuracy of acute stroke triage [109]. Similarly, AI-enhanced ECG analysis has shown promise in detecting silent or subclinical myocardial ischemia and arrhythmias such as paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, a known cause of cardioembolic stroke [110].

Predictive modeling using AI has also led to the development of dynamic risk assessment tools. Unlike conventional risk scores that rely on static inputs, AI models can integrate real-time data, such as heart rate variability, blood pressure trends, and lab values to forecast stroke or MI risk over short intervals. One study found that machine learning algorithms predicted cardiovascular events more accurately than traditional models like the Framingham Risk Score or CHA₂DS₂-VASc [111].

AI is also transforming rehabilitation by powering smart wearable devices and neurofeedback systems. These tools can tailor recovery protocols based on patient-specific motor or cognitive deficits following stroke or MI, improving outcomes and adherence.

While the promise is immense, challenges remain in integrating AI into clinical practice. Data privacy, model transparency, and the need for validation across diverse populations must be addressed. However, as these hurdles are overcome, AI is poised to be a cornerstone of future vascular care.

Ongoing clinical trials and registries

Clinical research continues to be the engine driving forward our understanding of ischemic stroke and MI. Numerous ongoing trials are evaluating innovative therapies, preventive strategies, and management protocols that bridge the disciplines of cardiology and neurology.

The COMPASS trial, which evaluated low-dose rivaroxaban plus aspirin in patients with stable atherosclerotic vascular disease, found a significant reduction in stroke and MI compared to aspirin alone, underscoring the value of dual pathway inhibition [112]. Similarly, the NAVIGATE ESUS and RE-SPECT ESUS trials explored the use of anticoagulation in patients with embolic stroke of undetermined source, many of whom had coexisting subclinical cardiac pathology [78, 113]. Though results were mixed, they reinforced the importance of refining stroke subtype classification and individualized treatment.

Several registries, such as the American Heart Association’s Get With The Guidelines and the European Society of Cardiology’s EURObservational Research Programme (EORP), are generating invaluable real-world data. These databases track treatment patterns, outcomes, and disparities across populations, enabling continuous quality improvement and identification of care gaps [114].

Additionally, trials are now investigating the timing and sequence of interventions in patients who present with concurrent or sequential stroke and MI. Questions such as when to initiate antiplatelet therapy after thrombolysis or how to manage dual antithrombotic therapy in the setting of atrial fibrillation and recent stroke are being addressed in ongoing studies.

The future also promises greater global inclusion. Historically underrepresented populations, particularly from low- and middle-income countries, are increasingly being enrolled in trials and registries. This will improve the generalizability of findings and support equitable access to evidence-based care worldwide.

The intersection of ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction is a frontier of modern medicine where innovation, biology, and technology converge. Molecular and genetic research is demystifying the shared roots of these conditions, while artificial intelligence is revolutionizing prediction and diagnostics. Meanwhile, clinical trials and registries are translating these advances into patient-centered care. As we look ahead, the goal remains clear: to move from reactive treatment to proactive prevention, from generalized protocols to personalized medicine. The momentum is strong, and the future is undeniably promising.

Prognosis and outcomes

Understanding the prognosis and long-term outcomes in patients affected by ischemic stroke and MI, especially those with overlapping events, is crucial for tailoring management, rehabilitation, and follow-up strategies. Both conditions independently impose a significant burden in terms of mortality, disability, and recurrent events. However, when stroke and MI occur concurrently or sequentially, the impact is often amplified, posing serious challenges to patient recovery and quality of life. Prognostic outcomes in such patients are influenced by numerous factors, including age, sex, comorbidities, initial severity, and access to timely and specialized care.

Mortality and morbidity after stroke and MI

Ischemic stroke and MI are among the leading causes of death worldwide. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, ischemic heart disease remains the top cause of mortality, while stroke ranks second globally in terms of years of life lost and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [13]. Individually, each event carries substantial mortality risk, but when coexisting, the mortality risk increases significantly. Studies have shown that the 30-day mortality in patients suffering both stroke and MI is nearly twice as high as in those with only one of these conditions [54].

The temporal sequence of events also matters. MI occurring shortly after a stroke, especially within the first 30 days, is associated with a higher risk of sudden cardiac death, possibly due to autonomic dysfunction and systemic inflammation post-stroke [115]. Conversely, patients suffering stroke after MI are also at increased risk, especially those with pre-existing arrhythmias or left ventricular dysfunction [116]. The dual burden often leads to prolonged hospital stays, increased rehospitalizations, and a greater need for long-term care.

Functional outcomes and disability

While survival is one aspect of prognosis, the level of post-event disability and quality of life are equally important. Ischemic strokes often result in long-term neurological deficits, affecting motor function, cognition, and speech. On the other hand, MI may leave patients with physical deconditioning, fatigue, and psychological issues such as depression and anxiety. When these conditions overlap, functional recovery can be severely compromised.

For instance, a stroke occurring after MI often leads to poor rehabilitation engagement due to decreased cardiac reserve, while MI after stroke may go unnoticed due to the communication limitations in aphasic or cognitively impaired patients [117]. One multicenter study found that patients with both conditions had a 45% higher chance of institutionalization at discharge compared to those with isolated stroke or MI [118].

Moreover, silent or subclinical infarctions—both cerebral and myocardial can—cumulatively impair physical and cognitive functions. Repeated small-vessel strokes or chronic ischemia can lead to vascular dementia, while recurrent ischemia in the myocardium predisposes one to heart failure with preserved or reduced ejection fraction [119].

Recurrence and rehospitalization

Patients who have experienced either ischemic stroke or MI are at increased risk of recurrent vascular events. This risk is further amplified when both have occurred. The REACH Registry, a large international observational study, revealed that patients with a history of both stroke and MI had a 25% risk of subsequent major cardiovascular events over 4 years, significantly higher than those with a history of just one of these events [120].

This high recurrence rate underscores the need for aggressive secondary prevention. Unfortunately, evidence suggests that adherence to guideline-directed therapies such as antiplatelets, statins, and antihypertensives is often suboptimal in this patient population due to polypharmacy concerns, cognitive limitations, or socioeconomic barriers [121]. Rehospitalizations due to heart failure, recurrent ischemic events, or complications of stroke are also more frequent, adding to the emotional and economic toll on patients and caregivers alike.

Neurocognitive and psychological outcomes

Neurocognitive decline is a well-documented long-term consequence of both ischemic stroke and MI. Vascular cognitive impairment can develop gradually due to cumulative ischemic burden or suddenly after a large cortical stroke. Additionally, shared risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes mellitus contribute to microvascular damage in both the brain and heart [122].

Psychological disorders are also prevalent. Depression and anxiety occur in up to 50% of stroke survivors and are similarly common after MI. When both conditions are present, the psychological impact is often more profound, leading to reduced treatment adherence, impaired social functioning, and worsened outcomes [123].

Furthermore, post-stroke fatigue, often underestimated, can be exacerbated by coexisting heart disease, further reducing quality of life. These neuropsychological outcomes necessitate a holistic approach to care that includes mental health support alongside physical rehabilitation.

Predictors of poor outcomes

Several clinical and demographic variables have been consistently associated with worse outcomes in patients with both ischemic stroke and MI. These include older age, female sex, history of atrial fibrillation, reduced left ventricular ejection fraction, renal impairment, and elevated inflammatory markers such as CRP and NT-proBNP at presentation [124].

Frailty, a syndrome characterized by reduced physiological reserve, is emerging as a particularly strong predictor of poor prognosis in elderly patients with both conditions. Frail patients are less likely to tolerate aggressive therapies and more prone to complications, hospital readmissions, and death [125]. Therefore, early identification of frailty and comorbidity burden is essential in guiding care planning.

Impact of timely and integrated care

Outcomes are not solely determined by the biological severity of disease but also by the healthcare system’s response. Access to early reperfusion therapies, whether thrombolysis for stroke or percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for MI, significantly improves outcomes. Studies show that integrated stroke–cardiac care units, where neurologists and cardiologists co-manage patients, lead to better outcomes, including lower mortality, reduced readmissions, and improved functional recovery [126].

The stroke–heart syndrome, a recognized clinical phenomenon, exemplifies the need for such integrated care. It refers to cardiac complications occurring in the aftermath of a stroke, such as arrhythmias or myocardial injury, which if not promptly recognized and managed, can worsen prognosis [19].

The prognosis and outcomes in patients with ischemic stroke and MI, especially when co-occurring, are shaped by a complex interplay of biological, clinical, and systemic factors. These include not only mortality and recurrence but also long-term functional, cognitive, and emotional consequences. Understanding these dimensions is vital for optimizing patient outcomes. Timely diagnosis, evidence-based treatment, and integrated care strategies are critical to mitigate risks and enhance recovery. Future research should focus on personalized medicine approaches, targeting specific risk profiles to improve prognosis in this high-risk population.

Conclusion

The intricate link between ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction (MI) is more than a coincidence of vascular events; it reflects a deep-rooted, shared pathophysiological foundation that involves systemic atherosclerosis, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and embolic phenomena. These two conditions, once viewed as separate specialties under neurology and cardiology, now demand a unified understanding due to their frequent co-occurrence, overlapping risk factors, and mutual influence on prognosis and outcomes.

Ultimately, decoding the connection between ischemic stroke and MI is not merely an academic exercise; it is a pressing clinical imperative. By embracing a multidisciplinary approach, refining diagnostic tools, and expanding our research horizons, we can improve not only survival but also the quality of life for millions of individuals affected by these intertwined vascular pathologies.
