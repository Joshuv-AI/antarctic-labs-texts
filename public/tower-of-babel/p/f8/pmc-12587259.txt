Early insulin degludec with continuous intravenous insulin infusion in the management of diabetic ketoacidosis: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Kitti Thammakosol, Methus Jantarapootirat, Sirinapa Traiwanatham, Chutintorn Sriphrapradang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo determine the effectiveness and safety of early combination therapy with insulin degludec and intravenous insulin infusion (IVII) compared with IVII alone in diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) management.Materials and methodsThis prospective, open‐label, randomised controlled trial included 80 adults (≥18 years) with DKA. Participants were randomised to either the intervention group, which received early subcutaneous (SC) insulin degludec (0.3 units/kg SC within 3 h of diagnosis) plus standard IVII, or the control group, which received standard IVII alone. The primary outcome was time to DKA resolution. Secondary outcomes included rebound hyperglycaemia, rebound DKA, hypoglycaemia, hypokalaemia, length of hospital stay (LOS), and in‐hospital mortality.ResultsEighty patients were enrolled; 67.5% of participants had type 2 diabetes. Baseline characteristics were comparable between groups. DKA resolution was significantly faster in the early degludec group by 3.25 h (7.75 h, IQR 6.00–9.00 h vs. 11.00 h, IQR 6.25–15.00; p = 0.039). At 72 h after transition to SC insulin, mean capillary blood glucose (CBG) was significantly lower with early degludec (213.9 ± 25.8 mg/dL vs. 240.1 ± 42.0 mg/dL; p = 0.012). Rates of rebound hyperglycaemia at 12 h after bridging, mean CBG levels at 12, 24, and 48 h among those with rebound hyperglycaemia, as well as rates of rebound DKA, hypoglycaemia, hypokalaemia, LOS, and in‐hospital mortality, were not significantly different between groups.ConclusionsEarly administration of SC insulin degludec in combination with IVII accelerated DKA resolution and improved blood glucose levels at 72 h in patients with rebound hyperglycaemia after discontinuation of IVII, without increasing the risk of hypoglycaemia or hypokalaemia.Keywords: basal insulin, diabetic ketoacidosis, hypoglycaemia, hypokalaemia, long‐acting insulin, rebound hyperglycaemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is a critical and life‐threatening complication that occurs in people with diabetes, particularly in those with type 1 diabetes (T1D), with an increasing proportion of cases in type 2 diabetes (T2D). 1 , 2 , 3 The incidence of diabetes is rising globally, contributing to a reported 55% increase in DKA admissions over the past decade. 1 , 4 , 5 The key to effective management of this condition is prompt diagnosis and the timely administration of appropriate treatment to correct the metabolic derangements in DKA (hyperglycaemia, ketonemia, and metabolic acidosis), which result from a combination of absolute or relative insulin deficiency and elevated levels of counterregulatory hormones (e.g., glucagon, cortisol, catecholamines, and growth hormone). 1 , 6

Insulin therapy is one of the cornerstones of DKA management. Most patients are treated with a continuous intravenous infusion of regular insulin until DKA resolves, at which point they are transitioned to subcutaneous (SC) insulin. 1 Early use of basal insulin in combination with intravenous insulin infusion (IVII) is increasingly common in clinical practice, as evidenced by several prospective studies. These studies have reported that coadministration of a low dose (0.15–0.3 units/kg) of glargine U‐100 during IVII reduces the time to DKA resolution, the duration of IVII, 7 , 8 and the length of hospital stay (LOS), 7 while also preventing rebound hyperglycaemia without increasing the risk of hypoglycaemia. 8 , 9 In accordance with the UK guideline, long‐acting basal insulin should be continued in patients already receiving it, or initiated at a dose of 0.25 units/kg once daily in newly diagnosed patients. 3 Although insulin degludec has become increasingly utilised as a basal insulin in diabetes management, evidence supporting its effectiveness in the management of DKA remains lacking.

Compared to glargine U‐100, degludec has been shown to result in lower glycaemic variability, less hypoglycaemia (especially nocturnal hypoglycaemia), and greater flexibility, while providing an equivalent potential for glycaemic control. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Furthermore, trials of degludec in hospitalised T2D patients have demonstrated that it is equally effective and safe compared with glargine U‐100. 16 However, the evaluation of the hypothesis that early administration of degludec following a DKA diagnosis may accelerate DKA resolution, prevent rebound hyperglycaemia, and reduce LOS presents significant challenges. This is particularly due to its pharmacokinetic profile, as steady‐state concentrations are typically achieved only after 3–4 days of therapy. Addressing this knowledge gap serves as the primary aim of the present study.

RESEARCH DESIGN AND METHODS

Study design and participants

This prospective, open‐label, randomised controlled trial (RCT) included Thai adults aged 18 years or older who were diagnosed with DKA between March 2023 and September 2024. The study was conducted at two centres of a university‐based hospital in Bangkok and Samut Prakan, Thailand. All patients diagnosed with DKA, regardless of initial location, were subsequently treated in the high‐dependency unit. The study received approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Before participant recruitment, the trial was prospectively registered with the Thailand Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR ID: TCTR20230408002). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment. For patients who were confused or comatose, consent was obtained from a legally authorized representative, or the patient's next of kin. Inclusion criteria included patients aged 18 years or older who were diagnosed with DKA based on the 2009 American Diabetic Association (ADA) consensus statements 6 : plasma glucose of 250 mg/dL or higher, serum beta‐hydroxybutyrate of 3 mmol/L or higher, arterial/venous pH of 7.3 or less, serum bicarbonate of 18 mEq/L or lower, or an anion gap of 12 mEq/L or higher. People with plasma glucose levels lower than 250 mg/dL, history of insulin allergy, haemodynamic instability (requiring vasopressors to maintain haemodynamic status), pregnancy, breastfeeding, and those planning to receive a SC treatment regimen for mild DKA were excluded from participation.

Procedures

After exclusions (Figure 1), 80 patients were randomised in a 1:1 ratio using a computer‐generated sequence with variable block sizes of 2–8. Allocation was concealed using sealed, opaque envelopes, and participants were assigned to either the early degludec or control group. The early degludec group received SC degludec at a dose of 0.3 units/kg within 3 h of DKA diagnosis, with a dose reduction to 0.15 units/kg for patients with creatinine clearance (CrCl) <30 mL/min (calculated using the Cockcroft–Gault equation), in addition to the standard IVII protocol. The selected dose was based on prior prospective studies of glargine U‐100, 7 , 9 , 17 , 18 which reported doses ranging from 0.25 to 0.4 units/kg, as well as the 2022 UK guideline, 3 which recommends a basal insulin dose of approximately 0.25 units/kg. Together, these findings provide both pharmacologic and clinical rationale for selecting 0.3 units/kg of degludec. The control group was administered only the standard IVII for DKA and did not receive placebo injections. Orders for intravenous insulin bolus, fixed‐dose IVII regimen, intravenous fluids, potassium, and bicarbonate administration were consistent in both groups and followed the 2009 ADA recommendations, 6 with the full protocol detailed in Appendix S1. A fixed IVII regimen of 0.1 units/kg/h was used until the capillary blood glucose (CBG) dropped below 200 mg/dL. Once CBG fell below 200 mg/dL, the insulin infusion rate was adjusted to 0.02–0.05 units/kg/h according to the patient's CBG level, aiming to maintain CBG between 150 and 200 mg/dL until DKA resolution. CBG was measured hourly, and serum electrolyte levels were monitored every 4 h in both groups. DKA resolution was defined as CBG of less than 200 mg/dL, in combination with a normalised serum anion gap (≤12 mEq/L), a venous pH above 7.30, or a serum bicarbonate greater than 15.0 mEq/L. 6 After resolution, patients were transitioned to a SC insulin regimen as soon as they could tolerate oral or enteral intake, using a newly calculated weight‐based total daily dose of approximately 0.6 units/kg/day in both groups. SC insulin aspart was injected 5 to 15 min before meals in both groups as prandial insulin. For basal insulin, the control group received insulin glargine U‐100 SC with the first dose of insulin aspart, while the intervention group received a second dose of insulin degludec approximately 24 h after the first dose. IVII was discontinued 1 h after transitioning to full SC insulin. Following DKA resolution, while patients remained hospitalised, the internal medicine and endocrinology teams primarily adjusted multiple daily injections of basal‐bolus SC insulin as part of standard inpatient care, and CBG was routinely monitored four times daily (before meals and at bedtime). In the event of hypoglycaemia, patients received 15 g of oral glucose or 25 g of intravenous glucose, depending on their level of consciousness and ability to tolerate oral intake. CBG was rechecked 15 min later, and treatment was repeated as necessary until levels exceeded 70 mg/dL. A meal or snack was subsequently provided to prevent recurrence. Insulin doses were individually adjusted based on CBG trends and the patient's clinical status. 19 Continuous glucose monitoring was not included in the study protocol.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the time to DKA resolution, defined as the duration between diagnosis and resolution of ketoacidosis. The secondary outcomes included the incidence of rebound hyperglycaemia; average CBG levels in patients with rebound hyperglycaemia at 12, 24, 48, and 72 h; rebound DKA; LOS; in‐hospital mortality; and safety outcomes (e.g., the incidence of hypoglycaemia and hypokalaemia). Rebound hyperglycaemia was defined as a CBG higher than 180 mg/dL within 12 h after discontinuation of IVII, based on the normal inpatient target range of 100–180 mg/dL and consistent with previous studies. 7 , 8 , 9 , 17 Average CBG levels at 12, 24, 48, and 72 h in patients with rebound hyperglycaemia were calculated as the mean of CBG values higher than 180 mg/dL, including only cases with complete data at each time point. In‐hospital mortality was defined as death from any cause occurring during hospitalisation for the current DKA admission. Hypoglycaemia was defined as a CBG or plasma glucose level less than 70 mg/dL that developed between the initiation of IVII and 24 h post‐discontinuation. 7 , 17 , 19 , 20 , 21 Level 2 hypoglycaemia was defined as CBG or plasma glucose level less than 54 mg/dL. 7 , 19 , 20 Hypokalaemia was defined as serum potassium less than 3.5 mEq/L between the initiation of IVII and 24 h post‐discontinuation. 7 , 8 , 21

Statistical analysis

According to the results of a previous study, 7 the difference in the time to DKA resolution between the “early basal” intervention group and the control group was estimated to be 3 h. The average standard deviations (SD) were 3.81 in the intervention group and 5.37 in the control group, respectively. A minimum of 76 patients (38 patients per group) was required to detect the difference in time to DKA resolution with a two‐sided significance level of 5% and a statistical power of 80%.

Analyses of all endpoints were based on the full analysis set (all randomised patients) following the intention‐to‐treat principle, using STATA statistical software version 18 (Stata Corp, LLC, College Station, TX). The data are presented as mean ± SD for normally distributed values; as median (interquartile range [IQR]: 25th–75th percentiles) for non‐normally distributed values; and as counts and percentages for categorical variables. For normally distributed data, group differences were assessed using the independent sample t‐test for continuous outcomes and Pearson's chi‐square test for categorical outcomes. For non‐normally distributed data, a quantile regression model was applied to compare continuous outcomes, while the Fisher's exact test was used for categorical outcomes. Two‐tailed p‐values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The primary outcome was analysed using a quantile regression model due to the non‐normal distribution of the data. This method facilitated comparison of the median time to DKA resolution between groups. Regarding adjustment for potential confounding factors, baseline characteristics—including age and comorbidities—were well balanced between groups (Table 1); therefore, no adjusted analyses were performed.

RESULTS

A total of 111 adult patients were enrolled as eligible for this study (99 patients from a centre in Bangkok and 12 patients from another centre), of whom 31 were excluded, primarily due to euglycemic DKA and haemodynamic instability (Figure 1). After exclusions, 80 patients were randomised equally into two groups (40 per group). In the early degludec group, 37 patients (92.5%) received degludec 0.3 units/kg within 3 h of DKA diagnosis, while three patients (7.5%) required a dose reduction to 0.15 units/kg due to CrCl <30 mL/min. Baseline characteristics of the participants are shown in Table 1 and were similar between the two groups. The average age of participants was approximately 55 years, and the majority of them (67.5%) had T2D, while around 30% had T1D or latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA). Prior to this admission, participants had been using insulin and sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) at rates of 45% and 12.5%, respectively. Among participants with current insulin use, the most common precipitating factor for DKA was insulin discontinuation for more than 24 h, occurring in 12 of 18 participants in the early degludec group and 12 of 17 participants in the control group. In the remaining participants, infection was the primary precipitating factor. All five participants with prior degludec use developed DKA following insulin discontinuation. Variables at diagnosis—including plasma glucose, serum beta‐hydroxybutyrate, serum bicarbonate, anion gap, serum potassium, serum creatinine, and disease severity as defined by the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation IV (APACHE‐IV) score 22 —were similar between the two groups. At the point of DKA resolution, both groups received comparable mean total amounts of intravenous fluids, IVII, and intravenous bicarbonate therapy during DKA treatment (Table 2).

Secondary outcomes

The incidence of rebound hyperglycaemia within 12 h was not significantly different between the groups (early degludec 75.0% vs. control 67.6%; p = 0.483). There were no cases of rebound DKA in the early degludec group, while the control group had four cases of rebound DKA, which was not statistically significant (0% vs. 10%; p = 0.116).

However, the average CBG in hyperglycaemic patients at 72 h after transitioning from IVII to a SC regimen was significantly lower in the early degludec group (213.9 ± 25.8 mg/dL vs. 240.1 ± 42.0 mg/dL; mean difference − 26.2; p = 0.012) (Table 4 and Figure S1).

Three participants in the early degludec group received an initial dose of 0.15 units/kg due to CrCl <30 mL/min. One had end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) and was prescribed a total daily SC insulin dose of 0.3 units/kg/day after DKA resolution, with an average CBG of 182 mg/dL during the rebound hyperglycaemic episode 72 h after transition from IVII. The other two participants had acute kidney injury with CrCl recovering to >30 mL/min the following day after fluid replacement; both were transitioned to SC insulin at 0.6 units/kg/day after DKA resolution, with average CBGs of 264.5 and 205 mg/dL at 72 h post‐IVII. Additional clinical outcomes are presented in Table S1.

Regarding safety outcomes, the incidence of hypoglycaemia (CBG <70 mg/dL) and level 2 hypoglycaemia (CBG <54 mg/dL) was comparable between groups (12.5% vs. 10.0%; p = 1.0 and 2.5% vs. 2.5%; p = 1.0, respectively). One patient with ESRD in the early degludec group who received a reduced dose of insulin degludec developed hypoglycaemia (CBG 58 mg/dL) within 24 h after transition to the full SC insulin regimen (Table S1). The incidence of hypokalaemia (serum potassium <3.5 mEq/L) was common in this study and showed no significant difference between the groups (50.0% vs. 40.0%; p = 0.369). The serum potassium levels during hypokalaemic events were mild and comparable between groups (3.22 ± 0.19 mEq/L vs. 3.24 ± 0.18 mEq/L; p = 0.715) (Table 4). Additional information on hypoglycaemia and hypokalaemia is provided in Table S2. None of the participants developed symptomatic cerebral oedema.

LOS and in‐hospital mortality were also not significantly different between groups (5.23 days [3.65–8.04] vs. 6.63 days [4.04–13.32]; p = 0.218, and 0% vs. 7.5%; p = 0.241, respectively).

DISCUSSION

This RCT evaluated the effectiveness and safety of early SC administration of ultralong‐acting basal insulin, insulin degludec, in addition to standard DKA treatment with IVII. The results demonstrated that early degludec administration in combination therapy with IVII was associated with faster DKA resolution compared with the control group. Exploratory analyses of patients with classic T1DM and severe DKA showed a trend toward shorter time to resolution, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. Additionally, patients in the early degludec group had significantly lower CBG at 72 h after DKA resolution compared to the control group. The early combination of degludec within the first 3 h after DKA diagnosis was safe, as evidenced by similar rates of hypoglycaemia and hypokalaemia compared with the control group. LOS and in‐hospital mortality were not different between the groups.

Insulin degludec, an ultralong‐acting insulin analogue, provides up to 42 h of peakless basal insulin coverage to control plasma glucose and suppress ketogenesis. 23 , 24 Due to these pharmacodynamic properties, the early use of SC degludec alongside IVII for DKA treatment may decrease the requirement for intravenous insulin, facilitate faster DKA resolution, and prevent excessively high glucose levels after transitioning to a SC insulin regimen. Although prior prospective studies of glargine U‐100 have revealed the benefits of early use of SC basal insulin in DKA treatment, 7 , 9 , 17 no studies on ultralong‐acting degludec were conducted before this one.

Compared to the previous RCT of glargine U‐100, which showed that early use of glargine significantly reduced the time to DKA resolution by 2.87 h, 7 our study of degludec demonstrated a comparable benefit, shortening the time to DKA resolution by 3.25 h. Furthermore, early use of glargine U‐100 showed potential benefits in decreasing the incidence of rebound hyperglycaemia at 12 and 24 h, depending on the study. 9 , 17 In our study, the benefit of blood glucose control was observed 72 h after transitioning to the full SC regimen. One possible explanation for this delayed effect, compared to the more rapid response seen in the glargine U‐100 trial, is that insulin degludec reaches steady‐state levels around days 3–4 after initiation. 25 , 26 Assessment using CBG rather than venous plasma glucose, along with non‐strict control of dose and timing of full SC insulin initiation after DKA resolution, may have reduced the precision of this secondary outcome.

In the management of DKA, patients with ESRD are generally treated with the same IVII doses as those without renal disease. 3 Expert recommendations suggest that initiating a lower insulin infusion rate (e.g., 0.05 units/kg/h) may be considered. 27 Subcutaneous insulin doses are also typically reduced in advanced CKD (CrCl <30 mL/min) due to impaired insulin clearance and an increased risk of hypoglycemia. 28 In our study, three patients in the early degludec group with CrCl <30 mL/min received a reduced degludec dose (0.15 units/kg/day) in addition to IVII. One patient with ESRD developed hypoglycaemia despite dose reduction, whereas two patients with acute kidney injury experienced rebound hyperglycaemia, highlighting the challenge of appropriate dosing. Evidence to guide early SC basal insulin use in this population is limited, warranting further research to optimise safety and efficacy.

The total IVII dose from initiation to DKA resolution was slightly lower in the early degludec group, but this difference was not statistically significant. This may be explained by the pharmacokinetics of degludec, which is peakless and requires time to reach steady state, as well as the fact that the study was powered for the primary outcome rather than for comparing total insulin dose.

Our study also found an interesting point: the control group had four cases of rebound DKA after DKA resolution, compared to zero cases in the early degludec group. However, this difference did not reach statistical significance due to the relatively low event rate. A larger study may be needed to establish the correlation between early use of basal insulin and rebound DKA events.

One prior trial of glargine U‐100 demonstrated a reduction in LOS 7 ; however, this benefit was not observed in other prospective studies, 17 , 18 including our own (Table 4). The absence of an effect on LOS may be attributed to hospital discharge decisions being primarily influenced by the resolution of underlying precipitating factors—as such as infection—rather than by DKA resolution alone.

For safety outcomes, the two most common complications during DKA management are hypoglycaemia and hypokalaemia. There was no increase in the incidence of hypoglycaemia and hypokalaemia in prior prospective studies of glargine U‐100. The present study of degludec also did not show an increase in these two common complications, which is consistent with the peakless insulin profile of degludec. 10 , 14 , 29 , 30 The incidence of hypokalaemia was relatively high in both groups: 50% in the early degludec group and 40% in the control group. These rates are consistent with previously reported data on DKA treatment complications, which indicate that hypokalaemia occurs in up to 55% of cases. 7 , 21 However, it was generally mild (>3.0 mEq/L), without significant clinical consequences, and comparable between groups. The likely cause of hypokalaemia was IVII, as potassium levels recovered within the first few days and no cases of persistent hypokalaemia were observed.

Our study is the first known trial to provide data on the early co‐administration of degludec with IVII in the management of DKA. This study is also larger compared to other early basal insulin trials in DKA management, with an adequate sample size that includes participants with both T1D and T2D. The open‐label design is considered a study limitation, as awareness of treatment allocation by both clinicians and patients may have subtly influenced decisions regarding laboratory monitoring, the timing of intravenous insulin discontinuation, and discharge planning. However, all treating clinicians followed the current ADA guidelines recommendations. 1 , 6 , 31 A second limitation is that ketone concentrations were not routinely monitored or used to define DKA resolution, due to cost considerations, longer turnaround times compared with standard laboratory tests, and the study being conducted prior to the ADA 2024 consensus statement. 1 Another limitation is the exclusion of participants with plasma glucose levels below 250 mg/dL at diagnosis, such as patients with euglycemic DKA. In this population, early use of combination basal insulin is particularly challenging due to concerns about hypoglycaemia, as baseline plasma glucose levels are lower than those typically seen in classic DKA cases. However, in our study, which did not include euglycemic DKA, no increase in hypoglycaemia was observed, attributable to frequent CBG monitoring and a careful follow‐up protocol. 21 Additionally, the comparison of early degludec with late conventional glargine U‐100 administration after DKA resolution in this study may not fully demonstrate the benefit of early degludec use, due to the different pharmacokinetic properties of the two insulins. Larger, blinded studies across broader DKA populations, including euglycemic patients, with direct comparison of early versus late degludec administration should be considered to confirm the effectiveness and safety of early degludec use.

CONCLUSIONS

Early administration of insulin degludec, an ultralong‐acting basal insulin analogue, in combination with IVII within 3 h of DKA diagnosis resulted in faster DKA resolution and lower CBG levels at 72 h among patients experiencing rebound hyperglycaemia after transitioning to full SC insulin treatment, without increasing the incidence of hypoglycaemia or hypokalaemia. This approach may reduce the utilisation of personnel, laboratory testing, and intensive care resources in DKA management. It may be considered for all types of diabetes and DKA severity, particularly in patients with prior degludec use and insulin‐naïve individuals experiencing DKA for the first time who are planned to receive degludec for long‐term glycaemic control.
