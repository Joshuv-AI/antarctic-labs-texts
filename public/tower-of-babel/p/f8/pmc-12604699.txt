Optic nerve changes in PTPN11-related Noonan syndrome
Authors: Tarek Saad Shoala, Linda M. Reis, Jenina Capasso, Elena Semina, Alex V. Levin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Characteristic features of Noonan syndrome include dysmorphic facies, short stature, and congenital cardiac defects. Pathogenic variants in PTPN11 are one of the common causes and may result in optic nerve head anomalies. We describe the optic nerve findings in two families with Noonan syndrome due to pathogenic variants in PTPN11. Optic nerve anomalies, including an enlarged cup/disc ratio, are a feature of Noonan syndrome due to pathogenic variants in PTPN11. This optic nerve anomaly could potentially lead to the misdiagnosis of glaucoma. PTPN11 is important for functional fibroblast growth factor signaling, which, if interrupted, may lead to optic nerve dysgenesis and ocular coloboma.

Introduction

Noonan syndrome is an autosomal dominant genetic condition characterized by dysmorphic facies, short stature, and congenital cardiac defects. The syndrome is caused by defects in the rat sarcoma (RAS)/rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma (RAF)/mitogen-activated protein kinase/extracellular signal-regulated kinase signal transduction pathways. These pathways are essential for cell differentiation, proliferation, and survival. Pathogenic variants in multiple genes may cause Noonan Syndrome, including PTPN11, SOS1, SOS2, RIT1, RAF1, KRAS, BRAF, NRAS, RRAS2, MRAS, and LZTR1.[1] Disease-causing variants in PTPN11, encoding the Src-homology 2 domain-containing protein tyrosine phosphatase (PTP), account for approximately 50% of Noonan syndrome.[2] These variants result in gain-of-function changes to the nonreceptor PTP PTPN11 protein.[3] Optic nerve head changes are reported in some individuals with Noonan syndrome.[4] This subtle clinical finding may be a clue to diagnosing the syndrome and warrants ophthalmic examination in patients diagnosed with Noonan syndrome.

Case Reports

Case 1

This 5-month-old girl was the product of a full-term pregnancy, complicated by maternal anemia and factor VII deficiency. At 14 days old, she was found to have a right eye iris and chorioretinal inferior-nasal coloboma involving the optic disc and fovea [Figure 1]. She was able to fix and follow equally with each eye. There was no nystagmus. Intraocular pressures (IOP) and anterior segment examination were otherwise normal. Systemic features include mild left upper pole caliectasis with mild pelviectasis in the kidney, pulmonary artery stenosis with a small secundum atrial septal defect with a fenestrated septum primum, and mild dysmorphic features. Chromosomal microarray (CMA) performed using the Cytocan® HD platform, which uses over 743,000 SNP probes and 1,953,000 NPCN probes, with a median spacing of 0.88kb (Integrated Genetics – Lap Corp, Durham, NC), was normal. Trio exome sequencing performed in the research setting (ES) and analyzed as previously described[5] revealed an NM_002834.3:c.853T>C (p.Phe285Leu) variant in PTPN11, which was confirmed by Sanger sequencing in a clinical laboratory (Precision Medicine Laboratory, Milwaukee, WI). The variant meets ACMG criteria[6] to be considered pathogenic with high CADD (33) and REVEL (0.929) scores. This variant is located in a mutational hotspot, with the same variant reported in over 20 publications, and six other pathogenic variants reported in the same codon in HGMD® professional.[7] The variant is not reported in gnomAD v3.1.2 but is seen in 1/1589234 alleles in gnomAD v4.1.0.[8] The variant was maternally inherited. The mother was found to have a large cup/disc ratio of 0.6 in both eyes and received a clinical diagnosis of glaucoma suspect with an IOP of nine millimeters of mercury (mmHg) and vision of 20/20 in each eye, along with mild factor VII deficiency and short stature. The proband’s younger sister, who has tested negative for this variant, was not examined.

Case 2

A 6-month-old boy who was the product of a full-term pregnancy that was complicated by an absent ductus venosus. Ocular examination showed cup/disc ratios of 0.8 in both eyes, and the optic nerve head appeared anomalous [Figure 2]. Non-invasive prenatal testing and amniocentesis for CMA (same as Case One) and chromosome fluorescence in situ hybridization for chromosomes X, Y, 13, eight and 21 (DNA probe vendor Kreatech, Leica BioSystems, Deer Park, IL and digital image analysis vendor Applied Spectral Imaging systems, Carlsbad, CA) was normal. Postnatal echocardiogram revealed moderate supravalvular pulmonic stenosis, mild right ventricular hypertrophy, and a moderate-to-large atrial septal defect. Additional systemic features included a short stature, unilateral cryptorchidism, and gastroesophageal reflux. A clinical RASopathies and Noonan Spectrum Disorders Panel (A2ML1, A2ML1, ACTB, ACTG1, BRAF, CBL, HRAS, KAT6B, KRAS, LZTR1, MAP2K1, MAP2K2, MRAS, NF1, NRAS, NSUN2, PPP1CB, PTPN11, RAF1, RASA1, RASA2, RIT1, RRAS, RRAS2, SHOC2, SOS1, SOS2, SPRED1, YWHAZ, Invitae Laboratories, San Francisco, CA), showed a heterozygous PTPN11 c.181G>A (p.Asp61Asn) variant. The variant meets ACMG criteria to be considered pathogenic with high CADD (31) and REVEL (0.648) scores. This variant is located in a mutational hotspot, with the same variant reported in over 20 publications, and four other pathogenic variants reported in the same codon in HGMD ® Professional.[7] The variant is not reported in gnomAD v3.1.2 but is seen in 1/1613262 alleles in gnomAD v4.1.0.[8] Testing of the parents was negative for this variant.

Discussion

The first paper describing the association of the PTPN11 gene with Noonan syndrome was by Tartaglia et al. in 2001.[9] In 1987, a single case of optic disc coloboma in the right eye and a chorioretinal coloboma in the left eye inferior to the optic disc was reported as perhaps the first ocular manifestations to be described in Noonan syndrome.[10] In a subsequent report, Lee et al. described ocular findings in 58 patients.[11] Fifty-one patients had fundus examination, and ten patients (approximately 20%) had fundus abnormalities, including optic nerve drusen, disc hypoplasia, coloboma, and myelinated retinal nerve fibers. A paper with genotyping described 23 patients with optic nerve head excavation in five patients and optic nerve hypoplasia in one patient.[12] A retrospective study of 105 patients with Noonan syndrome reported optic nerve head abnormalities (excavation, coloboma, or hypoplasia) in ten patients (about ten percent), most often associated with variants in PTPN11.[4] Other ocular manifestations of Noonan syndrome include refractive errors, secondary strabismus, nystagmus, limited ocular motility, hypertelorism, ptosis, epicanthal folds, and downslanting palpebral fissures. Keratoconus, posterior embryotoxon, prominent corneal nerves, and cataract have also been observed.[4]

The protein encoded by PTPN11 is a member of the PTP family. These proteins have an important role in major cellular processes, including cellular growth and differentiation. It has a downstream action that leads to transduction from the cell surface to the nucleus of different cytoplasmic and receptor protein kinases.[13] PTPN11 is also involved in fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling.[14] FGFs have an important role in the creation of intricate connections within the nervous system. Signaling pathways from the FGF and FGF receptor (FGFR) are pivotal in guiding axons toward the targets needed for synapse.[15] FGF2, FGFR1, and Anosmin-1 are expressed during the development of the optic nerve and have a modulatory effect on the migration of oligodendrocyte precursors during the development of the optic nerve.[16] Defective FGF signaling causes optic nerve dysgenesis and ocular coloboma.[17]

We recommend early ocular examination in patients with Noonan syndrome, in particular when due to pathogenic variants in PTPN11. Recognition of these optic nerve abnormalities as a feature of Noonan syndrome may also prevent confusion with glaucoma or other disorders associated with coloboma.
