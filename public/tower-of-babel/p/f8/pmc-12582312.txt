Nir2 crystal structures reveal a phosphatidic acid–sensing mechanism at ER–PM contact sites
Authors: Dongyoung Kim, Seowhang Lee, Youngsoo Jun, Changwook Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificancePhosphatidic acid (PA) is a lipid intermediate produced during phospholipase C (PLC) signaling, and its levels must be precisely regulated to maintain proper signaling and lipid homeostasis at the membrane. Nir2 is a crucial lipid transfer protein that operates at endoplasmic reticulum (ER)–plasma membrane (PM) membrane contact sites (MCSs) by detecting PA and shuttling phosphatidylinositol and PA between membranes. Despite its importance, the detailed structural mechanisms underlying PA recognition and membrane association by Nir2 have remained unclear. In this study, we present crystal structures of the Nir2 domains involved in PA binding, ER anchoring, and dimer formation. Our results reveal how Nir2 couples PA sensing with ER–PM tethering, providing molecular insights into the maintenance of PI(4,5)P2-dependent PLC signaling.

Agonist-induced phosphoinositide-specific phospholipase C (PLC) signaling mediates responses to extracellular stimuli such as hormones and growth factors (1, 2). In response to extracellular stimuli, a signaling receptor activates PLC to hydrolyze phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate, abbreviated as PI(4,5)P2, at the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane (PM), generating inositol 1,4,5-triphosphate and diacylglycerol (DAG), that both act as important secondary messengers for cellular processes including calcium signaling and protein kinase C activation (3, 4). A series of biosynthetic reactions of PI(4,5)P2 requires phosphatidylinositol (PI) to be transferred to the PM from its site of synthesis in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (5). DAG is phosphorylated by DAG kinases in the PM to form phosphatidic acid (PA) (3, 6), which is transferred from the PM to the ER for continuous generation of PI (7). Without constantly replenishing PI at the PM following receptor activation, cells cannot maintain PLC signaling (8, 9). However, little was known about how PA and PI are transported between the ER and PM. In the 2000s, studies explored nonvesicular lipid transport mechanisms at membrane contact sites (MCSs) (10–14). Regarding the PLC signaling pathway, it was first suggested that Drosophila protein retinal degeneration B (RdgB) has a characteristic phosphatidylinositol transfer protein (PITP) domain and transfers PI from ER to PM (15). In addition, PYK2 N-terminal domain-interacting receptor 2 (Nir2), a mammalian homolog of RdgB, transfers PI from ER to PM (16). Furthermore, Nir2 transfers both PI and PA in the opposite direction to maintain continuity of the PI(4,5)P2 cycle (9). It is now known that Nir2 is directly recruited to ER–PM junctions by PLC activation to form ER–PM MCSs and transfer both PI and PA. Despite recent discoveries revealing crucial roles for Nir2 in the PI cycle, the molecular mechanisms by which Nir2 specifically recognizes PA at the inner leaflet of the PM, specifically targets the ER–PM junction, and positively regulates PLC signaling remain unresolved. In this study, we determined crystal structures of three truncated domains of Nir2 at atomic resolution and revealed the molecular basis of how functional domains of Nir2 contribute to the recognition and association of PA in the PM, eventually forming ER–PM MCSs to mediate continuity of the PI(4,5)P2-dependent PLC signaling pathway.

Results

Structural Basis for PA Recognition by the Nir2 LNS2 Domain.

Primary structure analysis of Nir2 revealed several distinct domains. The N terminus has a PITP domain that is required to facilitate the exchange of PI and PA at their associated membranes (17), followed by a conserved diphenylalanine [FF] in an acidic tract (FFAT) motif that is necessary to associate with vesicle-associated membrane protein-associated proteins (VAPs), an ER membrane protein (18). The conserved middle and C-terminal domain of Nir2 is predicted to include an Asp-Asp-His-Asp (DDHD) domain and a Lipin/Ned1/Smp2 (LNS2) domain, while the region connecting N terminus and middle domains (residues 360 to 420) appears to be disordered (16). Previously, colocalization analysis demonstrated that the LNS2 domain is involved in association with the PM (19). To confirm that the LNS2 domain alone is sufficient to physically and directly form contact sites between ER and PM in vitro, we prepared truncated constructs of Nir2 and vesicle-associated membrane protein-associated protein B/C (VAPB) (Fig. 1A) and tested membrane association using liposomes and dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements (Fig. 1B). Purified human Nir2 proteins were mixed with liposomes comprising phosphatidylcholine (PC) and PA at a molar ratio of 70:30 for 30 min in the presence of Hisx6-tagged Mus musculus VAPB (residues 1 − 125, Hisx6-mVAPBMSP)-anchored PC-based liposomes. DLS analyses confirmed that the almost full-length human Nir2 lacking only the N-terminal PITP domain (residues 343 to 1,191, hNir2) is clearly able to make much larger liposome clusters. By contrast, truncated Nir2 lacking the LNS2 domain (residues 343−882, hNir2ΔC-term) or with a single point mutation (F350S) within the FFAT motif (hNir2 F350S) could not (Fig. 1B). This revealed that both the LNS2 domain of Nir2 and specific interaction between the FFAT motif of Nir2 and VAPB are essential to form MCSs in vitro.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which Nir2 and VAPB establish ER–PM MCSs, we attempted to solve the structure of hNir2 at atomic resolution. However, hNir2 could not be crystallized; therefore, an N-terminally truncated form (mNir2C-term) of the Mus musculus homolog comprising residues 910−1190 was used to probe PM association by Nir2. The mouse Nir2 protein comprising 1,243 residues shares 93.4% sequence identity with the human ortholog (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). We successfully obtained orthorhombic crystals of mNir2C-term and collected X-ray diffraction data to 2.35 Å resolution using synchrotron radiation. The structure was determined by the single-wavelength anomalous diffraction (SAD) method using selenium as the anomalous scatterer and refined to R/Rfree of 19.7/25.7% (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The crystals contained three mNir2C-term molecules in the asymmetric unit. However, no intermolecular relationship or discernible structural differences based on RMSD of 1.44 Å were observed between molecules in the asymmetric unit, suggesting that monomeric mNir2C-term is the biologically functional unit. Indeed, mNir2C-term migrated as a monomer on a size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) column (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

Fig. 1C shows the overall structure of mNir2C-term in cartoon representation. The mNir2C-term form has two distinctive domains placed side by side with overall dimensions of 50 × 25 × 35 Å. The N-terminal region consisting of mNir2C-term residues 910 to 1,017 forms an Ig-like domain, comprising eight antiparallel β-strands. We propose that this domain supports stability and membrane docking of Nir2 (discussed in detail below). The C-terminus (residues 1,018 to 1,190) of mNir2C-term adopts an α/β structure that is organized into a typical α/β sandwich resembling a Rossmann fold. The five parallel β-strands in the center are encircled by six α-helices (from α1 to α4 and α6 to α7), and a 310 helix (α5) that are sequentially arranged. Intriguingly, the overall fold of the mNir2C-term is essentially identical to that of the Lipin/Pah PA phosphatase previously determined by the Airola group (20), see also SI Appendix, Fig. S4. However, a recent preprint on Nir1 (21) and earlier work on Lipin/Pah (20) have both suggested that an N-terminal extension helix within the LNS2 domain plays a critical role in membrane binding. In particular, deletion or mutation of this amphipathic helix in Lipin/Pah was shown to severely disrupt membrane association (20). To determine whether the N-terminal equivalent region of the Nir2 LNS2 domain similarly contributes to membrane binding, we generated an extended construct (residues 895 to 1,190, mNir2895–1190) and compared its activity with that of the previously analyzed LNS2 domain (residues 910 to 1,190, mNir2C-term). As shown in the SI Appendix, Fig. S5A, mNir2895–1190 displayed a modest but reproducible increase in liposome binding, approximately 1.3-fold higher at 15% PA, and 1.1-fold higher at 10% PA relative to mNir2C-term. Although the N-terminal extension modestly enhances membrane association of Nir2, the significance of this effect for PM binding is not yet clear, especially when compared to the well-established importance of the N-terminal extension in Nir1 and Lipin/Pah. Based on these findings, we have more precisely defined the LNS2 domain of Nir2 as spanning residues 895 to 1,190, consistent with the domain boundaries established for both Nir1 and Lipin/Pah (Fig. 1A).

Previous studies reported that PA is specifically recognized by the LNS2 domain of Nir2 (9, 19). This was confirmed by our liposome-tethering assay by DLS as described above (Fig. 1B). To further investigate how Nir2 specifically binds PA among the pool of glycerophospholipids within the membrane, we determined the crystal structure of mNir2C-term bound to PA at 2.15 Å resolution (Fig. 1 D and E). For this experiment, we used the synthetic compound 1,2-dihexanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphate (06:0 PA). The structure of mNir2C-term bound to PA is shown in sphere representation in Fig. 1D. Electron density was clearly observed for the PA head group including phosphate (PO4) plus ester bonds from the glycerol moiety. However, the other hydrocarbon chains of PA were disordered in the electron density map (Fig. 1E). The structure revealed that the phosphoryl group of PA was tightly embedded into the hydrophilic cavity generated by α3, α4, β9, β10, and guide loop (G-loop, residues 1,025 to 1,030) in one end of the LNS2 domain.

Key Residues and Conformational Dynamics Underlying PA Recognition by Nir2.

Having resolved the overall architecture of the Nir2 LNS2 domain and identified a conserved PA-binding cavity, we next sought to delineate the specific molecular interactions that mediate PA recognition and membrane association. In particular, the three oxygen atoms of the phosphoryl group engage in an extensive H-bond network with side chains of S1025, T1065, K1103, K1126, and D1127 that are all absolutely conserved among Nir2 orthologs (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). To assess whether these residues indeed play important roles in PA binding in solution, we performed mutagenesis and liposome sedimentation assays. PC-based liposomes containing different concentrations of PA were incubated with purified mNir2C-term protein for 30 min, then centrifuged to separate the liposome pellet and supernatant. As shown in Fig. 2B, wild-type (WT) mNir2C-term was exclusively associated with only PA-containing liposomes, and the binding was severely attenuated by mutation of S1025, K1103/K1126, or T1065/K1103/K1126 residues. Quantification of the bound protein revealed stepwise reductions in liposome association, with the triple mutant retaining about 11% of WT binding, and the double mutant approximately 21%, indicating that these residues form a core phosphate-coordination site essential for PA recognition.

To further examine whether PA binding affects the overall structure of mNir2C-term, we superimposed the structure of mNir2C-term bound to PA on that of apo-mNir2C-term. No noticeable changes were found in the overall structure of mNir2C-term bound to PA compared with the apo form (RMSD = 1.02 Å). However, intriguingly, a calcium ion was unexpectedly coordinated by the side chain of D1027, which is highly conserved among Nir2 family members. In the apo form, D1027 is located within a well-ordered G-loop, with the side chain projecting toward the vicinity of the PA-binding site. In fact, we found that the side chain of D1027 in apo mNir2C-term would sterically clash with PA through a hydrogen bond with the nitrogen atom of G1066 when we overlaid the structures. Strikingly, in the structure of PA bound to mNir2C-term, the loop undergoes a dramatic conformational change, resulting in a short helical segment. Consequently, the side chain of D1027 is distant from the PA-binding site, and instead points toward the solvent-exposed surface. The observed calcium ion directly binds to the side chain of D1027 in a different conformation, stabilizing D1027 in a new orientation (Fig. 2C).

To further explore the molecular role of D1027 in the association with PA, we attempted to generate mutants for this residue. However, despite much effort, we failed to obtain mutant proteins owing to severe aggregation in solution. We assumed that mutation of this residue destabilizes the protein, potentially indicating that D1027 may play a structural role by stabilizing the conformation. Instead, we mutated both I1026 and S1029 residues to proline to disrupt helix formation in this loop containing D1027. Surprisingly, the I1026P/S1029P double mutant displayed impaired association with PA (Fig. 2D). To assess whether PA-binding residues and G-loop conformational change influence membrane clustering, we performed DLS analysis using previously characterized mutant proteins. While WT hNir2 robustly induced liposome clustering (Fig. 1B), the PA-binding pocket mutant (T1066A/K1104A/K1127A) completely abrogated this activity (Fig. 2E). In contrast, the G-loop mutant (D1027P/S1030P) showed a moderate but significant reduction in clustering, suggesting that G-loop flexibility contributes to—but is not sufficient for—membrane tethering. These findings indicate that direct PA coordination is required for liposome clustering, working in concert with G-loop conformation. Next, we tested whether the calcium ion itself could induce conformational changes in the Nir2 helical loop structure. Despite adding a high concentration of calcium ions, reorganization of D1027 was not observed in the absence of PA, suggesting that calcium alone does not affect the conformation. However, concentration-dependent conformational changes were observed when soaking crystals with sodium phosphate (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Therefore, we propose that calcium ions might stabilize the open conformation resulting from PA binding.

A Positively Charged Concave Surface of the Nir2 LNS2 Domain Is Required for Membrane Association.

From the structure, we observed that mNir2C-term forms a slightly concave surface around the periphery that encircles the PA-binding region. Interestingly, protein surface analyses found that this concave surface is strongly positively charged (Fig. 3A). Therefore, we wondered whether Nir2 might dock into the membrane through this positively charged concave surface. Positively charged residues including R1074, R1149, and K1150 that are involved in forming the concave surface, but not directly involved in binding to PA, were mutated to aspartate, and purified mutant proteins were subjected to liposome sedimentation assays. As shown in Fig. 3B, the mutants lost the ability to associate with liposomes. In addition to the PA binding domain, the concave structure extends toward a hydrophobic loop (residues 940−946) from the adjacent Ig-like domain. When the structure of mNir2C-term is manually docked into the membrane surface, the side chains of two hydrophobic residues (L943 and V945) project deeply inside the membrane, suggesting that this loop may also contribute to a tight association with the membrane (Fig. 3A). As expected, mutation of these residues severely impaired membrane binding (Fig. 3B) despite no large structural changes according to circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). We then tested whether membrane association through the concave surface affects the formation of MCSs between subcompartments by performing DLS using hNir2 and liposomes, as shown in Fig. 1B. Both R1150D/K1151D and L944D/V946D double mutants completely failed to form liposome clusters, indicating that both the positively charged concave structure around the PA contacting region and the PA interaction are essential for Nir2 to extensively interact with the membrane (Fig. 3C).

Our structural and biochemical analyses showed that Nir2 interacts with the headgroup of PA, not the fatty acyl chains, to peripherally associate with the membranes. We then wondered how Nir2 could selectively recognize only PA within the phospholipid pool in the membranes. Based on the structure of mNir2C-term bound to PA, a phosphoryl group of the PA head group is complementarily accommodated into the small cavity generated by S1025, I1026, T1065, K1103, and K1126 (Fig. 3D). Indeed, the size of the cavity generated by these residues is ~37 Å3, ideal to accept a phosphoryl group of the PA head group. Geometric analysis suggests that the binding cavity of mNir2C-term not only supports the appropriate charge distribution, but is also optimally shaped to accommodate a phosphate group on the PA head group, resulting in strong and selective binding. Both charge and shape complementarity are critically important for selecting PA among other phospholipids in the pool. Other phospholipids may encounter steric hindrance caused by their head groups attached to the phosphate, preventing entry into the cavity, even though they may be negatively charged. In the case of PI4P, the phosphate group is positioned beyond the inositol ring, increasing the distance between the membrane and Nir2, and thereby weakening their interaction. This unique binding mode highlights the importance of the cavity of Nir2 in governing its interaction with PA. We further examined the lipid selectivity of mNir2C-term by performing liposome binding assays with PS-, PI4P-, and PI(4,5)P2-containing liposomes. As expected, these experiments revealed weak but detectable binding to PI4P, very weak binding to PI(4,5)P2 and negligible binding to PS (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). These results are also similar to a previous study of human Nir2, which demonstrated limited interaction with PI4P and no detectable binding to PS, PI, or PI(4,5)P2 (19). Collectively, our comprehensive structural observations and functional analyses underscore the critical importance of both the binding of the PA head group and the surface charge distribution on Nir2 in governing preferential binding to PA.

Structure of the Nir2 FFAT Motif–VAPB Complex.

Analysis of protein sequence showed that Nir2 contains a conserved FFAT motif adjacent to the PITP domain in the N terminus (16, 18). This motif, with the consensus sequence EFFDAxE, acts as a targeting signal responsible for tethering proteins to the cytosolic surface of the ER by interacting with the major sperm protein homology (MSP) domain of the ER integral membrane proteins VAPA/B (Fig. 4A) (24–27). Consistently, previous studies suggested that Nir2 associates with the ER membrane through its FFAT motif, and consequently mediates the ER–PM contact sites (19).

With this in mind, we wondered how the FFAT motif of Nir2 may be recognized by VAPB. The crystal structure of the mouse VAPB MSP domain (residues 1 to 125, mVAPBMSP) bound to the 10-residue mouse Nir2-FFAT peptide (mNir2FFATxtal, SEEEFFDAHE) was determined using the molecular replacement method and refined at 2.05 Å resolution to a crystallographic R/Rfree factor of 25.0/27.5% (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). The Nir2-FFAT peptide binds in an extended conformation to the surface of the characteristic MSP domain, as observed in structures of other MSP–FFAT complexes (24, 26, 28). Binding of mNir2FFATxtal did not appear to affect the overall conformation of mVAPBMSP (RMSD = 1.05 Å), when compared to the VAPB MSP domain (PDB ID:3IKK). The hydrophobic F350 and A353 residues of mNir2FFATxtal project toward two distinctive pockets formed by conserved residues K45, T47, M89, and K118, and V44, T46, V54, and F88, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). We measured the binding affinity between glutathione S-transferase (GST)-fused mouse Nir2 FFAT motif (residues 343−362, GST-mNir2FFAT) and mVAPBMSP using isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). mVAPBMSP bound to GST-mNir2FFAT with a dissociation constant (Kd) of 0.92 µM (Fig. 4C), consistent with previous reports of a binding affinity of 0.65 µM between VAPB MSP domain and FFAT peptide (EEEFFDAHE) of human Nir2 (29). However, the F350S mutant lost the ability to interact with mVAPBMSP, and the binding affinity of the A353S mutant was reduced 3.5-fold compared with the WT protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S10).

Recently, crystal structures of VAPA bound to the FFAT motif of StAR‐related lipid transfer domain‐containing 3 (STARD3), and VAPB bound to the FFAT motif of Mitoguardin-2 (Miga2) have been determined, revealing that phosphorylation within the FFAT motif is essential for interaction with VAPA/B (24, 27). These proteins have serine or threonine residues before and after the FF sequence instead of typical acidic amino acids (glutamate or aspartate), and hydrophobic interaction alone through hydrophobic residues is insufficient to tightly interact with the MSP domain of VAPA/B. Recognition of conserved hydrophobic residues in the FFAT motif by the MSP domain is preceded by ionic interactions between acidic residues of the FFAT motif and basic residues of the MSP domain. Consistently, no interactions were observed for Miga2 with VAPB or STARD3 with VAPA in the absence of phosphorylation in vitro (24, 27), even though both have an FFAT motif. In Miga2, phosphorylation of both S292 and S295 was required for interaction, as expected (27). Ionic interactions mediated by phosphorylated S209 are needed for STARD3 to directly interact with VAPA (24). These residues are absolutely conserved among orthologs. This raises questions about the Nir2 FFAT motif. We were unable to detect ionic interactions mediated by the acidic amino acids (E349 and D352) matching phosphorylated residues in Miga2 or STARD3. Additionally, mutating mVAPBMSP residues K43 and K45, which interact with phosphorylated residues, to leucine resulted in a Kd value of 0.87 µM, and no significant differences to binding with WT mVAPBMSP. Instead, we clearly observed that E355 engages in an ionic interaction with R55 of mVAPBMSP (Fig. 4B). This interaction was not present in the Miga2 and STARD3 structures (Fig. 4B). Consistent with the structural findings, the substitution of R55 with alanine in mVAPBMSP significantly weakened its interaction with the Nir2 FFAT motif (Fig. 4C), with a sixfold increase in Kd value. Similarly, the Nir2 E355A mutation resulted in approximately a fivefold increase in Kd value, indicating a comparable reduction in binding affinity (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Moreover, this impaired binding translated into a functional consequence, as DLS measurements revealed a strong reduction in liposome clustering ability (Fig. 4D), highlighting the importance of the E355–R55 ionic interaction for tethering activity. Therefore, we propose that ionic interactions between Nir2 E355 and VAPB R55 help the MSP domain of VAPB to accurately recognize the FFAT motif of Nir2.

Self-Association of the Nir2 DDHD Domain Enhances PM Binding.

Previous studies suggested that the DDHD domain might play a key role in binding phospholipids and the PM (30). To further investigate the molecular functions of the Nir2 DDHD domain, we attempted to solve the crystal structure at atomic resolution. Despite extensive efforts to obtain diffraction-quality crystals using human Nir2 DDHD domain (residues 422 to 882, hNir2DDHD), the crystals diffracted poorly. To resolve this problem, we deleted the predicted long disordered loop (residues 584 to 682) from the construct (hNir2DDHDΔ), and finally obtained trigonal crystals in space group P3121 with three copies of the DDHD domain in the asymmetric unit. The structure was determined by molecular replacement using the structure predicted by AlphaFold 2.0 (31) as a search model and was refined to 2.8 Å resolution (see Materials and Methods and SI Appendix, Table S1).

The molecular model of hNir2DDHDΔ is presented in Fig. 5A. Since SEC revealed that hNir2DDHDΔ forms a dimer in solution (Fig. 5B), we concluded that the dimer might be the biologically relevant form of hNir2DDHDΔ. In the structure, hNir2DDHDΔ adopts a typical α/β sandwich fold and forms a compact structure with dimensions of 28 × 33 × 98 Å. We renumbered the secondary structural elements within hNir2DDHDΔ for clarity. Six parallel β-strands are enveloped by five α-helices (from α1, α4 to α5, and α11 to α12) and seven extra α-helices (from α2 to α3, and α6 to α10) distinct from the core structure (Fig. 5A). Among these extra helices, α2, α3, and α6 are directly involved in forming the symmetric twofold dimeric interface of hNir2DDHDΔ by assembling two pairs of α3 and α6 helices into a four-helix bundle structure. In particular, L520 from α3 and L706 from α6 engage in extensive hydrophobic interactions, establishing a stable twofold dimeric interface. To prove whether the hydrophobic core is indeed important for dimer formation, we generated the L520D mutant. As expected, the L520D mutant eluted as a monomer from the SEC column (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, to explore whether breaking the dimer interface in the DDHD domain affects the organization of the full-length (ΔPITP) human Nir2 protein, we conducted DLS experiments using purified hNir2 protein. While WT hNir2 protein detected as a peak at 24.4 nm, the peak of the hNir2 L520D mutant was shifted to 18.2 nm, suggesting that disruption of the dimer interface conferred by DDHD domain mutation impaired the overall organization of the Nir2 structure (Fig. 5C).

It was previously reported that oligomerization of membrane-binding proteins can increase their affinity for membrane association (33). Therefore, we wondered whether self-association of Nir2 actually affects membrane association. We performed liposome flotation assays as described in a previous study (34) with WT or L520D mutant hNir2 proteins mixed with PC-based liposomes in the absence/presence of PA and centrifuged in Nycodenz layered solution. As shown in Fig. 5D, WT hNir2 associated with membranes in the presence of PA, whereas membrane association of the L520D mutant was decreased by ~30%. Interestingly, liposome clustering was severely impaired in DLS experiments (Fig. 5E), suggesting that the DDHD domain promotes the ability of Nir2 to effectively associate with the PM by driving oligomerization.

The structure of the DDHD domain resembles that of the hydrolase domain of Lipoprotein lipase (LPL, RMSD = 4.75 Å) despite their negligible sequence homology (12.7% sequence identity between the ~363 amino acids of the DDHD domain and the hydrolase domain; SI Appendix, Fig. S11A). LPL is a lipase with a GXSXG catalytic motif (35). When the two structures were aligned, we realized that catalytic triad residue S159 of LPL is replaced by G565 in hNir2DDHDΔ (SI Appendix, Fig. S11B). We also confirmed that the substrate-binding region of LPL aligned roughly with the dimeric interface of hNir2DDHDΔ. Although the DDHD domain mimics the LPL fold structurally, it lacks catalytic machinery, and no lipase activity has been reported for Nir2. This structural resemblance suggests evolutionary repurposing toward a scaffolding or lipid-binding function rather than enzymatic catalysis.

Finally, we tested whether the DDHD domain of Nir2 interacts with phospholipids, even though it is incapable of hydrolyzing substrates. The hNir2DDHDΔ protein was incubated with nitrobenzoxadiazole (NBD) fluorescently labeled phospholipids and assessed by native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). Interestingly, the DDHD domain specifically bound to PA and PS but not to PC and ceramide (SI Appendix, Fig. S11C). However, we found that hNir2DDHD alone could not associate with PC-based PA containing liposomes (SI Appendix, Fig. S11D), indicating that DDHD–lipid interactions are limited to free phospholipid headgroups and are insufficient for stable membrane association.

Discussion

In this study, we determined three crystal structures of Nir2, revealing the molecular mechanisms by which Nir2 selectively recognizes PA at the PM through its LNS2 domain. Subsequently, Nir2 binds to the ER via its FFAT motif through interaction with VAPB, thereby associating with the ER–PM tethering region where its lipid exchange activity can be exerted (Fig. 6). Based on the structures, the center cavity of the concave surface of the Nir2 LNS2 domain directly interacts with the phosphate group of the PA head, rather than the long acyl chains, with a molar ratio of 1:1 (Fig. 1 D and E). To specifically recognize PA among the various phospholipids at the PM, the LNS2 domain of Nir2 features a small cavity that preferentially accommodates PA through both shape and surface charge complementarity. In more detail, we identified four key residues (S1025, T1065, K1103, and K1126) that play a significant role in interacting with the PA headgroup (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, we demonstrated that mutation of these residues severely reduced the ability of Nir2 to associate with PA-containing liposomes in vitro, suggesting a critical role in PM association under physiological conditions (Fig. 2B). This is consistent with previous results showing that the LNS2 domain mediates the translocation of Nir2 to the PM in response to cellular stimulation (19). Compared to other PA-binding proteins, which often rely on extended electrostatic surfaces or flexible amphipathic elements to engage anionic lipids, the LNS2 domain of Nir2 contains a preformed, confined cavity that selectively accommodates the PA headgroup. For example, Opi1 and Spo20, two representative PA-binding proteins, utilize amphipathic helices and charge interactions to bind PA, but also interact with PS or PI(4,5)P2 (36–38). In contrast, the binding site in Nir2 is structurally restricted to exclude larger headgroups, conferring enhanced specificity (Fig. 3D). This unique binding mode allows Nir2 to act as a lipid sensor that selectively detects transient PA accumulation at the PM, thus supporting its recruitment to ER–PM contact sites upon PA accumulation. To support the continuity of the PLC signaling pathway, phosphoinositide intermediates including PA must be dynamically regulated at the PM (39). Therefore, cells possess dedicated machinery to sense PA in the PM, and we propose that Nir2 might act as a sensor to selectively bind PA among the various phospholipids in the PM. To exhibit clear specificity toward PA, Nir2 has a precise cavity that snugly accommodates only the PA headgroup (Fig. 3D). Therefore, Nir2 responds to PA accumulation by associating with ER–PM MCSs, enabling its function in lipid metabolism. The structural model presented in Fig. 3D suggests that, for PA recognition in the membrane, the Nir2 LNS2 domain must be positioned in close proximity to the membrane surface, thereby potentially restricting the accommodation of neighboring phospholipids with bulky headgroups. This structural arrangement may favor the local enrichment of additional PA molecules, DAG, or cholesterol, which could interact with the surrounding basic patch through electrostatic or hydrophobic interactions. Consistently, our in vitro liposome binding assays (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A) showed that Nir2 associates with membranes containing at least 10% PA, with binding efficiency increasing dramatically in a PA concentration–dependent manner. These results suggest that Nir2 can sense relatively low PA levels while achieving stronger membrane association under PA-enriched conditions. This observation aligns with previous cell-based studies reporting that PA accumulation at the PM is required for Nir2 recruitment (9, 19). Together, our findings support a model in which both the structural geometry of the LNS2 domain and the local lipid composition cooperate to regulate Nir2 localization at ER–PM contact sites.

The PLC signaling pathway is intimately involved in controlling calcium levels in cells, suggesting that Ca2+ might regulate components involved in ER–PM contact formation, including Nir2. Nir2/3 depletion leads to reduced receptor-induced calcium signaling, reflecting disrupted PI cycle continuity at ER–PM junctions (40). Previous studies demonstrated that Nir2 binds Ca2+ via acidic residues located adjacent to its VAPA/B-binding FFAT motif (16). However, Ca2+ ions were not observed in our structure of the FFAT–VAPB complex (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). Rather, we observed a Ca2+ ion in the PA-binding region of the Nir2 LNS2 domain (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Additional biochemical studies demonstrated that association of Ca2+ did not affect the conformational change of the LNS2 domain that facilitates PA binding (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Instead, PA binding itself may induce the conformational rearrangement of a G-loop, resulting in the formation of a short helical segment (Fig. 2C). The observed Ca2+ ion was coordinated by the side chain of residue D1027 in this open conformation and may stabilize the rearranged structure of the LNS2 domain bound to PA. These results suggest that the translocation of Nir2 to the ER–PM junction is more sensitive to PA accumulation than to cytosolic Ca2+ levels. Thus, while Nir2 localization is primarily driven by PA sensing, other ER–PM tethers such as extended synaptotagmin 1 respond directly to Ca2+ elevations (40).

Nir2 primarily resides in the ER peripheral membrane since the FFAT motif of Nir2 is specifically associated with the MSP domain of VAPB, an ER membrane protein. Recent studies showed that phosphorylation of residues within the FFAT motif is required for interaction with the MSP domain of VAPA/B (24, 27). The distinctive interaction module might be due to differences in residues within the FFAT motif, which may contribute to controlling the formation of organelle contact sites through elaborate cellular regulation. However, no such modifications have been reported for Nir2. The Nir2 FFAT motif binds to VAPB with high affinity (Kd in the nanomolar range; Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S10), without requiring posttranslational modifications. This is consistent with previous reports showing that Nir2 is an ER-associated protein in the quiescent state, with no contact sites in the PM (41). Upon PLC activation and subsequent accumulation of PA at the PM, Nir2 is recruited to ER–PM contact sites through PA recognition by its LNS2 domain, as confirmed by our structural data.

To date, no distinct functions for the DDHD domain of Nir2 have been reported. Although the structure of the DDHD domain resembles that of LPL lipases, our data confirm that the domain is not catalytically active due to steric hindrance at the substrate entry site and substitution of key catalytic triad residues (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 A and B). In this study, we showed that Nir2 forms a homodimer through the DDHD domain, and this oligomerization promotes stable membrane association (Fig. 5 D and E). Recent studies suggested that Nir2 can form a heterodimer with Nir1, a homolog of Nir2. Oligomer formation of the Nir1–Nir2 complex is known to contribute to localization of Nir2 to the PM (42). Nir2 shares 52.80 and 58.01% sequence identity with Nir1 and Nir3, respectively; therefore, Nir2 can form a heterodimer with Nir1 or Nir3 through their DDHD domain. We compared the overall structures of the DDHD domains of Nir1, Nir2, and Nir3 by overlaying the structures of Nir1 and Nir3 (predicted AlphaFold 2.0) with one molecule of the hNir2DDHDΔ. Surprisingly, the four-helix bundle present at the twofold dimeric center was perfectly superimposed in Nir2–Nir1 and Nir2–Nir3 heterodimer complexes (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). In addition, key hydrophobic residues involved in dimer formation are also conserved in Nir1 and Nir3, raising the possibility that Nir2 might form a heterodimer with Nir1 or Nir3 through DDHD domains. Further structural and biochemical studies will be required to validate the formation and functional relevance of these heterodimers in cells.

In summary, we propose a molecular model for PA recognition and MCS formation by Nir2 at ER–PM contact sites (Fig. 6). These findings provide molecular insights into how Nir2 supports the continuity of PLC signaling by coordinating lipid metabolism at MCSs.

Materials and Methods

Cloning, Protein Expression, and Purification.

The mNir2C-term (910–1190), mNir2895–1190 (895–1190), and Hisx6-mVAPBMSP (1–125) fragments were cloned into modified pETduet-1 vectors containing an N-terminal Hisx6 tag followed by a TEV protease cleavage site. GST-mNir2FFAT (343–362), mVAPBMSP, hNir2 (343–1191), and hNir2ΔC-term (343–882) were cloned into pGEX-6P-1 with an N-terminal GST tag, whereas hNir2DDHD (422–882) and hNir2DDHDΔ (422–882, Δ584–682) were inserted into modified pET-28b vectors containing an N-terminal Hisx6–Smt3 tag and a ULP1 cleavage site. Point mutations were introduced by PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis.

mNir2C-term, mNir2895–1190, hNir2, hNir2ΔC-term were expressed in Escherichia coli Rosetta (DE3). Se-Met–labeled mNir2C-term was produced in E. coli B834 (DE3). Hisx6-mVAPBMSP, hNir2DDHD, and hNir2DDHDΔ were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3). Protein expression was induced with 0.3 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside at 18 °C for 18 h.

Hisx6- or Hisx6–Smt3-tagged proteins (mNir2C-term, mNir2895–1190, hNir2DDHD, hNir2DDHDΔ, Hisx6-mVAPBMSP) were purified by Ni2+-affinity chromatography, followed by proteolytic cleavage with TEV or ULP1 protease. Cleaved samples were subjected to a second Ni2+-affinity step to remove proteases and uncleaved proteins. GST-tagged proteins (GST-mNir2FFAT, mVAPBMSP, hNir2, hNir2ΔC-term) were purified on glutathione affinity columns, and the GST tags of mVAPBMSP, hNir2, and hNir2ΔC-term were removed by PreScission protease. For crystallization and biochemical assays, all proteins were finally purified by SEC into buffer (25 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 5 mM DTT). Additional experimental procedures, including detailed cloning, expression and purification protocols, crystallization methods, and biochemical assays, are described in the SI Appendix.
