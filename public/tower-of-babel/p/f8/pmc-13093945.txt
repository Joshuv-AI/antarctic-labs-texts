Bridging evidence and care for knee osteoarthritis in China: a randomized feasibility trial with RE-AIM-guided process evaluation of the PEAK-CHN model of care
Authors: Ziru Wang, Shuning Duan, Xier Chen, Huili Deng, Guoxin Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExercise therapy and education are recommended first-line treatments for knee osteoarthritis (KOA), yet uptake in routine care remains limited globally, including in China, which bears the largest disease burden. Barriers include limited access to allied health services, low awareness of guideline-based care, and poor integration across care sectors. To address these challenges, we developed PEAK-CHN (Physiotherapy Exercise and Physical Activity for Knee Osteoarthritis in China), a multidisciplinary implementation model covering care content, care delivery, and care providers. This study aimed to implement PEAK-CHN in real-world settings and evaluate its feasibility and early implementation outcomes using the RE-AIM framework.MethodsWe conducted a parallel-group, two-arm randomized feasibility trial (n = 73; mean age 66.4 years; 65.8% female). Participants were randomized to receive either PEAK-CHN or usual care. The intervention targeted three components: (1) care providers, a multidisciplinary team trained through workshops and online modules; (2) care content, including five structured telehealth consultations, personalized home-based strengthening exercise, tailored education, daily behavior-change messaging, and comorbidity management; and (3) care delivery within a policy-supported primary care setting using a hybrid model. Usual care was documented via the local health insurance system. Primary outcomes assessed feasibility (recruitment, adherence, engagement, staff workload, and preliminary costing). Secondary outcomes assessed clinical, psychological, and behavioral changes at baseline, 3, and 6 months. Semi-structured interviews (n = 11) explored participant experiences.ResultsRecruitment was completed within four weeks with a 63% enrollment rate. Retention at 6 months was 97%. All intervention participants completed all five telehealth consultations, and 90% achieved the prescribed exercise dose of at least three sessions per week. Delivery was feasible within routine workflows, with consultations averaging 30 min and no intervention-related adverse events. Participant satisfaction was high (mean 8.8/10), and most rated key components as helpful. Exploratory analyses suggested potential improvements in pain, function, and self-efficacy compared with usual care.ConclusionsPEAK-CHN was feasible and acceptable in a policy-supported primary care setting in China and can be delivered using existing workforce and digital infrastructure. Background

Osteoarthritis (OA) represents one of the major worldwide public health concerns [1], and knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is the most prevalent type [2]. Despite robust evidence supporting exercise therapy and education as core components of KOA management [3, 4], their systematic integration into routine care remains limited across health systems worldwide, including China [5–9]. This implementation gap is particularly concerning given China’s disproportionate share of the global KOA burden [10], with 109.6 million prevalent cases in 2021, the highest national case count worldwide [11], largely driven by rapid population aging and escalating rates of obesity and physical inactivity [12, 13]. While national health strategies such as “Healthy China 2030” have emphasized the importance of chronic disease management and rehabilitation [14], existing care pathways for KOA remain fragmented, medically oriented, and poorly aligned with international guideline-based standards [9]. The underutilization of non-pharmacological interventions reflects both structural and behavioral challenges: allied health services are scarce and unevenly distributed; public awareness of evidence-based management remains low; and coordination between hospitals and primary care is minimal, limiting opportunities for continuity and self-management support.

Implementation science offers a framework for addressing these entrenched barriers by focusing not only on what works, but how, for whom, and under what conditions [15]. In particular, the Knowledge-to-Action (KTA) [16] model emphasizes the dynamic adaptation of evidence to context, stakeholder engagement, and sustained capacity-building. A Model of Care (MoC) operationalizes implementation science by translating evidence and clinical guidelines into a structured, context-tailored way of delivering care, and by specifying how delivery will be implemented and evaluated using implementation outcomes such as adoption, feasibility, and fidelity [17–19]. While numerous MoCs [20] for OA have been developed internationally to address the barriers of implementing core KOA care in their primary care settings, few adequately address the unique socio-economic and cultural context of China, including high reliance on hospital-based specialist care, rural–urban disparities and unmet rehabilitation needs, and widespread reliance on passive therapies and traditional Chinese medicine [9, 21, 22]. In response to the system-level and individual-level implementation barriers identified in China, the PEAK-CHN (Physiotherapy Exercise and Physical Activity for Knee Osteoarthritis in China) was developed as a context-specific multicomponent MoC incorporating key implementation strategies (e.g., provider training, digital tools, multidisciplinary collaboration, and policy alignment), grounded in the KTA framework to support real-world delivery (Fig. 1). The model targets three interdependent dimensions essential for effective delivery of guideline-based KOA care: (1) care content, by embedding structured exercise prescription, behavior change techniques, and educational tools into routine practice; (2) care providers, by building capacity among multidisciplinary teams spanning hospital-based rehabilitation specialists, community health managers, and general practitioners; and (3) care delivery, by leveraging telehealth and community coordination mechanisms to extend reach and improve engagement within a policy-supported primary care infrastructure. Unlike existing KOA primary care models in China [23], which often prioritize pharmacologic or surgical interventions and defer exercise therapy to early-stage or mild cases, PEAK-CHN centers exercise and education as foundational across disease severity levels, and is one of the first OA MoC guided by implementation science frameworks in China.

PEAK-CHN theoretical model components

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the feasibility of implementing PEAK-CHN in real-world primary clinical settings in China and assess its implementation process using the RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance) framework, following the design of the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) implementation trials recommendations [19].

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a parallel two-arm randomized feasibility trial evaluating a multicomponent osteoarthritis model of care (PEAK-CHN), with an embedded implementation package. The primary intent was to assess feasibility of intervention delivery and research procedures in a real-world primary care context, while exploring preliminary patient outcome signals to inform a future definitive trial. Concurrently, an implementation process evaluation guided by the RE-AIM framework was conducted using a mixed-methods approach that combined quantitative and qualitative data collection and analysis from clinical practitioners and patients. The study has been registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400091007) with accessible online protocol and the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) checklist for randomized feasibility studies [24] was used to guide reporting (Additional file 1). Ethics approval was obtained from the local ethics committee (XMFHIIT-2023SL140) in accordance with institutional guidelines. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and data were anonymized to ensure privacy and confidentiality. No compensation was provided for participation.

Setting

The trial was conducted in Xiamen, China, where community health centers form a well-developed primary care network and operate within a policy environment that supports integrating exercise into chronic disease management, including national policy initiatives promoting the integration of sports and medicine under the Healthy China 2030 strategy [25, 26]. Community health centers have become major primary care providers in China and serve as the main platform for chronic disease management, making them an appropriate setting for delivering long-term OA care [27, 28]. This context aligns with national priorities to strengthen primary care capacity and embed digital health tools into routine chronic disease management [29, 30].

Participants

Participants (patients) were recruited through local community healthcare systems from May to June 2024. Recruitment materials were disseminated through both established patient networks and newly presenting outpatients attending community healthcare centers, including a featured community site (WuCun) in Xiamen. The recruitment process included (1) an initial pre-screening conducted by the admin director from community healthcare centers, followed by (2) detailed eligibility assessment by health managers and paired general practitioners. Community general practitioners conducted an initial assessment of outpatients in their normal practice routine, excluded contraindications to exercise, and provided a brief introduction to the study before referring interested patients to health managers. Health managers then delivered detailed study information, conducted full eligibility assessments, and completed the enrolment process. For patients identified from existing records, recruitment was conducted directly by telephone. Recruitment activities were documented in real time using a shared electronic tracking system, including patient source, consent and baseline completion, and recruitment workload metrics. Sample size

As this was a feasibility study, the sample size was not designed to provide adequate statistical power to test hypotheses regarding clinical outcomes. Instead, the primary aim was to estimate key feasibility parameters and to obtain preliminary estimates of outcome variability to inform the design of a future definitive randomized controlled trial. A minimum total sample size of 60 participants was planned to enable estimation of feasibility outcomes with acceptable precision and to provide preliminary estimates for planning. Allowing for up to 20% attrition, the target recruitment was set at 73 participants.

Randomization and blinding

After providing informed consent and completing baseline assessments, participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group or the usual care group. Randomization was conducted using a mixed block randomization method with block sizes of 4, 6, and 8. The randomization sequence was generated by an independent statistician who was not involved in any other aspect of the study. To ensure allocation concealment, the randomization list was stored in a secure, password-protected digital file accessible only to a designated research assistant (ZJ) who had no contact with participants or outcome assessors. Group assignments were revealed only after the participant was fully enrolled and baseline assessments were complete. Upon unlocking the allocation sequence, the assistant informed the relevant health managers and provided them with task lists and necessary resources. Participants allocated to the intervention group were assigned to rehabilitation specialists followed a sequential assignment approach, ensuring that each rehabilitation specialist was assigned approximately the same number of participants.

This study employed a single-blind design. Participants and their corresponding care teams were aware of group allocation, but quantitative and qualitative analyses were undertaken by analysts who were not involved in intervention delivery and did not have access to allocation information during analysis. Allocation information was strictly encrypted, and all data records were anonymized using coded identifiers. Data analysts remained blinded to group allocation until statistical analyses were completed. Specifically, participant questionnaires were collected electronically via QR codes, with anonymized results exported by the research team and provided to independent statisticians for analysis. Interview data were collected by independent interviewers uninvolved in patient management, and transcripts were anonymized and analyzed thematically by independent qualitative analysts.

Intervention- PEAK-CHN model of care

The intervention targets three components in the PEAK-CHN: who delivers (care providers), what to deliver (care content), and how to deliver (care delivery). Specifically,

Care providers

The intervention was delivered through a multidisciplinary team, including rehabilitation specialists from a regional public hospital, health managers from community health centers, and paired community GPs. Prior to patient management, all care providers underwent training through three targeted workshop sessions (each lasting 2 h), covering topics about OA knowledge, chronic pain management, core competencies, and exercise prescription skills. Each workshop provided learning materials including strategies to support behavior change, overcome exercise barriers, enhance self-efficacy, and manage pain etc. Additional resources for rehabilitation specialists included structured consultation scripts, communication tips, and access to a comprehensive online learning course [31] (comprising four modules, totaling approximately 8 h) focusing on KOA exercise interventions and teleconsultation skills. All training and materials were delivered in simplified Chinese. After the third workshop session, all providers completed quizzes, followed by group discussions of the answers to ensure accurate understanding of relevant KOA concepts. Rehabilitation specialists also provided evidence of e-learning completion, achieving at least 80% accuracy in online module quizzes, and successfully passed mock consultations with the research team and volunteer participants. Care providers were formally engaged in the study only upon demonstrating sufficient competence and confidence to deliver high-quality interventions. Intervention delivery was limited to study-trained rehabilitation specialists, community health managers, and general practitioners. These providers did not deliver care to participants in the control group.

All consultations were recorded to ensure fidelity, and exercise plans were adjusted according to the American College of Sports Medicine guidelines [32], with intensity set at RPE 5–7 out of 10 (“hard” to “very hard”) [33]. Progression was determined by patient-reported exertion scale, symptoms or flare-ups, movement quality, and confidence. Adjustments included changing the resistance band level, modifying repetitions or sets, or selecting easier or more advanced exercises based on patient feedback and tolerance. Care delivery

All communication and management were conducted via WeChat app, which served as the primary platform in China for efficient scheduling and coordination between patients and the healthcare team, while enabling regular follow-up. Intervention participants engaged in online consultations through WeChat video calls, accessible via mobile or desktop. Therapists conducted consultations from the clinic using WeChat-enabled computers, with patients participating from home or other convenient locations. Consultations were recorded to ensure fidelity for research purposes. Post-consultation inquiries were addressed by therapists via online messaging, though patients were typically directed to community health managers for additional support.

Control—usual care

Participants in the control group followed their usual care pathways throughout the study, which included standard KOA treatments such as physical modalities therapy (e.g., electronic or infrared therapy), Traditional Chinese Medicine (e.g., acupuncture, herbal pads), pharmacological therapies (e.g., anti-inflammatory drugs, supplements), and injections (e.g., corticosteroids, hyaluronic acid). For complex cases, referrals to other specialists were made.

To ensure the completeness and accuracy of healthcare utilization data, all medical visits and service records during the study period were automatically documented through an existing integrated medical information system. This system is able to capture detailed information on each encounter, including service types, medications used, treatment plans, and associated healthcare costs, across both community health centers and public hospitals. These system-level records helped reduce the risk of data loss or incomplete tracking. In parallel, participants were asked to regularly self-report any healthcare services accessed throughout the trial period, including medications, physical therapies, or alternative treatments not captured in institutional records. They were also encouraged to report relevant health behaviors, such as changes in diet, exercise routines, or other self-management practices. This dual data collection approach ensured a comprehensive understanding of real-world care and treatment exposure in the control group. Usual care was delivered by routine clinical staff at community health centers or hospitals and was not standardized. These providers were not part of the trained intervention team.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes focused on feasibility, including both study feasibility and intervention feasibility. Study feasibility outcomes included recruitment (source and enrollment success rate), retention (completion of follow-up assessments), adherence (completion of all five consultations), safety (intervention-related adverse events), and staff workload (time spent on consultations, between-session Q&A, preparation, and scheduling coordination). Intervention feasibility outcomes included perceived benefit of key components (% rated as helpful), satisfaction (0–10 rating scale), and engagement (participation frequency in weekly strength training sessions, handbook use, exercise log completion, and WeChat group check-in posts). Secondary outcomes assessed preliminary signals on patient-reported health outcomes (e.g., pain, function), psychological determinants, and health behaviors at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months. Full detailed outcome measures are listed in Additional file 4.

Data collection and analysis

Quantitative data were collected via online questionnaires or phone call by paired community health managers. Semi-structured interviews with intervention participants were conducted within one month post-intervention to explore their participation experiences, perceived benefits, barriers, and suggestions. Interviews (15 min each) followed a structured guide (Additional file 5) and was led by an independent, trained qualitative researcher (HD).

Data collection for the implementation process evaluation was guided by the RE-AIM framework, assessing multi-level factors (patient, clinician, system) influencing implementation by summarizing both quantitative and qualitative data. Reach was assessed through patient demographics and recruitment processes. Effectiveness was explored through preliminary signals in clinical outcomes and participant feedback; health insurance records were used to inform preliminary costing. Adoption focused on the number and characteristics of participating sites and staff. Implementation measured intervention fidelity, adherence, and provider engagement. Fidelity of intervention was evaluated through review of rehabilitation specialists’ work logs (including each participant’s individualized physical activity and resistance training plans), random real-time audits of online consultations, and documentation of communications between intervention staff and the research team regarding resources requests or support needs. Maintenance assessed retention, follow-up participation, and perceived sustainability. Maintenance was assessed primarily at the individual level within the trial period (e.g., retention and sustained engagement), while longer-term organizational sustainability beyond the study period was not formally evaluated.

Quantitative data were analyzed using R Studio (v2024.12.0) with descriptive statistics for feasibility outcomes and linear regression models for continuous secondary outcomes. Qualitative data underwent inductive thematic analysis [36] with Nvivo (v14.0), coded independently by two researchers (XC and SD), with themes developed inductively from the data. Discrepancies were resolved through team discussion with the lead researcher (ZW). A semantic coding approach was adopted, focusing on participants’ explicit meanings rather than underlying assumptions. Analytic rigor was enhanced through double coding, iterative refinement of themes, and team-based review. All interviews were conducted and transcribed verbatim in Mandarin, and thematic analysis was undertaken in Mandarin. Illustrative quotations were translated into English by a bilingual researcher for English manuscript publication purposes only, with accuracy and conceptual equivalence checked by a second bilingual researcher.

Results

A total of 73 people with symptomatic knee OA completed baseline surveys and were randomized into groups. The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 2. In the intervention group, eight participants withdrew during the first part of the initial consultation and did not commence the intervention. Over half of the participants (37/73,50.6%) reported knee pain lasting more than five years. The baseline pain score was 5.05 (SD = 1.57), functional score of 71.44 (out of 100), low KOA knowledge level (mean 31.6/55), and low physical activity and strength training levels (mean 1.2 days/week). The demographic characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. Care was provided by 11 healthcare managers and 11 paired GPs from the community, and 7 hospital practitioners from the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine, all of whom had successfully completed their interventional training for the study. All professionals were nationally registered healthcare providers and were actively engaged in medical services related to OA. None of the professionals involved in this study had conflicts of interest related to their medical practice or profession. The primary care setting has active policy support (“integration of healthcare and sports”, and the " Tri-professional collaborative management system") and supportive working environment for the professionals.

Study feasibility

aRecruitment: Over a four-week recruitment period, each health manager screened 11 patients, of whom 7 met the eligibility criteria and were enrolled, yielding an enrolment rate of approximately 63%. Discussion

This study evaluated the feasibility and acceptability of PEAK-CHN, a telehealth-enabled OA model of care delivered within China’s community health center system, alongside a concurrent implementation process evaluation guided by the RE-AIM framework. Overall, the intervention and study procedures were feasible, with efficient community-based recruitment, high retention, strong engagement, and minimal disruption to routine primary care workflows. Participants reported high satisfaction and perceived benefit of key intervention components, supporting the acceptability of the model in this context. While the trial was not powered to test effectiveness, exploratory findings suggested potential improvements in patient-reported outcomes and key behavioral and psychological determinants. Importantly, the RE-AIM evaluation identified multi-level enablers of implementation (including workflow integration, staff roles, and supportive local policy priorities), as well as areas for refinement such as ensuring consistent delivery of education components. Together, these findings support progression to a future definitive trial and a broader implementation evaluation focusing on longer-term sustainability and scale-up.

The global management of KOA has shifted from isolated medical interventions to integrated models of care MoC. However, most existing MoC research is derived from high-income countries, limiting its direct applicability to China’s unique healthcare system. Unlike many systems with formal gatekeeping, China’s health system allows substantial patient choice and hospital-first care seeking, and patients can often present directly to higher-level hospitals without referral, despite ongoing policies to strengthen primary care and promote tiered delivery [37, 38]. In this context, community health centers function as the core primary care platform for chronic disease management, but their “gatekeeping” role is policy-driven and locally implemented, rather than uniformly enforced across China [39, 40]. They typically employ general practitioners (public health physicians), nurses (health managers), pharmacists, and traditional Chinese medicine specialists in a community health center, however, multidisciplinary capacity and especially rehabilitation staffing vary substantially by locality, which affects the feasibility of delivering guideline-based exercise and education at scale. Consistent with broader evidence on primary care challenges in China, barriers relevant to first-line KOA care include uneven availability of rehabilitation and allied health resources, weak continuity between care levels, and documented inequities in access to quality primary care and services across settings [41, 42]. In response, the PEAK-CHN was developed as one of the first KOA MoCs designed specifically for the Chinese healthcare context. It integrates international best practices with local policies, leveraging online training and telehealth services to mitigate the uneven distribution of resources and ensure consistent delivery of evidence-based KOA care.

The pilot site was intentionally selected because it aligns with national and local policy priorities supporting “health-sport integration” and strengthened primary care delivery, which provided a favorable context to test implementation feasibility in a real-world setting. The Xiamen setting is relevant because it represents a relatively mature primary care network operating under active local implementation of governmental policies and digital health infrastructure [43, 44]. At the same time, the broader system context of patient-driven hospital access and variable primary care gatekeeping means that models like PEAK-CHN must work across fragmented pathways, using telehealth and community healthcare coordination to preserve continuity of exercise and education delivery [45, 46]. Our findings indicate that PEAK-CHN can be feasibly delivered using existing community and hospital rehabilitation resources supported by telehealth-enabled workflows. While longer-term organizational sustainability and broader scale-up were not evaluated in this feasibility trial, the current results provide a foundation to inform the design of a future definitive trial and a subsequent implementation evaluation focusing on sustainability and wider scale-up. Future research may also include the development of training toolkits and a formal economic evaluation (e.g., cost-effectiveness) alongside a definitive trial.

Compared to established KOA MoCs with integrated care pathways in high-income local health system settings such as PARTNER (Australia) [47], CONNACT (Singapore) [48], and SAMBA (Norway) [49], PEAK-CHN shares core principles including guideline-based exercise and education, structured follow-up, and multidisciplinary involvement [20]. Moreover, PEAK-CHN was intentionally adapted to China’s health system and society cultural context. One key point of divergence lies in the policy and governance environment underpinning implementation. In China, healthcare delivery is strongly policy driven, with organizational behavior shaped by top-down administrative directives and leadership mandates. Because remuneration and professional incentives for public-sector healthcare workers are largely fixed, compliance with policy guidance and institutional leadership plays a central role in shaping practice change [50, 51]. This contrasts with models such as PARTNER, CONNACT, and SAMBA, which operate in more decentralized systems and rely more heavily on clinician autonomy, professional motivation, and locally driven behavior change strategies [52–54]. As a result, PEAK-CHN may benefit from strong policy alignment and leadership endorsement to facilitate uptake and standardization, while facing distinct challenges in fostering bottom-up engagement, intrinsic motivation, and sustained behavioral change among providers and patients. Moreover, the model leverages existing community workforce roles (health managers and GPs) and hospital-based rehabilitation expertise through telehealth-enabled delivery, which may help address common constraints in China [55, 56] as well as in other developing countries [57, 58], including limited and uneven access to rehabilitation services and fragmented care pathways. Consistent with behavior change-oriented KOA models such as PARTNER and CONNACT, our findings indicate encouraging engagement with exercise and education components, suggesting that structured follow-up and team-based delivery can support self-management in community settings. However, compared with these established programs, we observed greater variability in patient adherence and readiness for remote care, echoing prior evidence highlighting the need for more explicit psychosocial support strategies within PEAK-CHN. Sustained multidisciplinary collaboration and cross-sector integration may therefore require not only coordination mechanisms but also supportive policy and financing arrangements. These considerations highlight priorities for refining PEAK-CHN and informing the design of a future definitive trial and subsequent implementation evaluation.

This study has several limitations. First, the long-term sustainability of the PEAK-CHN model is uncertain, as it currently relies on manual interventions by the team, such as sending behavior change messages and managing online activity logs. Future research should focus on developing automated digital tools, including mobile applications, to facilitate self-management. Second, although many outcomes were self-reported and may be subject to reporting bias, key feasibility and engagement indicators were supported by behavioural records (exercise logs and WeChat check-in data), which strengthened confidence in adherence estimates. Third, while the educational materials employed are evidence-based, the effectiveness of various dissemination strategies in promoting adherence and sustained behavior change remains underexplored. Fourth, limited participation from orthopedic and traditional Chinese medicine physicians, due to conflicting interests, underscores the need for more inclusive and collaborative care models. Finally, future work may further strengthen intervention design by testing additional engagement strategies to support long-term adherence and self-management.

Conclusions

This study provides empirical evidence that PEAK‑CHN can be feasibly embedded within China’s primary care system and delivered within existing clinical and policy structures with generally good fidelity. The model demonstrates the practicality of translating guideline‑recommended exercise and education into team‑based care using digital and community‑integrated approaches. These findings support progression to a future definitive trial and a broader implementation evaluation focusing on sustainability and scale-up testing. By aligning implementation strategies with system priorities such as professional capacity building, telehealth infrastructure, and coordinated service delivery, PEAK‑CHN offers a promising approach to inform the development and adaptation of OA models of care in similar primary care contexts, particularly in settings with constrained allied health capacity.
