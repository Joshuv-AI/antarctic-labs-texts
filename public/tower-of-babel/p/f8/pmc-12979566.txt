Theoretical analysis of prestressed unequal-walled rectangular concrete-filled steel beams
Authors: Qi Su, Zeyu Zhang, Siping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
By adding prestressed reinforcements and designing the cross-section of steel pipe-concrete beams, a prestressed unequal-walled rectangular concrete-filled steel beam (PURCFSB) was proposed, which solves the problem of underutilizing the strength of the material due to the large deformation of the beams in large spans. In order to study the flexural performance of PURCFSB beams, a four-point bending test of 10 rectangular steel box-concrete beams was designed to analyze the effects of prestressing level and concrete filling rate on the flexural performance of the beams.The beams were categorized into high and low prestress groups. Each group covered five concrete filling ratios (a = 0, 1/3, 1/2, 2/3, 1). L = 3000 mm, b = 150 mm, h = 200 mm, t1 = 8 mm, t2 = 10 mm, tw = 5 mm, hp = 162 mm. Test results reveal that applying prestress can dramatically boost the cracking moment (by as much as 108.3%), while increasing the concrete filling rate improves the ultimate moment by up to 50.5%. Higher prestress levels consistently lead to greater cracking resistance. Building on these findings, the study develops theoretical formulas to predict both cracking and ultimate moments, along with exploring optimal cross-section designs. Concrete remains crack-free during service when the filling rate stays under 60%. Interestingly, the beam achieves peak load-bearing capacity at a 41% filling rate when partition plate thickness is disregarded. The research highlights how PURCFSB beams excel at controlling deformations and strengthening bending resistance in large-span structures.

Introduction

Concrete-filled steel tube (CFST) composite structures fully utilize the advantages of both steel and concrete structures1, while compensating for the brittle failure of concrete and local buckling of steel structures. With simplified construction and strong spanning capacity, they are widely used in bridge construction2,3. As bridge development in China extends into the mountainous regions of the southwest, construction environments with significant terrain fluctuations and wide river surfaces demand longer spans and increased system flexibility. Consequently, fully utilizing material strength, reducing structural deflection during normal service, and achieving lightweight structures have become critical engineering challenges4,5.

To address these issues, new composite cross-section forms of CFST structures continue to emerge, Mahdi Shariati et al. designed 11 double honeycomb steel composite encased concrete beams6, Hamed Ghohani Arab et al. designed 9 novel steel-concrete-steel sandwich composite beams with box-type shear connectors7, Shun-ichi Nakamura et al. proposed a new type of partially concrete-filled steel box girder8, and Yaping Lai et al. introduced a cable-stayed arch (CSDA) bridge, which combines cable-stayed and arch structures, providing a solution for long-span bridge applications in mountainous areas9. These studies experimentally and numerically validated the superiority of composite sections. Meanwhile, prestressing technology, as a new system that improves structural performance, saves materials, reduces costs, and enhances durability, has rapidly developed and been widely applied in the 21st century, for example, continuous beams10, deep beams11, and high-strength concrete composite beams12,13. At the same time, prestressing is widely used in the later reinforcement of beams14, and the materials for prestressing tendons have also evolved, progressing from steel bars to CFRP tendons15. Regarding unbonded prestressed concrete beams, Oukaili et al. experimentally investigated the serviceability and ductility of partially prestressed concrete beams under a limited number of repeated loads16. Through nonlinear analysis, they proposed two modeling methods, deepening the understanding of the mechanical behavior of unbonded prestressed flexural members. Additionally, they introduced a systematic monitoring approach for the flexural behavior of structural concrete members containing unbonded prestressing tendons, predicting key mechanical characteristics throughout the entire bending process, including cracking moment, ultimate moment, stiffness, and stress increment in prestressing tendons17,18. Furthermore, based on 218 experimental data points, they developed a prediction model for the ultimate stress of unbonded prestressing tendons using genetic expression programming (GEP). Their work highlighted the efficiency of GEP in structural optimization19. These studies provide valuable references for the analysis and design of unbonded prestressed systems. Li Siping et al. first proposed the concept of non-uniform wall thickness rectangular CFST composite structures and conducted a series of experimental and theoretical studies on long compression columns, short eccentric compression columns20, and bending members21,22. Results showed that non-uniform wall thickness rectangular CFST structures exhibit excellent ductility and post-yield capacity, with greater bending resistance compared to uniform wall thickness CFST. Zhong Xingu and Mo Shixu et al. introduced a new cross-section form—steel box-concrete composite beams23, and analyzed their bending capacity and stiffness through experiments and theory. Studies revealed that, at the same bending capacity, steel box-concrete beams reduce weight by 59.5% compared to conventional steel-concrete composite beams24. Their ultimate bending strength is 40% higher than that of steel box beams, with ductility improved by approximately 8 times23. However, these studies rarely analyzed the influence of concrete filling ratios, with only reference25 conducting numerical simulations. Zhou Chunli conducted bending tests on prestressed steel box-concrete composite beams, proposed a formula for calculating their ultimate bearing capacity, and explored the effects of steel plate thickness, concrete strength, and prestressing tendon quantity. The study concluded that prestressed steel box-concrete beams significantly enhance stiffness and ductility while effectively reducing deflection26. Xie Mengjie et al. performed bending tests and numerical simulations on four groups of prestressed corrugated steel-concrete composite beams and conventional composite beams. By analyzing parameters such as prestress, shear connectors, and concrete flange width, they plotted load-midspan deflection, load-strain, and moment-midspan deflection curves, deriving a bending capacity formula for practical engineering27. In these studies, calculations for partially concrete-filled steel box beams assumed the neutral axis at the upper and lower chamber diaphragms, neglecting cases where it deviates.

Given the gaps in existing research and the potential of prestressing technology, our research group proposed a prestressed rectangular steel box-concrete cross-section with non-uniform wall thickness.The research team previously conducted experimental and numerical simulation studies on this new type of prestressed beam28. The earlier experimental research designed and fabricated a series of rectangular steel box-concrete composite beam specimens. All specimens had a span of 3000 mm, with cross-sectional dimensions of 150 mm(width)×200 mm(height), and employed a non-uniform wall thickness design (8 mm thick top flange plate, 10 mm thick bottom flange plate, and 5 mm thick web plate). The specimens were divided into two groups based on prestress levels (high and low), with each group covering five concrete filling ratios (a = 0, 1/3, 1/2, 2/3, 1) to systematically analyze the mechanical performance under the coupled effects of concrete filling ratio and prestress. The prestressing tendons were eccentrically arranged along the height of the cross-section (with the centroid of the tendons 162 mm from the lower edge of the top flange plate), and external prestressing was adopted during construction to enhance operability. Through the aforementioned design and finite element simulation, the influence patterns of different concrete filling ratios and prestress levels on the yield moment and ultimate moment of composite beams were revealed. Based on experimental and numerical results, a discrete scatter analysis was conducted to investigate the mechanisms of concrete filling ratio and prestress level, preliminarily clarifying their trends.

Building on this research, this paper further explores the theoretical analysis methods for such beams, shifting the focus from the yield moment to the more design-oriented cracking moment, revealing the influence patterns of different concrete filling ratios and prestress levels on the cracking moment and ultimate moment of composite beams. Analytical expressions for the cracking moment and ultimate moment were derived, progressing from trend analysis to quantitative optimization, breaking through the limitations of earlier discrete analyses. Continuous parameter analysis was performed based on theoretical formulas, explicitly proposing the theoretically optimal solution for cross-section design, with the aim of providing referenceable optimization basis for engineering design. By comparing numerical simulations with theoretical formulas, the reliability and universality of the research conclusions were enhanced.

Experiment

Overview of the experiment

The study tested 10 beams, categorized into high and low prestress groups. Each group covered five concrete filling ratios (0, 1/3, 1/2, 2/3, and full filling). Specimens were labeled BR(1–5)-(A-E), where numbers denoted filling ratios and letters indicated prestress levels (ranging from 0 to 288 MPa). The test took place in the Structural Laboratory of Shanghai Jiao Tong University, with the setup illustrated in Fig. 2. A four-point bending setup was used for loading, creating a pure bending zone at the midspan where shear force is theoretically zero, thus effectively decoupling bending behavior from shear effects and focusing specifically on the flexural performance of the beam. A hydraulic jack applied displacement-controlled loads while tracking load, deflection, and strain responses. The measurement uncertainties in this study, primarily for load, deflection, and strain, stemmed from instrument precision, environmental interference, and operational errors. To quantify and control these uncertainties, the load was measured using a hydraulic jack with an accuracy of ± 1% of the full scale (F.S.), deflection was measured by linear variable differential transformers (LVDTs) with an accuracy of ± 0.01 mm, and strain was captured by strain gauges with an accuracy of ± 2 µε. This instrumentation met the precision requirements for engineering tests. To mitigate environmental interference, all tests were conducted on clear and dry days to avoid the adverse effects of humidity and low temperature on the stability of strain gauge readings.

The test ran for about 2.5 h, stopping once the deflection at the loading point reached 1/30th of the span length. All instruments were calibrated prior to testing, and loading was applied smoothly and continuously at a constant rate to minimize dynamic effects and manual intervention. The specific test procedure and observed phenomena are described in detail in Reference28.

Analysis of bending moment at mid-span cross-section

The mid-span moment-deflection curve of the test beam reflects the overall working performance of the flexural member, as shown in Fig. 3, which can be roughly divided into two stages: (1) Elastic stage (from initial loading to the yielding of the upper/lower flange steel plates of the beam): Before the yielding of the upper/lower flanges of the steel box, the test beam exhibits elastic deformation characteristics, with the moment-deflection curve approximating a straight line, the stiffness remaining essentially unchanged, and the tensile stress in the prestressed steel increasing. Test data revealed that fully filled beams experienced concrete cracking during the elastic stage, leading to a reduction in stiffness, after which the elastic stage continued; (2) Plastic phase. The moment-deflection curve develops a noticeable kink. Once a plastic hinge forms, the beam continues to bend further without any significant increase in the applied load, demonstrating the beam’s excellent ductility. In Fig. 3, both BR2-C and BR2-D exhibit a noticeable load drop segment during the later loading stage, which is attributed to local buckling. The hollow steel boxes BR1-B and BR1-C also experienced local buckling, but without a significant drop in load-bearing capacity, indicating that local buckling caused the partially filled concrete to lose the constraint of the steel tube. Local buckling has a greater impact on steel box-concrete beams than on steel box beams. Therefore, when designing steel box-concrete beams, attention should be paid to ensuring that the filling rate is not too low. When the concrete filling rate is low, stiffeners should be installed to avoid local buckling. By comparing specimens in Fig. 3 with the same filling rate but different prestress levels, and specimens with different filling rates but the same prestress level, it can be observed that the filling rate has a greater influence on the flexural performance of the beam than the prestress level. Additionally, the structural load-bearing capacity shows little difference between filling rates of 1/2 and 1/3, but the ductility and safety of the structure are better when the filling rate is 1/2.

Strain analysis at mid-span cross-section

Since the strain patterns at the mid-span sections of different specimens were similar, specimen BR3-D was taken as an example to illustrate the changes in strain at the upper and lower flanges of the mid-span section, as well as the strain distribution along the beam height during loading. As shown in Fig. 4, positive strain represents tensile strain, while negative strain represents compressive strain. After the application of prestress, the beam section was under compression. In the early stages of loading, the strains at the upper and lower flanges of all specimens increased linearly with the bending moment. As the bending moment increased, the upper and lower flanges of the steel box yielded successively, leading to a reduction in the growth rate of the specimen’s load-bearing capacity. The slope of the curve decreased until it approached zero when the bending moment neared the ultimate moment. The load-bearing capacity of all specimens remained essentially unchanged until the strain at the edges of the steel box reached 0.01.

The variation of strain along the beam height at the mid-span section of the specimen under different displacement loads is shown in Fig. 528. From Fig. 5, it can be observed that the strain distribution along the beam height remained approximately linear under different bending moments. This indicates that the prestressed steel box girder not only maintained a planar deformation in the elastic stage, conforming to the plane-section assumption, but also largely retained planar deformation after partial sections entered the plastic stage, still adhering to the plane-section assumption.

Analysis of stress increment in prestressed reinforcement

Figure 6 reveals that during the final loading phase, the Q235 prestressed steel bars reached a yield strength of approximately 288 MPa, while the 45# steel bars neared their tensile strength limit of 600 MPa. The stress in the Q235 bars increased linearly up to the yield point, and the 45# bars showed similar linear behavior until the steel box started yielding. Post-yielding, the stress in the bars spiked rapidly, while the steel box’s load-bearing capacity grew more slowly.

For specimens with the same filling ratio but different prestress levels, the results showed that higher prestress levels led to slower stress growth in the prestressed reinforcement. This is because greater prestress can partially offset the stress increase caused by the load, thereby delaying the stress growth in the prestressed reinforcement. This effect helps to improve the load-bearing capacity and ductility of the structure, while also helping to reduce deformation under normal working loads.

When comparing specimens with different filling rates but the same prestressing force, it can be observed that the higher the filling rate, the slower the rate of stress increase in the prestressed steel bars. This is because steel boxes with higher filling rates have greater stiffness. In practical applications, higher-strength prestressed steel bars should be selected, and a safe prestressing force should be chosen to avoid situations where the prestressed steel bars yield before the steel box reaches its ultimate load-bearing capacity.

Theoretical analysis

The analysis of the behaviour of PURCFSB beams under bending assumes the cross-sections remain planar throughout deformation and ignores shear effects29, which is justified by a Timoshenko beam theory based quantitative assessment indicating that shear contributes less than 2% to the total deformation under the present experimental configuration. This section explains how to calculate the cracking load and maximum load capacity of such beams. We also explore how prestressing force and concrete filling ratio affect structural strength using these calculations.

Ultimate moment

The ultimate moment is determined as follows: if the strain at the tensile edge of the beam hits 0.01 without any load drop, that moment is taken as the limit. If the load drops before reaching 0.01 strain, the highest moment recorded up to that point is used instead. The analysis assumes that a plastic hinge forms in the cross-section at peak flexural strength. Tests show that the prestressed Q235 steel yields, while the 45-grade rebars achieve their full tensile strength. Figure 8 illustrates the simplified calculation model, which ignores concrete contribution in the tension zone, residual stresses, and local buckling effects in the steel box section.

Numerical simulation verification and parameter analysis

To verify the reliability of the theoretical model, this study established a refined finite element model for parametric analysis. The validity of this finite element model has been confirmed in Reference28. It should be specifically noted that in the complete theoretical model used to validate the core experimental results (particularly the peak bearing capacity), the mechanical contribution of the intermediate diaphragm has been fully incorporated. However, in subsequent parametric studies (including numerical simulations), the influence of the intermediate diaphragm was intentionally excluded, primarily based on the following two considerations: First, to focus on the core optimization variable—the concrete filling ratio—ensuring that the parametric analysis results clearly reflect the independent impact of filling ratio variations on structural performance; Second, to align with the conventional conservative approach in engineering design, where the bearing contribution of intermediate diaphragms is typically not accounted for in flexural capacity design. Through systematic comparison between theoretical solutions and numerical results, the influence patterns of key parameters were further revealed.

Overview of finite element modeling process

The model was developed using the general finite element software ABAQUS. The beam body was simulated using 3D solid elements (C3D8R) for concrete and steel boxes, while prestressed tendons were modeled with beam elements (B31). Interface settings included normal hard contact and tangential friction (friction coefficient set to 0.4) to simulate actual bond-slip behavior. The boundary conditions matched the four-point bending test: simply supported with symmetric loading applied to the pure bending segment. The material constitutive models were the same as in reference28: a quadratic plastic flow model for the steel box, an ideal elastic-plastic bilinear model for prestressed steel bars, and a plastic damage model for concrete. The stress-strain relationships and diagrams for these models are detailed in reference28. Prestress was applied using the cooling method. The steel box has a yield strength of 235 MPa, the prestressed steel reinforcement has a yield strength of 450 MPa, and the standard cubic compressive strength of concrete is 60 MPa.

To study the influence of concrete filling ratio and prestress level on flexural capacity, two groups of parametric models were designed:

Fixed prestress level (σpc = 0.5fpy2), varying concrete filling ratios (a = 0, 1/6, 1/3, 1/2, 2/3, 5/6, 1).

Fixed concrete filling ratio (a = 1), varying prestress levels (σpc/fpy2=0, 1/6, 1/3, 1/2, 2/3, 5/6, 1).

The model structure is shown in Fig. 9. Since the intermediate diaphragm’s primary function is to separate the upper and lower box chambers without bearing structural loads, its thickness was set to nearly 0 mm in the simulation to eliminate its influence on the structure. Other outer dimensions matched those of the test beam.

In the ultimate moment calculation Eq. (9), terms related to prestress are constants, and their derivatives with respect to prestress are zero. In such cases, the ultimate moment calculation should account for the assumption of prestress increment.

Conclusion

This study examines the flexural performance of PURCFSB beams through both theoretical and experimental approaches, providing calculation formulas for the cracking moment and ultimate moment. The theoretical model developed herein is primarily suitable for beams with a span-to-depth ratio greater than 10, where shear deformation effects are minimal. The theoretical formula derived in this study is applicable to PURCFSB beams with h/t ≤ 80. If h/t exceeds this limit, measures such as increasing the plate thickness or adding stiffeners should be implemented to prevent local buckling.For scenarios involving deep beams or regions with significant shear forces, it is recommended to adopt refined models that explicitly account for shear deformation effects. The main conclusions are as follows:

The test results indicate that when the filling ratio is less than 2/3, the concrete remains uncracked within the normal serviceability deflection range. According to the cracking moment calculation formula, PURCFSB beams with a filling ratio below 60% will not crack during the service stage; when the filling ratio exceeds 60%, the cracking moment decreases as the filling ratio increases. It should be noted that the critical value of 60% is derived from experimental observations based on specific parameters of the test specimens in this study (e.g., material strength, cross-sectional dimensions). For structures where concrete cracking is not allowed during the service stage, in practical engineering, the theoretical formula derived in this study should be applied, combined with specific design parameters, to determine the safe upper limit of the filling ratio suitable for the project.The test results show that the ultimate bending moment reaches its maximum value at a filling ratio of 2/3. Beyond this ratio, as the concrete cracks under ultimate load, increasing the filling ratio cannot enhance the sectional strength, and the additional self-weight may even lead to a reduction in strength. The maximum improvement in ultimate bending moment due to the filling ratio is 50.5%. Theoretical analysis (ignoring the thickness of the diaphragm) reveals that the optimal filling ratio corresponding to the maximum ultimate bending moment is 41%. This value reflects the intrinsic relationship of the filling ratio variable in the theoretical model, while the experimental peak at 2/3 is attributed to the contribution of diaphragms to flexural capacity in actual construction and the combined results of discrete tests.
