Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPathologic extrusion of the meniscus, defined as displacement of more than 3 mm beyond the tibial plateau border, can disrupt tibiofemoral contact mechanics by reducing hoop strain resistance and increasing articular cartilage contact pressure. Meniscal extrusion following meniscal allograft transplantation is well documented, with studies reporting extrusion rates of over 40%. This Technical Note aims to describe the centralization of a meniscus allograft during transplantation.

Pathologic extrusion of the meniscus, defined as displacement of more than 3 mm beyond the tibial plateau border, can disrupt tibiofemoral contact mechanics by reducing hoop strain resistance and increasing articular cartilage contact pressure.1,2 Despite a well-executed root repair, meniscus extrusion may still persist.3, 4, 5 Meniscal extrusion following meniscal allograft transplantation (MAT) is well documented, with 1 systematic review reporting an extrusion rate of 41.1%.6 Some studies have even observed pathological extrusion rates as high as 75% after MAT.7 The underlying causes of extrusion remain debated, but significant attention has been directed toward preventing postoperative meniscal extrusion.8,9 To address this issue, arthroscopic techniques have been developed to centralize the meniscus on the tibial plateau, showing promising early outcomes.10,11 This Technical Note aims to describe the centralization of a meniscus allograft during transplantation.

Surgical Technique

MAT is indicated for younger patients with a history of partial or total meniscectomy, joint line pain localized to the meniscectomized compartment, and no significant osteoarthritis. Contraindications include inflammatory arthritis, a body mass index over 30, previous or active joint infections, synovial disease, and diffuse arthritic changes (Kellgren-Lawrence grade 3 or higher). MAT typically relies on preoperative radiographs to determine the appropriate size of the meniscal allograft. Accurate graft sizing is incredibly important to avoid contact pressures in unwanted areas.12 Size matching can be performed using anteroposterior and lateral radiographs, with distinct techniques for the medial and lateral menisci.

The fixation of meniscal horn attachments is generally categorized into 3 techniques: soft tissue fixation, bone plugs, and bone bridge. Our preferred technique for MAT is the bone-plug technique, given familiarity with meniscus root repair, bony healing, and improved biomechanics, as well as the ability to adjust anterior root position in situations of graft-patient size mismatch. The meniscus allograft plugs are prepared with 8 mm in diameter and 5 mm in height bone plugs for our meniscus transplant technique to allow ease of passage in the compartment (Fig 1). For medial MAT, the patient is positioned supine with the contralateral lower extremity in a stirrup leg holder to allow the end of the bed to be “flexed” and an assistant access to the posteromedial compartment to retrieve inside-out sutures.

Left knee medial meniscus allograft prepared with 2 cylindrical bone plugs (8 × 5 mm) secured with sutures through the bone block and meniscus root attachment. (A, anterior horn [left]; P, posterior horn [right].)

After performing standard diagnostic arthroscopy and confirming medial meniscus deficiency (Fig 2), the medial compartment is accessed with the knee under valgus load. Additional techniques such as notchplasty, reverse notchplasty, tibial spine debridement, or pie-crusting of the medial collateral ligament may be employed to achieve optimal visualization of the posterior horn and avoid articular cartilage injury.13 The remnants of the meniscus are then debrided with biter and shaver, leaving a 1- to 2-mm meniscal rim to maintain graft stability and prevent extrusion during suturing.

Left knee medial compartment viewed through an anterolateral portal showing medial meniscus deficiency after debridement of remnant tissue.

Next, a spinal needle is used to localize a proximal anteromedial portal for placement of a subchondral bone anchor at the posterior horn–body junction. This is then exchanged for a curved drill guide for a knotless all-suture anchor (1.8-mm Knotless FiberTak Anchor; Arthrex), the anchor is inserted, and suture tails are left docked in the percutaneous portal (Fig 3).

Left knee medial compartment, anterolateral portal view. After medial collateral ligament pie-crusting, absence of the medial meniscus is confirmed. A curved drill guide (red arrow) is introduced through a percutaneous, proximal anteromedial portal just adjacent to the medial patella, targeting the posterior third of the tibial plateau for centralization anchor placement.

A transtibial drill guide (Fig 4) is then placed at the anatomic posterior root position, and a flip-cutting drill is used to create a socket 8.5 mm in diameter and 10 mm long (Fig 5). Shuttle sutures are passed, and the prepared meniscus allograft is shuttled into the compartment via the medial portal via posterior bone plug sutures (Fig 6). Great care is taken to ensure adequate orientation and rotation of the graft. A Kelly clamp is used to clamp the sutures to the tibia to maintain distal tension. We then proceed with posterior meniscocapsular fixation using 3 all-inside meniscus repair implants (FiberStitch 1.5; Arthrex) in vertical mattress fashion, usually 2 on the superior surface and 1 on the inferior surface (Fig 7).

Left knee medial compartment, anterolateral portal view. The transtibial meniscus root drill guide is placed at the anatomic posterior root attachment (red arrow), used to place a transtibial guide pin.

Left knee medial compartment, anterolateral portal view. FlipCutter (Arthrex; red arrow) positioned at the posterior root footprint of the medial meniscus, set at 8.5 mm targeting the posterior root attachment.

Left knee medial compartment, anteromedial portal view. Meniscal allograft and posterior bone block (red arrow) introduced and docked into the joint.

Left knee medial compartment, anteromedial portal view. Posterior horn fixed with all-inside knotless implants. Centralization suture (red arrow) is passed around the peripheral meniscus body and shuttled through the tibial anchor.

Next, the viewing portal is switched to a standard anteromedial portal, and a flexible cannula (Arthrex PassPort 8 mm) is placed in the lateral cannula. The fixed repair suture from the anchor is retrieved from the lateral cannula and shuttled through the meniscus in an inferior-to-superior orientation using a suture passing device (Meniscus Scorpion; Arthrex) (Fig 8). The shuttle suture from the anchor is then retrieved and used to shuttle the repair stitch through the anchor. Finally, the centralization knotless construct is tensioned to create a vertical mattress repair to bone that will help buttress against meniscal extrusion (Fig 9).

Left knee medial compartment, anteromedial portal view. A PassPort (Arthrex) cannula is placed via the lateral viewing portal. Repair suture is shuttled using a looped passing suture (red arrow) with a Meniscus Scorpion (Arthrex) and passed in an over-under vertical mattress configuration.

Left knee medial compartment, anteromedial portal view. Final tensioning of the centralization/buttress knotless anchor suture when pulling on the tension suture (red arrow), securing the medial meniscus to the tibial plateau.

From this point, attention is turned toward circumferential stabilization of the allograft using a traditional inside-out approach with a posteromedial counterincision. Eight to 10 pairs of sutures on long needles are placed in a vertical mattress fashion above and below the meniscus, retrieved, and tied (Fig 10). Finally, the arthrotomy is enlarged, and the optimal anterior root position is identified by placing gentle tension on the anterior bone block. The anterior horn, which allows for greater flexibility in positioning, should be secured last.14 A transtibial drill pin is fired at the site of the anterior root position, an 8.5-mm × 10-mm socket is created, and anterior bone block sutures are shuttled down the tibia.

Left knee medial compartment, anteromedial portal view. Completion of meniscus repair using the inside-out technique with mini-suture tape placed in a vertical mattress fashion from an anterolateral portal.

Finally, the knee is cycled with tension held on the sutures, and the sutures from the anterior and posterior root attachments are tied over a bone bridge on the anterior tibia. The sutures may optionally be loaded into a knotless anchor (4.75 mm SwiveLock; Arthrex) for fixation. Video 1 displays our surgical technique in detail.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

For the first 6 weeks, the patient should be limited to toe-touch weightbearing only, with the knee locked in extension using a brace and crutches. Range of motion is restricted to 90° of flexion, which can be increased as tolerated after 4 weeks. The knee brace can be removed at week 6, after which lower extremity strengthening and nonimpact aerobic activities can begin. Jogging and impact activities should be delayed until 4 to 6 months postoperatively, with return to sport typically occurring 6 to 9 months postoperatively.

Discussion

Meniscal extrusion following MAT has been well documented, with an extrusion rate reported of 41.1%.6 Potential contributing factors include improper graft size matching, incorrect positioning, loss of fixation, suture-only fixation compared to osseous fixation, and improper ligament repair.13,16,17

Although extrusion has traditionally been thought to result from posterior meniscal root tears, this idea has been questioned recently. First, meniscal root repair alone has been shown to be ineffective in eliminating extrusion.18,19 Additionally, Krych et al.20 showed that meniscotibial ligament disruption and meniscal extrusion were present in 27 patients prior to medial meniscus posterior root tears, suggesting that extrusion may contribute to increased hoop stress and subsequent posterior root tears.

Regardless of the cause, meniscal extrusion is well documented to negatively impact the weightbearing function of the knee, contributing to the development of osteoarthritis, osteophytosis, chondral lesions, meniscal tearing, and cartilage loss.21, 22, 23, 24 Additionally, Foreman et al.25 showed that meniscal extrusion accelerates knee degeneration.

The purpose of meniscus centralization is to reduce the risk of postoperative extrusion. Our technique uses the centralization technique described by Krych et al.15 in the context of meniscal allografts. This technique focuses on repairing the meniscotibial ligaments alone, without suturing the meniscus or meniscocapsular junction, thus preserving the natural motion and biomechanical relationship between the meniscus, meniscotibial ligament, and tibia. Video 1

This video shows our technique for centralization of a meniscus allograft during transplantation. After preparing an 8-mm × 5-mm bone plug from the graft, standard diagnostic arthroscopy confirms medial meniscus deficiency, and remnant tissue is debrided. A subchondral bone anchor is placed at the posterior horn–body junction via a proximal anteromedial portal using a knotless all-suture anchor. A transtibial socket (8.5 × 10 mm) is created at the posterior root using a flip-cutting drill, and the graft is shuttled into position. Posterior meniscocapsular fixation is performed with 3 all-inside vertical mattress sutures. Centralization is achieved by passing the anchor’s repair suture through the meniscus, shuttling it back through the anchor, and tensioning the construct. Circumferential stabilization follows via inside-out sutures (8-10 vertical mattress pairs) with a posteromedial counterincision. The anterior horn is addressed last, using gentle tension to identify optimal root positioning, with an 8.5-mm × 10-mm transtibial socket drilled and bone block sutures secured over an anterior tibial bone bridge or with a knotless anchor.
