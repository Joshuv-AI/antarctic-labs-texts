Design and application of a novel surgical transfer sheet: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Liping Wu, Ying Lu, Rujie Gong, Bin Yan, Xiaoning Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the safety of an innovative surgical transfer sheet, already designed to integrate “safety, comfort, convenience, and cost-effectiveness,” and to quantify its impact on staff workload. The assessment was performed via a randomized controlled trial to generate evidence for improved postoperative transfer practices.MethodsAn experimental design was applied for this study. This study included 124 postoperative surgical patients transfer events from a Shanghai tertiary hospital between November 2023 and February 2024. Participants included both the patients and the clinical staff involved in the transfer evaluations. The patients were randomly allocated via random number table to experimental (n = 62) and control (n = 62) groups. The experimental group utilized a polyurethane foam-nylon fiber composite transfer sheet, while the control group employed standard linens. The clinical staff participating in the post-transfer evaluation comprised one anesthesiologist, two ward nurses, and one operating room support staff per procedure. Outcome measures included transfer time, pre-post transfer vital sign variability (respiratory rate, heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation), pain scores, adverse event rates, as well as staff musculoskeletal strain and satisfaction levels.ResultsCompared to controls, the experimental group demonstrated significantly shorter transfer times (P < 0.01), superior hemodynamic stability with lower heart rates (P < 0.05) and reduced systolic blood pressure variability (P < 0.05). No significant between-group differences were observed in tube dislodgement rates or critical vital sign fluctuations (P > 0.05). Clinical staff reported significantly decreased physical exertion (P < 0.01) and higher satisfaction scores (P < 0.05) when using the novel device.ConclusionThe novel transfer sheet effectively enhances transfer efficiency, maintains hemodynamic stability, reduces staff physical strain, and improves clinical workflow satisfaction. This innovation provides an evidence-based solution for safe postoperative patient transfer.Clinical trial registrationThe full date of first registration: June 10, 2025, Registration Number: ChiCTR2500104043.Trial registrationThis study was retrospectively registered as a randomized controlled trial in the Chinese Clinical Trial Center. Introduction

Patient transfer is a critical component in ensuring continuity of care and facilitating a seamless transition to subsequent diagnostic and treatment procedures [1, 2]. A safe transfer practice involves multiple essential elements, including: decision-making based on informed consent, effective communication among stakeholders, stabilization and adequate preparation of the patient before transfer, selection of appropriate transfer methods with corresponding personnel and equipment, continuous monitoring during transfer, comprehensive documentation throughout the process, and smooth handover to the receiving unit [3]. Within this framework, this study focuses on the physical transfer process of post-general anesthesia patients from the operating room to the ward, rather than the informational handover component. The postoperative transfer of surgical patients under general anesthesia represents a pivotal component of operating room workflow, directly impacting the assurance of medical safety and quality.

Surgical interventions induce tissue trauma that triggers systemic stress responses through immune, metabolic, and neuroendocrine mechanisms [4, 5]. Post-anesthetic patients face compounded risks during transfer due to residual anesthetic agents, impaired thermoregulation, and unrecovered protective reflexes, which can lead to hemodynamic instability and respiratory compromise [6–8]. The presence of multiple indwelling catheters further escalates transfer-related complications, with studies reporting 11%-34% incidence of adverse events attributable to suboptimal transfer equipment design [9–11]. These findings underscore the pivotal role of scientifically engineered transfer devices in perioperative safety protocols.

Occupational safety for healthcare providers during patient transfer also demand equal attention. Anesthetized patients’ complete dependency on manual transfer exposes clinical staff to repetitive heavy lifting, a major contributor to work-related musculoskeletal disorders [12–15]. Global epidemiological data show substantial variations in work-related musculoskeletal disorders prevalence: 23% in U.S. nurses, 31.6% in Pakistani nurses, 61.9% in African nurses, 76% in Japanese nurses, and 96% in Chinese nurses [16–20]. In China’s resource-constrained clinical settings, manual transfer using conventional sheets remains predominant. This practice frequently results in biomechanical stress from improper force distribution during obese patient handling, manifesting as lumbar strain and wrist injuries. Furthermore, patient repositioning during sheet-based transfers may induce postoperative discomfort and iatrogenic complications such as catheter dislodgement [21].

Emerging assistive technologies offer partial solutions: sliding stretchers minimize tube displacement but require substantial physical exertion [22, 23]; mechanical aids reduce lifting through friction-reduced translation, yet necessitate hazardous patient rolling [24–26]. While ceiling/floor lifts optimize ergonomic principles, their spatial requirements and operational complexity limit feasibility in typical Chinese clinical environments [27–29].

Existing domestic transfer devices show limitations in both stability and effort-saving functionality, highlighting the need for safer, more comfortable, and ergonomically optimized solutions. Therefore, guided by the design principles of “safety, comfort, convenience, and effort-saving efficiency,” this study developed a novel surgical transfer sheet. Based on ergonomic and clinical safety frameworks, the following hypotheses are proposed: (1) For patients, the novel surgical transfer sheet will improve transfer efficiency and reduce adverse events compared to conventional methods; (2) For clinical staff, the novel surgical transfer sheet will reduce physical exertion and increase satisfaction during patient transfer procedures. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of this device in enhancing postoperative transfer safety and ergonomics, thereby providing an evidence-based solution to optimize clinical practice.

Methods

Study design

Randomized controlled trials were used.

Research scope

A prospective randomized controlled trial was conducted at a tertiary care hospital (Grade III Level A) in Shanghai.

Inclusion criteria

①Adults ≥ 18 years undergoing surgery under general anesthesia, with a post-anesthetic recovery level meeting a modified Aldrete score of ≥ 9, indicating eligibility for transfer from the post-anesthesia care unit to the inpatient ward; ②Postoperative indwelling devices (gastric tube, urinary catheter, central venous access, or drains) required, except endotracheal tubes or laryngeal masks; ③ Written informed consent provided.

Staff inclusion criteria: Ward nurses, anesthesiologists and the operating room support staff members directly participating in patient care who agreed to participate and complete questionnaires after each transfer.

Exclusion criteria

①Procedures conducted under regional/local anesthesia;②Patients undergoing bariatric or cardiothoracic surgeries;③Cognitive impairment affecting consent capacity.

Sample size calculation

The sample size calculation was based on the number of patient transfers required to detect a clinically meaningful difference in the primary outcome—ward nurses’ perceived exertion scores (Borg RPE scale) during transfer procedures.

with α = 0.05 (two-sided) and β = 0.10 (power = 90%), a pre-experimental pilot study was conducted. The pilot data showed a mean difference (δ) of 4.14 points on the Borg RPE scale and a pooled standard deviation (σ) of 4.24 between the two groups. This yielded a minimum sample size of 44 postoperative surgical patients transfer events per group. To account for potential attrition at a rate of 20%, the sample size was increased to at least 53 postoperative surgical patients transfer events per group. Therefore, a total sample size of 106 postoperative surgical patients transfer events was required. Ultimately, we enrolled 124 postoperative surgical patients transfer events to ensure adequate power.

A total of 4 clinical staff members were involved in the evaluation of each postoperative surgical patient transfer event: one anesthesiologist, two ward nurses, and one operating room support staff. All patient transfers were conducted by clinical staff drawn from a pre-defined, pre-trained cohort of 40 healthcare professionals (24 ward nurses, 12 operating room support staff, and 4 anesthesiologists). While the specific combination of staff for each transfer varied based on clinical scheduling, all evaluators were thus familiarized with the study protocols and assessment criteria prior to participation. It is important to note that while the composition of the transfer team remained consistent for each event, the specific individuals participating rotated from this fixed cohort across different transfers. Following each transfer, all four staff members independently completed the Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) and satisfaction questionnaires. Therefore, for the 62 transfer events in each group, a total of 248 staff evaluations (62 events × 4 staff members) were collected for each outcome measure (RPE and satisfaction). However, it should be acknowledged that inter-individual variability in physical strength among staff members, which may influence perceived exertion scores, was not controlled for as a confounding variable in the study design or statistical analysis. This represents a potential limitation of this study.

Randomization, assignment and blinding

Random numbers were generated using Excel and sequentially sealed in opaque envelopes to ensure allocation concealment. These envelopes were stored and managed by the principal investigator. When an eligible patient was enrolled, a sealed envelope was retrieved in sequential order and delivered to the research staff responsible for group assignment. Patients were allocated to the intervention group if the random number was odd, and to the control group if the number was even, with an allocation ratio of 1:1 between groups. Due to the tangible differences between the novel transfer sheet and standard linens, blinding of clinicians and research staff involved in administering the transfer was not feasible. However, patients were blinded to group assignment throughout the study period to minimize performance bias. Consecutive sampling was used to enroll 124 patients undergoing elective surgery under general anesthesia between November 2023 and February 2024. This trial adheres to the CONSORT 2025 guidelines for reporting randomized trials [30]. A completed CONSORT flow diagram is provided in Fig. 1. Participants were sequentially numbered based on surgical scheduling and randomly allocated via computer-generated random number tables into experimental (n = 62) and control (n = 62) groups. The experimental group utilized a polyurethane foam-nylon fiber composite transfer sheet, while the control group utilized standard surgical linens. The study received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Approval Number: 2022258) in compliance with Helsinki Declaration principles. All patients voluntarily participated in this study and signed an informed consent form.

Intervention

The experimental group utilized a novel surgical patient transfer sheet, measuring 190 cm × 55 cm (Figs. 2 and 3). It was developed by a team at Ruijin Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, with the inventors being Liping Wu, Rujie Gong, Wei Wang, and Xing Yang. The device has been officially registered as a utility model patent by the National Intellectual Property Administration of China (Patent No: ZL20232 3 246365.6), under the title “A Transfer Sheet for Surgical Patients”. The patent application was filed on November 30, 2023, and the patent right was officially granted on August 13, 2024 (Authorization Announcement No.: CN 221512583 U). This device comprises two integrated components: (1) a polyurethane (PU) soft foam transfer pad providing stable support and cushioning to enhance patient comfort, and (2) a low-friction nylon-glass fiber sliding cloth designed to eliminate the need for patient repositioning during transfers. The sliding cloth is centrally fixed to one side of the transfer pad, with free ends traversing the upper and lower surfaces of the pad. A zipper mechanism detachably connects the sliding cloth to the pad, incorporating interlocking chain racks (first rack on the pad, second rack on the cloth) engaged via pull heads for rapid disassembly and cleaning. Ergonomic handles are strategically positioned to optimize operational efficiency: the primary handles (two longitudinal handles) are mounted on transverse reinforcement straps at both ends of the pad and the secondary handles (four lateral handles) are affixed to orthogonal longitudinal reinforcement straps. This cross-bracing reinforcement system ensures structural stability, supporting a maximum payload of 120 kg. The sliding cloth features quick-release buckles at its free ends, which secure to the secondary handles after encircling the pad, maintaining cloth tautness during transfer procedures. After each use, the transfer pad was wiped with 0.25% povidone-iodine solution. The detachable sliding cloth was machine-washed after every use. Additionally, the entire transfer sheet was sent monthly to a professional sterilization service for comprehensive cleaning and disinfection.

Terminology

To ensure clarity and consistency, the following terms are defined for this study:

Operating Room Transfer Bed: The wheeled stretcher used to transfer the patient from the operating room to the ward.

Ward Bed: The fixed, adjustable bed in the patient’s room that receives the patient upon arrival.

Novel Surgical Transfer Sheet: The experimental device being evaluated.

Standard Linens: The conventional sheets used in the control group.

In the experimental group, the novel surgical transfer sheet was pre-positioned on the operating room transfer bed prior to surgery completion. Before transfer, support staff aligned and secured the operating room transfer bed parallel to the ward bed. The anesthesiologist and the ward nurse 1 were positioned at the cephalic and caudal ends of the patient, respectively, grasping the primary handles at the upper and lower ends of the novel surgical transfer sheet. Concurrently, operating room support staff and the ward nurse 2 stationed themselves bilaterally, each securing secondary lateral handles on both sides of the novel surgical transfer sheet. Upon audible commands from the ward nurse 2, coordinated team effort facilitated seamless patient transfer to the ward bed. Post-transfer, operating room staff activated the lateral handles to extract the transfer sheet via sliding interaction between the low-friction nylon-glass fiber interface and polyurethane foam pad.

The control group employed standard linens for transfer. The anesthesiologist and the ward nurse 1 maintained cephalic/caudal positions while operating room staff and the ward nurse 2 flanked the patient bilaterally, collectively gripping standard linens. Following the ward nurse 2’s commands, synchronized lifting transferred the patient. Post-transfer completion, sequential bilateral elevation maneuvers by staff were required to remove linens from beneath the patient.

Outcomes

Transfer time

This study defines core transfer time as the total duration commencing when the Operating room support staff arrives at the patient’s bedside and the bed panel of the operating room transfer bed is flipped down (timing initiated instantaneously at this point), until clinical staff complete the patient’s transfer to the ward bed, withdraw the transfer device, and ensure proper patient positioning.

Pre- and post-transfer vital signs and oxygen saturation

For post-general anesthesia patients, respiratory rate, heart rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation were recorded using a multiparameter monitor. Measurements were obtained at two time points: 1) pre-transfer: after patient arrival in the ward and prior to transfer initiation,2) post-transfer: immediately following complete patient repositioning on the hospital bed.

Pain assessment

Pain intensity was evaluated using the Numerical Rating Scale [31]. The scale quantified the degree of pain on a scale of 0 to 10, with 0 being no pain and 10 being severe pain. Assessments were conducted at two time points: 1) pre-transfer: prior to transfer initiation,2) post-transfer: completed patient transferring and repositioning.

Incidence of adverse events

Adverse events were categorized into three main types: (1) Hemodynamic instability: defined as a sudden decrease or increase in systolic blood pressure (a change of > 20 mmHg from baseline), a significant increase or decrease in heart rate (a change of > 20 bpm), or a reduction in oxygen saturation (a decrease of > 5% in SpO₂) [32]; (2) Catheter dislodgement: this referred to the unplanned displacement of nasogastric tubes, urinary catheters, central venous lines, or surgical drains; (3) Infusion line dislodgement: this involved the accidental removal of a peripheral intravenous access, requiring re-establishment. These events were assessed after the patient was transferred to the ward bed.

Rating of perceived exertion (RPE) for clinical staff fatigue assessment

Subjective fatigue during patient transfer was evaluated using the Borg 6–20 Rating of Perceived Exertion Scale [33], originally developed by Swedish researcher Gunnar Borg. This validated instrument quantifies physical exertion intensity through integrated psychophysiological perception during task execution, demonstrating significant reliability in healthcare settings in China [34–36]. The RPE scale employs a 6–20 scoring system where higher values indicate greater perceived exertion intensity and fatigue severity: 6–8 points indicate extremely easy, 9–10 points indicate very easy, 11–12 points indicate slightly strenuous, 13–14 points indicate somewhat tired, 15–16 points indicate somewhat tired, 17–18 points indicate very tired, and 19–20 points indicate extremely tired. Immediately following each patient transfer event, independent RPE assessments were completed by: anesthesiologist, two ward nurses and operating room support staff. The final RPE score for ward nurses was calculated as the arithmetic mean of two independent ratings to minimize individual bias.

Clinical staff satisfaction assessment

A researcher-developed 5-point Likert scale questionnaire was implemented to comprehensively evaluate four critical dimensions of the patient transfer experience. These dimensions included safety, defined as the prevention of patient injury during transfer procedures; comfort, assessed through the ergonomic design of transportation equipment; operational efficiency, characterized by the time and effort requirements; and energy conservation, measured by the reduction in physical exertion. The questionnaire provided an overall satisfaction score ranging from 1 (“very dissatisfied”) to 5 (“very satisfied”), with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction with the transfer devices. Immediately following each patient transfer event, independent evaluations were completed by all four participating clinical staff members: one anesthesiologist, one operating room support staff, and two ward nurses. To ensure objectivity, the overall satisfaction score for the ward nurses was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the ratings provided by the two nurses participating in that specific transfer event, thereby providing a more objective representation of the nursing team’s satisfaction level for that particular transfer procedure. The full protocol and the English version of the satisfaction questionnaire are provided in the Supplementary Material (File S1).

Data collection and quality control

The principal investigator conducted standardized training sessions for all participating staff on the implementation of the novel surgical patient transfer sheet, accompanied by detailed operational guidelines to ensure procedural consistency. To maintain methodological rigor, all data collection procedures were exclusively performed by the principal investigator through direct observation and instrument verification, thereby ensuring data accuracy and protocol adherence.

Data analysis

Initial data processing involved dual-entry verification through Microsoft Excel 2016 to establish a validated database, minimizing transcription errors. Subsequent analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency counts with percentages, and between-group comparisons were conducted using chi-square test. For continuous variables demonstrating normal distribution, data were presented as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed via independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were reported as median with interquartile range (25th-75th percentiles), with group differences assessed using Mann-Whitney U tests. A two-tailed significance threshold of α = 0.05 was applied throughout the study, with P-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The new surgical patient transfer sheet significantly shortens the transfer time and enhances the stability of the patients’ vital signs, demonstrating both statistical and clinical relevance

Transfer time represents a critical “dangerous edge period” during the postoperative transfer of surgical patients, with a well-established positive correlation between prolonged transfer duration and the incidence of adverse events [9, 37–39]. In this study, the use of the novel transfer sheets notably reduced transfer time and improved the work efficiency of clinical staff compared with conventional methods. These findings are consistent with earlier domestic studies on advanced transfer aids such as air suspension mattresses and folding transfer stents [40, 41]. This improvement can be attributed to two key innovations: (1) the integration of primary and secondary handles at both ends offers stable grip points, improving team coordination and minimizing transfer time; and (2) functionally, the device eliminates the need for repeated turning and repositioning of the patient required with traditional sheets. Instead, it enables horizontal sliding for seamless removal, substantially saving time and reducing procedural complexity. Patient transfer constitutes a high-risk procedure, especially under general anesthesia, where residual anesthetic agents may compromise hemodynamic, circulatory, and respiratory stability—potentially leading to severe outcomes including ventricular fibrillation or transient cardiopulmonary arrest [9, 42]. No significant intergroup differences were observed in preoperative vital signs or pain scores, ensuring baseline comparability. After transfer, the experimental group exhibited significantly smaller fluctuations in heart rate and systolic blood pressure, underscoring the clinical advantage of the new sheet in promoting hemodynamic stability. The polyurethane soft foam pad provides cushioning against transfer-induced jolts and vibrations, markedly improving patient comfort. Reinforced longitudinal and transverse straps enhance structural support and transfer smoothness, contributing to safer transitions. The sliding mechanism, facilitated by low-friction interaction between the sliding cloth and transfer pad, allows effortless sheet extraction without patient repositioning, thereby minimizing physiological disturbances and supporting hemodynamic consistency.

The new transfer sheet also reduces exertion-related fatigue and increases satisfaction among clinical staff, with important implications for workplace safety and operational sustainability

High-intensity transfer tasks trigger pronounced neuromuscular and hemodynamic stress, reduce local oxygen saturation, elevate perceived fatigue, and may compromise long-term musculoskeletal health [43, 44]. With growing surgical volumes, clinical staff are required to perform more frequent patient transfers, often involving repetitive bending and lifting. These activities contribute to muscle overuse, fatigue accumulation, and elevated risk of work-related injuries [45, 46]. In this study, ratings of perceived exertion among anesthesiologists, ward nurses, and support staff were significantly lower when using the new transfer sheet, affirming its role in mitigating occupational strain. This reduction is largely due to the low-friction nylon–glass fiber composite material used in the sliding layer, which minimizes resistance during transfer and reduces the force required from operators. Compared to conventional sheets, which often require lateral turning of the patient for sheet removal, the new design simplifies the process, reduces bending frequency, and decreases physical burden. Although no statistically significant difference in adverse event rates was observed—possibly due to sample size limitations—a downward trend was noted in the intervention group. Staff reported higher satisfaction regarding safety, comfort, ease of use, and efficiency, reflecting the device’s alignment with practical clinical needs. The dual-fastener and secondary handle design prevents misalignment and wrinkling during use, further supporting smooth and safe transfers. Overall, the new transfer sheet is strongly preferred by clinical staff for its ergonomic benefits and effectiveness in facilitating efficient, low-strain transfers.

Limitation

While this study demonstrates the clinical efficacy of the novel transfer sheet, several methodological limitations should be noted. First, as a single-center trial conducted in a large, well-resourced tertiary hospital, the findings may not be generalizable to primary care settings with different staffing ratios and infrastructure. Second, although the sample size (n = 124) provided sufficient statistical power (β = 0.1) to detect differences in primary outcomes, it limited the ability to perform subgroup analyses of rare adverse events with an incidence of less than 5%. Third, the potential influence of variability in staff physical strength on outcomes such as transfer time and perceived exertion scores was not assessed. Fourth, standardized training was provided only for the use of the novel transfer sheet, whereas conventional transfer methods lacked unified operational protocols, which may have introduced performance bias. Fifth, the three-month evaluation period did not allow for assessment of material durability under repeated use cycles, and the time and workload required for cleaning and disinfection after use were not evaluated. Finally, a cost-benefit analysis was not included, despite its importance in healthcare technology adoption decisions, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial demonstrated that the novel transfer sheet offers significant advantages in postoperative patient transfer by improving workflow efficiency, enhancing patient physiological stability, and reducing physical burden on healthcare staff. The device contributed to more streamlined clinical processes and aligned well with enhanced recovery principles. Future studies should focus on validating these findings across multiple centers, evaluating cost-effectiveness, and assessing long-term durability of the device. systolic blood pressure.
