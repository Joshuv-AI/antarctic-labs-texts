Clinical and imaging correlates of hyperorality in syndromes associated with frontotemporal lobar degeneration
Authors: Daniele Altomare, Valeria Bracca, Enrico Premi, Anna Micheli, Maria Sofia Cotelli, Roberto Gasparotti, Antonella Alberici, Barbara Borroni
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimEmpirical research investigating hyperorality in syndromes associated with frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD) is limited. The present study aims to assess and describe hyperorality and its clinical and imaging correlates in patients with FTLD‐associated syndromes.MethodsThis retrospective longitudinal study included consecutive patients with FTLD who underwent a clinical, cognitive, and behavioral assessment. The presence and severity of hyperorality was assessed using the Frontal Behavior Inventory.ResultsA total of 712 patients with FTLD were included in the study. Hyperorality was reported by 29% (204 of 712 [95% CI: 25–32%]) of patients; was more frequent in those with severe dementia than in those with prodromal or mild to moderate dementia (P < 0.05); was associated with younger age (odds ratio [OR] = 0.96 [95% CI: 0.94–0.99]), (P = 0.003) and positive family history for dementia (OR = 2.03 [95% CI: 1.18–3.49], P = 0.010); was overall more probable in the behavioral variant of frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD) and frontotemporal dementia with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis phenotypes, and less probable in other language or motor phenotypes; and was associated with higher severity of neuropsychiatric symptoms (OR = 1.08 [95% CI: 1.06–1.10], P < 0.001) and with the presence of several behavioral symptoms (P < 0.05). Moreover, hyperorality severity increased over time only in patients with bvFTD (β = +0.15, P = 0.011) or semantic variant of primary progressive aphasia (β = +0.34, P = 0.010). Finally, the presence of hyperorality was significantly associated with greater atrophy in the right anterior insula and right orbitofrontal region (false discovery rate–corrected P < 0.05).ConclusionHyperorality is common in certain FTLD‐associated syndromes. Understanding its correlates can help clinicians define pharmacological and educational interventions and clarify related anatomical circuits.Keywords: behavioral symptoms, dietary habits, eating behavior, frontotemporal lobar degeneration, hyperorality

Changes in eating behavior and dietary habits are common in patients with dementia. 1 Hyperorality is characterized by excessive and compulsive consumption of food, drink, or nonnutritive substances and can be associated with craving for sweets or rigid food preferences. The presence of hyperorality may require caregivers to provide dietary oversight, 2 causing significant stress and making its management complicated. The syndromes associated with frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD) present distinct challenges and opportunities for investigating hyperorality as it manifests with varying degrees of frequency and severity across different syndromes.

FTLD causes a heterogeneous group of neurodegenerative disorders with a wide range of clinical, genetic, and neuropathological features. 3 Specific clinical symptoms define different FTLD‐associated syndromes: the behavioral variant of frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD) is mainly associated with behavioral and personality changes 4 ; the nonfluent (or agrammatic) variant of primary progressive aphasia (nfvPPA) is associated with progressive deficits in speech, grammar, and word output 5 ; and the semantic variant of PPA (svPPA) consists of progressive deficits in semantic knowledge and naming. 5 Moreover, a proportion of patients have associated extrapyramidal symptoms, which may form part of either corticobasal syndrome (CBS) 6 or progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), 7 while others present with overlapping motor neuron disease defining the frontotemporal dementia with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (FTD‐ALS). 8 Changes in eating behavior and dietary habits in patients with FTLD‐associated syndromes are expected to be modulated by the clinical phenotype. For instance, food intake might increase (i.e. hyperorality) in patients with a prevalent behavioral phenotype.

To date, empirical research investigating hyperorality and its clinical and imaging correlates across the whole FTLD spectrum is limited, mostly focused only on bvFTD, 9 and longitudinal studies exploring trajectories over the disease course are not currently available. A few studies, mostly performed in small series of patients, have reported that hyperorality is associated with atrophy in the right structures of the brain reward system 10 , 11 and damage to the right orbitofrontal‐insular‐striatal circuit. 12

Understanding the nuances of hyperorality and its evolution during the disease progression among FTLD‐associated syndromes is crucial for delineating their clinical heterogeneity and underlying neuroanatomical mechanisms, increasing knowledge and awareness among healthcare providers and caregivers, and creating adaptive treatments and health policies.

The present study aims to assess and describe hyperorality and its clinical and imaging correlates in a large cohort of patients with FTLD‐associated syndromes.

Methods

Participants

This retrospective longitudinal study included a sample of consecutive patients meeting clinical criteria for an FTLD‐associated syndrome, namely bvFTD, 4 nfvPPA or svPPA, 5 CBS, 6 PSP, 7 or FTD‐ALS, 8 recruited at the Centre for Neurodegenerative Disorders of the University of Brescia (Italy) from October 2009 to February 2023, as previously described. 13 Patients were included if scores of the Frontal Behavior Inventory (FBI) 14 , 15 and the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) Dementia Staging Instrument plus National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center Behavior and Language Domains (CDR plus NACC FTLD) 16 were available.

Participants underwent a clinical, cognitive, and behavioral assessment at baseline and then at follow‐up (712 at baseline and 331 at follow‐up, for a total of 1043 assessments). In all cases, the diagnosis was accomplished by clinical criteria 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 and supported by routine brain structural imaging. In a subset of cases, Alzheimer disease was ruled out by cerebrospinal fluid (Aβ1‐42, p‐tau181, and t‐tau) assessment or amyloid positron emission tomography, and inherited FTLD was proven by genetic screening of monogenic forms.

Participants' disease was classified as prodromal (CDR plus NACC FTLD – global score = 0.5), mild dementia (CDR plus NACC FTLD – global score = 1), moderate dementia (CDR plus NACC FTLD – global score = 2), or severe dementia (CDR plus NACC FTLD – global score = 3). 16

Full written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The local ethics committee in Brescia (Italy) approved the study protocol (NP 2189).

Assessment of hyperorality and other behavioral symptoms

Hyperorality and other behavioral symptoms were assessed using the FBI. 15 FBI is a caregiver questionnaire consisting of 24 items referring to either negative behavior symptoms (apathy, aspontaneity, indifference, inflexibility, personal neglect, disorganization, inattention, loss of insight, logopenia, semantic deficit, aphasia, and alien hand) or positive behavior symptoms (obsessions, irritability, euphoria, impulsivity, hoarding, inappropriateness, restlessness, aggression, hyperorality, hypersexuality, utilization behavior, and incontinence). Hyperorality, for instance, is investigated with this question: ‘Has s/he been drinking or eating excessively anything in sight, or developing food fads, a sweet tooth, eating bananas or cookies excessively, or even putting objects in his/her mouth, or has s/he always had a large appetite and the eating habits have not changed? Has s/he lost table manners?’. The patient's caregiver rates the severity/frequency of each symptom using a 0 to 3 Likert scale where 0 means ‘none’, 1 ‘mild/occasional’, 2 ‘moderate’, and 3 ‘severe/most of the time’. 15 Moreover, it is possible to compute a total score (sum of all of the individual items; FBI‐AB, 0–72 range), as well as a negative behavior subscore (sum of the negative behavior items; FBI‐A, 0–36 range) and a positive behavior subscore (sum of the positive behavior items; FBI‐B, 0–36 range). 15 The presence or absence of hyperorality and other behavioral symptoms was defined based on the respective FBI items (absent: individual FBI item score = 0, or present: individual FBI item score ≥1). The FBI – hyperorality score was also used as an ordinal (0–3 range) measure.

Variables of interest

Demographic and clinical data such as age, sex, age at onset, education, family history for dementia, FTLD‐associated syndrome phenotype, disease severity, loss in basic (BADL) and instrumental (IADL) activities of daily living, and dementia‐related behavioral and neuropsychiatric symptoms were considered as predictors in the analyses. For the purpose of the present study, the body mass index (BMI) was computed when weight and height were available.

Age at onset was defined as the age in which one of the core symptoms of FTLD was first noted by the patient, family member, health care provider or allied health professional, as recorded in the medical record. Education was measured both as years of formal education and through the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) 2011. 17 ISCED ranges from 0 (early childhood education) to 8 (doctoral or equivalent level), 17 and was categorized into two groups: low education (ISCED 0–3) and high education (ISCED 4–8). Family history for dementia was defined and categorized into two groups based on the modified Goldman score: positive family history (Goldman score = 1 or 2, where 1 is an autosomal dominant family history of FTLD or FTD‐ALS, and 2 is familial aggregation of three or more family members with dementia but not meeting criteria for 1) and negative family history (Goldman score = 3 or 4, where 3 is one other affected family member with dementia, and 4 is no or unknown family history). 18 Disease severity was defined based on the CDR plus NACC FTLD – global score. 16 Severity of dementia‐related behavioral and neuropsychiatric symptoms, which are common in patients with frontotemporal dementia, 19 , 20 , 21 was measured through the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI), a structured caregiver interview, for which a total score (0–144 range) can be computed. 22

Brain magnetic resonance imaging acquisition and processing

Brain structural images (three‐dimensional T1‐weighted magnetization‐prepared rapid acquisition with gradient echo [MPRAGE] magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) were collected using three scanners (Siemens Skyra 3T, Siemens Avanto 1.5T, and Siemens Symphony 1.5T). The raw DICOM scans were converted into Neuroimaging Informatics Technology Initiative (NIfTI) format using the MRIcroGL software (www.nitrc.org/projects/mricrogl). T1‐weighted images were then processed and analyzed with the voxel‐based morphometry (VBM) pipeline implemented in the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12 v.1742) (www.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat) for Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12 v. 7219) (www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12) running on MATLAB R2019b (the MathWorks, Inc.). The VBM pipeline consists of several stages (tissue segmentation, spatial normalization to a standard Montreal National Institute [MNI] template, modulation, and smoothing), as previously described. 23 CAT12 potentially provided more robust and accurate performances compared with other VBM pipelines 24 in the calculation of gray matter volume. The normalized and modulated gray matter images were then smoothed with 8 ×8 × 8 mm full width at half‐maximum Gaussian kernel.

A two‐sample t test was performed in SPM comparing patients with FTLD with or without hyperorality. Age, sex, disease severity (CDR plus NACC FTLD – global score), FTLD‐associated syndromes phenotype, total intracranial volume (gray matter volume + white matter volume + cerebrospinal fluid volume), and scanner type were used as nuisance variables in the model. A statistical threshold of P < 0.05 corrected for multiple comparisons (false discovery rate [FDR]) was adopted, with a minimum cluster size of 400 voxels. Finally, we also used a more stringent statistical threshold (P < 0.05 corrected using the family‐wise error rate) to identify the most relevant region to be used for a region‐of‐interest analysis and to calculate mean gray matter density for each patient.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are described as medians and interquartile range (IQRs), and categorical variables as percentages (raw numbers).

Differences among groups in the sociodemographic and clinical features were assessed using Kruskal‐Wallis rank sum tests for continuous variables, or tests for equality of proportions for categorical variables. If significant, in case the number of groups in the comparison was larger than two, post hoc pairwise comparisons (Dunn all‐pairs rank comparison test for continuous variables or pairwise comparisons for categorical variables) were adjusted using Bonferroni correction.

Demographic and clinical predictors (i.e. age, sex, age at onset, education, ISCED group, family history, FTLD‐associated syndrome, CDR plus NACC FTLD – global score, BADL, IADL, NPI – total score) as well as the presence of other behavioral symptoms (i.e. FBI individual items used as dichotomous measures: present or absent) associated with the presence of hyperorality were assessed through univariate and multiple/multivariable logistic regression models with presence of hyperorality (i.e. present or absent) as the dependent variable: all independent variables with P < 0.05 in univariate models were included in multiple models to which the stepwise method for automatic variables selection was applied to build the best‐performing (in terms of achieving an optimal balance between model complexity and goodness of fit) predictive models.

The longitudinal change in hyperorality was assessed using a linear mixed model with longitudinal FBI – hyperorality scores as the dependent variable, FTLD‐associated syndrome (i.e. bvFTD, nfvPPA, svPPA, CBS, PSP, FTD‐ALS), time (i.e. years) from baseline, and their interaction as independent variables; with random intercepts and slopes at the subject level.

All statistical analyses were performed with R version 4.3.0 (R Project for Statistical Computing, https://www.r-project.org/).

Prevalence of hyperorality

In the whole sample, 29% (204 of 712 [95% CI: 25–32%]) of patients reported hyperorality. Disaggregating by disease stage, hyperorality was reported by 16% (42 of 269 [95% CI: 12–21%]) of patients in the prodromal, 25% (44 of 176 [95% CI: 19–32%]) in the mild dementia, 38% (63 of 168 [95% CI: 30–45%]) in the moderate dementia, and 56% (55 of 99 [95% CI: 45–65%]) in the severe dementia stage. Hyperorality was more frequent in those with severe dementia than in those in the prodromal (P < 0.001), mild dementia (P < 0.001), or moderate dementia (P = 0.037) stage, as well as in those with moderate dementia than in those in the prodromal stage (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1a).

Prevalence of hyperorality disaggregating by disease stage (a) and frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD)–associated syndrome (b), and body mass index (BMI; in kg/m2); by Frontal Behavior Inventory (FBI) – hyperorality score (c). bvFTD, behavioral variant of frontotemporal dementia; CBS, corticobasal syndrome; FTD‐ALS, frontotemporal dementia with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis; nfvPPA, nonfluent variant of primary progressive aphasia; PSP, progressive supranuclear palsy; svPPA, semantic variant of primary progressive aphasia.

Disaggregating by FTLD‐associated syndrome phenotypes, hyperorality was reported in 39% (133 of 337 [95% CI: 34–45%]) of bvFTD, 17% (15 of 88 [95% CI: 10–27%]) of nfvPPA, 31% (16 of 52 [95% CI: 19–45%]) of svPPA, 7% (seven of 103 [95% CI: 3–14%]) of CBS, 18% (19 of 104 [95% CI: 12–27%]) of PSP, and 50% (14 of 28 [95% CI: 33–67%]) of FTD‐ALS. Hyperorality was more frequent in bvFTD and FTD‐ALS than in nfvPPA (P = 0.002 and P = 0.017), CBS (P < 0.001 and P < 0.001), and PSP (P = 0.002 and P = 0.021), respectively, as well as in svPPA than in CBS (P = 0.003) (Fig. 1b).

Hyperorality was associated with increased BMI, being higher in patients with an FBI–hyperorality score of 3 than in those with an FBI – hyperorality score of 0 (mean 26.5 [SD: 4.8] vs 24.6 [SD: 5.0], P = 0.018) (Fig. 1c).

Predictors of hyperorality

The multiple model showed that the presence of hyperorality was associated with younger age (OR = 0.96 [95% CI: 0.94–0.99], P = 0.003) and positive family history for dementia (OR = 2.03 [95% CI: 1.18–3.49], P = 0.010); was overall more probable in the bvFTD and FTD‐ALS phenotypes and less probable in CBS, nfvPPA, and PSP; and was associated with higher severity of behavioral and neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPI – total score: OR = 1.08 [95% CI: 1.06–1.10], P < 0.001) (Table 2a).

Other behavioral symptoms associated with hyperorality

The multiple model showed that the presence of hyperorality was associated with the presence of personal neglect (OR = 2.62 [95% CI: 1.71–4.02]), P < 0.001), inattention (OR = 1.78 [95% CI: 1.12–2.85], P = 0.015), hoarding (OR = 2.50 [95% CI: 1.37–4.62]), P = 0.003), inappropriateness (OR = 2.09 [95% CI: 1.37–3.19]), P < 0.001), and restlessness (OR = 1.89 [95% CI: 1.11–3.23], P = 0.019) (Table 2b), while apathy (OR = 1.58 [95% CI: 0.98–2.58], P = 0.065), indifference (OR = 1.46 [95% CI: 0.95–2.24], P = 0.084), and utilization behavior (OR = 1.69 [95% CI: 0.86–3.39], P = 0.130) passed the stepwise selection process but did not reach statistical significance (Table 2b).

Change of hyperorality severity over time

A follow‐up assessment was available in 46% (331 of 712) of patients (142 bvFTD, 24 nfvPPA, 26 svPPA, 69 CBS, 61 PSP, and nine FTD‐ALS), with a median follow‐up duration of 378 days (IQR: 118 days).

Hyperorality severity increased over time only in patients with bvFTD (β = +0.15, P = 0.011) or svPPA (β = +0.34, P = 0.010), while it remained stable in all other syndromes (nfvPPA: β = −0.06, P = 0.659; CBS: β = +0.07, P = 0.414; PSP: β = +0.04, P = 0.692; and FTD‐ALS: β = −0.01, P = 0.972) (Fig. 2). Longitudinal change in svPPA was statistically significantly different than that in nfvPPA (P = 0.037) (Fig. 2).

Imaging correlates of hyperorality

MRI suitable for VBM analyses was available in 34% (244 of 712) of patients (111 bvFTD, 42 nfvPPA, 13 svPPA, 34 CBS, and 44 PSP, not including six FTD‐ALS because of the small sample size of this subgroup), of whom 30% (71 of 244) with hyperorality (FBI – hyperorality = 1 in 22, =2 in 29, and = 3 in 20 patients) and 70% (173 of 244) without hyperorality.

The presence of hyperorality was significantly associated with reduced gray matter density (as a proxy of brain atrophy) in the right anterior insula (x = 41, y = 21, z = −12; T = 4.69) and in right anterior orbital gyrus (x = 27, y = 42, z = −17; T = 4.69) (cumulative cluster of 1746 voxels; FDR‐corrected P < 0.05) (Fig. 3a). Using a more stringent statistical threshold (family‐wise error rate–corrected P < 0.05), a single cluster of 30 voxels in the right insula (x = 41, y = 21, z = −12) was found. In particular, hyperorality was associated with decreased right insula volume, being lower in patients with an FBI – hyperorality score of 2 (72 [9], P < 0.001) or 3 (76 [23], P = 0.024) than in those with an FBI – hyperorality score of 0 (86 [16]) (Fig. 3b).

Discussion

Hyperorality may be a clinically relevant symptom in FTLD, but its role in these syndromes is still partially unexplored, resulting in a lack of proper pharmacological and educational interventions aimed at restoring eating habits. Moreover, hyperorality is detrimental for patients' health and is a cause of distress for caregivers. Identifying variables associated with hyperorality, tracking its changes over time, and understanding its imaging correlates might inform the development of targeted interventions aimed at alleviating its impact on patients' quality of life and enhancing overall care provision.

In the present study, we observed that the prevalence of hyperorality was 29% in patients with FTLD‐associated syndromes and increased with increasing disease severity. The prevalence of hyperorality was higher in patients with behavioral phenotypes (i.e. bvFTD and FTD‐ALS) than in those with language or motor phenotypes (i.e. CBS, nfvPPA, and PSP). Moreover, the presence of hyperorality was associated with younger age and positive family history for dementia, suggesting that it may be driven by genetic background beyond clinical phenotype, as well as with other behavioral symptoms such as personal neglect, inattention, hoarding, inappropriateness, and restlessness. Interestingly, hyperorality severity was associated with increased BMI, while no sex effect was observed. The longitudinal assessment of patients with FTLD demonstrated that the severity of hyperorality worsened over time only in patients with bvFTD or svPPA, with a statistically significant difference between svPPA and nfvPPA. Finally, using a whole brain structural approach, which can be used as a measure of disease severity, 25 we found that patients reporting hyperorality had greater atrophy in the right anterior insula and right orbitofrontal region. This confirms and extends previous study findings reporting atrophy of right structures of the brain reward system 10 , 11 and that damage to a right orbitofrontal‐insular‐striatal circuit is associated with overeating behavior in humans, 12 supporting the hypothesis that these regions integrate internal satiety signals with environmental food cues to produce adaptive eating behaviors. 12

Our findings are in line with previous evidence. Indeed, hyperorality is one of the six core features of bvFTD and can manifest as altered food preferences, binge eating, increased consumption of alcohol or cigarettes, and oral exploration or consumption of inedible objects. 4 Moreover, the high prevalence of hyperorality in FTD‐ALS is likely explained by the behavioral symptoms characteristic of this syndrome, 8 despite the progressive motor impairment associated with ALS may cause negative calorie balance, inadequate nutrition, and eventually weight loss. 26 The different patterns of hyperorality observed in the two variants of PPA reflect the specific clinical features of each variant. Indeed, patients with svPPA might be more prone to exhibit hyperorality as well as ritualistic behaviors, both at the time of diagnosis and increasingly over time, because they share behavioral changes with bvFTD 27 as a result of similar anatomical involvement of the orbital‐frontal cortex, 27 and most of them meet diagnostic criteria of bvFTD within approximatively 5 years after clinical onset. 28 Indeed, eating changes such as increased preference for sweet foods have been reported both in patients with bvFTD and in those with svPPA, 29 albeit with different features: e.g. gluttony and indiscriminate eating in bvFTD, and food fads in svPPA. 29 Moreover, patients with bvFTD and svPPA consume more carbohydrates and sugar than healthy individuals, and this abnormal eating behavior is not explained by increased hunger or decreased satiety, resulting in increased weight, BMI, and waist circumference. 30 In contrast, hyperorality in patients with nfvPPA might be a secondary and limited clinical manifestation as behavioral disturbances are less common, while the onset of motor deficits during the disease evolution is more common, often resulting in a co‐diagnosis of motor variants within approximatively 5 years. 28 In CBS and PSP, we reported a low frequency of hyperorality consistently with the fact that its clinical presentation may be limited by the prominent motor symptoms, which can limit feeding behaviors and oral intake. Moreover, clinical and neuropathological data indicate that CBS and PSP have a close relationship with nfvPPA, 31 , 32 resulting in similar and predominant language and motor deficits rather than behavioral disturbances. 33

This work contributes to advance our understanding of hyperorality within the context of FTLD‐associated syndromes. Our findings might inform the development of targeted interventions such as assistance with daily activities or nursing care, as well as pharmacological and nonpharmacological treatments of hyperorality and comorbid symptoms. Such interventions might require changes in health policies and more active support from healthcare providers. This might alleviate the impact of hyperorality on the quality of life of patients and caregivers and enhance overall care provision.

Strengths and Limitations

The main strengths of this paper include its wide focus on the whole FTLD spectrum rather than only on specific syndromes (e.g. bvFTD), the large sample size, and the availability of longitudinal and imaging data. Taken together, this allows us to draw strong and reliable conclusions that can be generalized to the broader FTLD population.

Nevertheless, we acknowledge that this study has some limitations. First, this is a retrospective study based on clinical reports collected over more than 20 years. Consequently, some relevant clinical measures (e.g. neuropsychological functions) were not systematically collected, and therefore were not included in the present study. Moreover, we assessed hyperorality using the relative FBI item, which only allows a rough assessment of this clinical manifestation. The use of more sophisticated tools (e.g. specific questionnaires such as Food Frequency Questionnaires, 34 or structured interviews), as performed in previous studies, 35 , 36 would provide a more in‐depth and nuanced characterization of hyperorality focusing on its different aspects (e.g. changes in appetite, food preference, eating habits, and stereotypic eating). Furthermore, some of the data used in the present study (e.g. weight and height used to compute BMI) were only self‐reported and not objectively assessed. Finally, in the present study, there are no neuropeptide examinations, despite previous evidence highlighting their role in eating behavior. 37 , 38

Conclusion

This work contributes to advancing our understanding of hyperorality within the context of FTLD‐associated syndromes and might help identify patients at higher risk for developing hyperorality and targeted intervention strategies.
