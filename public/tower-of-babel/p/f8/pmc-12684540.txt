Subclinical Cardiac Dysfunction in Women With Prior Gestational Diabetes: The Hispanic Community Study/Study of Latinos
Authors: Eric K. Broni, Lisa D. Levine, Jennifer Lewey, Ravi Retnakaran, Allison G. Hays, Justin B. Echouffo‐Tcheugui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGestational diabetes (GD) is associated with heart failure risk. However, the association of GD and postpartum early stages of myocardial dysfunction (a robust predictor of heart failure) as assessed by imaging has seldom been examined, especially among Hispanic women, who represent the fastest‐growing ethnic minority population and have the highest prevalence of GD in US women.MethodsWe examined Hispanic women recruited to the Hispanic Community Study/Study of Latinos cohort, who reported at least 1 prior pregnancy and GD history at either visit 1 (2008–2011) or visit 2 (2014–2017) and echocardiographic assessments at visit 2. We used multivariable linear and logistic regression models to evaluate the associations between GD history and echocardiographic parameters.ResultsAmong 2894 participants (mean age 53±9 years), 9.3% (n=270) had a GD history. After adjusting for cardiovascular disease risk factors, including current diabetes status, Hispanic women with GD history had higher mean adjusted left ventricular relative wall thickness (β=0.01 [95% CI, 0.00–0.02]), lateral peak early mitral inflow velocity to early diastolic velocity of the mitral annulus ratio (β=0.49 [95% CI, 0.09–0.89]), abnormal left ventricular diastolic function (adjusted odds ratio, 1.41 [95% CI, 1.04–1.91]), and lower mean adjusted left ventricular end‐diastolic diameter (β=–0.07 [95% CI, −0.12 to −0.02]) compared with those without prior GD history. Similar associations were observed in results stratified by the most recent glycemic status.ConclusionsHistory of GD was associated with a higher frequency and severity of myocardial diastolic abnormalities. Echocardiographic‐based screening for myocardial dysfunction in women with GD history has the potential to help avert overt cardiovascular disease in this high‐risk population.

Gestational diabetes (GD) is highly prevalent in pregnancy, affecting ≈8% of pregnancies each year in the United States. 1 GD confers a significantly higher long‐term risk for all forms of cardiovascular diseases (CVD), including heart failure (HF). 2 , 3 The relative risk of CVD associated with a prior GD history is estimated at ≈30% to 56%, independent of intervening postpartum type 2 diabetes. 2 In the United States, GD disproportionally affects racial and ethnic minority populations, especially women with a Hispanic background. 4 Hispanic women are the fastest‐growing ethnic minority population in the United States, with high rates of traditional and obstetric CVD risk factors, including obesity, GD, and preeclampsia. 5 However, the burden of GD‐related subclinical CVD has been understudied in this population.

Abnormalities of myocardial structure and function detected on noninvasive imaging are indicators of subclinical CVD and are cardinal predictors of future CVD events in the general population. 6 , 7 , 8 Hence, their identification among young women with a history of GD can be leveraged to prevent overt CVD, especially HF. Emerging evidence suggests a link between GD and subclinical left ventricular (LV) dysfunction in pregnancy 9 and postpartum. 10 However, there is overall a dearth of data on the long‐term effects of GD on cardiac structure and function in the US population in general, and particularly among Hispanic women. Knowledge of the independent effects of GD on LV structure and function in Hispanic women can provide a targeted window of opportunity to mitigate subclinical disease before the development of overt CVD.

Using data from the Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos (HCHS/SOL) cohort, we investigated the effect of prior GD on LV structure and function. We hypothesized that prior GD is associated with a greater likelihood of LV abnormalities, including (1) LV structural abnormalities such as abnormal LV geometry, LV hypertrophy, relative wall thickness (RWT), and (2) LV functional abnormalities such as ejection fraction and stroke volume.

METHODS

Study Design

The data underlying this article are based on the HCHS/SOL study and are available at the National Institutes of Health’s Biologic Specimen and Data Repository Information Coordinating Center, which can be accessed at: https://biolincc.nhlbi.nih.gov/studies/hchssol/.

The design and methods used in the HCHS/SOL have been previously documented. 11 , 12 HCHS/SOL is a multicenter, population‐based cohort study of several risk factors and diseases among individuals of Hispanic heritage (Central American, Cuban, Dominican, Mexican, Puerto Rican, and South American) living in the United States. 13 Briefly, between March 2008 and June 2011, HCHS/SOL recruited 16 415 Hispanic adults residing in 4 communities, namely Bronx, New York; Chicago, Illinois; Miami, Florida; and San Diego, California, using a multistage probability sample design. All study protocols were approved by the institutional review board at each site, and written informed consent was obtained from all study participants.

For the current investigation, we analyzed data from 12 647 participants in the data set obtained from the Biologic Specimen and Data Repository Information Coordinating Center. We included women who reported at least 1 prior pregnancy and prior GD history at either visit 1 (2008–2011) or visit 2 (2014–2017), have data on transthoracic echocardiography at visit 2, and on all our covariates of interest. We excluded men (n=5527), nulligravid women (n=822), female participants missing data on GD (n=1817), those missing echocardiogram assessments (n=1768), as well as participants missing data on our covariates of interest (n=38). Our final analytical sample was 2894 participants. The study flowchart is shown in Figure S1. A comparison of the characteristics of the women included in our study and those of women excluded is shown in Table S1.

Ascertainment of GD and Other Glycemic Phenotypes

Self‐reported history of prior GD was obtained as part of reproductive history at visits 1 and 2. At both visits, female participants were asked if they had any history of diabetes in pregnancy diagnosed by a physician, with possible responses of yes or no. 12 Prior validation studies comparing maternal recall of GD 2 to 10 years postpartum, with medical records, showed a sensitivity of 70% to 100% and a specificity of 90% to ≥98%. 14 , 15

The most recent glycemic status of participants at visit 2 in the HCHS/SOL was assessed using the American Diabetes Association criteria. 16 Normoglycemia was defined as fasting plasma glucose <100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L), 2‐hour plasma glucose <140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L), and glycated hemoglobin <5.7%. Prediabetes was defined as fasting plasma glucose of 5.6 to 7.0 mmol/L, or 2‐hour plasma glucose of 7.8 to 11.1 mmol/L, or glycated hemoglobin of 5.7% to 6.4%, and diabetes was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L), or 2‐hour plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L), or glycated hemoglobin ≥6.5%, or self‐reported diabetes medication use.

Echocardiographic Measurements

Using previously standardized protocols, 17 comprehensive 2‐dimensional echocardiographic evaluations were conducted during exam 2 by trained and certified sonographers and followed the recommendations of the American Society of Echocardiography. 18 , 19 All images were analyzed by technical specialists at the core HCSL/SOL Echocardiography Reading Center. 20

The echocardiographic parameters examined in this study include: LV ejection fraction, LV stroke volume, LV mass index (LVMI), LV end‐diastolic diameter, LVMI/end diastolic volume (EDV) ratio, LV RWT, peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity, and peak early mitral inflow velocity (E) to early diastolic velocity of the mitral annulus (e’) (E/e’) ratio. In keeping with prior studies, 18 , 21 we defined concentric LV remodeling as LVMI ≤95 g/m2 and RWT >0.42. Concentric LV hypertrophy (LVH) was defined as LV mass index >95 g/m2 and RWT >0.42, and eccentric LVH as LVMI >95 g/m2 and RWT ≤0.42. We defined abnormal LV geometry as presence of concentric remodeling, concentric LVH, or eccentric LVH. We graded abnormal LV diastolic function based on an algorithm that integrated the American Society for Echocardiography guidelines with Redfield criteria 20 and dichotomized as previously described. 21

Covariates

We considered the following covariates assessed at visits 1 or 2: age, self‐reported Hispanic background/heritage (Central American, Cuban, Dominican, Mexican, Puerto Rican, South American), education, body mass index, physical activity (metabolic equivalent of task [MET] in minutes per week), smoking status, and alcohol use. We also included clinical risk factors such as total number of prior pregnancies, diastolic and systolic blood pressure, use of hypertension medications, current diabetes status, total cholesterol/high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio, urine albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio, and estimated glomerular filtration rate based on serum creatinine, cystatin C, age, and race. 22

Statistical Analysis

We examined the characteristics of study participants by their GD history status: women with prior GD history versus those without a GD history. Continuous variables were compared using either the t test or Mann‐Whitney test, and categorical variables were assessed using the χ2 test.

We evaluated the associations between GD history and echocardiographic measures of LV structure and function, using multivariable linear (for continuous outcomes) and logistic (for categorical outcomes) regression models, with adjustments conducted sequentially. All continuous outcomes in our study were normally distributed and therefore did not warrant any natural log‐transformations before linear regression analysis. Our initial model (Model 1) adjusted for age and Hispanic background/heritage. A subsequent model (Model 2) additionally adjusted for cigarette smoking, alcohol intake, body mass index, physical activity, number of prior pregnancies, systolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, and estimated glomerular filtration rate. Finally, Model 3 controlled for Model 2 covariates plus current diabetes status.

In secondary analyses, we assessed the associations between the underlisted 6 glycemic status categories and echocardiographic outcomes at visit 2. Participants were categorized according to their most recent glycemic status in the following categories: no GD history and normoglycemia (referent group), no GD history and prediabetes, no GD history and diabetes, GD history and normoglycemia, GD history and prediabetes, and GD history and diabetes. In exploratory analyses, we tested the modifying effect of current diabetes status by inserting an interaction term in Model 3 but found no heterogeneity by current diabetes status at P‐interaction <0.05.

All our analyses were survey‐weighted to account for the complex study design of the HCHS/SOL cohort and nonresponse at visit 2. 11 Survey weights were trimmed and calibrated to the 2010 census characteristics by age, sex, and Hispanic background. 11

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 16.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). A 2‐sided P value <0.05 was indicative of statistical significance.

GD and Echocardiographic Measures

After adjustment for relevant confounders, compared with participants with no GD history, those with positive GD history had higher mean adjusted LV RWT (β=0.01 [95% CI, 0.00–0.02]) and lateral E/e’ ratio (β=0.49 [95% CI, 0.09–0.89]). In minimally adjusted models, GD was associated with abnormal LV geometry (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.33 [95% CI, 1.01–1.74]) and diastolic dysfunction [aOR, 1.50 [95% CI, 1.12–2.01]) (Table 2, Model 1). With further adjustments, GD remained associated with a greater extent of abnormal LV diastolic function (aOR, 1.41 [95% CI, 1.04–1.91]) (Figure and Table 2 [Model 2]). Women with GD history had lower mean adjusted LV end‐diastolic diameter compared with those without prior GD (β=–0.07 [95% CI, −0.12 to −0.02]) (Figure and Table 2 [Model 2]). Except for abnormal LV diastolic function, these associations persisted even after adjusting for current diabetes status (Table 2, Model 3).

GD, Glycemic Phenotypes, and Echocardiographic Measures

After controlling for confounders, women with diabetes and no GD history and those with combined GD history and diabetes at visit 2 showed significantly different echocardiographic indices, compared with Hispanic women with negative GD history who were normoglycemic at visit 2 (reference group). Compared with women without GD and without diabetes, those with combined GD history and diabetes exhibited more pronounced changes in LV ejection fraction, LV end‐diastolic diameter, lateral E/e’ ratio, concentric LVH, and LV geometry and diastolic function than those who had diabetes alone (Table 3). Compared with those without GD history and without diabetes, women with isolated diabetes had greater abnormalities in LV stroke volume, LVMI/EDV ratio, and LVMI compared with those with combined GD history and diabetes. As compared with women without diabetes and without a history of GD, participants with isolated GD history had the highest LVMI/EDV ratio (β=1.43 [95% CI, 0.11–2.76]), greatest magnitude of concentric LVH (aOR, 5.82 [95% CI, 1.55–21.94]), and lowest mean peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity (β=–26.88 [95% CI, −48.31 to −5.44]) (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

In a large multicenter community‐based cohort of Hispanic women, we found that a history of GD was associated with significant subclinical myocardial dysfunction, and these associations persisted even after accounting for current diabetes status. A history of GD was related to abnormal LV geometry and diastolic abnormalities (especially an elevated E/e’ ratio), which are a potential precursor of HF with preserved ejection fraction. Furthermore, stratification by current glycemic status revealed an independent effect of GD, but this effect was potentiated by the presence of current diabetes. Women with prior GD history without diabetes (isolated GD) and those with GD history and current diabetes exhibited a much higher degree of myocardial abnormalities compared with normoglycemic Hispanic women without a prior GD history.

To our knowledge, our study is the first to evaluate the independent subclinical effects of GD on the myocardium among Hispanic women, accounting for current glycemic status. Our study provides additional and valuable evidence on the links between GD and postpartum subclinical myocardial dysfunction. Our results are congruent with those of prior smaller investigations showing intrapartum and postpartum GD‐related echocardiographic changes. 23 , 24 , 25 However, such studies did not always specifically examine the effects of subsequent diabetes following the diagnosis of GD. Our study is unique in that the assessment of GD and myocardial function association accounted for the full spectrum of the current glycemia status, showing that isolated GD is independently associated with abnormal cardiac function in Hispanic women. Our observations also add evidence on the intermediate effects of various subsequent glycemic phenotypes (especially diabetes) in the relation of GD and myocardial structure and function in Hispanic women. The observed GD effects on cardiac function and structure may be mediated through important precursors, such as type 2 diabetes and hypertension. Hence, accounting for these factors may be an overadjustment, which may have attenuated the magnitude of our effect estimates. Nevertheless, the persistence of significant associations despite these adjustments indicates the robustness of our estimates.

Our findings hold significant clinical and practical public health relevance. Abnormal LV structure and geometry, particularly LV RWT, has been identified as an independent predictor of CVD. 6 , 7 , 8 For example, in the Offspring Framingham Heart Study, including adults free of CVD, a 0.1‐unit increase in LV RWT was associated with a 59% increased risk of CVD. 26 Similarly, an increased E/e’ ratio, which has been linked to GD, is recognized as a strong indicator of diastolic dysfunction 27 and is associated with an increased incidence of HF, especially HF with preserved ejection fraction. 28 We also observed a nonsignificant association between GD and abnormal LV diastolic function, which is linked to a higher incidence of cardiovascular events in healthy cohorts and serves as a strong predictor of HF progression. 29 Our results reinforce the importance of GD as a potential screening and therapeutic target to achieve CVD prevention among young women. Our study suggests that early and possibly systematic CVD detection after a pregnancy complicated by GD should be a priority, because it has the potential to prevent future CVD. Our findings underscore the need for regular myocardial function monitoring in the aftermath of a pregnancy complicated by GD, starting as early as a year after the pregnancy. The relevance of such screening in the years following a pregnancy complicated by GD is illustrated by the rising CVD burden 30 , 31 and the high and possibly rising prevalence of GD, especially in minority groups such as Hispanic women. 4 This prevention opportunity is congruent with the recommendations of cardiovascular 32 , 33 as well as obstetrician and gynecologists professional societies. 34 , 35

The mechanistic pathways linking GD to myocardial abnormalities are incompletely understood. However, insulin resistance is a possible pathway linking GD to abnormal myocardial indices on echocardiography. 36 The effect of GD on the myocardium can be mediated by its effect on precursors, including cardiovascular risk factors such as type 2 diabetes. 37 Pregnancy could also potentiate the effects of risk factors, such as obesity, on the myocardium. 38 However, the persistence of the GD and CVD association after adjustment for well‐known cardiovascular risk factors, including diabetes, and results stratified by various glycemic phenotypes suggest an intrinsic GD effect on the myocardium. GD can induce endothelial dysfunction and microvascular damage, a precursor of CVD. 39 , 40 Other possible pathways linking GD and CVD are similar to that observed with type 2 diabetes, including the effects of glucotoxicity, lipotoxicity, and hyperinsulinemia on the myocardium; increased deposition of advanced glycation end products; and systemic oxidative stress leading to myocardial injury and fibrosis and mitochondrial dysfunction. 41

There are limitations to our study. First, although participants who self‐reported GD history at visit 1 were prospectively analyzed, the cross‐sectional analysis of visit 2 participants limits the inference of causality, although the timing of reported prior GD preceded the timing of assessments of cardiac traits at visit 2. Second, prior GD status was ascertained using self‐report, which is subject to recall bias; nonetheless, self‐report has been shown to be a valid GD ascertainment method used in most cohort studies. 14 , 15 Third, we did not have data on the severity of GD and the extent of glucose control during pregnancy. Fourth, our exploratory analyses examined multiple echocardiographic measures, thus increasing the type 1 error rates. Last, we lacked data on the postpartum use of cardioprotective medications.

Our study has notable strengths. First, our study represents a large, community‐based investigation of Hispanic women, an increasingly significant yet understudied subpopulation in the United States. The assessment of echocardiographic traits was conducted using standardized image acquisition protocols and centralized image processing at a core center with high reproducibility. We also conducted a rigorous adjustment for confounders including known cardiovascular risk factors and pregnancy‐related and nonpregnancy‐related factors. Finally, the assessment of current glycemic status in our study was comprehensive, including fasting and postload glycemic measures (fasting plasma glucose, 2‐hour plasma glucose, and glycated hemoglobin), thus allowing a detailed investigation of the contribution of the intercurrent postpartum glycemic status in the association of GD history and subclinical myocardial dysfunction.

CONCLUSIONS

In a large multicenter cohort of Hispanic women, a history of GD was associated with a higher frequency and severity of myocardial abnormalities, even after accounting for cardiovascular risk factors, including current diabetes. Our findings underscore the potential usefulness of screening for myocardial dysfunction in the aftermath of a pregnancy complicated by GD. They also emphasize the need for lifestyle modification practices and other potential therapeutic opportunities to avert overt clinical CVD in this high‐risk population.
