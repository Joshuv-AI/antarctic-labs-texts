APOE ε4 and Alzheimer's disease biomarkers in an American Indian community
Authors: Matti J Matheson, Rene Labounek, Adam D Block, Corey Strong, Ann J Robertson, Adam Hansen, Meghan Kremer, Ashley J Petersen, Annamarie Hill, Breton M Asken, Igor Nestrasil, Christophe Lenglet, Danni Li, J Neil Henderson, William G Mantyh, for the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTION Apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4's effect on Alzheimer's disease (AD) depends on ancestry and environmental risk factors. The APOE ε4–AD link in American Indian (AI) populations appears attenuated, with current understanding largely informed by a single research study. This study seeks to extend this evidence in an AI population in Minnesota.METHODSCross‐sectional analysis of APOE ε4, medical comorbidities, and AD plasma biomarkers with 182 tribal elders.RESULTSMedian age was 67; 70% were female; median Mini‐Mental State Examination score was 29. Age‐, sex‐, and chronic kidney disease‐adjusted log odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals for APOE ε4 carrier status on amyloid beta 42/40, tau phosphorylated at threonine 217 (p‐tau217), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) were 0.86 (0.80 to 0.94), 1.04 (0.87 to 1.25), and 1.00 (0.86 to 1.16).CONCLUSIONCompared to non‐Hispanic White populations, APOE ε4 has an attenuated effect on p‐tau217 or GFAP in AI tribal elders. Limitations of this study include survival bias and race‐associated differences in social determinants of health that can influence the observed results.

INTRODUCTION

APOE has three alleles (APOE ε2, APOE ε3, and APOE ε4) and APOE ε4 is the most significant genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's disease (AD). 1 APOE ε4 leads to earlier‐onset AD, and, for a given age, APOE ε4 carriers have more AD pathology. 1 APOE ε4 is part of direct‐to‐consumer tests to foretell future AD risk, 2 a component of some AD blood test algorithms, 3 , 4 a critical aspect of risk/benefit discussions for new anti‐amyloid drugs due to the higher risk of brain swelling and bleeding in APOE ε4 carriers, 5 , 6 and used for AD clinical trial eligibility criteria. 7 , 8

Despite the growth and promise of APOE ε4‐based AD personalized medicine, converging evidence suggests that ancestry and environmental risk factors heavily impact the APOE ε4–AD relationship. 9 , 10 , 11 Notwithstanding the major limitations of race/ethnicity in approximating genetic ancestry, 12 important race/ethnic group differences exist for the APOE ε4–AD relationship: East Asian (mostly Japanese and Korean) APOE ε4 homozygotes are at approximately 20 times the risk of developing AD compared to Hispanic (mostly Caribbean Hispanic) APOE ε4 homozygotes, 9 , 10 the latter having the lowest known APOE ε4 –AD risk. 9 American Indian (AI) populations have the highest estimated rates of AD and related dementias (ADRD). 13 A growing body of literature exists regarding the role of APOE ε4 in driving AD risk in AI populations. Prior work in the Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma and Cherokee Nation demonstrated that APOE ε4 carriers, particularly those with high degrees of AI ancestry, had no discernible increased risk of clinically suspected AD, although sample sizes were relatively small. 14 , 15 More recent data from the largest AI biomedical study, the Strong Heart Study, which includes AI from the Northern and Southern Plains and Southwest United States, demonstrated null effects of APOE ε4 on neurocognitive testing and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) measures of medial temporal lobe atrophy or vascular brain injury. 16 , 17 The Strong Heart Study also demonstrated that APOE ε4 was associated with lower plasma Aβ42/40 but without a statistically significant increase in plasma phosphorylated tau at threonine 181 (p‐tau181). 18 Prior studies demonstrated variability in the relationship between APOE ε4 and AD among populations with substantial Native American ancestry from Central and South America. 19 This variability, together with the substantial genetic heterogeneity and population substructure observed across Indigenous populations, 20 , 21 raises uncertainty regarding the generalizability of whether the attenuation of APOE ε4 on plasma AD p‐tau extends to AI populations based in Minnesota.

METHODOLOGY

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents

All participants (or their legally authorized representative if cognitive loss was to an extent that precluded informed consent) provided written informed consent to participate in this cross‐sectional study. Participant capacity to provide informed consent was assessed by trained study personnel using procedures adapted from the MacArthur Competence Assessment Tool for Clinical Research (MacCAT‐CR), 22 , 23 including evaluation of the participants’ ability to express a choice, demonstrate understanding, reason about participation, and appreciate the potential risks and benefits of the study. All participants determined to lack decisional capacity provided assent, and informed consent was obtained from a legally authorized representative according to Institutional Review Board (IRB)‐approved procedures.

The Bois Forte Band of Chippewa partnered with the research team, approved this work through a signed, unanimously agreed‐on tribal resolution, and approved the publication and public dissemination of this article. The University of Minnesota IRB also approved this study.

Participants and study procedures

Participants were recruited and evaluated on the same day primarily via walk‐ins at our study sites located at the Health Services Center in Nett Lake or Lake Vermilion, Minnesota, or at the Bois Forte Band of Chippewa Urban Office in Minneapolis, Minnesota. Participants lived in the surrounding Minneapolis—St. Paul Metro Area for the Minneapolis site and on the Bois Forte Band of Chippewa Tribal Nation, or in the surrounding rural areas for the Nett Lake and Lake Vermilion, Minnesota sites. The study was advertised via flyers posted on local bulletin boards and social media, health fair booths and presentations, the study team's website, and tribal radio advertisements. These advertisements noted that researchers hoped to include AI participants in a study of cognitive aging. Other than walk‐ins, participants could also contact the study team via telephone to request a specific study visit date. Participants had to meet the following criteria to be eligible for the study: (1) currently enrolled member of a federally recognized AI Tribal Nation, (2) aged > 54 years, (3) speak English fluently, and (4) have no unstable medical conditions requiring frequent hospitalization.

Each participant underwent a comprehensive neurological examination with the study's subspecialty‐trained behavioral neurologist (W.G.M.) or a behavioral neurology specialty‐trained nurse practitioner (M.M.). The study collected demographic information, including each participant's age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and percent Native ancestry. Percent Native ancestry was based on self‐reported “blood quantum,” a flawed but rigorously kept historical record of AI ancestry dating back to the 18th and 19th centuries that has major implications for treaty rights, self‐identity, and tribal enrollment. 24 , 25 All participants were asked whether they had high cholesterol, diabetes, high blood pressure, or heart disease. For those who provided a blood sample, we obtained hemoglobin A1C (HbA1C) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).

ADNI data

To contextualize AI results with a non‐Hispanic White (NHW) cohort, data of NHW participants used in the preparation of this article were obtained from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database (adni.loni.usc.edu). ADNI was launched in 2003 as a public–private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD. The primary goal of ADNI has been to test whether serial MRI, positron emission tomography (PET), other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early Alzheimer's disease (AD).

To assemble a NHW comparison cohort with data available for clinical, APOE ε4 genotype, and AD plasma biomarkers Aβ42/40, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and p‐tau217 on June 30, 2025, we downloaded all data from the ADNI database under the filename UPENN_PLASMA_FUJIREBIO_QUANTERIX. Plasma p‐tau217 and Aβ42/40 in the ADNI cohort were measuring using the Fujirebio Lumipulse platform, whereas plasma GFAP included in this manuscript was measured using the single molecule array (Simoa) Quanterix HD‐X platform. We then filtered all available records for NHW participants who had MMSE≥24 – a cut‐off used in an attempt to approximately match cognitive status across the AI and NHW cohort – and available APOE ε4 genotype. Serum creatinine was obtained from the records available in the table with the filename All_Subjects_LABDATA, and estimated glumerular filtration rate (eGFR; measured as mL/min/1.73 m2) for both ADNI and AI participants, was calculated based on the 2021 CKD‐EPI Creatinine Equation. 26

Plasma AD biomarkers for AI cohort

We employed the same pre‐analytical methodology as the National Centralized Repository for Alzheimer's Disease (NCRAD), which involves venipuncture with an EDTA tube, centrifugation at 3000 rpm, aliquoting 2 mL of plasma into a polypropylene cryovial, and deep freezing in a −80°C freezer within 1 h of sample collection. 27 We used the same catalog numbers for EDTA tubes, aliquots, canonical vials, and cryovials as NCRAD. All samples underwent one freeze‐thaw cycle prior to analysis in the present report.

Plasma samples were analyzed using the Quanterix Simoa HD‐X system. Samples were brought out of the freezer, thawed at room temperature, and mixed well, and 80 µL of plasma per sample was added to a 96‐well plate. The samples were automatically diluted in Quanterix sample diluent, three times for the p‐tau 217 assay and four times for the other assays, then run by the HD‐X instrument. P‐tau217 was measured using the ALZpath assay. 28 Aβ42/40 and GFAP were measured using the Quanterix Neurology 4‐Plex E Advantage kit. Each kit contains calibration curves and controls for proteins to be analyzed. Calibrator curves and controls were analyzed to ensure assay quality. Coefficients of variation for plasma p‐tau 217, Aβ42/40, and GFAP for each of our participants in the pilot project were all below our quality control cut‐off of 10%.

APOE genotyping

APOE genotyping was performed using standard procedures as described by Hixson and Vernier. 29 In brief, genomic DNA was amplified by polymerase chain reaction, followed by restriction digestion with Hhal. Resultant fragments were separated on a high‐resolution agarose gel.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using MATLAB R2020 (Natick, MA, USA). Between‐group differences were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test or chi‐squared test. Tests with p < 0.05 identified variables that demonstrated statistically significant between‐group differences. Linear regression models with log‐transformed outcomes were fit to estimate the association between APOE ε4 and the plasma biomarker outcome of interest. Regression estimates were exponentiated to report results as geometric mean ratios. The plasma biomarkers of interest were p‐tau217, GFAP, or Aβ42/40. Models were fit with APOE ε4 modeled as either the number of APOE ε4 alleles: zero (genotypes: ε2/ε2, ε2/ε3, and ε3/ε3), one (ε2/ε4, ε3/ε4), or two (ε4/ε4) or the binary APOE ε4 carrier status. Our primary model adjusted for age, sex, and chronic kidney disease (CKD; defined as eGFR < 60). Additional models were fit with continuous eGFR or Aβ42/40 covariates. The outcomes of interest were log transformed due to the skewed distributions of plasma biomarkers. The regression models were estimated separately on NHW (ADNI) and AI cohorts.

Post hoc analyses of the AI cohort assessed whether adjustment for additional covariates, including hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), ischemic heart disease (IHD), stroke, time from clinical evaluation to blood draw, and BMI, impacted the estimated associations between APOE ε4 and the outcome measures.

The participant flowchart ending in the final AI cohort is presented in Figure 1. One hundred eighty‐two participants completed the behavioral neurology evaluation, AD plasma biomarkers, and APOE genotyping (one of these participants’ p‐tau217, however, could not be calculated). The mean time between the behavioral neurology evaluation and blood draw was 8.67 ± 0.49 months. In the ADNI cohort, we identified baseline records for 592 NHW participants with MMSE ≥ 24, a cut‐off chosen to maximize comparability with the AI cohort, as this was the lower range of MMSE in the AI cohort. Two hundred ten of these individuals had available records in the ADNI LABDATA table to calculate eGFR. Because of the strong effect of CKD on AD plasma biomarkers, we restricted the ADNI analysis to the 210 who had eGFR available. Effects of demographics and comorbidities on AD plasma biomarkers

Aging was positively associated with higher p‐tau217 and GFAP in both cohorts and inversely associated with Aβ42/40 in the AI cohort (Figure 2, Tables 2 and 3). Male sex had an inverse relationship with GFAP in AI (narrowly missing statistical significance in NHW participants) (Table 3). Aβ42/40 had an inverse relationship with p‐tau217 in the NHW cohort (Tables 2 and 3). CKD was associated with increased p‐tau217 in both races (GFAP narrowly missing statistical significance) (Figure 2, Tables 2 and 3). Results remained consistent when using continuous eGFR in place of CKD binarization (Table S2).

In the AI cohort, we observed that a history of IHD was associated with increased plasma GFAP (Table S3). BMI was not associated with AD plasma biomarkers (Table S4). Higher AI ancestry was not associated with differences in p‐tau217, corrected for age and sex (geometric mean ratio with 95% confidence interval for ancestry*APOE ε4 interaction term was 1.00 [0.92; 1.11]).

DISCUSSION

This study, conducted in rural and urban areas of Minnesota, aimed to measure the association between APOE ε4 and AD blood‐based biomarkers, namely, Aβ42/40, p‐tau 217, and GFAP, in a community‐partnered study of AI tribal elders. Our primary finding was that, in AI tribal elders, APOE ε4 had an attenuated association with plasma p‐tau 217 or GFAP. This contrasts with both our NHW comparator cohort from ADNI and prior published work, where APOE ε4 followed the expected pattern of pathologically affecting plasma p‐tau 217 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 and GFAP, 35 , 36 , 37 although the latter is less reproducibly demonstrated. 33 , 38 Our observation that AI have an attenuated rise in p‐tau in the presence of APOE ε4 parallels AI populations included in the Strong Heart Study, 18 along with other more well‐studied racial minorities in the medical literature: African American APOE ε4 carriers had either an attenuated or non‐existent increase in amyloid PET 39 – which closely mirrors p‐tau – or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) p‐tau, 40 respectively, compared to NHWs.

An unexpected finding was that, among our AI participants, we did not observe a relationship between plasma Aβ42/40 and p‐tau217 or GFAP, whereas this relationship was evident in our ADNI comparator cohort. Caution is warranted when interpreting this result given that the AI cohort Aβ measurements stem from the Neurology 4‐Plex Tool Kit assay, which has a low overall accuracy, with an area under the curve of approximately 0.65 when benchmarked to Aβ‐PET. 41 We note, however, that our p‐tau and GFAP results stem from intercomparable assays: We used the ALZpath p‐tau 217 assay, which is equally accurate as the p‐tau217 immunoassay used in our ADNI comparator cohort on the Fujirebio Lumipulse system, 42 , 43 and both cohorts measured GFAP using a Simoa HD‐X platform. While this Aβ–p‐tau/GFAP disconnect is viewed as exploratory, it is consistent with the Strong Heart Study's observation that APOE ε4 was associated with statistically significant lowering of Aβ42/40 but not p‐tau181. 18 It also parallels the pattern observed in African American participants, where lower CSF levels of p‐tau are observed in African Americans compared to NHW even when CSF Aβ levels are similar. 40 , 44

Several explanations for this race/ethnicity APOE ε4–AD relationship have been proposed: (1) local genetic ancestry near the APOE ε4 locus: Caribbean Hispanic individuals with local African ancestry have a substantially attenuated AD risk, but if the local ancestry is European, the risk is at the full European level 45 ; (2) enrichment in microglial genetic variants that modify AD biology: African Americans have five times the rate of a TREM2 mutation that results in lower levels of soluble TREM2, which results in lower p‐tau levels despite similar Aβ42/40 levels 44 ; microglial activation (or lack thereof) as the basis for uncoupling of Aβ with downstream GFAP and/or p‐tau has also been supported in NHW participants 46 , 47 ; (3) race, while a poor surrogate for ancestry, 12 is associated with differential social determinants of health that alter AD and/or APOE ε4 biology 11 , 48 ; and (4) the interpretation of AD plasma biomarkers is confounded by peripheral/systemic conditions associated with social determinants of health, such as IHD, 31 systemic amyloidosis, 49 or chronic kidney disease, 31 the latter having a more complex relationship to AD plasma biomarkers than can be explained by eGFR alone, such as proteinuria in the setting of normal eGFR, which can theoretically result in lower plasma p‐tau due to renal protein loss. 50 Understanding the disconnect between lower AD biomarker positivity but higher rates of ADRD in AI and other minority populations is crucial to developing broadly effective ADRD diagnostics and therapeutics.

This study has strengths and limitations. First, the inclusion, recruitment, and retention of AI participants requires extensive community outreach/engagement, and for many AI participants, participation in research is difficult due to competing life pressures, unfamiliarity with or distrust of biomedical study teams, lack of a reliable means of communication, and difficult‐to‐predict schedules. A strength of our study was that it was conducted on tribal land, with close community advisory board oversight/feedback, tribal council approval, and with members of the study team who were enrolled members of the participating tribal nation. While all these factors facilitated comfort, accessibility, and cultural competence to enrolled and evaluated participants, one major problem was that the blood draw occurred on a different day, which resulted in a significant number of participants being lost to follow‐up, particularly in our urban setting, where participants live across the entire metro area. Thus, our final population sample likely suffers from survival bias. Highlighting this limitation is that 12.8% of consented participants were homeless, whereas only 6.6% of participants who were able to return later for the blood draw were homeless. Furthermore, the APOE ε4 carriers had lower rates of CKD than non‐carriers, which could have indicated that we were studying resilient APOE ε4 carriers. Another limitation is the argument that our sample size is too small or too young to discern an effect of APOE ε4 on AD plasma biomarkers: Despite our attempt to control for age in our regression models, our participants were younger than the ADNI comparator cohort, meaning that they could have abnormal Aβ42/40 without yet developing abnormal p‐tau. 51 We note, however, that prior published work including NHW participants with comparable levels of cognition and even younger ages 30 , 32 and/or lower sample sizes 30 , 33 , 34 still demonstrated increased levels of plasma p‐tau and/or GFAP in association with APOE ε 4. Another key limitation of this study is that the AI cohort was recruited through community‐based methods, whereas the NHW ADNI cohort was largely derived from academic, clinic‐based populations, which tend to be healthier and more selectively enrolled. Accordingly, the observed race‐associated differences in plasma p‐tau217 may reflect residual confounding related to recruitment strategy, unmeasured cardiovascular conditions – past medical history was obtained according to participant self‐report – and other underlying social determinants of health. In particular, while the AI and NHW ADNI cohorts had similar MMSE scores, cognitive test performance is not fully comparable across groups that differ in educational background, socioeconomic status, and cultural context, and thus the groups may not be ideally matched on underlying cognitive status. 52 , 53 Prior work demonstrated that standard cognitive measures, including the MMSE, are influenced by education, literacy, and cultural factors. 52 , 53 However, the MMSE remains a widely used screening tool in AD research, 54 facilitating cross‐study comparisons. Its use in this study was guided by community input prioritizing culturally sensitive approaches and minimizing potential distress associated with more extensive neuropsychological testing.

CONCLUSION

Despite a possible lower (more pathological) level of Aβ42/40 associated with APOE ε4, this allele has no observable relationship with GFAP or p‐tau 217 in AI tribal elders. While the results of the current study cannot generalize across all AI populations due to significant inter‐ and intra‐tribal heterogeneity, our study extends and parallels findings from AI elders recruited in the Northern Plains, Southern Plains, and Southwest, where APOE ε4 has an attenuated association with neuropsychological test scores, MRI measures of medial temporal lobe atrophy and cerebrovascular disease, and plasma p‐tau. 16 , 17 , 18 This growing body of evidence suggests that the effect of APOE ε4 on AD is attenuated in AI or, alternatively, that unmeasured residual confounders associated with AI race obfuscate the APOE ε4–AD link. A major clinical implication is that a “one‐size‐fits‐all” model of APOE ε4 in AI is problematic, which compels future research to gain insight into the race‐associated social determinants of health and/or unique genetic risk/protective factors that influence these observations.
