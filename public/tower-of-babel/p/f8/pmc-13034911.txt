Association between climate change awareness and psychological distress among Turkish pregnant women: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Yakup Baykuş, Rulin Deniz, Alihan Tiğli, Dilek Aydin, Nazli Şener, Yasemin Ercan Değirmenci, Güzide Ece Akinci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate change is increasingly recognized as a major global health threat, disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations such as pregnant women. However, limited research has examined its psychological impacts on this group. This study aimed to examine whether higher climate change awareness is associated with increased symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress among pregnant women in Türkiye. A cross-sectional study was conducted between April 2025 and June 2025 with 267 pregnant women attending routine obstetric care at a public hospital. Data were collected using a personal information form, the Pregnancy-Specific Climate Change Awareness Scale (PS-CCAS), and the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale. Descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation, and multiple linear regression analyses were used to assess relationships between variables. The mean total PS-CCAS score was 66.81 ± 20.97. Although no significant association was observed with depression, both climate change awareness (PS-CCAS) and climate change knowledge, assessed as a separate variable, emerged as significant predictors of anxiety and stress. Depression was more common among participants with lower education, longer marriage duration, urban residence, and pregnancy-related illness. Anxiety and stress were significantly linked to urban living and pregnancy-related illness, while stress was additionally associated with lower income. These findings highlight the need for targeted, psychologically safe climate education programs during pregnancy. Sociodemographic factors such as education level, income, and urban living should be considered when designing perinatal mental health interventions in the context of climate change.

1. Introduction

Climate change is the greatest global health challenge of the 21st century and poses a threat to every aspect of the society we live in.[1] It gives rise to a wide range of environmental issues such as heatwaves, air pollution, floods, wildfires, storms, vector-borne diseases, and other extreme weather events – whose frequency and severity are expected to increase further in the coming years.[2] This global crisis can affect human health both directly and indirectly, particularly among vulnerable populations such as pregnant women and newborns.[3,4] Pregnancy is one of the most sensitive periods of life, during which both the mother and the developing fetus become more vulnerable to environmental stressors.[5] Exposure to environmental degradation during this period can pose both short- and long-term health risks, potentially leading to lasting effects across generations.[4,6]

The literature highlights that climate change has been linked to a wide range of adverse pregnancy outcomes, including premature rupture of membranes,[7] congenital anomalies,[8] preeclampsia,[9] gestational diabetes,[10] gestational hypertension,[11] preterm birth, low birth weight, and stillbirth.[12] In addition, extreme weather events and natural disasters can create serious risks, ranging from limited access to safe food and clean water to disruptions in access to healthcare services, and may even increase maternal and perinatal mortality rates.[4,13] Furthermore, climate change has also been associated with various mental health outcomes in pregnant women, including depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder.[14] Therefore, raising awareness is essential to reducing climate-related health risks during pregnancy.[15]

Climate change awareness is commonly conceptualized as encompassing both knowledge of current and future environmental threats and perceptions of these threats.[16] A higher level of awareness may positively influence knowledge, attitudes, and preventive behaviors.[17] However, it can also intensify psychological distress and hopelessness, particularly among vulnerable populations.[18,19] This issue is especially critical in pregnant women, who are physiologically and psychologically more sensitive to environmental threats. Despite this relevance, the literature on climate change awareness in pregnant populations remains scarce,[17,20] and its potential mental health consequences have been largely overlooked. Investigating the relationship between climate change awareness and mental health during pregnancy – a period marked by intense hormonal and emotional changes – is essential for informing both clinical interventions and public mental health policies.[13] Evidence generated from such research may support the development of hope-centered, resilience-enhancing awareness programs that promote adaptive and protective behaviors in this vulnerable group while also guiding broader climate-sensitive health strategies.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This cross-sectional study was carried out at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Outpatient Clinic of Bandirma Training and Research Hospital in Türkiye, between April and June 2025. The study followed the guidelines of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement.

2.2. Sample

The study population consisted of pregnant women who presented for routine checkups at the obstetrics and gynecology outpatient clinic. The minimum required sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1.9.2 (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany) statistical software,[21] based on a multiple linear regression. The analysis was conducted using the following parameters: a significance level of 0.05, an effect size (f2) of 0.15 (medium), a statistical power of 0.80, and the number of independent variables included in the model. As a result, a minimum of 125 participants was deemed necessary. The final sample consisted of 267 pregnant women who met the inclusion criteria and were consecutively recruited during routine obstetric visits. The inclusion criteria were as follows: being pregnant, being aged 18 years or older, being literate, being able to speak and understand Turkish, and volunteering to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were defined as the presence of a high-risk pregnancy (e.g., placenta previa or abruption, preeclampsia/eclampsia, severe polyhydramnios/oligohydramnios) and a clinically diagnosed serious mental disorder (e.g., schizophrenia, bipolar disorder).

2.3. Measures

Data for the study were collected through the personal information form, the Pregnancy-Specific Climate Change Awareness Scale (PS-CCAS), and the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21).

2.3.1. Personal information form. The form was developed by the researchers based on the relevant literature.[17,22–24] It consists of 17 items assessing the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics (age, education level, marital duration, employment status, family structure, income level, and place of residence), obstetric characteristics and history (gravidity, trimester, number of living children, and history of abortion, miscarriage, preterm birth, and stillbirth), physical and pregnancy-related health conditions, as well as prior knowledge of climate change (yes/no).

2.3.2. Pregnancy-Specific Climate Change Awareness Scale. The PS-CCAS is a 21-item instrument developed by Kisrik in 2022 to assess pregnant women’s awareness of the impacts of climate change on maternal and fetal health. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). It comprises 3 subscales: general, fetal, and maternal. Scores obtained from each subscale reflect the level of awareness in that specific domain. The total score ranges from 21 to 105, with higher scores indicating greater awareness of climate change. The overall Cronbach alpha coefficient of the original scale was reported as 0.946,[25] while in the current study, it was calculated as 0.954.

2.3.3. Depression Anxiety Stress Scale. The DASS-21 is a self-report instrument developed by Lovibond and Lovibond in 1995 to assess the severity of psychological symptoms.[26] The psychometric properties of the Turkish version were evaluated by Sariçam.[27] The scale consists of 21 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 3 (almost always), and includes 3 subscales: depression, anxiety, and stress. Each subscale yields a score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity in the respective domain.[26,27] The recommended cutoff scores for conventional severity classifications (normal, mild, moderate, severe, extremely severe) are as follows: depression – normal: 0 to 4, mild: 5 to 6, moderate: 7 to 10, severe: 11 to 13, extremely severe: 14+; anxiety – normal: 0 to 3, mild: 4, moderate: 5 to 7, severe: 8 to 9, extremely severe: 10+; and stress – normal: 0 to 7, mild: 8 to 9, moderate: 10 to 12, severe: 13 to 16, extremely severe: 17+.[26] In Turkish clinical samples, Cronbach alpha reliability coefficients were reported as 0.87 for the depression subscale, 0.85 for anxiety, and 0.81 for stress.[27] In the present study, Cronbach alpha values were calculated as 0.88 for depression, 0.85 for anxiety, and 0.89 for stress.

2.4. Data collection process

Pregnant women who met the inclusion criteria were informed about the purpose and nature of the study and provided written informed consent. Data were then collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by the researchers. Each participant completed the forms in approximately 15 to 20 minutes under the supervision of a researcher in a designated interview room within the obstetrics and gynecology outpatient clinic.

2.5. Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Bandirma Onyedi Eylül University Health Sciences Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee (IRB approval number: March 10, 2025/ 35). In addition, institutional permission was obtained from the Chief Physician’s Office of Bandirma Training and Research Hospital. All eligible pregnant women were informed about the study and were assured that participation was entirely voluntary, with the right to decline or withdraw at any time without any consequences. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to data collection.

2.6. Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago). Cases with missing data were excluded from the analyses using listwise deletion. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive statistics were reported as frequencies (n), percentages (%), and means with standard deviations (M ± SD). Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to examine the relationships between climate change awareness and psychological distress. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to determine the effects of climate change awareness and sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics on depression, anxiety, and stress. All sociodemographic and obstetric variables, together with PS-CCAS total scores and climate change knowledge, were entered simultaneously into the regression models. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor, tolerance, and condition index; variables with a variance inflation factor < 10, tolerance > 0.2, and condition index < 30 were retained, and all values were within acceptable limits, indicating no evidence of multicollinearity. The explanatory power of the regression models was evaluated using adjusted R-squared (Adj. R2). Statistical significance was set at P < .05.[28]

3. Results

3.1. Sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics of pregnant women

The mean age of the pregnant women was 28.47 years. Among the participants, 44.6% were university graduates, 63.3% had been married for 5 years, 71.5% were housewives, and 89.9% lived in a nuclear family structure. In addition, 70.8% reported a moderate income level, and 72.7% resided in urban areas. In terms of obstetric characteristics, 43.4% were experiencing their first pregnancy, 62.9% were in the third trimester, and 48.7% had no children. Regarding obstetric history, 13.5% had a history of abortion, 14.6% of miscarriage, 5.6% of preterm birth, and 3.4% of stillbirth. Furthermore, 16.5% stated that they had a physical illness, while 8.2% reported a pregnancy-related health condition. The proportion of participants who reported having knowledge about climate change was 35.2%.

3.2. Climate change awareness and psychological distress among pregnant women

Pregnant women had a total PS-CCAS score of 66.81 ± 20.97. The mean scores for the PS-CCAS general awareness, maternal awareness, and fetal awareness subscales were 18.85 ± 6.63, 25.29 ± 7.90, and 22.67 ± 8.83, respectively. In terms of psychological distress, the mean scores on the DASS-21 subscales were 3.73 ± 3.15 for depression, 6.16 ± 4.45 for anxiety, and 5.87 ± 4.53 for stress (Table 1).

3.3. Relationship between climate change awareness and psychological distress

Pearson correlation analysis revealed a weak positive correlation between climate change awareness and depression; however, this association was not statistically significant (r = 0.080, P = .193). In contrast, moderate and statistically significant positive correlations were observed between climate change awareness and both anxiety (r = 0.310, P < .001) and stress (r = 0.312, P < .001; Table 2).

3.4. Predictors of psychological distress among pregnant women

The regression model significantly predicted depression (Adj. R2 = 0.307, P < .001). Lower education level (β = −0.157, P = .020), longer duration of marriage (β = 0.142, P = .023), urban residence (β = 0.141, P = .022), and pregnancy-related illness (β = 0.161, P = .007) emerged as significant predictors of depression. In contrast, climate change awareness (β = 0.045, P = .508) and climate change knowledge (β = 0.044, P = .490) were not significantly associated with depressive symptoms (Table 3).

4. Discussion

Pregnancy represents a natural yet multifaceted stage in a woman’s life, during which substantial social, biological, and psychological changes may heighten susceptibility to emotional disturbances such as depression, anxiety, and stress.[22,23] Although mental health is recognized as a critical component of reproductive well-being, it often remains overlooked, with psychological conditions during pregnancy frequently underdiagnosed and undertreated.[29]

In the present study, which investigated the relationship between climate change awareness and psychological distress, 20.6%, 42.3%, and 25.8% of pregnant women reported symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress, respectively – ranging from mild to extremely severe. These rates appear higher in certain domains compared with previous studies using the DASS-21 screening tool, which have reported depression prevalence ranging from 6.9% to 42.8%, anxiety from 18.8% to 60.3%, and stress from 4.2% to 59.5%.[22,23,30–32] The variability observed in the prevalence of psychological distress across studies may reflect contextual factors such as individual differences, sociocultural and economic conditions, and environmental exposures. These findings underscore the importance of routine screening and culturally sensitive interventions to effectively address perinatal psychological distress.[22,30]

Climate change is increasingly recognized as a major environmental risk factor affecting both maternal and fetal health outcomes.[4,13] Nevertheless, research examining climate change awareness specifically among pregnant women remains scarce. The current study revealed low levels of awareness, consistent with the limited number of existing studies in this area.[17,20] Importantly, higher climate change awareness and knowledge were significantly associated with increased anxiety and stress levels, whereas no significant association was found with depression. This distinction is critical, as it indicates an associational – rather than causal – relationship, whereby increased awareness co-occurs with heightened psychological distress rather than directly causing it. To date, no study has explicitly explored this relationship within pregnant populations, highlighting a clear gap in the literature. However, findings from other populations support this association. For example, 1 study involving young individuals reported a significant relationship between climate change awareness and both anxiety and hopelessness.[18] Similarly, among individuals with preexisting mental health conditions, greater climate change awareness has been linked to elevated symptoms of anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder.[19] Although the conceptual understanding of climate change awareness and its mental health implications is still evolving, emerging climate-specific mental health literature suggests that heightened awareness may amplify anticipatory threat perception and emotional arousal, particularly in vulnerable groups.[6,19] The absence of a significant association between climate change awareness and depression in the present study warrants particular attention. Unlike anxiety and stress – which are often more reactive to immediate or perceived threats – depression may be more closely related to chronic, cumulative, and structural factors, such as long-term socioeconomic disadvantage or persistent relational stressors.[27] This distinction may help explain why climate-related awareness appears to influence anxiety and stress more acutely than depressive symptoms during pregnancy.

In this study, lower education was found to be significantly associated only with depression. This finding aligns with previous research reporting similar patterns among pregnant women.[33] However, the literature remains mixed, with some studies identifying associations between education and anxiety or stress,[23,34] and others reporting no significant relationships.[31,35] Such inconsistencies may reflect differences in sample characteristics, cultural context, socioeconomic conditions, or temporal factors, including data collection during periods of heightened societal stress such as the COVID-19 pandemic. These findings further underscore that psychological distress during pregnancy is shaped by interacting rather than isolated factors.

Similarly, in the present study, a significant association was observed only between lower income and stress, which aligns with some previous research.[33] Nonetheless, other studies have reported relationships between economic status and depression or anxiety,[23,31] while some have found no association with any psychological outcomes.[32] These results may suggest that financial hardship is more likely to trigger acute psychological responses such as stress, particularly those rooted in uncertainty about the future.[36] Another noteworthy finding of the current study was the positive association between the duration of marriage and depression. It is plausible that long-term marriages may involve cumulative stressors such as communication breakdowns, role conflicts, diminished spousal support, or relationship monotony, that can contribute to heightened psychological vulnerability during pregnancy.[23,33] Overall, these findings suggest that the influence of individual factors on mental health is neither fixed nor unidirectional. Future studies should incorporate mediating and moderating variables to gain a more comprehensive understanding of how factors such as education, economic status, and marriage duration shape psychological outcomes.

In this study, residing in an urban area was identified as a consistent and significant predictor of depression, anxiety, and stress. This finding underscores the complex and multifactorial impact of urban living on mental health. Urban settings frequently expose individuals to persistent stressors such as high population density, environmental pollution, noise, traffic congestion, social isolation, and elevated living costs.[37] During pregnancy – a period marked by heightened physical and emotional vulnerability – such environmental challenges may exacerbate psychological distress and overwhelm coping mechanisms. Supporting this observation, prior research has similarly found a positive link between urban residency and psychological distress.[38,39] In light of these findings, it is crucial to expand access to psychosocial support systems for pregnant women in urban areas, implement interventions to reduce environmental stressors, and promote protective factors such as contact with nature.[40]

The study also revealed that pregnant women with pregnancy-related health conditions exhibited significantly higher levels of depression, anxiety, and stress. These results suggest that such complications contribute not only to physiological strain but also to considerable psychological burden. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have reported associations between physical complications during pregnancy and increased psychological distress.[23,39] This emphasizes the importance of integrating routine mental health screening and psychosocial support services, particularly in the management of high-risk pregnancies.[41]

5. Limitations

Although this study is among the first to examine the relationship between climate change awareness and psychological distress specifically in pregnant women, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow for causal inferences between climate change awareness and psychological symptoms. Second, the data were collected from a single public hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or healthcare settings. Third, self-reported measures may be subject to response biases, such as social desirability and recall bias. Finally, while the PS-CCAS is a promising tool, further validation in diverse populations and longitudinal contexts is recommended. Despite these limitations, the study provides novel insights into the intersection of climate change awareness and perinatal mental health.

6. Conclusions

This study adds to the limited evidence on the psychological correlates of climate change awareness during pregnancy. Overall, the findings indicate that climate change awareness and knowledge are linked to elevated anxiety and stress – but not depression – among pregnant women, alongside sociodemographic and obstetric factors that also shape psychological vulnerability. Rather than implying causality, these results highlight the relevance of climate-related concerns to perinatal mental health, particularly for stress- and anxiety-related responses. Importantly, the findings suggest that climate change awareness represents a modifiable contextual factor that may coincide with heightened psychological distress during pregnancy. This underscores the need for psychologically safe, nonalarmist climate communication within perinatal care settings – approaches that inform and empower without amplifying fear or uncertainty. From a public health perspective, integrating balanced climate-related content into perinatal mental health frameworks may help address emerging climate-related distress among vulnerable groups, including pregnant women. Future research employing longitudinal designs and more diverse samples is warranted to clarify temporal pathways and potential causal mechanisms. Furthermore, examining mediating and moderating factors – such as health literacy, coping strategies, and social support – may deepen understanding of how climate change awareness translates into psychological outcomes during pregnancy and inform the development of targeted, supportive interventions.
