Biocatalytic kinetic resolution for efficient and enantioselective synthesis of δ-haloalcohols and tetrahydrofurans
Authors: Yuan-Fei Wang, Xia Hua, Zhang-Yang Wang, Xiao Jin, Hui-Hui Wang, Yong-Zheng Chen, Nan-Wei Wan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Expanding biocatalytic reactions to access previously inaccessible chemical transformations is of significant demand. Halohydrin dehalogenases represent a class of remarkably versatile biocatalysts that have been successfully explored in the asymmetric synthesis and transformation of epoxides as well as oxetanes. In this study, we extended their catalytic capabilities to establish a biocatalytic approach for the kinetic resolution of δ-haloalcohols. This approach enables the highly efficient and enantiocomplementary synthesis of diverse chiral δ-haloalcohols and tetrahydrofurans with good isolated yields (up to 50%) and high optical purities (up to >99% ee) on a preparative scale. These molecules are of significant interest in both synthetic and pharmaceutical chemistry. Remarkably, this biocatalytic method tolerates high substrate concentration up to 185 g/L, underscoring its synthetic potential for industrial applications. Furthermore, we propose a potential reaction mechanism based on a comprehensive analysis of various mutants and docking studies.

Introduction

Biocatalysis has provided a universal and powerful approach for the synthesis of various chemicals due to its high efficiency and exquisite selectivity in terms of chemo-, regio-, and stereoselectivity, as well as its inherent ability to be performed under environmentally friendly conditions1,2. Compared to the developed chemical reactions, however, known enzyme-catalyzed reactions are quite rare, significantly limiting the development and potential applications of biocatalytic reactions. Undoubtedly, developing new enzymatic reactions to expand biocatalytic approaches remains a challenging topic in this field. Thanks to the effective protein engineering strategies and the in-depth understanding of enzyme structure and functionalities, researchers have become committed to exploring new-to-nature enzymatic reactions3. Notable advances in this field have led to a remarkable expansion of abiological enzymatic transformations, achieved by using established active sites for new enzymatic functions4–7, taming metalloenzymes for non-native reactions8–11, capitalizing on the catalytic potentials of organocofactors12–18, and creating new enzymes with artificial cofactors19–27. Although natural evolution has honed active sites to optimally orient substrates with respect to catalytic groups and exclude competing substrates, the catalytic promiscuity allows enzymes to catalyze physiologically irrelevant chemical transformations28–30. By leveraging the inherent flexibility of enzyme active sites to accommodate and transform non-native substrates, researchers have been able to design and implement novel enzymatic reactions, broadening the scope of biocatalytic applications in synthetic chemistry31,32.

Halohydrin dehalogenase (HHDH), also known for its impressive catalytic promiscuity, belongs to the superfamily of short-chain dehydrogenases33. It is capable of not only catalyzing the formation of epoxides via the dehalogenation of β-haloalcohols but also mediating the transformation of epoxides into various β-substituted alcohols in the presence of several negatively charged nucleophiles (Fig. 1a), such as azide (N3−), cyanide (CN−), and cyanate (OCN−)34–36. Structural and mechanistic studies have revealed that HHDHs catalyze the formation and ring-opening of epoxides through respective intramolecular and intermolecular SN2 reaction mechanisms37,38. This dual functionality underscores the potential of HHDHs as versatile biocatalysts for facilitating a wide range of chemical reactions39–41, making them valuable tools in the synthesis of various chiral compounds including epoxides42–45, β-haloalcohols46–49, β-azidoalcohols50–56, β-hydroxynitriles57–59, and oxazolidinones60,61. Due to the scarcity of identified HHDHs for a long period of time, research on their synthetic applications had been limited to only a few available enzymes. In 2014, the Schallmey group developed an effective strategy for identifying novel HHDHs, significantly enriching the available enzyme resources63. This breakthrough has opened new avenues for exploring the catalytic promiscuity of HHDHs and has motivated our research to focus on broadening their biocatalytic utility in synthetic chemistry64–73. Recent studies by our group and Sun’s group have revealed that HHDH is capable of catalyzing the formation and ring-opening of oxetanes, providing a biocatalytic approach for highly efficient and enantioselective synthesis of oxetanes as well as a variety of chiral γ-substituted alcohols (Fig. 1b)74,75.

In this context, we envisioned extending the promiscuous activities of HHDHs for synthesizing chiral δ-haloalcohols and tetrahydrofurans, both of which are key structural motifs in pharmaceutical synthesis and natural products76–78. For instance, chiral 4-chloro-1-(4-fluorophenyl)butan-1-ol and 2-(4-bromophenyl)tetrahydrofuran serve as critical synthons for producing haloperidol metabolite II prodrug and adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activator, respectively (Fig. 1c)79,80. Given the significant utility of these compounds, several efficient synthetic methodologies have been successfully developed81,82. The ketoreductase-mediated enantioselective reduction of ketones to chiral alcohols is a well-established biocatalytic strategy, which has been demonstrated on an industrial scale in several cases83,84. However, the asymmetric synthesis of chiral δ-haloalcohols from ketone precursors via remote stereocontrol remains relatively underexplored85. In this study, we describe the development of a biocatalytic kinetic resolution method for the enantioselective synthesis of δ-haloalcohols as well as tetrahydrofurans with high yields and excellent optical purities (Fig. 1d).

Results

Screening of HHDHs

Our study commenced with the comprehensive screening of a panel of recombinant HHDHs using racemic 4-chloro-1-phenylbutan-1-ol (1a) as a model substrate. In the first stage, we tested 38 wild-type HHDHs encompassing members from groups A to G using whole-cell biotransformation (Supplementary Tables 1-2)63. The results indicated that most of these enzymes exhibited low catalytic activity, and only eight enzymes demonstrated >10% conversion (12–59%, Fig. 2a). Additionally, these active enzymes exhibited low to moderate enantioselectivity (E = 6–23; 1a: 11–97% ee, 1b: 62–89% ee), generating R or S enantiomers of 2-phenyltetrahydrofuran (1b) with 62–89% ee. Notably, the enzyme HheC that originated from strain Agrobacterium tumefaciens AD1 demonstrated the highest conversion of 59%, coupled with a relatively good enantioselectivity (E = 20; 1a: 97% ee, 1b: 67% ee). As a series of HheC mutants had been previously constructed and deposited in our laboratory, we subsequently evaluated 17 mutated enzymes which contained mutations in the enzyme active site of HheC-WT (Supplementary Table 3). Three mutants, HheC-SM4 (T134L), HheC-TM2 (F86V/T134V/N176G), and HheC-QM1 (P84V/F86P/T134A/N176A), which involved 1–4 mutations at four residues (P84, F86, T134, and N176, Fig. 2b), were identified with improved enantioselectivity. As shown in Fig. 2a, the single-mutant HheC-SM4 slightly enhanced the S-enantioselectivity compared to the wild-type HheC (HheC-WT), achieving the E value of 32 (1a: 43% ee, 1b: 91% ee). Interestingly, the triple-mutant HheC-TM2 was found to exhibit a higher but reversed R-enantioselectivity (E = 49; 1a: 57% ee, 1b: 93% ee). Encouragingly, the quadruple-mutant HheC-QM1 was identified as having dramatically improved catalytic activity and excellent R-enantioselectivity (E > 200; 1a: 92% ee, 1b: 99% ee). Remarkably, the HheC-QM1 mutant achieved 48% conversion of 20 mM (rac)-1a after just 15 min, resulting in the formed product (R)-1b with 99% ee and the remaining (S)-1a with 92% ee. Furthermore, an N-terminal 6×His tag was introduced into the HheC-QM1 to facilitate enzyme purification. Substrate scope for R-enantioselective dehalogenation of δ-haloalcohols

With the excellent R-enantioselective variant HheC-QM1 in hand, we explored the scope of this kinetic resolution methodology on a preparative scale using whole-cell biotransformation (Fig. 2c). Biotransformation of the model substrate 1a yielded (R)-1b with 44% yield, 98% ee, as well as (S)-1a with 50% yield, >99% ee. When the methyl substituent was at the ortho, meta, or para positions of the phenyl ring (2–4a), the reactions proceeded smoothly, demonstrating very good efficiency and enantioselectivity. In addition, the reaction also showed good compatibility with other δ-haloalcohols 5–9a with either electron-withdrawing or electron-donating substituents on the aromatic ring, affording chiral (R)-tetrahydrofurans and (S)-δ-haloalcohols with good isolated yields (42–50%) and high optical purities (86– > 99% ee). Interestingly, the presence of two substituents on the aromatic ring was also found to be compatible (10a). It is worth noting that the reaction tolerated the substrate containing a bulky naphthalene moiety (11a). Additionally, the α,α-disubstituted δ-chloroalcohol (12a) was successfully converted into the corresponding tetrahydrofuran (12b), albeit with low enantioselectivity. The observed low enantioselectivity toward α,α-disubstituted δ-chloroalcohol may be attributed to its steric hindrance effects. Remarkably, the heterocyclic substrate (13a) featuring a thiophene moiety underwent effective and enantioselective dehalogenation, yielding chiral tetrahydrofuran (R)-13b with 44% yield, 95% ee, and δ-chloroalcohol with (S)-13a 48% yield, 96% ee, respectively. Initial evaluation revealed that the HheC-QM1 mutant displayed limited catalytic efficiency (16% conversion) toward the alkyl-substituted δ-chloroalcohol 14a in analytical-scale reactions. Through testing additional mutants, we identified HheC-DM2 (T134I/N176A) as exhibiting significantly improved catalytic performance (52% conversion). Subsequent preparative-scale bioconversion of substrate 14a using HheC-DM2 successfully afforded the corresponding chiral tetrahydrofuran and δ-chloroalcohol in good yields with moderate enantiomeric purity. Furthermore, kinetic resolution of racemic δ-bromoalcohol 15a was also achieved with high catalytic efficiency and enantioselectivity.

Mechanism analysis

Based on previous insights on the mechanism of HheC for the substrate dehalogenation37, a potential reaction pathway for the conversion of δ-haloalcohol into tetrahydrofuran was depicted in Fig. 3a. In addition to the catalytic triad S132-Y145-R149, the halide binding site (HBS) and the R-group binding site (RBS) in the active cleft are also crucial in positioning the substrate in a productive conformation (Supplementary Fig. 2a). The HBS is primarily composed of the residues N176-Y177-L178-H179-S180 and Y187-F186, which interact with the haloalkyl side chain of the substrate, positioning the halide appropriately within the region. The N176A (SM1) and N176G (SM2) mutations result in the enzyme almost completely losing activity (Fig. 3b). T134 is spatially close to N176 (Supplementary Fig. 2b), and although it does not directly form part of the HBS, it influences substrate binding in the HBS through its interaction with N176. Additionally, the backbone (NH) and side-chain (OH) of T134 form two hydrogen bonds with the hydroxyl group of Ser132 (Supplementary Fig. 2b), which would influence the ability of S132 to bind the substrate. When T134 is mutated to a smaller alanine (T134A, SM3), the reaction activity is significantly enhanced, but the enantioselectivity is reduced (Fig. 3b). Conversely, when it is mutated to a larger leucine (T134L, SM4), the enantioselectivity of the enzyme is improved, but its reaction activity is decreased (Fig. 3b). Interestingly, introducing T134A and T134I mutations into the SM2 (N176G) and SM1 (N176A) mutants, respectively, significantly enhances their catalytic activity (DM1 vs SM2, DM2 vs SM1, Fig. 3b). However, introducing other mutations, such as T134L, T134S, and T134V, does not lead to a noticeable increase in enzyme activity (DM3-DM6 vs SM1-SM2, Fig. 3b). Moreover, by introducing the T134A mutation into SM1 (N176A), we constructed another double mutant, DM8 (T134A/N176A), which also significantly enhances catalytic activity (Fig. 3b). By comparing mutants DM8 (T134A/N176A) and SM3 (T134A), it can be observed that the introduction of the N176A mutation directly reversed reaction enantioselectivity of the SM3 mutant (Fig. 3b). On the other hand, the RBS is primarily composed of residues P84, F86, W139, F186, and W249 (from another chain of the tetramer). These residues point towards the internal active center and interact with the R substituent of the substrate, which is a phenyl ring in the case of the template substrate (1a). Mutating P84 to larger residues such as threonine (P84T, SM5) or aspartate (P84D, SM6) reduces the size of the active site, increasing steric hindrance with the substrate and consequently leading to decreased enzyme activity (Fig. 3b). Additionally, mutation of W249 to the smaller phenylalanine residue (SM7, W249F) likely weakens the aromatic interaction with the substrate, resulting in reduced enzyme activity (Fig. 3b). Through site-directed mutagenesis (SDM), we constructed three single-mutants, F86A (SM8), W139A (SM9), and F186A (SM10). These mutants showed significantly decreased activity and enantioselectivity compared to HheC-WT (Fig. 3b). In particular, the F186A mutation nearly abolished enzymatic activity. These effects are likely due to the weakened interactions with the aromatic ring (R group) of the substrate, leading to reduced binding affinity between the enzyme and the substrate. Interestingly, the mutant DM6 (T134V/N176G) exhibits relatively low activity, while the mutant TM2 (F86V/T134V/N176G) shows a significant improvement in catalytic activity and enantioselectivity compared to DM6 (Fig. 3b). Similarly, when we introduced F86P mutation into the DM2 (T134A/N176A) to create the mutant TM3 (F86P/T134A/N176A), there was also a dramatic enhancement in catalytic activity and stereoselectivity (Fig. 3b). The three mutations are present in the high-performance mutant QM1 (P84V/F86P/T134A/N176A) and likely contribute to its superior activity and enantioselectivity.

To structurally understand the control of the reaction enantioselectivity, we docked both (R)- and (S)-1a the into the active site of HheC-WT. Based on the proposed reaction mechanism, the potential poses of HheC-WT/(R)-1a and HheC-WT/(S)-1a were selected for further analysis. Overlaping the two poses indicated that the (R)- and (S)-1a share a similar conformation (Fig. 4a), consistent with the observation that both enantiomers can be effectively catalyzed by the HheC-WT. Further analysis revealed the hydrogen bond distances between (R)-1a and Ser132 and Y145 are 2.8 Å and 3.7 Å, respectively (Fig. 4b). The (S)-1a forms hydrogen bonds with Ser132 and Y145 at respective distances of 2.6 Å and 3.5 Å (Fig. 4c), both of which are shorter than those with (R)-1a. This suggests that (S)-1a may be more likely to occur compared to (R)-1a during the proton abstraction step (Fig. 3a). In addition, the distance between the hydroxyl oxygen (O) and Cδ in (R)-1a is 4.7 Å, while for (S)-1a, this distance is shorter, measuring 4.3 Å. This difference might make (S)-1a more likely to undergo the halide release process compared to (R)-1a (Fig. 3b). By superimposing the HheC-QM1/(R)-1a and HheC-WT/(R)-1a poses, it was revealed that (R)-1a occupies a similar position within the active site of both enzymes (Supplementary Fig. 4). Compared to the HheC-WT/(R)-1a complex, the distances of the hydrogen bonds between the (R)-1a and Ser132 and Y145 are shorter, providing potential evidence for its increased activity. The distances between the hydroxyl oxygen (O) and Cδ in (R)-1a are similar for HheC-QM1 (4.8 Å) and HheC-WT (4.7 Å). However, the orientation of the C–Cl bond of (R)-1a differs significantly between HheC-WT and HheC-QM1, pointing outward in HheC-WT and inward in HheC-QM1. Interestingly, the C–Cl bond of (S)-1a also points inward in HheC-WT, similar to the orientation of (R)-1a in HheC-QM1. The main-chain N–H groups of residues within the HBS region establish hydrogen bonds with the Cl atom of the halohydrin substrate, aiding in the cleavage of the C–Cl bond44. In the docking pose of HheC-WT/(R)-1a, the distances between the Cl atom of (R)-1a and the N–H groups of residues L178 and Y177 are 2.4 Å and 2.5 Å, respectively (Fig. 4b). These close N–H···Cl hydrogen bonds likely cause the C–Cl bond to orient outward. Conversely, for (S)-1a in HheC-WT, the corresponding distances are 3.0 Å and 4.2 Å, which may weaken the hydrogen-bonding interactions and result in the C–Cl bond orienting inward (Fig. 4c). This orientation phenomenon was similarly observed for (R)-1a in the mutant HheC-QM1, with distances measuring 3.2 Å and 4.7 Å, respectively (Fig. 4d). These results indicate that the positioning of the substrate’s C–Cl bond in the HBS region might affect its dehalogenation activity. Overall, the residues in the HBS and RBS regions work together to control the binding orientation of the δ-haloalcohol substrate within the active site. This control leads to the creation of either favorable or unfavorable reactive conformations, which in turn dictate the enzyme’s catalytic performance. The differences in reaction efficiency between R and S substrates result in the enzyme displaying enantiopreference.

Development of S-enantioselective enzyme

To achieve enantiocomplementary dehalogenation of δ-haloalcohols, we turned our attention to improving the S-enantioselectivity through protein engineering of HheC-WT. Based on the above understanding of the potential determinants of the enzymatic dehalogenation process, our subsequent site-saturation mutagenesis (SSM) efforts focused on the residues P84, F86, T134, W139, N176, and W249. As shown in Fig. 5a (see Supplementary Table 7 for details), SSM at position T134 identified the known mutant HheC-SM4 (T134L), which demonstrated enhanced enantioselectivity. Further efforts were made to create another 17 mutants at residue T134 using the SDM strategy. As expected, no mutant with better performance than HheC-SM4 was found (Supplementary Table 8). While Hu et al. reported excellent S-enantioselectivity for the T134M variant in the conversion of (rac)-1a87, this mutant demonstrated low conversion under our reaction conditions, which might be due to the low protein expression level for the T134M variant (Supplementary Fig. 5). This likely provide a plausible explanation for its absence in our SSM screening results. In addition, two beneficial single-mutants, HheC-SM11 (F86W) and HheC-SM12 (N176Q), were identified from F86 and N176 mutant libraries, respectively. Remarkably, the HheC-SM11 variant exhibited a good enantioselectivity (E = 111; 1a: 87% ee, 1b: 95% ee). Enantioselective transformation of 20 mM (rac)-1a achieved 48% conversion, affording (S)-1b and (R)-1a with 95% ee and 87% ee, respectively. Unfortunately, screening of P84, W139 and W249 mutant libraries did not identify any beneficial variants. Further SSM studies were conducted targeting at the T134 and N176 residues, using HheC-SM11 as the parent enzyme. Unexpectedly, no variants with increased enantioselectivity were identified from the mutant libraries. Additional SSM studies targeting residue F86 using HheC-SM4 and HheC-SM12 as the parent enzymes also did not yield further improved beneficial variants. Subsequent attempts were made to create three double-mutants, HheC-DM9 (F86W/T134L), HheC-DM10 (F86W/N176Q), and HheC-DM11 (T134L/N176Q), through the SDM strategy. These double-mutants demonstrated lower catalytic efficiency and enantioselectivity compared to HheC-SM11. Substrate scope for S-enantioselective dehalogenation of δ-haloalcohols

Subsequently, we investigated the scope of these S-enantioselective enzymes for the dehalogenation of various δ-haloalcohols (rac)-2-15a. As shown in Fig. 5b (see Supplementary Table 9–22 for details), these enzymes exhibited low to excellent enantioselectivity toward these substrates, producing chiral tetrahydrofurans and δ-haloalcohols with up to >99% ee. However, none of the enzymes acted on the α,α-disubstituted δ-chloroalcohol (12a). Overall, each enzyme demonstrated different substrate preferences. For example, HheC-DM9 and HheC-DM11 showed the best catalytic performance with substrates 11a and 6a, respectively. Intriguingly, for a few substrates, some enzymes exhibited opposite R-enantioselectivity. For instance, with the meta-methoxy substituted δ-chloroalcohol 5a, all enzymes except WT and SM11 showed R-selectivity. To verify the scalability of the synthesis, we further performed representative preparative scale reactions using E. coli (HheC-SM11) cells as the S-enantioselective biocatalyst. As shown in Fig. 5c (see Supplementary Table 23 for details), chiral (S)-tetrahydrofurans were obtained with 43–49% yields and 88–95% ee, and chiral (R)-δ-haloalcohols were isolated with 39–49% yields and 90– > 99% ee. These results demonstrate the feasibility of the biocatalytic approach for enantiocomplementary synthesis of tetrahydrofurans and δ-haloalcohols.

Large-scale and transformation reactions

Finally, we assessed the bioconversions at higher substrate concentrations to demonstrate the practicality of this biocatalytic approach. The enantioselective dehalogenation reactions of 200–500 mM (rac)-1a were investigated using E. coli (HheC-QM1) whole cells as a biocatalyst (Fig. 6a, see Supplementary Table 24 for details). The reaction courses showed that the conversion of 200, 300 and 400 mM (rac)-1a proceeded smoothly, reaching completion after 6 h (50% conversion), 12 h (49% conversion), and 24 h (49% conversion), respectively. Even when the substrate concentration was increased to 500 mM, the reaction still achieved a 45% conversion after 24 h. In the dehalogenation reaction, an alkaline environment is advantageous as it facilitates the deprotonation of the tyrosine in the catalytic triad. At high substrate concentrations, the release of protons during the reaction might significantly decrease the pH, which could hinder the deprotonation process and reduce the catalytic efficiency. To overcome this issue, a pH-stat was used to maintain the reaction pH at 8.0 ± 0.1 through the addition of 5 M aqueous sodium hydroxide solution. As shown in Fig. 6b (see Supplementary Table 24 for details), a large-scale reaction for the conversion of 10 mmol of (rac)-1a at a substrate concentration of 1 M (185 g/L) was evaluated and found to have completed smoothly after 20 h. The reaction exhibited the E value > 200, yielding chiral (R)-1b with 48% isolated yield and 97% ee, along with chiral (S)-1a with 49% isolated yield and 95% ee (Fig. 6c). Further transformations of the chiral δ-haloalcohol were also explored, as illustrated in Fig. 6c. The azidolysis of (S)-1a yielded the chiral δ-azidoalcohol (S)-1c88, which contains an azide group that can be transformed into an amino group and utilized in click chemistry applications. The synthesis of (R)-1d, featuring a chiral C-F bond, was accomplished via a stereoselective fluorination reaction89. Additionally, chiral (S)-1e was efficiently generated and can serve as an intermediate in the synthesis of MRJF4 analogues, promising therapeutic candidate for gliomas therapy79. Importantly, the synthesis of chiral (S)-1f was also accomplished, which can be utilized in the production of nitric oxide synthase inhibitors, useful for the treatment or prevention of inflammatory diseases and pain90. These representative transformations resulted in the generation of useful derivatives and showcased the synthetic scalability of the biocatalytic methodology.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed an efficient enzymatic strategy for the kinetic resolution of δ-haloalcohols and tetrahydrofurans. This biocatalytic method exhibits broad substrate scope, enabling the preparation of aryl-, heterocyclic-, alkyl-, and disubstituted chiral δ-haloalcohols and tetrahydrofurans in high yields and with good optical purity. While enantioselective ketone reduction via ketoreductases can also furnish δ-haloalcohols, our approach uniquely provides direct access to enantioenriched tetrahydrofurans that are valuable structural motifs in pharmaceuticals and natural products. Additionally, we have provided insights into the structural and mechanistic basis for the observed catalytic activity and enantioselectivity through in-depth analysis of various mutants and docking studies. Furthermore, it is important to highlight that the biocatalytic method can withstand a high substrate concentration, emphasizing its synthetic potential for industrial applications. This achievement not only broadens the catalytic capabilities of HHDH in generating valuable chiral compounds but also showcases the strength of enzymatic promiscuity in the fields of synthetic and pharmaceutical chemistry.

Methods

Preparative-scale biocatalytic kinetic resolution of δ-haloalcohols

In a 200 mL round-bottom flask, a resting cell suspension of E. coli (HheC) at a concentration of 5 g dcw/L was prepared in 100 mL of PB buffer (200 mM, pH 7.5). To this suspension, 2 mmol of δ-haloalcohols (rac)-a was added to a final concentration of 20 mM. The reaction mixture was then stirred at 30 °C. Upon completion of the enzymatic reaction, the mixture was subjected to extraction using ethyl acetate (3 × 70 mL). The organic phases were separated by centrifugation (8800 × g, 3 min), combined, dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and evaporated at reduced pressure. The resulting mixture was purified by flash chromatography (petroleum ether: ethyl acetate = 100:1 ~ 10:1) on silica gel to afford the desired chiral Tetrahydrofurans b and chiral δ-haloalcohols a.

Enzyme purification and kinetic characterization

To purify the HheC-QM1 and HheC-SM11 mutants, a 6×His tag was introduced at the N-terminal of each mutant for constructing the recombinant strains E. coli (His-HheC-QM1) and E. coli (His-HheC-SM11). A single colony of the recombinant E. coli strain was picked to inoculate to 100 mL LB culture containing 50 µg/mL kanamycin. The cells grew at 37 °C until the OD600 was around 0.6–0.8. IPTG was added to the culture with a final concentration of 0.2 mM, and the culture was then incubated at 28 °C overnight to induce the expression of the recombinant enzyme. The cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8800 × g for 4 min at 4 °C. The enzyme protein was then purified using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography, utilizing the BeyoGoldTM His-tag Purification Resin kit (Beyotime). The purification procedures were carried out as per the instructions provided by the kit. The cell pellet was resuspended in buffer A (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, pH 8.0. The buffer pH was adjusted by adding a 1 M NaOH solution.) and disrupted by sonication. The resulting solution was centrifuged (10,000×g, 4 °C, 30 min) to obtain supernatant. The supernatant was loaded into a Ni-NTA column pre-equilibrated with buffer A. To remove nonspecifically bound proteins, the column was washed with 5 column volumes of buffer B (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, 2 mM imidazole, pH 8.0). Then the target protein was eluted and collected with buffer C (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, 50 mM imidazole, pH 8.0). Fractions containing the target protein were identified by SDS-PAGE analysis. The protein concentrations of His-HheC-QM1 and His-HheC-SM11 were determined as 21.15 and 36.05 mg/mL, respectively. Kinetic parameters of HheC-QM1 and HheC-SM11 toward (rac)-1a were determined in PB buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5) at 30 °C using purified enzymes. The reaction system is 800 μL, and the reaction was initiated by the addition of purified enzyme (7.5 μL for HheC-QM1, 10 μL for HheC-SM11) varying the concentration of the substrate (rac)-1a (0.1–10 mM). Reaction samples in each moment were taken, extracted using 800 μL of ethyl acetate, and analyzed by chiral HPLC. Conversions were calculated from the ee values of the substrates and products, which were then used to determine reaction rates. Kinetic data were fitted with the Michaelis-Menten equation using the Origin software program to obtain the kinetic parameters. The enzyme concentrations were measured by BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). All kcat values are defined as per monomer.

Chromatography analysis

The enantiomeric excess (ee) values of chiral compounds and analytic yields were determined by chiral HPLC or GC analysis. The chiral HPLC was performed on Shimadzu LC-20A, equipped with Chiralcel OJ-H chiral column (4.6 mmΦ × 250 mmL, particle size 5 μm), Chiralpak OB-H chiral column (4.6 mmΦ × 250 mmL, particle size 5 μm), Chiralcel OD-H chiral column (4.6 mmΦ × 250 mmL, particle size 5 μm), Chiralcel IF chiral column (4.6 mmΦ × 250 mmL, particle size 5 μm). Chiral GC analysis was performed on Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID) using Rt-bDEXcst column or BGB-175 column with nitrogen as the carrier gas.
