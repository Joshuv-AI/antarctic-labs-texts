Prolonged intermittent home video-EEG monitoring for drug-resistant epilepsy: the cost-availability model
Authors: Tatiana Vander, Tatiana Stroganova, Rozalya Bikmullina, Michal Balberg, Tal Benoliel, Tal Gilboa, Mordekhay Medvedovsky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Video-EEG monitoring (VEM) is an obligatory step in drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) management. The common way of VEM is in-hospital VEM (IVEM), which is limited by the need to admit the patient to the epilepsy monitoring unit. Prolonged intermittent home VEM (PIHVEM) can be a more available alternative. Since reducing antiseizure medication at home is impossible, PIHVEM can be associated with long monitoring and, therefore, relatively high cost. To regulate the parameters of PIHVEM (the patient selection criteria and length of PIHVEM), we introduce the cost-availability model (CAM), which balances the VEM availability and VEM cost, considering the ratio between the annual number of VEM studies performed for DRE and annual DRE incidence (VEM DRE/VEM incidence). The lowest CAM value corresponds to the most optimal parameters of PIHVEM. CAM value decreases with longer PIHVEM. With a lower VEM DRE/VEM incidence ratio, CAM value reduction can be achieved by including patients with lower seizure frequency in PIHVEM. In such a situation, the considerations of VEM availability outweigh the VEM cost. With the increase in VEM DRE/VEM incidence ratio, the influence of VEM cost increases relative to VEM availability. The conclusions: PIHVEM can increase VEM availability without increasing the cost, and CAM can estimate the optimal PIHVEM parameters.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12962-025-00650-8.

Introduction

Epilepsy affects approximately 0.7–1% of the global population [37]. Approximately 25–33% of individuals with epilepsy fail to achieve seizure freedom, a phenomenon known as drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) [19]. DRE is defined as failure of adequate trials of two tolerated, appropriately chosen and used antiseizure medications, either as monotherapies or in combination, to achieve sustained seizure freedom [23].

Several surgical options have been developed for managing DRE, including open resection of the epileptogenic zone, laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT), radiofrequency ablation, vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), deep brain stimulation (DBS), and responsive neurostimulation (RNS) [12]. However, only 0.5–1.2% of DRE patients ultimately undergo surgery [4, 5, 10, 31].

Most patients with DRE continue to experience seizures unless treated surgically [28]. It is widely recommended that all patients with DRE undergo at least one VEM (video-EEG monitoring) study during their disease course. As such, the number of VEM studies performed annually for DRE patients should ideally align with the estimated annual incidence of DRE. Epilepsy occurs at an incidence rate of 0.07% in the general population [14], and with DRE affecting approximately 30% of these individuals, the annual incidence of DRE is roughly 0.02%. This estimation is corroborated by Vaughan et al. [37], who reported an annual incidence of 4.6 million new epilepsy cases and 1.4 million new DRE cases globally. Although VEM is employed for various purposes beyond pre-surgical evaluations, it is most commonly used for spell classification, accounting for 39% of all VEM admissions [1]. The widespread demand for VEM, therefore, has implications for its availability and, consequently, the timely diagnosis and treatment of DRE.

There are several causes of epilepsy surgery underutilization; probably the most important are a lack of awareness among neurologists and concerns among patients. Additionally, the low availability of diagnostic tests, such as VEM, contributes to the underuse of epilepsy surgery.

While specific guidelines may vary, it is generally accepted that pre-surgical VEM should capture at least three to four typical seizures to accurately localize the epileptogenic focus [36]. Achieving this goal within a 5–7-day period typically necessitates the withdrawal of antiseizure medications (ASMs), requiring patient monitoring in a hospital-based epilepsy monitoring unit for safety reasons. However, data on the annual number of VEM studies performed remain limited. According to the 2019 annual report from the National Association of Epilepsy Centers (NAEC), a total of 91,069 VEM studies were conducted, with 28% of these studies involving patients with DRE undergoing pre-surgical evaluations [1]. Considering the population of the United States (333 million), the number of VEM studies for DRE patients represents only 0.008% of the general population, which is less than half of the estimated annual incidence of DRE. Similarly, a report from the Maudsley Biomedical Research Centre (2024) indicated that in the UK, with a population of 67 million, only 4,200 VEM admissions were recorded annually, corresponding to 0.006% of the population, which is lower than a third of the annual incidence of DRE (https://www.maudsleybrc.nihr.ac.uk/stories-of-research/taking-video-eeg-from-hospital-to-home/).

There is growing interest in home video-EEG monitoring (HVEM). HVEM has shown efficacy in capturing non-epileptic events [22] and has recorded epileptic seizures in 10.6% of patients with interictal epileptiform activity over a median monitoring period of three days [34]. In a study by Vander et al. [38], during a one-week monitoring period without ASM reduction, only 23.4% of adults and 29% of children with epilepsy recorded three seizures on separate days. Notably, ASM reduction is not feasible during out-of-hospital VEM due to safety concerns.

HVEM typically involves the placement of EEG electrodes in a clinical setting, followed by home-based monitoring for 3–6 days, with electrodes reattached every three days [3, 8]. Recent innovations have enabled extended HVEM, with some studies reporting continuous monitoring for up to 10 days using water-soluble glue for electrode reattachment by caregivers [26, 40]. While continuous, long-term monitoring remains impractical, intermittent home-based video-EEG monitoring, with sessions lasting up to one week followed by rest periods, is a promising alternative. We propose this method as prolonged intermittent home video-EEG monitoring (PIHVEM).

The present study aims to evaluate whether PIHVEM can improve the availability of VEM while maintaining or enhancing cost-efficiency. To this end, we have updated our previous cost-efficiency model [39] and developed a new cost-availability model (CAM) to assess the feasibility and benefits of this novel approach.

Methods

Software and code availability

The modeling of VEM cost, IVEM proportion, and CAM value was conducted using MATLAB 2022a (MathWorks). The code used in this study is publicly available at https://github.com/VideoEEGPolicy/PIHVEM_policy, under the filename PIHVEM_policy_050725.m. A user guide for this code is provided in the supplementary material (Appendix 2). This code version is an updated iteration of the model originally published by Vander et al. [39].

The modelling of EEG and video success rate in PIHVEM

In addition to variables used in the MATLAB code in the study of [39], two new variables were included: EEG success and video success, which correspond to proportion (from 0 to 1) of successfully captured seizure by EEG and video respectively. We set the value of 0.998 for EEG_success [27] and of 0.85 for video success. The value 0.85 for video success was chosen based on [26], who reported that 5% of events were not captured by video during PIHVEM. We suppose that for one uncaptured event, there can be two captured events, but of inadequate video quality,therefore, we added 10% more. Thus, we accepted that 15% of events were missed (completely or partially) by video, which corresponds to a value of 0.85 for the video success variable.

Estimation of HVEM and IVEM costs

The cost of IVEM was derived from published data, with a value of 13,821 USD [42]. The HVEM cost calculation follows the methodology outlined in Vander et al. [39] (Eqs. 16–18), where the term"mean HVEM-DRE cost"is equivalent to the"HVEM cost"used in this study. Briefly, HVEM costs include expenses for disposables, technical and administrative work, and staff salaries. If the HVEM goal of capturing three seizures on different days is not achieved, the costs of IVEM are included in the HVEM model, as patients will subsequently be referred to for IVEM.

Estimation of HVEM and IVEM proportions

The HVEM proportion refers to the fraction of patients who successfully meet the diagnostic goal of capturing three seizures on different days during HVEM. The calculation of this proportion is described in Vander et al. [38], where HVEM was simulated using the Monte Carlo method. Seizure occurrences were randomized based on the distribution of seizure frequencies among patients with epilepsy [13] and seizure cycling patterns [24].

The IVEM proportion represents the fraction of patients who, after HVEM, require IVEM. This proportion varies according to seizure frequency and can range from 0 to 1. The group fraction denotes the proportion of patients experiencing a specific seizure frequency per month within the total epilepsy patient population. This fraction may vary between adult and pediatric populations and was obtained from Vander et al. [38] (Table S1, supplementary material). The IVEM proportion acts as a limiting factor for VEM availability—lower IVEM proportions indicate higher VEM availability.

Modeling in different patient groups.

VEM cost, HVEM cost, HVEM proportion, and IVEM proportion were modeled as functions of time, with weekly resolution, over an 8-week monitoring period. Separate models were constructed for adult and pediatric populations. Patients with seizure frequencies greater than one per day were excluded, as they typically do not require extended monitoring.

Four patient groups were modeled based on their minimal seizure frequency threshold (MSFT), which represents the minimum number of seizures experienced per month: one, two, three, or four seizures. Modeling was performed for HVEM recordings of 1 to 8 weeks (in separate sessions), where the recording ended either when three seizures are recorded on different days or after the predetermined period, even if seizures are not captured.

CAM value

The CAM value is an arbitrary unit that integrates VEM availability (i.e., IVEM proportion), VEM cost, and the ratio between the annual number of VEM studies for DRE (DRE VEM) and the DRE incidence. The annual number of VEM studies for DRE and the DRE incidence are either expressed as absolute numbers or percentages. For this study, the DRE incidence was set at 0.02%, based on published data [14, 37]. CAM in generalized form

In a more generalized form, the CAM value can be expressed as the weighted sum of various diagnostic modalities used in the diagnostic workup for DRE:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}CAM value &=\sum_{i=1}^{n}modalit{y}^{\prime}s \:fraction\\&\quad* modalit{y}^{\prime}s \:deficit\\&\quad* modalit{y}^{\prime}s \:cost power\end{aligned}$$\end{document}5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}{\text{power }} &= {\text{annual number of studies in given modality}}\\&\quad/\left( {modality^{\prime}s{\text{ fraction}}*{\text{incidence of condition}}} \right)\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

In this equation, n refers to the number of diagnostic modalities involved in the diagnostic workup. For DRE, these include VEM, anatomical MRI, functional MRI, positron emission tomography (PET), and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), among other tests.

•The modality’s fraction is the proportion of patients with a given condition who require a specific diagnostic study to achieve optimal diagnostic results. For example, nearly all DRE patients undergo VEM, so the modality’s fraction for VEM is 1. Conversely, fewer DRE patients undergo SPECT, so its fraction will be less than 1.

•The modality’s cost represents the average cost associated with each diagnostic modality.

•The modality’s deficit quantifies the availability of the modality. For instance, the number of MRI machines per population represents availability, while the number of people per MRI machine is considered a deficit. The choice of deficit measure depends on the research question. For this study, the deficit is defined as the proportion of IVEM due to the focus on how the introduction of PIHVEM affects VEM availability.

Results

VEM cost

(Figs. 1A-B, Tables S1 and S2 in Appendix 1).

The cost of video-EEG monitoring (VEM) per patient was calculated for all individuals referred to VEM: both IVEM and HVEM. As the duration of HVEM increased, a larger proportion of patients received a diagnosis, thus reducing the necessity for referral to in-hospital video-EEG monitoring (IVEM). Consequently, the overall VEM cost decreased progressively from the second to the eighth week of HVEM.

However, the cost during the first one to two weeks of HVEM increased for all patients and during the first three weeks for a group of adult patients with MSFT of one seizure per month. This increase was due to a lower proportion of patients achieving the clinical goal of prolonged intermittent HVEM (PIHVEM), which resulted in their referral to IVEM after insufficient home monitoring.

Patients with higher seizure frequencies were more likely to receive a diagnosis during HVEM and were less likely to require subsequent IVEM, leading to a reduction in VEM cost as seizure frequency increased. Thus, the total VEM cost generally declined with increasing seizure frequency.

IVEM proportion

(Figs. 1C-D, Tables S3 and S4 in Appendix 1).

The proportion of patients requiring IVEM decreased as HVEM duration increased from the first to the eighth week. Furthermore, a lower minimal seizure frequency threshold (MSFT) per month was associated with a decreased need for IVEM, as more patients were eligible for HVEM. The reduction in IVEM proportion paralleled an increase in VEM availability, demonstrating the impact of extended monitoring on resource optimization.

CAM

(Figs. 1E-F, 2 and 3; Tables S5 and S6 in Appendix 1).

The cost-availability model (CAM) value decreased with longer monitoring durations, with lower CAM values indicating optimal VEM optimization in terms of both availability and cost-effectiveness. In regions with limited access to VEM, the CAM value for patients with frequent seizures plateaued after 3–4 weeks of monitoring, suggesting that this duration balances both availability and cost. For patients with infrequent seizures, CAM plateau was not reached even after 8 weeks of monitoring, rendering them unsuitable for HVEM. In a scenario of full VEM availability, CAM plateau occurred after 4 weeks of monitoring across all MSFT groups, as the cost burden associated with longer monitoring durations became more substantial.

The CAM value can be utilized to determine patient eligibility for HVEM, depending on the patient’s MSFT and the local DRE VEM/DRE incidence ratio. In regions where the DRE VEM/DRE incidence ratio is less than 1 (Figs. 2A-B, 3A-B), referring patients with an MSFT of one seizure per month to HVEM yields the lowest CAM value. In regions where the DRE VEM/DRE incidence ratio equals 1 (Figs. 2C, 3C), an MSFT of two seizures per month for up to 2 weeks of monitoring, or one seizure per month for 3–8 weeks of monitoring, results in the optimal CAM value. In the rare scenario where the DRE VEM/DRE incidence ratio exceeds 1 (Figs. 2D-F, 3D-F), the optimal CAM value shifts with increasing MSFT, initially corresponding to shorter monitoring durations and later extending to longer ones. Discussion

In this study, we introduce the CAM, a model that integrates both VEM cost and VEM availability. The CAM helps determine which patients should be initially referred to HVEM and which should proceed directly to IVEM without a prior HVEM. Each element of the CAM may have a different weight in various situations. For example, in regions with low VEM availability, it is reasonable to allocate resources to increase VEM availability, even when the VEM cost is not minimized. In contrast, if VEM availability is suitable, the aim should be to reduce the study’s cost.

We suggest expressing the degree of insufficient VEM availability with the ratio: DRE VEM/DRE incidence. Powering VEM cost by this ratio in Eq. (3) regulates the VEM cost influence in the CAM value calculation.

We applied the CAM to several patient groups based on their MSFTs, ranging from 1 to 4 seizures per month (Fig. 1E and F; Tables S5 and S6). To achieve the diagnostic goal of recording 3 seizures on different days in patients with one seizure per month, home monitoring for three months would be required. This is impractical. For patients with two seizures per month, approximately six weeks are required. This is also impractical, at least with the current HVEM technology. Therefore, HVEM is more suitable for patients experiencing at least 3 to 4 seizures per month. Recording for a long period should be done intermittently. For example, three or four one-week monitoring sessions are separated by resting periods. Such lengthy recordings, even in separate sessions with long periods of rest, are difficult for patients and their families and require high compliance. Professional online support is crucial for such monitoring. This approach can be considered when the availability of in-hospital monitoring is low. The interference with usual daily activities and with the privacy of the patients and their families should be taken into account when PIHVEM is planned. Nevertheless, motivated patients with DRE generally demonstrate good compliance. This is reported by Zijlmans et al. [41] and further supported by Wong et al. [40], who reported successful continuous monitoring for 7–10 days.

We demonstrated that extending the duration of PIHVEM reduces VEM costs and enhances its availability. As lower MSFT levels make more patients eligible for PIHVEM, VEM availability increases with decreasing MSFT. However, at lower seizure frequencies, the overall required monitoring time is longer, leading to higher VEM costs. Balancing this trade-off requires an optimization strategy to identify the optimal MSFT threshold for prioritizing PIHVEM over IVEM as the initial diagnostic approach. We propose the CAM as the optimization algorithm: The lowest CAM value corresponds to the optimal MSFT. Our findings indicate that when the DRE VEM/DRE incidence ratio is low—representing limited VEM availability—setting a lower MSFT and conducting a longer PIHVEM duration results in a lower CAM value. As the DRE VEM/DRE incidence ratio increases, indicating sufficient VEM availability, the impact of costs on the CAM calculation is amplified (Fig. 3).

Based on our modeling results, in all cases where the DRE IVEM/DRE incidence ratio is less than 1, which matches the US data [1], the MSFT of three seizures per month results in a lower CAM value than an MSFT of four seizures per month. Therefore, considering both cost and availability, the preferred parameters for PIHVEM in the USA and probably in many other regions worldwide are an MSFT of three seizures per month and a maximum PIHVEM duration of four weeks. This slightly differs from that of [39], which suggests an MSFT of four seizures per month and a maximum intermittent home monitoring of three weeks. However, their results were based solely on the cost without considering the availability of the VEM. In low-resource developing countries, where the number of hospital beds per population is highly limited, a primary objective is to increase the availability of VEM. In such scenarios, PIHVEM plays a particularly critical role. Vander et al. [39] highlighted the cost-related challenges associated with VEM in these settings.

It is important to note that PIHVEM cannot completely replace IVEM. Even if PIHVEM lasts up to 8 weeks, approximately 10% of patients will not achieve the clinical goal and will require admission for IVEM with ASM reduction. If PIHVEM lasts up to 4 weeks, this percentage would be 20–30% (Figs. 3 and 4). Thus, in both high-resource and low-resource countries, there is a need to develop monitoring services based on both home and intermittent monitoring. In low-resource countries, due to low availability of IVEM, PIHVEM can be especially important. However, some obstacles to PIHVEM in low-resource countries should be considered: a low number of nurses and physicians per population, poor cellular and internet coverage, and suboptimal housing. Moreover, in low-resource countries, even if VEM is done, the availability of epilepsy surgery can be extremely low. Thus, for such countries, it is not enough to improve VEM availability alone; rather, it is important to provide a comprehensive program against DRE and epilepsy in general.

In addition to the cost and availability advantages, complementing IVEM with PIHVEM in the pre-surgical workup offers clinical benefits. Its longer duration allows for the capture of multiple seizure events, which is crucial when differentiating between seizure types. Moreover, in patients undergoing pre-surgical evaluation where multiple seizure onset zone is suspected, the International League Against Epilepsy and the International Federation of Clinical Neurophysiology recommend recording at least one seizure that is separated by more than one month from other recorded seizures [36]. This requirement is challenging to meet during IVEM, as its duration is typically limited to several days due to high costs and low availability. Additionally, ASM reduction is often employed in IVEM to enhance the likelihood of capturing seizures within this short timeframe. The risk of secondary generalized tonic–clonic seizures doubles with ASM reduction [16], and the likelihood of postictal psychosis may also increase [11, 21]. Given the high risks associated with ASM reduction, it is not performed during HVEM. In this respect, PIHVEM offers a safer alternative, enabling the capture of sufficient seizures without the associated risks.

Although HVEM has been utilized for over forty years [18], it has yet to become a standard component of epilepsy pre-surgical evaluations [35]. Only a few studies have demonstrated the successful application of HVEM in the pre-surgical assessment of patients with temporal lobe epilepsy [6, 15, 30]. However, to date, no reports have documented the use of HVEM in the context of extra-temporal epilepsy surgery [29].

The limited use of HVEM in epilepsy pre-surgical evaluations can be attributed to several additional factors, including concerns regarding the quality of EEG and video signals, the excessively long monitoring duration, technical challenges, and patient safety. Existing literature [8, 9, 20, 25, 27] suggests that some of these limitations can be addressed, with reports highlighting the sufficient quality of this method. Moreover, recent technological advancements offer promising solutions to expand the use of pre-surgical HVEM. These include simplified at-home EEG electrode placement [17, 33, 40], improved home video-recording quality [7], and automated analysis of large-scale EEG data over prolonged monitoring periods [2, 32]. Together, these innovations may facilitate broader clinical adoption of HVEM in the near future.

The reliability of the EEG signal recording during HVEM has been established in several studies ([8, 9, 20]), which report very high-quality recordings for at-home EEG, comparable to IVEM recording. Nurse et al. [27] reported that 99.8% of the events reported by patients were captured by EEG during HVEM.

Video recording at home can be more challenging than in a hospital setting. While studies by Dash et al. [8] and DiGiovine et al. [9] reported high-quality video recordings during HVEM, Michaeli et al. [25] found that video quality during IVEM was superior to that of HVEM. Nurse et al. [27] demonstrated that 94.9% of the events reported by patients during HVEM were successfully captured on video.

To improve the global planning and management of DRE diagnostics and treatment, having comprehensive, up-to-date information on DRE incidence, prevalence, and the number of VEM studies—as well as other aspects of epilepsy monitoring units’ activities—is essential. A key challenge in VEM policy research is the lack of publicly available, current data on the number of VEM studies conducted across different regions. One notable example is Bagić et al. [1], who provided such data for the USA. Kobalashvily et al. (2016) reported a survey from 27 European epilepsy centers, but it only partially covers the activities of European epilepsy monitoring units.

Although our CAM model can help optimize VEM policy based on available resources in a given region, it has several limitations. We used computer modeling, which may not account for all real-life nuances. However, the algorithm is based on real-life data, as referenced in Vander et al. [38] and Vander et al. [39]. Additionally, VEM costs in this study refer only to direct costs, while the CAM algorithm does not explicitly consider indirect costs (e.g., work or school absences, mobility limitations). Nevertheless, since the indirect costs are mainly linked to test duration, and the algorithm accounts for test duration, the indirect costs are somewhat represented in the model. The CAM value, calculated for different maximal PIHVEM durations, also considers these indirect costs indirectly through the decision on the test duration. Further studies are needed to validate the CAM model based on real-world economic and clinical data. Validation parameters may include hospital utilization rates, cost and availability data from epilepsy inpatient and outpatient services, video-EEG and surgery waiting times, the number of surgeries for DRE in each country, and the number of repeated video-EEG studies. Our MATLAB code enables users to modify the parameters according to their country or region, so the CAM model can be tailored to individual economic and clinical settings.

Moreover, our calculations are based on the available VEM cost data from Slater et al. [42] and annual VEM study numbers for DRE from Bagić et al. [1] for the USA. However, the CAM algorithm can be adapted to data from any region, and readers can access our publicly available MATLAB code to do so.

CAM, in its generalized form (Eqs. 4 and 5), can be applied not only to VEM but also to the whole epilepsy pre-surgery workup and to any diagnostic workup in different fields of medicine by finding the optimal balance between the cost and availability of the diagnostic modalities.

Lastly, although HVEM is not yet a standard part of DRE pre-surgical workups, there is growing support for its use in the literature [6, 15, 30], including this study. Further research is needed to clarify the role of HVEM and PIHVEM in managing patients with DRE.

These parameters include the minimum seizure frequency threshold per month (MSFT) required for patients'eligibility and the optimal monitoring duration. Specifically, in regions where the annual number of VEM studies is lower than the yearly DRE incidence, the optimal parameters are an MSFT of three seizures per month and a maximum monitoring duration of four weeks.
