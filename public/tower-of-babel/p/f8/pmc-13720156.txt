Cathepsin resistance mediated by key amino acid substitutions drives intracellular crystallization in light chain proximal tubulopathy
Authors: Xin Wang, Shuang Wang, Chu-qiao Wang, Yue-yue Zhu, Xin Zhang, Jin Xu, Yi Liu, Xiao-juan Yu, Su-xia Wang, Li Yang, Fu-de Zhou, Ming-hui Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Monoclonal free light chains drive diverse renal pathologies in plasma cell dyscrasias. However, the mechanism of light chain proximal tubulopathy remains poorly understood because sequence data are scarce. Here we characterize full-length kappa free light chains from five patients with light chain proximal tubulopathy, four of whom had intracellular crystals. All showed the highest homology to the IGKV1-33 germline, while crystal-forming light chains contained distinct single-residue substitutions. These light chains generated a stable ~12-kDa fragment resistant to digestion by cathepsin B and D, a pattern not observed in non-crystal-forming light chains. Mechanistically, a single-residue substitution contributed substantially to cathepsin D resistance and stable fragment formation. In vivo comparison of recombinant κWJJ and its single-amino acid mutant further supported an important role for this substitution in intracellular crystal formation. These findings establish a sequence-based link between impaired lysosomal processing and crystal accumulation in light chain proximal tubulopathy.

Introduction

Excess production of monoclonal free light chains (FLCs) by malignant plasma cells often leads to multi-organ involvement, with the kidneys being most commonly affected. Among light chain-related kidney diseases, glomerular disorders (like AL amyloidosis) are the most prevalent1,2. Proximal tubule disorders are less common; specifically, light chain proximal tubulopathy (LCPT) is rare, occurring in only about 0.2% of patients undergoing kidney biopsy3,4. Most LCPT patients present with partial or complete light chain-associated Fanconi syndrome (LC-FS); complete Fanconi syndrome is characterized by low-molecular-weight proteinuria, generalized aminoaciduria, glycosuria, phosphaturia, uricosuria, and type II renal tubular acidosis5.

FLCs are small proteins (usually 22–25 kDa) with two isotypes: kappa (κ), which are primarily monomeric, and lambda (λ), which are mostly dimeric6. They easily pass through the glomerulus into the proximal tubules. Normally, proximal tubule cells (PTCs) reabsorb excess FLCs using megalin and cubilin receptors for subsequent degradation in lysosomes7,8. This process involves sorting in early endosomes and degradation in late endosomes/lysosomes9. If the endosomal system fails to catabolize abnormal FLCs, this results in the lysosomal accumulation or the formation of inclusions (crystalline or fibrillary) within the PTCs9.

The main class of lysosomal proteases comprises cathepsins (Cts), categorized by the active-site amino acids that determine their catalytic function. These include cysteine proteases (CtsB, CtsC, CtsF, CtsH, CtsK, CtsL, CtsS, CtsV, and CtsX), serine proteases (CtsA and CtsG), and aspartyl proteases (CtsD and CtsE)10. A previous study revealed that CtsB incompletely degraded FLCs, producing a protease-resistant ~12-kDa fragment identified by Western blotting as the variable region of light chains (VL)11. The proteolytic resistance of VL was believed to prevent the complete degradation of light chains, resulting in crystal formation in patients’ PTCs. These hexagonal crystals, mostly surrounded by a smooth membrane, contained a 107-amino acid fragment corresponding to VL with a low proportion of intact κ light chains12.

The properties of the VL influence the clinical presentation of light chain-associated renal disorders, such as AL amyloidosis and LC-FS13–15. Six VL sequences of FLCs associated with LC-FS have been identified via RNA sequencing of bone marrow samples: four κ FLCs (CHEB, TRE, TRO, and κIM) that induce intracytoplasmic crystals12,16,17, and two κ FLCs that cause LC-FS without crystallization (DEL and κSU)16,18. Recently, two VL sequences related to LCPT, light chain crystalline podocytopathy (LCCP) and crystal-storing histiocytosis (CSH), were determined19. The hydrophobic residues at positions 30 (Ala) and 94 (Ile) within the V region of the LCPT-associated FLC CHEB (belonging to IGKV1–39) were hypothesized to be critical for crystallization12. Site-directed mutagenesis at these two sites may inhibit the formation of intracellular crystals in tubular cells14.

Through the integration of de novo sequencing and mutational mapping, we identified the key residues in monoclonal FLCs contributing to intracytoplasmic crystallization. This study elucidates the mechanism by which lysosomal cathepsins fail to degrade specific FLCs, establishing a sequence-based link to pathogenesis and offering new directions for diagnostic strategies.

Patient-derived FLCs underwent de novo sequencing via ultra-high-resolution LC-MS/MS to resolve full-length amino acid (AA) sequences. Subsequent validation through peptide-spectrum matching achieved 100% sequence coverage across all pathological FLCs (Supplementary Fig. 2). This process, however, often yields multiple candidate sequences; therefore, intact mass analysis was necessary for sequence determination. For instance, one candidate sequence (κWJJ) was selected as the best match because its theoretical molecular mass of 23535.23 Da (monoisotopic mass for the reduced FLC) agreed with the experimentally measured mass (23535.00 Da) within 0.5 Da.

This comparison was chosen because AL amyloidosis is the most common monoclonal gammopathy-related kidney disease, and its pathology primarily affects the glomerular, vascular, and interstitial regions, with limited primary involvement of proximal tubular epithelial cells. Amyloidogenic light chain sequences were sourced from AL-Base (https://wwwapp.bumc.bu.edu/BEDAC_ALBase/), a database of human Ig light chain sequences from patients with AL amyloidosis and controls20,21. Twenty AL sequences were retrieved from the database (Supplementary Fig. 3) for comparative analysis with five FLCs in this study.

Intracytoplasmic crystals were identified in the proximal tubular epithelial cells of four patients with crystalline LCPT, whereas no such inclusions were observed in patient κPWL (Fig. 2A). Although the pathogenic monoclonal FLCs were all derived from the same IGKV1–33 germline, the resulting intracytoplasmic inclusions exhibited significant morphological diversity in both size and shape. These variations suggest that specific AA substitutions in FLCs may dictate both the formation and morphology of inclusions. Notably, position 30 (36-IMGT) in CDR1 and position 76 (92-IMGT) in FR3 may contribute to intracytoplasmic inclusion formation. Specifically, two patients (κDEH and κWWJ) presented with a concurrent LCCP, CSH, and LCPT. In these cases, hexagonal crystals were observed in podocytes, endothelial cells, mesangial cells, and histiocytes (Fig. 2C). The AA changes at position 76 may be associated with LCCP or CSH. Digestion of FLCs with lysosomal proteases

Filtered FLCs are reabsorbed by PTCs, a process that relies heavily on lysosomal proteases. We investigated the in vitro degradation of eight FLCs related to LCPT by CtsB, CtsD, and CtsL under optimized in vitro conditions. In 96-h digestion studies, a ~ 12-kDa fragment resistant to further digestion by CtsB was consistently observed across all tested LCPT-associated FLCs. During CtsL digestion, the same ~12-kDa resistant fragment was seen only in the κ FLCs causing LCPT, while all tested λ FLCs were completely digested. This finding is consistent with the clinical observation that patients with λ-type tubular pathogenic FLCs are less commonly reported4,23. Notably, all FLCs causing intracytoplasmic inclusions produced a single ~12-kDa fragment after CtsD digestion. Conversely, of the three FLCs that did not form crystals, only κPWL showed partial degradation and multiple fragments; the others were degraded normally by CtsD. The consistent generation of a single ~12-kDa fragment after both CtsB and CtsD digestion may represent a characteristic feature of FLCs associated with intracytoplasmic inclusion formation, regardless of their κ or λ light chain type.

Conversely, two amyloidogenic FLCs, κPYZ and κSJH (both assigned to IGKV1–33 germline), were susceptible to CtsB and CtsL degradation. To determine the composition of the digestion-resistant ~12-kDa fragments, the corresponding bands were excised from the SDS-PAGE gel for LC-MS/MS analysis (Fig. 3B). Peptide-spectrum match (PSM) counts were used as a semi-quantitative analysis of the protein band composition. After digestion with CtsB, CtsD, and CtsL, the remaining fragments consisted predominantly of the VL domain (Fig. 3C). Intracytoplasmic λ light chain crystals are exceptionally rare5. However, we previously identified λXSY as a unique case (IGLV2–14/J2 by RNA-seq)22, in which CtsB and CtsD digestion primarily yielded CL-derived fragments.

Integration of QTOF-MS and LC-MS/MS data allowed us to precisely map the proteolytic boundaries. As shown in Fig. 4, the major ~12-kDa fragments across all four crystal-forming FLCs exhibited similar cleavage patterns at the N-terminal region of the CL domain (residues 107–117). QTOF-MS analysis revealed that the most abundant products generated by CtsB and CtsD digestion corresponded to residues 1–109 and 1–114, respectively. These results indicate that CtsB and CtsD effectively truncate the CL domain while leaving the VL domain intact. Production of recombinant FLCs and biophysical characterization

Notably, κWJJm30 features a single AA substitution within its loop region at position 30, where lysine (K) is replaced by serine (S) (Fig. 5A). The supernatants from the CHO-express cultures were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and western blotting using an anti-human κ antibody (Supplementary Fig. 4). The soluble FLCs exhibited a characteristic far-UV CD spectrum with a minimum at 216 nm, indicating a typical β-sheet secondary structure of immunoglobulin proteins (Fig. 5E). CD thermal stability experiments revealed that κWJJ possessed a Tm of 52.737 ± 0.101 °C, which was slightly higher than that of κWJJm30’s of 50.446 ± 0.136 °C (Fig. 5F).

Mutational analysis in murine models

We hypothesized that the resistance of FLCs to CtsB and CtsD degradation is linked to intracellular crystal formation. To elucidate the mechanistic relationship among the key residue, cathepsin resistance, and intracytoplasmic crystal formation, we conducted an in vivo study (Fig. 6A). Recombinant FLCs were readily detected in both serum and urine following injection and were significantly higher than in vehicle-treated controls. The concentration of κWJJm30 was significantly lower than that of κWJJ in mouse urine, whereas no significant difference was observed between their blood concentrations (Fig. 6B, C). Compared to control mice, samples from mice with κWJJ injection displayed morphologic alterations characteristic of crystalline LCPT. Discussion

A pivotal insight from this study is the identification of a specific proteolytic signature associated with crystal-forming FLCs. We observed that all crystal-forming FLCs associated with LCPT generated a consistent ~12-kDa fragment following degradation by CtsB and CtsD. This stable fragment represents the VL core—historically identified as the primary component of intracytoplasmic crystals12. While VL stability is required for lysosomal persistence, crystallization only occurs when protease-resistant fragments accumulate beyond a sufficient threshold. In contrast, the single-point mutant κWJJm30, which lost its resistance to CtsD degradation, concurrently failed to form crystals in vivo despite equivalent dosing. The close association between cathepsin resistance—specifically the generation of the ~12-kDa fragment—and intracytoplasmic crystallization highlights the potential of proteolytic stability assays as an in vitro diagnostic tool for identifying nephrotoxic light chains.

Our findings also provide a mechanistic hypothesis for the biased distribution of light chain isotypes observed in renal pathology. CtsL successfully degraded all tested λ FLCs, whereas none of the κ FLCs causing LCPT were fully catabolized. This differential susceptibility to CtsL may explain the lower incidence of λ light chain-associated tubular disorders, supporting the hypothesis that κ light chains may possess intrinsic structural features that render them more resistant to proteolysis and thus more nephrotoxic. However, given the limited number of λ variants analyzed, these findings remain hypothesis-generating and require further validation. While a pepsin-resistant fragment of similar size (~ 12 kDa) has been previously reported11, the LC-MS/MS analysis indicated that the fragment identified here consists of a mixed VL and CL composition (Supplementary Fig. 5), reflecting the distinct enzymatic environment of the lysosome.

Regarding the structural determinants of crystallization, previous studies on the IGKV1–39 germline emphasized the role of hydrophobic residues at positions 30 and 94. However, our analysis of IGKV1–33 derived FLCs challenged the universality of this rule. We demonstrated that introducing the hydrophilic residue lysine at position 30 significantly impacted intracellular crystallization, suggesting that the physicochemical determinants of pathogenicity are not solely driven by hydrophobicity but likely involve complex conformational alterations specific to the germline background. Furthermore, position 76 in the IGKV1–33 germline appears to be a hotspot for amyloidogenic and crystalline propensity, as substitutions here are also implicated in LCCP and CSH. The mechanisms driving tubular damage in non-crystallizing LCPT cases remain elusive, warranting further investigation into additional key residues.

To rigorously validate the pathogenic potential of the sequences identified by de novo sequencing, we utilized a murine model. Although intraperitoneal injection models relying on acute, high-dose delivery do not perfectly mimic the chronic, low-level exposure seen in human monoclonal gammopathies24, this strategy effectively stresses the proximal tubule’s endocytic capacity. The results demonstrated that C57BL/6J mice receiving recombinant FLCs derived from patient sequences successfully recapitulated the human phenotype, developing intracytoplasmic crystals within the PTCs. This successful model development serves a dual purpose: it supports the intrinsic propensity of the specific FLC sequences for crystallization in vivo and validates the reliability of our proteomic sequencing and CHO-expression workflow.

Finally, this study underscores the critical role of mass spectrometry-based de novo sequencing. Unlike B-cell receptor sequencing, which requires invasive bone marrow sampling and overlooks post-translational modifications, de novo sequencing of urinary FLCs directly characterizes the pathogenic proteoform. We confirm that this method yields high-fidelity sequences given sufficient purity25. While unnecessary for non-glycosylated FLCs, enzymatic deglycosylation is required to accurately sequence glycosylated variants in AL amyloidosis26,27.

Methods

Patients

All patients provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (approval number: 2025R0176-0001). All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. Five patients (κDEH, κWJJ, κWWJ, κZGN, and κPWL) presented with LCPT and partial Fanconi syndrome. Unlike the global transport defects seen in complete Fanconi syndrome, these patients exhibited selective reabsorption impairments. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed the inclusions within the PTCs of four patients (κDEH, κWJJ, κWWJ, and κZGN). Among them, κDEH and κWWJ also had crystalline deposits in podocytes, mesangial cells, and histiocytes. In contrast, no cytoplasmic inclusions were observed in patient κPWL. The included patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

FLC samples purification

FLC samples were purified as we previously described in ref. 25. Briefly, patient-derived urine samples were concentrated using a Pellicon 2 Mini Cassette with a Biomax 10 kDa membrane (Merck Millipore, catalog P2B010A01). Crude FLCs were enriched by affinity chromatography on an ÄKTA pure chromatography system (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) using a HiTrap KappaSelect or LambdaFabSelect column (Cytiva, catalog 17545812 or 17548212), based on the diagnosis. HiTrap Protein G HP (Cytiva, catalog 17040501) was used to remove intact IgG. Finally, a Superdex 75 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva, catalog 29148721) was then used to further improve product purity for protein de novo sequencing. Using a Nanosep centrifugal device with an Omega membrane MWCO 3 kDa (Pall, catalog MAP003C38), FLC samples were solvent-exchanged with ddH2O and concentrated. Protein concentration was determined using Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, catalog P0012) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Intact mass analysis

Intact mass analysis was performed using a Vion IMS QTOF mass spectrometer coupled to an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). Reduced protein samples were separated with an Agilent PLRP-S column (1.0 × 50 mm, 5 μm, 1000 Å). Mobile phase A consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile also containing 0.1% formic acid. Elution was performed at a flow rate of 0.200 mL/min over a 10-min gradient: 10% B (held for 1 min), 10–55% B (2.5 min), 55–95% B (3.5 min), 95% B (held for 2 min), 95–10% B (0.1 min), and 10% B (held for 0.9 min). The capillary voltage was set at 2.8 kV and the scan range was from 500 to 2000 m/z. Data acquisition and processing were managed using UNIFI software (Waters, Milford, MA, USA).

Enzymatic digestions and LC-MS/MS analysis

Proteomic analysis was performed using a previously described liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) workflow with modifications25. A total of 100 μg of protein was denatured, reduced with dithiothreitol, and alkylated with iodoacetamide. Samples were divided into equal aliquots and digested with trypsin, Lys-C, Asp-N, pepsin, chymotrypsin, Glu-C, elastase, trypsin/Lys-C, trypsin/Glu-C, or trypsin/elastase. Peptides were desalted and enriched using ZipTip C18 tips (Merck KGaA, catalog ZTC18S096).

Peptides were analyzed on an UltiMate 3000 LC system coupled to an Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). Peptides were separated on a reversed-phase C18 column at a flow rate of 300 nL/min using a 40-min gradient with 0.1% formic acid in water as buffer A and 0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile as buffer B. Data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition mode with a 2.5-s cycle time. Full MS scans were acquired in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 60,000 over an m/z range of 400–1800. MS/MS spectra were acquired in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 15,000 using HCD (30% normalized collision energy) and EThcD with supplemental activation energies of 25, 55, and 75%. Dynamic exclusion was set to 10 s after one observation.

De novo sequencing and characterization

The obtained data were analyzed using PEAKS Studio 10.6 (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc., Waterloo, ON, Canada) for peptide identification and de novo sequencing. Searches were performed with precursor and fragment mass error tolerances of 0.1 and 0.2 Da, respectively. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, whereas oxidation of methionine and deamidation of asparagine and glutamine were set as variable modifications. Enzyme specificity was assigned according to the digestion condition for each sample. Peptides were identified by combining NCBI database searches with de novo sequencing. Leucine and isoleucine residues were distinguished using LC-MS/MS data from pepsin and chymotrypsin digestion, based on the preferential cleavage of these enzymes at leucine sites. Overlapping peptides were assembled to generate full-length FLC sequences.

The raw files from LC-MS/MS were searched with novor.cloud platform (https://novor.cloud/)28 for validation. Sequences were analyzed using IMGT/DomainGapAlign (http://imgt.org/)29 to determine V-region amino acid properties, following IMGT-numbering/nomenclature for Ig domain FR/CDR delineation. The advanced AI model AlphaFold3 was employed for protein structure prediction (https://alphafoldserver.com/)30. The top-ranked models were used for structural analysis. The models showed pTM scores of 0.78 for κWJJ and 0.77 for κWJJm30, while the analyzed CDR1 regions showed high local confidence with pLDDT scores >90. Pairwise Structure Alignment Tool was then utilized to align the protein 3D structures31.

Protease treatment

In 0.2-mL PCR tubes, FLCs (0.5 μg/μl) were incubated with human cathepsins (0.2–0.72 μg/reaction, 50 μL/reaction), CtsB (Sigma, catalog C8571-25UG), CtsD (Sigma, catalog C8696-25UG), and CtsL (Sigma, catalog C6854-25UG) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The reaction buffer for CtsB and CtsD contained 50 mM NaOAc, 8 mM DTT, and 4 mM EDTA at pH 5 and 4, respectively. For CtsL, the buffer contained 400 mM NaOAc, 8 mM DTT, and 4 mM EDTA, at pH 5.5. The degradation of FLCs by pepsin (Solarbio, P8390-5g) was also tested, with 0.5 μg/μl of FLC reacting with 0.02 μg/μl pepsin in 50 mM Glycine-HCl (pH 1.5) buffer. The reaction mixture was agitated in a microplate thermostatic oscillator (Allsheng, Hangzhou) at 200 rpm at 37 °C for 96 h. At the endpoint, samples were mixed with Protein Sample Loading Buffer (Denaturing, Reducing, 5×) (Epizyme, catalog LT101S) and heated at 100 °C for 5 min. Equal amounts of the mixture (1–2 μl) were separated using LabPAGE 12% Tris-MOPS Gel (Lablead, catalog P01212) or 15% Tris-Glycine Gel (Lablead, catalog P0155) electrophoresis.

Silver staining

The gels were subjected to silver staining using the Fast Silver Stain Kit (Beyotime, catalog P0017S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, gels were fixed in a solution containing 50% ethanol and 10% acetic acid, washed with 30% ethanol, and then rinsed with ultrapure water. The gels were then sensitized, rinsed, stained with silver staining solution, rinsed again, developed until visible protein bands appeared, and stopped using the stop solution supplied with the kit. The stained gels were rinsed with ultrapure water and photographed.

Gel electrophoresis and LC-MS/MS

Protease degradation samples were run on a 12% SDS-PAGE gel and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) Fast Staining Solution (Solarbio, catalog G4540). The bands of interest were excised, digested with trypsin, desalted for LC-MS/MS analysis on a Fusion Lumos Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mass spectrometry analysis was performed in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode with all tandem mass spectra produced using higher-energy collision dissociation (HCD) method. Raw files were analyzed using the pFind Studio32. Database searches were conducted with a parent mass error tolerance of 20 p.p.m. and fragment mass error tolerance of 0.2 Da. Carbamidomethylation (cysteine) was set as a fixed modification, while oxidation (methionine) and deamidation (asparagine and glutamine) were set as variable modifications.

Production of FLCs in eukaryotic expression systems

After obtaining the FLC sequence, protein was produced using a standard method. Recombinant protein production was performed by GenScript (Nanjing, China). Briefly, codon-optimized FLC sequences with a leading peptide sequence were synthesized, cloned into an expression vector, and transfected into CHO-express cells for recombinant protein expression. The cell culture supernatant containing secreted FLCs was harvested for purification. FLCs were purified by affinity chromatography as previously described and analyzed by SDS-PAGE and western blotting.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy

CD measurements were conducted on a J-1400 CD spectrometer (Jasco, Inc., Easton, MD). Experiments were conducted using 10 μM protein in phosphate buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), using 1-mm quartz cuvettes. Far UV-CD spectra between 200 and 260 nm were acquired in triplicate at 25 °C. Protein thermal denaturation were recorded from 20 to 80 °C at 205 nm using a heating rate of 1 °C/min. The melting temperature (Tm) was determined by fitting the thermal denaturation data to a Boltzmann sigmoidal function.

Animal studies

All animal experiments were performed according to the guidelines and procedures approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (S2025378). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Six-week-old female C57BL/6J mice, weighing approximately 15 g, were allocated to three groups (n = 6 mice per group) and intraperitoneally injected with normal saline (vehicle control) or a single 100 mg dose of recombinant FLCs (κWJJ or κWJJm30) dissolved in normal saline. This group size was justified by the “resource equation” method33, providing a necessary buffer against potential attrition while ensuring sufficient statistical power. The mice were humanely euthanized 72 h later by carbon dioxide asphyxiation. The mouse kidneys were examined by light microscopy, TEM, and IEM. Mouse kidney tissue sections, fixed in 10% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin, were stained using PAS. For ultrastructural examination, kidney samples were fixed with 4% glutaraldehyde in PBS. Ultrathin sections were prepared and subjected to uranyl acetate and lead citrate staining for conventional contrast. Immunogold labeling was performed using a rabbit anti-human kappa-light chain antibody (DAKO Omnis, catalog GA506; 1:1000) followed by a 10-nm gold-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Aurion, catalog 110.011; 1:40). Subsequently, these sections were examined with a JEM-1400Flash transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). FLC concentrations were measured in 24-h urine, with simultaneous blood samples drawn via retro-orbital puncture under anesthesia.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA) and Origin 2024b software (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). For comparisons between two groups, a two-sided Mann–Whitney U test was used for nonparametric data. A P value < 0.05 was considered significant. Results are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise indicated. Sample sizes are provided in the corresponding figure legends.
