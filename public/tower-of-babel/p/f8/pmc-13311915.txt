Low-molecular-weight polysaccharide and polyol impregnation enhances the rehydration recovery capacity of freeze-dried potato slices
Authors: Xueqian Wan, Xuemei Gao, Hongwei Xiao, Wei Zhang, Jingyao Zhang, Qinshu Ma, Xinyi Li, Xiaoyue Zhu, Tianlan Xia, Haiou Wang, Huichang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFresh fruits and vegetables often undergo irreversible cellular damage during freeze-drying and subsequent rehydration, preventing recovery of their original moisture status, microstructure, and texture. This study investigated the effects of impregnation with low-molecular-weight polysaccharide and polyol protectants, including glucose, trehalose, sorbitol, and glycerol, on the rehydration recovery capacity of freeze-dried potato slices. The rehydrated samples treated with sorbitol exhibited a higher rehydration ratio and moisture content (dry basis), and a moisture distribution more similar to that of fresh samples, as well as lower relative electrical conductivity and improved overall textural properties (hardness and chewiness). Microstructural analyses (by optical microscopy and transmission electron microscopy) and assessment of cell viability (by trypan blue staining) confirmed that sorbitol reduced cell wall and membrane damage in the rehydrated tissues, resulting in a microstructure resembling that of fresh potatoes. These findings provide a basis for using these protectants to improve freeze-dried and rehydrated products.Keywords: Low molecular weight, Polysaccharides, Polyols, Potato slices, Vacuum freeze-drying, Rehydration, Recovery

Introduction

Potatoes are globally important and widely cultivated crops and rich in carbohydrates, vitamins, and minerals (Zaheer & Akhtar, 2016). In modern diets, they serve as a staple food and vegetable, as well as a raw material for snacks and convenience foods. Fresh potatoes have a high water content, making them prone to sprouting and spoilage, which increases storage and transportation costs (Gupta & Gupta, 2019). Processing fresh potatoes into dried products enables long-term storage, reduces transportation costs, and enhances postharvest economic benefits. Among available methods, vacuum freeze-drying removes moisture through sublimation under low temperatures and vacuum conditions, effectively preserving nutritional content and structural integrity (He et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025). It is considered one of the best methods for producing high-quality dried fruits and vegetables.

Rehydration is the reverse process of freeze-drying and is necessary before consumption or further processing. It aims to restore texture, flavor, and nutritional value, returning the product as closely as possible to its fresh state. However, irreversible changes during freeze-drying, such as mechanical damage caused by ice crystal formation, along with cell membrane rupture, protein denaturation, and cell wall structural collapse, result in severe cellular damage and death (Wolkers & Oldenhof, 2020). Consequently, the original cellular network and functional structures cannot be fully restored after rehydration. This leads to texture softening, reduced water-holding capacity, nutrient loss, and color deterioration, significantly lowering product quality compared to that of fresh materials (Krokida & Philippopoulos, 2005; Sagar & Suresh Kumar, 2010).

Rehydration recovery capacity is a comprehensive property reflecting the quality of dried fruit and vegetable products. It is mainly used to evaluate the extent to which dried fruits and vegetables can recover their fresh (or near-fresh) state after rehydration, as reflected in rehydration capacity, moisture status, texture recovery, cell viability, microstructure recovery, and so on. Currently, the rehydration recovery capacity and quality of freeze-dried fruits and vegetables have received significant attention, with research focusing primarily on the dynamic behavior of various materials, such as the rehydration ratio, as well as changes in the cellular structure (Ammelt et al., 2021; Benseddik et al., 2019; Marques et al., 2009). Qiu et al., 2021, Qiu et al., 2022 reported significant structural collapse of freeze-dried shiitake mushrooms after rehydration due to loss of cell membrane integrity, mechanical damage caused by ice crystals, and protein denaturation. Protein denaturation alters cell-wall rigidity and porosity, while structural loosening during freeze-drying significantly improves the hydration ability of the shiitake mushroom fiber matrix. Zhou et al. (2021) evaluated various rehydration methods for dried garlic slices and found that freeze-dried garlic slices exhibited high water absorption and a porous structure. However, residual internal air bubbles hindered water absorption and reduced water-holding capacity.

Low-molecular-weight polysaccharide and polyol (LMWPP), including glucose (MW 180.16 g/mol), trehalose (MW 342.30 g/mol), sorbitol (MW 182.17 g/mol), and glycerol (MW 92.09 g/mol), exhibit excellent hydration ability and permeation regulation properties due to their polyhydroxyl groups and are widely used in fruit and vegetable processing. In recent years, LMWPP solutions have been applied as osmotic pretreatments to improve freeze-drying efficiency and product quality (Dziki, 2020; Liu et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2023). Numerous studies have shown that these low-molecular-weight polyhydroxy compounds (polysaccharides and polyols) also function as freeze-drying protectants by lowering the freezing point, inhibiting ice crystal growth, increasing the glass transition temperature (Tg), and replacing water via hydrogen bonding during dehydration (Aschenbrenner et al., 2015; Jia et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2019). However, limited research has examined their effects on rehydration recovery capacity after freeze-drying, warranting further investigation.

Furthermore, pulsed electric fields (PEF) have been widely recognized as an efficient non-thermal pretreatment to enhance mass transfer, impregnation efficiency, and cellular permeabilization during drying and rehydration of plant tissues (Barba et al., 2020; Fauster et al., 2020). Previous studies have confirmed that PEF can effectively improve rehydration performance and microstructure preservation of freeze-dried products (Fauster et al., 2020). In addition, hybrid technologies combining PEF with bio-protective agents such as trehalose have been developed to further enhance structural stability and rehydration capacity (Tylewicz et al., 2017). However, few studies have focused on the use of LMWPP alone as impregnation protectants to improve rehydration and microstructure recovery of freeze-dried potato slices without PEF. Therefore, this study provides a complementary and practical strategy that can be used independently or integrated with emerging electro-technologies to upgrade the quality of freeze-dried fruit and vegetable products.

This study used potatoes as a model system. Four types of LMWPP protectant solutions—glucose, trehalose, sorbitol, and glycerol—were used to impregnate potato slices prior to freeze-drying. The freeze-dried products were subsequently rehydrated to analyze their rehydration ability and assess moisture state, texture, cell viability, and microstructure. These findings provide a practical basis for applying LMWPP to improve the quality of freeze-dried fruits and vegetables.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fresh potatoes (Kexin No.1, Inner Mongolia, China) were purchased from the Nanjing Suguo Supermarket. For each experiment, a single batch of fresh potato samples was meticulously selected to ensure the absence of mechanical damage and uniformity in size (about 10 cm in diameter and 200 g in weight) and stored at 4 °C in a refrigerator prior to use. Food-grade glucose, trehalose, sorbitol, and glycerol were obtained from Shijiazhuang Ruixue Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.

Preparation of LMWPP-impregnated potato slices

Impregnated potato slices were prepared in triplicate according to the methods described by Sun et al. (2024) and Liu et al. (2025). Fresh potatoes were washed and sliced into round discs (20-mm diameter and 5-mm thickness). For each treatment, 40 potato slices were placed in a 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask, and a 10% (w/w) protectant solution (glucose, trehalose, sorbitol, or glycerol dissolved separately in distilled water) was added at a sample-to-solution ratio of 1:4 (mass ratio). Following this, the Erlenmeyer flasks with the potato slices were placed in a vacuum glass container (30 cm in diameter), applying a pressure of 10 kPa for 10 min using a vacuum pump (SHZ-111, Jiangsu Runhong Science and Education Equipment Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) and restoring atmospheric pressure for 50 min. After vacuum impregnation, the potato slices were carefully removed, surface moisture was drained using a filter paper, and vacuum freeze-drying was performed. Non-impregnated potato slices were used as the controls.

Preparation of freeze-dried potato slices

Potato slices from five groups (control, glucose, trehalose, sorbitol, glycerol) were freeze-dried in triplicate using a vacuum freeze-dryer (SCIENTZ-50F; Ningbo Sante Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Ningbo, China). The freeze-drying temperature program was set as follows: −40 °C for 4 h (freezing stage at atmospheric pressure), then −30 °C for 2 h, −20 °C for 2 h, −10 °C for 2 h, 0 °C for 2 h, 10 °C for 2 h, 20 °C for 2 h, 30 °C for 2 h, and finally 40 °C for 4 h. After the initial freezing stage (−40 °C for 4 h) was completed, the vacuum pump was turned on to maintain the chamber pressure below 100 Pa for the remaining steps. After 22 h of freeze-drying, five groups of freeze-dried potato slices with a moisture content of less than 5% wet basis were obtained.

Rehydration of freeze-dried potato slices

Freeze-dried samples were immersed in distilled water with a sample-to-water ratio of 1:10 (mass ratio) at 25 °C for 40 min. The samples were then removed and gently blotted with filter paper to eliminate surface water. The rehydrated potato slices were ready for the measurement of the following indicators.

Moisture content measurement

The moisture content (dry basis) of the rehydrated and fresh potato slices was determined using the direct-drying method specified in the national standard GB 5009.3-2016 (GB, 2016). Grind the sample to a particle size of less than 2 mm. Accurately weigh approximately 2 g of the sample and spread it evenly in a weighing bottle with a diameter of 50 mm and a height of 30 mm. Record the total mass of the weighing bottle and the sample. Place the weighing bottle into an electric thermostatic drying oven (DHG-9070, Shanghai Xinmiao Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) with the lid partially open to allow moisture to escape. Dry at 105 °C for 3 h, then remove the bottle, close the lid, and cool it in a desiccator for 0.5 h before weighing. Return the bottle to the oven for an additional drying period of about 1 h, then cool and weigh again. Repeat this process until the mass difference between two consecutive weighings is ≤ 2 mg, which is considered constant weight (dried to constant mass). Relative electrical conductivity (REC)

The REC of the rehydrated and fresh potato slices was determined using the method described by Wang et al. (2022) with a few modifications. After rehydration, two samples were randomly selected for each treatment. They were then washed with deionized water to avoid introducing any exogenous ions that may affect conductivity and placed in a 100-mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 50 mL of deionized water. The initial conductivity (E0) was measured using a conductivity meter (FE38-Standard; Mettler-Toledo Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Subsequently, the Erlenmeyer flask was sealed with plastic wrap and incubated for 1 h at 25 °C using a shaker incubator (BS-1EA; Changzhou Runhua Electric Appliance Co., Ltd., Changzhou, China), after which the second conductivity (E1) was measured again. Subsequently, the solution in the Erlenmeyer flask was boiled at 100 °C for 20 min on a hot plate and then cooled under running tap water to approximately 25 °C, and deionized water was added to the original volume after boiling. Texture profile analysis (TPA)

Rehydrated and fresh potato slices were subjected to TPA using a texture analyzer (TMS-PRO, Food Technology Corporation, VA, USA), following the parameter settings described by Xuan et al. (2025). The TPA test was performed using the TPA-1000 N program with P50 probe; the parameters were set to a trigger force of 0.5 N, a starting height of 20 mm, a deformation of 30%, and a test speed of 60 mm/min. Texture-related parameters (hardness, cohesiveness, springiness, and chewiness) were measured by mechanically compressing each sample twice. Three measurements were performed per group, and the results were averaged.

Puncture test

The rehydrated and fresh potato slices were subjected to a puncture test using a TMS-PRO texture analyzer to obtain the local mechanical properties of the sample (penetration hardness and penetration work), following the parameter settings described by Xuan et al. (2025). The puncture-1000 N program with P/5 probe was used; the parameters were set to an initial force of 0.5 N, a puncture depth of 2 mm, a return distance of 20 mm, a test speed of 60 mm/min. Three measurements were performed per group, and the results were averaged.

Sensory evaluation

The sensory evaluation was performed according to the method described by Yang et al. (2023). The samples in each group were evaluated for crispness, stickiness, color, sweetness, flavor, appearance, cooling effects of polyols, and potential aftertaste. The sensory evaluation panel was composed of ten voluntary students majoring in food science from Nanjing Xiaozhuang University. They were well trained in basic sensory evaluation skills. Their mouths were rinsed with water before evaluation, and the samples were evaluated on a 9-point intensity scale. Not intense corresponded with “1” and the strongest intensity corresponded with “9”. The scale indicated the intensity of the corresponding indicator of the samples in order from lowest to highest. Samples were distributed randomly for scoring, and the score values were averaged. This study focused solely on assessing food sensory quality and did not involve invasive procedures or health risks. It was conducted in accordance with institutional research management regulations and sensory evaluation protocols. All evaluators participated with full knowledge and voluntary consent.

Low-frequency nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) analyses

The transverse relaxation times (T2) of the rehydrated and fresh samples were measured using an LF-NMR analyzer (NMI-20-025V-I, Niumag Electric Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), following the parameter settings described by Sun et al. (2024). One potato slice sample in each group was randomly selected and subjected to the test. The parameters of the multi-pulse echo Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) sequence were as follows: main frequency (SF) = 20 MHz, testing points (TD) = 750,000, time waiting (TW) = 4000 ms, two peak intervals (TE) = 0.25 ms, number of points of determination (NECH) = 15,000, the interval between points (SW) = 200 kHz, and number of scans (NS) = 16. The transverse relaxation distribution curves and the individual T₂ peak areas of the protons in the potato samples were obtained. Three measurements were performed per group, and the results were averaged. The mathematical relationship between the moisture content (dry basis, y) and total peak area (x) was fitted using the six models in Table S1, employing the linear fit and nonlinear curve fit modules in Origin 2022 software (OriginLab).

MRI (NMI-20-025V-I; Niumag Electric Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was performed to visualize the moisture distribution in the rehydrated and fresh samples using proton density-weighted images, following the parameter settings described by Sun et al. (2024). One potato slice sample in each group was randomly selected and subjected to the test. The main parameters were as follows: SF = 20 MHz, field of view = 68 × 68 mm, slice gap = 1.6 mm, slice width = 3.8 mm, repetition time (TR) = 1000 ms, echo time (TE) = 20 ms, average = 4. The resulting images were processed in pseudocolor (version 14.2; Mestrenova, Shanghai, China) and exported in bitmap format. Three measurements were performed per group, and the typical images were used.

Assessment of cell viability

The method described by Liu et al. (2025) was used to assess cell viability, in which the rehydrated and fresh samples were cut into less than 1 mm thick slices, then stained with 0.4% trypan blue solution for 2 min. The dyed potato slices were placed in 70% chloral solution (a decolorizing solution) that was changed every 5 h until the solution became light blue or colorless. The decolorized potato slices were placed on an LED light panel (A3, Shenzhen Huibao Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) and photographed with a camera. Three measurements were performed per group, and the typical images were used.

Microstructure analysis by optical microscopy (OM)

OM (Eclipse LV150N; Nikon Solutions Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) of the rehydrated and fresh samples was performed according to the method described by Sun et al. (2024). The rehydrated and fresh samples were formed into 10 mm × 10 mm × 5 mm slices, which were then fixed in a formalin-acetic acid-alcohol solution (FAA, 90% ethanol, 5% acetic acid, and 5% formalin, v/v/v) for 24 h at 4 °C. The fixed samples were eluted with ethanol, processed using an automatic histoprocessor, and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded tissues were then sliced into 5-μm sections with a tissue slicer, baked to remove paraffin, stained with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS), and finally sealed on glass slides for optical microscopy.

Microstructure analysis by transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM (Model JEM-1400; JEOL Inc., Tokyo, Japan) observations for the rehydrated and fresh samples were conducted according to the method described by Sun et al. (2024). The rehydrated and fresh samples were cut into 1 mm × 1 mm pieces, fixed in 3.5% glutaraldehyde phosphate buffer, flushed with 0.1 mol/L Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS, pH 7.4), fixed in 1% osmium acid (OsO4), and flushed again in 0.1 mol/L PBS. The samples were then dehydrated in graded ethanol solutions of 30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 95%, and 100% (v/v), followed by propylene oxide. They were then embedded in Spurr resin and polymerized for 48 h at 60 °C. The samples were pruned and cut into thin slices using an LKB ultramicrotome, stained, and observed under TEM at ×7000 magnifications to obtain micrographs. All the microstructural examinations were performed at 25 °C.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were expressed as mean values with corresponding standard deviations and were analyzed and plotted using Origin 2022 software (OriginLab). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey's honestly significant difference (Tukey HSD) tests were performed using SPSS software (version 19.0; IBM, Chicago, IL, USA), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Rehydration ratio and moisture content of the rehydrated potato slices

RR is a key indicator of the ability of dried products to recover their original state. It reflects the degree of internal looseness of the dried products. A higher RR indicates shorter impregnation time required before cooking or consumption and improved restoration of texture and juiciness. The RR of the five rehydrated samples is shown in Fig. 1. The RR of the glucose, trehalose, and sorbitol groups of samples did not show significant differences (p > 0.05), and was significantly higher than the control group samples (p < 0.05), with increases of 11.11%, 29.17%, and 23.15%, respectively, compared to the control group samples. There was no significant difference in the RR between the glycerol and control group samples (p > 0.05). This may be attributable to the migration of glucose, trehalose, and sorbitol into potato tissue, where their hydroxyl structures are more conducive to cross-linking with cell-wall components (cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin substances), thereby enhancing structural integrity (Said et al., 2023). During freeze-drying, the tissue framework was better preserved, forming a honeycomb porous structure with unobstructed water channels, facilitating water absorption during rehydration (Qian & Zhang, 2011). Therefore, the rehydration rate was relatively high, which contributed to achieving a higher RR.

Rehydration ratio and moisture content (dry basis) of fresh-cut potato slices (fresh) and rehydrated slices with different protectant treatments (control, glucose, trehalose, sorbitol, glycerol).

Another possible reason could be that these LMWPP protectants exhibit glass transition behavior. Compounds with higher glass transition temperature (Tg) more readily form stable glassy states during freeze-drying and storage, preserving tissue structure and preventing collapse (Guerrero Sanchez et al., 2022). Glycerol, with a very low Tg (approximately −93 °C), exhibits strong hygroscopicity and plasticizing properties that may promote structural collapse and channel blockage, thereby reducing rehydration capacity. Consequently, the RR of the glycerol group was significantly lower than that of other treatment groups.

The moisture content was closely related to the RR, and the magnitude of the RR determined the moisture content of the rehydrated samples. Therefore, the five groups of rehydrated samples showed different moisture contents corresponding to their RR values, but all were significantly lower than that of the fresh potato slices (p < 0.05). The moisture content of the six groups decreased in the order: fresh > glucose, trehalose, and sorbitol > control and glycerol, with significant differences between adjacent groups (p < 0.05). Compared to the control group, the moisture content of the rehydrated samples in the glucose, trehalose, and sorbitol groups increased by 19.70%, 51.52%, and 40.91%, respectively (with no significant differences among the three groups, p > 0.05). However, these values reached only 39.90%, 50.51%, and 46.97% of the moisture content of the fresh sample, indicating that after rehydration, freeze-dried potato slices failed to restore the original moisture level of the fresh tissue.

LF-NMR and MRI analyses of the rehydrated potato slices

The transverse relaxation time (T2) measured by LF-NMR reflects the degrees of freedom of water in the potato samples, i.e., the extent to which water molecules can undergo translational, rotational, and vibrational motions. A shorter T2 indicates stronger binding forces on the protons and thus smaller degrees of freedom. Degrees of freedom can measure the extent to which water molecules can undergo translational, rotational, and vibrational motions within the sample. A higher signal intensity and peak area indicate more protons in the samples (Hansen et al., 2010; Luo et al., 2020). Fig. 2 shows the transverse relaxation distribution curves of the protons in the potato samples. Three distinct T2 relaxation peaks were observed in all samples: T21 corresponded to water chemically bound to cellular tissue components, T22 corresponded to immobile semi-bound water constrained by physical retention and gel network structure, and T23 corresponded to free water with high fluidity between cell tissues (Hu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022).

Curves of LF-NMR transverse relaxation time T2 of fresh-cut potato slices (fresh) and rehydrated slices with different protectant treatments (control, glucose, trehalose, sorbitol, glycerol).

Compared to the fresh samples, the T21 and T22 peaks of rehydrated control samples shifted to the right, indicating that after freeze-drying and rehydrating, the chemically bound water and immobile semi-bound water in the sample tissue decreased, and the degree of freedom increased. This likely resulted from irreversible damage during freeze-drying, including cell-wall and cell-membrane disruption, protein denaturation, and starch chain recrystallization (Nath et al., 2022). These changes compromised the original hydrophilic gel network formed by starch and pectin within the tissue, thereby reducing the capacity of rehydrated freeze-dried samples to restore tightly bound water (Said et al., 2023). The number of polar binding sites linked with bound water (T21), such as –NH2 and –COOH groups of proteins and –OH groups of starch, is reduced, along with their binding ability. The ability of the immobile semi-bound water (T22) to be trapped by the semi-permeable membrane and the gel network structure also decreased. Therefore, the fluidity of the water molecules in the same phase increased, and the LF-NMR relaxation time increased; thus, the T21 and T22 peaks of the rehydrated samples shifted to the right (Song et al., 2018).

Compared to the untreated control group, the positions of the T21, T22, and T23 peaks of glucose, trehalose, sorbitol, and glycerol-treated samples shifted to shorter relaxation times to varying degrees. This leftward shift indicates that the protectant treatment resulted in a decrease in the degree of freedom and an increase in the binding force of the chemically bound water, immobile semi-bound water, and free water in the rehydrated samples. This may be because these protectants have certain abilities for glassy state formation and water substitution, which change the physical state and chemical interactions of the intracellular microenvironment of potato tissue during freeze-drying (Van Engeland et al., 2025). Moreover, the protectant itself contains abundant hydroxyl (-OH) hydrophilic polar groups, which exhibit strong hygroscopicity and plasticization in the matrix. This enhances the binding force between water molecules and the matrix (including proteins, starch, and cell walls) and limits the fluidity of water in rehydrated tissues.

Fig. 3 shows the MRI proton density-weighted images of the potato samples. The signal intensity reflects the number of protons in the water molecules, thereby visualizing the moisture distribution in the sample. The higher the proton density, the higher the moisture content of the sample, leading to a stronger signal in the image and a redder hue (Agudelo-Laverde et al., 2013). The fresh sample showed the highest density of protons in the MRI image (Fig. 3a), while the rehydrated samples in the sorbitol (Fig. 3e), trehalose (Fig. 3d), and glucose (Fig. 3c) groups showed a significantly lower MRI proton density in their images. The images of the rehydrated samples in the control and glycerol groups appeared with the lowest proton density, which is consistent with the difference in moisture content of the potato samples in Fig. 2. The MRI and T₂ relaxation spectra suggested that, among the four protectant pretreatments, the freeze-dried and rehydrated potato samples protected by sorbitol exhibited a moisture status and distribution most comparable to those of the fresh samples, but still fell far short of the fresh samples.

MRI pseudo-color images of fresh-cut potato slices (a) and rehydrated slices with different protectant treatments: (b) control, (c) glucose, (d) trehalose, (e) sorbitol, (f) glycerol.

The peak areas and proportions of T2 relaxation spectra of the potato samples in each group are listed in Table S2. The peak area of T23 in each group of samples was significantly higher than that of T21 and T22. The proportion of T23 peak area in each group of samples ranged from 60 to 80%, indicating that the moisture in the sample tissue was mainly composed of free water that flowed easily, followed by immobile semi-bound water, whereas chemically bound water accounted for the least proportion. From Table S2, it can also be observed that the peak areas and total peak areas of the T21, T22, and T23 moisture states in the fresh group samples were significantly higher than those in the other groups, indicating that there were still significant differences in the moisture content and moisture state distribution between the five rehydrated samples and the original fresh samples. From the three aspects of the total peak area, peak area of each group, and the proportion of peak area, it can be comprehensively judged that the moisture status of the rehydrated samples of the sorbitol group is relatively closer to that of fresh samples than the control group, although it still differs markedly from the fresh samples.

Many existing studies have shown a close relationship between the LF-NMR peak area and moisture content in food samples (Chen et al., 2022; Shao & Li, 2013). In this study, six models (linear, quadratic polynomial, allometric, exponential, logistic, and Gompertz) were fitted to describe the mathematical relationship between the moisture content in dry basis (y) and total peak area (x) fresh and rehydrated potato slices, as shown in Table S1. Among these models, the linear and allometric models yielded high coefficients of determination (R2 = 0.92296 and 0.92279, respectively) and adjusted R2 values (0.90370 and 0.90348, respectively), both exceeding 0.9. The quadratic polynomial and Gompertz models exhibited lower adjusted R2 (0.8716 and 0.87174), while the exponential and logistic models showed considerably lower adjusted R2 (0.80782 and 0.81140), indicating poorer predictive ability. Moreover, the linear and models are parsimonious and have been widely used for moisture prediction by LF-NMR measurement (Chen et al., 2022; Li et al., 2018; Nielsen et al., 2016). In contrast, the higher-order polynomial and sigmoidal models (logistic, Gompertz) risk overfitting given the limited data range and do not provide a clear improvement in adjusted R2. Therefore, the linear and allometric models were considered suitable for predicting the moisture content in dry basis of potato samples from the LF-NMR total peak area. The other models were not selected due to their lower adjusted R2 and/or excessive parameter complexity.

Relative electrical conductivity of the rehydrated potato slices

REC can be used to determine the degree of damage to the cell tissue (Gurova & Denisyuk, 2021). The RECs of the six potato-slice sample groups are shown in Fig. 4. The control and glycerol-treated groups of the rehydrated samples showed the highest REC (no significant difference between the two), followed by the rehydrated samples in the sorbitol, trehalose, and glucose groups (no significant difference among the three groups). The fresh sample showed the smallest REC (p < 0.05). All rehydrated samples showed substantially higher REC values than fresh samples, indicating that freeze-drying and rehydration cause severe cellular damage. The increase in REC is attributed to ice crystal-induced mechanical disruption, osmotic stress during freezing, and the loss of membrane-bound water during drying, which collectively compromise membrane integrity and lead to electrolyte leakage upon rehydration (Monalisa et al., 2025; Nath et al., 2022). However, compared with the control group, treatment with sorbitol, trehalose, and glucose significantly reduced REC. This protective effect can be explained by the water replacement hypothesis, where LMWPP hydroxyl groups (-OH) form hydrogen bonds with membrane phospholipids to maintain bilayer fluidity, and the vitrification theory, where LMWPP forms a stable glassy matrix that immobilizes membranes during dehydration and rehydration (Deng et al., 2023; Guerrero Sanchez et al., 2022; Wolkers & Oldenhof, 2020). These mechanisms help minimize structural damage to cells, reduce leakage of intracellular components, and effectively lower REC.

Textural properties of the rehydrated potato slices

TPA was used to evaluate the overall textural properties including hardness, cohesion, springiness, and chewiness (Table 1). Hardness reflects the compressive strength of a sample under specific strains and is closely related to the microstructure of the sample. The hardness of rehydrated samples is very low, while the hardness of fresh samples is much higher, indicating that freeze-drying and rehydration transformed the tissue from a dense, firm structure to a loose and soft one. The hardness of the rehydrated samples in the sorbitol and trehalose groups was significantly higher than that in the other three groups, indicating that these two protectants had a certain strengthening effect on the hardness of the rehydrated samples.

Cohesion describes the internal binding force of food, reflecting its ability to resist breakage and maintain integrity during chewing. The lower the cohesiveness, the more easily the sample breaks apart when chewed (Guo, 2021). The cohesiveness of the five groups of rehydrated samples did not show significant differences, but was significantly higher than that of fresh samples, indicating that the fresh sample tissue was brittle and fragile, while the rehydrated sample tissue was soft, tough, and not easily broken or scattered. Springiness reflects the deformation recovery ability of a sample under the action of a compressive strain release. Fresh samples had the highest springiness, followed by the glucose, trehalose, and sorbitol rehydrated samples, whereas the control group and glycerol rehydrated samples had the lowest springiness.

Chewiness is a comprehensive indicator of the effort required to chew a sample to a swallowed state. This indicator is the product of hardness, cohesiveness, and springiness, and can comprehensively reflect the texture and quality of the sample (Chen et al., 2026). The chewiness and hardness indicators of samples from each group were similar (Table 1). The chewiness of the fresh samples was much greater than that of the five rehydrated samples, and the chewiness of the rehydrated samples in the sorbitol and trehalose groups was significantly higher than that of the other three rehydrated samples.

Fresh potato slices contain intact cells with robust cell walls. The intracellular spaces are densely filled with starch granules and water. The cells are turgid, and during simulated mastication, these intact structures provide substantial resistance, resulting in high overall hardness and chewiness. Although freeze-drying technology can achieve rapid rehydration by creating porous structures, freezing and dehydration operations physically disrupt the original cellular structure of tissues and the natural conformation of biopolymers. Rehydration replenishes water content but does not repair structural damage. Consequently, rehydrated samples lack the dense texture and turgor pressure characteristics of fresh potatoes, with no swelling pressure and a soft structure, resulting in a significant decrease in indicators related to structural strength, such as hardness and chewiness, in TPA testing. After entering the tissue, sorbitol and trehalose protectants crosslink with biomolecules such as cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin, starch, etc. in the cellular tissue, which can improve the firmness and stability of the cytoskeleton network of the rehydrated product to a certain extent, resulting in relatively high hardness and chewiness of the rehydrated product (Vicente et al., 2012).

Puncture testing evaluated local mechanical properties of the potato samples, including the penetration hardness and work (Table 1). The penetration hardness of each group of samples from high to low was as follows: glucose group>control group and glycerol group (showing no significant difference) > trehalose group and sorbitol group (showing no significant difference) > fresh group (p < 0.05). The penetration work of each group of samples, from high to low, was as follows: glucose group>control group, trehalose group, and sorbitol group (showing no significant difference) > glycerol group>fresh group (p < 0.05). The penetration hardness and work of all rehydrated potato samples were significantly higher than those of fresh potato samples, indicating that the ability of rehydrated sample tissues to resist local damage is higher than that of fresh potato tissues, which presents a result opposite to the overall textural properties of TPA.

This may be because fresh potato tissue is tightly packed with intact, water-filled, thin-walled cells with high cell turgor pressure and crisp cell walls (Edwards, 1995). During puncture testing, a 5-mm diameter cylindrical probe can easily penetrate multiple layers of cell walls, resulting in relatively low puncture resistance and work. However, after freeze-drying and rehydration, fresh potatoes cannot recover their original state of fresh tissues. Their structure is no longer a cell system but is based on the original cell wall skeleton. It is a continuous, porous, sponge-like gel network formed by the re-crosslinking of cell wall matrices, such as pectin, cellulose, starch, cell contents, rehydration water, and other components. Although the network system is loose and soft (resulting in low hardness of TPA), its gel skeleton itself may have higher local toughness and strength owing to the interaction of macromolecular substances, such as retrogradation starch, denatured protein, pectin, and cellulose (Wang et al., 2025). Therefore, when using a 5-mm diameter cylindrical probe to locally destroy potato tissues, a greater puncture force needs to be exerted.

Sensory evaluation of the rehydrated potato slices

The sensory evaluation results for all groups are presented in Table S3. The fresh sample scored highest in crispness, color, and appearance (p < 0.05). Among rehydrated slices, the glucose, trehalose, and sorbitol groups showed significantly higher crispness than the control and glycerol groups (p < 0.05). For stickiness, all four LMWPP-treated groups scored significantly higher than the fresh sample (p < 0.05) but showed no significant difference from the control (p > 0.05).

No significant differences in color or appearance were found among all rehydrated groups (p > 0.05). For flavor, the sorbitol group scored highest, comparable to the fresh sample and significantly higher than the control (p < 0.05). The glycerol group exhibited the highest sweetness and aftertaste scores, possibly due to the lingering taste of glycerol.

All rehydrated groups had significantly lower total sensory scores than the fresh sample (p < 0.05), but no significant differences existed among them (p > 0.05). Numerically, sorbitol and glycerol had the highest totals, followed by glucose and trehalose, with the control lowest. In conclusion, LMWPP protectants partially improved specific attributes (e.g., crispness by glucose/trehalose/sorbitol; flavor by sorbitol; sweetness/aftertaste by glycerol), but total scores showed no statistical improvement. Sorbitol appeared most favorable for texture, flavor, and overall acceptability.

It is important to clarify whether the applied protectant concentration (10% w/w) is sensorially relevant. According to our sensory results (Table S3), the glycerol-treated group exhibited significantly higher sweetness and aftertaste scores than other groups, indicating that 10% glycerol imparts a perceptible sweet taste and lingering mouthfeel. In contrast, glucose, trehalose, and sorbitol at the same concentration showed moderate effects on sweetness and aftertaste, with no significant differences from the control in most cases. This suggests that 10% sorbitol or trehalose may be sensorially acceptable for rehydrated potato products, whereas glycerol requires careful consideration. From a product development perspective, several strategies can manage sensory impacts: (i) reducing the concentration of glycerol while maintaining protective efficacy; (ii) combining glycerol with other protectants (e.g., sorbitol/glycerol blends) to balance sweetness and texture; (iii) masking undesirable aftertastes by incorporating flavorings or seasonings commonly used in potato-based snacks. Furthermore, the intended application matters—if the final product is intended for savory snacks, glycerol might be less suitable, whereas for sweetened formulations, its sweet taste could be advantageous. Thus, optimizing protectant type and concentration based on the target sensory profile is essential for practical product development.

Assessment of cell viability by trypan blue staining of the rehydrated potato slices

Freeze-drying and rehydration can cause irreversible damage to cell membranes, leading to cell death. Trypan blue staining can be used as a qualitative method to indicate the number of dead cells in tissues, which can intuitively reflect the degree of damage to the cell structure (Song et al., 2023). The results of trypan blue staining of potato samples are shown in Fig. 5. The fresh potato slices appeared transparent and colorless because the tissues contained a large number of living cells with intact membrane structures, which effectively excluded the trypan blue solution and prevented the cells from being stained. However, the rehydrated potato slices showed pronounced blue staining, indicating that after freeze-drying and rehydration, a substantial proportion of cell structures in fresh potatoes were destroyed. As a result, the trypan blue solution easily penetrated the tissue and underwent a color reaction with intracellular substances. Among the five groups of rehydrated potato slices, the samples treated with trehalose (Fig. 5d) and sorbitol (Fig. 5e) had relatively smaller staining areas and lighter coloration, indicating that these two protectants provided better protection for the potato cell structure during vacuum freeze-drying and were more conducive to the tissue structure recovery of rehydrated samples.

Trypan blue staining images of fresh-cut potato slices (a) and rehydrated slices with different protectant treatments: (b) control, (c) glucose, (d) trehalose, (e) sorbitol, (f) glycerol. (For interpretation of the references to color in this figure legend, the reader is referred to the web version of this article.)

The protective effect of LMWPP pretreatments on cell viability can be explained by the water replacement hypothesis and the vitrification theory (Deng et al., 2023;Guerrero Sanchez et al., 2022 ; Wolkers & Oldenhof, 2020). During impregnation, trehalose, sorbitol, and glucose penetrate the intercellular spaces and partially enter the cells. The water replacement hypothesis states that the hydroxyl groups of these solutes form hydrogen bonds with membrane phospholipids, substituting for lost water molecules and maintaining the lipid bilayer in a functional liquid-crystalline state during drying (Monalisa et al., 2025). The vitrification theory adds that these LMWPPs form a stable glassy matrix around and inside cells during freeze-drying, which immobilizes membrane components, reduces ice-induced mechanical damage, and prevents membrane fusion (Guerrero Sanchez et al., 2022; Wolkers & Oldenhof, 2020). Consequently, cell membranes remain largely intact after freeze-drying and rehydration, effectively excluding trypan blue.

Observation of the microstructure of the rehydrated potato slices using OM

The integrity of cell walls and tissue structures significantly influences the texture of fruits and vegetables (Nunes et al., 2008). Fig. 6 shows the optical microscope images of PAS-stained potato samples. In these images, purple-red regions generally represent polysaccharides (cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin) in the cell wall, including pectin viscous polysaccharides in the outer cell-wall layer and glycoproteins on the plasma membrane surface, whereas purple-brown regions represent starch granules in the cytoplasm. As shown in Fig. 6a, cells in fresh potato slices were tightly arranged with complete plump structures and clearly defined contours. However, the rehydrated potato slices of the control group (Fig. 6b) exhibited noticeable deformations in the cell wall contours, such as rupture, shrinkage, and collapse. The rehydrated potato slices in the glucose (Fig. 6c), trehalose (Fig. 6d), and glycerol (Fig. 6f) groups also experienced shrinkage and rupture in some cell wall structures, although the degree of deformation was less severe than that observed in the control group. Minimal deformation was observed in the rehydrated potato slices in the sorbitol group (Fig. 6e), which appeared to have the most similar cell-wall contour morphology to that of fresh potato slices. Based on these optical microscope observations, it can be inferred that sorbitol impregnation pretreatment can effectively protect the integrity of the cell structure and reduce structural deformation in potato tissues subjected to freeze-drying and rehydration through mechanisms such as lowering the freezing point, inhibiting ice crystal growth, increasing the Tg, replacing water, and forming hydrogen bonds with biomolecules during dehydration.

OM images at ×10 magnification of fresh-cut potato slices (a) and rehydrated slices with different protectant treatments: (b) control, (c) glucose, (d) trehalose, (e) sorbitol, (f) glycerol.

In the glucose- and trehalose-treated groups (Fig. 6c, d), the starch-rich regions appeared more intensely stained than in other rehydrated groups. This is likely due to residual impregnation agents within the tissue. Both glucose and trehalose contain vicinal hydroxyl groups, which are oxidized by periodic acid during PAS staining to form aldehydes that react with Schiff's reagent, producing a purple-brown color. Thus, the exogenous sugars contribute to the PAS-positive signal, superimposing on the intrinsic staining of starch and cell walls. In contrast, sorbitol and glycerol are less efficiently oxidized under the same conditions, resulting in weaker or no staining enhancement. Therefore, the intense coloration in glucose- and trehalose-treated samples does not indicate better preservation of starch, but rather a staining artifact from residual protectants. This interpretation is consistent with the more pronounced cell wall deformation observed in these groups (Fig. 6c, d) compared with the sorbitol group.

Observation of the microstructure of the rehydrated potato slices using TEM

Fig. 7 shows TEM micrographs of the potato samples at ×7000 magnification, displaying the specific structure of the cell wall and its surrounding area in the potato sample. As shown in Fig. 7a, the cell wall structure of the fresh potato sample was intact with tightly arranged cellulose microfibrils in the inner and outer layers of the cell wall, a clear and continuous cell membrane interface, and tight adhesion to the cell wall. The cytoplasm and cell contents were distributed around the cell walls of fresh potato samples.

TEM images at ×7000 magnification of fresh-cut potato slices (a) and rehydrated slices with different protectant treatments: (b) control, (c) glucose, (d) trehalose, (e) sorbitol, (f) glycerol. CW = cell wall, ML = middle lamella, CM = cell membrane, CC = cellular content.

In the control group of rehydrated samples (Fig. 7b), the edges of the cell wall were partially dissolved, and the cellulose microfibrils were loose. The boundary contours of the middle lamina and the inner and outer layers were no longer clear or complete, and the cell membrane that adhered to the cell wall no longer existed. Compared to the control group, the four rehydrated samples treated with LMWPP protection (Fig. 7c–f) exhibited relatively clearer and more complete cell wall contours, as well as a tighter arrangement of cellulose fibers. In particular, in the micrographs of the trehalose (Fig. 7d) and sorbitol (Fig. 7e) treatment groups, some cell membranes were still adhered to the surface of the cell wall. By comparing the TEM micrographs of different groups of potato samples in Fig. 7, sorbitol treatment had a better effect on protecting the cell wall and membrane structure of freeze-dried rehydrated potato tissues, and the microstructure of the rehydrated potato samples was closer to that of fresh potato samples (Xuan et al., 2025).

As shown in Fig. 7, fresh-cut potato slices undergo complex and irreversible changes in their cell structure after vacuum freeze-drying and rehydration and cannot be restored to their fresh state. During the freezing stage, water in potato tissues forms ice crystals, which expand in volume and cause physical compression and puncture of the surrounding cell walls and organelles, resulting in irreversible structural damage. During the drying stage, the vast majority of the water in the tissue is removed, with free water sublimating from ice crystals into water vapor during primary drying. The bound water evaporates and is removed during secondary drying, and the original positions occupied by ice crystals become voids. The basic skeleton of the cell (such as the cellulose in the cell wall) was preserved, and a porous sponge-like structure was formed after freeze-drying. During rehydration, water molecules can re-enter these porous structures through capillary action. However, damaged cell walls, membranes, and other structures cannot be repaired, and the cells cannot re-establish their original turgor pressure. The microstructure of the rehydrated tissue is fundamentally different from that of the fresh tissue. Therefore, the texture of the rehydrated tissue is completely different from that of fresh tissue, becoming soft and brittle.

Glucose, trehalose, sorbitol, and glycerol are commonly used freeze-drying protectants and are rich in hydrophilic polar groups, such as hydroxyl groups (-OH). These hydroxyl groups form hydrogen bonds with water molecules in fruit and vegetable tissues and polar groups of biomolecules (proteins, phospholipids, and polysaccharides) to achieve key protective functions, such as water substitution, increasing glass transition temperature, and maintaining cell structure stability (Ali et al., 2025). These four protectants differed in their protective effects on freeze-dried and rehydrated potato tissues. Sorbitol offered superior protection, which can be explained by several physicochemical mechanisms (Liu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). First, sorbitol stabilizes cell membranes via direct hydrogen bonding with phospholipid phosphate groups, lowering the membrane phase transition temperature and preserving membrane fluidity during freeze-drying. Glucose and trehalose appear less effective in this regard. Second, sorbitol readily penetrates plant cells due to its moderate molecular size, accumulating in the cytoplasm to protect both plasma and organellar membranes. Trehalose, being larger, penetrates less efficiently. Third, sorbitol is a linear polyol with all hydroxyl groups in equatorial orientation, enabling optimal hydrogen bonding. The cyclic structures of glucose and trehalose impose steric constraints, while glycerol has fewer hydroxyl groups per molecule. Fourth, sorbitol maintains a stable amorphous glassy state under typical freeze-drying conditions, avoiding the crystallization of glucose and the excessive mobility of glycerol. Although trehalose has a high glass transition temperature, its large size limits tissue permeability. Moreover, as a non-reducing sugar alcohol, sorbitol does not participate in Maillard reactions, unlike glucose. Thus, sorbitol uniquely combines favorable permeability, effective membrane stabilization, suitable glass-forming properties, and chemical inertness – a combination not fully achieved by glucose, trehalose, or glycerol alone.

Regulatory and country-specific labeling considerations

The use of glucose, trehalose, sorbitol, and glycerol as impregnation agents is regulated differently in major markets including China, the United States, and the European Union. In China, glucose, trehalose and glycerol are permitted as food ingredients; only sorbitol is listed as a food additive under GBGB 2760 (2024). In the US, they are generally recognized as safe in GRAS Notice Inventory (U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2026), while in the EU, sorbitol (E 420) and glycerol (E 422) are authorized food additives under Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008 (European Parliament and Council, 2008).

Labeling requirements also differ: added sugars and polyols must be declared in the ingredient list in all regions, and EU products with more than 10% polyols require laxative effect warnings. Such regulatory and labeling differences affect formulation design and industrial application. Commercial production must comply with local food safety and labeling rules to ensure market access and consumer acceptance.

Scalability and process integration

The present study was conducted at laboratory scale using vacuum impregnation in Erlenmeyer flasks. For industrial scale-up, vacuum impregnation would need to be converted from batch-wise laboratory operation to continuous or semi-continuous systems compatible with existing fruit and vegetable processing lines. Uniform distribution of protective agents across large batches requires precise control of vacuum pressure, impregnation time, and solution-to-sample ratio to ensure consistent uptake throughout potato slices. Upscaling would also introduce additional considerations including equipment footprint, pumping and filtration systems for protectant solutions, and energy consumption associated with extended vacuum steps. Despite these challenges, the proposed impregnation step can be integrated feasibly prior to freeze-drying without disrupting conventional drying workflows. Further process optimization focusing on continuous impregnation, homogeneous protective agent distribution, and cost efficiency would facilitate industrial implementation and improve the practical viability of this technology.

Outlook: potential combinations with emerging technologies

The present impregnation strategy using LMWPP (glucose, trehalose, sorbitol, glycerol) has proven effective in improving rehydration recovery capacity of freeze-dried potato slices. To further enhance process efficiency and product performance, future studies could explore synergistic combinations with emerging electro-technologies. For instance, PEF applied prior to vacuum impregnation could electroporate cell membranes, thereby facilitating deeper and more uniform penetration of protectants into the tissue matrix (Barba et al., 2020; Fauster et al., 2020). This combination may reduce the required protectant concentration or impregnation time while maintaining or even improving rehydration recovery. Similarly, ultrasound-assisted impregnation—leveraging acoustic cavitation and microstreaming—could accelerate mass transfer and enhance solute distribution, particularly for viscous protectant solutions such as glycerol (Yılmaz & Bilek, 2018). Another promising direction is the sequential or simultaneous application of mild thermal blanching with PEF or ultrasound to achieve controlled cell permeabilization without compromising texture. Additionally, integrating impregnation with osmotic dehydration under combined vacuum-ultrasound conditions may offer a scalable pathway for continuous processing. These hybrid approaches, when optimized, could lower energy consumption, reduce protectant usage, and expand the applicability of the proposed strategy to a wider range of freeze-dried fruit and vegetable products.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the effects of four LMWPP (glucose, trehalose, sorbitol, and glycerol) on the rehydration recovery capacity of freeze-dried potato slices. Fresh-cut potato slices underwent irreversible damage to the cell walls and membranes after freeze-drying and rehydration, resulting in a loss of structural integrity and a significantly softer texture after rehydration. The findings revealed that compared to the control group, sorbitol protective treatment significantly improved the RR of freeze-dried potato slices, enhanced moisture distribution, reduced REC, and improved overall TPA-derived properties (hardness and chewiness). Further validation through methods such as trypan blue staining for cell viability assessment and microscopic observations using OM and TEM confirmed that sorbitol played a superior protective role in maintaining potato cell wall and membrane structures during vacuum freeze-drying. This treatment facilitated better tissue structure recovery in the rehydrated samples, with microscopic characteristics more closely resembling those of fresh potato slices.
