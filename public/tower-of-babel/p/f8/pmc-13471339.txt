Epidemiological and genotypic analysis of Candida albicans outbreak in a neonatal ward
Authors: Xia Yu, Jia-hui Liang, Dong-yan Zheng, Cun-wei Cao, Shuang-jie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCandida albicans is the primary pathogen responsible for candidemia, particularly in hospital settings, and is often associated with outbreaks in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs). In this study, we aimed to investigate the transmission dynamics and identify the potential source of a C. albicans candidemia outbreak in an NICU, focusing on neonates at high risk of infection.MethodsThis retrospective, single-center study focused on analyzing 10 neonates with candidemia and 23 C. albicans strains, including 6 isolates obtained from the environment and staff members in a neonatal ward. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was employed to trace the outbreak of candidemia in neonatal wards and reconstruct the transmission path of infection based on epidemiological data.Results70% (7/10) of neonates had either extremely or very low body weight as well as catheter-related bloodstream infection. Multilocus sequence typing revealed that the isolates could be categorized into three sequence types (STs), including two novel STs, ST3601 and ST3602, with ST3601 being predominant (82.6%, 19/23). Background

Candida albicans is an opportunistic fungal pathogen responsible for candidemia and ranks as the fourth most prevalent cause of hospital-acquired bloodstream infections in the United States [1, 2] affecting patients of intensive care units, and in particular, low-birthweight infants [3, 4]. C. albicans infections are difficult to control owing to its wide spread in the medical environment [5]. Over the last decade, the proportion of invasive candidiasis caused by the predominant species, C. albicans, has remained stable [6–9]. In 2022, Candida auris and C. albicans were incorporated into the Critical Priority Group of the World Health Organization fungal priority pathogen list [10], which consists of fungi that lead to life-threatening infections with a high mortality rate [11, 12]. Similar to the emerging yeast C. auris [13–15], C. albicans has drawn attention as an emerging antifungal-resistant pathogen that leads to healthcare-associated infections and outbreaks [16]. Candidemia caused by C. albicans is difficult to treat and poses serious therapeutic challenges, resulting in great financial and medical burdens. Early identification of the types of C. albicans through a rapid test would be helpful for patient management and treatment, particularly for the prevention of nosocomial C. albicans infection outbreaks. In our study, a potential outbreak was detected following an abnormal increase in candidemia cases in 2016. The bloodstream infection rate for C. albicans in neonates was 0.07% (12/17,030) in 2016. In contrast, the rates in subsequent years were markedly lower: 0.005% (1/17,979) in 2017, 0.02% (4/17,949) in 2018, and 0.01% (3/18,876) in 2019. Multilocus-sequence typing (MLST) [17] was employed to characterize the genetic profiles of C. albicans isolates. Additionally, single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) and insertion/deletion (InDel) analyses were conducted to detect gene variants, combined with phylogenetic and epidemiological analyses, to demonstrate how they were propagated by nosocomial transmission. This approach traces potential sources and transmission pathways of infection, employing strategies, such as hand hygiene, disinfection, and isolation to control hospital-acquired infections. These measures are essential for understanding the evolution and outbreak dynamics of C. albicans and preventing further disease transmission.

Methods

Clinical data and fungal strain collection

Neonatal patients diagnosed with C. albicans candidemia and admitted to the neonatal ward of a tertiary children’s hospital in Guangxi Province, China, during the outbreak period (August to September, 2016), were included in this study. Patients’ medical histories, along with other pertinent clinical and biochemical information, were extracted from their medical records.

Candida albicans isolates were obtained from two types of clinical specimens, blood and central venous catheter of neonatal patients with suspected candidiasis (NICU). Blood samples (approximately 2 mL per bottle) were collected and inoculated into pediatric-specific aerobic blood culture bottles (BacT/ALERT PF, bioMérieux, France) and incubated at 37 °C in a BacT/ALERT automated blood culture system (bioMérieux) until a positive growth signal was detected. Subsequently, liquid media containing blood from the bottles were cultured on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) media with chloramphenicol at 37 °C for 48 h. The central venous catheter (CVC) tip culture was performed using the Maki roll plate technique. Briefly, upon aseptic removal of the suspected CVC, the distal 5 cm of the catheter tip was sectioned using sterile surgical scissors. This segment was transported immediately to the laboratory in a sterile container. There, it was rolled carefully across the surface of a 5% sheep blood agar plate (bioMérieux, France) for approximately four complete rotations. The plate was then incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 48 h. To identify the source of the outbreak, epidemiological and environmental investigations were carried out in October with the assistance of hospital management. Potential contamination sources, including the hands of healthcare personnel (all NICU staff, including doctors, nurses, interns, and cleaning staff, were tested before the end of their shifts and hand hygiene), as well as surfaces, such as the incubator, ventilator, operating table, door handles, treatment tables, medical carts, indwelling catheters, and bed sheets in the neonatal ward, were thoroughly examined. The Ethics Committee of the People’s Hospital of Guangxi (KY-KJT-2023-49) approved this study. All participants, or their legal guardians, provided written informed consent to participate in the study.

Identification and antifungal susceptibility testing

Isolates were identified as C. albicans using the chromogenic medium and VITEK 2 YST ID card (bioMérieux), and were confirmed by conducting fungal internal transcribed spacer region (ITS) sequencing. The in vitro antifungal susceptibility was assessed using the microdilution method in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standard Institute (CLSI) guidelines (CLSI M27-A4, M60, and M59), employing clinical breakpoints or epidemiological cutoff values (ECVs). The antifungal susceptibility tests were performed by utilizing Fungal Antifungal MIC Test Plates (Zhuhai Meihua Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Zhuhai, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, and following nine commonly used antifungal agents: fluconazole (FLU), voriconazole (VOR), itraconazole (ITR), posaconazole (POC), 5-flucytosine (5FC), anidulafungin (ANI), micafungin (MCF), caspofungin (CAS), and amphotericin B (AMB). The antifungal susceptibilities (minimum inhibitor concentration-MIC ranges) were as follows: 5FC = 0.03–0.125 µg/mL, AMB = 0.5–1 µg/mL, POC = 0.0075–0.06 µg/mL. VOR = 0.0075–0.5 µg/mL, ITR = 0.015–0.25 µg/mL, FLU = 0.125–0.5 µg/mL, MCF ≤ 0.125 µg/mL, ANI ≤ 0.125 µg/mL, and CAS ≤ 0.125 µg/mL.

Whole-genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted using the magnetic bead method with a plant genome DNA extraction kit (Sangon, Shanghai, China) following pre-grinding in liquid nitrogen. The concentration and purity of the genomic DNA were determined using a Qubit dsDNA HS assay kit (Sangon), and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis was used to confirm integrity. Library preparation and next-generation sequencing were outsourced to Sangon Biotech Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The libraries were pooled and loaded onto a Novaseq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA)/DNBseq-T7 (BGI, Shenzhen, China) sequencer using a 2 × 150 bp paired end sequence kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The qualified reads from each sample were mapped to the assembled reference genome (C. albicans NC 032089.1) using BWA (0.7.17) with default parameters. Primary variation calling (SNP/indel) was performed for each isolate using the Genome Analysis ToolKit (GATK, v4.1.1.0).

Genotypic analysis, heat maps, and phylogenetic trees

MLST [18] analysis was performed using PubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/organisms/candida-albicans). To infer the population structure and genetic relatedness, the eBURST algorithm was used to cluster STs into clonal complexes (CCs), a total of 3601 global STs were included in the analysis(as of January 26, 2021). A standardized MLST scheme for C. albicans was selected based on seven conserved housekeeping genes encoding aspartate aminotransferase, acetyl-CoA carboxylase, ATP-dependent permease, mannose phosphate isomerase, alanyl-RNA synthetase, vacuolar protein sorting, and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase. To account for the highly heterozygous diploid nature of C. albicans, we implemented a diploid-aware pipeline as follows: (i) Variant calling was performed using GATK to identify heterozygous SNPs across all samples. (ii) Sequencing reads from each sample were aligned to the reference genome using Bowtie2, and consensus base calling was performed using SAMtools and VarScan2. At positions identified as heterozygous, IUPAC ambiguity codes were introduced (e.g., R for A/G, Y for C/T, S for G/C, W for A/T, K for G/T, M for A/C), thereby preserving diploid information. (iii) The resulting IUPAC-encoded consensus sequences for each of the seven MLST loci were assembled into complete DNA sequences. (iv) These sequences were then used as queries in BLASTN searches against the reference allele sequences of the target species retrieved from the PubMLST database. For each locus, the allele number with the highest sequence similarity and complete coverage to the corresponding reference allele was retained. The combination of allele numbers across all loci determined the ST for each strain. The pairwise relatedness of SNPs/InDels was calculated according to the allelic and SNP/indel differences between samples. SNPs and indels were identified using Genome Anlysis Toolkit (GATK, v 4.1.1.0), with the following parameters: SNP(QD < 2.0 || FS > 60.0 || MQ < 40.0 ||MQRankSum < -12.5 || ReadPosRankSum < -8.0 || SOR > 3.0). InDel(QD < 2 || FS > 200 || ReadPosRankSum<-20 || SOR > 10). Sequence depth were analyzed using a read depth-based approach: any variant site with a read depth (DP) below 10× and a allele frequency (AF) below 0.2 in a single sample was excluded from further analysis. Additionally, a variant was retained only if it was supported by at least five reads in total, including at least one read on each strand. Heat maps were generated using the R package pheatmap. To construct the phylogenetic tree across the 23 samples, we calculated the matrix of pairwise genetic distances and used two different phylogenetic methods, maximum likelihood and neighbor-joining, to build the phylogenetic tree; the R package ggtree was used with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. All nodes with bootstrap values < 70% were removed. We independently performed a principal component analysis (PCA) of the SNP genotyping dataset using EIGENSOFT (v.7.2.1).

Results

Characteristics of patients

A retrospective review of the medical records was performed for neonates diagnosed with candidemia during the C. albicans outbreak in the NICU from August to September 2016. Ten patients developed C. albicans sepsis and were diagnosed with fungal septicemia: of these, seven were males and three females; with the mean onset time of candidemia at 14.8 days after birth, which met the definition of late-onset disease (LOD, neonatal candidemia at > 7 days of life). Nine of the ten patients were premature infants; the gestational age varied from 26 ± 2 to 37 ± 1 weeks, whereas the birth weight ranged between 900 and 1,790 g. None of the patients received parenteral nutrition or had a history of previous operation. The sample also included seven premature infants with extremely low birth weight (ELBW < 1,000 g) and very low birth weight (VLBW, 1,000–1,499 g). Seven patients had CVC-related bloodstream infections, with catheterization times ranging from 5 to 19 days. Among the seven patients diagnosed with catheter-related candidemia, six (all except Patient 3) underwent prompt catheter removal within 24 h of the first positive blood culture. Blood1,3-β-D-glucan testing was conducted concurrently, revealing significantly elevated levels in patients 1–4 and 6–7. Empiric antifungal therapy was subsequently initiated based on clinical judgment. Eventually, all patients recovered. Comprehensive details are presented in Table 1.

Fungal strain collection and antifungal susceptibility testing

In total, 23 C. albicans isolates were collected. Seventeen C. albicans isolates were obtained from ten neonates who were diagnosed with fungal septicemia caused by C. albicans (ten isolates from blood and seven isolates from the catheter tip). Six other C. albicans isolates were detected in the hospital environment, including three isolates from the hands of medical staff and three isolates from indwelling catheters and bedsheets. Further details are provided in Table 2. Table 3 provides a summary of the in vitro antifungal susceptibility of C. albicans isolates to the nine antifungal agents. The data were analyzed according to the clinical breakpoints and epidemiological cut-off values outlined in CLSI M60-Ed2 and M59. The MICs of AMB and POC had MICs less than the defined ECVs. In addition, all the isolates were sensitive to FLU, MCF, ANI, and CAS. All C. albicans infections were treated with fluconazole in combination with Amphotericin B. Antifungal treatment, along with catheter removal, was administered to seven cases of central venous catheter-associated bloodstream infection. In each of these cases, the fungemia was finally cleared.

Genome typing and phylogenetic analysis

A total of seventeen C. albicans isolates from ten candidemia patients, along with six environmental isolates, were genotyped using the C. albicans MLST method. The quality metrics for each isolate presented in Table 4-including total reads, clean reads, coverage (≥ 1×), and numbers of SNPs and InDels, collectively support the robustness of the phylogenetic and PCA results. MLST analysis identified three different sequence types (STs: ST3601, ST3602, and ST79; Fig. 1), of which we identified two novel STs (ST3601 and ST3602) that were submitted to the PubMLST public database (https://pubmlst.org/). Three STs representing two different clonal complexes (CCs) and one singleton were identified. Seventeen isolates (C1–C17) from ten patients with candidemia belonged to ST3601 from CC19. The three isolates (C18, C19, and C20) from medical staff belonged to different STs, whereas C18, C21 (from V-tube), and C23 (from U-tube) clustered together, all belonging to the same ST79 from CC 1. Notably, C20 (ST3602) did not belong to any recognized CC. Besides, isolates C1–C17 clustered with isolates C19 (from medical staff hands) and C22 (from the mattress), sharing the same STs, which account for 82.6%. All seven isolates from CVC, central venous catheter isolates had the same genotype profile as the blood isolates, indicating catheter-related bloodstream infection.

After genome mapping (against the reference genome, C. albicans NC 032089.1), the SNP and InDel variants for each sample were analyzed, and differences were observed among groups. A strong similarity was observed among 17 C. albicans isolates from 10 patients (C1 to C17) in the heatmap of sample-to-sample distances, with isolates C19 from medical staff hands and C22 from mattresses forming the same cluster, indicating a relatively low level of genetic variability (Fig. 2A). The isolates C18, C20, C21, and C23 showed a large number of mutations, displaying high genetic dissimilarity. Notably, although C18, C21, and C23 clustered together, the results showed that the patients in this outbreak were not infected with this cluster. Similarly, C20 (medical staff) formed a separate cluster with a large number of SNP and InDel variants. We performed the same comparisons using a cluster dendrogram based on genetic distances and obtained similar results (Fig. 2B). Next, we extracted the SNP loci and constructed phylogenetic trees for these loci. The phylogenetic trees created using neighbor-joining and maximum likelihood methods (Fig. 1) showed similar results, and PCA gave consistent results (Fig. 3). All these analyses showed phylogenetic concordance with the MLST typing results for these strains, suggesting that strains C19 (from medical staff hands) and C22 (from mattresses) was associated with C. albicans infection outbreak, either directly or indirectly. Based on these results, we formulated an infection control strategy and preventive measures for C. albicans transmission and infection control measures.

Discussion

Although outbreaks of C. albicans bloodstream infections are rare [4], C. albicans is a prevalent pathogen responsible for invasive fungal infections in hospitalized patients. This study focused on investigating an outbreak of C. albicans in a neonatal unit because of an increased number of abnormal candidemia cases that were phylogenetically closely related to each other, implying that these strains could have originated from the same ancestor. The hands of healthcare personnel, transient contamination with C. albicans, and environmental contamination are likely sources from which the organism spreads within healthcare facilities. Overall, appropriate and timely antifungal therapy and source control are essential to improving the prognosis of patients with candidemia [19–21].

Invasive fungal infections in neonates may have different clinical characteristics from those in other age groups. Although advances in neonatal medicine and therapeutic procedures have improved the survival of ELBW and VLBW infants, they remain at high risk of infection [22, 23]. In our study, while only one patient was a full-term infant (37w + 1), nine (90%) were preterm newborns. The high infection rate in premature VLBW and ELBW infants was consistent with that of previous reports [24]. Factors, such as immunosuppression, low birth weight, intravenous lines, total parenteral nutrition, the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, extended CVC placement, mechanical ventilation, and complicated surgery were identified as the most common predisposing factors [25, 26]. All patients received broad-spectrum antibiotics upon admission, 7 out of 10 neonates (70%) received third-generation cephalosporins, and 4 out of 10 (40%) received carbapenems prior to candidemia onset. The administration of these agents markedly increased the risk of Candida albicans colonization and superficial infection, with severe cases potentially progressing to invasive disease [27]. The underlying mechanisms are primarily attributed to the extensive elimination of gram-negative bacteria-along with gram-positive and anaerobic microorganisms-across sites such as the intestinal, respiratory, and genitourinary tracts. This eradication disrupts the ecological equilibrium of the microbiota, thereby abrogating the competitive suppression of C. albicans colonization. Concurrently, alterations in the immune microenvironment further facilitate fungal adhesion and invasion [28]. During this nosocomial outbreak, the duration of CVC catheterization was long (range: 5–19 d, duration: 12 d), which increased the risk of candidemia [12]. Therefore, early detection and therapy of candidemia may have increased the survival. Our findings are consistent with a model of horizontal transmission. All cases occurred beyond 7 days of hospitalization, with a mean onset time of 14.8 days after admission-a temporal pattern that aligns closely with healthcare-associated infection rather than vertical acquisition. Candida albicans was the predominant species, which is known to be more frequently associated with nosocomial acquisition in high-risk units. Furthermore, the identified independent risk factors represent classic facilitators of horizontal transmission. Blood testing for 1,3-β-D-glucan (BDG), a diagnostic method independent of culture, can accelerate candidemia diagnosis and is often used to help reduce the delay in starting antifungal treatment [29–31]. However, several results may be false-negatives because of the widespread use of antifungal drugs (such as fluconazole) or the differences in the sensitivity of different Candida species, in which BDG sensitivity in C. albicans candidemia was 72% [32]. Negative BDG results in patients with a high risk of invasive candidiasis should be carefully considered [33]. In antifungal susceptibility tests, we observed a high degree of similarity among the outbreak strains of cluster ST3601 and the singleton ST3602, which were sensitive to all antifungal agents, with low MIC50/90 values. Most of the MIC50/90 values for most of the antifungals tested in our study were consistent with those reported in a global survey published in 2013 [34]. Notably, ST79 strains were isolated from the hands of medical staff, V-tube, and U-tube with similar drug susceptibility patterns, but they did not cause this infection; they may, however, be a potential source of infection. In line with the guidelines for managing invasive candidiasis in neonates [35], all infected patients were treated with amphotericin B or fluconazole, with satisfactory results. In this study, a noticeable rise in candidemia cases was observed, indicating the necessity for candidemia prevention measures and an investigation into a potential outbreak.

WGS is widely employed to trace foodborne outbreaks and to type bacterial and fungal infections [4, 36]. By typing C. albicans, we identified variations in the infection patterns. Whole-genome MLST identified two major colony-forming complexes and revealed that the new type, ST3601, was the predominant strain attributable to the outbreak. The ST3601 strains seemed to be more likely to cluster, and a high number of clusters were noted in the neonatal unit that had CC19. According to a previous study [37], a few fluconazole-resistant isolates belonging to CC19 have been detected. In contrast, only three strains (ST79) clustered together; no infection developed in any of the patients with ST79 or the singleton strain C20 (ST3602), although these may serve as potential sources of infection. In China, the majority of the vulvovaginal candidiasis isolates are clustered in clade 1, with ST1867 and ST79 as the dominant genotypes [38]. Notably, 74.4% of isolates of C. albicans belonging to ST 79 are reportedly non-susceptible to fluconazole [39]. ST79 was first isolated in 1993 from the oral swab of a patient with AIDS and has a high frequency worldwide. All C. albicans isolates in our study exhibited sensitivity to fluconazole, indicating possible genetic diversity.

WGS further confirmed the presence of genetically related genotypes. We performed WGS SNP and InDel analyses of 23 C. albicans isolates. The mutation distances of each isolate, based on their SNP/InDel counts, further confirmed the MLST results. SNP/InDel pairwise mutation distances between isolates from Patients 1 to 10 and isolates C19 and C22 were low, indicating clonality and a common source that caused a nosocomial outbreak within the neonatal unit. Although no clear cutoff exists for the number of accumulated SNPs to determine the relatedness between C. albicans within a given cluster, low intra-cluster diversity can be used to determine major clusters in the case of hospital outbreaks [13]. As previously reported, the outbreak of C. auris began globally in healthcare facilities. Using WGS, C. auris typically exhibits low intra-cluster diversity, with fewer than 100 SNP differences, and in some cases, even less than 10 SNP differences [40]. However, the high number of SNPs identified in C. albicans in our study indicates that mutations or recombination events can occur under stressful conditions [41]. C. auris is a haploid species with low intra-species variability, and C. albicans is a highly heterozygous species, exhibiting significant intra-cluster variability, similar to other diploid species (such as Candida parapsilosis) [4]. The intra-cluster variability observed in these species may be linked to their diploid nature. To characterize the genetic divergence between these cluster populations, we constructed phylogenetic trees based on genetic distances, as well as the PCA of the SNP genotype matrix. These findings indicated that all strains within the cluster were closely related, implying that the outbreak was caused by the clonal transmission of a particular C. albicans strain, and that the contaminated hands of the medical staff were most likely responsible for this outbreak.

Following the outbreak, we collaborated with the infection control department to implement a comprehensive set of interventions aimed at containing the spread. First, all affected cases and their contacts were placed under strict contact precautions. Second, environmental cleaning and disinfection protocols in the NICU were intensified. Third, hand hygiene compliance was reinforced through targeted education and regular feedback. Fourth, mandatory in-service training sessions were promptly conducted for all staff in the affected areas. As a result, the outbreak was considered under control, with an extremely low infection rate sustained over the subsequent 3 years.

However, our findings should be interpreted considering the limitations of this study. One limitation of our study is that we included isolates from patients with invasive infections, while only including a few isolates from the environment. However, the temporal sequence of outbreak events cannot be determined, and their exact origins in hospitals remain unclear. Another limitation is that while we did not have a clear understanding of why some clusters caused nosocomial outbreaks, others seemed to be related to fungal colonization. Additional research studies are warranted to verify the pathogenic properties of the adherence and biofilm-forming abilities of different C. albicans. The present study did not include in vitro biofilm formation assays. Therefore, while we observed ST79 isolates from catheter specimens, we cannot conclude whether this sequence type has an intrinsic predisposition for biofilm formation compared with other STs. Future functional studies are warranted to investigate the biofilm-forming capacity of different C. albicans STs. Third, due to the retrospective nature of the investigation, we did not conduct routine screening for Candida colonization in asymptomatic neonates who lacked clinical signs of infection during the outbreak. Consequently, the full scope of colonization and the possible reservoir of the organism within the neonatal population may not have been fully captured. To better elucidate the transmission dynamics of Candida in neonatal wards, future prospective studies should include active surveillance for colonization in both asymptomatic infants and healthcare workers.

Conclusions

In summary, we analyzed the molecular characteristics of C. albicans isolates from a neonatal unit using WGS and found that ST3601 was the predominant strain attributed to the outbreak. WGS of multiple isolates can provide valuable information for epidemiological investigations. It can rapidly distinguish sporadic and epidemic cases, identify potential sources of infection, and predict transmission routes. Therefore, infection control measures including hand hygiene and environmental disinfection should be reinforced to reduce the spread of C. albicans.
