Bisphenol A disrupts renal metabolism via MAPK-mediated steroid hormone dysregulation identified by transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses
Authors: Rui Gao, Jiangsong Ma, Xinru Chen, Zihao Jiang, Chuxin Zhang, Jiaxing An, Ruifeng Yan, Keming Yun, Zhongyuan Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bisphenol A (BPA) continuously leaches from consumer products and accumulates in the human body, with detectable levels in urine. Emerging evidence suggests potential associations between prolonged BPA exposure and subclinical nephrotoxicity accompanied by metabolic dysregulation, though the gene regulatory mechanisms underlying these associations remain insufficiently characterized. In this study, bioinformatics analyses were employed to systematically reveal the potential transcriptional regulation of chronic renal injury due to BPA exposure. Complementary positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) imaging revealed pronounced renal inflammatory responses and localized tissue damage in murine models following 6-week oral BPA administration. Histopathological examination of the kidney revealed exfoliation of renal tubular epithelial cells, with collagen fibers and mucus deposits. Further multi-omics integration (transcriptomic-metabolomic) analysis identified significant perturbations in cortisol biosynthesis, aldosterone regulation, and MAPK signaling cascades induced by BPA exposure. These findings were further validated through molecular docking studies and MAPK inhibitor intervention studies in cellular models. In summary, a critical pathway in BPA-induced renal injury involves the modulation of 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2) expression, which is mediated by p38 MAPK. This process impacts cortisol metabolism and aldosterone synthesis, suggesting that the biosynthesis of renal steroid hormones is intricately linked to MAPK regulation in renal injury following BPA exposure. Our findings furnish a theoretical foundation for elucidating the mechanisms underlying BPA-induced nephrotoxicity and contribute a holistic viewpoint to environmental health risk assessments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20141-3.

Introduction

Bisphenol A (BPA) is present in numerous daily-use products including food packaging, pediatric products, medical equipment, and personal care items1. Exposure to BPA primarily occurs through ingestion of contaminated products, as well as dermal absorption2–4. BPA can be detected in urine and blood5–7. Previous human biomonitoring data indicate that detectable levels of BPA in blood and urine are typically in the nanogram per milliliter range8. For example, epidemiologic data from 2004 to 2019 found BPA levels as high as 2.90 ng/mL in urine and 2.54 ng/mL in blood in the Chinese population9. Previous reports have indicated that BPA can accumulate in various human and animal tissues and impair their physiological functions10. In addition, chronic BPA exposure is associated with toxic effects in metabolically active organs and alters the metabolism of biomolecules involved in energy homeostasis11. Various studies have demonstrated an association between BPA exposure and the development of metabolic syndrome12,13. Despite this established link, the precise molecular pathways through which BPA exerts its metabolic effects across diverse organ systems remain unresolved.

The kidney is a metabolically active organ and is vulnerable to chemical pollutants. Recent clinical studies have shown that the urinary BPA detection rates are as high as 90% of the total population in the United States5 and 84% in China6. The correlation between BPA exposure and human kidney damage, including proteinuria (excessive protein in urine), renal failure, and chronic kidney disease, has been increasingly documented in recent studies14–17. The severity of chronic kidney disease is associated with BPA levels in the plasma of patients7. In addition, BPA can decrease the rate of glomerular filtration, which is an important parameter of renal function18. BPA exposure-induced renal impairment causes apoptosis, autophagy, and oxidative stress in in vivo studies15,19,20. Oxidative stress is an important factor in renal damage caused by BPA, according to the latest risk assessment report from the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA)21. Renal injury in diabetic studies with BPA exposure shows the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade pathway is regulated22. The identification of these regulatory pathways may shed new light on the renal damage inflicted by BPA. Although these findings illuminate potential molecular nexuses between BPA exposure and nephrotoxicity, it is yet to be determined whether these pathways play a role in the metabolic consequences of BPA exposure on the kidneys.

Utilizing data from both the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD), bioinformatics analyses have been demonstrated to accurately predict interactions and delineate molecular mechanisms linking environmental pollutants to disease etiologies23, which offer fresh perspectives on the potential nephrotoxic effects of BPA. Here, we utilized a bioinformatics approach to comprehensively investigate the transcriptional regulation underlying chronic renal injury induced by BPA exposure. To further elucidate the precise regulatory mechanisms governing BPA’s effects on renal metabolism, we established both in vivo murine exposure models and in vitro cellular models subjected to BPA. To render otherwise imperceptible renal injuries detectable, we employed positron emission tomography with computed tomography (PET/CT), enabling the identification and visualization of specific tissue damage sites within the kidneys of BPA-exposed mice. Histopathological examination complemented by an integrative analysis of transcriptomics and metabolomics was conducted on renal biopsy samples. Transcriptome sequencing facilitated the identification of pivotal genes and pathways implicated in BPA-associated renal pathologies, while metabolomics offered a comprehensive overview of the metabolic dynamics within renal cells and the prevailing physiological conditions of the kidney. The synergistic application of transcriptomics and metabolomics illuminates the complex cascade encompassing gene expression alterations, metabolite production, and pathway engagement in BPA-induced nephrotoxicity24. Consequently, our findings provide a theoretical foundation for understanding the mechanisms behind BPA-induced renal damage and contribute a comprehensive perspective to environmental health risk assessments.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

The keywords “chronic kidney disease” (CKD), “Homo sapiens” and “bisphenol A” were used to search in the GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and CTD (http://ctdbase.org/) databases. In the GEO database, gene microarray datasets without intervention for other conditions such as drugs and without comorbidities other than chronic renal injury were selected for the study. Differentially expressed genes (DEG) in CKD and non-CKD patients were obtained at P < 0.05 and |log fold change|>1 conditions. The genes obtained from GEO and CTD were intersected using Venny 2.1 online software (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were performed using DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/).

Animal treatment

Six-week-old male ICR (CD1) mice were acquired from the SPF (Beijing) biotechnology Co., Ltd. and were housed in an environment with a constant temperature (22 ± 2℃) and humidity and free access to food and water. All animal experiments complied with the ARRIVE guidelines and were approved by committee of scientific research in Shanxi university (animal experiment license No. SXULL2023076). All animal studies were performed following the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

After one week of acclimation, the 20 mice were randomly divided into vehicle group with 10 mice and BPA exposure group with 10 mice (five mice per cage). The mice in the exposure group received a daily gavage of BPA dissolved in an aqueous solution containing 0.1% ethanol at the dose of 50 µg/kg/day, which is the tolerable daily intake (TDI) dose of BPA25. The mice in the vehicle group received the same dose of aqueous solution containing 0.1% ethanol. After 6-week exposure, the mice were euthanized by a lethal dose of sodium pentobarbital (80 mg/kg) and cervical dislocation. Blood samples were collected and 3 left kidneys were fixed with formaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. The remaining tissues were frozen with liquid nitrogen and stored in -80℃ refrigerator.

18F-FAPI-74 PET/CT imaging

Each mouse was anesthetized with 2% isoflurane in oxygen after 1-hour intravenous injection of 18F-FAPI-74 via the tail vein. A static PET scan was then conducted for 10 min, maintaining a 2% isoflurane concentration to ensure continued anesthesia during imaging. The three-dimensional ordered subset expectation maximization (3D-OSEM) algorithm, based on a Monte Carlo system model, was employed to reconstruct the PET data. Concurrently, the CT images were reconstructed using Feldkamp filter back projection algorithm.

The region of interest (ROI) was delineated using cross-sectional CT images. In this study, the ROI area was defined as the three-dimensional volume encompassing the kidney, comprising five consecutive layers. PMOD software (PMOD Technologies LLC, Zurich, Switzerland) utilized to analyze the maximum Standardized Uptake Value (SUVmax) in the ROI, expressed in g/mL.

Quantifications of blood urea nitrogen (BUN), uric acid (UA), and serum creatinine (CR) levels

Whole blood samples were incubated at room temperature for 2 h, subsequently centrifuged at 3000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was utilized to measure the concentrations of BUN, UA, and CR with commercially available kits (Stanbio Laboratory, TX, USA).

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) stain

The paraffin-embedded tissue sections were dewaxed. Subsequently, the nuclei were stained with hematoxylin, and the cytoplasm was stained with eosin. The tissue sections were then dehydrated with xylene and covered with neutral resin. Observation and photography of the slides were conducted under a light microscope (Olympus IX71).

Periodic acid-schiff (PAS) stain

The paraffin-embedded tissue sections were dewaxed. Subsequently, periodic acid was employed to oxidize the hydroxyl group on the adjacent two carbon atoms into an aldehyde group. Schiff’s reagent was then applied, resulting in a purplish-red reaction product indicative of the aldehyde group. The nuclei were stained blue with hematoxylin. Following dehydration with xylene, the sections were mounted and covered with neutral resin. Finally, the slides were examined and photographed under a light microscope (Olympus IX71).

Masson staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were dewaxed and stained sequentially with iron hematoxylin, lichun acid fuchsin, phosphomolybdic acid, and aniline blue. Subsequently, the sections were dehydrated with xylene and covered with neutral resin. The prepared slides were examined and photographed under a light microscope (Olympus IX71).

RNA transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was purified and subsequently assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit of the Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Once qualified, different libraries for transcriptome sequencing were pooled and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000. The raw data were processed using the fastp software to filter, and clean data with a sequencing error rate of individual bases lower than 1%, stable GC content, Q20 greater than 95% and Q30 greater than 85% were screened out. Following this, the clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 software for rapid and accurate alignment (filtering out reads with alignment quality values below 10). Quantitative analysis was performed using the feature Counts (1.5.0-p3) software package, and differential expression analysis between the two groups was conducted using the DESeq2R package (1.20.0). The P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate. Corrected P-values ≤ 0.05 & |log2 (fold change)| ≥ 0.58 were set as the threshold for significant differential expression (DEG). Differential gene enrichment analysis was performed using the cluster Profiler (3.8.1) software.

High-throughput non-target metabolomics

Kidney tissue (100 mg) was ground with liquid nitrogen and then vortex with pre-chilled 80% methanol. The supernatant that containing 53% methanol was then injected into the UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher) coupled with an Orbitrap Q Exactive TM HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) for analysis. The raw data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.1, after which the peaks were matched against the mzCloud, mzVault, and MassList databases to obtain accurate qualitative and relative quantitative results. Metabolites with a screening threshold of |log2 (fold change)| ≥ 0.58 and a p-value < 0.05 were considered to be differentially changed metabolites (DCMs). Statistical analyses were performed utilizing R (3.4.3), Python (2.7.6), and CentOS (6.6).

Western blot

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer and its concentration was quantified with a BCA kit. A total of 30 µg of protein was utilized for sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) in accordance with established protocols26. The antibodies for MAPK, p38-MAPK, ERK, p-ERK were obtained from Abmart Shanghai co., Ltd, while the antibody for 11β-HSD2 (PB0747) was sourced from Boster Biological Technology co., Ltd.

Detection of aldosterone and cortisol by ELISA kit

Aldosterone (H188-1-2) and cortisol (H094-1-2) enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were sourced from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute27. All experiments adhered strictly to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Molecular docking

The 3D structure of the MAPK protein was obtained from Protein Data Bank (PDB) database (http://www.rcsb.org/). Concurrently, and the SDF file of BPA was sourced from Pubchem database (http://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and subsequently transformed it into the MOL format by Open Babel software. Prior to molecular docking simulations, the pivotal protein structures underwent dehydration and hydrogenation preprocessing with AutoDockTools version 1.5.6. Visualization of the proteins exhibiting the most substantial binding energies was achieved using PyMOL software.

Cell culture and exposure

TCMK-1 and HK-2 cells were obtained from Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. Two types of cell were cultured in DMEM medium (C11995500, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (SA101.02, CellMax), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (MA0110, Meilunbio). BPA stock solution (10 mM) was prepared in advance using DMSO, and the cells were exposed after gradient dilution with DMEM medium. For p38 MAPK inhibition, 10 µM Adezmapimo (SB203580, MCE) was used for pretreatment, added one hour prior to BPA exposure.

For the CCK-8 experiment, cells were digested with trypsin and seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well in a 96-well plate. Then cells were treated with BPA at gradient concentrations of 0, 0.3, 1, 3, 10, 30, 50, and 100 µM for 24 h. Then, 10 µL CCK8 reagent (MA0218-5, Meilunbio) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 30 min before immediately measuring the absorbance at 450 nm.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 5 and results are presented as the mean ± SE. The normality analyses were conducted using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Significant differences were assessed using the t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Bioinformatic analysis of the molecular mechanism involved in BPA-induced chronic kidney disease

As a ubiquitous environmental endocrine disruptor, BPA is implicated in the progression of chronic kidney disease28–30. Despite its recognized association, the mechanism pathways and metabolic consequences of BPA-induced renal damage remain poorly understood. To bridge this knowledge gap and elucidate the comprehensive impact of BPA on kidney health, we employed bioinformatics tools to identify and analyze the key genes implicated in BPA-mediated chronic renal injury. This analysis was conducted using the datasets from GEO and CTD, aiming to delineate their functional roles within the context of renal pathology.

After searching and screening the GEO database, a total of 6 sets of gene microarray datasets (GSE243078, GSE200306, GSE141295, GSE180395, GSE104954, GSE47185) associated with chronic renal injury were obtained, and most of the 382 samples involved were primarily from kidney tissue biopsies (Table S1). Combined with results from the CTD database, 333 common differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified (Fig. 1A). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the DAVID database to elucidate the biological processes and regulatory pathways involved in BPA induced chronic renal injury (Fig. 1B and C). GO analysis revealed that BPA affects chronic renal injury mainly through biological processes such as positive regulation of ERK1/2 cascade, response to hypoxia and cell adhesion. KEGG enrichment results showed that PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, AGE-RAGE signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, thyroid hormone signaling pathway, Cushing’s syndrome, steroid hormone biosynthesis and p53 signaling pathway were closely associated with the pathological changes of BPA-induced chronic renal injury.

Systematic evaluation of BPA exposure effects on renal injury in mice

Although bioinformatics analyses have elucidated the transcriptional regulatory process underlying BPA-induced renal injury, the metabolic effects of BPA on the kidney, one of the major metabolic organs, remain poorly understood. To systemically visualize the hidden renal damage caused by oral BPA exposure, PET/CT imaging was employed to detect inflammation and kidney damage in mice (Fig. 2A). The PET/CT imaging revealed high uptake of 18F-FAPI-74 in the kidney and intestine of both the BPA-exposed group and vehicle group, but no statistically significant uptake was observed in the brain, lung, and muscle. Compared to the vehicle group, the renal region of BPA-exposed mice exhibited varying degrees of high uptake at both transverse and coronal locations. The SUVmax of the ROI in cross-sectional CT images of the kidneys were significantly higher in the BPA group than that in the vehicle group (BPA group vs. vehicle group: 0.72 ± 0.03 vs. 1.52 ± 0.12), suggesting that BPA exposure could induce renal injury in mice.

The renal detoxification capacity and filtration function are indicated by BUN, UA and CR. Here, the BUN level in mice after BPA exposure was significantly elevated to 22.15 mmol/L, compared to the vehicle group (Fig. 2B). No statistically significant increase in UA or CR levels was observed in the BPA-exposed group. While nonspecific, increased BUN signals early glomerular or tubular stress and suggests that acute exposure to BPA may have an early renal stress, which would progress to overt dysfunction in chronic settings. Supporting this hypothesis, previous chronic exposure studies have found that long-term exposure to BPA leads to increases in urea nitrogen and creatinine in mice31.

BPA exposure at environmentally relevant levels altered renal architecture, as visualized and evidenced by H&E, Masson’s trichrome, PAS staining images of kidney sections. The H&E staining facilitated the assessment of glomerular and tubular morphology, revealing weakened cytoplasmic staining in renal tubular epithelial cells, detachment of some cells, and shrinkage and disintegration of certain nuclei. Masson’s trichrome staining was employed to gauge renal fibrosis extent, while PAS staining was utilized to quantify glycoprotein accumulation in renal tubular epithelial cells (Fig. 2C). The visualization of renal structure alteration above was confirmed by the injury profiles reported in other mouse models32,33, albeit less pronounced than those documented in diabetic mice22.

The above imaging, biochemical and histological evidence indicates that BPA exposure can cause pathological changes in the acute phase. Although it is milder than chronic exposure, the injury mechanism it initiates is similar to that of the chronic kidney disease model. Therefore, these studies suggest that continuous exposure to BPA may develop into overt renal dysfunction.

The transcriptomic alterations of kidney tissue following BPA exposure

Gene expression alterations were observed in the kidney tissue, which exhibited structural alterations due to BPA exposure. Transcriptomic analysis revealed 11,063 co-expression genes between the BPA-exposure group and the vehicle group (Fig. 3A). Principal component analysis (PCA) of the gene expression values (FPKM, Fragments Per Kilobase Million) indicated significant differences in gene distribution between the BPA-exposed group and the vehicle group (Fig. 3B). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined using a threshold of an absolute log2 fold change (FC) ≥ 0.58 and a p-value < 0.05. A total of 1,952 DEGs were identified, with 846 upregulated and 1,106 downregulated (Fig. 3C).

In addition, KEGG enrichment analysis of the top 25 pathways showed significant enrichment of the MAPK signaling pathway, Cushing’s syndrome, PPAR signaling pathway, and the steroid hormone biosynthesis, including cortisol synthesis and secretion (Fig. 3E). This finding is consistent with the KEGG results from the bioinformatics analysis presented in Fig. 1.

The metabolic alterations in renal tissue subsequent to BPA exposure

The transcriptome serves as a pivotal resource for investigating cellular functions at the genetic level. Metabolomics elucidates the roles of metabolites in biological processes by mapping their alterations, thereby furnishing robust empirical evidence to complement transcriptomic analyses. Here, non-targeted metabolomics assays were employed to gain deeper insights into the regulatory mechanism of BPA exposure on kidney tissue.

The metabolomics analysis identified 1153 metabolites, of which 736 were detected in positive ion mode and 417 in negative ion mode. The partial least squares discrimination analysis (PLS-DA) indicated significant differences between the BPA-exposed group and the vehicle group (Fig. 4A). The metabolites with an absolute value of log2FC ≥ 0.58, and a p-value < 0.05 were considered differentially changed metabolites (DCMs). There were 35 upregulated DCMs and 34 downregulated DCMs (Fig. 4B). Among the detected metabolites, levels of steroid hormone biosynthesis-related metabolites like cortisol and cortisone slightly decreased post-BPA treatment, whereas levels of cortisol metabolites 5α-tetrahydrocortisol (5α-THF) and 6β-hydroxycortisol (6β-OHF) increased (Fig. 4C). In addition, the KEGG enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment in glutathione metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, the HIF-1 signaling pathway, and steroid hormone biosynthesis (Fig. 4D). These findings align well with transcriptomic data indicating that BPA exposure disrupted steroid hormone biosynthesis in mouse kidney tissue.

Integration of transcriptomics and metabolomics analysis to reveal the molecular mechanism of steroid hormone biosynthesis disorder in kidney induced by BPA

The integrative KEGG enrichment analysis between transcriptome and metabolome was performed to elucidate the post-transcriptional regulation process of key genes involved in kidney tissue upon BPA exposure. 17 KEGG pathways enriched in the metabolome overlapped with those in the transcriptome (Fig. 5A). These included the HIF-1 signaling pathway, adrenergic signaling in cardiomyocytes and steroid hormone biosynthesis, which are closely associated with renal damage. Specifically, steroid hormone biosynthesis (encompassing cortisol and aldosterone) and the MAPK pathway - a classical regulator of HIF-1 signaling - were identified as crucial mediators of BPA-induced renal injury. To validate this, the levels of cortisol and aldosterone in mouse blood were quantified and showed a slight decrease following BPA exposure, consistent with metabolomics data (Fig. 5B). To further confirm the role of MAPK pathway in BPA-induced renal injury, the expression of MAPK-related proteins was detected (Fig. 5C). The results showed that a significant increase in p38 MAPK activation (1.91-fold compared to the vehicle group) after BPA exposure, while the activation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) remained unchanged statistically. These results suggest that the p38 MAPK pathway is implicated in renal injury induced by BPA exposure.

p38 MAPK and ERK1/2 are crucial regulators of placental 11β-HSD234,35. MAPK is involved in hypoxia-induced suppression of 11β-HSD2 activity in renal tubular epithelial cells36. 11β-HSD2 is a vital enzyme governing cortisol synthesis and modulating aldosterone effects37. The expression of hsd11b2 that encodes the 11β-Hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase 2 (11β-HSD2) was significantly decreased in this study (Fig. 3D). To corroborate these observations, we assessed the protein abundance of 11β-HSD2 in murine kidneys post-BPA treatment (Fig. 5D). The data demonstrated a decrease in 11β-HSD2 levels to 0.72-fold of the vehicle group, mirroring the transcriptional downregulation observed.

Cellular validation of BPA exposure inducing impaired renal steroid hormone biosynthesis via the MAPK pathway

To investigate the regulatory mechanism by which BPA exposure modulates 11β-HSD2 enzyme activity via the p38 MAPK pathway, we established BPA exposure models using human kidney cells (HK-2) and mouse kidney cells (TCMK-1). Initially, molecular docking simulations using AutoDock software accurately predicted the relative binding affinities of BPA for MAPK and ERK proteins (Fig. 6A). Notably, BPA exhibited a strong interaction with MAPK, forming hydrogen bonds with amino acid residues (including ILE-233) and a binding energy of -8.14 kcal/mol, which exceeds the threshold for significant binding activity. Conversely, the interaction between BPA and ERK was weaker, with a binding energy of -5.76 kcal/mol, indicating a lower binding affinity compared to that observed with MAPK.

The level of 11β-HSD2 protein was significantly decreased in TCMK-1 cells treated with 3 µM BPA, while the level was slightly decreased in BPA-exposed HK-2 cells, consistent with the trend observed in the mouse model (Fig. 6B and C). Notably, this decrease in 11β-HSD2 expression was effectively counteracted by pre-treating the cells with 10 µM of the p38 MAPK inhibitor Adezmapimod (SB203580). These findings highlight the central role of p38 MAPK as a key mediator in the suppression of 11β-HSD2 expression triggered by BPA exposure.

Discussion

Although bioinformatics analyses have elucidated the transcriptional regulatory processes of BPA-induced renal injury, mechanisms controlling metabolic homeostasis are still less understood. Therefore, to gain insight into the regulatory process of the metabolic effects of BPA on the kidney, we performed a combined transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis using a mouse exposure model.

Growing evidence suggests that inflammation plays a pivotal role in renal injury and is closely associated with metabolic reprogramming and functional loss38. Following drug stimulation, the necrosis or apoptosis of renal epithelial and endothelial cells results in the release of signaling molecules that can activate immune cells and lead to the production of chemokines and pro-inflammatory cytokines38,39. Acute injury to renal tubular epithelial cells, accompanied by a progressive and rapid increase in BUN and serum creatinine concentrations, represents a significant process in renal excretory failure40. The visualised inflammatory injury revealed by PET/CT, the tubular epithelial cell injury revealed by histochemical staining, and the increased BUN in this study collectively support the deleterious effects of BPA exposure on the kidney.

Transcriptomic analysis of BPA-exposed mice revealed decreased renal expression of Hsd11b2, which encodes 11β-HSD2 involved in steroid hormone biosynthesis. 11β-HSD2 is a key glucocorticoid-metabolising enzyme that converts cortisol to cortisone and reduces active glucocorticoid levels in the kidney41. Lack of 11β-HSD2 could result in glucocorticoid occupancy of renal mineralocorticoid receptor, leading to sodium ion retention and hypertension42. Patients with 11β-HSD2 deficiency may have hypertension, hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, low renin, and low aldosterone43. The studies suggest that BPA exposure can directly inhibit the activity of 11β-HSD2 directly in human and rat tissues44,45. This is consistent with our findings in vivo.

BPA exposure resulted in a decreased expression of genes related to cortisol and cortisone biosynthesis associated with steroid hormones, while simultaneously increasing the levels of cortisol metabolites, specifically 5α-tetrahydrocortisol (5α-THF) and 6β-hydroxycortisol (6β-OHF) (Fig. 5E). Normally, cortisol can be reduced to allo-dihydrocortisol (5α-DHF) and DHF by 5α- and 5β-reductase, respectively, and then metabolised to 5α-THF and THF, respectively46. In addition, a very small fraction (about 1%) of cortisol can be catalysed by the enzyme 6β-hydroxylase to form 6β-OHF, which is excreted in the urine47. Cortisol decreased, 5α-THF and 6β-OHF increased, suggesting that cortisol metabolism was enhanced after BPA exposure, as supported by previous studies. Exposure to 30-3000 ng/mL BPA causes significant reductions in androstenedione, testosterone, cortisol, and cortisone in an in vitro model of H295R human adenocarcinoma cells48. Exposure to high concentrations of Fluorene-9-bisphenol (an alternative of BPA) inhibits the production of aldosterone, cortisol, testosterone, and E2 by down-regulating steroidogenic genes in H295R cells49. Similarly, results from a birth cohort study reveal that for each natural logarithmic unit increase of urinary BPA concentrations in adolescent boys, serum cortisol levels decreased by 16%50. A longitudinal cohort study of pregnant women found that high urinary BPA levels during pregnancy were associated with daily salivary cortisol dysregulation51. These results are consistent with the multi-omics results of the current study, which confirm that BPA exposure can affect steroid hormone biosynthesis. The steroid hormones cortisol and aldosterone are produced by the adrenal cortex and impaired renal function is closely related to cortisol metabolism52. This is supported by the data from the present study.

A study of BPA-exposed diabetic male rats confirmed that BPA exposure can activate the MAPK cascade in both heart and kidney22. The transcriptomic and metabolomic data in this study also indicate the regulatory processes of MAPK-related cascade signaling pathways. Our findings show that BPA exposure modulates the metabolic enzyme activity of 11β-HSD2 by interacting with MAPK proteins and inducing MAPK phosphorylation. This interaction subsequently influences the synthesis of steroid hormones. Consistent with previous literature53,54, under pathophysiological conditions, a reduction in 11β-HSD2 activity leads to decreased cortisol conversion and excess glucocorticoids, resulting in overactivation of the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR). This cascade contributes to renal hypoplasia. Our results are consistent with these observations, suggesting that BPA’s effects on the p38 MAPK pathway play a pivotal role in mediating its effects on steroidogenesis, thereby implicating this mechanism in BPA-induced renal metabolic abnormalities.

Although the present study revealed that BPA exposure induces metabolic disruption effects in the kidney, there are still some limitations in the interpretation of the experimental results. First, there is a lack of consideration for genetic variability, which may significantly impact transcriptomic profiles and the interpretation of BPA-induced kidney damage. While our study focused on controlled in vivo conditions to minimise confounding factors, the future investigations incorporating diverse genetic backgrounds or population-based models will strengthen the translational relevance of the findings. For instance, although this study mainly focused on male mice, future work should take into account the gender-specific metabolic responses of female mice to BPA. Second, this study lacks verification of MAPK pathway blocking at the animal level. It is necessary to conduct in-depth and comprehensive verification analyses on the regulatory role of the MAPK pathway in the future.

Conclusion

Long-term BPA exposure is associated with invisible renal damage and hidden metabolic disturbances related to complex pathways of gene expression and metabolism in humans. In this study, bioinformatics analyses revealed a variety of bioregulatory processes involved in human renal injury induced by BPA exposure, and the renal metabolic disorders induced by BPA are closely related to these complex gene regulatory pathways. Therefore, the damage and dysfunction of the kidney by BPA exposure were visualized and confirmed by PET/CT, renal function analysis, and histopathological section staining in a mouse exposure model. Then, integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis of kidney tissues, along with validation results from cellular in vitro models, revealed that BPA exposure regulates the expression of 11β-HSD2 by influencing p38 MAPK, ultimately affecting cortisol metabolism and aldosterone synthesis. Alteration of steroid hormone biosynthesis is a key pathway in BPA-induced kidney damage. In summary, this study reveals the important molecular mechanisms underlying BPA-induced renal metabolic toxicity, providing a theoretical basis and experimental support for assessing its potential health risks.
