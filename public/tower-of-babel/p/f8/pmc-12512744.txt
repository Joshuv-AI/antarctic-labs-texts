Experiences of patients with cervical cancer undergoing concurrent chemoradiation therapy at the Korle-Bu teaching hospital in Ghana: an explorative qualitative study
Authors: Enam Adzo Setsoafia, Gifty Sarfo-Annan, Joseph Daniels, Jackline Addae, Susanna Aba Abraham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical cancer is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in women worldwide, especially in limited-resource settings. Concurrent chemoradiation followed by brachytherapy is the standard of care for patients with locally advanced cervical cancer. Concurrent treatment with external beam radiotherapy and chemotherapy is however, associated with a considerable risk of toxicity. Patients who are treated experience emotional, psychological, social, financial and physiological consequences. The aim of the study was to explore the experiences of women with cervical cancer undergoing concurrent chemoradiation therapy at the Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital in Accra, Ghana, and to identify aspects of care that could be improved to enhance their overall treatment experience.MethodsThe research was an explorative-descriptive qualitative study involving Ghanaian women with a histo-pathologically confirmed diagnosis of cervical cancer undergoing chemoradiation. Qualitative data were collected via semi-structured in-person interviews and thematically analysed.ResultsFour main themes were identified, namely: “deciding on the choice of treatment”, “cruising through therapy”, “losing self-image”, and “evolving into a new person: from caterpillar to butterfly”. Some of the patients experienced psychological effects such as fear and depression, whereas others encountered financial difficulties as well as disruption of social relations to the point of isolation. Many patients received support from their spouses, family members and/or care givers.ConclusionsThe study highlights significant gaps in cervical cancer awareness among patients prior to diagnosis, leading to fear and anxiety during treatment. Most patients deferred treatment decisions to healthcare professionals due to their expertise. Concurrent chemotherapy profoundly affects patients’ well-being, causing psychological distress, physical side effects, economic burden, and social isolation. Patients adopt various coping strategies, including inner resolve, faith in God, and social support.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14824-3.

Background

Cancer remains the second leading cause of mortality globally, with an estimated 10 million deaths in 2020 [1]. Even though cervical cancer is preventable through vaccination, the disease continues to claim the lives of many women worldwide. In 2022, cervical cancer was the fourth most frequently diagnosed cancer in women, with over 600,000 newly diagnosed patients and 342,000 deaths recorded globally [2]. Cervical cancer is a disease of global public health concern particularly in middle-aged women and is considered a disease of disparity due to differences in incidence and mortality rates between developing and developed countries. Africa bears a substantial portion of this burden, with cervical cancer accounting for 12% of all gynaecological cancer-related deaths on the continent. In 2020, the incidence of cervical cancer in Ghana was 31.7 per 100,000 women, while the mortality rate was 23.1 per 100,000 women, significantly higher than in developed nations [1]. The World Health Organization predicts that by the end of 2025, Ghana will record approximately 5,000 newly diagnosed cervical cancer patients annually, leading to around 3,361 cervical cancer-related deaths each year [3]. This rising incidence places a considerable strain on families and communities. Effective screening programs and changes in the risk factors for the disease, particularly the Human Papilloma Virus (HPV), play critical roles in the rate at which cervical cancer increases in various countries [4].

The management of cervical cancer typically involves a combination of treatment modalities, such as surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, tailored to the stage of the disease and other factors such as patient’s age (geriatric score), performance status, comorbidities and renal function. Radiation therapy combined with chemotherapy (chemoradiation), followed by brachytherapy, is the standard of care for patients with locally advanced cervical cancer. Treatment often leads to side effects, including urinary, gastrointestinal, sexual, and neurological issues, which can have a devastating influence on a patient’s quality of life [5]. These toxicities affect individuals differently, and no two patients experience them in the same way.

Previous research on cervical cancer in Ghana have predominantly focused on prevalence, prevention, screening, early detection and patients’ knowledge about the disease [6–10]. Other studies have also reported treatment outcomes [11, 12] and the sexuality of patients undergoing definitive chemoradiation for cervical cancer [13]. However, there is a scarcity of research focused on the experiences of cervical cancer patients in Ghana and other low- and middle-income countries.

Methods

Aim

To explore the experiences of women with cervical cancer undergoing concurrent chemoradiation therapy at the Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital in Accra, Ghana, and to identify aspects of care that could be improved to enhance their overall treatment experience.

Study design

The study employed an explorative-descriptive qualitative design, which was appropriate given the aim to gain an in-depth understanding of the complex and nuanced experiences of women undergoing definitive, curative treatment with concurrent chemoradiation therapy for cervical cancer in Ghana. This approach is particularly suited to research questions that seek to explore participants’ experiences in contexts where limited prior understanding exists [14].

Study setting

This study was conducted at the National Centre for Radiotherapy, Oncology and Nuclear Medicine, located within the Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital in Accra, Ghana. The Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital was founded on October 9, 1923, and is one of the largest teaching hospitals in Africa, serving as a major tertiary referral centre in Ghana, offering a wide range of medical services and training for healthcare professionals. The centre became operational in 1997 and is also one of the largest cancer treatment facilities in the West African subregion. The centre provides cancer treatment services for both paediatric and adult patients with solid tumours utilizing external beam as well as systemic radiotherapy and brachytherapy. The centre also offers other treatment modalities such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy, targeted and hormonal therapies.

Study population

The study involved adult female patients (≥ 18years) with a confirmed diagnosis of cervical cancer who were undergoing definitive concurrent chemoradiation therapy. Patients with cervical cancer who had not yet started treatment were excluded from the study due to their lack of experience with concurrent chemoradiation. Patients with locoregionally recurrent or metastatic cervical cancer, receiving sequential chemotherapy and radiotherapy were not included in the study. Patients with cervical cancer who were receiving adjuvant chemoradiation or radiotherapy alone following primary surgery were also not included in the study. Patients were included in the study only if they had no previous diagnosis or treatment for cancer at other primary sites, to ensure an unbiased assessment of their cancer treatment experience.

Sampling and study size

A purposive sampling technique was employed to recruit participants who could provide rich, relevant insights into the experience of undergoing concurrent chemoradiation therapy for cervical cancer. The electronic patient database at the study site was screened by two independent members of the research team to identify potential participants based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible Ghanaian women who met the criteria and consented to participate were selectively recruited into the study.

In all, ten (10) participants were targeted based on methodological guidance for qualitative studies employing in-depth interviews, particularly in resource-limited settings and within specific patient populations. Participants were selected from different age groups, socioeconomic backgrounds, stages of cervical cancer and religious affiliations to capture a broader range of experiences and perspectives whilst reducing the risk of bias from over-representing a specific patient group. The sample size was deemed sufficient to capture a wide range of experiences while remaining feasible given the intensive nature of qualitative data collection and analysis. While the target number was set in advance, data saturation was used as a guiding principle to assess the adequacy of the sample during data collection. Data saturation, defined as the point at which no new themes or insights were emerging [15] , was observed after the eighth interview. However, two additional participants were interviewed beyond the saturation point to confirm the consistency of the emerging themes and enhance the credibility of the findings. This approach also ensured adherence to the original sampling plan, upheld ethical commitments to participants who had already been recruited and consented, and allowed for verification, and refinement of the emergent themes to ensure that no potentially valuable perspectives were excluded. The relatively small but very selective sample allowed for in-depth qualitative data collection and analysis. Previous studies have confirmed the appropriateness and usefulness of such a small sample size for qualitative research of this nature, partly due to the richness of information provided [16]. Moreover, the limited study size reduced the risk of over-representing a specific demographic of cervical cancer patients in the study. Achieving data saturation ensured that the study’s findings were comprehensive and that the sample size albeit small, was sufficient to support the research conclusions [17].

Data collection instruments

A background information sheet and an interview guide were developed for data collection. The background information sheet (Appendix I) was used to collect data on the demographic features of the participants such as their age, place of residence, marital status, parity, occupation, and the highest level of formal education. Additionally, data were collected on participants’ sexual history, duration of cervical cancer diagnosis, smoking history and alcohol intake. The interview guide (Appendix II) included probes regarding decision-making prior the commencement of cervical cancer treatment as well as the physical, psychological and social well-being of the participants. Coping strategies employed by the patients were also explored. Pilot testing of the research instrument was conducted to ensure the suitability of the interview guide for eliciting the required information.

Data collection procedure

All interviews were conducted in-person at the hospital, in a private setting, to ensure participant comfort and confidentiality, and were digitally recorded with the consent of each participant. The interviewer was thoroughly trained on techniques for conducting unbiased interviews, such as active listening, avoiding leading questions, and maintaining neutrality, all of which reduced the risk of inadvertently influencing participants’ responses. Patients’ demographic information and treatment-related details were retrieved from their hospital-based medical records. Verbal responses were elicited from patients to confirm this information at the beginning of each interview. The interviews delved deep into the knowledge and understanding gained by the participants through direct, first-hand involvement with cervical cancer and its treatment with chemoradiation. Participants shared their subjective perceptions, emotions, and insights, providing rich, detailed insights into their thoughts, feelings and behaviours regarding cervical cancer. Each interview lasted between 25 and 35 min. All interviews were conducted in English, with no need for translation into other languages. The use of the same interview guide for all interviews as well as the incorporation of member checking, peer debriefing and transparent reporting, minimized bias in this study.

Data analysis

Data collection and analysis were done simultaneously. All ten interviews were transcribed verbatim and analysed using the thematic analysis approach proposed by Braun and Clarke [18]. The analysis followed an inductive approach, with themes emerging from the data without imposing pre-existing theoretical frameworks. Each transcript wasread multiple times to identify codes, categories and the primary patterns in the data. Relevant phrases were highlighted and short codes assigned to describe the content/context. The highlighted data were collated together into groups that were identified by the codes to get a condensed overview of the main points recurring throughout the participants narratives. Afterwards, themes were generated by identifying patterns among the codes and combining several codes. The themes generated were reviewed to ensure that they were useful and accurate representations of the participants narratives. The emergent themes were then defined by formulating their meaning by explaining exactly what the theme means in relation to the dataset.

Methodological rigour, validity & reliability

The credibility of the study was ensured by prolonged engagement, member checking, triangulation and peer de-briefing. Credibility was further established by generating research findings directly from the original data collected from the study participants [19]. After analyzing the data collected for this research, telephone calls were made to the study participants to verify the accuracy and authenticity of the data and interpretations derived from it, confirming that their perspectives and experiences were duly reflected. Triangulation was achieved through multiple strategies: first, data triangulation was conducted by cross-referencing participants’ verbal accounts with their demographic and clinical information obtained from electronic medical records. This allowed the researchers to verify factual accuracy and contextualize individual narratives. Additionally, investigator triangulation was employed by involving a peer with qualitative research expertise to independently review the coding process and emergent themes. Peer debriefing was conducted with by qualified colleague of the principal investigator - someone not directly involved in the study who independently reviewed and critiqued the entire research process. Aspects of the research reviewed by the peer included the study design, data collection tools & methods, data analysis, and the research findings. The feedback of the peer reviewer was incorporated into the analysis and interpretation of the findings to improve the quality and rigor of the research.

Themes and sub-themes identified from the analysis of the data

Four themes with several sub-themes were identified from the analysis of the data, namely, “deciding on the choice of treatment,” “cruising through therapy,” losing my self-image”, and “evolving into a new person: from caterpillar to butterfly” (Fig. 1).

Themes and sub-themes

Theme #1: deciding to undergo treatment

This theme focused on the processes that underscored the participants’ decision to undergo treatment with concurrent chemoradiation therapy. Participants were initially ignorant about the treatment options available to them and, had little or no hope of survival. Becoming aware of the available treatment options and subsequently deciding on receiving treatment for cervical cancer was associated with three sub-themes: “Distraught by the diagnosis of cervical cancer”, “Getting schooled about the risk factors, causes, management and prognosis of cervical cancer” and “Informed or deferred decision?”.

A. Distraught by the diagnosis of cervical cancer

For several participants, the diagnosis of cervical cancer was hope-shattering and akin to a “death sentence”. Participants revealed that being unaware of treatment options and survival outcomes made them “panic-stricken”.

“I was so afraid when I was told that I had cervical cancer… I thought that was my end”. (Patient #2)

“I was so afraid when I was told that I had cervical cancer… I thought that was my end”. (Patient #2)

This fear was rooted in participants’ knowledge of the diagnosis and prognosis of other patients with a similar diagnosis of cervical cancer. One participant explained that:

“I remember a woman from my church was also diagnosed with same disease [cervical cancer]. We [members of the women’s ministry] used to visit her. She always had a bad odour and complained of terrible pain. She lost all her hair and eventually died, looking very skinny. Some even suggested she had human immunodeficiency virus-acquired immune deficiency syndrome. It was later that we got to know that it was cancer”. (Patient #10)

“I remember a woman from my church was also diagnosed with same disease [cervical cancer]. We [members of the women’s ministry] used to visit her. She always had a bad odour and complained of terrible pain. She lost all her hair and eventually died, looking very skinny. Some even suggested she had human immunodeficiency virus-acquired immune deficiency syndrome. It was later that we got to know that it was cancer”. (Patient #10)

For this participant, the extrapolation of the experiences of her church member to her own situation when she received the diagnosis of cervical cancer caused her a lot of anxiety and made her distraught. For other participants, the lack of prior knowledge of the condition or the treatment options underscored their reaction to their diagnosis of cervical cancer. An excerpt from the narratives read:“If you ask me why I was afraid or worried, I will say it was because I had no idea about the disease before the doctor told me what she suspected”. (Patient #4)

“If you ask me why I was afraid or worried, I will say it was because I had no idea about the disease before the doctor told me what she suspected”. (Patient #4)

B. Getting schooled about the risk factors, causes, management and prognosis of cervical cancer

All the participants received some form of education on the risk factors, aetiology, management and prognosis of cervical cancer prior to the initiation of treatment at the Radiotherapy centre.“They discussed with me and my husband the risk factors and causes of cervical cancer and how they will treat the disease” (Patient #5)

“They discussed with me and my husband the risk factors and causes of cervical cancer and how they will treat the disease” (Patient #5)

For most of the participants, the information given to them by the oncologists and nurses at the study site formed the baseline of their knowledge about the aetiology of cervical cancer. A participant said:“They told me that a small animal [virus] called HPV causes it and that if I started having sex early, I might have gotten it along the line…” (Patient #2)

“They told me that a small animal [virus] called HPV causes it and that if I started having sex early, I might have gotten it along the line…” (Patient #2)

A few participants had the perception that, there was a spiritual cause of their disease and intimated that cancer-specific education provided at the radiotherapy centre debunked their perceptions, refocusing their efforts towards treatment. One participant explained that:“I kept asking why I had the condition, so they [nurses] explained to me that they also don’t know the cause, but many things could have contributed to me getting it… they also tried to change my mind about the existence of a spiritual cause and encouraged me to focus on the physical cause and agree to be treated”. (Patient #7)

“I kept asking why I had the condition, so they [nurses] explained to me that they also don’t know the cause, but many things could have contributed to me getting it… they also tried to change my mind about the existence of a spiritual cause and encouraged me to focus on the physical cause and agree to be treated”. (Patient #7)

The health information that participants received also shed light on the possible treatment options that were available for the management of cervical cancer. Some excerpts from the narratives read:“The oncologist explained to me that the treatment options available for my condition were operation [surgery] possibly followed by radiotherapy with or without chemotherapy and/or brachytherapy depending on the outcome of the surgery, or alternatively, I could undergo chemoradiation followed by brachytherapy …”. (Patient #4)

Knowledge that a good prognosis was associated with early initiation of treatment restored hope among several of the participants and propelled them towards consenting to treatment.“When the doctor explained to me that there was treatment which could cure the disease if we started early, I regained hope and made the resolve to do everything they [health workers] said”. “When the doctor explained to me that there was treatment which could cure the disease if we started early, I regained hope and made the resolve to do everything they [health workers] said”. (Patient #7)

They said I will have sores, hair loss, my body will not be in a good state, and it will take long before I come back to my normal body. But I just wanted to get started with the treatment”. Privacy was maintained during education sessions, with nurses anticipating and taking steps to address concerns of patients. C. Informed or deferred decision

For most of the participants, although they had received adequate information on the available treatment options for managing cervical cancer, the decision to undergo chemoradiation therapy was deferred to the treating physicians. A participant said that:“I decided to let them (multidisciplinary team of doctors) decide which treatment option was best for me… and I have not regretted…”. (Patient #4)

“I decided to let them (multidisciplinary team of doctors) decide which treatment option was best for me… and I have not regretted…”. (Patient #4)

Some participants deferred decisions about their cervical cancer treatment to their physicians primarily because they trusted them, as experts with extensive experience, to make the best treatment choices. A participant said:“They [health workers] explained the types of treatment to me… but I felt they knew best. They have seen so many patients and know which treatment will cure me, so I accepted it when they told me to undergo radiotherapy with weekly chemotherapy”. (Patient #10)

“They [health workers] explained the types of treatment to me… but I felt they knew best. They have seen so many patients and know which treatment will cure me, so I accepted it when they told me to undergo radiotherapy with weekly chemotherapy”. (Patient #10)

All the participants agreed with the choice of treatment and consented to treatment by signing the informed consent form.“I signed the forms [informed consent] for treatment myself…”. (Patient #6)“After they explained everything to me, they gave me the form, and I signed”. (Patient #2)

“I signed the forms [informed consent] for treatment myself…”. (Patient #6)

“After they explained everything to me, they gave me the form, and I signed”. (Patient #2)

Theme #2: cruising through treatment

Participants recounted their experiences when they commenced treatment. These experiences centred on psychological reactions as well as the interactions and support that made the treatment journey bearable. Four sub-themes were developed, namely, “Facing the elephant in the room”, “Building an inner resolve”, “Building a core support team”, and “Coping with God”.

A. Facing the elephant in the room

The participants’ experience of concurrent chemoradiation therapy was varied. Initial reactions of fear were reported by all the participants. The participants explained:“I was frightened. Truthfully, I had never been in such machine before, so, I was frightened.” (Patient #7)

“I was frightened. Truthfully, I had never been in such machine before, so, I was frightened.” (Patient #7)

For others, undergoing treatment made them anxious, resulting in some having to be counselled during treatment sessions. A participant said:“I was anxious to be in the radiotherapy machine. So anxious that they [radiotherapists] told me that they must pause my treatment until I calm down and remain still. They left me for a while before continuing my treatment”. (Patient #4)

“I was anxious to be in the radiotherapy machine. So anxious that they [radiotherapists] told me that they must pause my treatment until I calm down and remain still. They left me for a while before continuing my treatment”. (Patient #4)

Some participants became anxious because of therapy. For some, this anxiety was projected to the health facility such that visiting the facility for therapy had become difficult. This anxiety resulted in physical symptoms such as sleeplessness.“Initially, I was so anxious that whenever I had to come for a session [radiotherapy], I couldn’t sleep the night before. I was frightened to visit the hospital”. (Patient #4)

“Initially, I was so anxious that whenever I had to come for a session [radiotherapy], I couldn’t sleep the night before. I was frightened to visit the hospital”. (Patient #4)

The anxiety of several participants was underscored by anticipation of the occurrence of potential side effects of concurrent chemoradiation therapy.“As for me, I was very concerned about the occurrence of side effects… even though the nurses had told me, I kept looking at my skin and gently tagging on my hair to see if they had started shedding… I was very worried about how the treatment will make me look”. (Patient #8)

“As for me, I was very concerned about the occurrence of side effects… even though the nurses had told me, I kept looking at my skin and gently tagging on my hair to see if they had started shedding… I was very worried about how the treatment will make me look”. (Patient #8)

It was also evident that as treatment progressed, the participants gradually became more comfortable and presented for treatment without any prodding from their support persons.“After some time, I was no longer worried. I have become used to the radiotherapy machine.” (Patient #10)

“After some time, I was no longer worried. I have become used to the radiotherapy machine.” (Patient #10)

B. Building an inner resolve

Analysis of the data revealed that, the participants had to develop coping strategies to be able to continue with therapy. Other participants also inferred developing an inner resolve to undergo treatment to become well. This was mainly attributed to the desire of being youthful and yearning to live rather than succumb to death.

“I am young, why should I suffer like this and die with this pain when I can bear the therapy and survive… I decided it was “do or die” so I decided to do my best.” (Patient #1)

“I am young, why should I suffer like this and die with this pain when I can bear the therapy and survive… I decided it was “do or die” so I decided to do my best.” (Patient #1)

“The nurses told me that my survival depended on me to some extent, so keeping that in my mind, I decided to commit to the full course of treatment.” (Patient #4)

“The nurses told me that my survival depended on me to some extent, so keeping that in my mind, I decided to commit to the full course of treatment.” (Patient #4)

For others, their children were the main reason why they committed to treatment despite potential side effects.“… My children are young, and I can’t imagine dying and leaving them behind… for who? Hmmm. So, I had to just do what was needed to become well and it included all the pain and side effects of the treatment.” (Patient #2)

“… My children are young, and I can’t imagine dying and leaving them behind… for who? Hmmm. So, I had to just do what was needed to become well and it included all the pain and side effects of the treatment.” (Patient #2)

C. Building a core support team

For several participants, building a core supportive team made up of family and health care workers was a very important strategy that enabled them to cope with their treatment. A participant indicated that sharing her challenges minimised the burden of treatment-related loneliness and anxiety.“I know that I was not alone. The nurses and doctors were with me throughout the sessions. I like the fact that, I was asked to do a lot of X-rays and labs [laboratory investigations] for them to assure themselves [health workers] that everything was going as planned”. (Patient #7)

“I know that I was not alone. The nurses and doctors were with me throughout the sessions. I like the fact that, I was asked to do a lot of X-rays and labs [laboratory investigations] for them to assure themselves [health workers] that everything was going as planned”. (Patient #7)

The presence of nurses during treatment sessions also helped patients cope.“During the therapy session, nurses and doctor remain with me and talk with me… They laugh with me. When I can’t get up, they help me to get up. They give me encouragement and advice, if I lodged complains the way they respond to it makes me feel they are very good children”. (Patient #2)

“During the therapy session, nurses and doctor remain with me and talk with me… They laugh with me. When I can’t get up, they help me to get up. They give me encouragement and advice, if I lodged complains the way they respond to it makes me feel they are very good children”. (Patient #2)

All the participants considered themselves to be religious. They believed that adhering to the therapy regimen was an act of faith.“I believe that God is working through the doctors and nurses to heal me, so I am also doing my part by trusting God”. (Patient #4)

“I believe that God is working through the doctors and nurses to heal me, so I am also doing my part by trusting God”. (Patient #4)

Several participants believed that their faith was important in coping with the effects of cancer treatment. Some of the participants intimated that God was the reason for their ability to cope with the effects of the therapy.“I believe God has a hand in my treatment. God is my everything. He will heal me.” (Patient #7)

“I believe God has a hand in my treatment. God is my everything. He will heal me.” (Patient #7)

Prayer was also an important tool in maintaining their faith and hope for a better outcome of treatment:“I always pray before every session, and I also recite some bible verses that build my faith. God is with me… I believe that” (Patient #3)

Theme #3: losing my image of me

For some participants, concurrent chemoradiation therapy impacted their lives physically, socially and financially. Thus, the image of who they were prior to diagnosis and treatment gradually faded. Four sub-themes emerged under this theme; “Changes in my body”; “Undertaking the treatment journey alone or with company” and “the cost implications: my purse is empty now.”

A. Changes in my body

All the participants experienced some physical side effects while undergoing concurrent chemoradiation. Although, all the participants reported being informed of the side effects of the treatment, several of them explained that the occurrence of the physical effects was a major source of concern for them. A participant explained that:“I knew that they would happen ooh; my hair falling out and even my nails, you know, becoming bristle and all that… but I was really anxious and worried when I thought about it and waited for it to happen”. (Patient #2)

“I knew that they would happen ooh; my hair falling out and even my nails, you know, becoming bristle and all that… but I was really anxious and worried when I thought about it and waited for it to happen”. (Patient #2)

All the participants indicated feeling sick and nauseous as the initial effects they experienced.“… Vomiting! I kept vomiting after the drip [chemotherapy] was given to me. I became so sick after each session that; the nurses allowed me to lie down for a while after everything before allowing me to go home”. (Patient #6)

“… Vomiting! I kept vomiting after the drip [chemotherapy] was given to me. I became so sick after each session that; the nurses allowed me to lie down for a while after everything before allowing me to go home”. (Patient #6)

B. Undertaking the treatment journey alone or with company

Undergoing the concurrent chemoradiation therapy also influenced the women socially and emotionally. Discussion

The study revealed four main themes among patients undergoing concurrent chemoradiation therapy: initial anxiety and decision-making influenced by healthcare provider guidance, evolving coping mechanisms like family support and faith, significant impacts on self-image due to physical side effects, and a transformative journey towards renewed self-worth and advocacy for healthcare improvements. The study revealed that nearly all participants were not sufficiently aware of cervical cancer before their diagnosis, lacking knowledge about its causes, risk factors, prevention methods, and treatment options. This ignorance induced fear, anxiety, and a sense of fatalism. These findings align with other studies that report low levels of cervical cancer awareness among patients before diagnosis [20, 21], often due to inadequate education and sensitization efforts in regions like Ghana [6]. Despite receiving adequate information on available treatment options, most participants deferred the decision to undergo combined chemo-radiation therapy to their healthcare providers. This preference is supported by findings of a similar study where patients with advanced cancers preferred their physicians to make treatment decisions due to their expertise and clinical experience [22]. Patients believed that health professionals, with their in-depth knowledge and clinical acumen, were better positioned to make such critical decisions. However, this finding contrasts with other studies, including a study examining the association of decision roles with patient-reported quality of care. The study found that of 10,817 treatment decisions, 39% were patient-controlled, 44% were shared, and 17% were physician-controlled, indicating a significant portion of patients preferred to actively participate in their treatment decisions [23].

The commencement of the cancer therapy was associated with negative psychological influences on the participants. Many participants reported anxiety and fear before therapy due to the critical and uncertain nature of their condition. This aligns with findings from a study among Ghanaian women, where cancer diagnoses, pain, and symptoms like vaginal bleeding caused significant anxiety [24]. Similar psychological distress has been observed in Oman, where worry and fear of death, disruptions to family and work responsibilities, and hope for a cure were prevalent among cancer patients prior to treatment [25]. Also, patients with gynaecological cancers often experience stress, depression, anxiety, fear of cancer recurrence and guilt related to the burden of cancer treatment on patients’ relatives [26]. Despite these challenges, participants demonstrated a strong inner resolve, which helped them endure the therapy for their benefit and that of their families. Physically, all participants experienced a decline in well-being, with common symptoms including nausea, vomiting, weight loss, and changes in skin colour. These findings are consistent with other studies in East Africa, where chemotherapy side effects such as fatigue, diarrhoea, and loss of appetite compromised patients’ quality of life [27, 28]. Pain was also a significant issue, with severe pain reported in various body parts [29, 30]. Gastrointestinal disturbances like nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, and anorexia further impacted patients’ weight and quality of life, corroborating findings from other studies [26, 31, 32]. Chronic nausea and vomiting particularly hindered routine activities and exacerbated physical discomfort [33].

Economically, the cost of treatment posed a substantial burden on the study participants, many of whom reported losing their livelihoods and depleting their savings. This economic strain is consistent with other studies highlighting the high cost of cancer care, particularly for gynaecological cancers [34, 35]. Financial constraints often prevent cervical cancer patients from affording treatment, leading to indebtedness and exacerbating their economic challenges [36]. Socially, the physical changes due to therapy impacted patients’ social interactions. Some participants chose isolation to avoid pity and intrusive questions, reflecting findings from other studies on the social effects of body disfigurement and treatment side effects such as hair loss, skin changes and weight loss [37, 38]. Visible side effects of cancer treatment can lead to social maladjustment and poor interactions [39]. The well-being of patients with cervical cancer undergoing chemoradiation therapy is significantly negatively impacted across psychological, physical, economic, and social domains.

The study provided evidence that the patients adopted various coping strategies, reflected in these themes: “building an inner resolve,” “building a core support team,” “coping with God,” “flapping my wings,” and “becoming a ray of hope for others”. Other studies have reported themes such as “thought and feelings”, “acceptance”, and “actively dealing with discomfort” [28, 40]. Participants described dealing with therapy side effects by developing inner tenacity and mental strength, motivated by a desire to live for their children and maintain their self-image. This is consistent with a South African study where patients emphasized inner strength and the will to live for their children [41]. Education from healthcare professionals also played a crucial role in bolstering their mental resilience [28, 31, 41]. Faith in God emerged as another significant coping mechanism, with patients believing in divine support through the actions of nurses and doctors. This finding is supported by studies indicating that cervical cancer patients rely heavily on their religious beliefs during treatment [31, 41]. Support systems, particularly from family, friends, and healthcare professionals, provided patients with a sense of security and strength to endure therapy. This mirrors findings that social support positively influences patients’ well-being and recovery [26]. Another study that explored the coping strategies used by cancer patients at the beginning, middle and end of treatment revealed that at the beginning of the treatment the most frequent coping strategy was seeking social-support 61.4% whereas that at the end of the treatment was 64.3% [42]. Patients undergoing cancer treatment often become a source of hope for newly diagnosed patients. Health workers use experienced patients as references, which empowers them and reinforces their own hope for recovery, corroborating findings from other studies on cancer survivors [41].

The breakdown of radiotherapy equipment leads to unplanned treatment interruptions and increase in the overall treatment time, leading to suboptimal survival outcomes [43]. Timely repair of equipment malfunction or breakdown as well as routine maintenance can reduce unnecessary interruptions in patients’ treatment. Challenges pertaining to health financing negatively affected the experience of the participants of this study just as in other studies conducted in Nigeria and Kenya where most patients have to pay for full treatment costs out of pocket [44]. Widening to scope of conditions covered under the national health insurance scheme to include cervical cancer can go a long way to minimize the financial toxicity experienced by patients managed for this disease.

Limitations of study

The study employed a qualitative exploratory-descriptive design, which inherently limits the generalizability of the findings to other settings. Nonetheless, rigorous mechanisms were instituted throughout data collection and analysis to enhance the trustworthiness of the findings, thus allowing other researchers to apply these findings if deemed appropriate.

Secondly, the data collection method utilized telephone-based interviews, which precluded the researcher from observing non-verbal communicative actions, such as facial expressions or body language, that might have provided additional insights for further probing. This approach was chosen due to COVID-19 prevention guidelines requiring social distancing, as well as the breakdown of the radiation machine, which impeded face-to-face interviews. Despite these limitations, the researcher maintained heightened attentiveness to verbal cues, such as pauses and sighs, and employed strategies like rephrasing and clarification to encourage participants to elaborate on their experiences.

Also, the study aimed to elicit experiences of concurrent chemoradiation therapy from participants who, in some instances, knew the researcher as a nurse. This familiarity posed a risk of social desirability bias, wherein participants might share information they believe would please the researcher rather than their genuine experiences. To mitigate this risk, the researcher informed participants that the study was an academic exercise with no impact on their access to services, thereby minimizing potential bias.

Recommendations

To enhance the care and support for cervical cancer patients, it is essential to improve public education on cervical cancer. Comprehensive education and workshops on holistic care for cervical cancer patients should be provided to counsellors, pastors, nurses, doctors, imams, and other community stakeholders. Furthermore, incorporating aspects of cervical cancer treatment not currently covered by the National Health Insurance Scheme is necessary to alleviate the financial burden on patients and their families.

Conclusions

The study highlights significant gaps in cervical cancer awareness among patients prior to diagnosis, leading to fear and anxiety. This underscores the need for enhanced education and sensitization efforts. While most patients deferred treatment decisions to healthcare providers due to their expertise, this contrasts with other studies showing a preference for shared decision-making. Concurrent chemotherapy profoundly affects patients’ well-being, causing psychological distress, physical side effects, economic burdens, and social isolation. Patients adopt various coping strategies, including inner resolve, faith in God, and social support, which help them endure treatment. To improve patients’ overall experience, timely equipment maintenance, and expanding health insurance coverage for cervical cancer treatment are essential. Addressing these issues can lead to more holistic, patient-centred care and better support for families.
