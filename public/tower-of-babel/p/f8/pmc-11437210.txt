Lanthanum(III)-amino acid chelate mitigates copper(II) stress in rice (Oryza sativa)
Authors: Jiajia Chen, Yuqing Zhong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLanthanum (La(III)) is recognized for its ability to mitigate heavy metal stress in plants. However, the inorganic La(III) salts and lanthanum oxide nanoparticles (La2O3 NPs) extensively used in agriculture are prone to soil immobilization, thereby compromising their bioavailability and posing environmental risks. This study synthesized and characterized the lanthanum(III)-amino acid chelate (La(III)-AA) from soybean protein isolate (SPI) hydrolysates. Maximum chelating rate (94.95%) was achieved under the conditions of mole ratio 1:1.5, pH 8.0, 50 ℃ and 5 h. Glu, Asp and Pro represent the primary La(III)-binding ligands. UV-vis and FTIR demonstrated that amino nitrogen and carboxyl oxygen participate in metal-ligand recognition. Scanning and Transmission electron microscopy showed that La(III) chelates with amino acids in a core-shell structure of uniform size. Consequently, a proposed chemical structure for the La(III)-AA complex was presented. A concentration of 20 mg/L La(III)-AA outperforms inorganic La salts in growth promotion and Cu detoxification. La(III)-AA significantly reduces the content of Cu (II) in rice tissues and enhances seedling tolerance to Cu (II) stress. This study provides a novel La(III)-based candidate for crop protection and furthers our understanding of rare earth element-induced mitigation of heavy metal stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73771-4.Keywords: Lanthanum, Rare earth, Copper, Amino acid, Chelate, Rice (Oryza sativa)Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biocatalysis

Introduction

With the growing concern over the use of the Rare Earth Elements (REE) in agriculture, there is increasing evidence that REEs can protect plants against various abiotic stresses, such as salinity, cold and heavy metal stress1–3. Lanthanum (La(III)) is a REE known for its role in regulating plant growth and development. It has been observed to enhance photosynthetic capacity, increase the activity of antioxidant enzymes, and delay leaf senescence, thereby improving grain yield in crops4–6.

Current research predominantly focuses on the direct application of REEs or their inorganic salts. However, REEs are easily immobilized in soil due to the formation of insoluble substance with phytic acid or phosphate ion, therefore their effectiveness is reduced and environmental pollution may be caused.

Amino acid chelated metals are compounds in which metal ions are complexed with amino acids, forming stable and soluble complexes. The amino acid molecules protect the metal ions from being precipitated by other soil components, increasing the bioavailability of the metal ions, which can lead to less waste and a reduced environmental impact. Amino acid chelated metals have been extensively studied and reported in the literature7,8. Leibler et al.9 generated the salicylaldehyde copper amino acid (Sal Cu AA) complexes which were found to bind protein molecules as IMAC resins. In addition, it has been reported that glutamic acid as a primary ligand could form with copper(II)10, nickel(II)11, cobalt(II)12, and zinc(II)13 metals to make the new metal mixed-ligand complexes.

Soybean protein serves as a cost-effective resource for the large-scale production of mixed amino acids14. It can release abundant active peptides after enzymatic hydrolysis, and the amino acid and nutritional value are almost completely retained15,16. Many studies have been performed to generate health products e.g., calcium supplements with soybean protein hydrolysates17. However, the chelation of soy protein hydrolysate with La(III) has been seldom reported and its impact on plants is not well understood.

This study synthesized Lanthanum(III)-amino acid chelate (La(III)-AA) from soybean protein isolate (SPI) hydrolysates, representing a novel approach for developing a bioavailable form of La(III) for plant utilization. The chelate underwent detailed characterization to elucidate its core-shell structure and identify the primary ligands for La(III) binding. Additionally, this study evaluated the impact of La(III)-AA on rice growth, stress response and metal distribution under Cu(II) stress conditions. The study introduces a new strategy for crop protection against heavy metal stress using REE-based fertilizers. Furthermore, it contributes to the knowledge regarding REE-induced amelioration of heavy metal stress and offers insights into the potential agricultural applications of La(III)-AA chelates.

Materials and methods

Preparation of soybean protein isolate (SPI) hydrolysates

Soybean protein isolate (SPI) was dissolved in deionized water to a final substrate concentration of 10% (wt/vol). The solutions were hydrolyzed with serine protease under the following conditions: an enzyme dose of 10% (wt/wt), a pH of 8.0 and temperature of 60 °C. Hydrolysis was performed in a reactor fitted with a mechanical stirrer and temperature controller. The solution was reacted for 5 h and then immediately heated in a 90 °C water bath for 15 min to inactivate the proteases. After cooling to room temperature, the enzymatic hydrolysates were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to remove the fiber and other suspended solids.

Preparation of mixed amino acids

The solutions were decolorized using activated charcoal under the specified conditions: activated charcoal of 1 g/100 mL, at pH of 3.0 and temperature of 80 ℃. The solution was then filtered to eliminate insoluble and the volume was concentrated to 1/3 –1/2 volume by rotary evaporator. Double volume of absolute ethanol was added to the solution. The solution was stored at 0–4 °C overnight. The precipitated insoluble were removed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was purified using 732 cation-exchange resin, loaded onto a column at a flow rate of 2 BV/h, and eluted with 3% ammonia solution. The eluate was then concentrated and subject to crystallization, yielding amino acid crystals.

Preparation of La(III)-AA

Mixed amino acids were dissolved in deionized water at 25 °C to prepare 0.02 mmol/mL solution. La(NO3)3·6H2O was combined with amino acids at varying mole ratios (1:1.0, 1:1.5, 1:2.0, 1:2.5, 1:3.0), maintaining a pH of 6, a temperature of 90 ℃ and a reaction time of 2 h. To ascertain the individual effects of pH, temperature, and time, the pH was varied to 5.0, 6.0, 7.0, 8.0, and 9.0 while fixing the mole ratio at 1:2.0, the temperature at 90 ℃, and the time at 2 h. The temperature was adjusted to 50 °C, 60 °C, 70 °C, 80 °C, and 90 °C, with the mole ratio fixed at 1:2.0, the pH at 6, and the time at 2 h). The reaction time was varied to 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h, and 5 h, with the mole ratio fixed at 1:2.0, the pH at 6, and temperature at 90 ℃. The reaction was conducted in a thermostat water bath (HH.S21-4-S, CIMO, China). The resulting precipitate was removed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was concentrated until precipitation occurred. Quintuple volume of absolute ethanol was added to the supernatant to precipitate the unbound ions, resulting in the formation of La(III)-AA. The chelate was subsequently dried at 50 °C and collected for the determination of La(III) binding capacity and structural characterization. Analysis of amino acid composition

The sample (4 mL) was sealed in nitrogen-purged tubes, incubated at 110 °C for 22 h, and then diluted to a final volume of 10 mL. 1 mL solution was evaporated to dryness by nitrogen at 95 ℃, followed by dissolution in 1 mL of 10 mM HCl. Amino acid content was determined after derivatization with o-phthalaldehyde (OPA) and fluorene methoxycarbonyl chloride (FMOC) by HPLC, as per the protocol of Agilent Technologies Inc. The HPLC system utilized Agilent 1100 liquid chromatograph, equipped with a diode-array detector (DAD) and a ZORBAX Eclipse AAA column (4.6 × 75 mm, 3.5 μm). Elution was performed using a binary gradient at a flow of 1.0 mL/min. Solvents (A) and (B) were 40 mM sodium dihydrogen phosphate (pH 7.8) and a mixture of acetonitrile/methanol/water (45/45/10), respectively. The elution profile was as follows: 0.0–1.0 min with a solvent ratio of A/B (100/0); 1.0–27.0 min with a linear gradient to A/B (46/57); 27.0–40.0 min with a solvent ratio of A/B (0:100); and 40.0–41.0 min returning to A/B (100/0).

Ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy (UV-vis)

The chelate was prepared to a concentration of 1 mg/mL in deionized water. UV-vis absorption measurements were conducted using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (T1901, Shimadzu, Japan) at a controlled room temperature of 25 ± 1 °C. Prior to measurement, the baseline was calibrated using deionized water. The spectra were recorded over a wavelength range from 250 to 400 nm using 1 cm quartz cuvettes. The spectral peak signals were analyzed using Origin 9.0 software.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

An FTIR spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher IS5) was utilized to acquire the FTIR spectra of the mixed amino acid and La(III)-AA. The spectra were recorded over a wavelength range of 400–4000/cm at a resolution of 4/cm, with 32 scans. The measurements were conducted in a desiccated atmosphere at a controlled room temperature of 25 ± 1 °C. The results were expressed in transmittance units.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS)

The morphology of La(III)-AA was examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, SIGMA HD, Germany). Elemental analysis of La(III)-AA was conducted using energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) on the same SEM platform.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

TEM images were captured on a transmission electron microscope (JEM 2100, Japan) set at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. Samples were prepared by dispersing in alcohol.

Measurement of rice growth and the photosynthetic parameters

The rice cultivar used in this study, Oryza sativa L. subsp. Japonica cv. Suxiang 3, was sourced from Zhongnongke company, Jiangsu, China. The seeds were cultivated hydroponically for 14 days. A Cu(II) stress condition was simulated with 50 mg/L CuSO4, following the method of Thounaojam et al.19. The stressed groups were treated with 50 mg/L AA, 20 mg/L La(NO3)3-AA and 20 mg/L La(NO3)3 solution, respectively. The group without Cu(II) stress was used as control. Growth parameters, including shoot length, root length, and chlorophyll content, were measured from 1 to 7 days post-treatment. Chlorophyll content was assessed using a SPAD-502 chlorophyll meter. The photosynthetic parameters, Pn, Tr, Ci, and Gs, were quantified using a portable photosynthesis system (Li6400, USA).

Determination of MDA and antioxidant activities

MDA content, POD activity and CAT activity were quantified using MDA kit A003-1, POD kit A084-3 and CAT kit A007-1, respectively. SOD activity was assessed using the NBT assay.

Measurement of Cu content

The content of Cu in both underground and aerial parts of plants was measured using an FAAS-M6 atomic absorption flame spectrophotometer.

Statistical analysis

The experiments were conducted in triplicate, and data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data were analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple range test to assess significant differences among means.

Results

Effect of temperature, time, mole ratio and pH on La(III) binding capacity

SPI was hydrolyzed by serine protease, yielding a mixture of amino acids. These amino acids have the potential to complex with La(III), forming chelates20. However, numerous factors can restrict the efficiency of chelation. Further optimization of conditions is essential to maximize the chelating rate. The La(III) binding capacity was selected as a key parameter for monitoring the chelating reaction. The impacts of temperature, time, molar ratio, and pH on the La(III) binding capacity of amino acid mixture are depicted in Fig. 1. A typical inverted U-shaped curve, resulting from the experimental conditions, indicated the optimal temperature at 60 °C for La(III) binding (Fig. 1A). Given that chelation is an exothermic process, excessively high temperature can hinder the reaction per se21. Consequently, temperature ranging from 60 to 90 °C were detrimental to chelation, leading to a reduction in La(III) binding capacity.

Effects of different factors on the La(III) binding activity. (A) temperature. (B) time. (C) mole ratio. (D) pH. Results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent measurements. Different letters denote statistically significant differences among the groups (P < 0.05).

The molar ratio was a critical parameter influencing the La(III) binding capacity during chelation. An insufficient amino acid content failed to fully complex with La(III), while an excess led to a surplus, resulting in unnecessary consumption. At a molar ratio of 1:1.5, the La(III) binding capacity achieved its peak at 63.45% (Fig. 1B). As the molar ratio increased further, the binding capacity for La(III) decreased.

The La(III) binding capacity of the mixed amino acids increased progressively with time and pH, achieving maximum values of 88.78% and 93.33%, respectively (Fig. 1C, D). At a low pH (5–6), the solution was acidic, featuring a high concentration of H(I) ions. As H(I) and La(III) both compete for binding to electron-donating groups, this competition is detrimental to chelation, leading to a reduced chelation rate22. After taking into account economic factors, the optimal conditions were selected to be a reaction time of 5 h and a pH of 8.

Utilizing the La(III) binding capacity data, an orthogonal design23 was employed to determine the optimal condition. The results are detailed in Supplementary File 2, with the optimum condition summarized as follows: La(NO3)3·6H2O was combined with amino acid at a molar ratio of 1:1.5, with a pH of 8.0, a temperature of 50 ℃ and a reaction time of 5 h.

UV-visible spectra

The formation of chelates can result in the emergence of new absorbance peaks or the shifting and disappearance of existing ones24. Figure 2A displays the UV-vis absorption spectra for both the mixed amino acids and the La(III)-amino acid chelates (La(III)-AA). Absorption occurred in the near-ultraviolet region rather than the visible spectrum. A pronounced absorption peak at 268 nm in the mixed amino acids is attributed to the n → π* transition of C=O groups in the peptide bonds25. Relative to amino acids, the La(III)-AA exhibited an absorption wavelength of 273 nm, with a reduced peak intensity. The observed reduction in absorption and a hypochromatic shift of the absorption maximum suggest an interaction between amino acids and La(III), indicating that the chelate formation alters the ligand’s optical absorption characteristics.

Energy dispersive spectroscopy and scanning electron microscope

EDS was utilized for the elemental analysis and chemical characterization of La(III)-AA. The findings confirm the presence of carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, sulfur, and lanthanum in La(III)-AA (Fig. 2B), indicating effective chelation between La(III) ions and amino acids. After chelation, alterations in the electronic configuration and chemical bonding lead to structural modifications. Figure 3A depicts round-shaped La(III) aggregates with diameters ranging from 80 to 100 nm. SEM revealed uniform dispersion and size consistency of the particles within the amino acid matrix (Fig. 3B). Additionally, minimal voids due to particle shedding were observed within the material’s cross-section. This suggests a robust interfacial interaction between La(III) ions and amino acids.

SEM images at 2 μm (A) and 10 μm (B), and TEM images at 50 nm (C) and 100 nm (D) depicting the morphology of La(III)-AA.

Transmission electron microscope

Particle size is a pivotal attribute that dictates the properties of particles26. To examine the morphologies of La(III)-AA, TEM analysis was conducted. As shown in Fig. 3C, D, the particles exhibited uniformity and integrity, yet partial agglomeration was observed, potentially due to insufficient dispersion during sample preparation. The density and color contrast revealed a distinct core-shell morphology, with the darker regions corresponding to La(III) aggregates27. The aggregates were enveloped by a semi-transparent amino acid layer, appearing as approximately spherical structures with a mean diameter ranging from 60 to 80 nm. This proved observation confirms the successful binding of amino acids to the surface of La(III).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

Figure 4 displays the FTIR spectra of the mixed amino acid and La(III)-AA, highlighting the differences in their functional group vibrations. In the spectra of mixed amino acid, a prominent peak at 3019/cm was observed, which is attributed to the N-H stretching vibration and shifted to 2987/cm in La(III)-AA. The characteristic C=O absorption peak at 1691/cm in the mixed amino acid shifted to 1625 cm− 1 upon chelation with La(III). The COO– absorption peaks at 1643/cm, 1421/cm, and 1358/cm in the mixed amino acid shifted to 1594/cm, 1410/cm, and 1349/cm, respectively, after chelation with La(III). In the low-wavenumber region of spectrum, several distinct features were observed, corresponding to vibrations of C-O and C-N bonds. The IR analysis demonstrated that N and O atoms formed coordination bonds with La(III), contributing to the chelation process.

Estimated chemical structure of La(III)-AA

The FTIR and amino acid analysis indicated that N atom of the amino group and O atom of the carboxyl group are capable of coordinating with La(III). In addition, comparing the structure of 12 dominating amino acids (account for more than 5%), a common structural element, 2-α-aminoacetate group was identified among these 12 amino acids. Based on the research by Mautner et al.28, it is suggested that chelated La(III) could possess an eight- coordinate geometry. Based on these, there might be a general structure for the chelation of amino acids with La(III). The hypothesized configuration of La(III)-AA was illustrated using Chem draw 18.1 (Fig. 5). In Fig. 5A, the amino acids combined with La(III) to form a stable pentacoordinate ring structure, which aligns with the typical spatial structure of amino acids. N atom of amino group and O atom of carboxyl group formed coordination bonds with La(III), where R represents the amino acid residue. Given the high content of glutamate, two potential coordination configurations were proposed. During the coordination process of with La(III), four glutamate molecules were primarily involved in chelation. In Fig. 5B, the carboxyl group at the termini of the glutamate chain, separated by several C atoms, the O atom of the carboxyl group was more likely to form bonds with La(III). Furthermore, in Fig. 5C, N and O atoms combine with La(III).

Hypothesized chemical structure of La(III)-AA. (A) General structure of La(III)-AA; (B) The first speculative structure of La(III)-Glu; (C) The second speculative structure of La(III)-Glu.

In summary, the UV-vis and FTIR spectra data revealed that upon complexation with La(III), the amino acid exhibited a distinct shift in the characteristic absorption peak, suggesting a chemical interaction between amino acid and La(III). The wavenumbers corresponding to C =O and COO– were altered, indicating the participation of amino nitrogen and carboxyl oxygen atoms in the coordination process. Additionally, the SEM and TEM analyses demonstrated uniform dispersion of La(III) aggregates with in the amino acid matrix, providing further evidence for chelate formation. Ultimately, the proposed configuration for La(III)-AA and the two La(III)-Glu structures were inferred from the amino acid composition and FTIR spectral analysis.

Effect of La(III)-AA on rice growth

We initial assessment focused on the impact of La(NO3)3 and La(III)-AA chelates on rice growth under Cu(II) stress conditions (Fig. 6). Rice subjected to Cu(II) exhibited symptoms of toxicity, including stunted growth and chlorosis in the leaves. Compared to the control group, all plant growth indices were significantly impeded by the application of 50 mg/L Cu(II) stress. By the 7th day, leaf length, root length and chlorophyll content were inhibited by 39.95%, 46.77% and 47.83%, respectively. Numerous studies conducted over the past two decades demonstrated a significant and substantial occurrence of La-induced hormesis in plants, stimulating the growth, and enhancing tolerance to heavy metal stress29–31. The leaf length of rice treated with 20 mg/L La(III)-AA was significantly greater compared to other treatments (Fig. 6B). The application of La(NO3)3 even exacerbated the toxic effects of Cu(II). As s illustrated in Fig. 6C, the application of 20 mg/L La(III)-AA significantly increased root length by 32.55% compared to Cu(II) stress alone, while La(NO3)3 increased it by 17.66%. Figure 6D demonstrates that La(III)-AA outperformed La(NO3)3 in maintaining chlorophyll content, suggesting that La(III)-AA may mitigate the severe oxidative damage induced by Cu(II) in leaf cells.

Effects of La(NO3)3 and La(III)-AA on growth indices of Cu(II)-stressed rice seedlings. (A) Cu(II)-stressed rice seedlings with La(NO3)3 or La(III)-AA treatment; (B) leaf length; (C) root length; (D) chlorophyll content.

Effect of La (III)-AA on photosynthetic parameters

The impact of La(III)-AA on gas exchange parameters of Cu stressed rice seedlings is illustrated in Fig. 7A–D. A significant increase in Ci under Cu stress suggests a reduction in the activity of dark reaction enzymes, a decrease in the rate of CO2 utilization, and a weakening of the leave carbon fixation capacity. Pn, Gs, and Tr all exhibited a declining trend, reflecting a reduced utilization rate of light energy. The reduction in Pn corresponded with an increase in Ci, suggesting that non-stomatal limitations due to diminished photosynthetic activity in mesophyll cells were the primary constraints on photosynthesis. Pn, Gs and Tr increased significantly after La (III)-AA treatment by 26.57%, 40.85%, and 33.58%, respectively, as compared to the Cu treatment group. These findings indicate that La (III)-AA can counteract the reduction in photochemical efficiency induced by Cu stress, facilitate the resumption of electron transfer, and consequently enhance ATP production in leaves, contributing to the recovery of Pn. The observed increase in Tr suggests that La(III)-AA can diminishes the inhibitory effect of Cu stress on transpiration, enabling leaves to acquire more nutrients from the roots for metabolic processes, aligning with the observed enhancement in biomass under La(III)-AA treatment.

Effects of La (III)-AA on photosynthetic and biochemical parameters of rice seedlings. (A) Ci; (B) Gs; (C) Pn; (D) Tr; (E) Membrane permeability; (F) MDA; (G) SOD; (H) POD; (I) CAT. Different letters represent significant differences between the data (P < 0.05).

Effect of La (III)-AA on membrane permeability, MDA and antioxidant activity

The degree of cell damage is reflected by alterations in the plasma membrane permeability. Figure 7E illustrates the impact of various treatments on plasma membrane permeability in rice seedlings. In comparison with Cu stress group, the incorporation of AA, La(NO3)3 and La(III)-AA reduced the plasma membrane permeability in leaves. Conversely, in roots, La(III)-AA had no significant effect on plasma membrane permeability, whereas La(NO3)3 increased it, suggesting more severe oxidative damage to root cells under Cu (II) stress that was not reversible.

MDA, a product of lipid peroxidation, serves as an indicator of the extent of cellular damage in plants under stress, in conjunction with cytoplasmic membrane permeability. Figure 7F shows that the MDA content in rice leaves exposed to Cu (II) stress were markedly elevated compared to the control group. Treatments with AA, La(NO3)3 and La (III)-AA treatments all led to a reduction in leaf MDA content, with the La (III)-AA treatment demonstrating the most pronounced decrease.

Plants maintain redox balance by modulating the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, POD, and CAT, which protect against oxidative damage. Figure 7G-I indicates that the activities of SOD, POD, and CAT exhibited a similar response pattern under Cu (II) stress. The activities of SOD, POD and CAT increased in response to Cu (II) stress due to ROS accumulation, and subsequently decreased after treatment with AA, La (III), and La (III)-AA. The La (III)-AA treatment group exhibited the most significant reduction, with leaf SOD, POD, and CAT activity decreasing by 59.07%, 36.17%, and 85.71%, respectively. Correspondingly, root SOD, POD, and CAT activities decreased by 69.99%, 36.49% and 73.19%. These results suggested that La (III)-AA can mitigate the stress-induced damage in rice under Cu (II) stress conditions.

Effect of La (III)-AA on metal concentration distribution

To investigate the impact of La (III)-AA on Cu(II) transport, the distribution of Cu (II) in both underground and aerial tissues of rice seedlings was assessed. The Cu (II) content in rice roots, stems, and leaves is depicted in Fig. 8A. Across the treatments, Cu (II) predominantly accumulated in the underground parts of the rice seedlings. Figure 8B indicates that the addition of exogenous La (III)-AA effectively reduced Cu (II) content in rice tissues. The reduction in Cu(II) content in the aerial parts (59.46% in stems, 55.56% in leaves), was more substantial than the 26.29% decrease observed in the underground part. It is hypothesized that La (III) may act as a Cu (II) antagonist, competing for adsorption sites on rice cell surfaces, thereby disrupting Cu (II) transport and absorption mechanisms, resulting in decreased Cu (II) content in rice tissues.

Discussion

La(III) has garnered interest due to its potential applications in enhancing plant resistance against various abiotic stresses, including heavy metal stress. For instance, La(III) has been demonstrated to improve growth and mitigate the adverse effects of salt stress on soybeans32. La(III) supplementation has alleviated the suppression of root growth in tomatoes by promoting auxin-mediated cell elongation under low light stress in concentration-dependent manner33. At proper concentrations, La(III) has been confirmed to improve the cadmium tolerance of maize by upregulating the activities of various antioxidant enzymes, including ascorbate peroxidase, dehydroascorbate reductase, glutathione reductase, L-galactono-1,4-lactone dehydrogenase, and γ-glutamylcysteine synthetase34. These studies offer insights into the potential use of La(III) in plant protection strategies to ameliorate the detrimental effects of abiotic stresses. However, soluble REE compounds are susceptible to reactions with soil chemical components, resulting in sedimentation and the formation of insoluble substances, thereby impacting their mobility and bioavailability. Improper management could lead to the desorption of REEs from soil particles, potentially causing leaching and raising environmental concerns.

With the rapid advancement in nanotechnology, Rare Earth Oxide Nano-Particles (REONPs), including cerium oxide nanoparticles (CeO2 NPs) and lanthanum oxide nanoparticles (La2O3 NPs), are commonly used as additives in fertilizers. Their high surface area to volume ratio and porosity of REONPs contribute to their increased reactivity and potential for enhanced interaction with plant tissues. However, the bioavailability and mobility of REONPs in soil are pH-dependent and influenced by interactions with anionic ligands attributed to their high oxidation state.

Inorganic salts of REEs and their oxide nanoparticles share similar concerns regarding phytotoxicity. The impact of REE on plants is concentration-dependent. Optimal concentrations can promote plant growth, while excessive concentrations may impede development or potentially lead to plant mortality35. Notably, soluble REE compounds, including chlorides, nitrates, and sulfates, are considered the primary sources of phytotoxicity. Other evidence suggests that REONPs, such as CeO2, La2O3, Gd2O3, Yb2O3 nanoparticles, can induce oxidative stress, alter nutrient uptake, and compromise the structural integrity of various plant species at elevated concentrations36,37. Therefore, ongoing research is dedicated to optimizing the agricultural application of REEs for sustainable and efficacious plant protection strategies.

Metal chelates have attracted considerable interest in agricultural applications owing to their unique properties compared to non-chelated metal species. Metal chelates are compounds formed by the coordination of a metal ion with a chelating agent, or ligand. These ligands are typically organic molecules featuring multiple attachment points, enabling the formation of multiple bonds with the metal ion. The chelation process yields a stable, cyclic structure, thereby enhancing the metal’s bioavailability through increased absorption. Chelates shield metal ions from interactions with other soil components, including phosphates or organic matter such as phytic acid. Metal chelates exhibit greater stability across a range of soil conditions, including varying pH levels. Using chelated metal forms diminish the quantity of metal required, thereby mitigating the phytotoxity risk and alleviating the agricultural environmental footprint.

This study synthesized La(III)-AA from SPI hydrolysates and determined optimal chelation conditions including mole ratio, pH, temperature, and time, that yielded a maximum chelating rate of 94.95%. UV-vis, FTIR, SEM-EDS, and TEM were utilized to characterize the core-shell structure of the chelate, as well as the participation of amino nitrogen and carboxyl oxygen in metal-ligand recognition. Glutamic acid (Glu), aspartic acid (Asp), and proline (Pro) were identified as the primary ligands facilitating La(III) binding.

We subsequently evaluated the effects of La(III)-AA on rice growth under Cu(II) stress conditions. La(III)-AA supplementation alleviated the inhibition of leaf and root growth, suggesting its potential to enhance nutrient absorption and root development.

Additionally, the influence of La(III)-AA on photochemical reactions, including net photosynthetic rate, stomatal conductance, and transpiration rate, was assessed in rice seedlings. The findings suggest that La(III)-AA may enhance light absorption efficiency and carbon fixation, both essential for plant growth.

Analysis of antioxidant enzyme activity indicates that La(III)-AA potentially augments photosynthetic efficiency through increased activity of enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT). Concurrently with this is the reduced level of plasma membrane permeability and the MDA content, indicating that La (III)-AA can significantly increase the stability of membrane structure and mitigates the membrane lipid peroxidation induced by Cu stress.

The analysis of metal content distribution revealed that La(III)-AA significantly lowered Cu content in both the aerial and underground parts of rice seedlings, suggesting an antagonistic effect on Cu absorption.

By mitigating oxidative damage and Cu translocation, La(III)-AA preserves the structural integrity and functionality of the photosynthetic apparatus, safeguarding key photosynthetic enzymes, such as Rubisco and its activase complex facilitates an increased carbon fixation process. The elevated availability of the Rubisco enzyme complex, the carboxylation rate can be enhanced, leading to a more efficient conversion of carbon dioxide to organic compounds during the light-dependent phase of photosynthesis, aligning with the observed enhancement in biomass following La(III)-AA treatment.

Although La(III)-AA presents potential benefits, the risks associated with phytotoxicity and environmental accumulation necessitate careful management. Future research should focus on a broader range of concentrations to delineate the dose-response dynamics of La(III)-AA on plant growth and stress tolerance, with the aim of establishing safe application protocols to ameliorate potential adverse environmental impacts. An exploration of the molecular mechanisms underpinning the protective effects of La(III)-AA on rice at the genetic level is warranted, potentially yielding insights into its mode of action.

Conclusions

This study successfully synthesized La(III)-AA from SPI hydrolysates, presenting a novel bioavailable form of La(III) for plant utilization. The primary ligands for La(III)-binding were identified, and a speculative chemical structure of La(III)-AA was proposed. Compared to La(NO3)3, La(III)-AA demonstrated superior efficacy in ameliorating Cu stress in rice. The application of La(III)-AA decreases Cu (II) content in rice tissues and confers significant resistance to Cu(II) stress, including alleviated growth inhibition, decreased membrane permeability and increased antioxidant enzyme activity. Therefore, this study introduces a novel candidate of REE-based fertilizer for crop protection. The findings broaden the potential application of La(III)-AA chelates in crop protection and contribute to the understanding of REE-induced mitigation of heavy metal stress.
