Aptamer-guided upconversion nanoconstructs enable proximity-dependent and precise photodynamic therapy for MRSA-infected wounds
Authors: Pinghuang Tang, Zi Wang, Qian Liu, Bin Wang, Lu Wang, Hua Zuo, Yao Liu, Liang Fang, Zhi Xu, Zailin Yang, Yan Zhong, Hang Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) infections lead to slow wound healing, but treating these wounds with conventional photodynamic therapy (PDT) remains challenging because they induce non-specific oxidative damage on healthy tissue, thereby hindering wound repair. To achieve efficient antibacterial activity while avoiding non-specific tissue injury, we constructed a DNA aptamer functionalized upconversion nanoplatform (UC@PEI-RB@Apt) for spatially confined, proximity-dependent antibacterial of MRSA. Here, upconversion nanoparticles (UCs) convert near-infrared (NIR) light into visible light, activating polyethyleneimine-modified Rose Bengal (RB) to generate ROS. Crucially, the modified aptamers act as a specific molecular anchor, enabling the nanoplatform to directly adsorb onto the bacterial surface. This generates a proximity-dependent killing effect, wherein lethal ROS are generated around the bacteria to eliminate them, while sparing surrounding healthy cells due to the short lifespan of ROS. In vitro, UC@PEI-RB@Apt exhibited excellent targeting and biofilm disruption ability, with 99.9% bactericidal efficiency. In vivo, the MRSA infected wound model confirmed that this localized treatment significantly reduced bacterial load and accelerated wound closure compared to non-targeted controls. Additionally, histological analysis confirmed excellent biosafety with negligible damage to normal skin or major organs. This study proposes a precise, aptamer-guided targeted strategy that effectively balances high bactericidal activity with tissue safety for managing drug-resistant infections.

Introduction

Wound infections caused by multidrug-resistant pathogens, particularly Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), represent a major challenge in modern medicine [1]. The escalation of antibiotic resistance not only severely limits the antibacterial efficacy but also directly delays the healing of infected wounds [[2], [3], [4]]. Meanwhile, the development of new antibiotics faces challenges such as long development cycles, high costs, and low success rates [5]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop alternative therapeutic strategies that combine high antibacterial efficacy with minimal risk of resistance. Over the past few decades, nanotechnology has made significant progress in the treatment of drug-resistant bacterial infections [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. In this context, interventions based on nanotechnology that generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), including photodynamic therapy (PDT), chemodynamic therapy (CDT), and sonodynamic therapy (SDT), have emerged as promising approaches [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. These approaches induce multi-targeted, irreversible oxidative damage to bacterial membranes, proteins, and nucleic acids, thereby achieving broad-spectrum bactericidal effects without promoting resistance [14]. However, the lack of selectivity in these nanosystems limits their clinical applications. The non-specific generation of ROS inevitably leads to accumulation in healthy tissues, causing oxidative stress, cellular damage, and inflammatory responses [15,16]. Therefore, constructing a platform capable of precisely delivering ROS generating systems to infection sites is crucial for resolving the contradiction between high antibacterial efficacy and biosafety.

Targeted drug delivery has emerged as a fundamental strategy to eradicate drug-resistant bacteria while minimizing systemic toxicity [17]. Current targeting approaches generally fall into three categories: active targeting based on molecular recognition (e.g., antibodies, bacteriophages, and nucleic acid aptamers), passive targeting driven by physicochemical properties, and bio-inspired biomimetic strategies [18]. For instance, Zhang et al. engineered an antibody polysaccharide conjugate that specifically binds to bacteria, forming an in situ “calcified crust” on the surface to achieve bactericidal effects through physical entrapment [19]. Similarly, You et al. designed TiOx@C nanofibers that exploit structural compatibility with the bacterial outer membrane to achieve physical screening-based targeting [20]. However, despite these innovations, conventional targeting ligands face practical hurdles. Antibodies are limited by high production costs, large molecular dimensions, and restricted tissue penetration, while many biomimetic strategies struggle with fabrication complexity and batch-to-batch variability, hindering their clinical translation [21,22]. In this landscape, aptamers have emerged as a superior alternative. Selected via SELEX technology, aptamers offer distinct advantages, including low molecular weight, high structural stability, low immunogenicity, and ease of chemical modification [23,24]. Crucially, compared to non-specific systems, aptamer functionalized platforms facilitate the precise accumulation of nanocarriers at the infection site via high affinity binding to surface antigens. This proximity is particularly vital for ROS-based therapies, as ROS have extremely short half-lives and limited diffusion radii. thus, surface specific binding significantly enhances bactericidal efficiency while minimizing off-target damage to healthy tissues [25].

Selecting the optimal therapeutic approach while ensuring effective targeting is crucial for successfully controlling infections. Among various ROS-based strategies, PDT offers significant advantages due to its noninvasiveness and precise spatiotemporal controllability [[26], [27], [28]]. For instance, Dai et al. produced a Nile red derivative that showed strong in vitro bactericidal activity against drug-resistant strains when exposed to visible light [29]. Nevertheless, the shallow penetration of visible light hampers the clinical use of conventional PDT, limiting its effectiveness against bacteria located in deeper skin layers [30,31]. 980 nm near-infrared (NIR) light exhibits significant tissue penetration, with approximately 1.22% of the incident energy reaching a depth of 3 cm at 10-15 W power levels [32]. Upconversion nanoparticles (UCs) can convert deep penetrating NIR light into high-energy visible light, which can overcome the limitation of light penetration [[33], [34], [35], [36]]. This mechanism facilitates the activation of photosensitizers through skin tissue. Su et al. combined UCs with a visible-light activated photosensitizer (MC540) to achieve efficient suppression of drug-resistant bacteria [37]. Despite these individual advances, systems that successfully combine NIR-triggered PDT with aptamer-driven bacterial recognition remain scarce. Developing such a platform requires rational structural engineering to ensure high energy transfer efficiency and stable target specificity in physiological environments. Therefore, creating a multifunctional nanoplatform combining these capabilities is essential for the effective management of resistant wound infections.

Herein, we constructed a bacteria-targeted nanoplatform (UC@PEI-RB@Apt) that utilizes aptamer-mediated localized PDT for the efficient eradication of MRSA (Scheme 1). In this nanoplatform, core-shell UCs are employed as photoconverter to convert NIR light into visible light, which are loaded with a polyethyleneimine (PEI)-modified photosensitizer (Rose Bengal, RB) and functionalized with MRSA-specific aptamers [14] via electrostatic interactions and base complementary pairing. Crucially, serving as targeting units, the incorporated aptamers allow the nanoplatform to specifically recognize and anchor to the MRSA surface. This specific binding minimizes the distance between the ROS source and the bacterial wall. As a result, ROS is produced in the immediate vicinity of the pathogen for maximum lethality, while sparing surrounding healthy skin cells. By combining this precise targeting with the superior tissue penetration of NIR light, the system overcomes the inherent limitations of traditional PDT. Remarkably, in vitro experiments showed that the platform suppressed MRSA growth by more than 99.9%. Additionally, using a murine model for MRSA-infected wounds, we observed that the treatment significantly accelerated healing without systemic toxicity, as confirmed by lower bacterial loads, improved wound closure, and reduced inflammation. Results and discussion

Construction and characterization of the UC@PEI-RB@Apt

The design logic and synthesis pathway of nanoplatform are illustrated in Fig. 1a. We employed polyethyleneimine-modified RB (PEI-RB) as the photosensitizer. The PEI modification allows RB to be loaded onto nanoparticles by ligand exchange. We synthesized monodisperse core-shell UCs (NaYF4: Yb/Er@NaYF4) via a high-temperature thermal decomposition method. To suppress surface quenching and boost luminescence, an inert NaYF4 shell was grown epitaxially over the core. Once oleic acid ligands were removed, we loaded PEI-RB and MRSA-specific aptamers via electrostatic interactions and base complementary pairing. The combination of linker and aptamer was validated through PAGE analysis (Fig. S1). As shown in transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images, the core and core-shell UCs appeared uniform and well-dispersed (Fig. 1b). Their average diameters increased from 26.9 nm to 30.4 nm (Fig. 1c), indicating successful shell growth. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) revealed clear lattice spacing of 0.52 nm, which aligns with the (100) crystal plane (Fig. 1d). The lattice spacing together with X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern (Fig. 1e) confirmed that the synthesized UCs possess a pure hexagonal phase, the most efficient phase for upconversion luminescence. Additionally, high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) and elemental mapping confirmed the coexistence of Y, Er, and Yb, while the detection of P signals originating from the DNA phosphate backbone offered direct proof for the successful surface functionalization of the aptamer (Fig. 1f).Fig. 1Synthesis of UC@PEI-RB@Apt. Next, we examined the upconversion luminescence (UCL) spectrum of the UCs. As shown in Fig. 2a, the UCs exhibited two characteristic emission peaks at 525 nm and 541 nm under 980 nm NIR light irradiation, corresponding to the 2H11/2 → 4I15/2 and 4S3/2 → 4I15/2 transitions of Er3+, respectively. These emission bands match the absorption profile of RB. The spectral overlap between the green UCL of UCs and the absorption spectrum of PEI-RB (Fig. 2b) confirms its potential for NIR-induced PDT. Once PEI- RB was loaded, energy transfer caused a marked quenching of the emission intensities at 525 nm and 540 nm, whereas the peak at 650 nm remained stable (Fig. 2c). The stepwise assembly of the UC@PEI-RB@Apt was subsequently monitored by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential measurements (Fig. 2d and e). The hydrodynamic diameters increased from 107 nm (UC) to 174 nm (UC@PEI-RB) and finally to 283 nm (UC@PEI-RB@Apt). The substantial increase in hydrodynamic diameter directly demonstrates the successful layer-by-layer assembly of the hydrated PEI polymer and the extended DNA aptamer. TEM imaging confirms the monodisperse nature of the inorganic core, but cannot visualize the organic corona, explaining the apparent discrepancy between the dry-state and solution-state size measurements. Correspondingly, the zeta potential changed from +16.5 mV to +34.7 mV and finally to −13.3 mV after aptamer conjugation. This distinct charge reversal provides strong evidence of successful surface modification. Based on standard curve calculations, we estimate that approximately 62 aptamers are conjugated to each UC (Fig. S2). Furthermore, we evaluated singlet oxygen (1O2) production using 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF). Fig. 2f shows that when DPBF is mixed with UC@PEI-RB@Apt, the absorption intensity of DPBF continues to decrease under NIR irradiation. In contrast, no significant change was observed in the absence of irradiation, confirming that NIR light effectively triggers ROS generation from the PEI-RB cargo. Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy was further employed to directly identify the ROS generated by UC@PEI-RB@Apt under 980 nm irradiation. Using specific spin-trapping agents, characteristic signals corresponding to superoxide anion (O2•-), hydroxyl radical (•OH), and 1O2 were clearly detected (Fig. 2g–i), confirming the excellent multi-type ROS-generating capability of the nanoplatform. To verify the ROS generation at the bacterial level, we performed ROS staining using a specific fluorescent probe. As shown in Fig. S3, MRSA treated with UC@PEI-RB@Apt under NIR irradiation exhibited strong green fluorescence, indicating robust ROS production within the bacteria. In contrast, minimal fluorescence was observed in control groups. These results confirm that UC@PEI-RB@Apt effectively generates ROS at the bacterial level, supporting its photodynamic mechanism.

Evaluation of the bacterial targeting ability of UC@PEI-RB@Apt

Firstly, we evaluated the stability of Apt, UC@PEI-RB@Apt was incubated in DMEM containing 10% FBS, with free Apt as control. he fluorescence of Cy5-labeled Apt is quenched by BHQ2, and upon Apt degradation, the fluorescence intensity is expected to recover. Free Apt exhibited a marked increase in Cy5 intensity over time, whereas UC@PEI-RB@Apt showed less fluorescence changes (Fig. S4), confirming that nanoplatform conjugation effectively protects the aptamer from nuclease degradation. To achieve targeted enrichment of the nanoparticles on bacterial surfaces, we introduced an aptamer that specifically recognizes MRSA surface proteins, aiming to enhance the local concentration for ROS (Fig. 3a). To validate this targeting capability, TEM was first employed to visualize the physical interaction between the nanomaterials and bacteria, with PBS and UC@PEI-RB@Linker groups serving as controls. In the UC@PEI-RB@Linker group, only a sparse distribution of nanoparticles was observed on the bacterial surface (Fig. 3b), likely due to the lack of specific binding sites and weak non-specific interactions. In contrast, the UC@PEI-RB@Apt group exhibited a dense accumulation of nanomaterials tightly adhered to the bacterial cell wall, providing direct morphological evidence of the aptamer-mediated targeting effect. Moreover, we performed a competitive binding assay by co-incubating UC@PEI-RB@Apt with a mixed bacterial suspension containing both MRSA and Escherichia coli (E. coli). As shown in Fig. S5, the nanoplatform was predominantly enriched on the surface of MRSA, with minimal binding observed on E. coli. These results confirm that the aptamer-mediated targeting is specific to MRSA even in the presence of competing non-target bacteria. To further corroborate these findings, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was used to analyze the colocalization of bacteria and nanomaterials via fluorescence imaging (Fig. 3c). MRSA treated with UC@PEI-RB@Apt exhibited intense red fluorescence on the surface, which overlapped perfectly with the green bacterial autofluorescence. This indicates the precise homing of the UC@PEI-RB@Apt nanoparticles to the bacteria via molecular recognition. Conversely, the UC@PEI-RB@Linker group displayed only faint red fluorescence with minimal overlap, suggesting that non-targeted nanoparticles are easily washed away. In vitro antibacterial activity of UC@PEI-RB@Apt

Building on the excellent ROS generation and bacterial targeting capability of UC@PEI-RB@Apt, we systematically evaluated its in vitro antibacterial and anti-biofilm activity against MRSA. Strains specifically recognized by the aptamer were selected, and antibacterial efficacy was quantitatively assessed using the colony-forming unit (CFU) counting method. Briefly, bacteria were incubated with PBS, UC@PEI-RB@Linker, or UC@PEI-RB@Apt for 2 h, followed by exposure to 980 nm NIR light for 20 min (1 W cm−2, 5 min break after 1 min irradiation) or equivalent incubation in the dark. As shown in Fig. 4a and b, under dark conditions, both UC@PEI-RB@Linker and UC@PEI-RB@Apt groups showed negligible antibacterial activity. In contrast, significant bactericidal effects were triggered upon NIR irradiation. Notably, the UC@PEI-RB@Apt + L group exhibited markedly stronger antibacterial activity than UC@PEI-RB@Linker + L group, achieving a bacterial killing rate exceeding 99.9%. This enhanced efficacy is attributed to the aptamer-mediated active targeting, which facilitates the accumulation of photosensitizers on the bacterial surface, thereby overcoming the short diffusion distance of ROS to ensure effective bacterial damage. To investigate the enhanced antibacterial activity mediated by adapter targeting, we evaluated the toxicity of the nanoplatform to non-targeted bacteria Staphylococcus epidermidis, a representative beneficial skin commensal. As shown in Fig. S6, UC@PEI-RB@Linker and UC@PEI-RB@Apt exhibited comparable antibacterial effects against Staphylococcus epidermidis, with no enhanced killing observed for the aptamer-functionalized platform. These results confirm that the aptamer confers specific recognition toward MRSA without promoting toxicity against beneficial skin flora. We further verify the role of ROS in bacterial killing, a rescue assay was performed using vitamin C as a ROS scavenger. The potent bactericidal activity of UC@PEI-RB@Apt + L was almost completely abolished upon vitamin C addition (Fig. S7), confirming that the antibacterial effect is primarily mediated by photodynamically generated ROS. Moreover, bacterial viability was visualized using SYTO 9/PI double staining. Under dark conditions, PBS, UC@PEI-RB@Linker, and UC@PEI-RB@Apt groups displayed strong green fluorescence (SYTO 9, live bacteria) (Fig. 4c and d), indicating intact cell membranes. After NIR irradiation, both UC@PEI-RB@Linker and UC@PEI-RB@Apt treatments induced prominent red fluorescence (PI, dead bacteria). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was subsequently employed to visually assess the structural damage inflicted on bacteria by photodynamic treatment. As shown in Fig. 4e, bacteria in the dark control group exhibited smooth and intact surfaces. In contrast, those treated with UC@PEI-RB@Linker or UC@PEI-RB@Apt under NIR irradiation displayed clear morphological damage, including surface wrinkling and collapse (indicated by red arrows). The structural disruption was visibly more severe in the UC@PEI-RB@Apt + L group, further confirming that the localized oxidative stress leads to the physical disintegration of the bacterial envelope. Since bacterial biofilm formation significantly increases antibiotic resistance, effective biofilm disruption is crucial for clinical infection control. We further evaluated the biofilm disruption ability of the UC@PEI-RB@Apt using crystal violet staining (Fig. 4f). Results indicated that dense biofilms formed in the PBS group regardless of light exposure. Similarly, treatment with UC@PEI-RB@Linker or UC@PEI-RB@Apt alone (without light) did not significantly affect preformed biofilms. However, both UC@PEI-RB@Linker + L and UC@PEI-RB@Apt + L groups exhibited notable biofilm disruption under NIR irradiation. Quantitative analysis revealed a biofilm eradication rate of approximately 60.7% for the UC@PEI-RB@Apt + L group, which was superior to that of the UC@PEI-RB@Linker + L group (Fig. S8). Moreover, we have performed a live/dead biofilm staining assay using confocal laser scanning microscopy. As shown in Fig. S9, UC@PEI-RB@Apt + L treatment resulted in markedly increased bacterial death within biofilms compared to control groups, confirming the enhanced bactericidal efficacy and biofilm disruption capability conferred by aptamer-mediated targeting. Collectively, the incorporation of the aptamer significantly improved MRSA recognition and enrichment by the UC@PEI-RB@Apt. This targeted approach increased the local ROS concentration directly at the infection site, resulting in the efficient eradication of both planktonic bacteria and established biofilms through irreversible oxidative damage. These findings provide a solid foundation for subsequent in vivo studies on antibacterial activity and wound healing promotion.

In vivo healing of MRSA-infected wounds

Given the superior in vitro antibacterial efficacy of UC@PEI-RB@Apt, we established a mouse dorsal skin abscess model specifically infected with MRSA (1 × 108 CFU mL−1, 50 μL) to investigate its therapeutic potential in vivo. Prior to animal experiments, the biocompatibility of the nanoparticles was verified using a CCK-8 assay, which demonstrated negligible cytotoxicity towards HaCaT cells (Fig. S10). The hemolysis testing revealed a UC@PEI-RB@Apt hemolysis rate of 2.4%, indicating excellent biocompatibility (Fig. S11). We further performed histological analysis of skin tissues surrounding the wound following subcutaneous injection of UC@PEI-RB@Apt (without NIR irradiation). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed no obvious tissue damage or inflammatory infiltration compared to the PBS control group (Fig. S12), confirming that the nanoplatform is well-tolerated at the administered dose. To avoid the thermal effects generated by 980 nm NIR light irradiation, we employed an interval irradiation strategy to investigated the temperature changes of UC@PEI-RB@Apt both in solution and on the skin surface of mice after subcutaneous injection. As shown in Fig. S13, the temperature of UC@PEI-RB@Apt solution increased only slightly during each irradiation cycle, peaking at approximately 32 °C before rapidly returning to room temperature during the resting period. In vivo, the instantaneous peak temperature on the mouse wound surface reached 43.6 °C after 1 min of irradiation but quickly returned to baseline during the subsequent 5 min break (Fig. S14). Infected mice were randomly allocated into six groups (n = 5): PBS, PBS + L, UC@PEI-RB@Linker, UC@PEI-RB@Linker + L, UC@PEI-RB@Apt, and UC@PEI-RB@Apt + L. The treatment groups received a local dose of the respective nanoparticles (20 nM, 50 μL), followed by 980 nm NIR irradiation using the same protocol as the in vitro studies. The wound healing process was monitored and recorded every three days until day 9 (Fig. 5a). Representative photographs (Fig. 5b) revealed that on day 9, pronounced open wounds persisted in the PBS groups (with or without light) and the non-irradiated nanoparticle groups. In contrast, the UC@PEI-RB@Linker + L group showed accelerated wound contraction, while the UC@PEI-RB@Apt + L group achieved near-complete closure, highlighting the superior pro-healing capability of the aptamer-targeted PDT. Quantitative analysis of the wound area (Fig. 5c and Fig. S15) corroborated these observations. The control and non-irradiated groups showed limited recovery, with area reductions ranging from 76.1% to 79.2%. The UC@PEI-RB@Linker + L group exhibited an 87.2% reduction, whereas the UC@PEI-RB@Apt + L group demonstrated the most rapid recovery with a 91.7% reduction. To quantify the bactericidal efficacy in vivo, wound tissues were homogenized on day 9 for CFU enumeration. As presented in Fig. 5d and e, both NIR-irradiated groups displayed significantly lower residual bacterial burdens compared to controls. Notably, the UC@PEI-RB@Apt + L group achieved the highest inhibition rate of 95.7% (vs. 82.9% for UC@PEI-RB@Linker + L), confirming that aptamer-guided PDT efficiently eradicates MRSA from the infected tissue, thereby establishing a sterile microenvironment conducive to wound repair.Fig. 5In vivo therapeutic evaluation of UC@PEI-RB@Apt in an MRSA-infected wound model. Histological evaluation was subsequently performed to assess the quality of skin regeneration. H&E staining (Fig. 5f) of the control and non-irradiated groups showed incomplete re-epithelialization, disrupted tissue architecture, and inflammatory cell infiltration, indicating impaired healing. The UC@PEI-RB@Linker + L group displayed partial structural restoration. Conversely, the UC@PEI-RB@Apt + L group exhibited the most advanced tissue regeneration, characterized by a complete and thickened epidermis, the regeneration of skin appendages (such as hair follicles and sebaceous glands), and well-organized granulation tissue without obvious necrosis. Furthermore, collagen deposition, a critical indicator of wound remodeling, was assessed via Masson's trichrome staining (Fig. 5g). The UC@PEI-RB@Apt + L group displayed the most intense blue staining, reflecting robust and organized collagen fiber synthesis. This dense collagen network is essential for restoring the mechanical strength and integrity of the skin. Taken together, these results demonstrated that under NIR irradiation, UC@PEI-RB@Apt significantly accelerates the healing of MRSA-infected wounds by synergistically suppressing bacterial infection, promoting re-epithelialization, and enhancing collagen deposition, underscoring its immense potential as a precision therapeutic platform for infected wound management.

In Vivo mechanisms of wound healing and biosafety evaluation

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the accelerated healing, we investigated the modulation of the immune microenvironment within the wound tissue. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis was performed to detect the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α and the M1 macrophage surface marker CD86. As presented in Fig. 6a and b, the expression levels of both markers were markedly suppressed in the UC@PEI-RB@Apt + L group compared to the extensive positive staining observed in all control groups. Since neovascularization is pivotal for oxygen and nutrient supply during tissue reconstruction, angiogenesis was evaluated via immunofluorescence staining of CD31 (an endothelial cell marker) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) (Fig. 6c and d and Fig. S16). The UC@PEI-RB@Apt + L group exhibited significantly upregulated expression of both CD31 and VEGF compared to other groups. Furthermore, the microvessel density in this group was notably superior to that of the UC@PEI-RB@Linker + L group, demonstrating that the treatment creates a favorable microenvironment that robustly promotes angiogenesis and granulation tissue formation. To quantitatively corroborate the local anti-inflammatory effect, the levels of key pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α) in wound tissues were measured by ELISA (Fig. 6e–g). While the PBS and non-irradiated groups maintained high cytokine concentrations indicative of chronic inflammation, the UC@PEI-RB@Apt + L group showed a drastic reduction in all three cytokines. These results confirm that aptamer-guided PDT effectively mitigates the excessive inflammatory response caused by MRSA infection, accelerating the transition from the inflammatory phase to the proliferation phase.

Finally, the in vivo biosafety of the UC@PEI-RB@Apt was systematically evaluated to assess its potential for clinical translation. Throughout the treatment period, all mice exhibited normal behavior and steady body weight gain (Fig. S17), suggesting no adverse effects on general health. Histological examination (H&E staining) of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, revealed no discernible pathological abnormalities or inflammatory lesions in the UC@PEI-RB@Linker + L and UC@PEI-RB@Apt + L groups (Fig. S18). Furthermore, hematological parameters and serum biochemical indices, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (CREA), triglycerides (TG), and urea (UREA), remained within normal physiological ranges, with no statistically significant differences compared to the healthy control group (Fig. S19). Taken together, these findings demonstrated that treatment with UC@PEI-RB@Apt under NIR irradiation effectively promotes the healing of infected wounds by eliminating MRSA, without inducing systemic toxicity, highlighting its excellent biocompatibility and promising translational potential.

Conclusion

In summary, we have successfully engineered a smart, aptamer-guided upconversion nanoplatform (UC@PEI-RB@Apt) designed to overcome the limitations of shallow tissue penetration and off-target side effects inherent in conventional photodynamic therapy. By harnessing the deep-tissue penetration of 980 nm NIR light and the high specificity of aptamers, this nanoplatform achieves precise accumulation at the infection site and generates singlet oxygen in situ. This localized oxidative burst leads to the potent eradication of both planktonic MRSA (>99.9% killing rate) and established biofilms, effectively dismantling the physical barriers of bacterial resistance. Beyond its bactericidal capability, our in vivo investigations revealed that UC@PEI-RB@Apt plays a dual role in wound management. By efficiently reducing the bacterial burden, it facilitates a transition in the wound microenvironment from a chronic inflammatory state to a regenerative phenotype. This was evidenced by the significant downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, the upregulation of angiogenic factors (VEGF/CD31), and the accelerated deposition of collagen, ultimately resulting in rapid and high-quality skin restoration. Importantly, the nanoplatform exhibited an excellent biosafety profile with no detectable systemic toxicity. Conceptually, this work transcends traditional broad-spectrum antimicrobial strategies by integrating molecular recognition with spatially controlled activation. This “seek-and-destroy” approach aligns perfectly with the principles of precision medicine, offering a promising paradigm for treating deep-seated, persistent infections while minimizing collateral damage to healthy tissues. We envision that such targeted, NIR-activatable nanodevices will pave the way for future clinical applications in the precise management of drug-resistant infectious diseases.

For thicker wounds or those covered by necrotic tissue where external light penetration may be limited, interstitial fiber-optic delivery represents a clinically viable strategy to ensure uniform irradiation of deep infectious foci. This approach, which has been successfully implemented in photodynamic therapy for various deep-seated lesions [38,39], enables precise control over fiber placement and irradiation geometry, thereby extending the therapeutic applicability of our platform to complex wound presentations.
