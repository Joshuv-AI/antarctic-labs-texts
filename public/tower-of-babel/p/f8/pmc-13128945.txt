Investigation on the mechanical behavior of coconut leaf sheath and midrib of coconut leaf reinforced epoxy composites
Authors: Murugesan Palaniappan, Mohan Kumar Anand Raj, P. Manoj Kumar, Dawit Tafesse Gebreyohannes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Over the last two decades, researchers have focused on natural reinforcement composites as choice to conventional and man-made fiber composites due to their enhanced properties. While many research has studied the mechanical characteristics of renewable fiber composites, only limited work has been done on coconut fiber–based composites. In particular, no research has explored the reinforcement of two specific coconut fibers: coconut leaf sheath (CLS) and the midrib of coconut leaf (MCL). The aim of this study is to evaluate the mechanical characteristics of composites incorporated with CLS and MCL. Composite specimens were prepared by reinforcing MCL at 10%, 15% and 25% in CLS (20%) by hand moulding process. The specimens were tested in terms of tensile strength, flexural strength, impact resistance, and interlaminar shear strength (ILSS). The results show that the specimen with 25% MCL has the highest mechanical properties among all the combinations. ILSS testing confirms better fiber and matrix bonding at 25% MCL and hence better load transfer and strength. Similarly, flexural and impact tests show that the load-bearing capacity and energy absorption are improved through the use of higher MCL content to resist bending and sudden loads. However, water absorption has also been found to increase with an increase in fiber content because of the hydrophilic nature of MCL, which may influence moisture resistance. SEM analysis has been analyzed to identify the interfacial bonding and structural integrity of the material. The diffusion coefficient of the water uptake was determined using Fick’s thin plate approximation method. FTIR analysis shows the existence of important functional groups, which indicates that the fiber-matrix interaction and epoxy curing are successful. Although increased fiber content can lead to some processing difficulties, the 25% MCL composite provides the best balance between strength, flexibility and durability and is a promising material for structural and load-bearing applications.

Introduction

Natural fibre reinforced composites play a major role in many industries like automobile, construction and aerospace because of their desirable properties which comprise of low weight, high strength, availability, eco-friendliness and cost-effectiveness1. Natural fibres exhibit good mechanical properties and are biodegradable compared to synthetic fibres2,3. The mechanical characteristics of a fibre are strongly influenced by the fibre–matrix interface4,5. In composites, the stress transfer efficiency determines mechanical performance, and the internal adhesion can be improved by modifying the surface topology of the fibre through appropriate surface modification and by choosing appropriate components of the bonding system6–8. Fibres such as jute, coir, cotton lint, kenaf, flax, and sisal have been identified as green alternatives for the reinforcement of polymer composites9,10. Surnam et al.11 stated that alkali modification of fibres enhances tensile and flexural properties. Similarly, Dubey et al.12 assessed the physical characteristic performance of the midrib of coconut leaf (MCL) as a possible incorporation for polymer composites using ductile strength and fibre pull-out tests. Sajith13 observed that higher lignin content in bio-fillers dominates cellulose bonding in particle-reinforced composites, reducing cross-link density and mechanical properties, as identified using Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR). Kuan et al.14 examined the effects of fiber modification, density of fillers, and size of filler on the mechanical properties of organic natural filler–reinforced composites, concluding that suitable chemical treatment improves filler–matrix adhesion and homogeneity, thereby enhancing mechanical performance. Rajini et al.15 reviewed the response based mechanical and vibrational characteristic of hybrid composites and found that the effect of damping performance on the addition of nanoclay and surface modification of fibers. Jappes and Siva16 emphasized the significance of fibre volume fraction (FVF) in improving the mechanical properties of composites, with the exception of impact strength. The main aim of this research is to explore the mechanical behavior of various combinations of fibres and also to assess surface properties and internal bonding between fibre and polymer by some of the tests like FTIR and water absorption test. Bharath et al.17 said that natural fibre composites (NFCs), in particular those reinforced with coconut tree leaf sheath (CLS), are achieving response due to its eco-friendly and cost-effective nature. Chemically treated CLS fibres with phenol formaldehyde (PF) as well as UF resins exhibit better tensile strength, bending resistance, and dimensional stability. Alkali treatment (NaOH) increases fibre-matrix bonding by decreasing the absorption of moisture and swelling. These composites are promising for sustainable use in construction, automotive, and so on. Asyraf et al.18 investigated natural cellulosic fibres, especially kenaf, and pointed out their possibilities as replacement for man-made fibres in polymeric composites due to environmental awareness and petroleum resources exhaustion. Palani Kumar et al.19 conducted an assessment on the physical characteristics of coconut fibre filled polymer composites such as ductile property, shear stress, flexural rigidity, impact strength and hardness as per the ASTM standard. In addition, valuable information on the fibre–matrix interface was obtained from surface analyses based on scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Singh et al.20 reviewed the mechanical and physical behavior of jute and coconut coir filled polymer matrix composites. Their findings revealed that composites containing 85% and 15% jute and coconut coir respectively exhibited superior tensile strength, attributed to the higher weight fraction of jute fibre. In contrast, composites with a balanced 50% each of jute and coconut coir composition displayed brittle to ductile behaviour, demonstrating the influence of mixed fibre ratios. In this work, MCL and CLS reinforced epoxy composite specimen have been prepared and studied the mechanical characteristics. Dubey and Agnihotri21 reported that the MCL contains 30% and 16% of cellulose and lignin respectively, which shows the possibility of reinforcement in polymers. Bharat et al.22 revealed that the CLS having superior mechanical properties compare to the traditional coconut based composite materials. The both MCL and CLS are having higher potential in reinforcement with polymers and mechanical properties compare to traditional coconut based composite materials. However, no research work has been carried out in these composites namely MCL and CLS reinforced epoxy composites. Hence in this work, an attempt is made to investigate the mechanical characteristics of MCL and CLS reinforced epoxy composites. Furthermore, this study is to establish baseline mechanical characterization of the untreated coconut fiber-based composite. It is known that the chemical treatment can enhance the surface roughness and fiber-matrix compatibility. However, in future investigation will incorporate chemical treatment for improving long term durability.

Materials and methodology

The coconut leaf sheath and midrib of the coconut leaf were collected from the agriculture land in Perundurai, Tamil Nadu. The density, ductile behaviour and elastic modulus of coconut leaf sheath fibre are 1.375 g/cm³, 88.63 MPa and 4.4 GPa, respectively, and the midrib of the coconut leaf the density, tensile strength are 1.34 g/cm³, 177.5 MPa. The epoxy resin (LY556) and hardener (HY951) were procured from Covai Senu industry, Coimbatore, Tamil Nadu and India. It has good bond with man-made fibres, mechanical properties, it is very minimal shrinkage compared to other resins1.

Result and discussion

Tensile test analysis

The tensile test was shown in accordance with ASTM standards, and the results are shown in Fig. 4. The tensile test results of this work show that the accumulation of MCL into CLS enhances the tensile strength, with the 25% MCL specimen reaching 17.86 MPa, which is a 44.9% increase compared to the 10% MCL specimen. Similar trends are reported in the literature, where untreated coconut leaf stalk (CLS) fiber composites typically exhibit tensile strengths around 18–19 MPa25. In contrast, composites reinforced with surface-treated fibers or hybrid modifications, such as the inclusion of SiC nanoparticles, have shown significantly improved tensile strength values, reaching up to 68 MPa26. Additionally, woven CLS fiber-reinforced polyester composites have demonstrated tensile strengths as high as 44.6 MPa, depending on the weave configuration and fiber content11. In similar testing condition, dual reinforced composite fiber based composite namely, Glass/Abaca reinforced epoxy composite exhibits tensile strength of 38.46 MPa1. However, coconut fiber based composite exhibits lower tensile strength and it offers advantages such as biodegradability, low cost, low density and easy availability. The present results, though slightly lower than those obtained with chemical treatment or woven fibers, are in close agreement with untreated fiber composites and confirm that reinforcing CLS with 25% MCL gives a notable improvement in tensile strength and load-bearing capacity.

Flexural test analysis

The flexural test results show a significant improvement in mechanical properties with increasing MCL content in CLS-based composites, as shown in Fig. 5. The load-bearing capacity increases from 49.2 kgf at 10% MCL to 66.8 kgf at 15% MCL and further to 71.2 kgf at 25% MCL, while deformation rises from 1.36 mm to 2.90 mm and 10.2 mm respectively. Similar trends have been reported in recent studies, where increasing fiber content improves bending resistance and flexibility. For instance, Srinivasababu et al.27 reported a flexural strength of 85.39 MPa for chemically treated CLS/epoxy composites, while woven CLS combined with sisal fiber composites achieved values above 120 MPa. Likewise, CIF-reinforced polyester composites have shown maximum flexural strengths of about 122.7 MPa at 25% fiber loading28. Compared with these, the present results show lower absolute values, mainly due to the use of untreated fibers, hand lay-up processing, and possible limitations in resin infiltration. However, the trend of increasing load capacity and deformation with higher fiber content agrees well with literature reports29, confirming that 25% MCL reinforcement provides the best combination of strength and flexibility in CLS-based composites. Therefore, 25% MCL is identified as the most effective composition for improved flexural performance.

Impact test analysis

The Izod impact investigation results including error bars are illustrated in Fig. 7. It is evident from the findings that the impact energy absorbed by the composite increases with the rising percentage of midrib coir loading (MCL). Specifically, the composite with 25% midrib fibre had the highest impact strength of 1.851 J followed by the 15% fibre composite (1.567 J), and the 10% fibre composite showed the lowest value of 1.439 J. This progressive improvement in impact strength with fibre loading suggests that midrib fibres play a significant part in improving energy dissipation capability of the composite under the sudden impact condition. The presence of higher fibre content probably plays a role in the improvement of stress distribution and the higher toughness, which reduces the tendency of catastrophic failure on loading. These results are consistent with recent studies of natural fibre-filled composites. For example, Hossain et al.30 reported that as the fibre content increased in coir-polyester composites, the impact resistance increased, owing to the good fibre-matrix interaction and crack deflection mechanisms. Similarly, Islam et al.31 found that the use of higher weight fraction of untreated jute fibres improved the energy absorption and retarded the crack propagation behaviour under impact loading. Moreover, a study by Mulenga et al.32 highlighted that natural fibres combined with nanoparticles not only improve toughness but also promote micro-crack bridging, thereby increasing impact performance. Thus, the observed enhancement in impact strength with higher midrib fibre content aligns well with the literature, confirming that natural fibre reinforcement improves toughness and imparts greater resistance to sudden forces in polymer composites. It is absorbed that the improved impact strength of the specimen is related with energy absorbing mechanism namely, fiber pullout, fiber break and crack deflection. Crack propagation is delayed owing to the presence of fibers, it leading to energy dissipation, fiber pullout and interfacial debonding has attributed to the energy absorbing by frictional sliding, due to this enhancing the overall impact resistance.

Water absorption test analysis

The water absorption characteristics of the composites is presented in Table 4. It is detected that the proportion of water absorbed rises with the rise in midrib coir loading (MCL) including error bars is shown in the Fig. 8. For the composite containing 10% fibre, water absorption was 7.0%, which increased significantly to 16.2% for 15% fibre loading, and further to 17.7% for 25% fibre loading after 48 h of immersion. This trend can be recognized to the decline in resin content as fibre content increases, resulting in greater moisture uptake due to the hydrophilic nature of the fibres. Additionally, the non-uniform distribution of the epoxy matrix around the fibres at higher fibre loadings may create micro voids and pathways, allowing easier water penetration. The hydrophilic nature of natural fibres is well-documented and is responsible for the observed increase in water absorption with increasing fibre percentage. Similar results were reported by Deepan et al.33, who reported an increase in water uptake in the coconut coir-epoxy composites with fibre content due to capillary action along the fibre-matrix interfaces. Likewise, Islam et al.31 noticed jute - polyesters composites noticed higher uptake of moisture in the presence of fibre loads this can primarily be explained on the existence of hydroxyl group in cellulose structure, which readily attract water molecules. Additionally, Islam and Hasan34 reported that hemp fibre composites exhibited high moisture uptake with higher fibre proportion which was attributed to the discontinuity of the matrix and the formation of voids at higher fibre fractions. Thus, the present study is consistent with the recent literature and confirms the aforementioned fact that the hydrophilic nature of natural fibres like the ones from coconut midrib results in higher moisture uptake with increasing the fibre contents thus affecting the long-term durability and dimensional stability of the composites. The mechanical behavior of the CLS and MCL fibers-based composite has affected due to the moisture absorption and also it leads to swelling, matrix plasticization and weakening of the fiber-resin interface. Additionally, microcrack formation and interfacial debonding may formed, it causing decreasing load transfer efficiency, mechanical strength and dimensional stability. Further, evaluation of post immersion mechanical characterization is important for durability assessment and will be performed in future work. The diffusion coefficient of the water uptake was calculated using Fick’s thin plate approximation method35,36. The values obtained were 1.58 × 10⁻¹¹, 1.75 × 10⁻¹¹, and 1.92 × 10⁻¹¹ m²/s for 10%, 15%, and 25% MCL composites, respectively. The increase in diffusion coefficient with fiber loading confirms enhanced moisture transport through fiber–matrix interfaces and micro void pathways. The results indicate predominantly Fickian diffusion behavior in the initial absorption stage.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

The FTIR spectrum for the composites were analysed to identify the important functional groups for different percentage of midrib coir loading (MCL) in the coconut leaf sheath (CLS) composites. The spectra of the 10%, 15% and 25% MCL composites confirmed the presence of characteristic functional groups that are important for fibre and matrix interaction and structural stability. A strong absorption peak at about 1700 cm-1 present in all the composites (Fig. 11) is related to the C = O stretching vibration of lignin and hemicellulose and plays a critical role in the determination of thermal stability and mechanical strength. In addition, peaks in the range of 1230–1280 cm-1 (Fig. 10), which are related to the stretching vibrations of C-O bonds, indicate good fibre integrity and good bonding between midrib fibres and epoxy matrix. The presence of an epoxide band at 830–910 cm-1 (Fig. 9) further confirms the successful curing of the epoxy resin and therefore sufficient interfacial adhesion in a composite. The gradual increase in the midrib fibre content seemed to strengthen the fibre-matrix interaction, therefore improving the structural stability, moisture resistance and the overall performance of the composites. These observations are consistent with what was found in recent studies. For example, Ramachandran et al.37 described that the occurrence carbonyl (C = O) and hydroxyl groups in natural fibre composites caused enhanced thermal stability and mechanical properties due to enhanced bonding between polymer matrices.

Interlaminar shear strength

Fig. 12ILSS value for different proportion of MCL in CLS.

ILSS value for different proportion of MCL in CLS.

The interlaminar shear strength (ILSS) values of the composites with 10%, 15% and 25% midrib coir loading (MCL) in CLS are given in Fig. 12. The results indicate a significant increase in ILSS as the fibre content increases. Specifically, ILSS was found to be 5.801 MPa for 10% fibre which increased significantly to 9.033 MPa for 15% fibre and further to 9.448 MPa for 25% fibre. The increase in 10% to 15% fibre content yields a rise in ILSS of 55.7% which shows a better bonding of fibres and matrix and a better load transmission through the composite. However, the rate of increase beyond 15% fibre loading was quite low (4.6%) probably because of fibre agglomeration, void formation or inadequate resin impregnation at higher fibre fractions. This would be suggestive that 25% midrib reinforcement is the best approach for the best balance for enhanced ILSS without compromising material integrity. These observations are in line with trends found in recent studies on natural fibre composites. For instance, Saravana Kumar et al.40 found that ILSS improved with increasing fibre content in composites made of bamboo fiber up to an optimum fibre ratio after which agglomeration and poor wetting reduced performance gains. Similarly, Graupner and Mussig41 found that flax-epoxy composites had ample improvement of ILSS up to a 20% to 30% fibre loading, but further increases resulted in voids and decreased resin penetration that limited the shear strength. Moreover, AhmadvashAghbash et al.42 emphasized that fibre–matrix adhesion, satisfactory resin impregnation and homogeneous dispersion of the fibres are the important factors affecting ILSS and excess addition of fibre may result in poor stress transfer. Thus, the present study confirms the effect of increasing MCL content on ILSS up to an optimum value, with 25% reinforcement showing the best performance while not showing a significant decrease in structural integrity.

Conclusion

This study investigates the influence of hybrid fibers-based composites namely CLS and MCL on mechanical properties viz., tensile, flexural, impact, and water absorption. The investigation on CLS based composites with 10%, 15% and 25% MCL shows that increasing the MCL content significantly improves the mechanical properties of the composites with 25% MCL showing the best overall properties. Tensile, flexural, ILSS and impact tests have always confirmed that 25% MCL has better load-bearing capability, bending resistance, shear and energy absorption. SEM analysis gives insights about the fiber pullouts, fiber breakages and crack formation of matrix surfaces. FTIR analysis further confirms the good fiber–matrix interaction and the curing of the epoxy, which help to contribute to structural integrity. Although the greater fiber content results in greater water absorption and may cause slight processing difficulties, the 25% MCL composite results in the best combination of strength, flexibility, and durability and is the most promising composition for structural and load-bearing applications. The moisture absorption investigation shows the relationship between the fiber hydrophilicity, interfacial stability and also displays the degradation pathways of natural fiber-based composites. Scientifically, this work gives the baseline behaviour for untreated hybrid coconut fiber-based composites and identifies the effect of fiber loading on interfacial stress transfer and durability behaviour. Practically, the findings suggests that the developed composite, suitable for lightweight, environmentally sustainable application where mechanical strength and biodegradability are highlighted. Future research focused on effect chemical modified fiber-based composites on structural reliability and industrial applicability.
