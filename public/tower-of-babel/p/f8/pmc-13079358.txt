An insect that cooperates like bacteria
Authors: Stuart A. West, Ashleigh S. Griffin
Published: 2026

In cooperative breeding birds and mammals, some offspring give up the chance to breed independently and instead help their parents to breed. This type of cooperation involves a division of labor between the “dominant” individuals who do most of the breeding, and the “subordinate” individuals who help rear the offspring of others. Reproductive division of labor is taken to an extreme level in social insects, such as ants and termites, where the subordinate helpers are completely sterile and can be divided into different physical castes, that are specialized to do different jobs.

Division of labor and reproductive sterility can be found in microorganisms, but it is much less frequent than the communal production of public goods. Bacterial cells produce a range of factors that are released from the cell into the extracellular environment, where they perform some function which benefits the local group of cells (Fig. 1A). These factors are often called “public goods” because they benefit the whole group, analogous to the economic public goods games that economists make humans play. Examples of bacterial public goods include iron scavenging molecules, enzymes to break down proteins and enzymes which chop up antibiotics (1–3).

(A) Bacteria cooperate by producing public goods (4). (B) A defensive group of N. sertifer larvae. Image credit: Raphael Ritter (University of Helsinki, Helsinki, Finland).

Now, in exciting turn around, Ritter et al. describe an insect that cooperates like a bacterial cell by producing a public good (5). The insect is the pine sawfly Neodiprion sertifer, a common forest pest. The larvae of this species feeds on pine needles, and when abundant, can defoliate entire pine trees across vast areas. During their larval phase, this species forages in groups, which attract the attention of diverse natural enemies, including birds, ants, and parasitoids.

But these pine sawfly larvae do not offer an easy meal—they tackle their natural enemies collectively (Fig. 1B). Larvae perform a synchronous defensive display where they raise their head and tail in a “U-posture” and release a defensive secretion (5). This secretion contains volatiles that deter insect predators and a toxic resin that both irritates and sticks predators. This deters their natural enemies. And who would blame them—no one wants to get covered with an irritating toxic glue when having a snack, especially when the snacks are ganging up on you.

Riitter et al. have taken research on this sawfly to the next level by asking the kind of questions and performing the experiments required to answer those questions that we more normally associate with microorganisms. In bacteria, you sometimes find some cells that do not produce a certain public good. These cells act as “cheats” because they can gain benefit of the public goods produced by other cells, without producing themselves. Do you get cheating sawfly larvae? Is there a selfish benefit to cheating? What are the consequences for other larvae? In a mixed group, are cooperators more likely to avoid predation than cheats? Do cheats do less well as they become more common?

Some pine sawflies do cheat by not producing the fluid when provoked, even though their defense reservoirs are full (6). And this seems to provide a growth benefit by avoiding having to put resources into producing more defensive secretion. Microbiologists can study the consequences of cheating by using elegant genetic manipulations to produce cheats and experimentally vary their proportion in a population. Ritter et al. did not have that option, but they managed to do the equivalent by “manually depleting defensive fluid.” That is science-speak for “milking” larvae to turn them into cheats.

By manually producing cheats, Ritter et al. were able to vary the proportion of cheats in a group and then monitor the consequences. They compared groups where 70% or 20% of individuals had been “depleted” and found that the mortality rate was lower in groups with fewer depleted larvae. The presence of cheats therefore has a cost, decreasing survival. They also found that the benefits of defensive secretion were not shared equally—depleted individuals were more likely to be predated. But the depleted individuals were less likely to be predated when there were fewer depleted individuals in a group. Individuals that do not secrete therefore do act as cheats which can gain the benefit of other individuals secreting.

Because cheats can exploit cooperators, we must ask why cooperation is ever favored? Why do some larvae produce defensive secretions? Ritter et al. showed that, just like in bacteria and cooperative breeding vertebrates, the answer is that groups are composed of relatives. Roughly speaking, the larvae in a foraging group are equivalent to half-siblings. Inclusive fitness theory has shown that altruism can be favored when it is directed towards other individuals who share the gene or genes for altruism. This means that cooperative individuals who produce secretions are also gaining an indirect (kin selected) benefit from helping relatives (7).

As with any good study, answering one question raises other questions, and when you dig into the details, you find complexities. Ritter et al. found that behavior in natural groups depends upon sex and group composition and varied geographically. Females are more likely to deploy the defensive fluid and do so in larger quantities. Individuals are less likely to defend in larger groups, or when there are more males in a group, or when relatedness is lower.

Explaining how cheats and cooperators can be maintained in the same population is harder than you might think. In the simplest scenario, cheats do better when there are more cooperators about, but so do cooperators. Consequently, the relative fitness of cheats does not vary with their frequency in the population. This means that cooperation is either favored or not. Hamilton loved the simplicity of this result so much when he was developing inclusive fitness theory that he called it a “gift from God” (7).

To maintain a mixture of cooperators and cheats in a population, you need the relative fitness of the cheats to vary depending on how common they are, termed frequency dependence. A mixture of cheats and cooperators can be maintained if the relative fitness of cheats increases when they become rarer. This allows cheats to invade but stops them from going to fixation. In microorganisms, several factors can lead to frequency dependence but what about pine sawflies (8, 9)?

Now, in exciting turn around, Ritter et al. describe an insect that cooperates like a bacterial cell by producing a public good (5).

Ritter et al. did not find strong support for the fitness of cheats being higher when rarer. There was a trend in that direction, but it was not significant, and it remains unclear how cheats and cooperators can be maintained in the same system. A possibility is that the costs and benefits of cooperating vary across individuals—a hypothesis which is also consistent with the sex difference. Females are larger, and so the relative benefit of cooperating might be higher. Perhaps there are also finer level differences?

To conclude, while pine sawflies might appear to cooperate like microorganisms, there is an incredibly important difference in how they can be studied. Working on microorganisms offers some amazing opportunities for elegant laboratory experiments, but it is hard to examine what is happening in natural populations. Microbiologists have had to rely on the opportunities offered by the striking physical forms of fruiting bodies, examining social evolution in pathogenic infections, or looking for footprints of selection at the genomic level (10, 11). In contrast, Ritter et al. have demonstrated public goods cooperation in an organism that you can both observe and manipulate in the natural world (5). Social microbiologists are likely to look on with envy at the wonderful opportunities this offers.
