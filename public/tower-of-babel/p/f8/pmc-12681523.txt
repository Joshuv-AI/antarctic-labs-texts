Transradial uterine artery embolization for the treatment of symptomatic fibroids: A ten year experience
Authors: Carlos Jiménez-Rámila, Ana Redondo-Villatoro, Rafael J Ruiz-Salmerón, Sergio Rodríguez de Leiras-Otero, Antonio Jiménez-Caraballo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDUterine fibroids are the most common tumors in women. They are generally asymptomatic but can cause bleeding problems and/or compression of neighboring structures and infertility. Embolization is an effective treatment for fibroids. However, transradial access is underused for various reasons.OBJECTIVEThis study aimed to demonstrate that transradial access is safe and effective for treating symptomatic uterine fibroids to avoid the need for conventional surgery.STUDY DESIGNA single-center retrospective observational study of medical records between 2012 and 2022, in which 189 procedures were planned in patients with symptomatic uterine fibroids with fulfilled gestational desire, was conducted. The feasibility and safety of the technique, the need for subsequent surgery because of the lack of effectiveness of the treatment in women who could complete it from the date of the procedure until 24 months later, changes in uterine volume and in the largest fibroid, and changes in hemoglobin levels, follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone, and estradiol were analyzed before and 6 months after the procedure. Changes in the quality of life questionnaire (Uterine Fibroid Symptom and Health-Related Quality of Life questionnaire) were analyzed before embolization and at 2 and 6 months after embolization.RESULTSOf note, 184 of 189 procedures (97.35%) were performed with total or partial angiographic success. Surgical intervention was not necessary 24 months after the procedure in 85.4% of patients (158/180 patients with complete angiographic success) with a very low rate of complications, all of them mild (13/185 [7%]). Between the first visit and the visit at 6 months after the intervention, both the uterine volume decreased (n=176; previous volume, 458.11 mL; volume after 6 months, 311.87 mL; −146.24 mL [95% confidence interval, −115.41 to −177.06]; P<.001) and the largest fibroid (previous volume 132.31 mL; volume after 6 months, 78.33 mL; −53.98 mL [95% confidence interval, −42.79 to −65.17]; P<.001) and the scores in the quality of life questionnaires and the hemoglobin level improved (previous level, 11.4 g/dL; level after 6 months, 12.85 g/dL; −9.94 [95% confidence interval, −1.74 to −1.16]; P<.001). Follicle-stimulating hormone (previous level, 18.61; level after 6 months, 30.68; −4.18 [95% confidence interval, −6.35 to −17.77]; P<.001) and luteinizing hormone (previous level, 13.52; level after 6 months, 20.91; −3.60 [95% confidence interval, −3.31 to −11.45]; P<.001) levels were elevated, but estradiol levels (previous level, 97.98; level after 6 months, 99.01; 0.11 [95% confidence interval, −32.40 to 28.84]) levels were not affected.CONCLUSIONThe transradial approach should be adopted as a safe and effective alternative for the treatment of symptomatic uterine fibroids in patients with fulfilled gestational desire.Key words: embolization, fibroid, leiomyoma, transradial

Introduction

Uterine fibroids are the most common gynecologic tumors in women. They are smooth muscle tumors of benign nature. They can be a clinical problem because of their location and/or size.1 Generally, although myomas with submucosal involvement cause fertility problems and increase the amount and duration of menstrual bleeding, and subserosal myomas cause compression of neighboring structures, most myomas are asymptomatic.

In recent decades, various nonsurgical treatments, which are less radical and have less chance of success compared with traditional surgical treatments, have been developed. This has created the need to evaluate the clinical effect and improvement of symptomatology with these new techniques.

Among these techniques is selective embolization of the uterine arteries, a procedure first described by Ravina et al,2 which is traditionally performed via the transfemoral route but whose transradial access has been successfully described for more than a decade now.3

This study aimed to evaluate the effect of transradial uterine artery embolization (UAE) in avoiding myomectomy or hysterectomy in the first 2 years after the procedure and to observe changes in quality of life (QoL) and different analytical and volumetric parameters by presenting a long series of cases that represents the experience of the first 10 years of the technique in our center.

Materials and methods

A retrospective observational study of medical records of patients from February 2012 to December 2022 (N=189) to whom selective UAE was attempted for the control of symptoms produced by fibroids was conducted. The study design was presented to and approved by the ethics committee of the hospital.

The inclusion criteria included patients with fulfilled gestational desire and symptomatic myoma with a volume smaller than that of a 15-cm diameter sphere (1.755 cm3) or myomas that together did not exceed this volume, International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) type 2 to 6 myomas (or type I unresectable by hysteroscopy due to a size >4 cm), patients who expressed a preference for preserving the uterus, and patients in whom surgery was not advisable because of previous abdominal interventions or pathology affecting the abdominal cavity (eg, Crohn disease).

The exclusion criteria included unfulfilled gestational desire, pedunculated myomas (FIGO types 0 and 7) due to hypothetical necrosis of the vascular pedicle, known allergy to iodinated contrast, and adnexal pathology or suspected malignancy recommending traditional surgical treatment.

Before the procedure, a preliminary consultation was conducted to select the patients, always by the same team of 3 gynecologists, before referring them to the same team of 2 hemodynamists who performed the intervention. Follow-up consultations were conducted at 2, 6, and 24 months after the procedure.

The procedure was performed through the left transradial access. After a short 5F sheath insertion, 2.5 mg of verapamil and 3000 units of heparin were infused into the radial artery. A 125-cm-long Berenstein 5F catheter was manipulated to canulate both internal iliac arteries. Nonselective angiographies were performed to select the dominant uterine artery side, the one with the greater diameter and development.

The dominant uterine artery was first embolized. A Renegade microcatheter (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA) was advanced using a roadmap mask until the horizontal portion of the uterine arteries, surpassing the origin of the vaginal branch. Of note, 700 to 900 μm polyvinyl alcohol microspheres were prepared and charged in a 10-mL syringe and carefully infused through the microcatheter until the end of leiomyoma perfusion appeared. The same procedure was performed to embolize the contralateral uterine artery, which usually required less volume of microspheres to reach the end of perfusion.

Complete success was defined when flow stasis was obtained and maintained for at least 5 minutes for both uterine arteries, as presented in Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4. We considered partial success in the case of a single UAE.

All patients received the same analgesic protocol: in hospital (first 24 hours), morphine 1 mg intravenous (IV) slow (as needed), dexketoprofen 150 mg IV infusion every 24 hours, tramadol 100 mg IV slow every 8 hours, granisetron 3 mg IV continuous infusion every 24 hours, and famotidine 20 mg orally every 24 hours; after discharge (7 days), dexketoprofen 25 mg orally every 8 hours, metamizole 1 capsule orally every 8 hours, and omeprazole 20 mg orally every 24 hours; and rescue, paracetamol-codeine 1 tablet orally every 8 hours.

The main outcome variable was the avoidance of conventional surgery 24 months after the procedure in the event of technique failure. In addition, this study analyzed the feasibility and safety of the technique, changes in the QoL questionnaires (Spanish version of the 37-Item Uterine Fibroid Symptom and Health-Related Quality of Life questionnaire [UFS-QoL-37], validated after the start of the study4), improvement in hemoglobin levels and changes in hormonal profile (follicle-stimulating hormone [FSH], luteinizing hormone [LH], and estradiol) in the first phase of the cycle as a factor of possible ovarian involvement 2 and 6 months after the procedure.

The statistical analysis was performed using the IBM SPSS program (version 17.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY). The Student t test for paired data was used to evaluate the changes in the analytical parameters and the QoL questionnaires.

The ethics committee and institutional review board approved this study. The approval code is 2025GINEHUVM0001. The need for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective and observational nature of the study.

Of 189 cases attempted, angiographic success was achieved in 95.2% (180) of cases in both uterine vessels and only in 2.1% (4) of cases in one uterine vessel, in 2 uterine vessels due to vascular abnormalities, and in another 2 uterine vessels due to technical difficulties. The technique could not be completed in at least 1 vessel in 2.6% (5) of cases, in 1 case due to an allergy to contrast, in another case due to the large size of the myoma, and in the remaining 3 cases due to vascular anomalies.

Patients were admitted for a minimum of 24 hours, with an average stay of 1.16 days. Among the 180 patients in whom the procedure was considered successful and after eliminating 4 lost cases (n=176), the need for subsequent surgery due to lack of satisfactory results and persistence of the clinical condition for which the patients consulted was 11.9%, corresponding to 21 patients who underwent conventional surgery (myomectomy/hysterectomy) 24 months after embolization, with 88.1% (155) of women who underwent UAE in the first 2 years after embolization avoiding the need to go to the operating room. Of the 4 women who had only 1 of 2 uterine arteries embolized (incomplete procedure), 3 required conventional surgery. The 5 patients in whom the procedure was unsuccessful were referred directly to conventional surgery.

There was no vascular access complication in 182 procedures (96.8%) attempted. Of note, 7 cases were classified as complications at the point or time of access, all of them mild: 2 cases of vasospasm; 1 case of early postprocedure phlebitis; 1 case of pain in the upper limb of access; 1 case of transient cyanosis of <6 hours of evolution of the first, second, and third fingers; and 2 cases of allergy to contrast not previously reported.

Emergency department visits in the first month after embolization

Of 185 patients in whom the procedure was fully or partially completed, 13 patients visited the emergency department in the first month after the procedure. The most frequent reason was the demand for punctual IV analgesia (7 patients), followed by menstrual alterations/vaginal bleeding (4 patients); a vulvar ulcer of approximately 3 cm (1 patient), which was followed up until its complete resolution without the need for additional tests; and fever without apparent infectious focus resolved with antipyretics (1 patient). No visit to the emergency department was recorded in the first month after embolization in 172 patients.

Comment

Principal findings

The typical patient in this study was a woman in her late reproductive years with heavy menstrual bleeding due to a dominant myoma with at least 1 delivery. Although one-fifth of the samples studied had no previous pregnancies and did not plan to have offspring, a question that was asked before embolization was indicated, as the effect of embolization on future pregnancies was not known since the beginning of the study.

The main objective of the study yielded satisfactory results from our point of view, avoiding surgery for almost 9 of 10 women in the first 2 years after the procedure, with a very low rate of complications, even if we include the complications during the procedure and emergency room visits within a month after the procedure. Of note, none of the complications were serious, which carries significant weight when considering the advantages of this procedure.

Having performed a considerable number of interventions in the first 10 years, we believe that the results allow us to affirm that transradial embolization is a safe and effective alternative treatment for symptomatic myomas that can be considered in women who desire a less invasive and less radical therapy than conventional surgery. Surgery is avoided in a high percentage of cases after 2 years of follow-up with a low complication rate.

Concerning the other outcome variables, embolization seems to improve hemoglobin levels, including those of patients who did not undergo surgery due to bleeding.

In addition, embolization improved the QoL questionnaire score with a validated questionnaire.

It remains to be seen in subsequent studies whether embolization affects ovarian function, as it seems to affect FSH and LH significantly, although estradiol remained unaffected.

Results in the context of what is known

At the time of study initiation, there was no scientific literature on transradial myoma embolization. The first reference was published by Resnick et al3 in 2013 with a small number of patients. In the time elapsed, the use of the technique has not become widespread, as indicated by Himiniuc et al5 in their review of 2021, despite the lower rate of complications, faster mobilization, and earlier discharge, perhaps because of the longer learning curve, the challenge of anatomical variants, and the material necessary for techniques of this nature. In this review, the success rate of the technique ranged between 95.0% and 100.0%, and our study fell within the same range (95.2%).

During the last 2 decades, transradial access has become the default vascular approach for coronary interventions, both diagnostic and therapeutic, and in every clinical situation. This paradigm shift from transfemoral access to transradial access in the coronary field is justified by the evidence that transradial access is associated with fewer bleeding and vascular complications and probably less mortality.6

Although evidence for the superiority of transradial access over transfemoral access in peripheral interventions is lacking, we can assume the conclusions found in the coronary field. Although transradial access is safer than the transfemoral access, there are other advantages of transradial access: it allows for early ambulation and shorter length of hospitalization.

However, the transradial route is technically more challenging and requires more operator expertise. Radial spasm, tortuous subclavian artery, and acute angulation between the subclavian artery and the descending aorta are potential difficulties that should be addressed. In nonexpertise centers, transradial access could require more time for fluoroscopy and procedures.

In our experience, UAE is always performed on the left side to avoid the aortic arch and is not even safer than the transfemoral technique but is simpler. UAE is technically easy from above, using the same catheter, to engage the uterine arteries of both sides. The use of a large introducer sheath and a single curve extra-large catheter inside (mother and child technique) facilitates navigation maneuvers and is of great help in cases of aortic tortuosity or extra-tall patients.

Concerning our outcome variable, transradial UAE avoided surgery in the first 24 months by almost 90.0%, which is a somewhat higher result than that of the Toor meta-analysis,7 which estimates reintervention at 5.3% for a follow-up of up to 5 years, and lower than that of The EMbolization versus hysterectoMY study (EMMY) trial,8 which presents results, including 10 years of follow-up. Focusing on studies performed exclusively transradially, we believe that, to date, our study includes the largest number of patients with an exclusively transradial technique and with a follow-up of 2 years from the procedure. These 2 facts make this study unique.

Concerning the decrease in uterine volume and the volume of the dominant myoma, we observed significant reduction rates, similar to the case with the transfemoral approach.9

Concerning complications, the meta-analysis previously cited7 puts the rate of major complications at 2.9%, which contrasts with our study, in which there was no complication. We are convinced that this is due to the use of the transradial route instead of the femoral route. Furthermore, the minor complications are consistent with those reported in the literature for transabdominal and transradial embolization. Khayrutdinov et al10 reported an improvement in the QoL using the transradial approach in the immediate postoperative period compared with the transfemoral approach, with similar results in terms of procedure success, with less radiation time, catheterization, and total duration, and a similar rate of minor and major complications between both techniques.

Concerning QoL in the medium to long term, the Randomised comparison of uterine artery Embolisation (UAE) with Surgical Treatment (REST) trial11 concludes that, at 5 years after UAE, the QoL results, analyzed with the 36-Item Short-Form General Health Survey questionnaire, are comparable with conventional surgery despite the higher rate of reintervention, which is offset by a shorter hospital stay and the less invasive nature of the procedure.

However, the Fibroids of the uterus: Myomectomy versus Embolization (FEMME) collaborative group12 concluded that, at 2 years, UAE has worse QoL outcomes than myomectomy. The differences between the 2 studies may be due to the follow-up cadences or the variability in the questionnaires used.

In our case, the results are not compared with any other method, but the improvement in QoL is undeniable despite the fact that it was analyzed in a shorter period after the procedure.

Finally, the studies that analyze the possible ovarian involvement speak of a probable relationship between the parameters of ovarian function according to the age of the patient. Thus, levels would increase in women aged >45 years.11,13 Our study found a significant elevation of FSH and LH levels but without alteration of estradiol levels, which requires further investigation for its explanation. Moreover, this elevation was not stratified according to the patient’s age.

Clinical implications

We firmly believe that UAE is a safe and effective alternative treatment for symptomatic uterine fibroids that should be adopted in all referral centers in the management of this pathology. We believe that the use of UAE has not become widespread due to the technical difficulty that may be an anatomically narrower and more distant access than the femoral artery.

The results obtained could help health professionals make informed decisions and offer more personalized treatment options for women suffering from the symptoms of uterine fibroids. In addition, it is hoped that this work will contribute to the growing body of scientific evidence in this field and stimulate future research that will further advance the development of optimal therapeutic approaches for the management of uterine fibroids.

Research implications

We believe that further studies are needed to evaluate the role of UAE on ovarian function and in patients who have not yet fulfilled their gestational desire. Furthermore, prospective and randomized studies comparing this technique with other known alternatives are advisable.

Strengths and limitations

Our study covers a period of 10 years as the technique was implemented in our center and several patients, which we believe may be one of the longest series published on transradial access. In contrast, despite having a significant number of patients, this is a retrospective single-center study, and the follow-up is limited in time and has no control group (transfemoral embolization, medical treatment, or conventional surgery).

Conclusion

The UAE technique using the transradial route is effective in avoiding the operating room in a high percentage of cases, with a decrease in uterine and myoma volume, an increase in QoL, and a very low complication rate, lower than that estimated in the literature for the transfemoral route, which is still the most commonly used technique. However, further studies are required to analyze the effect on ovarian function.
