Anesthetic strategies for manual removal of retained placenta: an observational cohort study at a university referral center
Authors: Mohamad Yousef, Daniel Shatalin, Tehila Avitan, Yaacov Gozal, Yair Binyamin, Alexander Ronenson, Alexander Ioscovich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRetained placenta is a significant cause of postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) and maternal morbidity. Despite its clinical importance, limited data exists on the safety and effectiveness of different anesthetic techniques for manual removal of placenta (MROP). This study aimed to assess anesthesia-related outcomes and complications in a large tertiary care center with 20,000 to 22,000 annual deliveries.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on women who underwent MROP in the operating room between January 2018 and September 2024. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained prior to data collection. Patient demographics, risk factors, anesthetic techniques, and clinical outcomes were collected and analyzed.ResultsDuring the study period, there were 130,338 vaginal deliveries at our institution. Among 1,366 women (1.05%) undergoing MROP, 860 required placental revision and 506 underwent manual placental removal. Vacuum-assisted delivery was performed in 99 cases (22%). Neuraxial anesthesia was the predominant modality (81.9%), with spinal anesthesia most frequently used (73.7%) and 25.7% subsequently converted to epidural anesthesia. General anesthesia (GA) (16.6%) and sedation (1.5%) were less commonly employed. Women receiving GA had significantly higher risks of blood transfusion (RR 6.0 for RBC, RR 19.5 for FFP, p < 0.0001), longer hospitalization (4.1 ± 2.3 vs. 3.5 ± 3.4 days, p < 0.002), and increased need for ICU/PACU monitoring (RR 20.9, p < 0.0001). Difficult intubation occurred in three cases, with one reported case of aspiration. No hysterectomies were required.ConclusionsNeuraxial anesthesia was the preferred method for MROP, demonstrating a low failure rate. In contrast, GA was associated with increased morbidity, including higher transfusion requirements, prolonged hospitalization, and greater need for intensive monitoring. The occurrence of airway complications in GA cases underscores the importance of optimizing anesthetic management. Minimizing the use of GA, when feasible, may contribute to improved patient outcomes in MROP procedures.

Background

Retained placenta is a potentially life-threatening condition, defined as the failure to deliver the placenta within 30 to 60 min after childbirth. Its reported incidence ranges from 1 to 3% of vaginal deliveries [1]. As the second leading cause of postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) after uterine atony, retained placenta represents a major contributor to maternal morbidity in obstetric populations [2, 3].

Several risk factors have been identified for retained placenta, including induction of labor, high parity, preterm delivery, conception via in vitro fertilization (IVF), prior cesarean delivery or other uterine surgery, and a history of retained placenta [2–4].

Management typically involves both the administration of uterotonic agents to minimize bleeding, and manual removal of the placenta (MROP) which is usually performed in an operating room.

MROP, also referred to as manual lysis of the placenta, involves hand-assisted separation and extraction of the placenta when spontaneous detachment fails to occur. Placental revision, refers to inspection of the uterine cavity and removal of retained placental fragments following initial expulsion [5]. In rare cases, hysterectomy may be required, particularly in the context of placenta accreta spectrum or severe, uncontrolled hemorrhage [1]. As an invasive and painful procedure, MROP carries an increased risk of postpartum endometritis and necessitates appropriate anesthesia and pain management [1, 6].

A variety of anesthetic techniques may be employed for MROP, including neuraxial anesthesia (spinal and epidural), sedation, peripheral nerve blocks (paracervical and pudendal), and general anesthesia [6]. Epidural anesthesia administered via an indwelling catheter is generally preferred, as it provides hemodynamic stability and avoids airway-related risks. Spinal anesthesia is the second most commonly utilized neuraxial technique, whereas general anesthesia is typically reserved for cases involving significant hemorrhage, hemodynamic instability, or contraindications to neuraxial anesthesia, such as anticoagulant therapy [6].

In cases of significant PPH, neuraxial anesthesia may be contraindicated in hemodynamically unstable patients. However, general anesthesia also carries substantial risks, including circulatory collapse at induction and the well-documented airway challenges of pregnancy, such as anatomically difficult airways and an increased risk of aspiration [7–9]. Effective anesthetic management is therefore critical for the safe conduct of MROP. Despite this, few studies have systematically evaluated the efficacy, safety, and adverse effects of different anesthetic techniques in this context [6].

The present study aims to assess the utilization rates and safety profile of general anesthesia compared with alternative anesthetic techniques for MROP. A secondary objective is to evaluate the incidence of anesthesia-related and obstetric complications.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a high-volume tertiary care center and aimed to evaluate anesthesia-related outcomes and associated complications in women undergoing MROP in the operating room.

Data were obtained through chart review from electronic medical records and institutional databases, including electronic reporting platforms (ERPs), covering the period from January 2018 to September 2024. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained prior to data collection (0114–23-SZMC; approval date: July 13, 2023), and the requirement for written informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective design. The study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. The primary outcome was the rate of general anesthesia compared with other anesthetic techniques used during MROP. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of anesthesia-related complications (e.g., high or total spinal block, airway management difficulties, prolonged mechanical ventilation, ICU admission, and aspiration), obstetric complications (e.g., vaginal or perineal tears, blood product transfusion, hysterectomy, and endometritis), as well as patient demographic characteristics and their correlation with these complications.

Inclusion criteria comprised women diagnosed with retained placenta following labor who underwent MROP under anesthesia in the operating room, with complete anesthetic records available for review. Exclusion criteria included cases with incomplete data and those in which the procedure was not performed in the operating room.

General anesthesia was defined as the use of endotracheal intubation with a secured airway, whereas sedation was classified according to the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) standard sedation scales as deep sedation [10].

PPH was defined in accordance with the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) as a cumulative blood loss of ≥ 1,000 mL or blood loss accompanied by signs and symptoms of hypovolemia within 24 h after birth, irrespective of the mode of delivery. It is classified as primary PPH (within 24 h) or secondary PPH (24 h–12 weeks postpartum) [11].

Statistical analysis

Statistical data processing of the first stage included checking the series of numerical and quantitative indicators for the significance of differences with the normal distribution law using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, as well as descriptive statistics methods, the choice of which for each indicator was determined by the results of the above test. If the data did not have a statistically significant deviation of the sample distribution from the normal level of the probability distribution, the mean (M) and standard deviation (± sd) were used to represent the data. If the study data had a statistically significant deviation of the sample distribution from the normal level of the probability distribution, medians (Me) and interquartile ranges (Q1; Q3) were used to present the data. To compare the groups, statistical hypotheses were formulated about the difference between the samples in general, which were tested using the parametric Student's t test and the nonparametric U-Mann–Whitney test in the case of two compared groups. Relative risk (RR, 95% CI, NNT) was determined to assess the difference in events between groups. The significance level of accepting the hypothesis about the significance of the difference between the means or other statistical parameters, as well as the significance of effects or correlation coefficients, was taken equal to 0.05 with the power of the criterion at the level of 0.80. All data obtained in the study were processed using IBM SPSS software version 30.0.0.0 (172) (IBM SPSS Statistics).

Results

During the study period, there were 130,338 vaginal deliveries at our institution. Of these, 1,366 women (1.05%) required MROP in the operating room and were included in the study. The median maternal age was 29 years (19–47), with a median parity of 3 (1–14) and a median gestational age of 39 weeks (23–42). The majority of pregnancies were singleton (99.1%, n = 1,354), while multiple gestations accounted for 0.9% of cases.

The majority of deliveries were spontaneous vaginal deliveries (n = 1,170), accounting for 85.7% of cases, of which 5.7% (n = 67) were instrumental (vacuum-assisted) deliveries. Induced deliveries accounted for 196 cases (14.3% of the total), with 16.3% (n = 32) of these being instrumental.

The overall failure rate of neuraxial anesthesia was 1.3% (n = 15), while 9.9% of cases required multiple attempts. No cases of high or total spinal anesthesia were reported. Local anesthetic (LA) alone was used in 19.7% of neuraxial anesthesia cases, whereas LA combined with fentanyl was the most common regimen, accounting for 79.1% of cases. Combinations of LA and morphine were used in 0.5% of cases, and LA combined with fentanyl and morphine was administered in eight cases (0.7%).

Among cases managed with general anesthesia, rapid sequence induction (RSI) was performed using succinylcholine in 176 cases (77.5% of general anesthesia cases), rocuronium in 36 cases (15.8%), and a combination of succinylcholine followed by maintenance with rocuronium in 13 cases (5.7%). Atracurium was used in 2 cases (0.9%).

Difficult intubation was encountered in three women (1.32%) of the general anesthesia cases (3 out of 227 cases), and one case of aspiration was reported accounting for 0.44% of the general anesthesia cases (1 out of 227 cases).

PPH occurred in 447 cases (32.7%). The primary cause was perineal tears, accounting for 278 cases (62.2%), followed by uterine atony in 40 cases (8.9%). Severe hemorrhage complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) was observed in two patients.

Among perineal tears, grade 1 and grade 2 injuries were observed in 154 (55.4%) and 116 (41.7%) cases, respectively, while severe tears (grade 3 or higher) occurred in eight women (2.9%). Episiotomy was performed in 131 cases (9.6%). Uterine rupture was diagnosed in nine cases, and endometritis was reported in three cases.

PPH was managed with uterotonics in all cases. A Bakri balloon was inserted in 14 cases (1%), and no hysterectomies were performed.

The consumption of blood products for total cases was as follows: red blood cell (RBC) transfusions were administered in 166 (12.4%) of cases, fresh frozen plasma (FFP) in 39 (2.9%), platelets in 15 (1.1%), and cryoprecipitate in 21 (1.5%).

Further monitoring and postoperative care were required for 7.5% (n = 102) of the parturients. A total of 92 parturients (6.7%) required overnight observation in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU), while nine were admitted to the ICU. One parturient initially monitored in the PACU was subsequently transferred to the ICU. The relative risk of requiring additional monitoring was 2.69 (95% CI: 1.8–3.9, p < 0.0001) in the neuraxial anesthesia group, and increased to 20.9 (95% CI: 7.1–61.5, p < 0.0001) in the general anesthesia group.

Discussion

This study presents an analysis of anesthesia practices and maternal outcomes in women undergoing MROP following retained placenta after labor. Our findings underscore the predominant use of neuraxial anesthesia, delineate the relative risks associated with general anesthesia, and highlight the implications for maternal morbidity.

Anesthesia practices and outcomes

The findings of our study, showing that neuraxial anesthesia was the predominant modality in 81.9% of cases, are consistent with prior reports. This preference aligns with existing evidence supporting neuraxial techniques in obstetric patients, given their favorable safety profile, lower risk of airway-related complications, and superior maternal hemodynamic stability [8, 9].

Although general anesthesia was used in only 16.6% of cases, it was associated with a significantly higher risk of complications. For general anesthesia RSI predominantly employed succinylcholine (n = 212) over rocuronium (n = 36), with a subset of patients receiving both agents. The preference for succinylcholine reflects its rapid onset, short duration of action, and widespread historical use for RSI, preceding the broader adoption of sugammadex for neuromuscular blockade reversal. Reversal of rocuronium-induced neuromuscular blockade was achieved using sugammadex in 73.4% of cases and neostigmine in 6.1% of cases.

Among cases managed with general anesthesia, RSI was the predominant technique, whereas sedation without airway protection was rarely employed. The increased risk of aspiration in pregnant patients necessitating endotracheal intubation may contribute to the very low utilization of sedation without airway protection (1.5%) observed at our institution.

A total of 10 women were not extubated immediately following their procedure due to complications or hemodynamic instability and required continued monitoring. Difficult intubation occurred in three cases, and one case of aspiration was reported. These findings underscore the well-recognized challenges of airway management in pregnant women, including anatomical changes and increased aspiration risk, particularly in the third trimester and post-delivery. The observed rate of difficult intubation in our cohort aligns with previously reported rates in obstetric populations (approximately 2.0%) [9] and 0.45% (1 in 224) [12]. Our institution emphasizes meticulous obstetric airway management and routinely employs video-assisted laryngoscopy, which has been demonstrated in the literature to improve first-attempt intubation success rates [13, 14]. Notably, the incidence of aspiration in our cohort (0.44%) appears higher than reported estimates in the literature (0.1%) [8, 9].

Maternal morbidity and blood product utilization

According to the literature, RBC transfusion rates range from 1.14% to 1.5% in general PPH cases and can reach up to 33.7% in cases involving retained placenta [15, 16]. In the present study, the RBC transfusion rate was 12.4%. Notably, this is the first study to compare transfusion rates between general anesthesia and neuraxial anesthesia in the context of retained placenta. Women receiving general anesthesia demonstrated a substantially higher need for blood transfusions, with a relative risk (RR) of 6.0 for RBC transfusion and 19.0 for FFP transfusion. Platelet and cryoprecipitate administration were also more frequent in general anesthesia cases. These findings likely reflect the management of more severe PPH cases requiring general anesthesia due to hemodynamic instability, greater intraoperative blood loss, and impaired uterine tone compared with neuraxial techniques. Prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) was not utilized in any cases in this cohort, and its routine role in PPH management remains poorly established [17]. Furthermore, the availability and application of TEG in the management of retained placenta and postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) is of significant importance, as it facilitates a more evidence-based and targeted administration of blood products [18].

Importantly, the observed association between general anesthesia and adverse maternal outcomes should be interpreted in the context of confounding by indication. In contemporary obstetric practice, general anesthesia is often selected when rapid source control is required in the setting of hemodynamic instability and/or ongoing major hemorrhage, or when neuraxial anesthesia is contraindicated or unsuccessful. Accordingly, the higher rates of transfusion, prolonged hospitalization, and intensive monitoring may reflect baseline clinical severity rather than a direct causal effect of the anesthetic technique. Our findings therefore support a preference for neuraxial anesthesia when feasible, while emphasizing the need for optimized general anesthesia pathways for the sickest patients.

Postoperative monitoring and length of stay

Our findings underscore the increased need for postoperative monitoring in patients receiving general anesthesia for MROP. The relative risk of requiring extended monitoring was significantly higher in the general anesthesia group compared with the neuraxial anesthesia group. According to the literature, average hospital stay varies with the type of PPH: women with non-atonic PPH have an average stay of 3.67 days, those with atonic PPH 2.98 days, and women without PPH 2.63 days [19]. In our cohort, patients receiving general anesthesia had a longer mean hospital stay (4.1 ± 2.3 days) compared with those receiving neuraxial anesthesia (3.5 ± 3.4 days), likely reflecting increased transfusion requirements and the need for extended postoperative care. These findings reinforce existing evidence that general anesthesia in obstetric patients is associated with increased morbidity and higher resource utilization.

Clinical implications and future directions

These results highlight the critical importance of optimizing anesthesia choices to minimize maternal risk. Given the increased complications associated with general anesthesia, its use should be reserved for cases in which neuraxial anesthesia is contraindicated or unsuccessful.

While the aspiration signal warrants attention, it is based on a single event (1/227) and therefore carries substantial statistical uncertainty; nonetheless, it highlights the importance of continuous quality improvement in obstetric general anesthesia for MROP. Institutional strategies to further mitigate aspiration risk may include reinforcement of a standardized RSI checklist (team role assignment, left uterine displacement and head-up positioning when feasible), consistent pharmacologic aspiration prophylaxis, default use of video laryngoscopy, clear escalation pathways for difficult airway management, and regular multidisciplinary simulation/audit of obstetric airway events. Ongoing prospective surveillance of aspiration and airway complications will be important to evaluate the impact of these measures.

Future research should aim to refine general anesthesia protocols in obstetric patients and explore strategies to reduce transfusion requirements. Prospective studies comparing outcomes of general anesthesia with newer anesthetic techniques may provide valuable insights for improving the safety and efficacy of obstetric anesthesia management.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the timing of the procedure (daytime, nighttime, or on-call) was not considered, which may influence staff availability, anesthesia decision-making, and maternal outcomes. Second, the severity of bleeding and the need for general anesthesia were not stratified by the interval from delivery to intervention, potentially underestimating the impact of early intervention. Third, patient refusal of neuraxial anesthesia was not captured as a factor contributing to the use of general anesthesia. Additionally, residual confounding by indication represents a key limitation. We lacked sufficiently granular pre-induction clinical data (e.g., contemporaneous hemodynamic status, quantified blood loss prior to anesthesia, and urgency metrics) to fully adjust for baseline severity that likely influenced the decision to proceed with general anesthesia. Consequently, comparisons between anesthetic techniques should be viewed as associative rather than causal. Finally, the study did not distinguish between manual lysis and placental revision procedures, which may represent clinically distinct entities, with manual lysis potentially associated with greater blood loss and a higher likelihood of requiring general anesthesia.

Conclusion

This study highlights the increased peripartum anesthetic and obstetric risks associated with general anesthesia in obstetric patients undergoing MROP in the operating room, particularly regarding blood transfusion requirements, postoperative monitoring, and prolonged hospitalization. The findings reinforce the preferential use of neuraxial anesthesia whenever feasible, including the use of conversion of labor epidural analgesia to surgical anesthesia in parturients at high risk for retained placenta. Furthermore, the results underscore the ongoing need to enhance the safety of general anesthesia in obstetric practice. Strategies such as improved airway management, optimized transfusion protocols, and the exploration of alternative anesthetic approaches will be essential to further improve maternal outcomes.
