Large Language Model Chatbot Conversations vs Public Health Materials and Parental HPV Vaccination Intentions
Authors: Neil K. R. Sehgal, Sunny Rai, Manuel Tonneau, Anish K. Agarwal, Joseph Cappella, Melanie L. Kornides, Lyle Ungar, Alison Buttenheim, Sharath Chandra Guntuku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionDo brief conversations with a large language model (LLM) chatbot increase parental intention to vaccinate children against human papillomavirus (HPV) more than standard public health materials?FindingsIn this randomized clinical trial of 1297 parents of unvaccinated children, brief LLM chatbot interactions significantly increased HPV vaccination intent compared with no intervention but did not outperform public health materials, and their effects did not persist by 45 days.MeaningThis study’s results suggest that brief LLM chatbot conversations may increase short-term vaccination intentions but offer limited advantage over existing public health messaging for promoting HPV vaccination.

Introduction

Vaccination against human papillomavirus (HPV) prevents approximately 90% of HPV-associated cancers, including cervical and oropharyngeal cancers.1 Despite its high effectiveness, the vaccine remains significantly underused globally.2 US adolescent vaccination rates have stagnated at approximately 60%, far behind other adolescent immunizations, such as for tetanus and meningococcal disease.3 In addition to structural barriers, such as cost and access, parental hesitancy is one of the key barriers toward improving coverage.4,5

In response, health systems and public agencies are increasingly exploring artificial intelligence (AI)–based tools to support vaccine communication.6,7 Large language model (LLM)–based chatbots have garnered substantial attention for their potential to shape beliefs, attitudes, and decision-making by generating highly persuasive messaging. Early studies across domains such as political persuasion, misinformation correction, and health communication have found that LLMs can change beliefs and attitudes, often more effectively than humans and at scale.8,9,10,11,12,13,14,15,16 However, many existing studies8,9,12,17 rely on weak or minimal control conditions and focus on immediate attitudinal outcomes rather than behaviorally relevant end points or more distal outcomes.

Evidence for chatbot-based vaccine promotion is similarly mixed. Some studies report short-term improvements in health intentions when chatbots are compared with no intervention or brief informational controls, whereas others show limited or no effects on downstream behaviors.18,19 Recent randomized clinical trials in pneumococcal vaccination and HIV self-testing in China have reported improvements in behavioral outcomes, such as vaccine uptake or testing completion.20,21 However, both trials20,21 evaluated multicomponent or service-delivery chatbots—hybrid or rule-based systems rather than LLMs—embedded within structured health service workflows. These systems differ substantially from brief, one-time LLM interactions, and it is unclear how they perform relative to high-quality public health materials that reflect current practice.

HPV vaccination provides a useful test case for evaluating the value of LLM-based communication. Unlike COVID-19 vaccination, HPV vaccination is less politically polarized and involves a finite dosing schedule rather than recurring boosters, simplifying the interpretation of parental intentions.22

In this randomized clinical trial, we tested whether a brief, 3-minute, multiturn interaction with an LLM-driven chatbot increases parental intentions to vaccinate their child against HPV, relative to both no intervention and government public health materials. We tested 2 chatbot variants: a default LLM output style and a more conversational style. To assess durability, we measured vaccination intentions immediately after the intervention and again at 15 and 45 days, and we examined self-reported vaccination and related behavioral outcomes. This design allowed us to evaluate whether LLM chatbots offer meaningful advantages over existing public health resources for HPV vaccine promotion.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

We conducted a randomized clinical trial to evaluate whether brief interactions with an LLM chatbot could increase parental intention to vaccinate their child against HPV. Parents residing in the US, Canada, or the UK were recruited through 2 online platforms known for providing high-quality respondent samples (Prolific and CloudResearch Connect) between March 3 and May 25, 2025.23 Eligible participants were 18 years or older and had at least 1 child eligible for HPV vaccination (ages 11-17 years in the US and Canada and ages 12-17 years in the UK) who had not received any doses of the HPV vaccine or who were unsure of their child’s vaccination status. Participants were compensated at a rate equivalent to US $18 per hour. The trial protocol is given in Supplement 1.

The study consisted of a baseline survey (survey 1) followed by an intervention and immediate postintervention assessment approximately 1 week later (survey 2), with follow-up surveys administered 15 and 45 days after the intervention. This approach aimed to reduce demand characteristics and expectancy bias.

The baseline survey collected data on demographics, child vaccination history, trust in public health institutions and AI, general vaccine hesitancy using the 5-item Parent Attitudes about Childhood Vaccines (PACV-5) scale,24 and self-reported likelihood of vaccinating their child for HPV, influenza, and COVID-19 within the next 12 months (0- to 100-point scale, with 0 indicating extremely unlikely and 100 indicating extremely likely). To identify a consistent index child, participants were first asked to provide the first initial of their youngest HPV vaccine-eligible child. All subsequent child-specific questions referred to this index child using the initial provided. Reasons for HPV nonvaccination were adapted from the US National Immunization Survey–Teen (NIS-Teen); participants rated all reasons endorsed by more than 1% of respondents on the 2018 NIS-Teen on a 0- to 100-point scale, where 0 was marked not important at all and 100 was marked extremely important, in addition to an open-ended other response option.25 We separated the NIS-Teen category more information/new vaccine into 2 items (needing more information and the vaccine being too new) and did not include a direct analogue of the broader lack of knowledge category, which was considered conceptually overlapping with these informational items. These items were analyzed as individual indicators rather than as a composite scale.

All participants provided informed consent electronically via Qualtrics before participation. The study was approved by the University of Pennsylvania institutional review board, preregistered at AsPredicted, and retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov. The preregistration specified the primary outcome (postintervention HPV vaccination intent), follow-up outcomes (intent at 15 and 45 days and vaccination uptake), key secondary outcomes, exclusion criteria (attention check failure and implausibly short completion time), and the primary regression–based analytic approach. The ClinicalTrials.gov record was created after study completion and reflects the same primary and secondary outcomes and study design; no primary or secondary outcomes were added or modified, and no additional exclusion criteria were introduced. As described in the Statistical Analysis section, the primary analysis deviated from the preregistered exclusion criteria by retaining all randomized participants in an intention-to-treat (ITT) framework and handling missing data via multiple imputation. The overall study flow and timing of surveys, interventions, and follow-ups are shown in eFigure 1 in Supplement 2. This study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guidelines for randomized clinical trials.

Randomization and Interventions

Participants who completed survey 1 and met the eligibility criteria were randomly assigned to 1 of 4 conditions: (1) no-message control, (2) government public health materials (strong control), (3) LLM chatbot with default response style, or (4) LLM chatbot with a conversational response style. In the strong control condition, participants read government-issued HPV vaccination materials matched to their country of residence (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, National Health Service, or Public Health Agency of Canada).1,26,27,28 Materials ranged from 589 to 680 words and were displayed for a minimum of 3 minutes before participants were able to proceed. Readability of the materials corresponded to grade 9.3 (US), 8.3 (Canada), and 6.1 (UK) based on Flesch-Kincaid Grade Levels. Full stimuli are provided in eAppendix 1 in Supplement 2.

In the chatbot conditions, participants engaged in a 3-minute minimum, multiturn conversation with an LLM-based chatbot implemented using OpenAI’s GPT-4o (gpt-4o-2024-11-20) model. Chatbots were instructed to encourage parents to ensure their child receives the HPV vaccine and were provided each participant’s top-rated reason for HPV nonvaccination from the baseline survey. The default chatbot used the model’s standard output style (ie, often using paragraphs and bullet points), whereas the conversational chatbot was instructed to respond in a conversational tone using no more than 3 sentences per message. Prior work finds that informal, conversational cues can boost a chatbot’s social presence or the feeling of interacting with a real person, which is associated with greater engagement, trust, and perceived interaction quality, and may increase receptivity to persuasive health messages.29,30 No fine-tuning, rule-based scripting, or additional training data were used. A 3-minute session length was chosen to mirror realistic, practical constraints for public health use cases. Full chatbot instruction prompts and example exchanges are provided in eTables 1 and 2 in Supplement 2. Examples of the chatbot interface are provided in eFigures 2 and 3 in Supplement 2.

Outcomes

The primary preregistered outcome was self-reported likelihood of vaccinating the participant’s child against HPV within the next 12 months, measured immediately after the intervention on a 0- to 100-point scale, with 0 indicating extremely unlikely and 100 indicating extremely likely. Preregistered follow-up outcomes included HPV vaccination intent at 15 and 45 days after intervention as well as HPV vaccination uptake. Parents who reported that their child had received the HPV vaccine at follow-up were routed past the vaccination intent item by survey skip logic and assigned a follow-up intent score of 100, reflecting behavioral resolution of the outcome. Other preregistered secondary outcomes included general parental vaccine hesitancy (PACV-5), intentions to vaccinate the child against influenza and COVID-19 in the next 12 months, willingness to donate a portion of study compensation to an immunization-related charity, and click-through to a pharmacy vaccine scheduling link (US participants only). Participants in the intervention arms also rated perceived empathy, usefulness, and relevance, as well as message effectiveness (adapted from an existing public health message effectiveness scale)25 of the material or conversation.

Statistical Analysis

The primary analysis followed an ITT framework and included all randomized participants. The primary estimand was the effect of assigned study arm on immediate postintervention HPV vaccination intent.

Continuous intent outcomes were analyzed using analysis of covariance–style linear regression with HC2 robust SEs, adjusting for baseline HPV intent. Standardized effect sizes (Cohen d) were calculated by dividing adjusted arm contrasts by the pooled residual SD. Binary outcomes were analyzed using logistic regression with baseline HPV intent adjustment, and we report adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with marginal risk differences.

Missing postbaseline outcomes were handled using multiple imputation with 50 imputed datasets and predictive mean matching for bounded continuous outcomes. The imputation model included assigned arm, baseline HPV vaccination intent, baseline COVID-19 and influenza vaccination intent, baseline PACV-5 hesitancy score, country, recruitment platform, age, gender, race and ethnicity (provided by the Prolific/Connect platform), educational level, baseline child HPV vaccination uncertainty, postrandomization survey-completion flags, child HPV vaccination status at each follow-up wave, and observed postrandomization outcomes (immediate, 15-day, and 45-day intent; process evaluation ratings). Race and ethnicity categories included Asian, Black, White, multiracial, and other (selected other on the Prolific recruitment platform or other or prefer not to say in the Connect panel).

Because intent was measured on a bounded 0- to 100-point scale, we examined arm-specific distributions, residual vs fitted plots, Q-Q plots, and influence diagnostics, and we fit fractional-logit sensitivity models after rescaling intent to the unit interval. We also evaluated differential attrition by arm using χ2 tests and logistic regression, compared baseline characteristics of completers vs noncompleters using standardized mean differences, and fit inverse probability-weighted sensitivity models for the primary outcome.

Regression diagnostics were conducted to assess model assumptions, including Breusch-Pagan and White tests for heteroskedasticity, and D’Agostino-Pearson and Shapiro-Wilk tests for residual normality. Breusch-Pagan and White tests indicated heteroskedasticity (P < .05 for both), confirming the appropriateness of HC2-robust SEs. As an additional sensitivity analysis for the bounded 0- to 100-point scale, fractional logistic regression was estimated for the primary outcome contrast (default chatbot vs no-message control); inverse probability weighting was also estimated to cross-validate the primary ordinary least-squares approach.

We performed an exploratory analysis of whether treatment effects on immediate postintervention HPV vaccination intent differed across baseline participant characteristics. Using the randomized ITT cohort and the same multiple-imputation framework as the primary analysis, we fit separate linear models for each baseline moderator that included study arm, baseline HPV vaccination intent, the moderator main effect, and arm × moderator interaction terms. For categorical moderators, interaction effects were evaluated with omnibus Wald tests across all interaction terms for that moderator; P values were adjusted for multiple testing across moderators using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure.

Our preregistered analysis plan specified exclusion of participants who failed embedded attention checks or whose survey completion times were implausibly short. In the primary analysis reported in this article, we instead retain all randomized participants in an ITT framework, handling missing outcome data from noncompleters via multiple imputation. This approach constitutes a minor deviation from the preregistered exclusion criteria for attention checks and completion time; these flags are retained as descriptive covariates rather than exclusion criteria. Beyond the preregistered criteria, we also tracked several additional post hoc data quality indicators, including reCAPTCHA scores, child initial mismatches across survey waves, technical issues, and nonentry into survey 2, which informed a complete-case sensitivity analysis (eTable 5 in Supplement 2) but were not applied as exclusions in the primary ITT analysis. For these participants, we also conducted exploratory analyses examining the conversational and persuasive strategies used by the chatbots and their associations with postintervention vaccination intent (details in eAppendix 1 in Supplement 2).

Statistical analysis was performed using Python, version 3.10 (Python Software Foundation). All tests were 2-sided, with P < .05 considered statistically significant. Additional methodological details, including screening criteria and exploratory content analyses, are provided in the eAppendix 1 in Supplement 2.

Results

Participant Characteristics and Study Flow

A total of 1297 participants (mean [SD] age, 42.84 [6.93] years; 935 [72.1%] female and 357 [27.5%] male; 39 [3.0%] Asian, 129 [9.9%] Black, 1043 [80.4%] White, 48 [3.7%] multiracial, and 38 [2.9%] other) were randomized: 324 to no message, 325 to public health materials, 324 to the default chatbot, and 324 to the conversational chatbot (eFigure 4 in Supplement 2). Baseline characteristics were well balanced across conditions (Table 1). Mean (SD) baseline likelihood of vaccinating the index child against HPV was 27.4 (34.23) on a 0- to 100-point scale, reflecting substantial baseline hesitancy.

Intervention Fidelity

All participants assigned to the public health materials condition who provided an observed response (285 of 325 [87.7%]) reported reading the materials; 278 of 285 (97.5%) reported reading them carefully. Participants in the chatbot conditions engaged actively, sending a mean (SD) of 63.2 (43.4) words during the interaction, whereas the default and conversational chatbots generated means (SDs) of 820.6 (329.1) and 372.6 (162.4) words, respectively (eTable 3 in Supplement 2). Interactions with the conversational chatbot involved more frequent but shorter exchanges than those with the default chatbot.

Primary Outcome: Immediate HPV Vaccination Intent

Across all conditions, HPV vaccination intent increased by a mean (SD) of 9.2 (25.0) points from baseline to the immediate postintervention assessment. Relative to the no intervention control, all active interventions increased immediate vaccination intent (Figure and Table 2; eTable 3 in Supplement 2). Government public health materials produced the largest increase (Cohen d = 0.53; 95% CI, 0.36-0.70). The default chatbot also increased intent (d = 0.48; 95% CI, 0.30-0.65), whereas the conversational chatbot yielded a smaller effect (d = 0.33; 95% CI, 0.17-0.49). Results were qualitatively similar when restricting to a complete-case sample (n = 930) applying both preregistered quality screens (attention check failure and implausibly short completion time) and additional post hoc data integrity criteria (eTable 5 in Supplement 2). No significant moderation by participant demographics or baseline beliefs was detected (eTable 6 in Supplement 2).

Attrition

Attrition at the immediate postintervention time point differed significantly across arms (χ2 = 14.97; P = .002). Both chatbot arms showed significantly lower odds of providing primary outcome data relative to no message (default chatbot: OR, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.32-0.78; P = .002; conversational chatbot: OR, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.31-0.76; P = .002), whereas the public health arm did not differ significantly from no message (OR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.49-1.28; P = .34). The primary driver of differential attrition in chatbot arms was participants who entered the intervention survey but did not complete the chatbot interaction and subsequent intent item: 39 participants in the default chatbot arm and 33 in the conversational chatbot arm entered the intervention survey but did not provide primary outcome data compared with 11 in the no-message arm and 9 in the public health arm.

Noncompleters had a slightly higher mean (SD) PACV-5 hesitancy score at baseline (3.57 [0.98]) compared with completers (3.41 [1.04]; standardized mean difference, −0.16; P = .04), suggesting marginally greater baseline hesitancy among those lost to the immediate follow-up. Baseline mean (SD) HPV intent (27.57 [34.63] vs 26.55 [32.02]; P = .68) and age (42.81 [6.97] vs 43.01 [6.75]; P = .71) did not differ significantly. Overall, observed baseline differences were modest in magnitude, consistent with, although not definitive evidence for, a missing-at-random assumption (eTable 8 in Supplement 2).

Follow-Up Outcomes at 15 and 45 Days

At 15 days after intervention, both the conversational chatbot and public health materials increased HPV vaccination intent relative to the no-message control (Figure and Table 2). In contrast, the default chatbot did not differ from the no-message control. No intervention increased self-reported HPV vaccination at 15 days (Table 3). By 45 days, only the public health materials increased HPV vaccination intent relative to the no-message control (Cohen d = 0.17; 95% CI, 0.06-0.28) (Figure and Table 2). No intervention increased self-reported HPV vaccination uptake at 45 days (Table 3). Effects at day 15 and day 45 were robust to a sensitivity analysis excluding participants whose child had been vaccinated by follow-up (n = 63 at day 15; n = 82 at day 45), who were assigned an intent score of 100 in the primary analysis (eTable 9 in Supplement 2).

Secondary Outcomes

None of the interventions produced spillover effects on influenza or COVID-19 vaccination intentions, general parental vaccine hesitancy (PACV-5), willingness to donate to an immunization-related charity, or click rates on a vaccine scheduling link (US participants only) (Table 2 and Table 3). Across 13 prespecified reasons for nonvaccination, 4 were decreased by at least 1 chatbot or the public health reading material (eFigure 5 in Supplement 1). All active interventions significantly decreased the mean score across the 13 reasons relative to the no-message control.

Participants rated the default chatbot as more empathetic than the public health materials. The conversational chatbot was rated as less effective than the public health materials on an existing message effectiveness scale. Participants spent more surplus time with the default chatbot than with the public health materials (mean [SD], 123.9 [201.0] vs 55.4 [96.7] seconds; mean difference, 68.5 seconds; 95% CI, 41.7 to 95.3), despite the absence of larger or more durable effects (eTable 10 in Supplement 2).

Exploratory analyses examined the conversational and persuasive strategies used by the chatbots and their associations with postintervention vaccination intent among the per-protocol sample. Details are provided in the eAppendix 2 in Supplement 2, including principal component and regularized regression analyses, comparisons with public health materials, and evaluations of adherence to motivational interviewing principles (eTables 11-16 and eFigures 6 and 7 in Supplement 2).

Discussion

In this randomized clinical trial, 3-minute conversations with an LLM-based chatbot increased parental intentions to vaccinate their child against HPV immediately after exposure but did not produce durable effects and did not outperform existing government public health materials. In contrast, public health materials yielded modest but durable increases in vaccination intent through 45 days. None of the interventions increased self-reported HPV vaccination or related behavioral outcomes.

The absence of vaccination uptake effects across all arms reflects a combination of factors. HPV vaccination requires scheduling and may involve a pediatrician visit. In addition, 45 days may be too short for intent to convert to action; even among parents whose intentions shifted, structural barriers, such as access and competing priorities, remained unaddressed. Lastly, recruitment fell modestly short of our prespecified sample size, which may have reduced statistical power to detect effects at follow-up time points and for rare binary outcomes, such as self-reported vaccination uptake. This underpowering may have limited our ability to detect true differences at 45 days, and results at that time point should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Still, our findings suggest that, at least in their current form and with brief exposure, LLM chatbots may not provide meaningful advantages over established public health communication resources for promoting HPV vaccination. Although chatbots offer interactive and personalized exchanges, these features did not translate into greater or more persistent changes in vaccination intentions when compared with existing well-designed, evidence-based materials.

Two recent HPV-specific chatbot trials19,31 provide a useful contrast. In a US randomized trial,19 brief interactions with a GPT-4–based chatbot increased parental HPV vaccination intentions relative to a Centers for Disease Control and Prevention brochure, although parents spent substantially more time with the chatbot than with the control materials and outcomes were assessed within the same survey session. In a separate school-based cluster trial in China,31 parents given 2 weeks of on-demand access to an LLM-powered chatbot were substantially more likely to schedule or obtain HPV vaccination for their children compared with no intervention. In contrast, our study required equivalent minimum exposure across conditions, used existing public health materials as a strong comparator, introduced a delay between baseline and intervention assessment, and evaluated durability during 45 days. These design differences may help explain why we observed smaller and less persistent effects of chatbot-based communication.

The lack of durable effects is consistent with a broader literature showing that brief informational or persuasive interventions often struggle to produce lasting changes in vaccine-related decisions, particularly when structural barriers and contextual factors remain unaddressed.32 Although HPV vaccination is less politically polarized than COVID-19 vaccination, parental decisions still involve logistical considerations, competing priorities, and trust in medical institutions.4 These factors may not be readily shifted through short conversational encounters alone. This pattern is consistent with recent work in cancer screening, in which an LLM-based chatbot increased willingness to complete stool-based colorectal cancer screening but did not affect intentions to undergo colonoscopy, a more invasive and logistically demanding procedure.18

Exploratory analyses of chatbot conversations, reported in eAppendix 2 in Supplement 2, offer tentative insights into how different messaging approaches may relate to short-term changes in intention. Strategies emphasizing external validation and collective benefits were associated with higher postintervention intent, whereas more personalized empathy-focused exchanges were associated with lower intent. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because conversational content was shaped by participant responses and cannot be disentangled from underlying motivation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Vaccination intentions and uptake were self-reported and may be subject to social desirability or recall bias. Participants were recruited from online platforms, so findings may not generalize to all parents with low HPV vaccination intent, particularly those with limited digital access or more entrenched hesitancy. The sample was also predominantly White and female, which limits conclusions about whether chatbot- or materials-based communication would perform differently across other demographic groups. Parents encountered in clinic-based settings may differ meaningfully from online panel respondents in their baseline motivation, institutional trust, and receptivity to health messaging. Furthermore, the sample skewed toward moderate-to-high AI familiarity, and parents with lower digital literacy may engage with chatbot-based tools differently in ways that could attenuate or amplify the effects observed in this study. In addition, participants were required to engage with the chatbots or read the public health materials for at least 3 minutes, which may exceed feasible engagements. However, parents who naturally encounter chatbots might be more motivated to engage longer, possibly producing greater benefits than observed here. In fact, participants chose to spend a mean of 70 seconds more with the default chatbot than with the public health material. It bears emphasizing that shifts on a continuous 0- to 100-point intention scale do not necessarily translate into meaningful behavior change; the absence of vaccination uptake effects across all arms is the more behaviorally consequential finding, and future work should prioritize behavioral end points over attitudinal ones.

Despite these limitations, our findings have practical implications for clinicians, health systems, and public health agencies considering the deployment of AI-based communication tools. High-quality, evidence-based public health materials performed at least as well as brief chatbot interactions and demonstrated greater durability. Chatbots may still serve as a supplementary resource, particularly for addressing questions or reinforcing existing guidance, but should not blindly be used in place of established communication strategies.

Conclusions

In this randomized clinical trial of parents with low HPV vaccination intent, brief interactions with an LLM-based chatbot increased intentions immediately after exposure but did not produce durable effects or outperform existing public health materials. Future research should explore optimal strategies for integrating personalized AI interactions alongside structural interventions, ultimately aiming for a multifaceted approach to address deep-rooted vaccine hesitancy effectively. Rigorous comparisons against realistic control conditions and assessments of durability will be essential as AI tools are increasingly incorporated into public health practice.
