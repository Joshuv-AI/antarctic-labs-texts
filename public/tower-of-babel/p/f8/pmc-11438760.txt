Knowledge-map and research trends of circulating tumor cells in breast cancer: a scientometric analysis
Authors: Jinan Shi, Yin Duan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAssessing circulating tumor cells (CTCs) in early-stage breast cancer patients can help identify relapse risk for timely interventions. Molecular analysis of CTCs can reveal vulnerabilities for personalized treatment options in metastatic breast cancer. This study aims to summarize CTCs in breast cancer research understanding and evaluate research trends. Extracted from the Web of Science Core Collection, publications on CTCs in breast cancer studies spanning from January 1, 2008, to December 21, 2023, were included. Co-authorships, references, and keywords were analyzed using Bibliometrix R packages and VOSviewer software. References and keywords burst detection were conducted with CiteSpace, and BICOMB was utilized to generate high-frequency keyword layouts. Biclustering analysis of the binary co-keyword matrix was performed using gCLUTO. 1747 articles focusing on CTCs in breast cancer were identified. The USA and the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center demonstrated the highest productivity at the national and institutional levels, respectively. The journal “CANCERS” had the highest publication outputs on this subject. Pantel K emerged as the foremost author with the highest publication and co-citation counts. Analysis of co-keywords unveiled five prominent research areas concerning CTCs in breast cancer. The prognostic and predictive roles of CTCs in breast cancer have substantial implications for clinical practice. Nevertheless, precise assessment of CTCs, encompassing its quantities and attributes through advanced technologies, and its role in detecting minimal residual disease in breast cancer, continue to pose notable challenges. In conclusion, recent advancements and trends in CTCs research in breast cancer are examined through scientometric analysis in this study. The results provide valuable insights for the formulation of novel approaches in CTCs research, emphasizing the current research frontiers.Keywords: Breast cancer, Circulating tumor cells, Co-word analysis, Hotspots

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the second most common cancer worldwide and is the leading cause of cancer-related death in women [1, 2]. Despite technological and clinical progress, metastasis and its complications continue to be a leading cause of mortality in BC patients. Approximately 12% of BC patients suffer distant metastasis, which significantly decreases the 5-year survival rate to 25% [3, 4]. While endocrine therapy remains the standard treatment for hormone receptor (HR) positive BC, the risk of distant recurrence can reach up to 41% [5, 6]. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) patients exhibit significantly poorer clinical outcomes [7, 8]. Therefore, identifying patients at high risk of tumor metastasis is crucial for timely intervention.

Metastasis involves several stages beginning with the detachment of cancer cells from the primary tumor and their entry into the bloodstream, with systemic circulation serving as the critical juncture between primary and metastatic tumor sites [9]. The detection and isolation of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) is gaining promise as it offers a real-time, minimally invasive, cost-effective, and repeatable “liquid biopsy,” proven to prognostic outcomes for BC patients [10–12]. CTCs are viable cells that detach from either the primary tumor or metastatic lesions and enter the bloodstream to initiate distant metastasis [13]. The CellSearch® system, a pioneering assay approved by FDA, is capable of detecting CTCs in 2–55% of primary BC patients. However, its detection rate typically remains below 25% due to its reliance on immunomagnetic enrichment of epithelial CTCs [14, 15]. In early-stage breast cancer (EBC), CTCs are detectable in a significant number of patients and are generally considered surrogate markers for minimal residual disease (MRD) and potential precursors to metastatic disease. In metastatic breast cancer (MBC), CTCs may enhance diagnostic capabilities by reflecting both tumor burden and the predominant cell populations at metastatic sites. Over the past two decades, CTCs have been recognized as a robust prognostic factor in MBC, with data from clinical trials indicating their potential to inform treatment decisions [16, 17]. Notably, both the quantity and characteristics of CTCs offer crucial insights into disease progression and therapeutic response in BC. Consequently, substantial efforts have been made to develop protocols for detecting and characterizing CTCs via liquid biopsies. Currently, the predominant method for isolating CTCs involves capturing cells expressing the epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) on their surface [18]. The epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a critical process in tumor metastasis, often associated with EpCAM-based CTC capture. However, direct evidence supporting the presence of the EMT process in CTCs is still limited [19]. Besides EMT, the presence of a cancer stem cell (CSC) subpopulation within CTCs is another phenomenon warranting further investigation.

Despite many studies on CTCs in BC offering some insights, a comprehensive trend analysis in this field remains unperformed. This study aims to address the following research questions: What are the major trends and patterns in CTCs research in BC? Who are the key contributors (authors, institutions, and countries) to this field? What are the emerging hot topics and potential future directions in CTCs research for BC? Scientometrics employs mathematical and statistical approaches to quantitatively assess the research landscape, including various topics and trends by analyzing aspects like co-authorships, references, and keywords [20]. This study uses bibliometric techniques to conduct a thorough quantitative and qualitative assessment of CTCs research in BC over the past 15 years. The application of scientometric analysis is particularly suited to this field for several reasons. First, it allows for the identification of key research trends and emerging topics by analyzing publication patterns and citation networks. This is crucial in a rapidly evolving field like CTCs, where new findings and technologies frequently emerge. Second, scientometric methods facilitate the mapping of collaborative networks among researchers, institutions and countries/regions, highlighting influential contributors and fostering potential collaborations. Lastly, by examining the frequency and context of keywords, this analysis provides insights into the shifting focus of research, thereby guiding future investigations and funding priorities in breast cancer research.Through this comprehensive scientometric analysis, we aim to map the evolving landscape of CTCs research in BC, identify key contributors and emerging trends, and provide insights to guide future research directions in this critical field.

Materials and methods

Data source and search strategy

This study utilized the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) as its primary data source, a well-regarded digital repository frequently employed in bibliometric research. WoSCC is distinguished by its comprehensive coverage of high-quality, peer-reviewed journals across multiple disciplines, particularly relevant for our interdisciplinary study on CTCs in BC. Its stringent selection criteria ensure the inclusion of impactful publications, aligning with our research objectives. The database provides standardized metadata, crucial for accurate bibliometric analysis, enhancing the reliability of data extraction and the validity of our scientometric study. WoSCC’s robust citation tracking feature enables comprehensive analysis of citation networks and research impact, central to our study’s aims. Furthermore, its substantial historical depth allows for the examination of long-term research trends, providing valuable insights into the evolution of the field. The search strategy centered on the terms “breast cancer” and “circulating tumor cells,” employing numerous synonyms and related terms for comprehensive coverage: (TS=(“breast cancer*” OR “breast neoplasm*” OR “breast tumor*” OR “breast tumour*” OR “breast carcinoma*” OR “mammary cancer*” OR “mammary neoplasm*” OR “mammary tumor*” OR “mammary tumour*” OR “mammary carcinoma*”)) AND (TS=(“circulating tumor cell*” OR “circulating cancer cell*” OR “circulating carcinoma cell*” OR “circulating neoplasm cell*” OR “circulating tumour cell*”)).

A comprehensive search was conducted on March 26, 2024, to reduce biases from database updates, covering publications from January 1, 2008, to December 31, 2023. This timespan was carefully selected for several reasons: The year 2008 marked a significant advancement in CTCs research with the FDA approval of the CellSearch system for breast cancer prognosis, which revolutionized CTCs detection and analysis [15]. Following this technological milestone, there was a notable increase in breast cancer CTCs research, making 2008 a logical starting point to capture the evolving landscape of the field. This timeframe allows for a thorough analysis of the field’s development, including major breakthroughs, emerging trends, and shifts in research focus over a substantial period. Extending the analysis to the end of 2023 ensures the inclusion of the most recent developments, providing an up-to-date perspective on the field. The study was restricted to articles and reviews written in English to ensure consistency in the language analysis.

Data collection

The process of screening titles and abstracts for relevance was guided by predefined criteria focused on publications concerning CTCs in BC. Two researchers independently conducted the literature retrieval process using a systematic approach. To maintain consistency, both researchers regularly compared their findings during the screening process. Any discrepancies in the selection of articles were discussed in detail, allowing both researchers to articulate their reasoning. This collaborative discussion aimed to reach a consensus on the inclusion or exclusion of specific articles. Following the resolution of disagreements, a final list of included studies was compiled for full-text review and data extraction. This multi-step approach not only enhanced the rigor of the data retrieval process but also ensured that the final selection of articles was comprehensive and unbiased. The review involved analyzing each article’s title, publication date, author details, countries/regions, affiliations, journal, references, citation counts, and key keywords. Information was collected in “Plain Text File” format. Figure 1 clearly illustrates the detailed procedures for data collection and inclusion.

Bibliometric analysis

Bibliometric analysis employs quantitative methods to assess research outputs, uncover patterns, and identify trends in different publications. Utilizing bibliometric mapping tools for visual representations enables researchers to comprehensively evaluate the scientific landscape and forecast emerging trends and critical areas in specific fields. Below, we justify the selection of the primary tools used in our analysis.

VOSviewer VOSviewer aids in visualizing research profiles, references, and geographical data, illustrating productivity and impact across disciplines [21]. It supports a range of co-occurrence analyses including network, overlay, and density visualizations for entities such as countries, organizations, and authors. Employing VOSviewer 1.6.20, we analyzed collaboration networks among various countries, institutions and authors, generating a detailed graph where node sizes indicate publication volumes or citation counts. Nodes of different colors indicate specific clusters or time periods, with lines representing connections like collaborations or citations. The visualizations by VOSviewer are particularly valuable for addressing our research questions about international collaborations and evolving research themes in breast cancer CTC studies.

Biblioshiny Biblioshiny, an adaptable R-based tool, integrates seamlessly with various graphical and statistical functions. We imported raw data, including authors and cited references, into the R-4.2.3-based bibliometrics package Bibliometricx. Biblioshiny facilitated the extraction of key publication metrics, providing insights into total publications, annual scientific outputs, institutional impacts, author details, and word-cloud. This tool is particularly relevant for our research questions as it allows us to analyze annual scientific outputs and institutional impacts, thereby identifying trends over time and recognizing influential contributors in the field.

CiteSpace Developed in Java by Dr. Chaomei Chen, CiteSpace 6.3.R1 is a powerful bibliometric tool recognized for uncovering trends and key elements within specific research domains [22]. The burst analysis capability detects significant changes in subjects, offering crucial insights. In our research, burst analysis was conducted on cited references and keywords to emphasize foundational works. Furthermore, timeline visualization of keyword clusters was utilized to investigate the distribution and progression of subjects. This aligns with our research aim of uncovering the intellectual structure and evolution of the field over time.

HistCite Pro To identify key research findings in this area, we utilized HistCite Pro 2.1 to analyze the most frequently locally cited papers. This tool is crucial for recognizing seminal works that have shaped the field, which directly addresses our research question about influential studies in breast cancer.

BICOMB and gCLUTO Finally, using BICOMB 2.01, a text mining tool specialized in biomedicine, we extracted keywords from bibliographic databases to construct co-occurrence keyword matrices. The versatile graphical tool gCLUTO 1.0 was employed to cluster multi-dimensional datasets and analyze their characteristics. High-frequency keywords, extracted via BICOMB, were analyzed using gCLUTO’s co-word matrices to pinpoint research hotspots. Additionally, we created heat maps and mountain plots to visually depict our results. The combination of BICOMB for keyword extraction and gCLUTO for bi-clustering analysis provide a multidimensional view of the evolving research focus in CTCs.

The selection of these diverse yet complementary bibliometric tools enable us to comprehensively address our research questions regarding the evolution, current state, and future directions of CTCs research in BC. We can provide a multifaceted view of the field, offering valuable insights for researchers, clinicians, and policymakers in BC research.

Results

Main information and publication trends

By combining data from Biblioshiny and the “analyze results” tool from Web of Science, we assessed whether the outcomes met the comprehensive criteria. Between 2008 and 2023, our search strategy identified 1747 publications on CTCs in BC, including 1372 articles and 375 review papers, which we included in the final analysis. In total, these publications garnered 87,831 citations, of which 16,112 were self-citations. On average, these articles were published 6.83 years ago and received 50.28 citations each. Notably, 26.16% of the total publications involved international co-authorship, highlighting strong collaborations across countries and regions. Discussion

General information

Studies on CTCs in BC have demonstrated an association between CTCs and clinical outcomes in both early and advanced stages. Despite the abundance of literature, scientometric analyses and visual representations are still limited. This study offers the first comprehensive bibliometric evaluation to investigate research trends and emerging topics concerning CTCs in BC. We analyzed 1747 publications from 2008 to 2023, sourced from the Web of Science Core Collection, utilizing bibliometric tools (Biblioshiny, VOSviewer, CiteSpace) and biclustering software (BICOMB, gCLUTO). This approach facilitated the visualization of various dimensions, including annual trends, co-authorship of countries/regions and institutions, co-citation of authors, journals, and references, keywords usage. This scientometric analysis thus offers a unique and valuable perspective on the CTCs research landscape in BC, complementing traditional review methods and providing a data-driven foundation for future research directions.

From 2008 to 2023, there has been a consistent rise in the number of published articles in this field, indicating increasing interest and acknowledgment from the scientific community. This field has seen contributions from 9371 co-authors spanning 60 countries, with major contributions from the USA, China, and Germany. The USA remains the global leader in this research area, excelling in total publications, citation counts, and network link strength. Although China has significantly increased its publication output and ranks second globally, it still falls behind in generating collaborative high-impact research. Institutional analysis indicates that the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center leads among the top 12 institutions in terms of article count. Based on Bradford’s Law, 15 core journals contributing significantly to the field have been identified, with “CANCERS” publishing the most articles. “CLINICAL CANCER RESEARCH” was the most cited journal, while the “NEW ENGLAND JOURNAL OF MEDICINE” held the highest impact factor. This analysis provides essential guidance for researchers in selecting appropriate publication venues.

This study identified top authors by their publication count and H-index. Pantel K from University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf ranked highest, with Fehm T from Heinrich Heine University Dusseldorf and Cristofanilli M from Cornell University trailing closely. Yu M, affiliated with University of Southern California, authored two of the top 10 most cited articles both globally and locally. In 2013, Prof. Yu demonstrated the role of EMT in the hematogenous dissemination of human BC via CTCs, with findings published in SCIENCE [24]. In 2014, another article titled “Ex vivo culture of circulating breast tumor cells for individualized testing of drug susceptibility” by Yu on SCIENCE revealed novel mutations and potential therapeutic targets through the isolation, culture, and analysis of CTCs from estrogen receptor-positive (ER) BC patients [29]. Among the 5 isolated CTC lines, three demonstrated tumorigenic properties in murine models. Genomic analysis revealed mutations in the PIK3CA, ESR1, and FGFR2 genes. This approach offers significant promise for noninvasive monitoring and the optimization of personalized therapy in oncology.

Hotspots and trends

An integrated analysis of references and keywords unveils both current and prospective research trends. In a 2014 study by Bidard FC, titled “Clinical validity of circulating tumour cells in patients with metastatic breast cancer: a pooled analysis of individual patient data” [26], had the highest citation burst. This pooled analysis, incorporating data from 1,944 patients across 20 studies, confirmed that CTC count independently predicts progression-free and overall survival. Furthermore, incorporating CTC counts into comprehensive clinicopathological models improved the prognostic evaluation of MBC, unlike serum tumor markers. Yu M’s [24] study, titled “Circulating breast tumor cells exhibit dynamic changes in epithelial and mesenchymal composition,” was noted for its high citation rate and explored the significance of CTCs in EMT during the dissemination of BC cells [24]. Analysis of trending terms reveals a significant paradigm shift in comprehending CTCs in BC. This shift moves beyond merely observing CTCs characteristics to leveraging it for precision medicine applications. Recently, there has been a surge in keywords including “liquid biopsy”, “circulating tumor DNA”, “EMT”, “clusters”, “separation”, and “label-free isolation”. Researchers and clinicians now agree on the pivotal role of CTCs across different stages of BC, their influence on the tumor microenvironment, and the advancements in detection technologies. Co-occurrence and bicluster analyses of keywords have highlighted 5 main research topics, which highlight the multifaceted approach to studying CTCs in BC, encompassing detection methods, biological understanding, and clinical applications.

Cluster 0: CTCs in precision liquid biopsy of BC

Precision oncology has been greatly improved by recent progress in identifying and validating novel BC biomarkers. Liquid biopsy, such as analyzing blood or other bodily fluids, stands out as a significant illustration in identifying cellular and molecular alterations associated with BC [30, 31]. CTCs and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) are at the forefront among these developing biomarkers. Through the integration of cutting-edge molecular technologies and sophisticated bioinformatics procedures, liquid biopsy can reveal spatial intra-tumoral diversity and temporal molecular changes in distant metastatic tumors. This ability exceeds the limitations of traditional tissue biopsies, which frequently do not capture the complete spectrum of tumor cells [32–34]. Unlike ctDNA, CTCs, as intact cells, provide supplementary details such as protein profiling for HER2, ER, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), and even single-cell genomics [35–37]. Rossi and colleagues showed that single-cell analysis could pinpoint CTC abnormalities in early and metastatic breast cancer, shedding light on the molecular processes behind CTC persistence in inactive sites and guiding resistance mitigation tactics [38, 39]. Theoretically, CTC tests have the potential not just to identify impending relapse but also to unveil potential treatment targets for specific genetic changes.

Cluster 1: CTCs in detection of minimal residual disease of BC

Minimal residual disease (MRD) refers to the existence of a small quantity of tumor cells in patients seemingly cured of primary breast cancer following neoadjuvant therapy, surgery, radiation, and adjuvant therapy [40]. Recently, liquid biopsy has become a clinically effective method for monitoring MRD, predicting BC recurrence and metastasis, and guiding personalized treatment [41]. There are two main liquid biopsy techniques for detecting MRD: CTCs detection and ctDNA analysis. The role of CTCs in long-term surveillance and early relapse detection in HR-positive BC patients, who have undergone endocrine therapy (ET) for over 5 years without relapse but remain at risk for late recurrence, has been investigated [5].While CTCs provide intact, information-rich cancer cells, their rarity in circulation, especially in cases with minimal disease burden, poses a significant challenge. Studies indicate that only 4.8–18.2% of BC patients had at least one CTC detected after 2 to 5 years of adjuvant endocrine therapy, highlighting the limitations of current apheresis-based CTC detection methods in this context [42, 43]. Although the presence of positive CTCs has been associated with a poorer prognosis for late recurrence [42–44], the clinical advantage of this approach in enhancing quality of life (QoL) or overall OS remains unproven [45]. Although preliminary data indicate potential benefits of CTC monitoring in optimizing treatment strategies and surveillance, the direct clinical advantage of this approach on QoL or OS has yet to be conclusively demonstrated in randomized, controlled studies. Thus, while CTCs hold promise in tailoring personalized treatment, caution should be exercised until clearer evidence emerges.

Current literature supports the utility of CTCs in assessing prognosis and monitoring relapse in specific patient populations. However, many proposed clinical applications, especially concerning MRD detection, remain speculative and necessitate robust prospective studies to establish their validity. Nonetheless, it is essential to recognize that CTC and ctDNA levels might vary between early-stage and advanced BC patients [46]. Therefore, establishing recurrence thresholds, instead of relying on a simple binary classification of positive or negative, is crucial. Similar to our early-stage assessments, we conclude that although CTCs, ctDNA, and other liquid biopsies appear clinically valid for surveillance, their utility in detecting MRD remains unproven.

In conclusion, while the role of CTCs in the surveillance of BC and MRD detection presents an exciting avenue for research, it is imperative to underscore the current limitations of this approach. Ongoing studies are essential to ascertain the validity and clinical applicability of CTCs alongside ctDNA and to establish standardized metrics for these tools in intrinsic clinical practice.

Cluster 2: CTCs-driven metastasis mechanisms

In BC, a subset of cells known as cancer stem cells (CSCs) possess tumorigenic and metastatic capabilities [47–49]. These cells are characterized by their self-renewal and tumorigenic abilities [47]. A portion of these cells undergoes EMT, transforming into CTCs [50, 51]. During EMT, CSCs transition from a stationary to a migratory state. This enables them to travel through the bloodstream to distal sites and initiate metastasis. As CTCs transit from the primary tumor to metastatic sites, they undergo significant changes such as gaining and losing stemness traits, transitioning through EMT and mesenchymal–epithelial transition (MET), detaching, migrating, reattaching, intravasating, extravasating, and responding to DNA damage due to genotoxic stress. They also acquire genetic mutations that confer drug resistance [52, 53]. Additionally, CTCs can trigger both systemic and localized inflammation through neutrophil conversion, facilitating metastatic colonization [54, 55]. DNA damage within CTCs and the release of cell-free DNA from apoptotic CTCs induce inflammation, thereby enhancing their metastatic potential [56]. TNBC are strongly linked to EMT as a driving force for dissemination and exhibit characteristics of CSCs both molecularly and functionally, making linear progression the most likely course in this subtype [57]. Numerous studies have investigated the relationship between CTCs and the tumor immune microenvironment (TIM) in BC. Specifically, CTCs positive patients exhibited higher levels of regulatory T cell infiltration than those CTCs negative, suggesting an immunosuppressive phenotype [58]. The same researchers reported a positive linkage between mesenchymal-like CTCs and PD-L1-positive cells [59]. These findings suggest that CTCs could serve as promising indicators of anti-tumor immune activity.

Recently, it has been shown that CTCs in the blood of cancer patients exist both as single cells and in clusters. CTC clusters may contain anywhere from 2 to over 100 cells. Notable studies have revealed the molecular characteristics of CTCs and the critical role CTC clusters play in metastasis. These include the roles of cell-cell junctions, hypoxia, and heterotypic grouping with other circulating cell types like neutrophils [60–62]. Although CTC clusters are less common than single CTCs, they possess up to a 50-fold greater potential for distant metastasis [63]. CTC clusters gain a stronger survival advantage over single CTCs in the same environment through apoptosis resistance [64]. CTC clusters exhibit enhanced stemness traits and play a key role in immune evasion, enhancing their metastatic potential [65].

Cluster 3: clinical applications of CTCs in BC

The findings from our scientometric analysis have significant potential to impact clinical practice in breast cancer management. The prognostic importance of CTCs has been confirmed in both early-stage and metastatic BC. In EBC patients, the presence of CTCs is associated with tumor size, pathological grade, and lymph node involvement [66]. The detection of CTCs at diagnosis is linked to poorer prognoses, as shown by decreased DFS and OS rates in EBC patients without chemotherapy [67]. Furthermore, identifying CTCs after neoadjuvant chemotherapy correlates with poor survival outcomes in early-stage TNBC patients with residual disease post-treatment [68]. The prognostic value of CTCs, particularly in early-stage breast cancer, suggests that incorporating CTC detection into routine clinical assessments could improve risk stratification. This could lead to more personalized treatment strategies, potentially sparing low-risk patients from overtreatment while ensuring high-risk patients receive appropriately aggressive therapy. In MBC patients, counting CTCs can predict treatment efficacy, monitor disease progression, and estimate OS [69, 70]. A meta-analysis by Cristofanilli et al. involving 2436 BC patients, suggests classifying metastatic disease based on CTC count: an aggressive subgroup (≥ 5 CTCs) is linked to poorer OS compared to an indolent subgroup (< 5 CTCs) [71]. The predictive role of CTCs, particularly regarding the potential reduction of systemic treatment, has been explored, yielding inconsistent outcomes. In EBC, two clinical trials found no correlation between the pathological response of primary tumor and variations in CTC counts pre- and post-neoadjuvant chemotherapy [72, 73]. However, the presence of CTCs after therapy was identified as an independent prognostic indicator for early relapse [26, 73]. This suggests that detecting persistent CTCs post-treatment may offer more reliable insights into therapy response and relapse risk compared to the primary tumor’s chemosensitivity. In MBC, early transition to an alternative treatment regimen in patients with a positive CTC count after one cycle of first-line chemotherapy did not affect OS [70]. The role of CTCs in predicting treatment efficacy and monitoring disease progression could revolutionize patient care. The classification of metastatic disease based on CTC count could be integrated into clinical decision-making algorithms. This approach could guide the selection of treatment intensity and frequency of follow-up, potentially improving patient outcomes and resource allocation.

Beyond enumeration, detailed characterization of CTCs remains largely experimental, revealing significant heterogeneity. For example, CTCs isolated from MBC patients show dynamic transitions between mesenchymal and epithelial states, which is correlating with treatment response [24]. The plasticity of phenotypic markers warrants attention; for instance, the emergence of HER2-low disease in initially ER-positive, HER2-negative cases is a promising area for CTC liquid biopsy research. This is significant as HER2-low disease, a subset of ER-positive breast cancer, can benefit from HER2-targeted, antibody-drug conjugates treatments [74]. In HER2-positive BCs, researchers have hypothesized that HER2-positive CTCs can predict responses to HER2-targeted therapies. Two studies explored HER2-targeted therapies (T-DM1 and lapatinib) in MBC patients with HER2-negative primary tumors but HER2-positive CTCs. These studies showed marginal benefits in a subset of patients with HER2-negative primary tumors but HER2-positive CTCs during disease progression [75, 76]. Conversely, the STIC CTC trial demonstrated that CTCs can identify HR-positive, HER2-negative patients who benefit more from chemotherapy than endocrine therapy [17]. The DETECT III study (NCT01619111) compares standard therapy with or without lapatinib in HER2-negative MBC patients with HER2-positive CTCs [77]. Results indicated no significant difference in total and HER2-positive CTC clearance and PFS; however, the lapatinib arm showed significantly improved OS (HR: 0.55 95% CI 0.34–0.90) [16]. Currently, BC patients with bone metastases are treated with denosumab, an inhibitory antibody targeting RANKL. Pantano et al. demonstrated that RANK-expressing CTCs are present in the blood of BC patients with skeletal metastases [78]. Furthermore, the presence of RANK-positive CTCs correlated with an enhanced response to denosumab treatment.

Given that CTCs originate from various tumor locations, they offer a more comprehensive representation of PD-L1 expression heterogeneity compared to localized tissue samples. Previous research has established the utility of CTCs analysis as a means to assess PD-L1 status in cancer patients. In a prospective study conducted by Jacot et al., CTCs and PD-L1-positive CTCs were detected pre-treatment in 79.2% and 36.1% of MBC patients, respectively [79]. Notably, the presence of PD-L1-positive CTCs correlated with a shorter PFS compared to tissue PD-L1 expression. Furthermore, a decrease in PD-L1-positive CTCs was observed in response to treatment, suggesting their potential as pharmacodynamic markers for immunotherapy efficacy. It is worth mentioning that PD-L1-positive CTCs continued to increase even after the discontinuation of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), indicating the ability of these inhibitors to reduce the number of PD-L1-positive CTCs in BC patients [80] Thus, the presence of PD-L1-positive CTCs is associated with a poorer prognosis and can be used to monitor immunotherapy effectiveness while also eexplain potential resistance mechanisms.

Despite the promising clinical implications, several barriers exist in translating CTC research into routine clinical practice. These include: (a) Standardization: The lack of standardized protocols for CTC isolation and characterization hinders widespread clinical adoption. Efforts to establish consensus guidelines, such as those initiated by the European CANCER-ID consortium, are crucial for overcoming this barrier. (b) Cost and accessibility: Current CTC detection methods can be expensive and require specialized equipment, limiting their accessibility. Developing more cost-effective and user-friendly technologies is essential for broader implementation. (c) Clinical validation: While numerous studies demonstrate the prognostic value of CTCs, large-scale prospective clinical trials are needed to definitively establish their clinical utility. To address these challenges, we propose: Collaborative efforts between academic institutions, industry, and regulatory bodies to establish standardized protocols and quality control measures for CTC analysis. Investment in research and development of more affordable and accessible CTC detection technologies. Design and implementation of large-scale, multi-center clinical trials to validate the clinical utility of CTC-based decision-making in various breast cancer scenarios.

Based on our findings, several promising directions for future clinical trials emerge: Adaptive therapy trials: Studies could explore the use of CTC counts and characteristics to guide real-time treatment adjustments in MBC. De-escalation studies: In early-stage breast cancer, trials could investigate whether patients with persistently negative CTCs after neoadjuvant therapy could safely receive less intensive adjuvant treatment. Combination therapy trials: For patients with HER2-positive CTCs but HER2-negative primary tumors, studies could evaluate the efficacy of combining HER2-targeted therapies with standard treatment regimens. Immunotherapy optimization: Trials could assess whether dynamic monitoring of PD-L1-positive CTCs can guide the timing and duration of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. These proposed trials could significantly advance personalized medicine in breast cancer, potentially improving patient outcomes while optimizing resource utilization.

In the context of BC screening, while initial research on ctDNA as a screening tool shows promise, no studies have yet explored the use of CTCs in this field [81, 82]. Nevertheless, this approach is intriguing and merits further investigation employing more sensitive techniques.

Cluster 4: technological advances in CTC analysis

While studying CTCs offers a promising method for better understanding cancers, several intrinsic challenges must be addressed. CTCs are extremely rare, with estimates suggesting only one tumor cell per million blood cells in a sample [83]. Therefore, a cancer cell-specific enrichment step is crucial before analyzing CTC features. Various methods for detecting and isolating CTCs exist, such as negative selection (CD45-based depletion, multimarker-based depletion), marker-dependent positive selection (immunomagnetic methods, immunomicrofluidic methods, functional methods), and physical methods (membrane filtration, microfluidic filtration, inertial microfluidic separation) [84]. Currently, only two FDA-approved devices, CELLSEARCH® and Parsortix®, are available to monitor CTC presence and quantity in blood [15, 85]. CELLSEARCH® employs antigen-dependent immunomagnetic positive selection to capture CTCs based on EpCAM expression, whereas Parsortix® utilizes antigen-independent, size-based microfluidic technology.

Despite advancements in CTC detection technologies, sensitivity remains a significant challenge, particularly in early-stage breast cancer. For instance, the CELLSEARCH® system, while FDA-approved, has shown limited sensitivity in detecting CTCs in early-stage breast cancer patients. This limitation is partly due to the system’s reliance on EpCAM expression, which may be downregulated in some CTCs undergoing EMT [24]. The specificity of CTC detection methods is another critical issue. Physical property-based isolation techniques, such as size-based filtration, may inadvertently capture non-malignant cells or miss smaller CTCs, leading to false-positive or false-negative results [86]. The clinical applicability of current CTC detection methods is hampered by their complexity, cost, and the need for specialized equipment and expertise. This limits their widespread adoption in routine clinical practice, particularly in resource-limited settings [87].

The rarity of CTCs in peripheral blood poses a significant challenge to their detection and analysis. Current methods often struggle to achieve the necessary sensitivity and specificity required for reliable clinical use, especially in early-stage disease [88]. Moreover, the heterogeneity of CTCs, including the presence of CTC clusters and circulating tumor microemboli, further complicates their isolation and characterization [63]. The brief circulation time of CTCs (10–30 min) also presents a challenge for their capture and analysis [13]. This necessitates the development of more efficient enrichment strategies and potentially continuous monitoring approaches. Furthermore, the reliance on small blood volumes (1 to 30 mL) collected at a single time point may not provide a comprehensive representation of the tumor’s heterogeneity or its dynamic changes over time. This limitation underscores the need for technologies capable of processing larger blood volumes or enabling repeated sampling [13, 85].

Future research should focus on developing more sensitive and specific CTC detection methods that can reliably identify CTCs in early-stage breast cancer. This may involve combining multiple enrichment strategies or developing novel biomarkers for CTC identification [89]. Additionally, efforts should be made to standardize CTC detection and analysis protocols to improve reproducibility across different laboratories and clinical settings. The development of technologies capable of continuous or repeated sampling, such as implantable devices like direct intravascular coated guidewires [90], cytapheresis [91] for cell fraction enrichment from large blood volumes, and accessing tumor-draining vessels to enhance CTC enrichment [92], may provide more comprehensive insights into tumor dynamics and heterogeneity. Despite their significance, these technologies have not become standard practice and are inappropriate for patients with advanced-stage disease who are ineligible for surgery.

Strengths and limitations

This scientometric analysis provides a bird’s-eye view of the CTC research landscape in breast cancer, offering researchers, clinicians, and policymakers valuable insights into the field’s evolution, current state, and future directions. By identifying key trends, contributors, and emerging topics, this study aims to facilitate more targeted and impactful research efforts in this crucial area of oncology.

Nevertheless, there are certain limitations inherent in our study. While this study utilized the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) as its primary data source, we acknowledge that this choice has certain limitations. Specifically, the analysis relied exclusively on data from WoSCC, which may have led to the exclusion of relevant articles indexed in other databases such as Scopus and PubMed. These databases may contain important research findings that could enhance our understanding of CTCs in breast cancer. Consequently, there may be a risk of missing significant articles that could contribute valuable insights to our analysis. Future studies could benefit from integrating multiple databases to provide a more comprehensive overview of the field and potentially capture a broader range of publications. While Web of Science is a widely recognized and comprehensive database, it is important to note that it may not capture all relevant publications in the field, particularly those in languages other than English. This limitation could potentially impact the completeness of our analysis and introduce a bias towards English-language publications from indexed journals. Additionally, the use of bibliometric tools such as VOSviewer and CiteSpace, while powerful for visualization and analysis, may introduce biases due to their specific algorithms and methodologies. Different versions of these software tools may also lead to slight variations in results. To mitigate this, we have used the most recent stable versions of both tools and have cross-validated our findings where possible. Citation frequency was utilized as a quality metric, although it can be affected by the publication date of the article. Our study was limited to the timeframe of publications from January 2008 to December 2023, resulting in a potential omission of significant contributions in the field after this cut-off date. This omission highlights a critical area for further research, as subsequent publications could offer new insights that were not captured in the current analysis.

Conclusion

This research offers a comprehensive and insightful examination of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) in breast cancer (BC) through visual and bibliometric approaches. The literature in this field is rapidly growing, significantly contributed by researchers from the United States. Five key research hotspots within this domain were identified and reviewed. Notably, the prognostic and predictive roles of CTCs in BC have significant impacts on clinical practice. However, accurately determining the status of CTCs, including their counts and characteristics, using advanced technologies, and CTCs in detection of minimal residual disease of BC remain significant challenges. With the advancement of liquid biopsy, the importance of CTCs in disease monitoring and treatment planning will become increasingly prominent. This research could serve as a crucial resource for researchers globally.
