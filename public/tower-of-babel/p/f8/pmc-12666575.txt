Jugular bulb venous to arterial carbon dioxide gradient is associated with treatment-responsive phenotypes of hypoxic-ischemic brain injury

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe difference between the jugular bulb venous and arterial carbon dioxide (CO2) tension, the cerebral pCO2 gap, is a marker of global tissue perfusion and can be affected by various mechanisms that underlie hypoxic-ischemic brain injury (HIBI). We hypothesized the CO2 gap and SjvO2 together could identify treatment responsive pathophysiology among those with abnormally elevated SjvO2.MethodsIn this retrospective study including post-arrest patients, we quantified the relationship between pCO2 gap and SjvO2 using hierarchical regression. Among patients with elevated SjvO2, we assessed whether pCO2 gap predicted response to treatment with hyperosmolar therapy using an interaction between time, treatment, and pCO2 gap. We assessed the association between pCO2 gap and indicators of HIBI using mixed regression models.ResultsAmong the 109 patients included, pCO2 gap was lower among patients with normal (β = −4.3; 95 % CI, −5.6 to −3.1) or high SjvO2 (β = −7.8; 95 % CI, −9.1 to −6.6) compared to low SjvO2. In our interaction model, treatment with hyperosmolar therapy was associated with a 14.4 mmHg decrease in SjvO2 (95 % CI, −18.4 to −10.3) among those with a high pCO2 gap, but not those with normal pCO2 gap. pCO2 gap was significantly lower among patients with burst-suppression with identical bursts compared to those without (β = −1.8; 95 % CI, −2.8 to −0.9).ConclusionWhen interpreted with SjvO2, the cerebral pCO2 gap may uncover covert cerebral dysfunction, and can identify treatment-responsive causes of elevated SjvO2. Thus, the pCO2 gap may be a useful adjunct for phenotyping HIBI.

Introduction

The difference between central or mixed venous and arterial carbon dioxide (pCO2) tension, the systemic pCO2 gap, is a marker of tissue perfusion and metabolism.1, 2 The pCO2 gap was initially considered a surrogate marker of global oxygen delivery (i.e., cardiac output), with studies showing an association between inadequate cardiac output and elevated pCO2 gap.3 In states of reduced cardiac output, a widened pCO2 gap may reflect inadequate blood flow to remove CO2 delivered from cells to the venous compartment (i.e., stagnation phenomenon) or possibly tissue hypoxia with anaerobic metabolism (whereby CO2 is generated from buffering of hydrogen ions from lactic acid production and adenosine triphosphate hydrolysis during anaerobic metabolism).4

While the pCO2 gap can increase from inadequate blood flow, the pCO2 gap can also increase despite seemingly adequate global oxygen delivery. For example, microcirculatory dysfunction can cause inadequate regional blood flow and shunting, while perivascular edema may result in diffusion-limited tissue hypoxia.5, 6, 7 Alternatively, the pCO2 gap can be normal in the setting of cytopathic hypoxia from impaired mitochondrial respiration.8 Although many physiologic perturbations differentially affect pCO2 gap, when interpreted in concert with the mixed or central venous oxygen saturation (SvO2 or ScvO2), the pCO2 gap can help delineate the underlying pathophysiology. For example, a low SvO2 with a high pCO2 gap suggests inadequate macrovascular perfusion, whereas as an elevated pCO2 gap in the context of a normal or elevated SvO2 may unveil covert tissue hypoperfusion.5, 9 In this way, the pCO2 gap is used to guide management of patients with shock.4, 5

An analogous measure, the cerebral pCO2 gap, measured as the jugular bulb venous CO2 to arterial CO2 gradient (PjvCO2–PaCO2), may reflect the adequacy of cerebral blood flow relative to cerebral metabolic demand.10, 11, 12, 13 After cardiac arrest, available measures of cerebral oxygenation, such as regional oxygen saturation, brain tissue oxygen tension, and jugular bulb venous oxygen saturation (SjvO2) are often used in isolation, with limited ability to differentiate between various neuropathophysiological processes that contribute to secondary brain injury. Thus, when considered in conjunction with SjvO2, the cerebral pCO2 gap may provide complementary information, enabling a more nuanced understanding of the pathophysiology of hypoxic-ischemic brain injury (HIBI) and potentially identifying inadequate cerebral resuscitation and treatment responsive phenotypes.

We sought to describe the distribution of the cerebral pCO2 gap stratified by SjvO2, identify determinants of the cerebral pCO2 gap, and test associations with markers of HIBI in patients resuscitated from cardiac arrest. We hypothesized the cerebral pCO2 gap and SjvO2 together could identify treatment-responsive diffusion-limited hypoxia among patients with elevated SjvO2.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This was a retrospective study including post-cardiac arrest patients admitted to a single academic medical center from July 1, 2020 to October 31, 2023. We included patients who were unresponsive to verbal commands and underwent SjvO2 monitoring within 72 h after arrest. We considered jugular bulb catheter placement in post-arrest patients with evidence of HIBI but without irrecoverable brain injury (e.g., herniation on initial head CT) and no contraindication (e.g., cervical spine injury). Ultimately, the treating clinician decided to place a jugular bulb catheter or not, and we were unable to determine the clinical reasoning being that decision from chart review. When SjvO2 is pathologically elevated (>75 %), we may trial treatment with hyperosmolar therapy (e.g., hypertonic saline) to decrease perivascular edema. We do this irrespective of intracranial pressure.14 Patients may receive additional doses of hyperosmolar therapy depending on initial response and at the discretion of the treating clinician.

All post-arrest patients in this study received targeted temperature management (TTM). We select target temperature based on the severity of neurologic injury at presentation, considering neurologic exam and head CT, and systemic factors (e.g., coagulopathy). However, the specific target temperature was at the discretion of the treating clinician. We measure temperature continuously from either an esophageal or rectal probe.

We excluded patients who had a jugular bulb venous catheter placed but no interpretable SjvO2 values (e.g., catheter malposition, clotted samples) or had primary neurologic or traumatic arrest etiologies. This study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Human Research Protection Office (STUDY19020205).

Study procedures

We reviewed our prospectively maintained registry of post-cardiac arrest patients and identified potentially eligible patients. After jugular bulb venous catheter placement, we obtain lateral neck x-ray or computed tomography to confirm catheter placement.15 One study author reviewed diagnostic radiographs to ensure appropriate jugular bulb venous catheter placement.15 From our registry, we abstracted patient demographics (age, sex, race); arrest characteristics (time and date, location, initial rhythm, arrest duration); illness severity based on Pittsburgh Cardiac Arrest Category (PCAC),16 neurologic exam on presentation to our hospital (pupillary light reflex (PLR) and Full Outline of Unresponsiveness (FOUR) motor score), grey-white ratio (GWR) on initial non-contrast head CT,17 and presence or absence of burst-suppression with identical bursts (BSIB) on electroencephalography (EEG)18; and concurrent use continuous sedation, neuromuscular blockade (NMB), or antiseizure medication (ASM). We recorded all arterial and jugular venous blood gas values (partial pressures of oxygen (PaO2 and PjvO2) and carbon dioxide (PaCO2 and PjvCO2), temperature, mean arterial pressure (MAP), and hemoglobin. We calculated the cerebral pCO2 gap as the PjvCO2–PaCO2.

We examined treatment responsiveness when SjvO2 was pathologically elevated (>75 %). To do so, we defined paired measurements when an SjvO2 was >75 % and a second SjvO2 measurement was acquired within 8 h of the first. We considered the earliest SjvO2 the pre-SjvO2 and the subsequent measure the post-SjvO2 for each measurement pair. Thus, patients could have multiple SjvO2 pairs, and a post-SjvO2 could become the pre-SjvO2 in a subsequent pair for a patient. In this analysis, our primary outcome of interest was the change in SjvO2 between the pre- and post-SjvO2 values in a measurement pair. Our primary exposure of interest was treatment with hyperosmolar therapy (HTS) between the first and second SjvO2 measurements within a pair, which we defined as exposure to any sodium chloride or sodium bicarbonate solution with a concentration ≥1.8 %.

Analysis

We used descriptive statistics to summarize cohort characteristics, reporting mean with standard deviations (SD), medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and counts with percentages, as appropriate. To account for missing data, we performed multiple imputation by chained equations using predictive mean matching for continuous variables and logistic regression for binary variables and created 150 imputed datasets (Supplemental Table 1 shows missing covariates). We inspected Monte Carlo errors to ensure sufficient imputations.19 After imputation, we calculated the pCO2 gap from imputed values.

Physiological determinants of pCO2 gap

Given lack of existing data on determinants of the cerebral pCO2 gap, we first used mixed effects linear regression to test the association between pCO2 gap and biologically plausible predictors, including MAP, temperature, PaO2, hemoglobin, and concurrent continuous sedation, NMB, and ASM. We included patient as a random intercept to account for repeated measures. All covariates were time-varying. Covariates significantly associated with the pCO2 gap were included in subsequent models.

pCO2 gap as a marker of HIBI detected by other modalities

HIBI severity may influence pCO2 gap. We performed separate mixed linear regressions with patient as a random intercept to test for an association between pCO2 gap and two indicators of HIBI severity, BSIB and GWR, adjusting for confounders we found to be significantly associated with pCO2 gap in our initial regression model.17, 18 Next, we assessed whether there was a differential association with potential physiologic determinants of pCO2 gap based on the presence or absence of BSIB by including an interaction between BSIB and the potential predictor (MAP, temperature, PaO2).

pCO2 gap, SjvO2, and treatment-responsiveness

Finally, we quantified the relationship between pCO2 gap and SjvO2 using mixed linear regression. We then categorized each SjvO2 as low (<55 %), normal (55–75 %), or high (>75 %). SjvO2 was a repeated measure, so a single patient could have observations in one or more categories. To test for a differential association between pCO2 gap and concurrent physiologic variables based on SjvO2, we included interactions between SjvO2 category (low, normal, high) and the covariates we identified as significant predictors of the pCO2 gap (i.e., temperature, MAP, and PaO2) in mixed regression models.

We tested if pCO2 gap predicted responsiveness to hyperosmolar therapy among those with elevated SjvO2 (>75 %) using a three-way interaction between time, treatment, and pCO2 gap, with pCO2 gap dichotomized as low/normal (pCO2 gap ≤6) or high (pCO2 gap >6).3, 13 We included patient as a random intercept to account for repeated measures.

We performed several post-hoc sensitivity analyses. First, because there is limited data on the optimal cerebral pCO2 gap cutoff, we tested three additional pCO2 gap cutoffs – 4 mmHg, 8 mmHg, and 10 mmHg. As HIBI varies over time post-arrest, we performed a sensitivity analysis to assess the impact of time from initial collapse on change in SjvO2. Next, to account for the potential effect of sedation, we added exposure to sedation (e.g., propofol, midazolam, fentanyl, ketamine) to our model. Finally, although we defined treatment responsive as a decrease in SjvO2 after exposure to HTS, we did a post-hoc analysis comparing survival between those who did and did not respond to treatment as a patient-centered outcome. For this analysis, we used Firth’s penalized regression due to the rarity of survival.

We performed all analyses using Stata v18.5 and figures using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

We assessed the association between electrographic and radiographic markers of hypoxic-ischemic brain injury. BSIB occurred in 45 (43 %) of our cohort and median GWR on initial non-contrast head CT was 1.32 [IQR 1.22–1.37]. After controlling for temperature, MAP, and PaO2, the pCO2 gap was significantly lower among patients with BSIB compared to those without BSIB (β = −1.8; 95 % CI, −2.8 to −0.9; p < 0.001). However, there was no association between pCO2 gap and GWR on initial head CT (β = 3.0; 95 % CI, −1.4 to 7.2; p = 0.2). There was no difference in association with PaO2, MAP, or temperature between those with and without BSIB (p-values all > 0.05).

Finally, we tested if pCO2 gap predicted responsiveness to hyperosmolar therapy among patients with pathologically elevated SjvO2 (>75 %) who had at least one pair measurement (i.e., a first SjvO2 >75 % and a second SjvO2 within 8 h). There were 84 (77 %) individuals in our cohort who had at least one elevated SjvO2 and a subsequent (i.e., “post”) SjvO2, for a total of 426 paired measurements. Of these individuals, 35 (42 %) were treated with hyperosmolar therapy at least once and contributed 69 (16 %) paired measurements. There was no difference in mean dose of HTS between those who did (270 (SD 144) and those who did not (254 mOsm (SD 119)) respond to treatment with HTS (p = 0.7). In our interaction model looking at response to treatment with hyperosmolar therapy among patients with high SjvO2 stratified by pCO2 gap (<=6 or > 6), we found the effect of treatment differed by pCO2 gap (β for interaction = −10.3; 95 % CI, −15.2 to −5.4; p < 0.001). Among patients with a high pCO2 gap (>6), the marginal estimate for the effect of treatment with hyperosmolar therapy was a 14.4 mmHg decrease in SjvO2 (95 % CI, −18.4 to −10.3), whereas SjvO2 decreased by only 0.63 mmHg (95 % CI, −2.5 to 1.2) after treatment among those with a low/normal pCO2 gap (Fig. 3). In our post-hoc sensitivity analyses to determine optimal pCO2 cutoff, pCO2 values of 4 mmHg, 8 mmHg, and 10 mmHg did not identify response to treatment with HTS (all p-values > 0.05). Neither time from collapse (β = 0.001; 95 % CI, −0.004 to −0.006) nor sedation (β = 1.8; 95 % CI, −10.0 to 13.6) impacted treatment responsiveness. The odds of survival were 5.6 times higher among those with an abnormally high SjvO2 who responded to treatment with HTS compared to those who did not respond to HTS, but this was not significant (p = 0.2).

Discussion

In this retrospective study of comatose post-cardiac arrest patients who underwent jugular bulb venous oximetry monitoring, we found an inverse relationship between cerebral pCO2 gap and SjvO2. However, pCO2 gap could be elevated even when SjvO2 was normal or high, suggesting macrovascular cerebral perfusion is not the sole determinant of the cerebral pCO2 gap. We also observed that the pCO2 gap was significantly lower among those with electrographic features of severe HIBI. Additionally, SjvO2 decreased in response to treatment with HTS only when the pCO2 gap was elevated. Our results suggest the pCO2 gap, when interpreted in conjunction with SjvO2, may reveal covert cerebral dysfunction and serve as a valuable tool discern treatment-responsive phenotypes of HIBI at the bedside.

SjvO2 is used as an indicator of the balance between cerebral oxygen delivery and consumption in many disease states. Abnormally low SjvO2 (<55 %) suggests cerebral ischemia, while abnormally high SjvO2 (>75 %) may indicate diffusion-limited hypoxia, cellular energy failure (i.e., mitochondrial dysfunction), or severe neuroglial injury and cellular death.20, 21, 22 Both extremes of SjvO2 are associated with worse outcomes after various forms of acute brain injury.23, 24 However, neither assures irrecoverable brain injury and both may reflect potentially intervenable pathology. We previously found EEG could differentiate treatment-responsive diffusion-limited hypoxia from other pathophysiology in patients with elevated SjvO2.14 Given our finding that a high pCO2 gap was similarly able to predict treatment-responsiveness in this study, and as EEG is a marker of cortical neuronal activity, our results suggest the cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen may be contributor to pCO2 gap. Furthermore, we have identified the cerebral pCO2 gap as another means to distinguish modifiable from non-modifiable HIBI at the bedside. Thus, the pCO2 may be a valuable tool that, with currently used neuromonitoring modalities, may help advance post-arrest care toward a targeted, personalized approach.

We also found those with elevated SjvO2 and high pCO2 gap had a decrease in SjvO2 within paired SjvO2 measurements compared to those with a low/normal pCO2 even in the absence of treatment. This may reflect our finding that patients with an elevated pCO2 gap had less severe HIBI and spontaneously improving cerebral oxygenation over time. Alternatively, the median value for the second SjvO2 in a paired sample for these patients was still pathologically elevated and thus may not reflect a clinically meaningful change in SjvO2.

Our finding that those with low SjvO2 had significantly higher pCO2 gap is consistent with existing literature.4, 10, 13 Low SjvO2 occurs when global oxygen delivery is inadequate.12 Such states are associated with a widened pCO2 gap.10, 13 In one study, the pCO2 gap progressively increased as cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) decreased, until the lowest recorded CPPs, at which point the pCO2 gap narrowed to <6 mmHg.13 Thus, while elevated pCO2 gap may partly reflect inadequate perfusion, it also implies ongoing pCO2 production and thus some degree of cerebral metabolic activity. Our results, in context of existing data, suggest a higher pCO2 gap indicates at least some residual cerebral metabolic activity. There may be a threshold, above which critical or irreversible cerebral ischemia occurs and is an area for future investigation. However, when identified, patients with cerebral ischemia may benefit from interventions to optimize cerebral blood flow, which has been demonstrated to improve cerebral oxygenation.25 Although we did not find a differential association between pCO2 gap and MAP among those with low SjvO2 as expected, this may be explained by limited sample size and multiple determinants of cerebral blood flow.

Our study has several limitations. This was a retrospective, single center study, which limits generalizability. Furthermore, it included only a subgroup of post-arrest patients-- those who underwent SjvO2 monitoring. The decision to monitor SjvO2 and intervene on abnormal values was at the discretion of the treating clinician, which can further introduce bias. Although we did not find a significant association between treatment responsiveness and survival among those with pathologically high SjvO2, this likely reflects the small sample size and rarity of survival in the cohort, as we were not powered to detect this association. However, this study provides preliminary evidence that we can modify cerebral physiology that should be explored further. We used a cutoff of 6 mmHg as the cutoff to differentiate between normal and widened pCO2 gap from the systemic pCO2 gap literature. While this has not been validated as the optimal threshold for the cerebral pCO2 gap, a comparable value of 6.5 mmHg was found among patients deemed to have adequate cerebral perfusion in one study and alternate cutoffs in our sensitivity analysis were not associated with treatment responsivness.13 However, cerebral pCO2 gap thresholds for inadequate cerebral resuscitation may vary based on the underlying mechanism of secondary brain injury and this is an area for future research. We did not have direct measurements of cerebral blood flow or cerebral metabolism (e.g., jugular bulb venous lactate) and few patients had direct brain tissue oxygen monitoring, limiting our conclusions.

Conclusion

When interpreted with SjvO2, the cerebral pCO2 gap may reveal cerebral dysfunction not detected by traditional measures of cerebral physiology. Furthermore, the cerebral pCO2 gap may help differentiate patients with pathologically elevated SjvO2 due to treatment-responsive diffusion-limited hypoxia from those with non-modifiable HIBI. Thus, when integrated with other neuromonitoring modalities, the pCO2 gap may be a useful adjunct for phenotyping HIBI at the bedside.
