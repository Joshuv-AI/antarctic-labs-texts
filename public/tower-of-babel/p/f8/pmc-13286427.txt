Association between hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet (HALP) score and rheumatoid arthritis: A cross-sectional study from NHANES 1999–2018
Authors: Fan Yang, Xu Xu, Yongqing Liu, Weihua Sang, Jun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet (HALP) score is a potential composite biomarker that integrates nutritional and immune-inflammatory profiles. However, its association with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) prevalence remains undefined. In this large-scale cross-sectional study, we investigated the relationship between HALP and RA, explored the cross-sectional association between HALP score and RA prevalence, and evaluated HALP as a concurrent composite biomarker reflecting immune-inflammatory and nutritional status in RA. Our analysis included 34,079 participants from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 1999–2018 cycles. We calculated the HALP score using the following formula: Hemoglobin (g/L) × albumin (g/L) × lymphocyte count (×109/L)/platelet count (×109/L). Weighted multivariable logistic regression models, adjusted for demographic and clinical covariates, were used to assess the HALP-RA association, with restricted cubic splines evaluating potential nonlinearity. We performed comprehensive sensitivity analyses and subgroup examinations to test result robustness. For cross-sectional discriminative modeling, we used a 3-stage variable selection process: Univariate screening, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression with 10-fold cross-validation for dimension reduction, and multivariable logistic regression. Model performance was assessed to evaluate how well HALP and clinical variables distinguish participants with prevalent RA from those without. In this large national study, the HALP score was inversely and nonlinearly associated with RA, consistent across subgroups. HALP score is inversely and nonlinearly associated with prevalent RA in a large US population. Given the cross-sectional design, HALP likely represents a consequence of established RA rather than a predictive risk factor. These findings are hypothesis-generating and require validation in clinically confirmed RA cohorts. Current evidence does not support using HALP for RA case identification or prediction. In this cross-sectional study of 34,079 participants, including 2166 unweighted RA cases (unweighted percentage 6.36%, weighted prevalence 4.85%), higher ln(HALP) levels were inversely associated with RA (adjusted odds ratio, 0.74; 95% confidence interval, 0.64–0.85; P < .001), with a nonlinear inverse association (P for nonlinearity < .001). Subgroup analyses showed no significant effect modification (all P for interaction > .05). A HALP-integrated cross-sectional discriminative model yielded an area under the curve of 0.747, which was mainly driven by conventional clinical variables. The incremental value of HALP was not evaluated.

1. Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune disease characterized by chronic symmetrical polyarthritis, with a prevalence of 0.5%–1.0% in industrialized nations.[1–3] Its hallmark features include persistent synovial inflammation, progressive joint erosion, and autoantibody production (rheumatoid factor [RF] and anti-citrullinated protein antibodies [anti-CCP]).[4] The inflammatory response in RA is regulated by cytokines such as interleukin-6, IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor-α, with autoreactive CD4 + T cells playing a pivotal role in disease progression.[5–8]

Emerging evidence supports the clinical relevance of immune-inflammatory biomarkers derived from routine blood counts, including the systemic immune-inflammatory index, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio.[9–13] However, these markershave 2 critical limitations: oversimplified evaluation criteria and failure to incorporate nutritional status. The prognostic nutritional index has shown independent associations with RA outcomes, highlighting the importance of combined immune-nutritional assessment.[14]

The hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet (HALP) score is a composite biomarker that integrates both inflammatory and nutritional status. Hemoglobin indicates anemia, albumin (ALB) reflects nutritional status, lymphocytes (LYM) represent immune competence, and platelets (PLT) indicate inflammatory activity. The HALP score has demonstrated prognostic value in oncology,[15,16] but its association with RA remains unexplored.

Using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), this cross-sectional study aimed to investigate the cross-sectional association between HALP score and prevalent RA. Evaluate whether HALP can serve as a concurrent composite biomarker that reflects immune-nutritional disturbance in individuals with RA. As a cross-sectional study, this analysis cannot establish causality or support prospective risk prediction.

2. Methods

2.1. Patient selection and research framework

The NHANES is a nationally representative, cross-sectional program designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the US population through standardized physical examinations, laboratory tests, and interviews.[17] The NHANES protocol was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Extensive documentation and resources pertaining to the NHANES are openly available on the study’s official website (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx). For this analysis, we used data from 10 consecutive NHANES cycles (1999–2018); the present secondary analysis used publicly available de-identified data and was deemed exempt from additional ethical review.

In this study population, we excluded participants who were aged <20 years, pregnant, missing RA status data, lacking complete hematologic measures (ALB, LYM, PLT, hemoglobin [HGB]), or missing key covariate data. After exclusions, the final analytic sample comprised 34,079 individuals (Fig. 1). Mobile examination center (MEC) examination weights were used in analyses incorporating laboratory-derived hematologic parameters. For the 1999 to 2000 and 2001 to 2002 cycles, weights were calculated as (2/10) × wtmec4yr, whereas for 2003 to 2018, weights were derived as (1/10) × wtmec2yr.

Flowchart of study participant selection.

2.2. Definition of HALP score

Following the NHANES protocols, trained phlebotomists obtained venous blood samples during MEC visits. We computed the HALP score using the following formula: (HGB × ALB × LYM)/PLT, with all parameters in standardized units. All hematological assays were conducted in CDC-certified laboratories. Participants were classified into quartiles (Q1–4) of HALP scores according to established methodology,[18,19] with Q1 and Q4 denoting the bottom and top 25th percentiles.

2.3. Definition of RA

Arthritis status was ascertained based on self-reported responses to the standardized National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) item MCQ160a: “Has a physician or other licensed healthcare provider ever diagnosed you with arthritis?” (response options: yes/no). Participants who responded affirmatively were further asked to specify the type of arthritis diagnosed, with response options including RA, osteoarthritis, psoriatic arthritis, other specified arthritis (with a write-in option), refused to answer, or unknown/unsure. This method of ascertaining self-reported physician-diagnosed arthritis has been validated against clinical diagnosis, demonstrating approximately 85% concordance in prior studies.[20] For the primary analysis, we restricted the cohort to individuals who self-reported a diagnosis of RA, excluding those reporting other forms of arthritis or uncertain classifications. Although this self-report method shows reasonable validity, some misclassification is possible: particularly through inclusion of individuals with other arthritic conditions such as osteoarthritis. This potential misclassification may account for the elevated prevalence of RA observed in our sample (4.85%), which exceeds established population estimates (0.5%–1.0%).

2.4. Covariates assessment

NHANES household interviews provided demographic and lifestyle information, including age, sex, self-reported race/ethnicity (categorized as non-Hispanic White, Mexican American, non-Hispanic Black, and other), educational attainment (grouped as < 9th, 9–12th, and > 12th grade), and poverty-income ratio (PIR; < 1.3, 1.3–3.5, > 3.5). Body mass index (BMI) measurements obtained during MEC visits were categorized into <25, 25–30, and ≥30 kg/m2. Smoking status was dichotomized based on lifetime consumption of ≥100 cigarettes (smokers vs nonsmokers). Alcohol consumption was classified by self-reported intake of ≥12 alcoholic drinks/year. Diabetes status was determined by an affirmative response to the physician’s diagnosis. Hypertension status was determined through self-reported responses to the question: “Have you ever been told by a physician or other health professional that you have high blood pressure?”Medication usage, including disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, glucocorticoids, and biologic agents, was not available in the NHANES 1999–2018 database and therefore not adjusted in multivariable models.

2.5. Statistical methodology

Analyses utilized R (version 4.2; R Foundation) and Free Statistics (version 2.1.1) software. NHANES’s multistage sampling design was addressed through the application of examination weights (wtmec4yr and wtmec2yr). Continuous variables are reported as weighted mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables are described as weighted proportions. Weighted t-tests and χ2 tests facilitated group comparisons.

Given the right-skewed distribution of HALP scores, data transformation was performed before analysis. Weighted multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the RA-HALP association through 3 models: Unadjusted; adjusted for demographics (age, sex, BMI, and race/ethnicity); additionally adjusted for socioeconomic (PIR and education), behavioral (alcohol and smoking), and clinical factors (diabetes and hypertension). Sensitivity analyses included complete-case evaluation and unadjusted regression.

We examined the HALP score as follows: Quartile-based categories and continuous variables via restricted cubic splines (three knots) to test nonlinearity. Generalized additive models with smoothing functions were used to detect potential thresholds in the ln(HALP)-RA relationship.

Model development involved: Predictor selection via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression (optimal λ determined by 10-fold cross-validation); effect size estimation through odds ratio (OR) calculations; final model establishment using hierarchical regression. Validation included receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis assessing the area under the ROC curve area under the curve (AUC) (discrimination) and sensitivity/specificity (classification accuracy). Decision curve analysis was performed to determine the net benefit and clinical usefulness of the model across varying risk thresholds. A nomogram was constructed to visualize the strength of association between each variable and prevalent RA. Each variable was assigned a weighted score based on its contribution to the overall risk, enabling the quantification of the individualized probability of RA presence. Unmeasured confounding from RA-related medications could not be evaluated in this study.

Interaction and subgroup analyses were performed across demographic strata (sex, age groups, and race/ethnicity), socioeconomic strata (education and PIR), and clinical/lifestyle strata (BMI, diabetes, hypertension, alcohol consumption, and smoking). All statistical tests were two-sided with a P < .05 significance threshold.

3. Results

3.1. Study population characteristics

The study included 34,079 participants, with a nearly equal sex distribution (17,789 [51.5%] males; 16,290 [48.5%] females). Among these, 10,280 (20.4%) were aged >60 years, and 15,118 (69.0%) self-identified as White People. As presented in Table 1, the clinical characteristics were categorized by HALP score quartiles. Significant heterogeneity was observed across quartiles for the following variables (P < .001 for all comparisons): Demographic factors: sex, age, race/ethnicity, and educational attainment; socioeconomic measures: BMI and PIR; and clinical and behavioral profiles: Hypertension, diabetes mellitus, alcohol use, smoking status, and RA. A total of 2166 participants were unweighted RA cases (unweighted proportion 6.36%), with a weighted prevalence of 4.85%. The higher weighted prevalence relative to standard population estimates (0.5%–1.0%) suggests potential misclassification of self-reported RA.

3.2. Association between HALP score and RA

Weighted multivariate logistic regression analyses demonstrated a consistent inverse association between HALP score and RA across all models (Table 2). In the unadjusted model, each unit increase in natural log-transformed HALP (ln [HALP]) was associated with a 40% reduction in RA (OR: 0.60; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.51–0.71; P < .001). After demographic adjustment (Model 1), the association remained significant (OR: 0.77; 95% CI: 0.66–0.89; P < .001). Full adjustment (Model 2) demonstrated minimal attenuation (OR: 0.74; 95% CI: 0.64–0.85; P < .001). In the quartile analysis, the highest HALP group had significantly lower RA versus the lowest quartile across all models (fully adjusted OR: 0.80; 95% CI: 0.67–0.95; P = .01), supporting HALP’s biomarker potential.

To evaluate the robustness of the observed HALP score-RA association to methodological variations, we performed sensitivity analyses using unweighted regression approaches (Table 3). These analyses consistently replicated our primary findings, revealing a persistent inverse association between elevated HALP scores and RA. The relationship maintained both its direction and statistical significance across all model specifications, from unadjusted to fully adjusted models. Notably, the effect sizes were comparable to those obtained in the primary weighted analysis (unweighted OR range: 0.62–0.79 vs weighted OR range: 0.60–0.77 for the continuous HALP score). This consistency across analytical approaches enhances confidence in the reliability of the HALP score as a clinically meaningful biomarker for RA disease stratification. The stability of the association under different modeling assumptions, coupled with the preservation of effect magnitude, provides additional evidence supporting the biological plausibility of the HALP-RA relationship. The association between HALP score and clinical outcomes appears independent of the weighting methodology.

3.3. Subgroup analyses of HALP score and RA

To assess the consistency of the HALP score-RA association, we conducted stratified analyses across clinically relevant demographic and clinical subgroups. These included sex (male/female), age categories, racial groups, education levels, PIR tertiles, alcohol consumption status, smoking status, diabetes mellitus status, BMI categories, and hypertension status(Fig. 2). The inverse association between the HALP score and RA appeared generally consistent across most examined subgroups, with no statistically significant effect modification (all interaction P values > 0.05). However, as detailed below, these findings are limited by small stratum sample sizes and wide CIs, and the lack of significant interaction may be attributable to low statistical power rather than true homogeneity.

Forest plot for the correlation between HALP and RA. HALP score = Hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet score, RA = rheumatoid arthritis.

Limitation of subgroup analyses due to sample size constraints. Although the forest plot showed generally consistent inverse associations across most strata (Fig. 2), these subgroup findings should be interpreted with caution. With a total of 2166 RA cases (weighted prevalence 4.85%), stratification into multiple clinical and demographic subgroups inevitably resulted in small case numbers per stratum, particularly for intersections such as male diabetic smokers or elderly individuals with hypertension. Consequently, the CIs for several subgroup estimates were wide, and the interaction tests (all P > .05) may have been underpowered to detect true effect heterogeneity. Therefore, the observed consistency across subgroups does not necessarily indicate true homogeneity of the HALP-RA association; rather, it may reflect limited statistical power. Larger studies with greater numbers of confirmed RA cases are needed to reliably assess effect modification by age, sex, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors.

3.4. Nonlinear relationship between HALP and RA

restricted cubic splines analysis identified a significant nonlinear association between ln(HALP) and RA prevalence (P for nonlinearity <.05; Figure 3). The relationship exhibited an inverse trend, with increasing ln(HALP) levels corresponding to progressively reduced RA prevalence. This inverse correlation was most pronounced at lower ln(HALP) values, while the slope of the curve was attenuated at higher levels, suggesting a potential threshold effect.

3.5. Variable selection for cross-sectional discriminative model

Prior to model development, we applied least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression to identify key predictors from all available covariates. The optimal penalty parameter (λ = 0.006) was selected through 10-fold cross-validation (Fig. 4). Eight variables retained nonzero coefficients: age, sex, HALP score, hypertension, smoking status, diabetes, PIR, and BMI. These variables were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to construct the final RA prediction nomogram.

3.6. Evaluation of discriminative performance for prevalent RA

To assess the discriminative ability of the HALP score and RA discriminative model, ROC curves were constructed, and the corresponding AUC was computed (Fig. 6A). As illustrated in Figure 6, the HALP score demonstrated modest predictive performance (AUC = 0.562; 95% CI: 0.549–0.575), whereas the potential discriminative model exhibited significantly superior discrimination (AUC = 0.747; 95% CI: 0.737–0.758), with a sensitivity of 69.44% and specificity of 69.42%. To facilitate clinical implementation, we developed a nomogram integrating the key predictors from the final model (Fig. 5). This visually intuitive tool assigns weighted points to each variable based on its relative contribution to RA. The aggregate score, derived by summing the individual component scores, corresponds to the estimated probability of RA presence, with higher scores indicating a greater risk. Furthermore, decision curve analysis revealed that the model provided a greater net benefit than both the “treat all” and “treat none” approaches across a clinically relevant range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 6B), indicating modest discriminative performance for cross-sectional identification. However, the contribution of HALP to this model remains unclear because incremental value testing was not performed.

4. Discussion

This study leveraged data from the NHANES spanning 1999–2018 to investigate the association between the HALP score and RA. The final analytic sample included 34,079 participants, of whom 2166 had RA. Multivariable logistic regression analyses demonstrated a statistically significant inverse association between higher HALP scores and RA prevalence (P for trend <.05 for all models), suggesting that elevated HALP levels were associated with decreased odds of RA. This association persisted across progressively adjusted models, including an unadjusted model, a sociodemographic-adjusted model (Model 1), and a fully adjusted model incorporating lifestyle and clinical covariates (Model 2). Subgroup analyses showed generally similar point estimates across most strata, with no statistically significant evidence of effect modification (all interaction P > .05). However, given the limited statistical power due to small numbers of RA cases within individual subgroups- particularly for complex intersections of multiple covariates: these results should be interpreted as exploratory. The apparent consistency across subgroups does not definitively rule out clinically meaningful heterogeneity. Future studies with larger, clinically confirmed RA cohorts are needed to rigorously assess whether the HALP-RA association varies by age, sex, comorbidities, or lifestyle factors. We constructed a cross-sectional discriminative model incorporating the HALP score and clinical variables. Although the model showed moderate discriminative ability, this was mainly driven by conventional clinical factors. The marginal contribution of HALP was not validated by incremental value analysis.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to propose the HALP score as a potential biomarker for disease stratification in RA. Originally developed and validated for prognostic assessment in oncology,[15,16] the HALP score extends beyond prior research that has focused on isolated inflammatory or nutritional markers, such as NLR[10]or PNI.[14] By integrating multidimensional hematologic and nutritional parameters, the HALP score provides a more robust assessment of immune-nutritional dysregulation, a key contributor to RA pathogenesis.

RA, a chronic autoimmune condition marked by persistent systemic inflammation and aberrant immune responses, is the leading cause of inflammatory arthropathy worldwide.[21,22] Epidemiological trends project a significant burden escalation, with global RA cases expected to nearly double from 17.6 million in 2020 to 31.6 million over the next 3 decades.[23] Disease progression and severity in RA are modulated by 3 key pathophysiological factors: Nutritional status,[24] immune dysfunction,[25] and systemic inflammatory burden.[26] Previous studies have established associations between RA and various inflammatory markers and nutritional deficiency indicators.[27] The observed inverse association between HALP score and RA likely reflects the composite biomarker’s ability to capture multiple pathological pathways: Hypoalbuminemia reflects malnutrition and chronic inflammation, correlating with RA disease activity.[28,29] Anemia (reduced HGB) is a frequent RA comorbidity that may exacerbate tissue hypoxia and oxidative stress.[30] Lymphopenia/thrombocytosis indicates immune dysregulation and prothrombotic states associated with joint destruction and cardiovascular risk.[25,26] These findings align with existing evidence that RA progression is driven by the interplay between systemic inflammation and nutritional deficits.[24,27] The inverse association between HALP and RA is biologically plausible, as RA systematically affects all components of this composite score. Chronic inflammation in RA leads to anemia through interleukin-6-mediated hepcidin induction, hypoalbuminemia due to nutritional deficits and inflammatory catabolism, lymphopenia from immune dysregulation, and thrombocytosis as an acute-phase response. Therefore, HALP likely serves as an integrated measure of RA-related systemic inflammation and nutritional impairment rather than a predisposing factor.

The HALP score provides an integrated assessment of nutritional status and systemic inflammation.[31] Reduced HALP levels, reflecting decreases in ALB, hemoglobin, lymphocyte count, or increases in platelet count, are associated with heightened inflammatory responses, elevated thrombotic risk, malnutrition, and impaired immune function.[32] This composite measure has demonstrated prognostic value across various conditions, including cardiovascular mortality[33]and osteoarthritis progression,[34] establishing a theoretical foundation for its potential usefulness in RA. Notably, our analysis accounted for the right-skewed, non-normal distribution of HALP scores in the NHANES database by applying a logarithmic transformation, ensuring compliance with statistical modeling assumptions. In our study, the HALP score emerged as a robust measure of systemic inflammation in patients with RA, demonstrating strong predictive characteristics for RA disease stratification.

This study offers several important contributions to the field. First, we identify the HALP score as a potential inflammatory-nutritional biomarker for RA assessment. To our knowledge, this is the first study to establish this association. Second, our analysis leverages nationally representative data from the NHANES (1999–2018),[17] incorporating 34,079 participants (including 2166 RA cases) with appropriate sample weighting to ensure population generalizability. Third, we used rigorous methodological approaches, including logarithmic transformation of HALP scores, to address distributional concerns and comprehensive multivariable adjustment. Finally, we constructed a cross-sectional discriminative model that distinguishes individuals with prevalent RA with moderate performance. It is important to note that our cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The observed association between lower HALP scores and RA may reflect the consequences of established disease, including chronic inflammation, anemia of chronic disease, and malnutrition, rather than predisposing risk factors.

This is a cross-sectional study, which does not allow causal inference or prospective prediction. The observed inverse association between lower HALP score and RA should be interpreted as reverse causality: RA is a chronic systemic inflammatory disorder that directly causes anemia, hypoalbuminemia, lymphopenia, and thrombocytosis: all of which lower the HALP score. Therefore, HALP is a concurrent biomarker of disease-related inflammation and malnutrition, not a predictive risk factor for RA onset.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the diagnosis of RA was based on self-report without serological confirmation (RF/anti-CCP). The weighted prevalence of 4.85% is substantially higher than general population estimates (0.5%–1.0%), suggesting likely misclassification (e.g., inclusion of osteoarthritis), which limits the specificity of HALP as a biomarker for RA. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Third, we did not adjust for RA-related medications. Conventional and biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and glucocorticoids directly and profoundly affect hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte count, and platelet count: each a core component of the HALP score. Given that medication history is unavailable in NHANES, we could not distinguish whether lower HALP scores reflect RA disease activity or treatment-induced hematological changes, representing a major source of unmeasured confounding. Fourth, key variables were self-reported, introducing possible measurement bias. Moreover, the incremental value of the HALP score over conventional clinical predictors was not assessed using formal methods such as AUC comparison, net reclassification improvement, or integrated discrimination improvement. Therefore, the actual added value of HALP for RA discrimination remains unproven. Fifth, our model lacks external validation and does not incorporate established RA biomarkers (RF, anti-CCP, CRP). Finally, the generalizability of our findings may be limited to the U.S. population.

This study identified a significant nonlinear association between the HALP score and RA incidence. We developed and validated a robust RA case identification model incorporating HALP, demographic factors, and comorbidities, which demonstrated strong discriminative performance (AUC = 0.747) and clinical applicability. The constructed nomogram visually illustrates the association of selected variables with prevalent RA, serving as a hypothesis-generating framework rather than a clinical decision tool. Although these findings raise the hypothesis that nutritional or anti-inflammatory interventions might be relevant in RA patients with low HALP scores, such speculation remains untested. Given the cross-sectional design and unmeasured confounding, this index should not currently be used to guide clinical decisions in primary care or rheumatology practice. Our findings suggest HALP as a potential inflammatory-nutritional biomarker associated with prevalent RA, warranting further validation in clinically confirmed cohorts. Further research is warranted to validate its prognostic value and explore its mechanistic role in RA pathogenesis.

5. Conclusion

This study establishes an inverse association between HALP score and self-reported RA in a large US sample. However, due to substantial prevalence inflation, potential misclassification, and unadjusted pharmacological confounding, these findings require validation using clinically confirmed RA cases before any clinical application. Unmeasured confounding from RA medications cannot be ruled out, and further studies with detailed medication data are warranted. Therefore, the HALP score should not currently be used for RA case identification or risk prediction in clinical practice.
