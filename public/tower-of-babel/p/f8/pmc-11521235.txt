Genetic Markers of Spina Bifida in an Indian Cohort
Authors: Prabudh Goel, Mahima Sharma, Himani Kaushik, Sourabh Kumar, Harpreet Singh, Vishesh Jain, Anjan Kumar Dhua, Devendra Kumar Yadav, Neeta Kumar, Sandeep Agarwala
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:To identify the genetic markers of spina bifida through a systematic survey of the exome in an Indian cohort.Materials and Methods:Three consecutive patients (P1: 1 year, male; P2: 2.8 years, male; and P3: 10 years, female) with spina bifida (lumbosacral meningomyelocele) underwent whole-exome sequencing (libraries: SureSelect Human All Exon V8; sequencing: 2 * 150 bp paired-end run, 100×) with NovaSeq 6000. Data analysis was performed using SMART-One™ (secondary analysis) and SMARTer™ (tertiary analysis) for automated quality check, alignment (GRCh38/hg38), variant calling, annotation (ClinVar, OMIM, avsnp150, 1000 Genomes v5b, ExAC v0.3, gnomAD v4.0, and esp6500vi2all v0.0.25), v0.0.25), interpretation. The pathogenic and likely pathogenic (ClinVar/ InterVar), non-synonymous, exonic markers (read depth ≥ 5) were matched with the Familial Neural Tube Defects (Version 1.10) panel (FNTD panel).Results:Pathogenic variants overlapping with the FNTD panel were MTRR, CC2D2A, and ZIC2 in P1 and P2, TGIF1 in P1 only, and none in P3. Novel pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants common to all three patients were PRUNE1, PKD1, PDZD2, and DAB2 in the homozygous state as well as in the heterozygous state, PLK1 and NLGN2. The possible role of such markers in etiopathogenesis was explored through a literatur search.Conclusions:The genetic landscape of the spina bifida in an Indian cohort is diverse compared to that reported from other parts of the world. A comprehensive catalog of single-nucleotide variants in the etiopathogenesis of the spina bifida on a background of the Familial Neural Tube Defects Panel has been generated.

INTRODUCTION

Spina bifida continues to have a high prevalence (incidence of 3.9–8.8/1000 live births)[1] and adverse outcomes (motor sequelae and other neurological impairments and bladder–bowel dysfunction) despite optimal management. Only 60% of these patients are community ambulators and 80% are socially continent.[2] The risk of recurrence in the siblings of affected individuals is very high (50 times) and portends a strong genetic component;[34] the reported heritability for the malformation is about 60%.[5] Syndromic associations can be identified in about 10% of the cases of the spina bifida. The etiopathogenesis of the malformation cannot be attributed to a single genetic locus; the phenotype is a result of a combination of variants at multiple loci involving multiple genes and their interactions with environmental factors (omnigenic model of the spina bifida).[6]

Studies in mice have led to the identification of more than 240 genetic loci underlying the etiology of neural tube defects;[7] most of them pertain to the cellular processes involving the cell apoptosis, DNA transcription, planar cell polarity (PCP) pathways, or canonical Wnt/beta-catenin pathway.[8] Commonly implicated genetic loci in human beings include VANGL1 gene, VANGL2 gene, FUZ gene, the CELSR1 gene, and the TBXT gene.[9101112] The genes involved in the homocysteine–folate metabolic pathways such as the SNP R653Q polymorphism in the MTHFR1 gene,[13] MTHFR 1298 A-C combined with MTHFR 677 C-T alternation,[14] methionine synthase polymorphism,[15] and the 5-methyltetrahydrofolate-homocysteine methyltransferase reductase polymorphism (MTRR)[16] have also been implicated. Generation of protein-truncating variant and transcription factor defects as a consequence of loss-of-function de novo variants of SHROOM3, PAX3, GRHL3, and MYO1E genes have been identified through whole-exome sequencing.[171819] Novel genes like FREM2 and DISP1 have also been implicated in spina bifida.[5]

The authors have performed a systematic survey of the genetic architecture of spina bifida in Indian children to identify the genetic markers of the disease and compared them with those enlisted in the Familial Neural Tube Defects (NTDs) panel. The available information on the novel genetic markers has been explored to enhance the understanding of the potential mechanisms of interactions that contribute to the development of the phenotype.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a prospective study on three consecutive patients of spina bifida.

Peripheral venous blood (2.5 ml) was used for extracting genomic DNA (QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit).[20] Libraries were prepared using SureSelect Human All Exon V8 and sequenced in a 2 * 150 bp paired-end run with NovaSeq 6000.

Downstream analysis was performed using the SMART-One™ (secondary analysis) and SMARTer™ (tertiary analysis)[21] for automated quality check, alignment (GRCh38/hg38 reference genome), variant calling, annotation, and interpretation (public content sources including ClinVar, OMIM, avsnp150, 1000 Genomes v5b, ExAC v0.3, gnomAD v4.0, and esp6500vi2all v0.0.25 were utilized for annotation).

The following criteria were applied to identify the genetic variants relevant to the disease: (i) pathogenic and likely pathogenic (ClinVar and InterVar),[22] (ii) nonsynonymous, (iii) exonic markers, and (iv) read depth of ≥5. The shortlisted markers were intersected with the Familial Neural Tube Defects (Version 1.10) panel.[23]

DISCUSSION

The Familial Neural Tube Defects (NTDs) gene panel (Version 1.10) includes the genes related to the PCP pathway amongst other relevant genes.[35] The panel includes 44 entities (all red: low evidence), prominent among them being B9D1, B9D2, CC2D2A, CCL2, CDON, CEP290, DLL3, FOXH1, and FUZ. We tested the exome of the patients in our study cohort and identified MTRR, CC2D2A, and ZIC genes in 2 of 3 patients (P1 and P2). The TGIF1 gene was present in only one patient (P1). Forty of forty-four markers included in the gene panel were absent in each of the three patients. The third patient P3 did not exhibit even a single marker included in the gene panel. Such findings highlight the diversity of the genetic landscape and variations across different populations. The genetic distance of the Indian cohort may be related to geographical factors or ethnic roots. The findings from the Western world may not apply to our population, similiter.

The development of spina bifida or neural tube defect is a result of complex interplay between a multitude of nutritional, genetic, and environmental factors.[2] The phenotype is consequent to defective closure of the neural tube during early embryonic development. Cell motility, cell cycle regulation, and cell signaling are the key cellular mechanisms underlying the etiopathogenesis of the spina bifida. The motility of cells at the edges of the neural plate migrates and assists in folding of the plate into a tube. Disruption in the cell cycle can lead to developmental defects. Signaling pathways are essential to guide the proliferation, differentiation, and migration of cells during neural tube closure. The study has identified genetic variants of spina bifida affecting the cell–cell adhesions (PKD1, DAB2, and NLGN2),[36] cell proliferation (PRUNE1), cell cycle regulation (PLK1),[37] cell signaling (PDZD2), and cell motility (PRUNE1 and DAB2).[38]

In elucidating the specific mutations observed, the presence of a premature stop codon at position R211 in PRUNE1 leading to the truncation of the protein was discerned. Similarly, in PKD1, the identified mutation at position S2868 resulted in premature termination. Analogously, the mutations in PLK1 (S439X), NLGN2 (E320X), PDZD2 (L667X), and DAB2 (K180X) led to truncated forms of their respective proteins.

PRUNE1 (Prune exopolyphosphatase) is a protein-coding gene. It belongs to the DHH (Asp-His-His) phosphoesterase protein superfamily of molecules and associated with cell migration, motility, and adhesion. During fetal development, PRUNE1 is highly expressed in the central nervous system and influences cell migration and proliferation through protein–protein interactions. It also influences postnatal neurodevelopment and metastatic processes. Pathogenic variants of the PRUNE1 gene with autosomal recessive inheritance has been implicated in the etiopathogenesis of microcephaly, microtonia, intellectual disability, autism, and neurodevelopmental disorders. PRUNE1 gene is considered relevant in the diagnostic workup of any child presenting with dysmorphic features, developmental delay, microcephaly, central hypotonia, peripheral spasticity, delayed myelination, cerebral atrophy, and thin corpus callosum.[39] Although the three factors have not been linked to the development of the spina bifida in a row, PRUNE1 interacts with the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, which regulates cell growth, survival, and metabolism and PI3K-AKT pathway, in turn, has been associated with risk of neural tube defects.[40] It seems possible that disruptions in PRUNE1 expression or function could impact the PI3K-AKT pathway which will lead to aberrant signaling cascades and affect the neural tube development adversely. Yet, to the best of the author’s knowledge, this is the first time that PRUNE1 gene has been observed or implicated directly in the context of the spina bifida.

PKD1 or polycystic kidney disease 1 is a protein-coding gene and is associated with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease. The encoded polycystin-1 protein is a transmembrane protein involved in cell proliferations, apoptosis, cell–cell, and cell–matrix interactions, cell signaling, and regulation of various other cellular processes. Besides the renal tissue, the gene is also expressed in brain cortex, cerebellum, and hippocampus; the expression of PKD1 is much higher in the kidney.[26] It plays an important role in neuronal migration and differentiation during embryonic development. PKD1 is expressed widely in the neural tube of human embryos aged 5–6 weeks.[41] Homozygous PKD1 knockout mice have been shown to develop spina bifida occulta phenotype.[4243] PKD1 is also localized to primary cilia; cilia-mediated signaling pathways play important roles in neural tube formation, including patterning, proliferation, and differentiation. PKD1 polymorphisms have also been associated with a predisposition to febrile seizures, schizophrenia, and bipolar disorders as well.

None of our patients had an overt renal problem at the time of participation in this study. However, spina bifida can predispose to end-stage renal failure because of neurogenic damage; the propensity toward the same may be dictated by the presence or absence of PDK1 gene.

PDZD2 gene is a protein-coding gene; the protein share sequence similarity with interleukin 16 and localizes to the endoplasmic reticulum. It may be cleaved by a caspase; the resulting peptide has 2 PDZ protein–protein interaction domains involved in organizing signaling complexes at cell junctions. PDZD2 has been implicated in various cellular processes, including the regulation of ion channels, protein trafficking, and cell signaling. mRNA and protein of PDZD2 have been demonstrated in various regions of the developing brain (connective tissue and cytoplasm). Inherited, homozygous, and predicted damaging mutation in PDHD2 have been identified in the context of sacral agenesis in caudal regression syndrome.[44] It is expressed in both large diameter and peripherin positive small diameter neurons in the dorsal root ganglia and binds to a wide range of voltage-gated sodium channels in such neurons. Expression of PDZD2 has been reported in the para-axial mesoderm adjacent to the differentiating neural tube.[45]

The gene is also known to be involved in primary prostate tumors and has a role to play in early tumorigenesis.

DAB2 is a protein-coding gene. It encodes a mitogen-responsive phosphoprotein involved with cell signaling pathways, cell arrangement or migration, and differentiation of stem cells and receptor endocytosis. DAB2 has been shown to inhibit NGF-mediated neurite outgrowth in vitro. It is expressed spatiotemporally during the development of the central nervous system in mammals and postulated to be playing a critical role in early CNS and myeloid cell development. The expression in the nonneuronal cells inside the central nervous system like the circumventricular organs, secretory cells, immature neuroepithelial cells, endothelial cells, and mononuclear phagocytes is consistent with the cell surface positioning function of DAB2. Disruption in DAB2 expression or function in mice leads to neural tube closure defects including exencephaly.[46]

DAB2 has also been described as a tumor suppressor gene and a key regulator of anti- and protumorigenic pathways. Loss of DAB2 expression has been linked to malignancies of ovary, lung, and breasts.

PLK1 gene is a protein-coding gene; the protein is a serine/threonine protein kinase which regulates the cell cycle through its different processes during the M phase such as chromosomal maturation, removal of cohesins from chromosomal arms, formation of mitotic spindle and completion of cell division. PLK1 orchestrates a symphony of cellular events during fetal development and the levels are remarkable higher in fetal brain as compared to adult tissues. It is expressed in the brain during mitosis and neurogenesis; it also contributes to the formation of neural circuits and synaptic connections inside the spine. The mitotic spindle positioning pathway which is required for normal neural development is PLK1 dependent. HMMR (hyaluronan-mediated motility receptor; a nonmotor protein) is a key component of this pathway and loss of HMMR results in neonatal deaths and defective neural development and pleiotropic phenotypes through incorrect positioning of the spindle during cell division.[47]

It is also a proto-oncogene; due to overexpression in the tumor cells, it is being targeted for therapy of malignancies including rhabdomyosarcoma and neuroblastoma.

NLGN2 is a protein-coding gene. The encoded protein neuroligin 2 is involved in synaptic function, particularly in the formation and maintenance of synapses between neurons in the brain. It anchors the pre- and postsynaptic neurons together by interacting with presynaptic proteins called neurexins. Malfunction of NLGN2 has been linked to autism, schizophrenia, epilepsy, anxiety disorders, and intellectual disabilities. However, the published literature does not comment upon the role of NLGN2 in spina bifida or neural tube defects.

Understanding the genetic basis of the spina bifida and integrating this information into surgical practice is essential to offer better outcomes through a personalized and targeted surgical approach to the patients. Genotype–phenotype correlation can provide valuable insights into the severity and prognosis of the disease. The treating team may use this information to identify the high-risk patients in advance, better preoperative preparation, anticipate potential challenges, and plan interventions appropriately. Akin to the role of computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging over conventional radiography, genetic insights can transform the holistic management of the patient. They can also pave the way for novel treatment modalities through gene therapy and targeted pharmacological interventions and premarital genetic counseling.

CONCLUSIONS

The current study has attempted to generate a comprehensive catalog of single-nucleotide variants in the etiopathogenesis of the spina bifida on a background of Familial Neural Tube Defects Panel and underscored the significance of specific genetic markers and their truncating mutations. A detailed molecular characterization of the genetic aberrations and respective consequences at the molecular levels are essential to understand the biology of the malformation and lay the groundwork for potential therapeutic interventions and personalized medicine strategies.

The novel variants identified in this study have been reported to ClinVar with accession IDs SCV005046964 (PRUNE1), SCV005046967 (PKD1), SCV005046970 (PDZD2), SCV005046973 (DAB2), SCV005046975 (PLK1) and SCV005046976 (NLGN2).
