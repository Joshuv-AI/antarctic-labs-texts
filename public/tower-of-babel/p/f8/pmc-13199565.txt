Fast [177Lu]Lu-PSMA SPECT/CT with dual-energy acquisition and Monte Carlo-based scatter correction
Authors: Arda Könik, Benjamin Auer, Shir Hazut Krauthammer, Praful Ravi, Thomas S C Ng, Heather A Jacene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeWhole-body post-therapy SPECT/CT plays an important role in [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-617 (LuPSMA) radiopharmaceutical therapy for verifying biodistribution, assessing treatment response, and performing radiation dosimetry. However, conventional dual-head NaI(Tl)-based scanners typically require lengthy scans, which can be resource-intensive and challenging for patient compliance, limiting their routine use in clinical practice. This study explores reducing acquisition duration using dual-energy peaks to increase count statistics and Monte Carlo-based scatter correction (MCS) to maintain image quality and quantitative accuracy under low-count conditions.MethodsLuPSMA scans (33 studies from 29 patients) covering mid-thigh to vertex (3 bed positions) were acquired using a dual-head NaI(Tl)-based scanner (Siemens Intevo) with both 208 and 113 keV photopeaks and corresponding scatter windows. Full-duration 12 min/bed acquisitions were reconstructed using MCS (Hermia SPECT Reconstruction, Hermes) and the scanner’s energy-window-based scatter correction (EWS), where MCS reconstruction at 12 min/bed served as the reference standard. Shorter-duration projections (1.5, 3, and 6 min/bed) were synthetically generated and reconstructed using MCS. Image quality metrics, including bias, coefficient of variation (CV), contrast ratio (CR), and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), were evaluated across segmented organs and tumors.ResultsMCS reconstructions at 1.5–6 min/bed showed < 6% bias in mean organ uptake (median ~ 2%) relative to the 12 min/bed MCS (reference), significantly lower (p < 0.01) than the bias for 12 min/bed EWS (median ~ 3%). For mean tumor uptake, 3–6 min/bed MCS showed < 10% bias (median ~ 5%), with no significant difference from 12 min/bed EWS (median ~ 6%). CR remained consistent across all reconstructions (median ~ 5). MCS at 12 min/bed had slightly better CV, CR, and SNR than EWS at 12 min/bed, though differences were not statistically significant. MCS at 6 min/bed was not significantly different from either full-duration reconstruction. Visual assessments supported these findings, with MCS benefits over EWS more pronounced at lower count levels.ConclusionThese findings indicate that using dual-energy acquisition with MCS, scan times as short as 3–6 min/bed on conventional NaI(Tl)-based scanners maintain sufficient image quality for lesion detection, therapy monitoring, and dosimetry. These reductions in scan time could facilitate the routine implementation of post-LuPSMA SPECT/CT in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40658-026-00864-6.Keywords: Radiopharmaceutical therapy, PSMA, SPECT/CT, Scatter correction, Dosimetry

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the second most common cancer in men and ranks fifth in cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. While localized prostate cancer generally has a favorable prognosis [2], outcomes for patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) remain poor [3]. [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-617 (LuPSMA) is a radiopharmaceutical therapy (RPT) for mCRPC that targets overexpressed prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) on prostate cancer cells and delivers short-range beta-particle radiation (~ 1 mm in tissue), effectively damaging tumors with minimal impact on surrounding tissue [4]. In addition to beta-particles, 177Lu emits gamma rays at 113 keV (6.2%) and 208 keV (10.4%) [5], allowing imaging of LuPSMA biodistribution with single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) combined with x-ray computed tomography (CT).

Whole-body post-therapy SPECT/CT imaging plays an important role for verifying LuPSMA uptake in tumors and organs relative to pre-treatment PSMA positron emission tomography(PET)/CT findings and in assessing treatment response. A recent retrospective study reported that LuPSMA SPECT/CT changed clinical management for nearly half of the patients imaged [6]. Furthermore, SPECT/CT-based dosimetry allows personalized treatment planning by quantifying absorbed dose in tumors and critical organs [7]. However, these multi-bed SPECT/CT acquisitions, typically lasting 30–45 min on conventional dual-head scanners, are resource-intensive and can be challenging for patients, who must remain still for prolonged periods. Multiple scans throughout treatment present a substantial burden for patients and clinical facilities. The recent FDA approval of LuPSMA before taxane chemotherapy is expected to increase the demand for post-LuPSMA imaging. Reduced scan times could facilitate more efficient and practical implementation in routine clinical practice.

While faster scans can be performed with the cadmium zinc telluride (CZT)-based SPECT/CT scanners equipped with 360° detector modules surrounding the patient [8–10], their limited availability and higher cost restrict widespread adoption. Therefore, achieving similar scan time reductions on conventional NaI(Tl)-based scanners is essential for broader clinical impact. Imaging on conventional scanners is typically acquired using a single photopeak energy window centered at 208 keV [11], disregarding the 113 keV emissions. However, despite its lower emission abundance, the 113 keV emissions can contribute nearly half of the total detected counts due to higher detection efficiency in standard 1-cm-thick NaI(Tl) crystals [12]. Thus, incorporating a dual-energy window acquisition could improve count statistics, supporting shorter scan durations.

The main challenge of imaging with the 113 keV window is downscatter from 208 keV photons, which may undergo single or multiple Compton scatters in the patient, collimator, or crystal (i.e., iodine escape) or penetrate the collimator and crystal without interaction and subsequently backscatter at ~ 115 keV [13], registering in the 113 keV window despite originating from 208 keV photons. Another issue is the contamination of 113 keV scatter window from characteristic x-rays (72–88 keV) from a lead-alloy collimator [13]. While standard energy-window-based scatter correction (EWS) is often inadequate for addressing these issues, advanced methods such as Monte Carlo-based scatter correction (MCS) can provide more accurate compensation, thereby improving image quality and quantitative accuracy. Studies using NEMA, anthropomorphic, and Jaszczack phantoms filled with ¹⁷⁷Lu [13–15], as well as realistic [177Lu]Lu-DOTATATE SPECT/CT Monte Carlo simulations [12], have recommended using advanced scatter correction methods such as Effective Source Scatter Estimation (ESSE) [16, 17] and Analytical Photon Distribution–Interpolated (APDI) [18] for accurate quantification when employing 113 keV window, where EWS was not accurate.

Despite findings from phantom studies, clinical evaluation of the 113 keV photopeak or dual-energy acquisition on conventional scanners remains limited. This retrospective study evaluates the feasibility of reducing scan time in post-LuPSMA SPECT/CT imaging by employing a dual-energy approach with MCS across a range of scan durations.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board with a waiver of informed consent (Dana-Farber Cancer Institute Protocol No: 20–259). We analyzed 33 post-therapy LuPSMA SPECT/CT scans acquired from 29 patients (two patients had scans at treatment cycle 1 and 3) treated as part of standard clinical care between May and September 2024. The median patient age was 70 years (range: 55–85), with a median injected activity of 7.3 GBq (range: 3.8–7.6), a median post-injection imaging time of 20 h (range: 1 h–3 days), and a median body mass index (BMI) of 29.2 (range: 19.9–40.4).

SPECT/CT acquisition and EWS reconstruction protocol

Whole-body LuPSMA SPECT/CT studies from mid-thigh to vertex were acquired using a conventional dual-head Symbia Intevo SPECT/CT scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with dual-photopeak and corresponding scatter windows as listed in Table 1. 208 and 113 keV photopeak windows (208 PP and 113 PP) and 208 keV lower scatter window (208 LW) were set to 20% and 10% width, respectively, in accordance with MIRD Pamphlet No. 26 [12]. Narrower scatter windows were applied adjacent to the 113 keV photopeak: the 113 keV upper scatter window (113 UW) was set to 10% to better match the scatter distribution within the photopeak, which includes both downscatter originating from 208 keV and scatter from 113 keV photons. The 113 keV lower scatter window (113 LW) was set to 5% to minimize contamination from characteristic lead x-rays. Note, the scatter windows were adjusted only for EWS, as MCS relies solely on the photopeaks.

Each bed position was scanned for 12 min in continuous mode (120 projections total) using auto-contouring over 360° with medium-energy-low-penetration (MELP) collimators. The total scan time for the three-bed acquisition, which covered up to 1117 mm axially with ~2 mm overlaps between adjacent beds, was approximately 40 min, including 36 min for SPECT, 2 min for bed transitions (1 min per translation), and additional time for the x-ray topogram and CT. Projections were reconstructed using the scanner’s Flash3D iterative algorithm (OSEM: 10 iterations 8 subsets, with point spread function modeling, no pre/post image filtering) with low-dose CT-based attenuation correction (AC) and EWS. Projections for the 113 and 208 keV windows were first independently reconstructed using their respective attenuation maps, then averaged into a single reconstruction.

Synthetic images at reduced acquisition time

Due to the lack of list-mode capability on the Intevo scanner, projections simulating shorter acquisition times could not be generated from a single acquisition. Therefore, we adopted an alternative approach in which synthetic projections for 1.5, 3, and 6 min/bed were created by applying Poisson noise to the original 12 min/bed projection data. Using a Python script, Poisson noise was separately applied to the five energy windows for each dataset. Each pixel value in the projection images was scaled to a lower count, and then noise was added by sampling from a Poisson distribution with a mean equal to the new lower pixel value. The modified and original projection data were then reconstructed using dual-energy MCS Hermia HybridReconTM-Oncology 4.0.6 software (Hermes Medical Solutions, Stockholm, Sweden) with the same parameters as in EWS, except that 10 iterations and 10 subsets were used in this case. In this MCS approach, scattered photons, including the downscatter are modelled for each projection view [19]. Independent reconstructions with MCS + AC from two energy windows were averaged into a single reconstruction as described above. As a substudy, 3 min/bed EWS reconstructions were also obtained for three patients to compare with the corresponding 3 min/bed MCS reconstructions. The 12 min/bed dataset reconstructed with MCS, served as the reference standard. This choice was supported by prior phantom studies demonstrating superior performance of advanced scatter correction methods over EWS [13–15, 18] and our initial visual evaluation of patient reconstructions.

Segmentation and data preparation

Organ and tumor segmentations were performed using the MIM SurePlan™ MRT version 7.3.3 (MIM Software Inc., Beachwood, Ohio). CT-based organ contours for bone, lungs, liver, kidney, and spleen were generated using MIM Contour ProtégéAI, an AI-based automatic segmentation tool, and transferred to the registered SPECT reconstructions. Tumor segmentations were derived directly from the SPECT images using the MIM’s gradient-based PET edge tool. All visible lesions with volumes ≥ 0.5 ml and demonstrating sufficient uptake on SPECT images were included.

Images reconstructed with MCS at 1.5, 3, and 6 min/bed scan durations were linearly scaled by factors of 8, 4, and 2, respectively, to match the reference count level at 12 min/bed. Given the inherent differences between EWS (Siemens Intevo) and MCS (Hermes) reconstructions, patient-specific scale factors were determined from whole-body mean values and applied to the EWS reconstructions. Further details in data preparation are presented in the supplemental file.

Figure 2 presents box plots of total projection counts across all 33 studies for each energy window and for separate acquisition beds starting from mid-thigh (bed 1) to vertex (bed 3), as well as total counts from all three beds. Variations in counts across the patient cohort are primarily due to the differences in post-injection imaging time (delta time) and, in some cases, lower administered doses. The count ratios between the two photopeak windows (113 PP / 208 PP) showed a mildly decreasing trend from thigh to vertex and remained relatively consistent among patients, with mean ± SD of 1.57 ± 0.08 for 1st bed (thigh–pelvis), 1.51 ± 0.04 for 2nd bed (pelvis–chest), and 1.46 ± 0.10 for 3rd bed (chest–head). This trend is consistent with decreased tissue attenuation from lower to upper body regions, corresponding to reduced amount of downscatter from the patient’s body. A similar trend was observed in patients with high BMI (BMI > 35), with higher ratios (1.64 ± 0.06, 1.57 ± 0.04, and 1.53 ± 0.03, respectively), again indicating the relationship between tissue attenuation and the extent of downscatter. Supporting this observation, the ratio of the 113 keV upper scatter window to its photopeak (113 UW / 113 PP), used as a surrogate for downscatter, also showed a similar decreasing pattern (0.23 ± 0.01, 0.22 ± 0.01, and 0.21 ± 0.02, respectively). Similar trends were observed in patients with BMI > 35, with higher ratios (0.24 ± 0.01, 0.23 ± 0.01, and 0.22 ± 0.01, respectively), suggesting that downscatter contribution is not strongly dependent on patient size. All differences across bed positions were statistically significant (p < 0.0001). Similar bias trends were observed across individual organs (bone, lungs, liver, kidneys, and spleen). Among these, the spleen exhibited the highest mean % bias for all reconstructions (Supplemental Fig. 4). Using the median tumor volume (5 ml) as a cutoff, bias was higher for smaller tumors (< 5 mL) compared with larger tumors (> 5 ml) (Supplemental Fig. 5), consistent with reduced count statistics in smaller lesions. For MSC reconstructions, the difference in bias between small and large tumors decreased with increasing scan time, as quantified by the ratio of bias between small and large tumors: ratio of 1.5 at 1.5 min/bed, 1.3 at 3 min/bed, and 1.2 at 6 min/bed. This trend is consistent with smaller tumors being more susceptible to count deficiency and showing greater improvement with increased count statistics. In contrast, for EWS reconstructions, the bias ratio (1.3 at 12 min/bed) was similar to that observed for MSC at 3 min/bed despite higher count statistics. Overall, this suggests improved performance of MSC relative to EWS for smaller tumors.

Discussion

Post-RPT SPECT/CT has proven valuable in the management of patients undergoing LuPSMA treatment [21–23], motivating the need for more streamlined and practical imaging protocols. With growing RPT use, SPECT is increasingly utilized for response assessment, especially when PET access is limited. This underscores the need for simpler, shorter, and more patient friendly SPECT imaging. However, standardized guidelines for the post-LuPSMA SPECT/CT imaging protocols are lacking, with practices varying widely across institutions, ranging from 5 to ~ 40 min/bed for 2–3 bed positions [7, 23–30]. Dosimetry is not yet routinely implemented. For practical dosimetry, Madsen et al. [31] and Hänscheid et al. [32] introduced the theoretical foundation for single-time-point dosimetry, which has since been successfully implemented by several groups for 177Lu dosimetry [33–36]. In line with these efforts, we implemented protocol improvements aimed at reducing scan duration while maintaining quantitative accuracy and reasonable image quality for practical post-LuPSMA SPECT/CT and dosimetry. The continuous acquisition mode reduced the scan time by 3 min per bed position (a total of 9 min reduction for a 3-bed scan), which is particularly relevant for shorter scans. In this mode, detectors acquire data continuously throughout both rotational and radial motion providing approximately 3 min of additional counting time compared to a 60-step standard step-and-shoot acquisition, where data collection occurs only when the detectors are stationary. This counting time gain is proportional to the number of rotational steps (views) and independent of the scan duration per view. The dual-energy acquisition increased detected counts and MCS provided more accurate scatter compensation, especially under downscatter and low-count conditions where EWS performs poorly. The current version of the MCS method did not include collimator scatter modeling for 177Lu, which could further improve the scatter correction. Advanced noise reduction strategies, such as those leveraging deep learning approaches [23, 24, 37, 38], could further improve noise in low-count situations.

A series of publications from the SNMMI Lu-177 Dosimetry Challenge 2021 highlighted substantial variability in dosimetry estimates across participants [39–42]. Given the many sources of uncertainty in dosimetry, scan duration may not be as critical a contributor to overall error. Mean dose, averaged over dose values within tumor or organ, is typically reported for dose estimation and is closely related to mean uptake (or voxel-regions) [43], which tends to remain robust even in noisy reconstructions, as demonstrated in our study by bias measurements showing reasonable accuracy down to 1.5–3 min/bed scan durations. While maximum values were more sensitive to count levels and exhibited larger biases, they remained within an acceptable range (e.g., median bias: ~10% for tumors and organs at 3 min/bed) and their quantitative accuracy is less relevant for dosimetry.

Although high-quality images are always more desirable, in practice, scan duration and image quality should be balanced to meet diagnostic needs, while also considering scanner availability, patient comfort, and the increased risk of motion artifacts with longer acquisitions. For image quality assessment, we obtained CV, CR, and SNR metrics across scan durations (1.5–12 min/bed) for MCS and EWS at 12 min/bed. In terms of these metrics, MCS at 6 min/bed was not significantly different from either full-duration (12 min/bed) reconstruction. Visual assessment supported these findings, showing reasonable image quality for MCS at 3–6 min/bed (Fig. 3), though lesion detection at 1.5 min/bed was more challenging, potentially leading to false positives or missed lesions. Notably, the visual image quality was substantially better for MCS than EWS at 3 min/bed (Fig. 6), indicating the performance improvement with the MCS method over EWS at low count levels. Additionally, MCS at 3 and 12 min/bed showed comparable overall radiotracer distribution, with most lesions still identifiable, although with reduced certainty due to the increased noise. As discussed below, real-world image quality of short acquisitions would be slightly better than our simulated results. For shorter scans, image filtering and lower number of iterations can also be considered to suppress the noise, which would improve CV and SNR metrics as well.

In this study, we also provided a detailed projection count distribution across patients for the three beds separately and five different energy windows. We expect these data will be valuable for investigators in evaluating or optimizing their acquisition and reconstruction protocols. With our current energy window settings, we achieved similar count levels for all scatter windows despite their varying widths, ensuring sufficient counts for reliable scatter estimation. Downscatter accounts for a substantial portion of the detected counts in the 113 keV photopeak window, which was the main reason for using the MCS approach in this work. To further evaluate this effect, we analyzed the 113 PP / 208 PP and 113 UW / 113 PP count ratios, across the bed positions and patient BMIs. These ratios were only ~ 5% higher in patients with BMI > 35 compared to those with BMI < 25, indicating only a mild relationship between the tissue attenuation and extend of downscatter. This modest trend likely reflects two main competing effects with increasing tissue thickness: increased attenuation of primary 113 keV photons and increased downscatter from higher-energy photons into the 113 keV window, which partially offset each other.

While MSC and EWS are implemented using reconstruction frameworks from different vendors, reconstruction parameters were kept identical wherever possible, except for the number of subsets (MSC: 10 iterations × 10 subsets; EWS: 10 iterations × 8 subsets). In this study, the effective number of iterations was intentionally not adjusted as a function of count level. As a result, while the difference in subsets may have a modest influence on quantitative metrics, its impact is expected to be secondary to the effects of count reduction. Supporting this, a substudy performed using MSC that compared two effective iteration settings (100 vs. 80) across 15 lesions demonstrated a mean absolute difference of < 2%.

One limitation of this study was the lack of list-mode data. To simulate shorter scan durations, we applied Poisson noise to the clinical projections, although ideally this should have been applied to noise-free projections. As a result, the simulated low-count projections became noisier by a factor of 1.225 [44] than actual clinical projections at equivalent count levels. In fact, our phantom study with uniform activity (supplementary material) indicated that the reconstruction was noisier for the synthetic data (~ 20% higher SD and CV) compared to the real data. Therefore, we anticipate that real-world performance with shorter scans would be slightly less noisy than our simulated results. We also considered generating low-count datasets by discarding a subset of the projection frames, rather than altering the original count distribution with added noise. For example, to simulate 1.5, 3, and 6 min/bed scans, we would retain only 15, 30, and 60 projections, respectively, from the full 120-projection acquisition. Reconstructions using this method were noticeably less noisy than those generated using Poisson noise, supporting the expectation that real-world low-count projections will perform better than our simulations. However, several issues led us to prefer the Poisson-based approach. While discarding projections preserves the statistical properties of the acquired data, it also removes temporal variation, such as patient motion (e.g., respiratory and body motion), that is present in the full dataset. This alters the overall distribution of counts and can introduce mismatches with the full-count reconstruction. In fact, we observed substantial non-rigid misalignment in tumor locations in several cases using this approach. These temporal inconsistencies also impact attenuation and scatter corrections, which would further introduce discrepancies with the full-count reconstruction. It is worth noting that similar issues could arise even with list-mode data if the reduced dataset is not sampled randomly. Additionally, reduced angular sampling by removing projections may introduce artifacts unrelated to scan duration. There are also some implementation challenges with this approach. Reduced number of projections requires modification of numerous DICOM tags to ensure the reconstruction software properly interprets the modified data (a Python script provided in the supplementary material). Considering all these factors, the Poisson noise approach proved to be far simpler, required minimal modification, and was ultimately more suitable for the objective of this study.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of reducing post-LuPSMA SPECT/CT scan durations on conventional NaI(Tl)-based scanners by employing dual-energy window acquisition and Monte Carlo-based scatter correction. Quantitative metrics and visual assessments indicate that scan times on conventional NaI(Tl)-based scanners as short as 3–6 min/bed may be adequate for clinical tasks such as lesion detection, therapy monitoring, and dosimetry. Further improvements in image quality and reduction in scan duration may be achieved through optimization of reconstruction parameters, refinement of Monte Carlo-based scatter correction method, and application of deep learning-based noise-reduction techniques.
