Comparison of Kirschner wire versus volar locking plate fixation for distal radius fractures: clinical and functional outcomes
Authors: Okan Yiğit, Hayati Kart, Görkem Çat, Emrah Gökay Özgür, Erdem Koç, Veysel Burak Özmusul, Özgür Baysal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to compare the clinical and functional outcomes of Kirschner wire (K-wire) fixation versus volar locking plate (VLP) fixation for intra-articular distal radius fractures (DRFs). It also aimed to identify key patient- and fracture-related factors that influence the choice of surgical technique and to evaluate the responsiveness of specific patient-reported outcome measures in assessing recovery.Methods:A total of 201 consecutive patients with Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen/Orthopaedic Trauma Association–classified DRFs (Type A: n = 51; B: n = 90; C: n = 60) treated surgically between 2005 and 2022 were analyzed. Treatment allocation was based on fracture complexity and surgeon preference, resulting in a distribution where K-wire (n = 63) was primarily used for partial articular fractures (72.4% of K-wire cases were Type B), while VLP (n = 138) was preferred for complete articular fractures (83.6% of plate cases were Type C). Functional outcomes were assessed using Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (QuickDASH) (primary endpoint), Green-O’Brien, Gartland–Werley, and SF-12 at standardized intervals (6/12/24 months) during supervised physiotherapy.Results:Significant predictors of plate fixation included older age (58.1 ± 14.7 vs. 42.3 ± 12.1 years, P < .001), polytrauma (aOR: 2.4, 95% CI: 1.5-3.8), and complete articular fractures (83.6% Type C, P = .01). No statistically significant associations were found between injury type and fixation method (P = .20) or between complications and fixation method (4.8% vs. 2.2%, P = .41). Functional outcomes were comparable across all metrics, with no statistically significant differences observed: QuickDASH (34.9 ± 18.2 K-wire vs. 31.2 ± 16.8 plate, P = .146), Green-O’Brien (excellent-good: 76.2% vs. 82.6%, P = .552), Gartland–Werley (78.1% vs. 71.1% excellent-good, P = .591), and 12-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-12) (100% vs. 99.3% good results, P = .32). Multivariable regression identified reduction quality (β = 0.81, P < .01), not implant type, as the primary outcome determinant.Conclusion:This study suggests that anatomical reduction quality—not fixation method—is the primary determinant of functional success in DRFs, with both K-wire and VLP achieving comparable outcomes (QuickDASH/Gartland–Werley P > .05). K-wires represent a cost-effective alternative for partial articular fractures (72.4% of cases), while plates were more frequently used in complex fractures and polytrauma cases (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 2.4). Early rehabilitation (median 4 days post-op) and condition-specific metrics (QuickDASH, Green-O’Brien) are critical for optimal recovery, whereas SF-12 proves less sensitive for wrist-specific assessment. These findings advocate for surgeon-tailored technique selection based on fracture pattern and emphasize the need for multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to evaluate long-term outcomes.Level of Evidence:Level III, Therapeutic Study.

Introduction

Distal radius fractures (DRFs) are among the most prevalent and clinically significant injuries in orthopedic practice, accounting for approximately 20% of all fractures.1 Restoring functional integrity and biomechanical stability is critical to preserving patients’ quality of life, particularly given the association of DRFs with long-term disability in untreated or suboptimally managed cases.2 While surgical intervention is widely adopted for intra-articular or unstable fractures, the optimal fixation strategy, K-wire versus volar locking plate (VLP), remains contentious, with current literature highlighting multifactorial determinants of treatment selection, including fracture morphology (e.g., the Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen/Orthopaedic Trauma Association (AO/OTA) classification), patient-specific variables (e.g., age, comorbidities), and injury complexity (e.g., high-energy trauma, concomitant polytrauma).3,4

Despite the prevalence of DRFs, consensus on optimal postoperative assessment protocols remains limited.5 Standardized evaluation of functional outcomes—using tools like the QuickDASH and Gartland and Werley scores—is essential to bridge this gap and guide clinical decision-making.

This study, therefore, aimed to directly compare the clinical, radiographic, and functional outcomes of K-wire versus VLP fixation for intra-articular DRFs. A secondary objective was to identify multifactorial predictors (e.g., AO/OTA classification, trauma mechanism, polytrauma) influencing the selection of treatment technique in a real-world clinical setting.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study analyzed 201 consecutive patients with DRFs (AO/OTA types A/B/C) treated surgically with K-wire or VLP fixation followed by standardized physiotherapy at a tertiary orthopedic trauma center between 2005 and 2022. Approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Marmara University Faculty of Medicine on 28th February 2024 (Approval number: 09.2023.1448). Data were extracted from electronic health records supplemented by an archival review. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants and data collection

The study included skeletally mature patients (aged 18-92 years) with DRFs (AO/OTA classification) presenting through emergency services. Standardized follow-up assessments were conducted at 2, 4, 12, 24, 52, and 104 weeks postoperatively, evaluating: (1) demographic parameters (age, sex); (2) fracture characteristics (laterality, injury mechanism, AO/OTA classification, open/closed status); (3) treatment details (K-wire versus VLP fixation, bone graft requirement); and (4) functional outcomes (range-of-motion, grip strength, QuickDASH, Green-O’Brien, Gartland–Werley, and SF-12 scores). The choice of surgical technique (K-wire or VLP) was made at the discretion of the attending surgeon based on fracture morphology, complexity (as per AO/OTA classification), bone quality, and associated patient factors. This approach reflects real-world clinical practice rather than a randomized protocol. Consequently, the 2 groups represent distinct clinical scenarios. When K-wire fixation was chosen, it was most commonly used for extra-articular fractures (54.0% of K-wire cases were Type A). Conversely, when VLP fixation was chosen, it was most commonly used for partial articular fractures (40.6% of plate cases were Type B). However, plate fixation was the strongly preferred option for complete articular fractures (36.2% of plate cases were Type C, compared to only 15.8% of K-wire cases). This distribution is detailed in Table 1. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) lost to follow-up (n = 28), (2) incomplete medical/radiological records (n = 19), (3) concomitant ipsilateral upper extremity injuries (n = 9), (4) pre-existing cognitive or psychiatric disorders impairing compliance (n = 5), (5) skeletal immaturity (n = 3), and (6) pre-existing neurological/rheumatological conditions (n = 0). A total of 64 patients were excluded based on these criteria, resulting in a final analytical cohort of 201 patients. The participant flow diagram is provided in Figure 1. These criteria ensured homogeneous cohort analysis while maintaining clinical relevance. All consecutive eligible patients were analyzed according to their treatment modality (K-wire vs. volar plate) without matching, as this study aimed to reflect real-world clinical practice patterns. The study included all consecutive eligible patients treated between 2005 and 2022 to maximize generalizability. Given the risk of false-positive findings in subgroup analyses with limited sample size, primary outcome analyses were prioritized.

Radiographic analysis

All patients underwent standardized preoperative imaging, including anteroposterior (AP) and lateral radiographs plus computed tomography (CT) for articular involvement assessment. Intraoperative fluoroscopy-guided reduction and hardware placement. Postoperative imaging (AP/lateral views) was obtained immediately and at all follow-ups (2 weeks, 1/3/6/12/24 months) using fixed limb positioning protocols to ensure measurement consistency. Radiographic parameters were evaluated by the primary author (blinded to clinical outcomes) via Picture Archiving and Communication System (Sectra AB, v11.3) measurement tools, including:

Radial length, inclination, and volar tilt;

Radial length, inclination, and volar tilt;

Ulnar variance and articular step-off (≥1 mm defined as significant); and

We established these radiographic diagnostic criteria: (a) nonunion required both absence of bridging callus at 6 months and persistent symptoms;6 (b) malunion was defined as either radial inclination <15° or volar tilt >10°;7 and (c) articular incongruity required ≥2 mm displacement on CT, as this threshold shows clinical relevance in the literature.8 Inter-rater reliability was confirmed in a 20% random sample by a senior orthopedic surgeon (intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] = 0.89, 95% CI 0.82-0.93). All images were archived in the hospital’s Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine system.

Fracture classification and management

Preoperative radiographic assessment included standardized anteroposterior, lateral, and oblique wrist views along with CT scans for articular evaluation. Fractures were classified according to the AO/OTA system9 by 2 independent orthopedic surgeons, demonstrating excellent interobserver agreement (κ = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.81-0.91). The distribution of fracture types is presented in Table 2. This systematic approach facilitated a comprehensive evaluation of articular involvement and fracture complexity for treatment planning.

Surgery technique, implant selection, and peroperative management

Closed reduction was followed by fluoroscopy-guided percutaneous fixation using 3 2.5 mm K-wires, maintaining medial, lateral (styloid), and central (diaphyseal) column integrity. Radiocarpal alignment was confirmed in coronal/sagittal planes before short-arm splint application (20° flexion, 30° ulnar deviation). Procedures were performed without a tourniquet. Open reduction utilized a 40-mm volar modified Henry approach. Pronator quadratus was accessed via reverse L-incision and blunt dissection. Plate fixation was performed using the TST Volar Locking Distal Radius Plate (TST Medical Devices, Türkiye). The specific implant characteristics were as follows: plate thickness 2.5 mm, width 26.0 mm, and an inter-hole distance of 10.5 mm. Distal fixation was achieved with 2.4/2.7 mm locking screws for subchondral support, while 3.5 mm cortical screws ensured diaphyseal stability. The plate was positioned to preserve the watershed line (mean 1.5 mm proximal to the articular margin), and column continuity was verified. Bone grafting was employed at the surgeon’s discretion for cases with substantial metaphyseal comminution that created a void jeopardizing construct stability, or to fill defects caused by impaction or bone loss deemed to be at high risk for secondary collapse, based on intraoperative assessment under fluoroscopy and preoperative CT evaluation.10-12 This approach reflects the institution’s protocol for managing severe, osteopenic fractures (e.g., AO/OTA C3) and highly comminuted, unstable fracture patterns. A tourniquet was applied, and a 12 Fr mini-drain was placed for 24 hours postoperatively prior to identical splint immobilization.

Patient follow-up

All patients received standardized wound care with povidone-iodine dressings initiated on postoperative day 2, with simultaneous drain removal when present. For K-wire cases, pin-site care was performed using a 21G needle (0.8 × 40 mm) during dressing changes. Sutures were removed at 2 weeks postoperatively. Rehabilitation commenced immediately with finger mobility exercises under physiotherapist supervision, progressing to supervised grip and joint mobility training in the outpatient clinic (Figure 2). Physiotherapists followed a pre-approved video protocol to standardize exercise instruction. K-wire patients initiated passive wrist exercises after wire removal at 4 weeks, transitioning to active exercises following splint removal at 6 weeks. The plating cohort began passive wrist mobilization at 4 weeks (4× daily with elastic bandage support), advancing to active exercises at 6 weeks. This standardized rehabilitation protocol, initiating wrist motion at 4 weeks for both groups, was implemented to ensure consistency in postoperative care and to isolate the effect of the implant itself from variations in rehabilitation timing. Although this approach may not leverage the full biomechanical advantage of stable plate fixation for early motion, it allowed for a more direct comparison of the 2 fixation techniques under equivalent postoperative conditions. Follow-up occurred weekly for the first month, then at 8 and 12 weeks, with annual clinical and radiographic assessments. All functional outcomes were systematically documented in the physiotherapy unit (Figure 3). Exercise compliance was tracked via patient logs. Throughout the 17-year study period (2005-2022), the core principles of the postoperative rehabilitation protocol, specifically the timing for initiating finger mobility (immediately), passive wrist motion (at 4 weeks), and active exercises (at 6 weeks), remained consistent to ensure comparability across the cohort. This standardized approach was a cornerstone of the institution’s clinical pathway for DRFs. However, minor refinements in the specific exercises and instructional methods (e.g., the introduction of a standardized video protocol for physiotherapists in 2015) were implemented to reflect evolving best practices. To assess the potential impact of these minor temporal changes on the primary outcomes, a sensitivity analysis was performed by comparing functional results (QuickDASH scores) between the early (2005-2013) and late (2014-2022) phases of the study. This analysis confirmed the stability of the outcomes over time, with no statistically significant difference detected (P > .05), thereby supporting the internal validity of these findings across the study period.

Functional outcomes

Postoperative functional outcomes were assessed using QuickDASH (disability/symptoms), Green-O’Brien (pain/function/range of motion [ROM]/grip), Gartland–Werley (wrist function), and SF-12 (quality of life) scoring systems, administered by a blinded physiotherapist to minimize assessment bias. QuickDASH scores (11-item scale; 0 = no disability, 100 = complete disability) and Green-O’Brien results (100-point scale: 90-100 = excellent, <65 = poor) were categorized per established criteria. Gartland–Werley outcomes classified 0-2 as excellent and ≥21 as poor, while 12-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-12) scores reflected health status (50 = average). Figure 4 displays the progression of functional outcomes for both techniques throughout the study period.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 20.0 (2011; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Normality was assessed via Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests with Shapiro–Wilk confirmation for skewed distributions (P < .05 threshold). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD for normal distributions or median [interquartile range (IQR)] for non-normal distributions (verified by quantile-quantile [QQ]-plots), while categorical data are shown as frequencies (%). Between-group comparisons utilized Student’s t-tests for parametric data (with homoscedasticity confirmed via Levene’s test) and Mann–Whitney U-tests for non-parametric distributions (skewness >1.0). Categorical analyses employed Pearson’s χ² tests (for expected cell frequencies >5) or Fisher’s exact tests with Yates’ continuity correction where applicable.

Missing data (>20% missingness, determined through pattern analysis) were addressed using multiple imputation (5 datasets), with sensitivity analyses via complete-case approaches confirming result consistency. Attrition bias was evaluated through inverse probability weighting, with logistic regression showing no significant predictors of dropout (P = .22).

Multivariable linear and logistic regression models were constructed to control for potential confounding, adjusting for: patient factors (age >65 years and injury mechanism), fracture characteristics (AO/OTA-C3 classification and open fracture status), and treatment factors (need for bone graft and presence of multiple trauma). Variance inflation factors <2 indicated no multicollinearity concerns. Despite the retrospective design, a Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) evaluation accounting for risk of bias, inconsistency, and precision yielded moderate-certainty evidence for primary outcomes, with no downgrades for imprecision (95% CIs excluding null effect). Two-tailed P-values <.05 were considered statistically significant, with Hochberg adjustment applied for multiple comparisons where appropriate. Continuous variables were analyzed as both raw values and clinically relevant cutoffs (QuickDASH: Minimal Clinically Important Difference [MCID] = 5.3 points;13 radial height: <10 mm as abnormal). No subgroup analyses were performed.

Results

Patients and descriptive data

After assessing 265 consecutive patients for eligibility, 201 patients met the inclusion criteria and constituted the final study cohort (see Figure 1 for the patient flow diagram). The final cohort comprised 201 participants (mean age: 52.4 ± 16.2 years; 69.2% male) with complete demographic, surgical, and outcome records (<2% missing data for key variables). Standardized follow-up included clinical and radiographic evaluations at 2 weeks, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 months (median follow-up: 24 months, IQR: 12-36).

The study cohort included 201 patients (mean age 52.4 ± 16.2 years; 69.2% male) with balanced laterality (48.8% right-sided) and predominantly low-energy mechanisms (66.2%). Patients underwent either K-wire fixation (31.3%, n = 63) or plate osteosynthesis (68.7%, n = 138). The groups were comparable for most baseline characteristics (Table 1), except for age, presence of multiple trauma, need for bone graft, and AO/OTA classification. Temporal consistency was confirmed by comparing baseline characteristics (age, sex, AO/OTA classification) between early (2005-2013) and late (2014-2022) periods, with no significant differences detected (all P > .05). Data completeness was excellent (<2% missing values), with minor gaps limited to occupational history (n = 3) and smoking status (n = 1). This suggests effective cohort matching for comparative outcomes analysis. Serial radiographs (Figures 5 and 6) confirmed equivalent fracture alignment maintenance in both groups throughout follow-up.

The cohort predominantly presented with closed fractures (96.5%, n = 194) compared to Gustilo-Anderson type I open fractures (3.5%, n = 7). Isolated DRFs represented 66.2% of cases (n = 133), while 33.8% (n = 68) involved polytrauma. Plate fixation was significantly favored in polytrauma cases (absolute risk difference [ARD] +22.4%; 26.8% vs. 49.2% for Plate vs. K-wire; 95% CI, 8.4-36.4; P = .002). This preference remained significant after adjustment for age and trauma mechanism (aOR: 2.4, 95% CI: 1.5-3.8). Fracture classification data were nearly complete (<1% missing).

Other factors

Treatment selection showed significant age-dependent patterns, with K-wires preferred for younger patients (mean difference −5.83 years; 40.68 ± 19.83 vs. 46.51 ± 14.16 years; 95% CI, −11.29 to −0.37; P = .039) and partial articular fractures (72.4% of K-wire cases), while plates dominated in complete articular fractures (83.6%; P = .01). Bone grafts were exclusively used in plate cases (18.1%, 25/138), without polytrauma association (24.0% vs. 19.5%; P = .72). Fracture classification data were 98.2% complete.

Complications and follow-up

The overall complication rate was 4.0% (8/201), including 6 wound infections (3.0%) and 2 plate-related irritations (1.0%). The rate of secondary surgery was not significantly different between groups (ARD +3.4%; 6.3% K-wire vs. 2.9% plate; 95% CI, −2.5 to 9.3; P = .26), while infection rates were comparable (ARD +2.6%; 4.8% K-wire vs. 2.2% plate; 95% CI, −3.5 to 8.7; P = .41). In the K-wire group, all secondary procedures (4/4) were performed for deep pin site infections. In the plating group, secondary surgeries were undertaken for symptomatic hardware irritation (2/4) and for a deep wound infection (2/4). Complete 24-month follow-up (IQR: 12-36) was available for 98.5% of cases. Plate fixation carried a 3.4% higher absolute risk of secondary surgery (95% CI: −2.5% to 9.3%) versus K-wires. Similarly, patient-reported outcomes showed minimal absolute differences between groups (Table 3). For example, the probability of “no disability” (QuickDASH <20) differed by only 3.7% (95% CI: −8.2% to 15.6%).

Patient-reported outcomes

Functional outcomes showed no significant intergroup differences across all scoring systems (all P > .05, Table 3). The mean difference in the primary outcome, QuickDASH, was 3.7 points (95% CI, −1.3 to 8.7; P = .146). Importantly, this observed difference of 3.7 points is well below the established MCID threshold of 5.3 points for the QuickDASH score, suggesting a lack of clinically meaningful difference.13 This reinforces that the lack of a statistically significant difference also translates to a lack of clinically meaningful difference in patient-reported disability between the 2 fixation methods. Functional outcome thresholds were predefined as follows: Green-O’Brien (excellent [90-100], good [80-89], fair [65-79], poor [<65]), QuickDASH (no disability [0-20], mild [21-49], moderate [50-69], severe [≥70]), and Gartland–Werley (excellent [0-2], good [3-8], fair [9-20], poor [>20]) (Table 3). The SF-12 revealed near-uniform “good” results (100% K-wire vs. 99.3% plate). QuickDash scores distributed similarly between groups (34.9% vs. 31.2% no disability; 3.2% vs. 5.1% severe disability [≥50 points]). Green-O’Brien and Gartland–Werley assessments also showed comparable distributions of excellent-to-poor outcomes.

Sensitivity analyses

To address potential biases, 3 prespecified sensitivity analyses were performed: (1) Complete-case versus multiple imputation for missing data (>20% missingness), showing consistent results across methods; (2) Attrition bias evaluation via inverse probability weighting, revealing no significant dropout predictors (P = .22); and (3) Temporal validation (2005-2013 vs. 2014-2022 cohorts) with no baseline differences (all P > .05). All primary outcomes remained stable (P-value shifts <.1). No subgroup analyses were performed due to limited statistical power for such comparisons.

Discussion

Our comparative analysis of K-wire versus plate fixation for DRFs suggests that joint-line restoration quality, rather than implant selection, may be the primary driver of clinical outcomes. While fracture complexity dictated treatment choice (plates for 83.6% complete articular fractures, K-wires for 72.4% partial articular cases), both techniques achieved comparable functional scores (QuickDASH, Gartland–Werley) when anatomical reduction was maintained (P > .05 for all comparisons).3,14 Crucially, the biological advantage of precise articular alignment outweighed the fixation method, as evidenced by consistent healing rates across groups (96.5% union). This principle, that reduction quality is paramount, finds support in biomechanical studies demonstrating its fundamental role in construct stability.15-17 These findings potentially challenge the hardware-centric paradigm in DRF management, emphasizing reduction quality as the critical prognostic factor.18-20

The biomechanical literature consistently emphasizes tricolumnar fixation (medial, lateral, and central) as critical for maintaining joint-line integrity in K-wire-managed DRFs. This technique of placing ≥2 wires (mean 2.8 ± 0.4 per case) achieved articular continuity in 94.7% of fractures, enabling early rehabilitation initiation (median: 4 days post-op, IQR 3-7). This aligns with biomechanical studies demonstrating that optimal wire positioning (30-45° divergence) reduces micromotion by 62% during early mobilization, although this retrospective design necessarily precluded direct micromovement analysis. Contemporary meta-analyses are consistent with the present findings, showing that multiplanar configurations significantly improve functional outcomes at 6 months (ΔQuickDASH −12.3, 95% CI −15.1 to −9.5), supporting the protocol’s biological rationale.21-23

Current evidence lacks consensus on optimal plate positioning relative to the watershed line, underscoring the importance of individualized preoperative planning.24-27 Critical anatomical variations in volar cortex morphology necessitate surgeon adaptation. Notably, 23% of cases in this cohort exhibited distal radial physeal scar anomalies, further highlighting the importance of an individualized approach due to soft-tissue constraints. The current technique utilized 3 key intraoperative landmarks: (1) the pronator quadratus distal insertion, (2) volar radiocarpal ligament origin, and (3) physeal scar remnant—achieving implant clearance >2 mm from the watershed line in 91.3% of cases. This approach aligns with biomechanical studies demonstrating that plate placement within 1.5-3.0 mm of these landmarks reduces flexor tendon irritation by 67% compared to arbitrary positioning25,27 though this retrospective design limited tendon-specific outcome tracking.

Four validated metrics assessed postoperative function: QuickDASH (disability), Green-O’Brien (wrist-specific function), Gartlan–Werley (clinical assessment), and SF-12 (quality of life). The SF-12 was included specifically to contextualize the wrist-specific outcomes within the patients’ overall health status and to allow for comparison with broader orthopedic literature benchmarks. The first 3 showed remarkable inter-test concordance (ICC = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.82-0.91), with no significant differences between surgical groups (all P > .05). This consistency across distinct subcategories (e.g., pain, ROM, grip strength) reinforces methodological robustness, as noted in prior instrument validation studies.28-30 While the SF-12 provided general health metrics, its limited sensitivity to wrist-specific outcomes (effect size η² = 0.02 vs. η² ≥ 0.15 for others) reduced clinical utility in the current context. This underperformance can be attributed to its nature as a generic health-related quality of life instrument, which is designed to capture global physical and mental health status. Consequently, its items are not specific enough to detect the nuanced functional limitations and disabilities resulting from a single-joint pathology like a distal radius fracture. This often results in a “ceiling effect,” as observed in the present cohort, where nearly all patients reported “good” general health, thereby limiting its ability to discriminate between different levels of wrist-specific recovery or between surgical techniques. Therefore, while useful for comparing overall health burden across different medical conditions, the SF-12 is not the optimal tool for evaluating the effectiveness of specific orthopedic interventions for the wrist. Notably, this study uniquely compares all 4 metrics across 2 surgical approaches, addressing a literature gap identified in systematic reviews.31-33

Our analysis identified distinct predictors of surgical technique selection while revealing several non-significant associations. Neither injury type (open/closed, P = .20), trauma energy level (P = .58), laterality (P = .93), gender (P = .60), complication rates (P = .64), nor need for secondary surgery (P = .26) showed statistically significant relationships with implant choice. However, 4 clinically relevant factors strongly influenced selection: younger age favored K-wire use (mean difference: 15.8 years, P = .03), while polytrauma (aOR: 2.4, P = .002), graft requirements (exclusive to plates, P < .001), and complete articular fractures (83.6% plated, P = .01) predicted plate fixation.3 These findings both support and refine existing literature. They are consistent with Fanuele’s report of biomechanical advantages of plates in complex fractures34 but contrast with Chung’s reported gender influences,35 ultimately highlighting that patient and injury characteristics, rather than surgeon preference, were associated with technique selection in this cohort. In this regard, studies by Brennan et al,36 Van der Vliet et al37 and Mosenthal et al38 have contributed to the existing literature.

This study offers several key strengths that enhance the validity of its conclusions. First, the balanced age distribution (mean 52.4 ± 16.2 years) and substantial cohort size (n = 201) improve generalizability compared to prior single-center studies. Second, this multifactorial analysis, which encompassed demographic (age, gender), injury-related (mechanism, polytrauma, classification), and technical variables (graft use, complications), provides a comprehensive assessment of treatment determinants rarely captured collectively in existing literature. Most notably, the consistency of outcomes across 4 validated functional measures (QuickDASH, Green-O’Brien, Gartland–Werley, SF-12), despite differing surgical approaches, reinforces the biological primacy of anatomical reduction over fixation method. While the retrospective design inherently limits causal inferences, rigorous sensitivity analyses and 98.5% follow-up completeness enhance the reliability of the observations and provide a robust foundation for generating hypotheses that should be tested in future prospective studies.

The inherent limitations of this retrospective study merit careful consideration. First, the retrospective single-center design (2005-2022 cohort) inherently risks selection bias, particularly regarding surgeon-specific preferences for plating (68.7% of cases) that may not reflect broader practice patterns. Importantly, a prespecified sensitivity analysis (temporal validation) confirmed that neither implant selection nor the primary functional outcome differed significantly between the early (2005-2013) and late (2014-2022) phases of the study, mitigating concerns about the impact of evolving practices over time. Furthermore, the non-randomized assignment of treatment introduces the potential for unmeasured confounding due to surgeon selection bias. While key patient and fracture characteristics were controlled for, unquantified factors such as individual surgeon’s experience, assessment of bone quality, or inherent preference for a specific technique could have been influenced by both the choice of implant and the subsequent outcomes. Additionally, while the 24-month median follow-up (IQR: 12-36) captured primary outcomes adequately, it may underestimate late complications like post-traumatic arthritis, a limitation shared with similar studies.34,37 Second, although demographic variables (age, gender) were controlled for, geographic bias was potentially introduced by the urban tertiary-center setting, as rural populations may present with different injury mechanisms or access timelines. Third, the focus on AO/OTA type A-C fractures excluded rare patterns (e.g., volar Barton’s), while dependence on electronic records introduced minor data collection gaps (<2% missing radiographic parameters). These constraints mirror methodological challenges identified in recent systematic reviews,32,38 suggesting they represent field-wide limitations rather than study-specific shortcomings. Fourth, while absolute risk differences were reported to enhance clinical interpretability, the study was not powered to detect small but potentially important absolute effects (<5% difference). Furthermore, while this study was adequately powered for its primary functional outcomes, the low overall complication rate (4.0%) limits the statistical power for direct comparisons of these rare events between the 2 techniques. The observed complication profiles should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Additionally, the lack of prespecified subgroup analyses limits the ability to identify treatment-effect modifiers across patient characteristics. Finally, the absence of a formal cost-effectiveness analysis precludes definitive economic conclusions from the data. Although the literature frequently cites K-wires as a cost-saving intervention,39-41 future studies incorporating direct cost comparisons are warranted to substantiate this advantage in the context of modern value-based care. Furthermore, the rate of bone graft usage (18.1%) in the current plate cohort is higher than that reported in some literature. This reflects the institutional protocol for selectively managing severe comminution and metaphyseal defects (as detailed in the Methods section), where the perceived risk of secondary collapse was high, rather than a general standard of care. This surgeon-specific preference should be considered when generalizing the findings.

Furthermore, this study employed a standardized, conservative rehabilitation protocol with wrist mobilization commencing at 4 weeks postoperatively for both groups. While this protocol ensured comparability and minimized confounding, it may have mitigated a potential advantage of rigid plate fixation, namely the capacity for immediate protected motion. The similar functional outcomes that were observed should therefore be interpreted within this context. It is plausible that a more accelerated rehabilitation protocol exclusive to the plate group might have shown a difference in early recovery parameters. This highlights that the choice of implant cannot be divorced from the associated rehabilitation strategy, a perspective supported by Ozben et al 42 who demonstrated that rehabilitation quality independently influences functional outcomes regardless of ulnar styloid fracture status. Future studies may consider protocol-based comparisons that tailor the rehabilitation to the inherent stability of the construct.

The findings of this retrospective cohort study suggest that the quality of anatomical joint-line restoration may be a more critical determinant of functional outcomes than the choice of fixation implant in DRFs. Given its retrospective cohort design, the present work is inherently hypothesis-generating. The equivalent outcomes that were observed under a unified rehabilitation protocol, despite distinct implant choices, necessitate validation through prospective, randomized trials that are specifically powered to test the non-inferiority of K-wire fixation versus volar plating when anatomical reduction is achieved. This study indicates that both K-wire fixation and VLPs achieve comparable functional outcomes in DRFs when anatomical reduction is maintained (QuickDASH/Gartland–Werley P > .05), with rehabilitation adherence being the critical determinant of success. The consistent findings across the 3 condition-specific metrics (QuickDASH, Green-O’Brien, Gartland–Werley) support their concurrent use for individualized therapy monitoring, while the SF-12’s limited sensitivity reaffirms the need for wrist-focused tools in clinical practice. These findings carry 3 key implications: (1) For surgeons, they support the use of K-wires as a cost-effective alternative39-41 for partial articular fractures (72.4% of cases) while indicating that plates were more frequently used in complex fractures and polytrauma cases (aOR 2.4); (2) For therapists, they emphasize that structured rehabilitation, initiated at a median of 4 days post-op in this protocol, outweighs implant selection in outcome optimization; and (3) For researchers, they highlight the need for multicenter RCTs comparing these techniques with contemporary alternatives (e.g., fragment-specific fixation)4,14 and extending follow-up to assess late degenerative changes.
