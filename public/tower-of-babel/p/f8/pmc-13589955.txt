Red light-responsive bioorthogonal chemistry enabled on-demand prodrug activation for cancer therapy
Authors: Hanzeng Cheng, Yanjun Wang, Jiaxue Zhang, Zhipeng Pei, Qin Yan, Zeyu Cai, Dan Zhang, Huilong Ma, Hao Liu, Dongxue Peng, Zimeng Gao, Hongyu Yuan, Bing Cui, Xiaojing Wang, Rui Wang, Luping Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLight-driven chemistry has revolutionized modern medicine, enabling precise disease detection and on-demand therapeutic interventions with high spatiotemporal accuracy. However, despite tremendous advances in bioorthogonal reactions, photoclick chemistry-based therapeutics remain as a challenge in vivo. To address this limitation, we have developed a multifunctional BODIPY-caged glucamine dihydrotetrazine (BGdTz) that integrates cancer-targeting, fluorescence imaging, and photoactivable tetrazine bioorthogonal chemistry. By targeting the overexpressed glucose transporter 1 (Glut1) on cancer cells, BGdTz specifically accumulates at the tumor site, leading to high-contrast fluorescence imaging of tumor in murine models. Subsequent irradiation of BGdTz with red light results in a spatialtemporal formation of glucamine-tetrazine. Furthermore, we have demonstrated an image-guided bioorthogonal prodrug activation between BGdTz and trans-cyclooctene caged doxorubicin, revealing potent antitumor efficacy and minimal systemic toxicity in A375 xenograft mouse models. In summary, we present a red light-controllable tetrazine bioorthogonal platform operable in mammals, offering a powerful tool for dual cancer diagnosis and therapy. We anticipate that this photouncaging-driven tetrazine ligation will open new avenues for precision medicine.KEY WORDS: Cancer therapy, Prodrug, Bioorthogonal chemistry, Photouncaging, Red light, Tetrazine, Glut1, Precision medicine

Introduction

Over the past two decades, bioorthogonal reactions have emerged as powerful tools for labeling, visualizing, and modulating molecules of interest in living systems without disrupting native biological processes1,2, offering attractive chemical tools for biology and medicine3. Among these, inverse electron demand Diels–Alder (IEDDA) cycloadditions between tetrazines and dienophiles stand out in medicinal chemistry for their rapid kinetics, high biocompatibility, and unique “click-to-release” capability4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11. For example, tetrazine ligations have been widely explored for the activation of fluorogenic probes12 and prodrugs13,14. Notably, tetrazine-mediated prodrug activation has reached clinical trials, marking the first bioorthogonal reaction evaluated in humans15. Apart from ligand–targeting16, 17, 18, 19 and nanotechnology20, 21, 22, on-demand activations of diagnostic or therapeutic agents at pathological sites through tetrazine bioorthogonal chemistry23, 24, 25, 26 have gained dramatic attention recently to increase efficiency and safety. Tactics of enzymatic cleavage27, 28, 29, 30 and host–guest chemistry31 have been implemented to regulate tetrazine ligations, leading to substantial progress for cancer imaging. Moreover, recent studies have demonstrated the use of tetrazine-based activable photocages32,33, photosensitizers34,35 and photoredox catalysis36 for imaging and treatments in cancer cells. Despite these advances, achieving efficient on-demand bioorthogonal tetrazine chemistry in mammals remains challenging.

Photoactivatable bioorthogonal reactions have garnered significant interest for diagnostic and therapeutic development owing to their noninvasiveness and precise spatiotemporal control37. Recent advances in photoactivated tetrazine chemistry offer new opportunities for precision medicine in spatiotemporal control. Photocatalytic oxidation of dihydrotetrazines to tetrazines has been developed38, enabling precise imaging of subcellular organelles in living cells39, and light-triggered hydrogel formation in living mice40. Given the widespread application of photouncaging strategies in pharmacology41,42, efforts have been made to activate tetrazines43,44 or dienophiles45, 46, 47 via light-triggered cleavage. However, previously reported photouncaging tetrazine ligations rely on UV or blue light with poor tissue transparency and undesirable phototoxicity. Despite advances in photoactivatable tetrazine chemistry, its in vivo application still faces three major challenges: (1) Strategies for efficient uncaging under long-wavelength light (>650 nm) remain scarce; (2) Synergy between controlled release and tumor targeting is insufficient; (3) Platforms integrating activatable prodrugs with imaging guidance are lacking. To address this issue, photocages that are responsive to long-wavelength light from red to near-infrared within the therapeutic window are highly desirable48. Boron dipyrromethene (BODIPY), a widely used imaging dye, has recently been shown to undergo photocleavage upon red irradiation when modified with π-extension and boron alkylation at the meso-position49, 50, 51, 52, 53. Despite tetrazine has been reported as quenchers for BODIPY dyes12,33, we hypothesized that masking dihydrotetrazines with BODIPY would maintain fluorescence without significant quenching effects. Based on this premise, we envisioned that BODIPY-caged dihydrotetrazines would offer a promising platform for red light-responsive theranostics when combined with trans-cyclooctene (TCO)-caged cytotoxins (Scheme 1).

Schematic illustration of red light-responsive bioorthogonal chemistry enabling cancer diagnosis and treatment.

In this study, we report the development of a multifunctional BODIPY-caged glucamine dihydrotetrazine (BGdTz) that integrates cancer targeting, fluorescence imaging, and red light-activated tetrazine chemistry, enabling precision cancer phototheranostics in living mice. We have shown that modifying the BODIPY-caged dihydrotetrazine with a directing ligand of glucamine can significantly enhance its tumor cellular uptake. In addition, the BODIPY photocage in BGdTz exhibits red fluorescence, allowing an image-guided on-demand formation of tetrazine under red light irradiation (660 nm) at tumor sites. By coupling this versatile tetrazine precursor with “click-to-release” chemistry, we achieved targeted release of chemotherapeutic doxorubicin from a TCO-caged prodrug, resulting in a potent suppression of A375 tumor growth in xenograft models with enhanced therapeutic efficacy and minimal side effects.

Results and discussion

Synthesis of BODIPY-caged dihydrotetrazines

On the basis of the above insights and considerations, red light-responsive BODIPY-caged precursors for tetrazines have been envisioned (Scheme 2A). To reach this goal, we first prepared red-shifted photocages of 1 by modifying the meso-positions of boron-fluorinated BODIPY core with π-extension and boron methylation (see Supporting Information synthesis)51. A Boron-fluorinated BODIPY core was synthesized through a condensation of 2,4-dimethylpyrrole with acetoxyacetyl chloride in the presence of boron trifluoride etherate. Subsequential Knoevenagel condensation of the above BODIPY with p-anisaldehyde resulted in a red-shift of the absorption maximum. As alkylation of boron would significantly enhance the quantum yield of photouncaging, methylations of boron have been conducted, yielding a red light-triggered photocage of BODIPY-OH (1b). Attempts to synthesize carbonyl chloride derivatives of BODIPY alcohol failed in our initial study, therefore previous method43,44 was insufficient for the preparation of BODIPY caged dihydrotetrazine.

Synthesis of BODIPY-caged dihydrotetrazines. (A) Synthesis of BODIPY-caged dihydrotetrazines. (B) Single-crystal structure of compound 4.

To solve this problem, we set out to synthesize activated dihydrotetrazine derivatives. Gratifyingly, 6-methyl-3-phenyl-1,2,4,5-tetrazine-1(4H)-carbonyl chloride (2) was successfully obtained by reacting the corresponding dihydrotetrazine with triphosgene. To ensure the reactivity of BODIPY photocage with dihydrotetrazine carbonyl chloride, we further prepared a more nucleophilic BODIPY amine of 1a from 1b. To our delight, BODIPY-caged dihydrotetrazines of 3 have been obtained by reacting BODIPY photocages of 1 with a dihydrotetrazine carbonyl chloride of 2 under basic conditions (Scheme 2A). Although caged dihydrotetrazines have been widely used in recent studies27, 28, 29, the structure elucidation for these compounds is limited to nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and mass spectrometry (MS). In this study, we have synthesized a chloro-containing caged dihydrotetrazine of 4 and its crystal structure was achieved as shown in Scheme 2B, which was characterized by single crystal X-ray diffraction (Supporting information Fig. S1 and Supporting information Table S1).

With BODIPY-caged dihydrotetrazines in hand, we first characterized their stabilities under aqueous conditions. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis revealed that negligible degradation was observed for both 3a and 3b in H2O at 37 °C for 24 h in the absence of irradiation (Supporting information Figs. S2 and S3). Considering excellent stabilities of 3, we next investigated the light-triggered formation of tetrazine 5 (Fig. 1A). The absorbance spectra of 3a and 3b have been characterized, as shown in Fig. 1B, compound 3a has a maximum absorption wavelength of 654 nm within the tissue transparent window, while compound 3b has a slightly red-shifted λmax of 674 nm. Accordingly, a commercially available 660 nm-centered light-emitting diode (LED) was chosen for irradiation. The photouncaging reactions were carried out by irradiating photocaged dihydrotetrazines 3 (10 μmol/L) with LED light (660 nm, 100 mW/cm2) in an aqueous solution containing 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at room temperature (Fig. 1C). HPLC analysis revealed that irradiating 3a with the red LED light for 3 min yielded approximately 22% of tetrazine 5 (Fig. 1C, red line) and a slightly increased yield of 28% was observed by extending the irradiation duration to 10 min. Gratifyingly, upon 3 min of irradiation, a 62% yield of tetrazine 5 was immediately formed from 3b (Fig. 1C, blue line). The experimental results demonstrate that 3b exhibits faster formation of tetrazine and higher yield compared to 3a, indicating that the carboxylate linkage in 3b may facilitate the photouncaging process compared to the carboxamide linkage of 3a. To better understand the significant difference in photolysis efficiency between 3a and 3b, density functional theory calculations were conducted next (Supporting information Fig. S4). As shown in Fig. 1D, the excited-state energy (S1) of 3a is 51.3 kcal/mol, which is comparable to that of 3b (50.0 kcal/mol), aligning with their respective maximum absorption. The S1 energy of 3b is sufficient for the anticipated bond dissociation during photolysis (47.0 kcal/mol), which is consistent with previous mechanistic findings of BODIPY photocages53. However, BODIPY photocage in 3a is unlikely to be released upon red light irradiation, since a much higher heterolytic bond dissociation energy of 80.2 kcal/mol is required according to theoretical calculations (Supporting information Figs. S4 and S5, and Supporting information Table S2), leading to the above sluggish yields in the photouncaging experiments of 3a.

The photolysis of BdTz 3b in aqueous solution was followed by monitoring the formation of tetrazine 5 using HPLC spectroscopy (Fig. 1E). The results obtained through a series of controlled on/off illumination cycles using LED light (660 nm, 100 mW/cm2, 20 s) indicated that the generation of tetrazine was highly light-dependent. As shown in Fig. 1F, upon red light irradiation, the absorption peak of photocaged tetrazine precursor of 3b was dramatically diminished, while the absorption peak of tetrazine 5 was immediately formed. After 3 min of irradiation, 3b was almost completely consumed. Noteworthy, an intermediate of BODIPY alcohol (1b) was detected by LC–MS in the photolysis, consistent with our theoretical findings that BODIPY-caged dihydrotetrazine 3b underwent heterolytic bond dissociation upon irradiation.

Tumor-selective fluorescent tetrazine precursor

As tetrazine moieties are established fluorescence quenchers for BODIPY dyes12,32, we next questioned whether the photocaged tetrazine precursor of 3b maintains fluorescence and thus can serve as a diagnostic agent for cancer cells. To our delight, spectroscopic analysis of 3b revealed its maximum absorption at 674 nm with a high molar absorption coefficient (ϵ) of 26,600 L/(mol·cm), exhibiting excellent red fluorescence (λem = 722 nm, Φf = 0.14) in water with 0.1% DMSO (Fig. 2A, left), suggesting that the reduced dihydrotetrazine from tetrazine is incapable to quench BODIPY fluorophore and validated by theoretical study (Supporting information Table S3). Encouraged by these results, we sought to image A375 human malignant melanoma cells with 3b, but very weak confocal fluorescence microscopy imaging of cells was observed after a 6 h incubation (Fig. 2B), indicating the poor permeability of 3b through cytoplasmic membrane.

To solve this problem, we hypothesized that a saccharide unit could be engineered into BODIPY-caged dihydrotetrazine to promote tumor-specific cellular uptake, given that malignant cancer cells exhibit overexpressed glucose transporters (Glut) on tumor cell surfaces for a higher glycolytic rate compared to normal cells54. As shown in Fig. 2A, we appended BdTz 3b with a glucose analogue of glucamine55 via an amide bond, yielding BODIPY-caged glucamine dihydrotetrazine (BGdTz 3c). Molecular docking simulations revealed strong interactions between 3c and the primary glucose transporter 1 (Glut1) (Supporting information Fig. S6). Gladly, BGdTz 3c maintains similar spectroscopic properties (λabs = 678 nm, ϵ = 21,850 L/(mol·cm)) and exhibits maximum emission at 726 nm with a quantum yield of 0.1 (Supporting information Figs. S7 and S8). The stability of 3c was investigated (Supporting information Fig. S9), and HPLC analysis revealed that less than 1% degradation of 3c was observed under aqueous conditions (H2O containing 0.1% DMSO) at 37 °C for 24 h in the absence of irradiation, while 15% degradation was observed after incubation for 24 h in DMEM medium (with 10% FBS).

To evaluate the tumor-targeting capability conferred by the glucose moiety in compound 3c, we conducted a comparative analysis of cellular uptake efficiency between the glycosylated derivative 3c and its non-glycosylated analogue 3b in Glut1-overexpressing A375 human malignant melanoma cells. Capitalizing on the intrinsic fluorescence properties of the photocage moiety, which enables label-free tracking, we monitored the internalization of BODIPY-caged dihydrotetrazines in cancer cells by real-time imaging monitoring. Incubation A375 cells with 2.5 μmol/L of 3b or 3c in DMEM under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2), and a time-dependent cellular uptake was visualized using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). As shown in Fig. 2B and Supporting information Fig. S10, the glycosylated compound 3c demonstrated superior tumor cell targeting properties, achieving rapid cytoplasmic accumulation within 3 h and exhibiting progressive fluorescence intensification up to 6 h. In stark contrast, the non-glycosylated analog 3b displayed limited cellular penetration, maintaining consistently weak cytoplasmic fluorescence throughout the observation period. Quantitative flow cytometry analysis corroborated these findings, revealing significant glycosylation-dependent differences in cellular uptake efficiency (Fig. 2C and Fig. S10). These findings mechanistically validate our molecular design rationale, demonstrating that the glucamine conjugation significantly enhances both targeted binding to Glut1-overexpressing tumor cell surfaces and subsequent internalization efficiency, thereby establishing glycosylation as an effective strategy for improving tumor-selective drug delivery.

To confirm the Glut1-specific transport of compound 3c, we employed competitive inhibition assays using BAY-876, a potent Glut1 inhibitor. A375 cells pre-incubated with vehicle control or increasing concentrations of BAY-876 (0.2 or 0.4 μmol/L) for 1 h at 37 °C, were co-treated with 10 μmol/L of compound 3c for 15 min prior to analysis. Flow cytometric quantification of initial internalization revealed a concentration-dependent suppression, showing 30% (P = 0.06) and 54% (P < 0.01) reductions in cellular internalization at 0.2 and 0.4 μmol/L of BAY-876, respectively (Fig. 2D). This pharmacological evidence unequivocally confirms Glut1-mediated transport as the dominant entry pathway for the glycosylated 3c. Given the glucose moiety's critical role in cellular targeting identified in cell experiments, we next extended our investigation to in vivo settings using precision optical imaging in A375 tumor-bearing murine models. In this study, compounds 3b and 3c specifically utilize BODIPY as the photocage group, exploiting its fluorescence property as an ideal visual marker to trace with high spatiotemporal resolution. Through systematic longitudinal imaging of BODIPY-derived signals, we quantitatively mapped the biodistribution of compounds 3b (38 mg/kg) and 3c (50 mg/kg) in A375 tumor-bearing mice following intravenous administration of equimolar doses. Whole-body fluorescence imaging was conducted at pre-defined intervals: 0 h (pre-injection), 1, 3, 6, 9, 24, and 48 h post-injection. Compound 3c demonstrated superior tumor-targeting efficiency, with a strong fluorescence signal emerging at the tumor site by 3 h and peaking at 6 h (Fig. 2E and Supporting information Fig. S11). This tumor-selective enrichment pattern directly correlates with the cellular uptake dynamics observed in vitro, mechanistically attributable to Glut1-mediated glucose transport-a feature conspicuously absent in the non-glycosylated analog 3b, which showed diffuse systemic distribution without tumor-selective enrichment.

Imaging-guided bioorthogonal prodrug activation

Previous studies have demonstrated that the “click-to-release” reactions between tetrazines and trans-cyclooctenes (TCO) hold great potential in prodrug activation7. On this basis, we envisioned a light-triggered bioorthogonal prodrug activation between BGdTz 3c and axial trans-cyclooctene caged doxorubicin (TCO-DOX, see Supporting Information synthesis): upon light irradiation, 3c undergoes photolysis to generate tetrazine, which triggers the bioorthogonal cleavage of TCO-DOX and subsequently releases doxorubicin (DOX). For this purpose, we first examined the light-activated formation of glucamine-tetrazine from photocaged dihydrotetrazine of 3c (Fig. 3A). A commercially available 660 nm-centred LED light was selected to trigger the formation of tetrazine on the basis of the absorbance spectrum of 3c (Fig. S7). The reaction was carried out by irradiating BODIPY-caged glucamine-dihydrotetrazine of 3c (10 μmol/L) with LED light (660 nm, 100 mW/cm2) in H2O (containing 0.1% DMSO) open to air at room temperature. HPLC analysis revealed that a 68% yield of glucamine-tetrazine of 6 was immediately formed from 3c after irradiation (Fig. 3B and Supporting information Fig. S12). Encouraged by this result, we next investigated the light-driven “click to release” reaction in aqueous solution (Fig. 3C and Supporting information Fig. S13). As shown in Fig. 3B, compound 3c (5 μmol/L) exhibited rapid reaction kinetics with TCO-DOX (4 μmol/L) in water upon red light irradiation. Conjugated intermediates were confirmed by HRMS (Fig. 3D and Supporting information Fig. S14), and subsequent intramolecular tautomerization afforded efficient liberation of DOX and glucamine-pyridazine of 7. Next, we investigated the light-dependent release of doxorubicin in the human malignant melanoma A375 cell line (Fig. 3E and F). In A375 cells, co-treatment with 3c and TCO-DOX upon 5-min irradiation (660 nm, 100 mW/cm2) demonstrated cytotoxicity (IC50 = 0.37 μmol/L) comparable to that of active DOX (IC50 = 0.16 μmol/L), whereas TCO-DOX in the absence of 3c exhibited markedly reduced cytotoxic activity (IC50 > 3.33 μmol/L). Meanwhile, 3c exerted no cytotoxic effects on cells regardless of whether it was exposed to light or not (IC50 > 10 μmol/L) (Fig. 3E and Supporting information Fig. S15). Similarly, significant cytotoxicity was observed for the co-treatment system comprising decaging compound 6 and TCO-DOX (IC50 = 0.24 μmol/L). As shown in Fig. 3F, incubation of A375 with a mixture of photocaged compound 3c (2 μmol/L) and TCO-DOX (1 μmol/L) for 24 h-maintained cell viability comparable to untreated controls, subsequent LED irradiation (660 nm, 100 mW/cm2) for 5 min induced significant cytotoxicity, reducing cell viability by approximately 53%. This light-triggered response demonstrated strict irradiation-dependence, as non-irradiated cultures showed no detectable viability loss relative to control groups. While slightly attenuated compared to direct DOX treatment (75% viability reduction at 1 μmol/L), this photoresponsive effect exhibited remarkable specificity: there was no influence on cell viability when cells were irradiated with LED light for 5 min in the presence of either photocaged compound 3c (2 μmol/L) or TCO-DOX (1 μmol/L) alone (Supporting information Table S4).

Building on the promising in vitro efficacy, we next tackled the expected imaging-guided bioorthogonal prodrug activation in A375 xenograft-bearing BALB/c nude mice (n = 5 per group) for in vivo antitumor validation. The optimized therapeutic regimen comprised (Fig. 4A): (1) Tetrazine systemic delivery: photocaged tetrazine derivative 3c (80 mg/kg) administered intravenously as a stabilized formulation (DMSO: Solutol HS-15: saline = 1:5:44 (v/v)), prepared through sequential cosolvent optimization; (2) Tumor accumulation: a 6 h tumor accumulation period to maximize 3c concentration at tumor sites; (3) Spatiotemporal activation: transdermal photoactivation (660 nm, 200 mW/cm2, 4 min) to trigger tetrazine liberation; (4) Prodrug activation: intraperitoneal injection of TCO-DOX (30 mg/kg) for bioorthogonal drug release. To validate our hypothesis of on-demand drug release at the tumor site, we conducted a comprehensive biodistribution study in A375 tumor-bearing mice treated with TCO-DOX alone, free DOX, the combination of 3c + TCO-DOX, and 3c + TCO-DOX plus laser irradiation. When the tumor volume reached approximately 200 mm3, mice were randomly divided into four experimental groups (n = 3 per group). The TCO-DOX group received an intravenous (i.v.) injection of solvent control, followed by an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 30 mg/kg TCO-DOX 6 h later. The free DOX group received an i.p. injection of 5 mg/kg DOX. The 3c + TCO-DOX group received an i.v. administration of 80 mg/kg 3c, followed by an i.p. injection of 30 mg/kg TCO-DOX 6 h later. The 3c + TCO-DOX + laser group received an i.v. administration of 80 mg/kg 3c, then underwent laser irradiation (660 nm, 200 mW/cm2) for 4 min at 6 h post-injection, immediately followed by an i.p. injection of 30 mg/kg TCO-DOX. All mice were euthanized after 24 h of treatment and major tissues (liver, kidneys, and tumor) were then collected for LC–MS/MS analysis (Fig. 4B). DOX was undetectable in all tissues of the TCO-DOX control group. While DOX was only slightly detected in liver and kidneys in 3c + TCO-DOX group, which is consistent with a typical pharmacokinetic profile. In stark contrast, the 3c + TCO-DOX + laser group exhibited significantly higher DOX levels in tumors than the free DOX group (∗P < 0.05). Conversely, DOX accumulation in the kidney was markedly lower in this group compared to the free DOX group (∗∗∗P < 0.001). For the liver, DOX levels were slightly reduced relative to the free DOX group, but this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.095). Overall, a tumor localized, on-demand drug release in mice model has been established and we envisioned that this red light-responsive click-to-release reaction between BGdTz and TCO-caged cytotoxins holds great potential for precision cancer therapy.

We next evaluated the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of red light triggered BGdTz and TCO-DOX combination in an A375 tumor-bearing BALB/c nude mouse model. The experimental design incorporated rigorous controls to validate therapeutic specificity. Specifically, a positive control group received free DOX (5 mg/kg) to establish a benchmark for conventional chemotherapy efficacy. Additionally, four treatment groups were included: one without red light, one without compound 3c, one without TCO-DOX, and one with only the vehicle solution, to assess background effects. This therapeutic cycle was repeated on Days 1, 3, 5, and 8 to assess cumulative effects (Fig. 4A).

Tumor volumes and body weights of A375 xenograft-bearing BALB/c nude mice were measured daily. The combination therapy group of 3c + TCO-DOX + laser demonstrated significant tumor suppression compared to vehicle control (P < 0.0001), which was comparable to the doxorubicin positive control group (P = 0.47). In contrast, individual component treatment groups of TCO-DOX + laser, 3c + TCO-DOX and 3c + laser showed no statistically significant anti-tumor effects versus vehicle (P > 0.05). This clearly indicates effective prodrug activation in living mice triggered by external red light. Notably, despite exhibiting similar tumor-inhibitory efficacy between the 3c + TCO-DOX + laser group and the DOX group, distinct differences were observed in the body weights of the mice (Fig. 4C and D, and Supporting information Fig. S16). In the 3c + TCO-DOX + laser group, the body weights of the mice were maintained at a stable level throughout the experiment, and the general condition of the mice remained favorable. This suggests that the treatment approach employed in the experimental group had a relatively mild impact on the overall health of the animals, potentially indicating a lower propensity for off-target effects or systemic toxicity. Conversely, in the DOX group, where the standard chemotherapy agent was administered, a marked decrease in body weight was noted among the mice. Moreover, the mice in this group displayed signs of lethargy and a general decline in their physical condition. These manifestations strongly imply the presence of severe toxic side effects associated with doxorubicin treatment. Such findings highlight the potential advantages of the experimental treatment strategy in terms of improved tolerability and reduced toxicity attributable to precision drug delivery, which are crucial considerations in the development of effective and safe anti-tumor therapies.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of tumors from the red-light-activated 3c and TCO-DOX-treated group revealed hallmark DOX-induced cytotoxic effects characterized by condensed pyknotic nuclei, eosinophilic cytoplasmic shrinkage, and focal necrosis zones. Crucially, multi-organ analysis demonstrated preserved histological integrity in the red-light-activated compound 3c and TCO-DOX-treated cohort, confirming that the glucose-targeted photoregulation strategy effectively confines drug activity to tumor sites while avoiding systemic toxicity (Fig. 4E). These findings collectively validate the bioorthogonal containment mechanism through which localized red-light irradiation enables tumor-selective tetrazine liberation, spatial control over DOX activation, and dissociation of therapeutic efficacy from off-target organ damage.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a red light-activated bioorthogonal tetrazine chemistry and demonstrated its potential for precision cancer theranostics in both mammalian cells and mice. We designed and synthesized BODIPY-caged dihydrotetrazines (BdTz) as long-wavelength light-responsive tetrazine precursors, enabling spatiotemporal control over tetrazine ligation within the therapeutic window. Notably, the BODIPY moiety within BdTz retains strong red fluorescence, allowing image-guided formation of tetrazines from BODIPY-caged dihydrotetrazines in vivo. To enhance tumor selectivity, we appended BdTz with a glucose analogue of glucamine, yielding BGdTz, which facilitates tumor-specific uptake via the overexpressed glucose transporter 1 (Glut1) on tumor cells. In addition to confocal fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry analysis, the tumor-targeting capability of BGdTz was further confirmed by its competitive Glut1 inhibitor of BAY-876, suggesting Glut1-mediated cellular entry. With this versatile BGdTz, we have demonstrated an image-guided bioorthogonal prodrug activation of trans-cyclooctene caged doxorubicin at tumor sites in living mice, effectively suppressing tumor growth while minimizing systemic toxicity. We anticipate that the present red light-responsive tetrazine ligation system can be extended to the controllable activation of a wide range of cytotoxic agents and other molecules of interest in vivo, with high spatial and temporal precision. We believe the combination of photouncaging chemistry with bioorthogonal reactions holds significant promise for biological research and photopharmacology.

Experimental

Tetrazine formation

The test compound was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a stock solution (10 mmol/L), which was further diluted with deionized water to obtain a working solution (10 μmol/L). The working solution was distributed into a 96-well plate (200 μL/well). The samples were exposed to LED irradiation for designated time intervals (10, 20, 30, 60, 180, 300, 480, and 600 s). After irradiation, the reaction mixtures were thoroughly vortexed. An aliquot of 200 μL from each well was immediately collected for HPLC analysis. The remaining mixtures were incubated in a thermostatically controlled incubator at 37 °C, and subsequent samples were collected at 1, 2, and 4 h for further HPLC analysis.

Serum stability

Commercial mouse serum was purchased. The drug was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a stock solution (5 mmol/L). The drug was diluted with 10% mouse serum in PBS and thoroughly mixed. The mixture was incubated in a thermostatic incubator at 37 °C. At predetermined time points (0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 24, and 48 h), aliquots (80 μL) were withdrawn, and ice-cold acetonitrile (320 μL) was added to quench the reaction. After standing for 2 min, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min (twice), and the supernatants were collected. An aliquot (100 μL) of the supernatant was subjected to HPLC analysis to calculate the retention rate. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cellular uptake analysis

A375 cells were seeded in glass-bottom confocal dishes at a density of 2 × 105 cells/dish and incubated overnight at 37 °C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere to allow adherence. The cells were subsequently treated with compound 3b or 3c (2.5 μmol/L) for 1, 3, or 6 h. Following incubation, the culture medium was aspirated, and the cells were stained with Hoechst 33,342 (10 μg/mL) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. After being gently rinsed three times with PBS, the cells were imaged using a Zeiss LSM880 confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). The photosensitizers were excited at 633 nm, and the fluorescence emission was collected at 722 nm. All images were acquired under identical instrumental parameters and subsequently processed using ImageJ software for fluorescence quantification.

Biodistribution profiling of compounds in tumor-bearing mice

Healthy female BALB/c nude mice (6 weeks old, ∼20 g) were purchased from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and housed in a specific-pathogen-free (SPF) barrier facility. A suspension of 7 × 106 A375 cells in 100 μL PBS was subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse to establish the tumor xenograft model. On Day 11 post-inoculation, when the tumor volumes reached 80–100 mm3, the tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into groups. Compounds 3c and 3b were administered by tail vein injection at a concentration of 10 mmol/L in a total volume of 100 μL/mouse. The spatiotemporal distribution of the compounds in mice was monitored at 1, 3, 6, 9, 24, and 48 h post-injection using a small animal in vivo imaging system. All experimental protocols involving animals were approved by the Institute of Materia Medica, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences & Peking Union Medical College, the Animal Care & Welfare Committee (Approval Nos. 00002993 and IMM-S-26-0023).
