“It’s more than just blood pressure and stitches”: a critical examination of paramedic services from the perspectives of intimate partner violence advocates
Authors: Rory A Marshall, Tori N Stranges, Nicole Merritt, Keilin Gorman, Stephen Bartlett, Simon Sawyer, Paul van Donkelaar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs a detrimental and persistent criminal, social, and health issue, intimate partner violence (IPV) is a severe threat to the well-being of survivors. The healthcare system, specifically the emergency department, has provided access to services and supports for survivors of IPV when seeking healthcare. However, many survivors of IPV are unable to attend the hospital and access the corresponding services. Paramedics, versatile in-community service providers, could play a vital role in linking survivors to healthcare and support services. In-community IPV advocates understand the circumstances of survivors and have expert knowledge of services.ObjectiveTo examine how the perspectives of IPV advocates can inform our understanding of paramedic services.MethodsActioning an interpretive description approach in the context of paramedicine, IPV advocates in western Canada participated in semi-structured focus groups. Focus groups were transcribed verbatim and de-identified. De-identified transcripts were inductively analyzed (NVivo) for patterns.ResultsN = 17 IPV advocates (all women; age 42 ± 12) participated in focus groups. Participants indicated that primary and emergency healthcare, recognition of and validation for situations involving IPV, and the referral to specialized resources were desirable services paramedics could provide. Challenges for survivors accessing services through paramedics were paramedic conduct, paramedic capacity, paramedic service shortcomings, situational logistics, concurrent conditions, service access, and patient and perpetrator factors. Solutions for these challenges include adopting safe, equitable, and patient-centred practice, establishing and maintaining functional working capacities for paramedics, developing functional infrastructure and supporting policy, providing adaptable and versatile service options, avoiding biases and stereotypes, and considering holistic patient concerns, generating innovative methods to promote service access for all patients, and integrating techniques to facilitate patient engagement and mitigating perpetrator interference.ConclusionsGiven the desired services from paramedics and the challenges identified for survivors, it is likely that a practice gap exists among paramedics and paramedic services. IPV advocate-derived solutions may help inform paramedics and paramedic services to address these challenges. If the best available evidence is utilized to develop positive change, paramedics could advance into specialist resources for survivors of IPV.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14514-1.Keywords: Intimate partner violence, Paramedics, Paramedicine, Healthcare pathways, Public health

Background

Intimate partner violence (IPV), including physical, sexual or psychological acts of aggression, and coercive controlling behaviours by a current or former intimate partner [1], remains a public health pandemic. This preventable form of violence is characterized by a perpetrator using violence to cause harm and exert power and control over their partner [1]. While anyone can experience IPV, women and vulnerable populations are at a greater risk [2]. Globally, one in three women will experience one or more forms of IPV within their lifetime, with one in ten reporting an experience in the last 12 months [3, 4]. When considering physical IPV, comparable prevalence rates among women persist [3]. There is an increased risk of homicide/femicide when survivors are attempting to exit a violent relationship [5, 6]. As IPV progresses, instances of physical IPV, physical injury, and the potential for homicide are of substantial concern for survivors and support services.

IPV is typically cyclic, meaning it re-occurs in a predictable pattern, and tends to escalate with each episode of violence [1]. The health implications for survivors of physical IPV are severe. IPV has been well documented to cause harm to every bodily system (e.g., neurological, musculoskeletal, reproductive, etc.) [7, 8]. IPV-caused brain injury and non-fatal strangulation are emerging as common considerations that are likely to require medical attention [9]. Survivors of IPV are likely to access the healthcare system for injuries related to IPV (e.g., lacerations, orthopedic fractures, reproductive concerns, etc.). Many of the risk factors (e.g., socioeconomic status, race, education level) that exist for marginalized people accessing healthcare overlap with risk factors for experiencing IPV [10–12]. IPV alone or IPV with the co-occurrence of other risk factors make accessing healthcare and receiving the appropriate treatment and resources a challenging process with high barriers. Healthcare providers may experience confusion or misunderstanding about what constitutes IPV, and what interventions are warranted when encountering survivors of IPV, further contributing to the challenges faced by survivors of IPV [13]. Lowering the threshold for identification and recognition of IPV by healthcare providers and intervening in cases of IPV could reduce missed opportunities and potentially reduce violent or deadly outcomes. However, not all survivors are able or choose to access the healthcare system.

As IPV can impact all aspects of life, a holistic, equitable approach to addressing the complexities of IPV without structural barriers is needed [14, 15]. In-community services (e.g., shelters, transition houses, counselling, outreach, childcare, etc.), as opposed to in-hospital services, can support certain needs of survivors of IPV that are not met through healthcare utilization alone. Evidence-based holistic services built on academia-community, hospital-community, and large-scale partnerships could be useful in closing the gap between services required to support survivors of IPV and services available [14]. In-community services do not typically encompass healthcare, and the current in-hospital services are commonly limited to patients within the hospital. Insufficient overlap and communication exist as in-community services and healthcare services commonly operate differently. When there is a disconnect between overlapping agencies providing services to a client, the potential for survivors of IPV to be overlooked or neglected increases. Paramedics and emergency medical technicians (operationally grouped in this manuscript as paramedics) are versatile and mobile clinicians who primarily function in the in-community environment facilitating entry into the healthcare system [16]. While historically thought of in the emergency care setting of pre-hospital medicine, modern paramedicine is actively evolving to do more than manage immediately life-threatening physiological emergencies and transport patients to the emergency department. Considering most calls to paramedics are not for life-threatening emergencies, re-thinking the unique roles and opportunities of paramedics to provide support in more diverse and holistic roles (i.e., beyond strictly physiological emergencies) has been successful in a variety of in-community initiatives [17–19]. As multidisciplinary clinicians working in-community (frequently in people’s homes) and in the healthcare system, paramedics are uniquely positioned to function in both sectors. The opportunity for paramedics to act as expert resources for survivors of IPV has been recognized by researchers and healthcare professionals [20]. Community Paramedics (CPs), a sub-specialty within paramedicine, work in expanded roles beyond emergency and transport into the non-emergency and preventative health sector [21]. CPs are being increasingly used within healthcare and in-community spaces to bridge gaps in services [21]. An ever-growing body of knowledge is showing that community paramedicine programmes are resulting in a reduction in acute healthcare utilization, are appearing to be economically worthwhile, and are contributing to positive patient outcomes and satisfaction [22]. There is no published literature on the implementation of CPs in the IPV space. The opportunity to advance the position of paramedics and CPs, respectively, in the IPV space is immense. However, some challenges exist. In Canada, the Paramedic Association of Canada and Canadian Organization of Paramedic Regulators have historically governed paramedic practice on the national scale. The current iteration of the Paramedic Association of Canada’s National Occupational Competency Profile and Canadian Organization of Paramedic Regulators’ Pan-Canadian Essential Regulatory Requirements do not include educational or vocational competencies about IPV [23–25]. The National Occupational Competency Profile does include competencies for child abuse, elder abuse, abuse of bariatric people, and abuse of persons with disabilities, but not partner abuse (i.e., IPV) [25]. A more recent guidance document, the National Competency Framework for Paramedics, has been published by the Canadian Standards Association. This document includes IPV as an example of abuse under core competency A.3.4.e. [26]. This inclusion is promising. Unfortunately, the uptake of this document as the relevant national standard has not yet been widely adopted in Canadian paramedic education or practice. A lack of clarity and uniformity around national standards to guide paramedicine in Canada exists. The absence of clear IPV standards may comprise a missed opportunity to better paramedic care for patients experiencing IPV in Canada. A recent study among a population of practicing paramedics in western Canada found deficiencies in the readiness of paramedics to encounter patients experiencing IPV, specifically around self-efficacy and emotional readiness [27]. Those results paralleled previous studies among Australian paramedic students and workers, also reporting deficiencies in readiness [28, 29]. How readiness to encounter patients experiencing IPV translates to the clinical practice in this profession remains unknown.

Clinical situations and circumstances are often solely examined from the practitioner and patient perspectives. The paramedic and survivor perspectives have recently begun to be explored [30, 31]. The overlapping in-community and in-hospital service requirements necessitated in situations of IPV often warrant guidance from non-clinical in-field experts to fully elucidate and appropriately manage cases. Given the complexities of the biological, psychological, sociological, and ecological spheres [32] that can be impacted by IPV, specialist support is commonly required. The perspectives of expert social care providers who serve people experiencing IPV through in-community services (operationally defined as IPV advocates; e.g., domestic violence shelter workers, IPV outreach workers, case managers) have yet to be explored regarding the intersection of paramedicine and IPV. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to examine the intersection of paramedicine and IPV from the perspectives of IPV advocates who support survivors of IPV who have had contact with paramedics. The objective of this study was to examine how the varied perspectives and experiences of IPV advocates in western Canada may inform the understanding of current and aspirations for future clinical practice within paramedicine.

Methods

Ethical approval and research standards

This study was reviewed, approved (March 1st, 2024), and conducted in accordance with the permission of the University of British Columbia Okanagan Behavioural Research Ethics Board (#H22-02769). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrolment in the study. This research was conducted in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

Approach, positionality, and safety

Using a pragmatic worldview, relativist ontology, and constructivist epistemology, this research was purposefully conceptualized, designed, and conducted using an inductive interpretive description (ID) qualitative approach specific to the research objective and context within the applied discipline of paramedicine [33]. While ID approaches commonly follow the guiding principles of the methodology, each ID approach can be tailored to the specific demands of the research [33]. ID is used to address research questions informed by practice, findings generated are intended to advance disciplinary knowledge, and this knowledge is planned to be applied back to practice [33]. This ID constructivist-rooted epistemological approach used the experience of the research team as a valuable instrument in the co-creation of this research between researchers and participants, as opposed to an element of bias that required elimination [33]. Identifying diverse perspectives, patterns, or themes is a core focus of ID [33]. The use of ID in paramedicine is budding [30, 31], but presents as a promising methodology with implications specifically for paramedicine as opposed to off chutes from other disciplines.

The research team was composed of clinician-researchers (RAM, NM, KG, SB, SS) and researchers (TNS, PvD). These roles combined with the unique and diverse positionality of the research team were cumulatively leveraged to enhance the undertaking in alignment with the objectives of this work. The synergistic attributes of the research team in capacities as clinical healthcare providers and researchers were integrated into the strategy of this project to optimize the design.

Context

This work was conducted in western Canada by a team of international researchers and clinicians. Western Canada refers to the provinces of Alberta, British Columbia, and Saskatchewan. This region was selected because each province has similar population distribution (i.e., large centers and small centers spaced over a large geographical area), geographical proximity, demographic make-up, paramedic education and training, and paramedic scope. Paramedic education and training in Canada is brief and training-focused when compared to more developed, education-focused systems in Australia, Ireland, New Zealand, and the United Kingdom. The field of paramedicine faces international challenges in education and training largely owing to the breadth and depth required of vocationally adept paramedics. Healthcare in Canada is universal and publicly funded, but emergency paramedic services are commonly excluded from universal healthcare and require patient expense (e.g., transport fees).

Participants

To address the objective of this study – to examine how the perspectives and experiences of IPV advocates may inform the understanding of current paramedic practice and aspirations for future clinical practice – adult (18 + years of age) IPV advocates who work or volunteer with organizations that serve survivors of IPV in Alberta, British Columbia, or Saskatchewan were eligible to participate. There were no exclusion criteria.

Participant recruitment

Participants were recruited between March 1st, 2024, and August 30th, 2024. A reach-based (extending to as many potential participants within the network and scope of the research team as possible) and resource-conscious (functioning within the working constraints of the project) approach to recruitment was used. The research team reached out to existing contacts and sought out new contacts in organizations that serve survivors of IPV to share this research participation opportunity for IPV advocates. This included both existing community partners who were supporting separate research working with survivors of IPV and novel agencies who were not previously involved with the research team. A brief project summary was shared with IPV advocates to outline specifics about the focus group process. It was explicitly stated that participation was voluntary and independent of all work or volunteer related duties required by their organizations. Potential participants were encouraged to reach out to the research team or their organizational contact, decided by participant decision, to indicate interest and request more information.

The consent form was sent to all potential participants. Potential participants were advised to review the consent form and report back with any questions, comments, or concerns. If participants remained interested after being provided all the available pre-focus group information, then they were scheduled for a focus group. All those who expressed interest participated in focus groups.

Data generation

To explore how the varied perspectives and experiences of IPV advocates may inform the understanding of current and aspirations for future clinical practice within paramedicine, data were generated through focus groups. The interdisciplinary and strength-based design of many organizations that serve survivors of IPV are team-based. Focus groups were purposefully chosen as the IPV advocate role is typically collaborative, as opposed to individual, and the group setting was likely to best mimic the authenticity of the common IPV advocate environment versus individual interviews [34, 35]. In-person, virtual (via videoconferencing), and hybrid (in-person and virtual) options were provided to participants to accommodate the diverse locations and scheduling constraints of participants. Six focus group time slots were presented to participants. Participants were able to decide which options best met their needs and created the environment they felt they could share in openly.

The small focus groups (two to ten participants) were semi-structured and followed a flexible interview guide. The guide focused on four main areas: (1) experiences with paramedics in the context of supporting survivors of IPV, (2) desired experience and services from paramedics for survivors experiencing IPV, (3) challenges faced by survivors of IPV when accessing or utilizing service, and (4) solutions and actionable items for the corresponding challenges. The consent form, including the limits of confidentiality within a focus group, was reviewed with participants prior to the start of each focus group. Participants were encouraged to retain a copy of the consent form. Focus groups were approximately 60 min. All focus groups were conducted by a single researcher who also kept field notes (RAM). Focus groups were video and audio recorded (Zoom). All focus groups were conducted without incident. No crisis, safety concerns, or psychological upset were noted or reported during, or immediately following. Participants were offered a $50.00 honorarium for their participation. Some participants declined the compensation. Other participants were unable to claim the compensation as per organizational policies and procedures. All participants were aware of whether they would be receiving compensation prior to beginning the focus group. Participants were asked to complete a brief demographic survey that would be stored separately from the focus group outputs.

A hybrid of automated transcription (Zoom), line-by-line manual verbatim transcript confirmation, and descriptive field note integration (e.g., body language, tone, etc.) were purposefully used to meaningfully engage with the data beyond the use of automated transcription alone [33]. Transcripts were de-identified during this process by the researcher who conducted the focus group interviews (RAM) manually removing anything potentially identifying from the text (e.g., names, locations, service names, etc.). Participants were informed of this process in advance and did not report any concerns. Once de-identified transcripts were complete, all identifiable files were deleted as per standard operating procedures. De-identified focus group transcripts were analyzed.

Analysis

Demographic characteristics of focus groups participants (gathered from the demographic survey) were pooled and presented as basic proportions of the overall sample (mean±standard deviation or % (n/N); as appropriate). Pooling the data and deliberately not incorporating demographics tied to specific quotes was a conscious decision to attempt to avoid identification of participants.

To utilize the methodological rigour of open coding [36] and the ID principles of data interpretation [33], hybrid inductive line-by-line open pattern sorting was performed (RAM) to immerse the researcher in the focus group data, enhance the understanding of the data, and allow for spontaneous interpretation. While traditional coding can be useful in some methodologies, using the method herein aligned with the ID approach allowing for inductive pattern recognition in the context of the narrative. Data collection and analysis were conducted simultaneously (RAM), each informing the other [33]. The interpretation and description of the data were ongoingly reviewed, critiqued, and revised while being conscious of preconceived notions and bias (RAM, TNS). Once open pattern sorting had been completed, pattern labels were defined and organized (RAM).

The exhaustive list of open patterns was examined, re-examined, and refined (RAM) within three categories: (1) desired services, (2) challenges faced by survivors when receiving paramedic services, and (3) corresponding solutions for the identified challenges. Desired services was selected as a category to outline what services IPV advocates believe paramedics should provide for survivors of IPV. Challenges was selected because it outlined observed issues survivors of IPV faced when accessing services. The IPV advocate context adds knowledge of the systems in place to support survivors of IPV, which may be lacking from survivor perspectives. Solutions was selected to provide change stimulating direction from IPV advocate perspectives. Potential action items for paramedics and paramedic services were co-developed between the participants and the research team. In situations where participants had clearly identified action items, they were framed in the context of relevant outputs by the research team. In situations where participants did not identify specific action items, the research team used the interpretation of the solutions to guide developing action items for the context of paramedicine. This collaboration between the participants and research team to develop functional action items from multi-disciplinary, multi-sectoral perspectives is another strength of using an ID approach for applied disciplines [33]. This work was designed to stimulate change and equip paramedics and paramedics services with evidence to inform and enact change. To best describe challenges and corresponding solutions, the most impactful seven patterns are presented. This was a strategic decision by the research team to not overwhelm decision makers in the change process. Patterns within categories and exemplary quotes are presented. ID approaches do not typically conform to the notion of thematic saturation as the understanding that the experience and perspectives of a certain number of participants represent that of all participants is inherently flawed within this epistemology [33]. Correspondingly, recruitment ceased when reasonable effort had been exerted in each of the three selected provinces to engage with IPV advocates, and time and resource constraints led to a natural conclusion. The rigorous methods used to design and conduct this study aligned with the Big Tent Criteria for Qualitative Quality [37].

Results

The two focus groups that were not conducted were cancelled as there was minimal participant interest in those timings and interested participants had also selected other time slots that better met their scheduling needs. Desired practices

Participants reported that they perceived paramedics as versatile in-community health professionals. Survivors of IPV had reported to participants that paramedics attended to them in-community. Participants had also been present while paramedics attended in emergency shelters and transition houses. Participants appreciated the adaptability of paramedics in day-to-day operations across multiple settings. The clinical expertise of paramedics was valued, particularly given the frequency of attendance to traumatic and medical emergencies faced by survivors of IPV. Participants reported that survivors of IPV commonly encounter barriers to accessing traditional healthcare services and recognized paramedics could provide healthcare directly within the community. Specifically in emergency shelters and transition houses, participants observed that the additional education and training of CPs were sometimes being utilized to deliver a broader approach to care that encompassed the complex and intersecting needs of survivors.

As paramedics respond in-community, participants indicated paramedics’ ability to recognize a potentially unsafe situation and make it safe was a priority. Most healthcare professionals do not witness patient’s home lives first-hand, yet paramedics have this relatively unique insight. Participants reported that once a situation was recognized as potentially involving IPV, paramedics should validate the experience of the patient and refer the patient to specialized resources that can address the patient’s holistic health and well-being needs. Paramedics have a large breadth and depth of clinical circumstances to respond to, and facilitating access to specialized services was likely to reduce paramedic burden and meet the patient’s needs. Participants reported that they suspected the quality of care in situations involving IPV likely varied between at home visits and in shelter visits. Establishing a consistent standard of care provided by paramedics was suggested to be useful in creating a uniform approach where survivor needs were consistently being met. Cumulatively, participants viewed the role of paramedics and paramedic services as encompassing healthcare for traumatic and medical emergencies, the recognition of and validation for situations involving IPV, and the referral link to specialized resources.

Paramedic conduct was viewed as something that challenged survivors of IPV. Participants reported that while some paramedics provided empathetic and supportive care, the more common experiences were negative where survivors were battling bias, discrimination, racism, stereotyping, stigma, judgement, and ignorance. Negative experiences were common when the primary cause for paramedic attendance was injuries caused by IPV. The manifestations of these negative factors presented in rude interactions (e.g., victim blaming, inappropriate language, etc.) that were not focused around supporting patient needs. Disadvantageous paramedic conduct was viewed to impair building a safe and client-centered environment. From the perspectives of participants, paramedics were letting their personal beliefs and values interfere with their professional practice, even discouraging the use of paramedic services. Additionally, participants noted there was a power dynamic in paramedic attendances that is inherent with a uniform that was often inadvertently exacerbated by poor paramedic conduct.

Participants acknowledged that the professional capacities of paramedics did not situate them well to provide care for survivors of IPV. Given high call volumes, understaffing, and paramedic service utilization, participants believed paramedics were likely experiencing burnout and compassion fatigue. IPV calls were not always high acuity (e.g., cardiac arrests, motor vehicle accidents, etc.) in nature but required a substantial mental load for paramedics. With the reduced capacity of paramedics and the requirements for high-quality interpersonal skills (e.g., tone, body language, language, etc.) and empathy, the working conditions that precede IPV-caused paramedic attendance were believed to create challenges for survivors of IPV.

Systemic challenges within paramedic services were reported to have led to many shortcomings. Participants believed paramedics lacked specific education and training to support survivors of IPV. Specifically, paramedics were reported to commonly lack appropriate language, use of trauma-informed approaches, use of de-escalation techniques, understand­ing of the body’s response to trauma, and consid­eration for non-fatal strangulation and IPV-caused brain injury. Paramedics were frequently reported as not knowing what to ask, what to say, or what to do next in situations of IPV. In the absence of injuries or illness requiring immediate treatment, paramedics often conducted basic general assessments and offered transportation. In poorly handled situations, participants reported paramedics asking, “why are we here?” when there were no obvious injuries, which was viewed to invalidate the patient experience, neglect the survivor’s chronic health concerns, and show a lack of understanding of IPV. This suggested that paramedics were reluctant to look beyond the physical consequences of violence. This reluctancy led to participants prompting paramedics to provide services. Sexual IPV was often missed by paramedics, and participants believed there was a misunderstanding about sexual assault inside relationships among some paramedics. A general lack of understanding and awareness of IPV was noted and paired with a lack of direction or process. Participants reported an overall sense of inaction among paramedics and a lack of transitions into the healthcare system after paramedic contact. Participants felt that if transitions to services were not made immediately, that they often did not happen or were not pursued by survivors of IPV. There was a described failure to document IPV as relevant healthcare history in healthcare records or to report IPV to other relevant agencies (e.g., emergency departments, police, etc.). Paramedics attending survivors of IPV at repeated intervals were unaware of the history of IPV which led to repetitive questioning and repeatedly being asked to recount traumatic events. There was also concern for lack of interagency communication as survivors had often been in contact with multiple services (e.g., police, social work, shelters) and paramedic services. This commonly yielded multiple misaligned plans disjointedly and simultaneously moving forward in an unsuccessful manner. Concerns over which agencies had responsibility for what portion of care or service emerged. Participants reported that they observed multiple agencies attend, and then none of them reported the instance of IPV because each agency thought the other would report. Further, paramedics were described to have ignored participants (IPV advocates) when attending calls where they were on scene with survivors of IPV. Participants acknowledged that some direct assessments needed to be asked of the patient but argued that they often knew the in depth history of their clients and were better situated to share certain relevant information with paramedics compared to their client who had just experienced a traumatic event. This was echoed in terms of repeat attendances where survivors were resistant to share their stories again, especially if the last attendance resulted in inaction. Participants speculated there was a lack of process, standards, and policy within paramedic services which likely impacted paramedic care.

Situational logistics were reported to cause functional blockades to using paramedic services in their current context. Participants stated that survivors of IPV, many of whom are women with children, fear that child services will get involved and take away their children if they leave their home to attend the emergency department. As many survivors are also socially isolated, there are limited options for alternative childcare, especially when there is a safety concern leaving children with the perpetrator of IPV. Participants reported this caused a refusal of services and intentionally denying they had experienced IPV to paramedics. Beyond considerations for children, transportation concerns and insufficient regional availability of services were reported as logistical constraints. Each situation of IPV is different, and traditional paramedic service options were indicated to not be meeting the needs in most situations, often ignoring or failing to recognize IPV.

As IPV can cause and/or exacerbate acute and chronic health problems for survivors, the co-presentation of health conditions was reported to derail paramedics. Especially when mental conditions (e.g., post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, anxiety) and substance use were present, paramedics were reported to focus on the other health condition and ignore IPV. It appeared that paramedics became fixated on one piece of a patient’s health and then ignored additional issues. IPV during pregnancy was neglected, as paramedics focussed primarily on the pregnancy. The harm of IPV to both the well-being and health of child bearer and developing fetus were indicated to be concerning for participants. Participants reported that IPV was not viewed as a clinical circumstance by paramedics, despite being a root cause of poor health consequences and contributing to disadvantageous social determinants of health.

Paramedic service access was revealed to cause considerable challenges among survivors. Cost was identified as a major concern (in hospital or clinic services are covered through universal healthcare in Canada but emergency paramedic services are commonly excluded and require patient expense). Participants reported that survivors of IPV were concerned they did not have enough money to pay for paramedic services, and that the perpetrator of violence would be angered by receiving a bill for the services. Participants reported that paramedic co-response with police could complicate situations. Many survivors feared the police, had negative history with police, and/or did not want to cause legal trouble for the perpetrator. Police involvement frequently led to involvement of child protective services. Police were reported to be using a pro-charge approach which angered perpetrators and frequently caused violent consequences for the survivor when the perpetrator returned. Even in instances of good intentions, police involvement was reported as impairing the ability of survivors to access paramedic services. Participants acknowledged that safety concerns exist for paramedics, and that police involvement was often used to mitigate those concerns. Participants reported that paramedics do need to be safe as IPV situations can be volatile.

Participants reported survivors of IPV as patients were likely to contribute to challenges when being attended to by paramedics. Participants observed survivors blatantly deny injuries or misconstrue how they attained their injuries to paramedics. Additionally, participants reported that how survivors of IPV were feeling was likely relative, and because they were not being punched or strangled during paramedic assessment, they reported being alright in the moment. It was indicated that as survivors of IPV often live in situations of chronic violence, their perceptions of reality and safety had been inappropriately and unjustly disoriented. Either intentionally or unintentionally, a lack of disclosure of IPV by the survivor appeared to create challenges for paramedics. Fear of retaliation, love for their partner, a lack of options following disclosure of violence, and distrust of paramedics and the healthcare system were summarized to contribute to varied patient communication. Partner presence on scene was noted as another factor that swayed patient compliance. Participants stressed that speaking with the survivor in a private and safe manner was key. Further, participants reported that having a male paramedic speak to the survivor privately had caused accusations of sexual affairs between paramedics and patients by the perpetrators. Perpetrators were also reported to have been manipulative and fraternize with on scene responders, impairing the thorough and unbiased assessment of the patient. Participants described seeing paramedics be fooled by the public behavior displayed by perpetrators of IPV.

Solutions and actionable items

Participants identified numerous solutions and co-developed specific action items for their identified challenges survivors of IPV face when accessing and utilizing paramedic services. Corresponding solutions and actionable items have overlap that may contribute to mitigating multiple challenges (e.g., training paramedics in de-escalation techniques is a service level action item that may also improve individual paramedic conduct). Discussion

This study examined how the perspectives and experiences of IPV advocates may inform the understanding of current clinical practice and aspirations for future clinical practice within paramedicine. This study marks the first investigation of the perspectives of IPV advocates about the intersection of paramedicine and IPV. As IPV advocates are most commonly positioned as IPV experts outside of the hospital and healthcare system but with peripheral knowledge of the healthcare system and in-depth knowledge of in-community services, they are uniquely positioned to provide novel insight at the intersection of paramedicine and IPV. Paramedics typically operate as a first point of contact between the in-community environment and the healthcare system, and IPV advocates are likely to appreciate many of the nuances inherent to paramedicine.

Participants indicated that ideally paramedics would function as versatile emergency and primary care professionals capable of connecting patients with services and resources to meet both their health and well-being needs. Participants reported numerous challenges that clients faced when accessing and utilizing paramedic services, as evidenced through stories from clients and first-hand observations. Cumulatively, participants indicated improvements to education, training, and infrastructure would likely improve the services provided by paramedics to survivors of IPV. The findings herein align with survivor (patient) and paramedic perspectives reported previously, [30, 31] while adding additional context unique to the IPV advocate positioning. The synergized alignment from the three perspectives indicates a unified pathway for development of paramedicine at the intersection with IPV.

The intersection of paramedicine and IPV is emerging as an area requiring exploration. In the larger IPV landscape, areas like prevention and post IPV treatment interventions have been explored extensively [38, 39]. However, intersectionality continues to present with overlapping factors requiring consideration. The specifics of paramedicine and IPV must be explored within the sweeping IPV ecosystem.

Adopting trauma-informed care consistently emerged during the focus group process. The applications of a trauma-informed approach translates clinically into trauma-informed care [40]. Trauma-informed care relies on the principles that people are more likely than not to have experienced trauma, and that trauma may play a role in people’s lives and how they access services [41]. The implementation of trauma-informed care relies on understanding the impact of trauma and potential recovery paths, recognizing the signs and symptoms of trauma, responding appropriately, and avoiding re-traumatization [41]. The body’s acute physiological response to trauma may concurrently play a role. Further, trauma- and violence-informed care expands on trauma-informed care to address the intersecting influences of systemic and interpersonal violence and structural inequities across the life course, with attention to historical and ongoing violence and their effects [42, 43]. Adopting trauma- and violence-informed care may be relevant, applicable, and beneficial in the context of paramedicine. Validating statements like “violence is unacceptable” and “this is not your fault” can be useful back-pocket phrases to respond to disclosures. Planning questions like “are you safe?”, “how can I help?”, “will you be safe if I leave?” can prioritize patient safety and promote patient autonomy while progressing the call flow. Implementing trauma-informed care practices is crucial in advancing paramedics to become useful resources for survivors of IPV.

The role of the paramedic is not determining fact from fiction, but providing care for the patient based on the information they are presented. If a paramedic does not act on a disclosure from a patient and does not advance them in the process when an imminent safety, health, and/or well-being risk does exist, then the consequences could be harmful, if not lethal. The cost-benefit of not believing patients simply does not equate to acceptable practice.

Safety planning is a general term that covers the use of strategies to increase situational awareness and guide action in the event of threats to safety [44]. Basic safety assessments and planning may help paramedics provide options on the most appropriate pathways for survivors of IPV. Various IPV and lethality risk assessment tools have been developed [45, 46], but none have been applied in the paramedic setting. In the absence of established safety assessments, professional judgement may be used. With or without a formal safety assessment, building simple safety plans with patients is likely good practice [47]. Using extreme caution to minimize the risk of revictimization should be considered during safety planning approaches. With proper training, developing a basic safety plan can be done in about the amount of time it takes to complete a set of vital signs.

The cost of paramedic services for survivors of IPV must be addressed. While Canada boasts universal healthcare access, paramedic services frequently fall outside of those boundaries. For in-province residents with valid healthcare coverage, ground transportation fees range between $80.00 to $385.00 for one-way transport from a location to a healthcare facility [48–50]. In situations of paramedic attendance where the patient is not transported, fees range from $50.00 to $250.00 [48–50]. Although some populations (e.g., senior citizens, etc.) may be eligible for reduced or cost-free rates, fees of any kind create barriers to service use. Paramedic services must be included in the universal healthcare coverage to lower barriers to access and meaningfully integrate paramedicine into the healthcare system.

CPs are likely a viable option for addressing the unique clinical circumstance of IPV that requires the range of soft and hard skills paramedics possess. A recent review by Lunn et al. highlights along the dynamic continuum of health and social needs that CPs and community paramedicine programs can be mobilized to address the emerging demands [51]. Indeed, innovations within this sub-speciality of paramedicine show promise in both downstream and upstream capacities. Potential downstream innovations include bringing CPs into emergency shelters and transition houses as frontline holistic healthcare providers, and having CPs provide follow up at safe and neutral locations following situations of non-conveyance. Potential upstream innovations include using CPs to educate youth on healthy family and relationship dynamics, and working with those at risk of IPV to recognize early red flags before violence occurs.

Participants reported working with survivors of IPV who had experienced IPV-caused brain injuries. These injuries were reported from direct blows (e.g., punches, kicks), indirect blows (e.g., being slammed into a counter), and non-fatal strangulation (e.g., being “choked out”), all established mechanisms of IPV-caused brain injury [9]. The vast majority of survivors of physical IPV are likely to sustain a brain injury [52–55]. Interestingly, participants reported that survivors had been dismissed as “drunk” or “high” by paramedics, when in fact the participants suspected survivor substance use may have been disguising a brain injury. Further, these misdiagnoses were often made in the absence of substance use at all. Many signs and symptoms (e.g., confusion, vacant stare, dizziness, headache, etc.) are common presentations during situations of IPV, trauma, brain injury, substance use, and mental health exacerbations, respectively. In situations of IPV, it is likely that multiple circumstances may be simultaneously exhibited. Paramedics must exercise extreme caution as limited field diagnostic ability does not permit accurate differentiation. Thus, IPV-caused brain injury should be assumed until definitively ruled out by appropriate diagnostics. Additionally, non-fatal strangulation is a mechanism of brain injury and also a key risk factor for IPV-homicide [56, 57], further highlighting the need for safety assessment and planning.

The actionable items described in this research are not comprehensive but may still appear overwhelming to change makers when considering how to improve paramedic services. Any attempt to action any of the items listed in this article is a meaningful step toward improvement. The simultaneous development of education, training, infrastructure, and policy appears to be the most tangible approach if even for one item at a time. Improvement can be sectioned and layered. A simple plan might include: (1) a brief educational course taught by experts (IPV advocates can be great local experts) to help provide foundational knowledge for paramedics, (2) training on how to apply that knowledge through recognition, response, referral, and recording [20], (3) the integration of validated screening tools (Women Abuse Screen Tool (WAST), Abuse Assessment Screen (AAS), and Humiliation, Afraid, Rape and Kick (HARK) appear promising to identify IPV [58, 59], but none have been formally validated in the setting of paramedicine) into ambulance care report software, and (4) automated referrals triggered by screening tool use.

Additionally, a field that automatically selects if a potential identification of IPV is suspected through a screening tool or can be manually selected with a corresponding free text box to describe why may bolster the tool by allowing paramedics to adapt practice outside the constraints of a rigid format. Becoming more aware of communication styles (i.e., softer language like possible IPV or warrants further investigation for IPV) in the IPV field may promote paramedic use. This approach may mitigate the requirement for definitive evidence to utilize the field. Referrals when the corresponding field is selected should automatically advance the patient’s case to the appropriate resources in situations of conveyance and non-conveyance. This infrastructure could lower the burden on paramedics while still supporting progression down the appropriate pathway for survivors of IPV. Timely closed-loop feedback to paramedics may further facilitate uptake. A recent study at a single Canadian site found astounding violence rates against paramedics when tracked through functional infrastructure with feedback to reporting paramedics [60].

Novel pathways and processes are being developed for clinical circumstances that may not be best handled at the emergency department [61]. Policy and systemic support is another piece of critical infrastructure behind these changes. Paramedicine has made such progress over the last five years, and the opportunity for growth is greater than ever.

Limitations

Despite having a robust sample spanning three provinces in metropolitan to rural areas, the perspectives of the participants do not necessarily reflect the perspectives of all IPV advocates. Further research needs to be undertaken to increase the number of perspectives of IPV advocates in published literature. While IPV advocates may commonly interact with paramedics in emergency shelters and transition houses, the interactions reported where participants were not present were second-hand. The experiences and perspectives of survivors of IPV and IPV advocates should continue to be examined to gain a more well-rounded insight. This research was only conducted in the geographical area of western Canada, and this may not be generalizable to other regions. More research is needed to span the national breadth of these perspectives and expand to international contexts.

Conclusion

This investigation mobilizing the perspectives of IPV advocates about the intersection of paramedicine and IPV is the first of its kind. This research established a baseline for the services desired from paramedics, uncovered some key challenges faced by survivors of IPV when accessing and receiving services from paramedics, explored solutions for the identified challenges, and co-developed actionable items meant to facilitate positive change. Incorporating high-quality education and training, and the simultaneous development of functional infrastructure and policy, paramedics could be mobilized into specialized resources to bridge healthcare gaps for survivors of IPV. Further research is needed to continue to guide these innovations and trial their implementation.
