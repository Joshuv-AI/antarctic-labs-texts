Airway Risk in Angioedema: Validation of a Clinical Triage Algorithm for ENT Referral
Authors: Dan Yaniv, Igor Vainer, Dean Dudkiewicz, Aviram Mizrachi, Yaniv Hamzani, Eyal Yosefof, Nadav Granat, Gideon Bachar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify clinical factors associated with laryngeal involvement in patients with angioedema and to validate a triage algorithm for determining the need for nasopharyngoscopic evaluation in the emergency department (ED).Study DesignA retrospective review was performed of all patients presenting to the ED with angioedema or allergic reaction between 2014 and 2019 who underwent fiberoptic laryngeal (FOL) examination.SettingTertiary medical center.MethodsDemographic, clinical, and endoscopic data were analyzed to identify predictors of positive FOL findings. A multivariable logistic regression model was developed and tested in an independent temporal validation cohort (2023‐2025) using identical inclusion criteria.ResultsThe derivation cohort included 540 patients, 46 (8.5%) of whom had laryngeal edema on FOL. Age >50, shortness of breath, edematous uvula, and history of prior angioedema were independent predictors of laryngeal involvement, while lip swelling was protective. When the resulting triage algorithm was applied to the validation cohort (n = 43), 35 patients (81.4%) would have been managed without ENT referral; none required intubation or airway intervention. The algorithm correctly identified 79.5% of patients with a normal laryngeal examination and achieved an estimated 80% reduction in ENT consultations.ConclusionA simple triage algorithm based on age, shortness of breath, uvular edema, prior episodes, and lip swelling may safely guide the need for nasopharyngoscopy in patients with angioedema. Temporal validation confirmed that the approach can markedly reduce unnecessary ENT consultations without compromising airway safety.Level of EvidenceIII.

Angioedema is a self‐limited, localized swelling resulting from extravasation of fluid into interstitial tissues due to increased permeability of the vasculature supplying the subcutaneous and submucosal tissues and the deep dermis. 1 , 2 It may occur as an isolated event, accompanied by urticaria, or as a component of anaphylaxis. Areas with loose connective tissue are typically affected, such as the face, lips, mouth, throat, larynx, uvula, extremities, and genitalia. Bowel wall angioedema presents as colicky abdominal pain. 2 Critically, angioedema may be life‐threatening when the upper airway is involved. Two main types of angioedema are recognized: mast‐cell‐mediated (histaminergic) and bradykinin‐mediated. However, in many cases, the exact mechanism is unclear. Triggers may include allergic reactions, immune complex diseases, acquired deficiencies, or reaction to medications, such as angiotensin‐converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors. 3 The reported incidence and prevalence of angioedema vary according to the population studied and the data collection method (eg, self‐reported vs physician‐diagnosed cases). According to the World Allergy Organization, up to 20% of the population may be affected by urticaria and angioedema. 4 It is estimated that 10%–20% of individuals may experience at least one episode of angioedema during their lifetime. 5 , 6 In the United States, over one million ED visits annually are attributed to allergic reactions or angioedema. Among these, approximately 110,000 are coded specifically as angioedema (hereditary or acquired), and 979,400 as allergic reactions. Notably, 42.5% of allergic reactions also carry a secondary code for urticaria. 7 When patients present to the ED with suspected angioedema or urticaria, an otorhinolaryngologist is often consulted—sometimes emergently—to evaluate the airway and guide management decisions. 3 Despite the frequency of these consultations, there is limited evidence in the literature to guide which clinical features should prompt fiberoptic laryngoscopic (FOL) evaluation. To date, no standardized criteria have been established for otolaryngology referral or laryngeal examination in this population. The aim of this study was to identify clinical signs, symptoms, and physical examination findings associated with laryngeal involvement in patients presenting to the ED with angioedema. Our goal was to define features predictive of positive FOL findings in order to support evidence‐based triage and minimize unnecessary specialist evaluations.

Materials and Methods

Study Design: Retrospective Derivation And Temporal Validation Study

The healthcare database of a tertiary university‐affiliated medical center was retrospectively reviewed for all patients who presented to the ED with angioedema or allergic reaction between 2014 and 2019. Diagnoses included in the initial search were as follows: “other anaphylactic shock” (ICD 9 code 995.0), “angioneurotic edema” (ICD 9 code 995.1), “other and unspecified adverse effect of drug, medicinal and biological substance (due) to correct medicinal substance properly administered” (ICD 9 code 995.2), “allergy, unspecified” (ICD 9 code 995.3), “anaphylactic reaction due to unspecified food” (ICD 9 code 995.6), “other adverse food reactions, not elsewhere classified” (ICD 9 code 995.7), “acute epiglottitis without mention of obstruction” (ICD 9 code 478.6), “edema of larynx” (ICD 9 code 478.6), “stridor” (ICD 9 code 786.1), “epiglottitis” (ICD 9 code 464.3), and “epiglottitis (chronic)” (ICD 9 code 476.1). Only patients evaluated by an otorhinolaryngologist during the ED visit were included in the analysis. Both cohorts included only patients who underwent ENT evaluation and fiberoptic laryngoscopy, representing a higher‐risk subgroup.

Data were collected from the medical files on demographics, signs and symptoms at presentation, and findings on physical, otolaryngologic, and fiberoptic laryngeal (FOL) examination.

A triage decision algorithm for ENT consultation was derived from variables independently associated with positive FOL findings in multivariable logistic regression analysis, including age >50 years, shortness of breath, uvular edema on examination, history of prior angioedema. Patients were classified as high‐risk if ≥1 risk factor was present, and low‐risk if none of these features were identified. ENT consultation and fiberoptic laryngoscopy were recommended for high‐risk patients.

To validate our findings, we conducted the same electronic database search for a later time period (2023‐2025) using identical inclusion criteria. This represented a temporal validation cohort drawn from a distinct, subsequent period (2023‐2025) using the same inclusion criteria and data abstraction protocol. This search yielded 66 additional patients who met the study criteria, of whom 43 had documentation of an ENT examination. These 43 patients served as a test group to evaluate the predictive value of the factors identified in the initial cohort. The proposed decision algorithm derived from the first cohort was retrospectively applied to these patients to assess its potential clinical utility.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Rabin Medical Center with waiver of informed consent.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using the SPSS software package, version 25.0 (SPSS).

Variables were summarized as mean and standard deviation or number and percent. Shapiro‐Wilk test was applied to determine if data deviated from a normal distribution. Student t‐test was used to compare normally distributed continuous variables between groups, and Kruskal‐Wallis test was used for nonparametric variables. Chi‐square or Fisher's test was used to analyze categorical variables. Multiple binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify predictors of a positive FOL examination. The model included the independent variables that were statistically significant on univariate analysis and included—age (years), physical examination findings such as shortness of breath, swollen lips or uvular edema, and a history of recurrent episodes. A two‐sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant. Missing data were addressed by complete‐case analysis; however, no observations were excluded because all variables included in the final logistic regression model were complete.

Results

Of the 5282 patients who received an ED diagnosis concurring with angioedema/urticaria (see Materials and Methods) during the study period, 621 were examined by an otorhinolaryngologist. An additional 81 patients were excluded after our retrospective review failed to find documentation of laryngoedema or allergic reaction in the clinical chart. The remaining 540 patients formed the study group.

Mean patient age was 41.5 years (range 18‐95); 202 patients (37.4%) were male. A background of allergy (food or medications) was recorded in 189 cases (35%). Mean time from symptom onset to ED arrival was 8.8 hours (Range 20 minutes to 96 hours).

The main complaint at presentation was shortness of breath (46%), followed by skin edema (42%) and swollen lips (33.5%) (Table 1). The most common finding on physical examination in the ED was urticaria (41%), and on ENT examination, mucosal swelling (oral mucosa, uvula, or tongue) (27.2%) (Table 2). Findings on FOL study were positive (arytenoid, epiglottic, or vocal cord edema) in 46 patients (8.5%) of whom 22 (4% of cohort) were hospitalized; 2 of them were admitted to the intensive care unit and 2 were intubated.

Patients with positive FOL findings were older (age > 50) than patients with negative findings (49.8 vs 41.2, P = .003) and presented with more complaints of shortness of breath (67.4% vs 43.9%, P = .002) and fewer complaints of swollen lips (13% vs 35.4%, P = .002). They were characterized by a higher rate of mucosal edema and a lower rate of swollen lips on physical examination, and higher rates of uvular edema (21.7% vs 9.5%, P = .01) and respiratory findings of tachypnea, dyspnea, hoarseness or stridor (17.4% vs 4.7%, P < .0001) on ENT examination. Patients with a positive FOL examination had a higher rate of history of previous episodes of laryngoedema than patients with a negative study (43.5% vs 20.6%, P < .0001) and were hospitalized more often (19.6% vs 2.6%, P < .0001). On multiple logistic regression analysis, the factors independently predicting a pathological laryngeal examination were older age (age > 50) at presentation, shortness of breath, edematous uvula on ENT examination, and previous episodes of angioedema. Swollen lips at presentation was a protective factor.

Separate analysis of the patients with recurrent episodes showed that they had a more allergic background and mucosal swelling at presentation than patients without a history of angioedema, but fewer complaints of rash. On physical examination in the ED, they had a higher rate of swollen lips and mucosal findings and a lower rate of evident urticaria (29.5% vs 44%, P = .004). Their ENT examination yielded higher rates of all pathological parameters evaluated, with three times more positive FOL findings than first‐time patients (16.4% vs 6.5%, P < .0001). Their hospital admission rate was higher as well (7.4% vs 3.1%, P = .03).

Compared to patients discharged home from the ED, patients who were hospitalized for treatment or observation were older (55.1 vs 41.4 years, P = .001), presented to the ED with less delay after symptom onset (5.4 vs 8.9 hours, P = .001), and had more complaints of shortness of breath. Examination in the ED yielded more mucosal and respiratory findings, which were also prominent on the ENT examination. Importantly, hospitalized patients had a higher rate of positive FOL examinations (40.9% vs 7.1%, P < .0001) and of previous events (49% vs 21.8%, P = .03).

Patients with an allergic background presented with less delay after symptom onset, had more clinical findings of shortness of breath and dysphagia, and more ENT findings of mucosal swelling than patients without allergies.

Table 3 shows the parameters that were found to be predictive of a positive FOL examination. In multivariable logistic regression, older age (adjusted OR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01‐1.04, P = .005), shortness of breath (adjusted OR 2.19, 95% CI 1.10‐4.33, P = .025), uvular edema (adjusted OR 2.68, 95% CI 1.18‐6.11, P = .019), and recurrent episodes (adjusted OR 2.78, 95% CI 1.44‐5.38, P = .002) were independently associated with positive FOL findings. Swollen lips were inversely associated with positive FOL findings (adjusted OR 0.29, 95% CI 0.11‐0.81, P = .018). The model showed acceptable calibration (Hosmer‐Lemeshow P = .120) and fair discriminatory ability, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.757.

Following identification of the predictors of positive FOL findings, we next examined whether these factors could be used to guide triage decisions in an independent cohort. The proposed algorithm demonstrated high negative predictive value (NPV) across clinically relevant outcomes. For positive FOL findings, the NPV was 96.0% (95% CI, 92.3‐98.0). For hospital admission, the NPV was 99.5% (95% CI, 97.3‐99.9), and for airway intervention, the NPV was 100% (95% CI, 98.1‐100.0). Validation cohort analysis

In the validation cohort (n = 43), 4 patients (9.3%) had laryngeal edema on FOL and none required airway intervention. Application of the proposed triage algorithm suggested that 35 patients (81.4%) could have been managed without ENT consultation and FOL. Among these 35 patients, 31 (88.6%) had a normal laryngeal examination and 4 (11.4%) had mild laryngeal edema (Mild laryngeal edema was defined as limited mucosal swelling without airway narrowing, preserved glottic visualization, and no impairment of vocal fold mobility). Overall, 39/43 patients (90.7%) had no laryngeal findings on FOL, and the algorithm correctly identified 31 of them (79.5%) as not requiring FOL. Importantly, none of the patients who would have been managed without ENT consultation under the algorithm required intubation or any other airway intervention. Discussion

The present study identified several factors that may serve to distinguish patients presenting to the ED with angioedema and/or urticaria who may be at risk of airway distress and require FOL examination. This two‐cohort design, incorporating a temporal validation set, strengthens the reproducibility of our findings and supports the clinical utility of the proposed algorithm.

Given the potential of angioedema to rapidly progress to a potentially life‐threatening condition, the first step in the ED assessment of a patient with suspected angioedema is to consider the safety of the airway. This entails a detailed oropharyngeal examination with specific attention to edema in areas reported in the literature to be potential predictor of upper airway obstruction, namely, the lips, tongue, soft palate, uvula, and posterior pharynx. 8 The presence of stridor or hoarseness must be noted because it mandates further diagnostic tests. However, if the airway is deemed unsafe and the patient is unstable with no time for further assessments, the site of airway involvement should be evaluated by the clinical signs, 9 and the local anaphylaxis protocol should be followed with possible airway intervention by intubation or cricothyrotomy/tracheotomy. If the patient is stable, visualization of the airway, ideally by indirect nasopharyngeal laryngoscopy, should be considered before decision regarding airway management in the operating room is taken.

The validity and potential clinical impact of our triage approach were further explored in an independent validation cohort. Applying the decision scheme to 43 additional patients suggested that more than 80% of ENT consultations could have been avoided. Although 4 of 35 patients who would not have been referred under the proposed algorithm had mild laryngeal edema on FOL, none required airway intervention, and all had a favorable clinical course. These findings support the central aim of the algorithm, which is to identify patients at risk for clinically significant airway compromise rather than to detect all instances of mucosal edema. Nevertheless, the possibility of missing mild laryngeal involvement underscores the need for careful clinical judgment and highlights that the algorithm should complement, rather than replace, bedside assessment.

Regarding site of involvement, Felder et al, 10 found that pharyngeal and tongue edema were predictors of the need for hospitalization. Furthermore, they showed that urticaria was associated with peripheral and lip angioedema, and absence of urticaria correlated with tongue angioedema and trended toward a correlation with pharyngeal angioedema. 11 These results suggested that lip and skin angioedema were apparently less concerning in terms of upper airway obstruction than pharyngeal and tongue angioedema. This triage can be easily performed by a general practitioner or physician in the ED and does not necessitate special equipment (ie, nasopharyngoscope) or specific skills. The presence of edema posterior to the lips (ie, tongue, uvula, pharynx) or clinical signs such as shortness of breath or hoarseness should prompt early ENT consultation and FOL.

Accordingly, Ishoo et al 12 reported that clinical symptoms of voice change, hoarseness, dyspnea, and stridor were associated with the need for airway intervention. While patients presenting with rash and edema of the face, lips, or soft palate could be treated on an outpatient basis, patients with lingual, especially laryngeal, edema, required intensive care unit admission. They suggested that airway risk in angioedema may be predicted by anatomic site of presentation, allowing appropriate triage with preparation for airway intervention in selected cases.

Thus, as a general rule, the need for preventive protective intubation is significantly greater in patients presenting with difficulty breathing or swelling of the oral cavity, oropharynx, or larynx than in patients with swelling of the face or lips in whom drug treatment is likely to be sufficient.

Previous studies also found that the incidence of intubation increases with age. 11 Our study showed age to be a risk factor for more severe findings on fiber optic laryngeal examination at presentation.

Hospitalization can be used as a proxy measure of the severity of angioedema. Studies have shown that patients presenting to the ED with undifferentiated angioedema (ie, including both histamine‐ and bradykinin‐mediated angioedema) are admitted for inpatient care more frequently than patients with allergic reactions (11% vs 2.2% of ED visits). 13 , 14

In the present study, patients who were hospitalized were older (age > 50), presented to the ED with less delay, and had more complaints of shortness of breath. On examination in the ED, they had more mucosal findings and respiratory complaints, which were also prominent on the ENT examination. Significantly, they had more pathological findings on FOL examination, and more of them had had similar previous events. Thus, suspicions of airway risk should be raised, and laryngeal visualization performed, in patients with recurrent episodes of angioedema.

On statistical analysis of predictors of a positive FOL examination, we found that the absence of shortness of breath was associated with lower odds of a positive FOL examination, corresponding to an adjusted odds ratio of approximately 0.46 compared with patients presenting with shortness of breath. Similarly, the absence of uvular edema on ENT examination was associated with lower odds of positive FOL findings, with an adjusted odds ratio of approximately 0.37. Therefore, we suggest that clinicians should consider avoiding ENT referral when these easily identified findings are absent.

We propose a decision making flow chart for the ED physician (Figure 1) that is a basis for future prospective studies that can confirm or reject our approach. This decision making tool is based on the findings of our study and suggests which patients should undergo emergency airway management, which patients should undergo ENT consultation and nasopharyngoscopy and which patients could be managed conservatively without ENT consult.

Proposed triage algorithm for patients presenting to the emergency department with angioedema. The algorithm was retrospectively validated in an independent cohort (n = 43), where its application would have reduced ENT consultations by >80% with no adverse airway outcomes.

From a pragmatic ED perspective, the primary goal of triage is to prevent missed clinically significant airway compromise rather than to detect all instances of mucosal edema. In our validation cohort, the proposed algorithm would have reduced ENT consultations by more than 80%, and none of the patients who would have been managed without ENT review required airway intervention. Although mild laryngeal edema was present in 4/35 such patients, their clinical course was uneventful, suggesting that the algorithm is sensitive for identifying patients who do not need urgent laryngoscopy in terms of airway outcomes. Larger series are needed to more precisely quantify its performance and to confirm that this reduction in consults does not compromise safety.

Importantly, the primary goal of triage in this setting is not to detect all instances of mucosal edema, but rather to safely identify patients at risk for clinically significant airway compromise. In our validation cohort, although 4 patients in the low‐risk group demonstrated mild laryngeal edema on FOL, none required airway intervention or escalation of care. This finding highlights an acceptable and clinically relevant trade‐off; whereby minor findings may be missed without compromising patient safety.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, both the derivation and validation cohorts included only patients who underwent otolaryngology evaluation and fiberoptic laryngoscopy, representing a selected higher‐risk subgroup rather than all patients presenting to the emergency department with angioedema. This introduces potential spectrum bias and limits generalizability to lower‐risk patients who may not trigger specialist consultation. In the validation cohort, a substantial proportion of eligible patients lacked ENT documentation and were excluded, which may further contribute to selection bias.

Second, the retrospective design is subject to variability in clinical documentation and unmeasured confounders, particularly for symptom reporting and physical examination findings. Third, the primary outcome was laryngeal edema on fiberoptic examination rather than clinically significant airway compromise. However, in both cohorts, airway interventions were rare, and no patient who would have been managed without ENT consultation required airway intervention, supporting the clinical safety of the algorithm.

Conclusion

In patients presenting to the emergency department with suspected angioedema, older age, shortness of breath, uvular edema, and a history of angioedema should prompt urgent consultation with an otorhinolaryngologist, whereas isolated lip swelling is a reassuring sign. Incorporating these predictors into an evidence‐based triage algorithm may safely reduce unnecessary ENT consultations by approximately 80%, optimizing resource use and patient flow. None of the patients who would have been managed without ENT referral in the validation cohort required airway intervention, underscoring the potential safety of this approach. Prospective multicenter validation is warranted to confirm its generalizability and integration into routine ED practice.
