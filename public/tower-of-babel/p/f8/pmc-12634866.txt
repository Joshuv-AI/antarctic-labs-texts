Artificial intelligence in thoracic surgery: Perspectives and challenges
Authors: Wenwei Liao, Bin Peng, Guanggui Ding, Zhikai Li, Guangsuo Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With the advances of artificial intelligence (AI) in the medical field, particularly the widespread utilization of large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama, and Deepseek, clinical practice is undergoing an unprecedented technological revolution. These cutting-edge technologies facilitate efficient processing and analysis of vast datasets, providing medical professionals with auxiliary diagnoses and treatment suggestions, while markedly enhancing the quality and efficiency of medical services. Over the past decade, the field of thoracic surgery has achieved transformative progress, primarily driven by AI innovations. Consequently, thoracic surgeons must possess a foundational understanding of AI in order to grasp its implications on their daily practice and explore potential ways of integrating this technology into their work. This article reviews the fundamental elements of AI and the relationships between AI-based techniques. It further summarizes the application of AI in thoracic surgery, aiming to enhance thoracic surgeons' comprehensive understanding of the latest developments in this area. Additionally, this article explores the challenges and limitations faced by AI, including data security and privacy concerns, issues of bias and discrimination, challenges in verification and interpretability, ethical and legal considerations, technical obstacles, as well as training and educational requirements. Finally, it explores emerging AI architectures and their paradigm-shifting impacts on medical ecosystems.

Introduction

Despite rapid advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) across diverse domains, its integration into clinical practice remains limited. Lung cancer remains a significant and challenging disease and stands as the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. The widespread adoption of low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening has increased the detection of lung nodules [2]. AI has been utilized in lung nodule diagnosis for decades [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. It is anticipated to significantly enhance the early detection and characterization of lung cancer by distinguishing between benign and malignant nodules; ‌this progress has‌ the potential to greatly improve patient outcomes, reduce unnecessary treatments, and even save lives, as early-stage lung cancer is often treatable [7].

The term “AI” was first introduced in the 1950s, defined as the hypothesis that every facet of learning or any other characteristic of intelligence can, in principle, be articulated with such precision that a machine could be constructed to simulate it [8]. However, this definition is not sufficiently accurate. In fact, AI is an emerging technological science that revolutionizes problem-solving by simulating human intelligence, including perception, understanding, reasoning, learning, and decision-making through computer programs and algorithms [[9], [10]]. It is regarded as one of the three cutting-edge technologies of the 21st century, alongside genetic engineering and nanoscience. Generally speaking, AI comprises four key elements: big data, computing power, algorithms, and scenarios [10] (Fig. 1). a) Big data is defined as "extremely large datasets that can be computationally analyzed to uncover patterns, trends, and associations, especially in relation to human behavior and interactions" [11]. It also refers to a large amount of unstructured data from various sources which serves as the foundation for AI [[12], [13], [14]]. b) Computing power primarily relies on AI processors, which are specialized functional modules designed to address large-scale computational tasks in AI applications. The computing power serves as a solid foundation, providing strong support for AI [15]. c) The algorithm is the fundamental method for implementing AI and an effective way to extract data. It provides a set of rules for AI, enabling machines to execute specific tasks, such as classification. The current algorithms mainly include machine learning, classifiers, deep learning, neural networks, and natural language processing [16] (Table 1). d) Application scenarios constitute the terminus of AI, representing the practical arenas where AI technology is employed and serving as the touchstone for evaluating its value. Algorithms in AI.

Recent advances in AI have been especially pronounced in NLP, which has demonstrated exceptional capabilities in converting unstructured clinical narratives (e.g., medical reports, operative notes, discharge summaries) into analyzable structured data [25]. The Chat Generative Pre-trained Transformer (ChatGPT), a NLP tool grounded in deep learning, has garnered global attention across diverse sectors, including medical and academic research, since its inception [26] (Fig. 2B). Utilizing the GPT-4 model, ChatGPT surpassed the passing threshold of the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) by over 20 points [27]. In an impressive 90 % of subjective queries, ChatGPT provided coherent, insightful, and well-reasoned responses to medical inquiries, suggesting a considerable level of medical competence based on its performance [28]. Furthermore, several other prominent large language models (LLMs), including Claude [29], Gemini [30], Llama [31], and Deepseek [32], exhibit powerful capabilities in NLP as well (Fig. 2B). Notably, in January 2025, a new generative AI model named Deepseek-R1 triggered global attention [[33], [34], [35]]. In benchmark tests, it achieved 97.3 % on the MATH-500 problem set developed by University of California, Berkeley researchers, and ‌outperformed‌ 96.3 % of human competitors ‌in‌ the Codeforces programming contest [36]. In clinical practice, thoracic surgeons primarily base disease diagnoses on patient complaints, symptoms, and imaging data, while histopathological examination constitutes the gold standard for determining benign or malignant tumors [37]. AI presents ‌distinct advantages‌ for analyzing imaging data such as ‌chest X-rays, pulmonary computed tomography (CT) scans, and pathological sections of lung tissue‌. With the advent of AI, its capabilities in interpreting medical images and pathology slides have now reached physician-level standards in both speed and accuracy. Krizhevsky et al. [38] pioneeringly utilized a multilayered convolutional computational model, known as Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), to identify and classify 1.2 million high-resolution images, attaining unprecedented accuracy. Cicero et al. [39] applied CNN deep learning to an extensive dataset of 35,038 chest X-ray images to identify pleural effusion, enlarged cardiac silhouette, pulmonary consolidation, pulmonary edema, and pneumothorax. The precision and sensitivity of this AI system ‌exceeded 91 %‌ for these conditions. For CT images, the accuracy of pulmonary nodule classification and grading also approached 90 % [40]. A key advantage of CNN deep learning algorithms over traditional machine learning methods is that the first step does not require the calculation of features [21]. By utilizing historical data to build analytical models, AI rapidly detects lesions in medical images, ‌enhancing clinical efficiency‌ [41]. Current evidence demonstrates that AI has significantly transformed lung cancer management by improving multiple aspects, such as staging, prognosis evaluation, treatment prediction, response assessment, recurrence forecasting, and personalized prognostic analysis [42].

The structure of this article follows the clinical pathway, commencing with history-taking and electronic health records (EHR), progressing to lung cancer examination and diagnosis. We subsequently examine AI-assisted clinical decision-making and surgical procedures, investigate AI's potential for rapid intraoperative frozen-section analysis and pathological classification of lung cancer, and conclude by discussing postoperative health management. This article aims to review the fundamentals of AI and its applications in thoracic surgery, while critically analyzing the challenges and limitations associated with widespread AI adoption in thoracic surgery and healthcare at large‌.

AI in thoracic surgery

Assists in history-taking and writing EHR

To date, generative AI has emerged as a transformative force in healthcare through its advanced NLP capabilities. ChatGPT's performance is particularly notable. A study conducted a systematic evaluation of the performance of seven LLMs in generating programming code across diverse prompt strategies, programming languages, and task difficulties [43]. The results indicated that GPT-4 significantly surpassed the other LLMs, including Gemini Ultra, Llama 2, and Claude 2 [43]. AI models interact with patients through simulated conversations, inquiring about their relevant symptoms, disease progression, past medical history, and other information. They extract key information from patient responses and transforms it into actionable clinical data. This interactive approach not only enables clinicians to efficiently complete history-taking, but also effectively alleviate the workload for medical staff and improve the overall quality of patient care [[44], [45]] (Fig. 3).Fig. 3The potential applications of AI in thoracic surgery within a clinical pathway. Processing medical documents within EHR systems is a major factor leading to healthcare worker burnout. ChatGPT has the potential to enhance the efficiency of medical document processing by generating standardized templates and automatically filling in relevant clinical information, optimizing the workflow of medical records [46] (Fig. 3). Safavi et al. [47] developed a neural network model using EHR data to facilitate the discharge process for inpatient surgical care. ‌Their‌ findings demonstrated the model's ability to ‌accurately‌ predict daily discharges and identify potential discharge obstacles. Collectively, these advancements highlight AI's role in improving thoracic surgeons' workflow efficiency through enhanced history-taking and EHR documentation.

Assists in examination and diagnosis

Medical imaging examinations, commonly used in clinical practice for diagnosis and therapy, provide insights into the morphology and function of human tissues and organs, playing a pivotal role in the diagnosis and prognosis of various diseases [48]. The integration of AI with medical imaging has emerged as one of the most promising areas. In recent years, AI models have attained remarkable success in interpreting medical images [49], showcasing great promise in detecting and categorizing abnormalities across various imaging modalities, such as chest radiographs [50], CT scans [51], and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans [52]. Large-scale randomized controlled trials, including the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) and the Dutch-Belgian Randomized Lung Cancer Screening Trial (NELSON), have shown that low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening ‌significantly reduces‌ lung cancer mortality compared to chest radiography or no screening [[53], [54]]. The widespread adoption of LDCT for early lung cancer detection has generated substantial datasets ‌amenable to‌ AI analysis [[54], [55]]. Through training on multiple datasets, computers can develop reliable AI models whose‌ accuracy in identifying certain ‌disease images‌ approaches human-level performance [56]. ‌This technology contributes‌ to more accurate diagnoses in thoracic surgery and ‌optimized‌ treatment decision-making for lung cancer (Fig. 3).

Computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) was developed to assist radiologists in identifying lesions on chest radiographs and CT scans [[57], [58], [59], [60]]. A retrospective study of 50 solid lung nodules demonstrated that CAD can detect up to 70 % of cancers missed by radiologists, validating the practical value of CAD as a second reader [61]. In a prospective randomized trial of 10,476 screening participants, AI-based CAD significantly improved actionable lung nodule detection rates on chest radiographs [50]. Furthermore, Nam et al. [62] applied deep learning to identify malignant pulmonary nodules in chest radiographs, showing their algorithm outperformed physicians in both radiograph classification and nodule detection accuracy. Ardila et al. [63] developed a deep learning algorithm predicting lung cancer risk using current and prior CT scans. The algorithm matched radiologists' performance when prior scans were available and surpassed them without historical data. The integration of AI technology with chest radiographs/CT allows for quick and automatic marking of the location, size, nature, and relationship between the lesion and surrounding tissue, significantly reducing diagnosis time while improving accuracy [[64], [65]]. Another multicenter study of 120 pulmonologists from 16 European hospitals revealed AI-based software achieved 100 % compliance with American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) pulmonary function test guidelines and 82 % diagnostic accuracy, surpassing physicians' 74.4 % guideline adherence and 44.6 % diagnostic accuracy, with significantly lower interrater variability [66]. These technologies enhance disease screening precision and reduce misdiagnosis rates [67], proving particularly valuable for less experienced clinicians [[68], [69]].

AI demonstrates potential as a powerful auxiliary diagnostic tool, enhancing interpretation accuracy and physician performance in clinical workflows. its ongoing refinement holds promise for achieving early detection, timely diagnosis, and personalized treatment of lung cancer.

Assists in clinical decision-making

Patient-generated health data (PGHD) is increasingly utilized in regulatory decision-making processes and the evaluation of care quality, encompassing self-reported health and treatment histories, patient-reported outcomes, as well as biometric sensor data [11]. Leveraging its advanced big data analysis capabilities, AI efficiently extracts pivotal insights from PGHD, thereby enabling the discernment of disease patterns, the evaluation of therapeutic efficacy, and the forecasting of disease risks. This capability is of great significance in the clinical decision-making processes and the customization of individual treatment strategies. In addition to providing support to clinicians with assistive information, various initiatives have also shown the effectiveness of AI in the clinical decision-making stages of the workflow [70].

When considering surgical interventions for primary non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), lobectomies have a mortality rate of 2.3 %, significantly lower than the 6.8 % observed with pneumonectomies in high-volume specialized centers [71]. Accurate preoperative patient assessment is crucial for assessing surgical risks. In contemporary thoracic surgery, sublobar resection (including segmentectomy) has emerged as a principal surgical approach for solitary pulmonary nodules. Nevertheless, the considerable anatomical variations in segmental bronchovascular structures pose greater technical challenges compared to lobectomy [[72], [73], [74], [75]]. Consequently, precise delineation of the perinodular anatomical relationships is crucial for potentially malignant nodules. Li et al. [76] developed an AI-based three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction system (trained on 500 cases, Dice coefficient = 89.2 %) for surgical planning. In retrospective (n = 113) and prospective cohorts (n = 139) undergoing lobectomy or segmentectomy, this AI system reconstructed 13,608 pulmonary segmental branches, with 1573 target branches corresponding to phantoms detectable for intraoperative verification. Accuracy rates reached 100 % and 97 % for segmental bronchi, 97.2 % and 99.1 % for segmental arteries, and 93.2 % and 98.8 % for segmental veins, respectively. AI assistance reduced operative time by 24.5 min for lobectomy (p < 0.001) and 20 min for segmentectomy (p = 0.007), while reconstruction time was 14.2 min shorter than conventional software (p < 0.001). These results validate the system's anatomical prediction accuracy and operational efficiency. Notably, AI-assisted 3D reconstruction and 3D-printed models are increasingly adopted in thoracic surgery, providing intuitive visualization of pulmonary lesions and adjacent anatomy to optimize surgical planning and reduce operative risks [[77], [78]].

Furthermore, in ‌anatomic pulmonary resection procedures‌, ‌intraoperative frozen section analysis‌ is often necessary. The pathological results determine the surgical resection extent (e.g., sublobar resection vs. lobectomy), lymph node management (e.g., standard vs. extended lymphadenectomy), and postoperative therapeutic planning. In this context, rapid and precise pathology reporting not only conserves operative time but also provides critical guidance for intraoperative decision-making. A multicenter study demonstrated that a 3D-CNN-based AI system achieves high diagnostic accuracy for predicting pulmonary nodule malignancy and invasiveness, showing 97 % specificity in intraoperative frozen section correlation for surgical decision-making [79].

The importance of AI in surgical decision-making is growing, as it effectively integrates diverse information sources, including patient risk factors, anatomical considerations, disease progression, patient preferences, and cost implications. By utilizing AI, surgeons and patients can make more accurate predictions regarding surgical outcomes [80]. AI serve as a clinical decision support tool, providing surgeons with intelligent treatment plans and recommendations to facilitate precision surgical medicine for patients (Fig. 3).

Assists in surgical procedures

Reducing surgical trauma for patients has consistently been a primary goal of modern surgical medicine. In the 1920s, laparoscopy marked the beginning of the "minimally invasive" surgery era [81], which minimizes‌ trauma through smaller incisions than traditional methods [82]. Falcoz et al. [83] analyzed 5442 lobectomy patients from the European Society of Thoracic Surgeons (ESTS) database ‌comparing‌ video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) and thoracotomy outcomes. Results demonstrated significantly lower overall complication rates with VATS versus thoracotomy (29.1 % vs 31.7 %, p = 0.0357), including a 43 % reduction in major cardiopulmonary complications (15.9 % vs 19.6 %, p = 0.0094) and a 2-day decrease in hospitalization duration (7.8 days vs 9.8 days, p = 0.0003). However, laparoscopy also has limitations: the restricted rotation angle of surgical instruments creates surgical blind spots, and ‌provides only‌ a two-dimensional (2D) view. This hinders the application of laparoscopic technology in more complex operations and presents a bottleneck in its development.

To address these limitations, surgical robots were developed and rapidly adopted in clinical practice in the 21st century, establishing robotic surgery as the mainstream minimally invasive approach‌. After receiving U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval in July 2000, the ‌da Vinci‌ surgical robot system became the world's first robotic surgical platform ‌cleared‌ for operating room use [84]. Surgical robots are widely used in clinical and surgical applications, enhancing surgical accuracy, reducing surgical trauma, and mitigating the reliance on surgical experience [85]. Although commonly termed 'robotic surgery,' the da Vinci system technically falls short of this definition due to its inability to execute programmed maneuvers autonomously‌ [86]. Robotic-assisted surgery (RAS) therefore is not considered AI‌, despite frequent misuse of the term. Current RAS remain entirely dependent on surgeons' skills and experience‌. However, with AI advancements, ‌future AI-assisted robotic systems may autonomously perform interventions and adapt to various scenarios (Fig. 3).

Shademan et al. [87] compared intestinal anastomosis metrics among supervised autonomous systems (autonomous robots), manual laparoscopy, and clinically deployed RAS. ‌Evaluation metrics included‌ suture consistency, leak pressure, ‌number of needle extraction errors‌, procedure time, and luminal reduction post-anastomosis. Results demonstrated supervised autonomous surgery's superiority over RAS, laparoscopy, and manual techniques in ‌both ex vivo porcine tissues and in living pigs‌, establishing autonomous robotic surgery feasibility. Similarly, Saeidi et al. [88] developed an autonomous robotic laparoscopic system (ARLS) for intestinal anastomosis, comparing its quality‌ against manual laparoscopy and RAS ‌in phantom models and in vivo intestinal tissues‌. ‌Evaluation metrics included‌ needle adjustment corrections, suture spacing, suture bite size, completion time, luminal patency, and leak pressure. Results showed ARLS's ‌superior consistency and precision‌ over expert-performed manual techniques and RAS, ‌indicating that highly autonomous surgical robots may enhance procedural standardization, improve outcomes, and increase access to optimized techniques‌.

Furthermore, advancements in AI-assisted visualization technologies—such as 3D reconstruction/printing, virtual reality, augmented reality, and mixed reality—enable converting pulmonary CT imaging from 2D images into 3D models, thereby facilitating surgical education, planning, and simulation [[89], [90]]. Notably, AI-assisted surgical video analytics—an emerging surgical application—demonstrates high accuracy in instrument recognition, anatomical structure identification, and operative phase recognition [[91], [92], [93], [94]], while revealing its significant potential for thoracic surgical procedure analysis. This technology shows promise in thoracic surgery domains including surgical education, procedural quality assessment, intraoperative assistance, and postoperative analysis [90]. A study demonstrates that deep learning models exhibit promising potential in laparoscopic surgical video analysis [95]. Clinicians may advance AI in thoracic surgery ‌through‌ standardized visual databases and reporting.

Assists classification of lung cancer

AI-based image recognition technology can analyze diverse medical images‌, including X-rays, CT scans, MRI images, and pathological sections [96] (Fig. 3). Histopathological biopsy‌ remains the ‌gold standard‌ for ‌distinguishing benign from malignant tumors‌. A single pathological section typically contains millions of cells, yet pathologists are required to examine numerous slides daily. This high workload inevitably increases the risk of ‌interpretation errors due to fatigue‌. The ascendancy of AI technology has heralded a new era in imaging and pathology interpretation, where AI has achieved a level of proficiency comparable to medical practitioners in terms of both speed and accuracy.

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) represent the most prevalent subtypes of lung cancer, necessitating visual examination by an experienced pathologist to differentiate between them. Coudray et al. [97] employed a CNN to classify lung cancer subtypes (LUAD/LUSC) and normal tissue, achieving pathologist-level performance‌. ‌The AI model could also predict‌ gene mutations directly from histopathology images, ‌suggesting its utility in detecting‌ lung cancer subtypes and associated genetic alterations. Yu et al. [98] developed a machine learning algorithm that extracted 9879 quantitative features from 2186 H&E-stained whole-slide lung cancer images. To assess the biological relevance of the image features, they employed seven classifiers: naive Bayes, support vector machines (SVM) with Gaussian kernel, SVM with linear kernel, SVM with polynomial kernel, bagging for classification trees, random forest utilizing conditional inference trees and Breiman’s random forest. This model significantly stratified survival outcomes in Stage I LUAD (p < 0.003) and LUSC (p = 0.023) patients, demonstrating its prognostic predictive value and supporting precision oncology in lung cancer management. In addition, AI has the capability to automatically quantify radiographic features related to tumor phenotype, and this information holds considerable prognostic value for lung cancer [99].

AI-assisted classification of lung cancer demonstrates transformative capabilities in enhancing diagnostic accuracy and efficiency. By leveraging deep learning algorithms to analyze histopathological images and radiological data, it enables precise tumor subtyping and grading.

Assistant for patient health management

The virtual assistant for patient health management represents a significant application area of AI (Fig. 3). First, generative AI, such as GPT-4, plays a pivotal role in aiding patients to manage their medication therapy effectively. The information it provides concerning medication reminders, dosage instructions, potential side effects, and drug interactions is crucial. Second, AI fosters more effective disease management and optimizes treatment outcomes by analyzing patient data and offering personalized treatment recommendations. Furthermore, it plays a role in elevating patients' health awareness. Considering the scarcity of health education resources, numerous patients turn to search engines for medical advice, which may expose them to inappropriate content and result in misleading self-diagnoses. Third, AI can function as an invaluable educational resource by offering clear and readily accessible information that aids patients in understanding their condition and addressing their questions, thereby empowering them to actively engage in the treatment process [[100], [101]].

Kamminga et al. [102] utilized AI models to address patients' inquiries about melanoma, the quality of the responses generated by these AI models was benchmarked against established patient information resources. The results showed that ChatGPT exhibited the highest level of accuracy, whereas Gemini surpassed other models in terms of completeness, personalization, and readability. Chen et al. [103] evaluated‌ the capability of AI chatbots (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Claude AI) to generate cancer-specific informational responses. Results showed that these AI systems can produce high-quality, empathetic, and readable responses to patient inquiries. AI-driven health management is transforming thoracic surgical care through optimized clinical outcomes enabled by real-time physiological monitoring, personalized therapeutic recommendations, and predictive risk analytics. These intelligent systems integrate multimodal data from wearables, EHRs, and genomic profiles to deliver precision medicine interventions.

Challenges of AI

AI exhibits promising outcomes in various medical fields and holds the potential to substantially transform daily practices. However, it also poses considerable risks, such as inappropriate or inaccurate risk assessments, flawed treatment recommendations, diagnostic mistakes, and breaches of privacy [104]. The accuracy and representativeness of the data used to train LLMs are particularly crucial, as biased or incomplete datasets may result in unreliable predictive models. Consequently, it is imperative that we critically assess the various proposed applications of AI. The original software should undergo thorough evaluation, preferably through randomized controlled trials, to emphasize its advantages. Challenges of AI.

Data Security and Privacy. All existing AI algorithms are fundamentally data-driven, requiring vast amounts of data for training. Ensuring patient data security‌ represents an important challenge for AI advancement.‌ The advancement and implementation of AI present serious risks to patient privacy, particularly due to the potential exposure of sensitive patient data in training datasets [105]. For example, if an AI model is attacked or misused, a malicious user could easily extract sensitive data. Unauthorized access and data breaches may lead to the misuse of patient information, resulting in severe consequences for both patients and healthcare institutions. Consequently,‌ robust data governance frameworks and advanced encryption technologies must serve as foundational safeguards to balance AI innovation with protection of patient privacy.

Bias and Discrimination. The quality of AI responses primarily depends on the quality and quantity of the available data [18]. Algorithmic bias primarily originates from biased data. For example, in VATS, AI systems ‌may overlook rare pulmonary segment variations‌ due to insufficient training data on anatomical diversity. Statistical bias, on the other hand, may emanate from suboptimal sampling methods, errors in measuring predictor variables, as well as the heterogeneity of effects [106]. For instance, the epidemiological features of lung cancers in Caucasians within the European Union differ significantly from those observed in individuals residing in Asia. In Asia, a higher percentage of lung cancers cases are attributed to factors aside from smoking, especially among women [107]. Underrepresentation of specific populations or genders thus ‌introduces statistical bias‌ that compromises prediction outcomes.

AI models possess the capacity to reinforce existing biases and adversely affect marginalized communities. Such biases may be introduced during the training phase, particularly when datasets fail to sufficiently represent specific patient demographics or include detrimental correlations [[108], [109]]. In a retrospective study, Zhou et al. [110]. evaluated the alignment between International Business Machines Corporation (IBM)'s system-generated treatment recommendations and oncologists' clinical decisions. The results showed that a concordance rate of slightly over 80 % for lung cancer treatment recommendations, but only 12 % for gastric cancer. These discrepancies were not due to AI accuracy or physician competence; rather, they arose from differences in medication approval and marketing between China and the US, leading to varied cancer treatment protocols [110]. The ability of AI algorithms to generalize across different subgroups is fundamentally influenced by several factors, including the representativeness of the populations studied, the presence of missing data, and the impact of outliers. It is essential to consistently update and supply algorithms with new patient data to facilitate adaptive decision-making. This highlights the necessity of transparency to maximize the accuracy of the technology and mitigate potential biases [111].

Validation and Interpretability. AI will present unique challenges in validation due to its unprecedented versatility. Currently, AI systems are designed for specific tasks, which means they only need validation for those predefined use cases. However, in the future, AI may undertake entirely new tasks defined by end users for the first time, making it significantly more difficult to anticipate all potential failure modes. Another concern regarding AI models is the opacity of their decision-making processes. Evaluating the generalization behavior of AI algorithms in medical imaging poses a significant challenge due to this lack of transparency. Many AI algorithms, particularly deep learning models, are often characterized as "black boxes" in clinical applications. Furthermore, most commercially available AI products lack scientific, peer-reviewed evidence of their efficacy [112].

While these comprehensive AI solutions may facilitate the implementation and utilization of technology for medical professionals, concerns regarding validation and transparency persist. Consequently, rigorous validation of these AI models is essential to guarantee that they do not perform poorly with specific demographics, including minority groups. Within surgical contexts, surgeons need to understand the decision-making process of AI systems to trust and effectively use them in clinical practice [113].

Ethical and Legal. The ethical and legal implications of utilizing AI in thoracic surgery must be taken into account. Decisions that are informed by AI analysis should be made in conjunction with medical professionals and in alignment with established clinical best practices and guidelines. In 2018, IBM’s Watson encountered criticism for recommending treatments that were incorrect in some cases, potentially endangering patients' lives. For instance, the system suggested bevacizumab for a patient with a diagnosis of lung squamous cell carcinoma, despite this treatment being explicitly contraindicated [114]. The risk of legal implications arises when a patient suffers harm due to decisions based on AI analysis, leading to investigations into liability. As a result, it is essential for researchers and clinicians to possess adequate insurance coverage, secure approval from institutional review boards focused on ethics, and adhere to established procedures for addressing such issues.

Technical Obstacles. First of all, the integration of AI systems into existing healthcare information systems (HIS) and EHRs poses significant technical challenges, particularly in ensuring interoperability with heterogeneous medical technologies. Key obstacles encompass data schema harmonization, cross-platform interface compatibility, and multimodal device data pipeline integration. Second, given the diversity of surgical approaches for lung cancer resection, where ‌surgical access, procedural steps, techniques, and operative duration‌ may vary significantly among thoracic surgeons, developing an algorithm capable of ‌comprehensively interpreting the entire surgical workflow‌ presents a formidable challenge. Compared to autonomous driving‌, surgical visual recognition faces ‌unique complexities‌ due to intraoperative incidents like bleeding or smoke interference, often leading to AI misidentification [115]. Last but not least, during surgery, Can AI ‌make real-time clinical decisions‌ during emergencies (e.g., vessel rupture)? Can it ‌balance oncological precision‌ between tumor clearance and tissue preservation? These represent critical technical hurdles that AI must overcome in future surgical applications.

Training and Education: Successful implementation of AI in healthcare requires adequate training and expertise among medical professionals. A lack of sufficient education and understanding of AI technologies can hinder their effective adoption. Consequently,‌ seamless AI integration into thoracic surgery demands ‌multifaceted breakthroughs‌ across technical and clinical ‌spheres‌.

Conclusions

AI is currently viewed as the potential primary driving force behind the fourth industrial revolution, following steam engines in the 1760s, electricity and petroleum revolution in the 1870s, and computers in the 1970s [[116], [117]]. The potential of AI to revolutionize screening, diagnosis, and healthcare management is immense, especially given its capability to analyze unstructured data within big datasets, learn from errors, and generate pertinent insights that serve as valuable aids in decision-making processes.

To date, studies have demonstrated the feasibility of autonomous robotic surgery in live porcine models, representing a major advance in the application of AI to surgical procedures [[87], [88]]. In April 2023, a research team from Stanford University introduced a novel concept of medical AI, termed generalist medical AI (GMAI) [118]. GMAI can be applied in various medical scenarios and address a wide range of complex medical data and problems. It has the potential to impact medical practice through enhancing healthcare and alleviating clinician burnout [118]. Furthermore, the AI ecosystem is undergoing significant transformations with the emergence of AI models such as Deepseek and ChatGPT [119]. AI has the potential to address unique challenges in the field of global health and accelerate the achievement of sustainable development goals related to health [120].

In this review, we summarize the latest advances in the application of AI in thoracic surgery clinical pathways. Despite the increasing deployment of AI models in clinical practice, numerous challenges and limitations persist, and there is still a long way to go to achieve fully autonomous robotic surgery.
