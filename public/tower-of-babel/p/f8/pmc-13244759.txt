Use of balloon-expandable valves for the surgical treatment of mitral valve disease in the setting of severe mitral annular calcification
Authors: Cody W. Dorton, John Eisenga, Jennie Hocking, Austin Kluis, Kyle McCullough, Taylor Pickering, Tanushree Prasad, Lee Hafen, Amro Alsaid, Timothy George, Kelley Hutcheson, Justin Schaffer, Katherine Harrington, J. Michael DiMaio, Robert Smith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe treatment of mitral valve disease complicated by severe mitral annular calcification poses a significant challenge, with increased risk of morbidity and mortality. We describe the outcomes after surgical mitral valve replacement in patients with severe mitral annular calcification using a balloon-expandable valve.MethodsFrom 2015 to 2023, 82 consecutive patients received mitral annular calcification using a balloon-expandable valve. No patients were excluded from analysis. Patient records were retrospectively reviewed for baseline characteristics, echocardiography results, intraoperative details, postoperative events, and mortality.ResultsThe cohort was predominantly female (64.6%; 53/82) with a median age of 76 years [interquartile range, 71-80]. Comorbidities including hypertension (92.7%), New York Heart Association class III/IV (59.8%), atrial fibrillation (57.3%), and diabetes (51.2%) were common, contributing to a median Society of Thoracic Surgeons predicted risk of mortality of 5.0% [3.4-8.1]. Sixty-eight patients had moderate or greater mitral stenosis, and 42 patients had moderate or greater regurgitation. The median preoperative mean mitral valve gradient was 8.0 [6.0-11.0] mm Hg, which improved to 5.0 [4.0-6.0] mm Hg postoperatively. Paravalvular leak occurred in 3.7% of patients, with trace/trivial in 7.3%. Severe paravalvular leak requiring intervention occurred in 2 patients (2.4%). There were no instances of valve embolization. The incidence of stroke at 30 days was 4.8%. Thirty-day and 1-year mortality were 13.4% and 23.2%, respectively.ConclusionsSurgical implantation of balloon-expandable valve in the setting mitral annular calcification with low rates of significant paravalvular leak and no left ventricular outflow tract obstruction or valve embolization. This technique represents progress in addressing a challenging surgical condition, although further experience and long-term follow-up are needed.

Central Message

In the setting of severe MAC, preventing traditional MV intervention, BEV-in-MAC is an effective approach for treatment of MVD in our experience.

Perspective

Patients with MVD and severe MAC may be deemed inoperable for traditional surgical treatment. In these patients, we describe the successful treatment using placement of a BEV with good operative outcomes in this inherently very high-risk cohort.

Mitral annular calcification (MAC) is a progressive, degenerative process that has been associated with a number of concomitant cardiovascular and noncardiovascular comorbidities.1, 2, 3, 4 Although rheumatic disease and endocarditis may precipitate MAC, it is most commonly the result of an insidious degenerative process.5 The presence of MAC can be a marker of further cardiovascular disease, such as arrhythmias, stroke, concomitant mitral valve disease (MVD), and increased mortality.6, 7, 8 Its alteration of cardiac anatomy further complicates surgical procedures, often influencing both technical complexity and outcomes.5,9, 10, 11, 12

Because of the disease process and associated medical comorbidities, patients with MAC and concomitant MVD are a high-risk surgical population.5,13 Traditional approaches to MVD repair in the setting of MAC require excision of calcifications with subsequent annuloplasty before valve replacement or repair. However, this approach is hindered by significant risks including weakening of the annulus, left ventricular rupture, atrioventricular disruption, and circumflex artery injury.14,15 Despite these risks, surgical repair or replacement remains the gold standard for treatment of MVD in the setting of MAC, with multiple techniques being adapted to mitigate these risks.

Mitral valve replacement (MVR) using a balloon-expandable valve (BEV), without excision of calcifications, has been described through various approaches.16, 17, 18

Transcatheter BEV implantation has been described but is associated with left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction and paravalvular leak (PVL), particularly in the setting of a calcified annulus. LVOT obstruction is associated with high mortality after mitral surgery. Likewise, significant PVL after MVR often progresses, requiring further intervention.11,19 In contrast, a surgical transatrial approach provides excellent visualization and access, allowing surgeons to address complex anatomy and mitigate these complications. Indeed, data suggest high technical success and favorable mortality rates with this approach.15,20, 21, 22, 23 However, most reports are limited by small sample sizes and focus primarily on early outcomes. In this study, we present our single-center series of BEV implantation for MVD in the setting of severe MAC using a transatrial approach.

Material and Methods

Patient Selection and Study Design

This single-center, retrospective study reviewed consecutive patients with severe MAC who underwent surgical implantation of a BEV via a transatrial approach with direct or thoracoscopic vision (BEV-in-MAC) between 2015 and 2023. Patients possessed severe MAC associated with moderate or greater mitral stenosis and mitral regurgitation (MR) and underwent BEV-in-MAC along with any indicated concomitant procedures. All patients were evaluated by a multidisciplinary heart team, including specialized imaging cardiologists, interventional cardiologist, and cardiothoracic surgeons specializing in valvular heart disease and were deemed to be unable to undergo traditional mitral valve (MV) surgery due to severe MAC based on preoperative imaging criteria24 (Figures 1 and E1). The multidisciplinary heart team determined BEV-in-MAC to be the only available option for treatment.Figure 1Three-dimensional CT images showing severity of MAC in patients before undergoing BEV-in-MAC.

Three-dimensional CT images showing severity of MAC in patients before undergoing BEV-in-MAC.

A retrospective review of patient records was performed to collect baseline patient characteristics, comorbidities, imaging data, and specific procedural details. Preoperative imaging was interpreted by specialized imaging cardiologists, including cardiac computed tomography and echocardiography, and was used to determine the risk of postoperative LVOT obstruction. Standard of care postoperative echocardiography was performed and interpreted by a board-certified cardiologist.

Institutional Review Board Approval

This study was approved by our internal Institutional Review Board (#014-209; 4-27-2023). Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of this study. All patients provided informed consent for the off-label use of the BEV before undergoing surgery.

Surgical Technique

The procedure was performed via a minimally invasive thoracotomy, robotically assisted approach, or median sternotomy. A minimally invasive or robotically assisted approach was preferred if feasible, whereas a median sternotomy was reserved for patients requiring concomitant procedures not amenable to a minimally invasive approach. Detailed descriptions of the cardiopulmonary bypass protocol, myocardial protection approach, and surgical technique have been published.22,25

In brief, the MV is exposed via left atriotomy and the noncommissural portions of the anterior leaflet are resected, along with the associated subvalvular structures. For patients who displayed a neo-LVOT less than 170 mm2 and a diastolic septal thickness more than 13 mm, septal myectomy was conducted. A felt ring is then sewn in place around the circumference of the mitral annulus using 6 braided polyester sutures, placed in a horizontal-mattress fashion, and from the ventricular to atrial aspect of the annulus. These sutures may be passed through weak areas of the calcium bar or through the remaining leaflet tissue near the edge of the mitral annulus. This serves to encapsulate the MAC, provide a secure surface for the valve, and prevent PVL (Figure 2). The annulus is then sized using a balloon. In certain cases, the bulk or distribution of calcium presents an irregular surface, and another strip of felt may be used to allow for a smoother surface and improve the quality of seal between the valve and the annulus and prevent PVL. If another strip of felt is needed, it is placed directly on the uncrimped valve before deployment. The valve is then delivered and deployed in an atrial-biased direction under direct visualization. The commissural tags are rotated laterally from the mitral trigones before deployment, which further helps prevent LVOT obstruction. The valve is then checked with saline to ensure there are no leaks and may be reexpanded as needed. The valve is then secured using the previously placed sutures (Figure 3). Valve replacement involved use of either a Sapien XT or a Sapien 3 BEV transcatheter valve (Edwards Lifesciences) in an off-label fashion.Figure 2Felt strip in place before balloon dilation of the annulus. In patients at risk for LVOT obstruction, a septal myectomy was completed after resection of the anterior leaflet. Dissection began 8 mm below the nadir of the right coronary cusp and was carried apically beyond the area of narrowing, which was determined based on preoperative imaging. Of note, this dissection was carried out with the intent of preventing LVOT obstruction after implantation of the valve prosthesis, rather than with the intent of treating a specific pathology. Therefore, resection was intended to remove as little tissue as necessary to prevent potential obstruction.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was 30-day survival. Secondary outcomes included operative mortality, 1-year mortality, valve-related reinterventions, and echocardiographic data. Additional outcomes included intraoperative and postoperative complications, including PVL, stroke, bleeding, and LVOT obstruction. Standard-of-care echocardiography was performed postoperatively before discharge from the hospital and reviewed by board-certified cardiologists, who evaluated the mean mitral valve gradient (MVG), degree of MR, and presence of PVL.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are presented as counts with percentages. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and presented as mean ± SD or median [interquartile range] as appropriate. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to determine survival during the study period. Fisher exact test was used for comparison of categorical variables. Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired nonparametric data was used for data comparison.

Echocardiographic Data

All patients underwent pre- and postoperative echocardiography. Median preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction was 60% [55-65]. Mitral stenosis severity was classified as none (6 patients), mild (9), moderate (16), and severe (52). MR severity was none (14), trace/trivial (3), mild (24), moderate (20), and severe (22). The median preoperative MVG was 8.0 [6.0-11.0] mm Hg.

Postoperatively, all 82 patients completed formal echocardiography. No MR was noted in 69 patients (84.1%), trace/trivial MR in 11 patients (13.4%), and mild MR in 2 patients (2.4%). Trace PVL was noted in 6 patients (7.3%), and mild PVL was noted in 3 patients (3.7%). Trace central MR was noted in 6 patients (7.3%), and mild central MR was noted in 1 patient (1.2%). The median postoperative MVG of 5.0 [4.0-6.0] mm Hg represented a significant improvement from baseline (P < .001). No instances of severe MR or significant PVL were observed during the initial postoperative evaluation (Table E1).

A 29-mm valve was used in 85.4% of patients (70/82), and a 26-mm valve was used in the remaining 14.6% of patients (12/82). The Sapien 3 valve was used in 92.7% of patients (76/82), and the Sapien XT valve was used in 7.3% of patients (6/82) (Figures 3 and 4). Robotically Assisted Approach

A robotically assisted approach was used in 34.9% of patients (29/83). The median age among this group was 78.0 [73.0-81.0] years. Female patients comprised 62% of patients (18/29). Median BMI was 30.4 [27.3-33.7] kg/m2, and the median STS-PROM was 5.6% [3.7-7.7]. Concomitant procedures were performed in 13 patients (44.8%). Conversion to open thoracotomy was required in 2 patients (8.3%) due to adhesions. At 30 days, mortality was 10.3% (3/29) and the rate of documented stroke was 6.9% (2/29). One-year mortality was 20.7% (6/29). Seven patients (24.1%) required new permanent pacemaker implantation (Table E2).

Discussion

MVD in the setting of MAC poses a significant surgical challenge due to the anatomic complexity and high-risk nature of this patient population. As such, surgeons are presented with a difficult choice regarding treatment options. MAC is present in approximately one-quarter of patients with MVD and has been associated with a high rate of concomitant diseases.4,6,8,11,12,26 As a result, these patients are at high risk for operative intervention. The presence of MAC has been demonstrated to place patients at an increased risk of mortality and surgical complications.5,9,10,12 Despite these challenges, intervention is necessary, because untreated MVD is associated with high rates of all-cause mortality, highlighting the need for safe and effective approaches to manage MVD in the setting of MAC.27,28

Traditional approaches to MV replacement in severe MAC often require resection of the calcium bar with repair of the annulus.11 However, these approaches come with a high risk of catastrophic events, including atrioventricular disruption, circumflex artery disruption, and atrial or ventricular rupture.11,14 Transcatheter MV replacement has emerged as an alternative, but it is limited by complications such as LVOT obstruction and PVL.20 Surgical BEV implantation using a transatrial approach offers a middle ground, combining the benefits of direct surgical visualization with the ability to decrease the incidence of complications related to extensive annular decalcification.15,20, 21, 22, 23,29

Our study, to our knowledge, is the largest single-center retrospective analysis of BEV-in-MAC to date and demonstrates the utility of this technique. Operative mortality was only 1.2%, and 30-day mortality was higher at 13.4% in this inherently high-risk population. There were no instances of LVOT obstruction or valve embolization, which are major concerns in transcatheter MV replacement procedures, and have been reported as high as 9% and 6%, respectively.20 Furthermore, no incidents of atrioventricular disruption were noted, and significant reductions in MVG were observed, with a median postoperative MVG of 5.0 mm Hg compared with a preoperative median of 8.0 mm Hg (P < .001). Residual MR was minimal, with 84.1% of patients demonstrating no MR and only trace or mild PVL observed in 11.0%.

These findings are consistent with and build on prior studies evaluating the transatrial approach. Previous series combining transatrial, transseptal, and transapical approaches have reported LVOT obstruction rates approaching 10%, with high associated mortality.15,21,23,29 High rates of valve embolization have been demonstrated.20 The absence of LVOT obstruction and valve embolization in our cohort highlights the advantages of the transatrial approach, which allows for precise placement of the valve under direct visualization and use of adjunctive procedures, such as septal myomectomy, when necessary.

Consistent with previous reports, our series also demonstrates significant improvements in MVG after BEV-in-MAC.15,20,29 Further, our series demonstrates the resolution of significant MR. These echocardiographic data combined with the procedural data further demonstrate that the transatrial approach provides effective treatment for MVD in the setting of MAC.

Our analysis also suggests that the transatrial BEV-in-MAC approach offers favorable mortality outcomes compared with other strategies. Thirty-day mortality in our study was 13.4%, comparable to prior series using the transatrial approach and lower than rates reported in mixed-approach cohorts.20,21,23 This reflects the procedural safety and technical success of this approach in an inherently high-risk cohort. It should be noted, however, our cohort's median STS-PROM score of 5.0% is lower than that of some prior studies, potentially contributing to improved outcomes.

Limitations

This study is limited by its retrospective design, making it subject to the inherent biases and potential confounding factors associated with such analyses. Although it represents the largest single-center experience of BEV-in-MAC to date, the overall sample size remains relatively small. Additionally, the absence of a proper control group prevents comparison to alternative techniques. Procedures were performed at a specialty cardiac hospital by surgeons with extensive experience in MV surgery, minimally invasive cardiac surgery, and transcatheter interventions, potentially limiting the generalizability of these findings to other institutions. Despite these limitations, we believe our findings provide compelling evidence in support of the transatrial approach as a viable and effective strategy for managing MVD in the setting of severe MAC. Further multicenter studies and randomized trials are warranted to validate our results, refine patient selection criteria, and establish the long-term safety and efficacy of this technique.

Conclusions

Transatrial BEV-in-MAC addresses the unique challenges of MVD in patients with severe MAC. Our findings highlight the safety, technical feasibility, and efficacy of this approach. As surgical techniques continue to evolve, the transatrial approach to BEV-in-MAC represents a significant advancement in the management of this inherently high-risk patient population.
