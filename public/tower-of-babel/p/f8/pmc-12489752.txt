Acute-onset endophthalmitis caused by Staphylococcus warneri
Authors: Michael Y Zhao, Justin A Chen, Lauren C Kiryakoza, Landon J Rohowetz, Darlene Miller, Harry W Flynn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report a series of patients with acute-onset bacterial endophthalmitis caused by culture proven Staphylococcus warneri, and to characterize clinical outcomes and microbiologic susceptibilities of this organism.ObservationsThis study included 8 eyes of 8 patients. The etiologies included intravitreal injection (4), anterior segment surgery (3), and glaucoma surgery (1). All isolates (100 %) of S. warneri demonstrated sensitivity to vancomycin with minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) values ranging from <0.5 to 1, and to gentamycin with MIC values ranging from <0.5 to 4 mcg/ml. All isolates (100 %) were also sensitive to a beta-lactam with MIC values reported as ≤ 0.25 mcg/ml and to fluroquinolones with MIC values ranging from 0.12 to 0.5 mcg/ml. After initial treatment with intravitreal antibiotics, 6 of 7 (85.7 %) patients subsequently underwent pars plana vitrectomy. Best-corrected visual acuity at last follow-up was ≤5/200 in 5/7 (71.4 %) of patients, and was ≥20/150 in 2/7 (28.6 %) of patients.Conclusion and importanceAcute-onset endophthalmitis due to Staphylococcus warneri is associated with a range of intraocular procedures. After initial treatment with intravitreal antibiotics, additional treatments are often necessary. Vancomycin, amikacin, and ceftazidime remain consistent options, providing coverage against S. warneri in acute-onset endophthalmitis.Keywords: Acute-onset endophthalmitis, Staphylococcus warneri, Antibiotic susceptibilities, Patient outcomes

Introduction

Acute-onset endophthalmitis is a rare, but potentially devastating infection that can arise following intraocular procedures. Multiple pathogens can be the cause of endophthalmitis; however, coagulase-negative Staphylococci are the most common source of postoperative endophthalmitis after intraocular procedures.1,2 Coagulase-negative Staphylococci are responsible for approximately 70 % of patients with postoperative endophthalmitis, and of these isolates, Staphylococcus epidermidis accounts for ∼76.8 %.1,3 Other species, such as Staphylococcus warneri, have been reported in smaller proportions of patients.3 Specifically, Recchia et al. identified S. warneri in ∼4 % of endophthalmitis isolates caused by coagulase-negative Staphylococci.4 While S. warneri is part of the spectrum of endophthalmitis, there is a paucity of literature that focuses on treatment outcomes for acute-onset endophthalmitis due to S. warneri. This case series aims to describe the clinical presentation, antibiotic susceptibility patterns, and treatment outcomes.

Results

A review was performed of all patients with a diagnosis of acute-onset endophthalmitis at the University of Miami, Bascom Palmer Eye Institute Microbiology Department from January 2015 through February 2025. Patients were included in this study if vitreous and/or aqueous samples taken at the time of diagnosis identified isolates of S. warneri. In total, 8 patients (3 men, 5 women; mean age 67.1 years, median age 72.5 years) presented with acute-onset endophthalmitis with culture proven S. warneri.

These patients presented after intravitreal injection or anterior segment surgery and in general demonstrated pain, loss of vision, conjunctival injection, hypopyon, dense cell and/or fibrin in the anterior chamber, and marked vitreous opacities (Fig. 1). Of the 8 patients who presented, 1 patient did not follow up after initial evaluation. Clinical data of additional treatments and visual outcomes are described in Table 2. Best corrected visual acuities (BCVAs) were diverse at last follow-up. 71.4 % (5/7) of patients were ≤5/200 while patient 3 was 20/40 and patient 4 was 20/150. The visual potential was limited by postoperative retinal detachment in patients 1 and 2, chronic angle closure glaucoma and high myopia in patient 5, and end-stage primary open angle glaucoma in patient 8. At presentation, the mean BCVA was 2.0 logMAR (∼20/2000; SD 0.8), and the median was 2.3 logMAR (hand motion; IQR 1.2–2.7). At last follow-up, the mean BCVA was 1.8 logMAR (∼20/1260; SD 0.8), and the median was 2.3 logMAR (hand motion; IQR 0.9–2.3). Conversion between logMAR and Snellen visual acuity values were performed with the standardized conversion table from the American Academy of Ophthalmology Intelligent Research in Sight registry.5

S. warneri was isolated in microbiologic cultures in all 8 patients with antibiotic susceptibilities and minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) in mcg/ml described in Table 3. Of the 8 patients, 100 % of isolates were sensitive to vancomycin with MIC values ranging from <0.5 mcg/ml to 1 mcg/ml, and to gentamicin with MIC values ranging from <0.5 mcg/ml to 4 mcg/ml. All isolates were sensitive to oxacillin with MIC values reported as ≤ 0.25 mcg/ml. Susceptibility testing was not consistently performed on all 3 fluoroquinolones; however, all isolates were sensitive to at least 1 fluroquinolone. For susceptibilities to ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, and moxifloxacin, available MIC values in mcg/ml were ≤0.5, <0.25, <0.25, respectively.

Discussion

Staphylococcus warneri is part of the normal flora of the skin and mucosae. It is considered a low-virulence organism, but can become an opportunistic pathogen capable of infections ranging from skin and soft tissue infections to septic arthritis, meningitis, sepsis, and endocarditis.6 However, acute-onset endophthalmitis due to S. warneri remains understudied, as S. warneri is oftentimes grouped with S. epidermidis or other coagulase-negative Staphylococci.3

In this review, the presenting visual acuity (typically 20/1600 to hand motion or worse) and exam findings of S. warneri endophthalmitis (e.g., hypopyon) were similar to those of endophthalmitis from other coagulase-negative staphylococci.2,7 Initial treatment with intravitreal antibiotics was performed in 7/8 (87.5 %) patients with S. warneri endophthalmitis, a rate comparable to other coagulase-negative staphylococci.2 However, among patients with S. warneri endophthalmitis who followed-up, 6/7 (85.7 %) eventually underwent PPV, compared to 45 % of patients with endophthalmitis from other coagulase-negative staphylococci.7 Visual outcomes at last follow-up were generally poor. The BCVA for patient 4 was 20/150 after successful medical management of endophthalmitis and CEIOL. The BCVA for patient 3 was 20/40 after initial medical management and additional PPV with intravitreal antibiotics; patient 3 required another PPV for an epiretinal membrane that developed. However, there were several patients with poor visual outcomes after surgical treatment with PPV. These less favorable outcomes are in contrast to the trend that endophthalmitis from coagulase-negative Staphylococci generally has good visual outcomes (i.e., 20/100 or better) in approximately 80 % of patients.1 These poor visual outcomes may partially be explained by pre-existing ocular disease including advanced glaucoma as well as postoperative complications such as retinal detachments limiting the visual potential of the eye.

In a retrospective study, Zhang et al. reported that in endophthalmitis without concurrent retinal detachment there was no significant difference in BCVA at last follow-up between eyes initially treated with PPV and intravitreal antibiotics versus eyes treated with intravitreal antibiotics alone.8 However, Zhang et al. also reported that eyes receiving antibiotics alone as initial treatment more frequently underwent additional treatments, including PPV.8 In a retrospective study on postprocedural endophthalmitis, Soliman et al. reported no significant difference in BCVA at last follow-up in eyes treated with intravitreal antibiotics alone versus eyes treated with PPV within 1 week of presentation.9 The results in this review suggest initial management with intravitreal antibiotics alone may be associated with a need for additional treatments; however, it is important to note no patient was initially managed with PPV.

There are little data on the susceptibility patterns for S. warneri isolates in endophthalmitis specifically. In a review on antibiotic resistance in S. warneri, Ravaioli et al. reported multiple coagulase-negative Staphylococci species from oral specimens, with S. warneri as the most prevalent, are generally susceptible to vancomycin while resistance to beta-lactams, macrolides, aminoglycosides, and tetracyclines has been reported in varying degrees.6 Lower levels of resistance to clindamycin and fluroquinolones were also reported in multiple coagulase-negative Staphylococci species, including S. warneri.6 However, it is important to emphasize that none of the S. warneri isolates mentioned by Ravaioli et al. were collected from eyes with endophthalmitis; in fact, none were collected from the eye itself. Location of isolates ranged from oral specimens and urinary tract infections to veterinary and food samples. In a microbiological study of a neonatal intensive care unit, Center et al. reported isolates of S. warneri from mucosal and soft tissue samples had reduced susceptibility to vancomycin in vitro, but reported a lack of clinical evidence to support reduced susceptibility in vivo.10 As evident in this series, S. warneri were susceptible to vancomycin, aminoglycosides, beta-lactams, and fluroquinolones, but tend to parallel their general susceptibility patterns for macrolides, tetracycline, and clindamycin. While antibiotic resistance is emerging in certain Staphylococcus species in endophthalmitis, vancomycin, aminoglycosides (e.g., amikacin), and beta-lactams (e.g., ceftazidime) provide consistent coverage with no known resistance for S. warneri in endophthalmitis and should remain first line agents in treating acute-onset endophthalmitis caused by coagulase-negative Staphylococci.
