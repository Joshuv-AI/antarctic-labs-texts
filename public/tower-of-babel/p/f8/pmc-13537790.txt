Millet Consumption Patterns, Perceptions, and Barriers among Non-Communicable Disease Patients in an Urban Resettlement Colony of Delhi: A Mixed-Methods Study
Authors: Gurmeet Kaur, Garima Singh, Shubham Kansal, Anita S Acharya, Jyoti Khandekar, Radhika Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The year 2023 was celebrated as the International Year of Millet, which aims to promote the production and consumption of millet. However, little is known about the beneficial effects of millet on chronic conditions. This study aimed to assess patients’ perceptions of millet consumption among those with chronic conditions.Methodology:A mixed-method study was conducted among patients attending a noncommunicable disease clinic at the Urban Health Centre, Kalyanpuri, aged 30 years and above. A total of 369 participants were included. These participants were also asked for in-depth details for qualitative analysis. A pan-India consumer survey was applied to participants to assess their millet consumption.Results:Almost three-fourths of the participants (271/374, 73.4%) recognized any form of millet shown. Half of the participants (47.9%) consume some form of millet every couple of years, while only 15.4% consume it at least once a week. Based on in-depth interviews, five themes were identified: consumption issues, the effects of consuming millets, health-related problems, limited knowledge, and political will. Barriers to millet consumption included procurement and storage issues, high cost, unpalatability, seasonal preference, limited knowledge of millets, and post-consumption digestive issues. Facilitators identified were benefits for patients with diabetes, obesity, high energy content, and assistance in weight loss.Conclusion:Awareness of millet consumption was high, but actual intake was low. Challenges such as procurement, health concerns, lack of knowledge, and political challenges are interconnected barriers. Addressing these through targeted educational initiatives, supportive policies, and better market access could significantly boost millet’s role as a staple food in diets.

INTRODUCTION

There is growing interest in reviving millets in India and globally owing to their nutritional content and ability to grow in harsh climatic conditions due to their climate-smart traits.[1] India is the leading producer and consumer of different millets, such as finger millet, pearl millet, kodo millet, foxtail millet, barnyard millet, proso millet, and little millet.[2] However, their presence in the Indian food basket has been declining over the years, largely due to government policies favoring the production and consumption of fine cereals, such as rice and wheat, as well as rising incomes and urbanization.[1]

The Government of India declared 2018 as a National Year of Millets and followed up with preparing a National Millet Mission and a proposal to the Food and Agriculture Organisation of the United Nations (UN) for a UN International Year of Millets. Several states in India also followed suit to establish state millet missions. These initiatives recognized this need and built in components to engage with consumers to drive demand, and not only to invest in agricultural production and productivity. This transition phase, during which perceptions of millets are changing, along with greater health consciousness, is the right stage to assess current knowledge, perceptions, and practices related to millets, which will lay the foundation for a plan to promote millets as a staple effectively.[3]

Many recent studies show that millets help manage and reduce the risk of developing type 2 diabetes[4] and can reduce cholesterol, triglyceride, lipoproteins, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, and the risk of developing cardiovascular disease.[5] Their richness in iron also helps in improving hemoglobin levels and the iron status of the body.[6] In particular, finger millet is rich in calcium and zinc, which are essential for growth and immunity.[7] These nutritional and therapeutic effects of millets are vital and provide alternative ways to solve some of the global public health issues.

India has seen a steady rise in diabetes mellitus. While the prevalence of diabetes has risen from 7.1% in 2009 to 11.4% in 2023,[8] India also shows a 15.3% prevalence of prediabetes and nearly 57% of undiagnosed diabetes.[89] An average 20-year-old Indian man has a 55.5% lifetime risk of developing diabetes, while a woman has a 64.6% risk.[10] India has a high burden (76.6%) of poor glycemic control.[11] Management of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) consists of adequate self-care, effective pharmacotherapy, and regular follow-up for early detection and management of complications. Diet practices are an important part of self-care activities. Knowledge about the use and benefits of millet is also limited among people with diabetes and hypertension, as per limited studies.[12]

The shifting eating patterns are influenced by multiple variables (marketing systems, policy frameworks, social networks) within these overarching drivers (health-related issues).[131415] Measuring these intricate, multidimensional structures is necessary to close this information gap. Addressing this gap in the literature is critical because knowing what drives consumers to shift to millet can be key to increasing their uptake.

A study conducted across several cities in India showed that Delhi had the second lowest millet consumption, with 47.1% of respondents never or rarely consuming millet.[16] Despite the efforts spent on spreading awareness and increasing access to millet, a section of the urban educated populace has not yet made the shift. Kalyanpuri is an urban resettlement colony in East Delhi with a population of about 86,599. The area primarily features residential complexes catering to different income levels, including LIG (Lower Income Group), MIG (Medium Income Group), and HIG (High Income Group) flats, indicating a mixed community with a majority of middle to lower-income residents. The dietary pattern in Kalyanpuri is generally based on the staple foods common in North India, such as cereals (wheat for chapati, rice), legumes (dal), and a variety of vegetables and fruits. The reliance on the public distribution system (PDS) (ration cards) indicates a practical need for a safety net to ensure access to basic, low-budget food items.[17]

The NCD clinic of Urban Health Centre, Kalyanpuri, caters to an average of 300 people with NCDs each month, and approximately 70% of these cases are 60 years and above. The practices of carbohydrate spacing, five portions of vegetables and fruits, and the DASH diet are poorly followed. However, their knowledge of the beneficial effect of millet on diabetes, in particular, has not been explored. The study is grounded in the Health Belief Model (HBM), which suggests that a patient’s health-related behaviour is determined by their perception of a disease and the benefits/barriers to a proposed health action. In the context of NCD patients in Kalyanpuri, whose self-care practices and diet adherence are currently poor, understanding their ‘perceived benefits’ of millets versus the ‘perceived barriers’ to consumption is essential for designing effective nutritional interventions. In this regard, the purpose of the current study was to assess patients’ perceptions of different types of millets among those attending the NCD clinic at the Urban Health Centre in Kalyanpuri.

METHODOLOGY

It was a mixed-methods study (consisting of cross-sectional and qualitative studies). A mixed-methods design was employed to map the HBM constructs comprehensively. The quantitative phase measured the prevalence of millet recognition and consumption habits (perceived benefits and practices), while the subsequent qualitative phase used in-depth interviews to explore the complex barriers (perceived costs and accessibility) and systemic cues to action (political will and social awareness) that influence these health behaviors. The total study duration was 14 months. The study was conducted among all patients attending the NCD clinic of the Urban Health Centre (UHC), Kalyanpuri. All patients aged above 30 years attending the NCD clinic of UHC Kalyanpuri who were willing to participate and had given consent to be a part of the study were included, whereas individuals having known cases of intellectual disability and participants unwilling to be part of the study were not included. The sample size was calculated by assuming that the frequent millet consumption in Delhi[16] was 41.9%. After putting the values in the formula N = z2pq/d2, where p = prevalence of the study variable, q = 1 – prevalence, and d = absolute precision with the absolute precision of 5%, the calculated sample size was 374.

Ethical permission for the present study was taken from the Institute’s Ethics Committee, Lady Hardinge Medical College and Associated Hospitals, New Delhi, India, vide letter no. LHMC/IEC/2024/47 dated 16/05/2024.

Study tools

For the Quantitative component: A structured questionnaire with socio-demographic details and medical data. It included three parts: Part 1, the Patient’s Demographic variables; Part 2, The patient’s medical record and information about the diagnosed disease; and Part 3, The patient’s Physical examination and baseline laboratory investigation (Operational Definitions attached as appendix).

Pan India Consumer Survey for millets consumption. It is a pretested and validated questionnaire.[16] To ensure the instrument’s reliability within the specific socio-cultural context of the Kalyanpuri resettlement colony, a pilot study was conducted among a small representative group (n = 10) of NCD patients to confirm the linguistic and conceptual clarity of the items before formal data collection. It consists of nine questions: 1) Do you recognize any of these? (Show the picture of plant and grain of sorghum, pearl millet, finger millet) 2) How often do you eat something with millets? 3) How do you mostly eat millets? 4) Why do you eat millets? 5) Why don’t you eat more millets? 6) Do you think millets are healthy? 7) What do you think is healthy about millets? 8) How health-conscious are you and why? 9) Where do you get your information about health and food?

For the Qualitative component, an interview guide consisting of various variables was used to explore the possible reasons behind perceptions of millets. In-depth interviews were conducted with all study participants till data saturation was achieved.

Study Procedure: All patients aged above 30 years attending the NCD clinic of UHC Kalyanpuri were enrolled. Baseline assessment was done using a structured questionnaire, and NCD risk factors were assessed via anthropometric measurements, Blood pressure, RBS, and WHO ISH Score (2019 version). Millet consumption was assessed with the help of the Pan India Consumer Survey.

Based on quantitative data analysis, two groups were identified: those who consume millets and those who do not. To achieve data triangulation, an interview guide was prepared, and in-depth interviews (IDIs) were conducted with both groups. Interviews were conducted by GS, SK, and RS, who are clinical faculty/residents at the study site. Interviews were recorded with the aid of an audio recorder. Interviewers were trained in qualitative research methods and NCD counseling. Participants knew the researchers as clinical staff involved in the study. We acknowledge the potential for social desirability bias given the doctor–patient relationship. To minimize this, participants were assured that their responses would not affect their clinical care, and interviews were conducted in a separate counseling room. Participants were explicitly informed that their decision to participate, or their specific answers, would not impact their ongoing clinical treatment or access to medications at the NCD clinic. Qualitative analysis was conducted by investigators who were not directly involved in the daily clinical management of those participants to maintain analytical distance [Figure 1].

RESULTS

The current study included 369 NCD OPD attendees (out of 374) as five participants had missing or incomplete data. Over one-third of the NCD patients (39.3%) were elderly. Most of them were Hindu (79.6%) and married (80.3%). Over half of the participants (52.6%) had a nuclear family. Almost three-fourths (72.1%) belonged to upper-middle socio-economic status [Table 1].

Sociodemographic profile of participants

A little less than half of the NCD patients (48%) were either obese or overweight. The majority of participants reported engaging in adequate physical activity (88.3%), while only a fifth consumed fruits (20.3%) and vegetables (21.7%) for more than 5 days. Almost one-fifth of participants were consuming any form of smoking tobacco (8.1%), whereas 11.6% consumed smokeless tobacco and alcohol consumption was present in 8.4%. The most common morbidity was diabetes mellitus (36%), followed by both diabetes mellitus and hypertension (32%), only hypertension (23%), hypothyroidism (3.2%), hypothyroidism with hypertension or diabetes mellitus or both (4%), and others (Tuberculosis, arthritis, GDM, and COPD) (2%). Blood pressure was estimated for 351 of the total 369 participants. One-third of the participants had elevated systolic blood pressure (30.2%), which is more than 140 mm Hg. One-fifth of the participants had elevated diastolic blood pressure (19.3%), more than 90 mm Hg. One-fourth of participants had elevated random blood glucose values (80/338, 23.7%); more than 200 mg/dl. 20.3% of participants had a CVD risk score of more than 20 [Table 2]. Almost three-fourths of the NCD patients (73.4%) recognized any form of millet shown to them. Only 15.4% of the participants consumed millet at least once a week (with only 5.4% being daily consumers). Half of the study participants (47.9%) consumed millet rarely, every couple of years, or never. Nearly two-thirds of participants perceived millets as healthy (65.3%). The majority of millet consumers ate millet as chapatti (234/267, 87.6%), followed by breakfast porridge (34/267, 12.7%), rice (24/267, 8.9%), dosa (12/267, 4.4%), and a drink (10/267, 3.7%). Reasons for eating millet included health benefits (273 responses), being a healthy option (60.4%), being served at home (22.7%), liking the taste (8.4%), and others. Reasons for not consuming millet included no one else in the household eating it (40.3%), difficulty finding it (31.8%), disliking the taste (14.2%), and others. Most participants received information about millet from friends or family (74.3%) [Table 3]. There was a significant association (P = 0.021), with a higher percentage of Hindus (17.35%) consuming millets (daily, 2–3 times/week, 1 time/week) compared to other religions (6.67%). Participants in professional or semi-professional roles had a significantly higher rate of consumption (36.36%) compared to other categories (13.83%, P = 0.004) Higher education (Graduate/Professional) is significantly associated with millet consumption (P = 0.017) [Table 4]. For multivariable logistic regression, each variable was dichotomised. The correlation coefficient was calculated for all the independent variables, and those variables that were highly correlated were removed. All the variables were initially put in a multivariable logistic regression, and the variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated. Variables with VIF >5 were removed from the final model. Professionals/semi-professionals are 3.9 times more likely to consume millets compared to other occupations (aOR = 3.9, P = 0.022). Those with a graduate or professional degree were 3.37 times more likely to consume millets (aOR = 3.37, p = 0.008). Females were 2.67 times more likely to consume millets than males (aOR = 2.67, p = 0.017). Hindus were 2.67 times more likely to consume millets than other religious groups (aOR = 2.67, p = 0.021). Participants with a risk score of less than 20% were 2.19 times more likely to be millet consumers (aOR = 2.19, p = 0.038). Those with RBS <200 mg/dl were 2.06 times more likely to consume millets (aOR = 2.06, P = 0.039). The regression analysis indicates that being female, having a higher education/professional status, being Hindu, and having a lower CVD risk score or lower RBS are the most significant independent predictors for consuming millets in this study population [Table 5].

Qualitative analysis of in-depth interviews of those who do not consume millet

Theme 1: Consumption issues

Under this theme, coding included a) procurement issues, b) storage issues, c) cost issues, d) taste issues, and e) seasonal issues. These factors were among the key determinants of millet consumption challenges among NCD patients. Nearly all the interviewees faced difficulties in procuring millet as many shops do not sell it. The high price of raw millet or millet flour was also a significant barrier. Limited knowledge of recipes with millet contributed to taste issues, and the younger generation did not favor them. There was a common misconception and myth that millet is only consumed in winter. Table 6 shows the information provided by the NCD patients who consume millets. 16% of the participants did not report any issues in millet consumption.

Theme 2: Reason for consuming millets

Under this theme, verbatim statements of those who consumed it, considering it has some good effect on health, were included.

Theme 3: Health-related problems

Among those who consumed millet, some of them faced indigestion/constipation/bloating, while others experienced weight loss and stomach fullness. No such changes were observed among the seasonal consumers of millet.

Theme 4: Limited knowledge

The main reason for limited knowledge about millet was low social awareness and limited advertising (Television, Newspapers, Grocery shops) about it, including different types of millet, its benefits, other ways of consuming it, the forms in which it is available, and where it is available.

Theme 5: Political will

Wheat and rice are widely distributed throughout India through the Public Distribution System, but millet is not, which is one of the major contributing factors to all the procurement issues faced by consumers. Some of them also stated that farmers are not getting proper incentives for millet production, which hampers its availability to consumers.

Data saturation was achieved by the 20th interview, after which no new conceptual themes regarding millet consumption barriers were identified. Based on the in-depth interviews taken, a force field analysis for millet consumption was done, as summarized in Figure 2. Forces for change that had a positive effect on millet consumption were the health benefits of millet, good for NCD control, and political will (to be included in PDS and providing farmers incentives). Forces that were resisting change in millet consumption were consumption issues (taste, expense), local beliefs and customs, and storage and procurement issues.

Approximately 73.4% of patients recognized millets, yet 47.9% consumed them rarely or never. Qualitative data explained this through Theme 1 (Consumption Issues), specifically procurement difficulties and the “seasonal myth” that millets are only for winter. DISCUSSION

The findings of this study provide valuable insights into the dietary habits, health status, and socio-economic factors influencing millet consumption among the participants. The context of Kalyanpuri as an urban resettlement colony provides a unique lens through which to view millet consumption. Unlike more affluent urban areas, this community displays a high reliance on the PDS for food security. Comparing these results with national and international studies reveals several noteworthy trends and patterns. Our findings indicate that 72.1% of participants belong to the upper-middle socio-economic status, which reflects the socio-economic stratification seen in urban settings in India. Our findings show that while the majority of participants belong to upper-middle socio-economic status within this community, the high cost and poor market availability of millets remain significant barriers. Another study done by Manisha et al.[18] concluded that socio-economic factors play a crucial role in dietary choices, where higher socio-economic status often correlates with better access to diverse food options, including millets.. Interestingly, our study found that millet consumption was significantly associated with socio-economic status, suggesting that individuals from higher socio-economic backgrounds are more likely to incorporate millet into their diets, potentially due to increased awareness of their health benefits or better access to these grains. This mixed socio-economic profile, coupled with a practical need for low-budget food safety nets, makes the PDS a critical, yet currently underutilized, lever for dietary change in this specific urban landscape.

The data reveal that only 15.4% of participants consumed millets at least once a week, particularly given the growing body of evidence supporting the health benefits of millets. International studies, such as those by Kheya S A et al.[19] emphasize the nutritional superiority of millets, particularly in combating malnutrition and promoting sustainable agricultural practices. Furthermore, the perception that millets are healthy (65.3%) aligns with findings from research conducted in China, where traditional grains are often recognised for their health benefits but are underutilised Saleh Ahmed et al.[4]

Participants reported that the primary sources of information about millets were friends and family (74.3%), which is similar to findings in rural South Asian contexts where community and social networks significantly influence dietary practices Gowda et al.[20] This highlights the potential for targeted educational interventions to enhance millet consumption via community engagement.

Our study highlighted that females were 2.67 times more likely to consume millets than males (aOR = 2.67, P = 0.017). Similar results were found in a study conducted by Arora P et al[21] where it was found that 78.9% of middle-class women consume millets at least once a month, compared to 64.9% of lower-class women (P value 0.044, χ2 = 4.053). The regression analysis indicates that having a higher education/professional status was the most significant independent predictor for consuming millets in this study population. These results were concordant with the study done by Keshari P et al.[22]

The barriers identified for not consuming millets, particularly the accessibility (31.8%) and personal taste preferences (14.2%), resonate with the study done by Kane Patoka J in urban India.[16] Addressing these barriers through educational campaigns and promoting millet-based recipes may enhance consumer acceptance and consumption patterns.

The qualitative insights gathered from the study on millet consumption issues reveal a multifaceted landscape. The themes identified—procurement issues, health effects, limited knowledge, and political challenges—mirror similar barriers documented in prior studies, emphasizing the complexity of promoting millet as a sustainable food source.

The procurement challenges highlighted in this study align with findings from other qualitative research, where limited availability in markets has consistently been identified as a barrier to millet consumption Bose D et al.[23] Many participants noted that shops often do not stock millet, a sentiment echoed by Sangappa A et al[24] who reported that poor distribution channels hinder access to diverse grains like millet, particularly in urban areas. Additionally, the high cost of millet, specifically the raw grain and flour, poses a significant barrier. This issue resonates with the work of Anal et al.[25] who noted that price sensitivity among consumers can deter them from choosing healthier but more expensive options, including millet.

While some participants reported health benefits such as weight loss and increased satiety, others experienced digestive issues. This duality reflects findings from Jacob J et al.[26] which documented both positive health outcomes and adverse reactions among millet consumers. Such discrepancies can be attributed to individual dietary patterns and the preparation methods used, underscoring the importance of education in mitigating negative health outcomes.

The theme of limited knowledge about millet is particularly critical. Participants in this study cited a lack of awareness regarding millet’s nutritional benefits and culinary versatility, which is consistent with findings from Kane-Potaka J et al.[16] Their research indicated that insufficient public awareness and marketing strategies contribute to low millet consumption rates, stressing the need for comprehensive educational campaigns. Furthermore, the myth that millet is primarily a winter food, as reported in this study, highlights cultural perceptions that may restrict its year-round consumption, paralleling observations from Shah et al.[27] about seasonal biases in food choices.

The political dimension, specifically the inadequate distribution of millet through systems like the PDS, emerges as a significant barrier. The neglect of millet in governmental food policies exacerbates procurement challenges, as reported by Carter T et al.[28] The lack of incentives for farmers to produce millet further complicates the situation, leading to reduced supply and increased costs for consumers. This underlines the need for policy interventions aimed at integrating millet into mainstream food distribution channels, enhancing both production and consumption.

The findings of this study highlight a pronounced Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP) Gap regarding millet consumption among NCD patients. While Knowledge was high—with 73.4% of participants being aware of millets—and Attitudes were generally positive (65.3% perceiving them as beneficial for health), the Practice (actual consumption) remained low at only 15.4%. In public health nutrition, this discrepancy suggests that while cognitive awareness has been achieved through national health campaigns, it has failed to translate into behaviour change due to structural and cultural mediators. The KAP framework helps identify that the bottleneck in this population is not a lack of information, but rather a “Practice Barrier” rooted in the socio-ecology of the urban resettlement colony.

Limitations

The use of facility-based convenience sampling at a single NCD clinic in Kalyanpuri limits the generalizability of the findings to the broader community or other urban contexts. Dietary habits were self-reported, which may introduce social desirability bias, leading participants to potentially over-report “healthy” perceptions or behavior. The cross-sectional design of the quantitative phase allows for the identification of associations but precludes the ability to make causal inferences regarding millet consumption and health outcomes. The study population consists of NCD clinic attendees, who may have higher health awareness or different dietary motivations compared to the general urban population. The study relied on self-reported consumption frequency via a survey rather than validated dietary recall methods (e.g., 24-hour recall), which may affect the precision of intake data.

CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS

Overall, the findings from this mixed-method study reveal a critical gap: While awareness regarding millet consumption was high (73.4% recognized millet) among patients attending the NCD clinic, actual consumption was low (only 15.4% consumed millet at least once a week). This trend underscores that awareness alone is insufficient to drive dietary change. The research identified that consumption is significantly hindered by an interconnected network of challenges, such as Consumption Issues: Barriers include high cost, difficulties in procurement and storage, undesirable taste, and the seasonal misbelief that millets should only be consumed in winter; Health and Knowledge Gaps: While facilitators included perceived health benefits for diabetes and weight loss, limited knowledge regarding recipes, different forms of millet, and availability persists. Furthermore, some participants reported adverse effects like indigestion and bloating, which require targeted education on proper preparation. Political and Systemic Barriers: The exclusion of millets from the PDS and the lack of proper farmer incentives emerged as major contributing factors to all procurement issues, highlighting a critical failure in systemic support.

Based on our research, it is recommended to include millets in the PDS ration offerings specifically for NCD cardholders to alleviate the procurement and cost barriers identified in this study, training of community health workers (ASHAs/ANMs) and NCD clinic staff in specific millet-based dietary counseling, focusing on preparation methods to mitigate reported digestive issues such as bloating, and development and implementation of NCD clinic-based dietary counseling modules (transition from awareness posters to hands-on “Millet Cooking Demonstrations” in NCD clinics) that provide simple, low-cost millet recipes tailored to North Indian staple patterns (e.g., millet-wheat blended chapattis) to address taste and unpalatability concerns.
