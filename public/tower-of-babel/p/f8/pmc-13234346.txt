Bridging the Gap in Chinese Legal Conflict Review: A Dataset, Benchmark Tasks, and Framework
Authors: Siwen Zhao, Yunnuo Xu, Zhe Chen, Feng Qiao, Hailong Chen, XiaoRui Li, Sen Lin, Zhonghang Ji, Yujun Li, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural language processing has enabled rapid progress in legal artificial intelligence, but automated review of conflicts between superior and subordinate laws remains largely unexplored. Maintaining consistency across legal hierarchies in Chinese legislation is crucial, yet manual review is labor-intensive and error-prone. To address the absence of systematic research in legal conflict review, this study presents three key contributions. (1) we presented the Chinese Legal Conflict Review dataset (LCR-CN), a novel dataset containing 6,995 annotated legislative provisions with conflict types, explanations, and revision suggestions. (2) we proposed three benchmark tasks: (a). Superior Law Retrieval, (b). Conflict Classification, and (c) Conflict Explanation and Revision Generation, to establish a comprehensive evaluation framework for this domain. (3) we fine-tuned multiple language models on LCR-CN and observe a relative accuracy improvement of 72.7% over baseline methods, demonstrating the effectiveness of the dataset and the feasibility of automated legal conflict review. This work introduces the first dedicated dataset (LCR-CN), establishes a standardized task framework, and presents robust baselines for legal conflict review, thereby laying theoretical and methodological foundations for AI-assisted legal conflict resolution.

Background & Summary

In recent years, natural language processing (NLP) has achieved rapid progress in the field of legal artificial intelligence (Legal AI). NLP techniques are increasingly applied to automate and enhance complex legal tasks, assisting legal professionals in drafting documents, analyzing cases, and improving decision-making processes1,2. Legal AI not only enhances the efficiency and accessibility of legal services but also provides a technological foundation for achieving fairer and more transparent judicial decisions3,4.

Current research in Legal AI primarily focuses on a variety of NLP tasks, including Legal Judgment Prediction5–7, Legal Text Classification8,9, Legal Question Answering10,11, Legal Named Entity Recognition12–14, and Legal Argument Mining15–17. Several benchmark datasets have been developed to evaluate model performance in these areas, such as CaseHOLD18, LexGLUE19,20, COLIEE21, LEXTREME22,23, CAIL24, and SLARD25,26. Despite these efforts, a crucial research area remains largely unexplored — the automatic review of hierarchical legal conflicts, also referred to as legal conflict review. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no study has systematically addressed this task, leaving a significant research gap in the field of Legal AI.

In the Chinese legislative system, drafting new legal clauses must strictly adhere to superior laws to ensure consistency across legal hierarchies and maintain legal coherence27–29. Subordinate regulations are required to align with the principles and provisions of higher-level laws; otherwise, inconsistencies may arise in aspects such as responsibility allocation, applicable conditions, or penalty severity, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Manual conflict review is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and prone to human error, posing substantial challenges for legal experts30. The lack of dedicated datasets and intelligent tools for legal conflict review has therefore become a critical bottleneck, revealing an urgent and previously unaddressed research challenge in legal AI.

To bridge the long-standing deficiency in the field of legal conflict review, we present LCR-CN, a large-scale and richly annotated dataset specifically designed for Chinese Legal Conflict Review. We further define a set of benchmark tasks to quantitatively evaluate conflict review performance, establish a new research paradigm, and adapt representative language models to this framework. Unlike existing resources that primarily focus on judicial reasoning or case retrieval, LCR-CN targets legislative conflict review, a critical yet largely understudied domain. The dataset contains 6,995 normative provisions extracted from 173 legislative drafts. Each provision is annotated with conflict types, expert-written explanations, revision suggestions, and corresponding superior laws that serve as authoritative references. By shifting the research focus from post-judicial analysis to pre-legislative review, LCR-CN provides the first structured foundation for conflict detection and compliance analysis in Chinese legal texts. To establish a quantitative evaluation framework, we define three benchmark tasks that correspond to different stages of legislative review: (1) Superior Law Retrieval, which identifies relevant higher-level provisions as references for compliance assessment; (2) Conflict Classification, which categorizes detected inconsistencies into specific legal conflict types; and (3) Conflict Explanation and Revision Generation, which produces natural language explanations and revision suggestions to support human reviewers. These tasks form a comprehensive pipeline for detecting, interpreting, and resolving normative conflicts, providing a measurable and reproducible evaluation framework for legal AI research. To validate the effectiveness of LCR-CN, we perform supervised fine-tuning on six representative language models. Experimental results show that small- and medium-scale language models achieve notable improvements in conflict detection accuracy after fine-tuning on LCR-CN, with a relative increase of 72.7%. This observation suggests that the dataset provides effective supervision signals for learning fine-grained legal conflict patterns and supports supervised adaptation in this domain. In summary, our work delivers three major contributions:

We release LCR-CN, the first large-scale dataset dedicated to legal conflict review in Chinese legislation, establishing a solid data foundation for automated legal analysis;

Methods

This section describes the procedures for collecting legislative texts and annotating legal conflicts, including expert verification and dataset curation steps, for constructing the LCR-CN dataset.

Data collection and annotation overview

To construct the LCR-CN dataset, we employed eight legal professionals with experience in legislative drafting and legal compliance review. The overall data construction workflow is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Overview of the data construction and annotation workflow for LCR-CN. The workflow includes legislative text collection, candidate superior law identification, expert verification of hierarchical relationships, conflict classification, explanation annotation, revision generation, and quality control.

Legislative texts were collected from the National Laws and Regulations Database of China31, restricted to legal documents that were publicly available and marked as currently effective at the time of data collection. The data collection process was completed prior to August 2025, and only the latest valid versions of legal documents were retained. Drafts or provisions that had been repealed, superseded, or explicitly marked as invalid were excluded. This database includes legal documents such as the Constitution of China, national laws, administrative regulations, and local regulations. Based on this source, we collected 173 drafts of local normative documents issued at the municipal and provincial levels. From these drafts, 6,995 legislative provisions were selected as query provisions, covering 32 thematic categories. Query provisions were selected at the article or paragraph level through manual screening by legal experts. Only complete clauses containing concrete implementation rules, regulatory obligations, sanctions, or explicit definitional content were retained. Titles, headings, preambles, purely procedural statements, and fragmented or context-dependent text segments were excluded. All source documents are publicly available and were used in accordance with their open access status. For each query provision, candidate superior law provisions were identified from the same database as potential references for hierarchical compliance review. These candidate provisions served as the basis for subsequent expert verification and conflict annotation.

Candidate superior law retrieval and expert verification

During dataset construction, candidate superior law provisions were identified for each query provision using a combination of keyword-based matching and semantic similarity methods. Keyword-based matching was conducted using legally salient terms commonly appearing in legislative texts, such as conditions, responsibilities, and sanctions. Keyword terms were derived through a combination of automated and expert-guided processes. Specifically, legislative texts were first segmented using Chinese word segmentation tools, after which term frequencies were computed across the corpus. High-frequency terms were then reviewed and refined by legal experts based on established research and practice in legislative analysis. This process resulted in a curated list of legally salient keywords used for matching. In parallel, semantic similarity was computed based on vector representations of provision-level texts, and similarity scores were used to identify provisions with related legal content. The results from keyword-based matching and semantic similarity matching were merged and ranked to produce a candidate list of superior law provisions for subsequent expert review. This step was used solely to narrow the search space during dataset construction and did not determine the final superior–subordinate provision pairs.

All candidate superior provisions were subsequently reviewed by legal experts. For each query provision, experts examined the legislative basis, regulatory purpose, legal hierarchy, and scope of application to determine whether a direct hierarchical relationship existed. Only expert-validated provision pairs were retained in the dataset. This expert verification step ensured the legal correctness of provision pairing and formed the foundation for subsequent conflict annotation. Only direct hierarchical relationships between subordinate provisions and superior law provisions were considered. Comparisons between provisions at the same legal level or between non-direct hierarchical levels were excluded.

Conflict classification, explanation, and revision annotation

After constructing the provision pairs, the next stage of dataset construction involved the annotation of conflict classification, explanation, and revision. By systematically categorizing provision pairs and providing legal explanations, this step produced fine-grained annotated data for subsequent use.

Conflict classification annotation

Legal conflicts were divided into five categories: Responsibility Conflict, Condition Conflict, Sanction Conflict, Definition Conflict, and No Conflict. Responsibility Conflict referred to inconsistencies in the allocation of powers or responsibilities, such as when a subordinate regulation granted additional duties or powers without authorization from the superior law, or when both provisions conflicted in defining the responsible entity. Condition Conflict indicated the addition or alteration of conditions of applicability, meaning that the subordinate regulation introduced, removed, or substantially changed conditions compared with those established by the superior law. Sanction Conflict referred to inconsistencies in the scope or extent of penalties, such as when the subordinate regulation imposed a sanction not authorized by the superior law, or when the severity of the sanction exceeded the limits set by the superior law. Definition Conflict referred to inconsistencies in legal concepts or terminology, where differences in definitions between superior and subordinate provisions caused confusion or contradictions in application. No Conflict indicated that no normative inconsistency existed. Based on these five categories, annotation was carried out in three stages. In the first stage, legal experts manually annotated approximately 1,000 provision pairs to establish annotation guidelines and ensure category consistency. In the second stage, a transformer-based legal text classifier trained on these expert-annotated samples was applied to assist with preliminary labeling of the remaining data. In the final stage, all automatically generated labels were reviewed and corrected by legal experts, and only expert-validated annotations were retained in the final dataset. The distribution of conflict categories reflects their empirical prevalence in real-world legislative texts and was not artificially adjusted during dataset construction. As a result, certain conflict types (e.g., Responsibility Conflict) are more frequent than others (e.g., Definition Conflict), which is consistent with observed legislative drafting practices.

Conflict explanation and revision annotation

After conflict classification, legal experts were divided into two groups. Each group provided a concise textual explanation for every provision identified as conflicting, outlining the underlying reasons for the conflict. To minimize redundancy and control complexity, all explanations were written in a succinct manner. This ensured that the evaluation of explanation quality focused on accuracy and legal soundness rather than text length. Based on the conflict type and the explanation, experts then revised each provision to produce a conflict-free version as a standardized output. Finally, the two groups cross-checked each other’s explanations and revisions, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion to ensure consistency with the corresponding superior law.

Annotation quality control

To ensure the reliability and consistency of the annotations, quality control procedures were applied throughout the annotation process. Each data sample was independently reviewed by at least two legal experts, and any discrepancies were resolved through expert discussion until a consensus decision was reached. In addition to cross-review, a randomly selected subset of the dataset was independently re-annotated for quality assessment. Inter-annotator agreement (IAA) was examined to evaluate consistency across conflict type labels, conflict explanations, and revision outcomes. This procedure provided an additional check on annotation stability and supported the overall reliability of the dataset. The resulting statistics are reported in the Technical Validation section.

In LCR-CN, retrieval-based sanity checks were conducted using a fixed candidate set of 200 superior law provisions for each query provision. This design reflects practical legislative review settings, in which the number of legally relevant superior provisions associated with a given subordinate clause is typically small, often ranging from one to several articles. The candidate set was therefore constructed to substantially exceed the expected number of true relevant provisions, ensuring adequate coverage while maintaining a manageable retrieval scope. To improve representativeness, candidate provisions were not selected randomly. Instead, they were sampled through a stratified process that considered semantic similarity scores, thematic relevance, and legal category alignment. This procedure ensured that each candidate set contained a mixture of highly relevant, moderately related, and clearly irrelevant provisions, thereby providing a realistic and discriminative retrieval context. As a result, the 200-candidate setting serves as a sufficient and conservative approximation for evaluating retrieval behavior under practical legislative review conditions.

For data organization and reuse convenience, the dataset was divided into training, validation, and test subsets with a ratio of 7:2:1, as shown in the Table 1. The split was performed at the sample level after the completion of all annotation and quality control procedures. Samples were assigned to the three subsets using stratified sampling to preserve the original distribution of conflict categories across splits. The training and validation subsets were used to support model development and selection, while the test subset was reserved exclusively for evaluation. This data split was applied solely for downstream usage and did not affect the construction or technical validation of the dataset itself.

IAA for explanation and revision validity

In addition to conflict type labels, we assessed the consistency of the explanation and revision annotations, which are provided in free-text form. Because direct string-level comparison is not suitable for evaluating expert-written legal explanations and revisions, we evaluated these annotations using a set of structured and quantifiable checks. These checks were designed to capture key legal elements and normative outcomes. From the subset of provision pairs annotated as conflicting, we randomly selected 300 samples using stratified sampling to ensure balanced coverage of the four conflict categories. Each sampled provision pair was independently reviewed by two legal experts. The experts did not revise or rewrite the original explanations or revised provisions; instead, they assessed the existing annotations using a standardized evaluation questionnaire. All reviews were conducted independently under the same annotation guideline, without discussion between reviewers. For each sampled provision pair, experts completed a structured set of evaluation items formulated as a statistical checklist. This checklist assessed the alignment between the annotated explanations, revisions, and the corresponding superior law provisions, as shown in Fig. 4.

Structured evaluation checklist used for assessing explanation and revision annotations. The checklist is used in the inter-annotator agreement assessment and consists of two stages: (1) verification of alignment between the annotated explanation and the assigned conflict type (Q1–Q2), and (2) assessment of legal adequacy and semantic fidelity of the revised provision relative to the corresponding superior law (Q3–Q4).

Agreement was quantified separately for binary and multi-label items. For binary judgments (Q2–Q4), inter-annotator agreement was measured using Cohen’s kappa (k) together with overall percent agreement. Cohen’s k accounts for chance agreement and provides a standardized measure of consistency between two annotators. For the identification of conflict elements in explanations (Q1), which allows multiple selections per sample, agreement was measured using Jaccard agreement. For each sample, the Jaccard similarity was computed as the size of the intersection divided by the size of the union of the element sets selected by the two experts. Reported values correspond to the average Jaccard agreement across the sampled set.

As summarized in Table 4, the IAA results demonstrate that the explanation and revision annotations in the LCR-CN dataset exhibit substantial internal consistency when evaluated through structured criteria. Experts showed high agreement in identifying core conflict elements and in assessing the alignment between explanations and assigned conflict types, indicating that the annotations consistently capture the normative basis of legislative conflicts. Agreement on the effectiveness of revisions in resolving conflicts with superior law provisions was also substantial, suggesting that the revised provisions serve as reliable normative references. Although agreement on semantic deviation risk was comparatively lower, it remained within the range commonly reported for expert-driven legal annotation tasks, reflecting the inherently subjective nature of this judgment rather than inconsistency in the dataset. Overall, these results support the technical validity of the explanation and revision annotations provided in LCR-CN.

Model-based sanity checks for dataset validity

To examine whether the annotations in the LCR-CN dataset provide consistent and learnable supervision signals, we conducted a series of model-based sanity checks under retrieval, classification, and generation settings. In the following, we summarize observations from these sanity checks to support the technical validity of the dataset.

Superior law retrieval

We applied a range of representative retrieval models, including BM2533, general Chinese pre-trained models (chinese-bert-wwm-ext34 and chinese-roberta-wwm-ext34), and deep semantic embedding models (bge-large-zh-v1.535 and the Qwen3-Embedding series36), to examine whether the candidate sets and provision texts in LCR-CN contain sufficient signals for retrieval-based exploration. Specifically, BM25 served as a lexicon-based reference, allowing us to verify whether exact term overlap in legal texts could recover relevant superior law provisions. chinese-bert-wwm-ext and chinese-roberta-wwm-ext, which are pre-trained on large-scale general Chinese corpora, were included to examine the extent to which general-purpose contextual embeddings align with legal provision semantics. bge-large-zh-v1.5 was used as a representative domain-agnostic embedding model to assess whether dense semantic representations could capture relationships between query provisions and superior law provisions. The Qwen3-Embedding series, released in multiple parameter scales (0.6B, 4B, and 8B), was applied to examine whether variations in model scale affected the ability to utilize the semantic information provided by the dataset. The inclusion of different model sizes allowed us to check whether retrieval outcomes were sensitive to parameter scale rather than to the dataset structure itself.

For retrieval-based sanity checks, we reported Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR), Accuracy@k, and Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG@k) to summarize ranking behavior. MRR measured the rank position of the first relevant provision in the retrieval results. Accuracy@k evaluated whether the correct superior provision appeared within the top- k retrieved results, with k set to 1, 3, and 5, to assess accuracy at different retrieval depths. NDCG@k measured the overall ranking quality by considering the positions of relevant provisions in the retrieval list. We reported results at k = 3 and k = 5 to reflect ranking performance at different cutoffs.

Conflict classification

We selected a wide range of large language models to examine whether the annotations in the LCR-CN dataset provide effective supervision signals for conflict type classification, including both open-source and closed-source models. The open-source group included six representative models: Qwen-2.5-7B37, Llama-3.1-8B38, Qwen3-8B39, DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B40, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B40, and Ministral-8B41. In addition, we applied LoRA42 fine-tuning to these models using the LCR-CN annotations, in order to examine whether the dataset provides learnable, domain-specific supervision for conflict classification. The closed-source group included GPT-4o43, Qwen-plus44, DeepSeek-R140, DeepSeek-v345, Gemini-2.5-Pro, and Claude-3.7-Sonnet, which allowed us to examine whether the dataset annotations can be utilized by models with different scales and training backgrounds.

For classification-based sanity checks, we employed Accuracy and F1-score as metrics. Accuracy measured the proportion of correctly classified samples out of the total number of samples. The F1-score represented the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, providing a complementary indicator under class imbalance.

The classification outcomes obtained from these sanity checks are summarized in Table 5. Across all architectures, models showed consistent and substantial improvements after being fine-tuned on the LCR-CN annotations, compared with their non-fine-tuned counterparts. While non-fine-tuned models exhibited limited sensitivity to fine-grained conflict distinctions, fine-tuning on the dataset yielded notable gains in both Accuracy and F1, with improvements being more pronounced in F1. This observation suggests that the fine-grained annotations in LCR-CN provide effective supervision signals for mitigating class imbalance and reducing confusion between semantically similar conflict categories. Among the non-fine-tuned models, performance differences reflected variations in how well different pretrained representations aligned with the legal semantics encoded in the dataset. Both DeepSeek-R1 derivatives, which are primarily optimized for general reasoning and dialogue alignment, showed weaker sensitivity to the fine-grained legal category boundaries reflected in the dataset annotations.

Conflict explanation and revision generation

We applied the same set of representative models as in the classification-based checks and used two automatic similarity metrics, Bilingual Evaluation Understudy (BLEU)46 and BERTScore47, as auxiliary indicators for generation-based sanity checks. BLEU was used to examine the degree of lexical and phrasal overlap between generated texts and the corresponding reference explanations or revision suggestions provided in the dataset. BERTScore, based on contextual embeddings from pre-trained language models, was used to measure semantic similarity between generated texts and reference annotations.

In addition to these automatic metrics, we incorporated an LLM-based evaluation as a complementary indicator to assess aspects of legal adequacy that are difficult to capture through lexical or embedding-based similarity alone. Specifically, a GPT-4-based evaluator was used to assign a score on a 0–10 scale to each generated output. For conflict explanations, this score (desc_score) reflects whether the generated description accurately captures the core conflict point and aligns with the reference explanation in meaning. For conflict revisions, the corresponding score (revise_score) evaluates the alignment of the revised provision with the golden reference in terms of semantic content, legal reasoning, and key normative expressions.

The outcomes of the conflict explanation sanity checks are summarized in Table 6. Overall, models without domain-specific fine-tuning showed limited alignment with the reference explanations, as reflected by lower BLEU and BERTScore values, particularly in capturing legal terminology and key elements encoded in the dataset annotations. This observation suggests that, without alignment to domain-specific legal annotations, generated explanations may diverge from the structured legal reasoning reflected in the reference texts provided by the dataset. In contrast, models fine-tuned with LoRA showed markedly improved alignment with the reference explanations, indicating that the LCR-CN annotations provide precise and structured supervision signals aligned with legislative semantics. Among the evaluated models, Qwen3-8B achieved the highest or near-highest BLEU and BERTScore values, indicating strong alignment between its generated outputs and the expert-written explanations in the dataset. The desc_score values exhibit trends that are broadly consistent with the automatic similarity metrics, while offering additional validation from a legal reasoning perspective. Models fine-tuned on the LCR-CN dataset consistently obtained higher desc_score values, suggesting that the expert-written explanations provide clear and interpretable supervision signals that enable models to accurately identify and articulate the core normative conflicts. The coherence between BLEU, BERTScore, and desc_score indicates that the explanation annotations in LCR-CN are not only semantically consistent, but also legally meaningful when assessed under multiple evaluation criteria.

Qualitative analysis of confusion patterns

An analysis of the classification results indicates that confusion patterns are unevenly distributed across conflict categories and tend to concentrate in semantically adjacent classes. In particular, higher confusion rates are observed between Condition Conflict and Responsibility Conflict, as well as between Definition Conflict and Condition Conflict. These category pairs correspond to boundaries where legislative provisions frequently combine obligation statements with applicability constraints, or embed scope limitations within definitional clauses.

This pattern is further supported by annotation-level statistics. As reported in the inter-annotator agreement analysis, these same conflict categories exhibit comparatively lower per-class agreement, indicating reduced separability even under expert annotation. Such consistency between model-level confusion and expert-level disagreement suggests that the observed errors are closely related to intrinsic linguistic and normative characteristics of legislative texts, rather than inconsistencies in the annotation process.

From a dataset perspective, these findings highlight that LCR-CN captures fine-grained and realistic boundary cases commonly encountered in legislative drafting. While such cases increase task difficulty, they also reflect the authentic semantic structure of legal norms and contribute to the dataset’s value for studying nuanced legal conflict identification.

Usage Notes

The LCR-CN dataset can be used to support multi-stage legal conflict review involving text understanding and reasoning. One common usage scenario involves identifying relevant superior law provisions for a given query provision and assessing hierarchical consistency based on semantic content and scope of authority.

The dataset is structured to support three common usage types: superior law retrieval, conflict classification, and conflict explanation and revision. For superior law retrieval, each query provision is accompanied by a candidate set of superior law provisions, from which relevant provisions can be identified using retrieval methods. For conflict classification, paired query and superior law provisions can be used to predict or assign conflict types based on the annotated labels. For provisions annotated as conflicting, the dataset provides expert-written explanations and revised provisions, which can be used as reference outputs for explanation or revision generation.

The input and output formats for these usage scenarios are explicitly specified in the released data files, enabling consistent data access and processing. The provided annotations support both supervised learning and parameter-efficient adaptation methods.

For model-based reuse, the dataset can be used with a range of adaptation and training setups corresponding to the three usage types described above. In the retrieval setting, semantic similarity–based approaches may be used to capture relationships between query provisions and candidate superior law provisions. Such retrieval can be implemented without task-specific fine-tuning, for example through zero-shot matching or embedding-based similarity computation. Retrieved and ranked provision pairs may then be used as inputs for downstream classification or generation. For conflict classification and conflict explanation/revision, the annotated labels, explanations, and revised provisions can be used for supervised training or parameter-efficient adaptation of language models. For example, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) may be applied to train models directly on the provided labels and reference texts. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) may be used as a parameter-efficient alternative, reducing the number of trainable parameters during adaptation while retaining the original model structure.

Across all usage types, standardized input–output templates may be employed to format dataset fields for retrieval, classification, and generation. In practice, retrieval involves semantic matching between query provisions and candidate superior law provisions; classification operates on paired provisions to assign conflict types; and explanation and revision rely on expert-written explanations and revised provisions as reference outputs. Candidate sets are included within each data sample to facilitate retrieval experiments without requiring additional external collection. When using the dataset in automated settings, model outputs should be interpreted with care. Legal conflict review involves normative judgment and contextual interpretation, and automated outputs are intended to support, rather than replace, professional legal review.
