Assessing symptom clusters in post-total knee arthroplasty patients: development and validation of a new scale
Authors: Ke Wu, Weixia Bai, Jian Guan, Na Li, Baibai Wang, Hongwei Dong, CongCong Li, Jie Zhang, Hua Zhang, Huiling Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients undergoing total knee arthroplasty (TKA) often face various symptoms, but existing assessment tools mainly target functional recovery and efficacy, missing a comprehensive symptom cluster evaluation. This study seeks to create a symptom cluster assessment scale for TKA patients and assess its reliability and validity.MethodsUsing the Symptom Experience Model as the theoretical framework, a clinical test version of the scale was developed through literature review, semi-structured interviews, two rounds of Delphi expert consultations, and a pre-survey. Using convenience sampling, 476 TKA patients who visited orthopedic departments at three tertiary general hospitals in Hebei Province between January and June 2025 were selected for the formal survey. Following data collection, assessments of the scale’s reliability and validity were performed.ResultsThe formal version of the Symptom Cluster Assessment Scale for Total Knee Arthroplasty Patients comprises 5 dimensions and 22 items, demonstrating adequate internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.907, split-half reliability of 0.868). Content validity results indicate an I-CVI ranging from 0.824 to 1.000 and an S-CVI/Ave of 0.948. Exploratory factor analysis extracted five common factors, accounting for 68.176% of cumulative variance. Confirmatory factor analysis results indicated that all model fit indices met corresponding criteria, demonstrating good model fit. Internal consistency testing revealed strong correlations between each dimension and the total scale. Convergent validity and discriminant validity both met established standards.ConclusionThis study effectively developed and validated a specialized scale for assessing symptom clusters in patients following TKA. This scale provides healthcare professionals with a systematic and practical assessment tool to facilitate the implementation of individualized rehabilitation interventions. Additionally, previously underrecognized symptom clusters specific to TKA have been identified.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10122-6.Keywords: Total knee arthroplasty, Total knee replacement, Symptom clusters, Scale construction, Reliability, Validity

Introduction

Patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty (TKA) frequently experience a range of postoperative physical and psychological symptoms, including pain, swelling, joint stiffness, fatigue, fear of movement, anxiety, depression, and sleep disturbances [1–4]. Recent evidence indicates that these postoperative symptoms rarely occur in isolation but tend to interact synergistically, forming “symptom clusters”, thereby amplifying overall symptom burden and adversely affecting rehabilitation outcomes [5]. For example, persistent postoperative pain may exacerbate kinesiophobia and sleep disturbances, while psychological distress such as anxiety and depression can further heighten pain perception and reduce participation in rehabilitation exercises [6, 7]. Moreover, arthrogenic muscle inhibition and chronic postoperative pain have been identified as key contributors to impaired functional recovery and diminished health-related quality of life after TKA [8, 9]. In addition, unrealistic preoperative expectations and inadequate symptom management may adversely affect postoperative satisfaction and long-term rehabilitation trajectories [10]. Taken together, these findings underscore the need to shift from traditional single-symptom or functional assessments toward a comprehensive, symptom cluster-based perspective when evaluating postoperative rehabilitation efficacy in TKA patients.

Currently, symptom assessment following TKA primarily relies on two types of patient-reported outcomes (PROs) tools, neither of which fully addresses the specific requirements for evaluating symptom clusters. The first category comprises functional assessment scales incorporating symptoms, including the widely used Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) [11] and the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) [12]. The WOMAC scale was developed by Professor Bellamy’s team in 1988. Its applicability was confirmed through large-scale validation by the team in 1991 and cross-population validation by the Orthopaedic Nursing Association (ONA) in 2005. The KOOS questionnaire was developed by Professor Roos’ team in 1998. After validation by his team in 2001 and cross-cultural validation by the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) in 2010, it became a core assessment tool in this field. These tools primarily measure the efficacy of surgery in improving joint function and the ability to perform daily activities. While they include subdimensions such as “pain”, their item design focuses on the impact of pain on functional activities rather than the multidimensional characteristics of pain itself (e.g., frequency of occurrence, level of distress). More critically, above tools largely disregard other core symptoms like swelling, fatigue, and sleep disturbances, while non-symptom items disproportionately dominate the scales. Consequently, when used for symptom assessment, they lack sufficient specificity, limited sensitivity, and heavy measurement burden. They also fail to capture the intrinsic relationships among symptoms.

The second category encompasses unidimensional symptom assessment instruments, including the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for pain [13], the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK-11) [14], and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [15]. While these tools effectively measure the intensity or frequency of specific symptoms, their fragmented assessment approach fails to capture the synergistic relationships between different symptoms. Consequently, they fail to reflect the overall burden that symptom clusters impose on patients [16].

In addition, multisymptom assessment tools are primarily applied in the field of cancer, including the Memorial Symptom Assessment Scale (MSAS) [17], the M.D. Anderson Symptom Inventory (MDASI) [18], Edmonton Symptom Assessment System (ESAS) [19], among others. However, their item designs lack specificity for TKA postoperative symptoms (e.g., including unrelated symptoms like “hair loss” and “oral ulcers”), making it difficult to accurately reflect the actual symptom experiences of this population and limiting their applicability in orthopedic rehabilitation.

In summary, existing assessment tools demonstrate marked limitations in comprehensiveness, population specificity, and the ability to capture symptom cluster structures. To assess this gap, the present study aims to systematically develop and validate a multidimensional symptom cluster assessment scale apecifically designed for TKA postoperative patients, grounded in patient-reported outcomes. Compared with previous instruments, the proposed scale simultaneously evaluates both physical and psychological symptom dimensions, highlights the interrelationships among co-occurring symptoms, and explicitly distinguishes between transient/self-limiting symptoms and persistent postoperative symptoms. In addition, the reliability and validity of the scale were rigorously evaluated to provide a scientifically robust and clinically applicable tool for postoperative symptom management and rehabilitation assessment in TKA patients. It was hypothesized that: (1) common physical and psychological symptoms following TKA can be comprehensively identified and clearly categorized into transient/self-limiting and persistent symptom domains; (2) significant inter-symptom associations and discernible clustering patterns exist among post-TKA symptoms; and (3) the developed scale would demonstrate satisfactory psychometric properties, including good reliability, validity, and clinical applicability for evaluating postoperative symptom burden in patients undergoing TKA.

Methods

Establishing a research team on the subject

The research team consists of six members. A Deputy Director of Nursing (Master’s degree) oversees the overall study design, provides methodological guidance, and manages the expert consultation process to ensure the research adheres to scientific standards. A nursing graduate student (Master’s degree) conducts literature searches, constructs the item pool, and performs data collection and analysis under the Deputy Director’s supervision to guarantee operational accuracy. An orthopedic director (Master’s degree) and an orthopedic head nurse (Bachelor’s degree), leveraging their extensive experience in managing TKA patients, reviewed the item pool for clinical accuracy, completeness, and appropriate phrasing. This dual perspective from physicians and nursing administrators ensured the scale content authentically reflected postoperative symptoms. Two orthopedic nurses (bachelor’s degree) assisted the graduate student in conducting pre-survey and formal survey activities. Familiar with patient communication and the clinical setting, they ensured the data collection process ran smoothly and provided feedback on item clarity and patient acceptability.

Development of the scale item pool

Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in the Symptom Experience Model [20], which comprises three components: the antecedents of symptom occurrence, symptom perception, and the outcomes of symptom manifestation. The antecedents include demographic characteristics, disease attributes, and individual traits; symptom perception encompasses the frequency, severity, distress, and meaning attributed to symptoms; the outcomes pertain to patients’ adaptation to illness, quality of life, and functional status. Given that “meaning” refers to an individual’s subjective interpretation of the experienced symptoms and is not suitable for quantification, this study evaluates patient symptoms based on three dimensions: frequency, severity, and distress.

Literature review

A comprehensive search was conducted across seven databases, including China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data, VIP Database, China Biomedical Literature Database, PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase, covering the period from database inception to May 2024. The search strategy employed a combination of subject headings and free-text terms, utilizing keywords such as “total knee arthroplasty”, “total knee replacement”, “symptom”, “symptom cluster”, “symptom assessment”, “symptom management”, and “symptom experience”.

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria: (1) The study population consisted of patients after TKA; (2) The study focused on symptom profiles, symptom assessment, or symptom management strategies for TKA patients; (3) The study setting was hospital-based or home-based; (4) The study design was observational (e.g., cross-sectional, cohort) or interventional (e.g., randomized controlled trials).

Studies were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) No full-text article could be retrieved; (2) The article was published in a language other than English or Chinese.

Semi-structured interviews

From July to August 2024, purposive sampling was employed to recruit patients who had undergone TKA from the orthopedic department of a tertiary Grade A hospital in Hebei Province who had undergone TKA. In accordance with the principle of data saturation, a well-established methodological standard in qualitative research [21], semi-structured interviews were conducted, and data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved. Specifically, recruitment and interviewing proceeded iteratively with concurrent content analysis; after the 16th interview, no substantially new symptom-related themes emerged and the content became redundant, confirming that saturation had been reached. To comprehensively capture symptom experiences across different stages of postoperative recovery, each patient was interviewed at three time points: 3–5 days, 2 weeks, and 6 weeks post-surgery. Face-to-face interviews were conducted at the 3–5 days time point, while telephone interviews were carried out at 2 weeks and 6 weeks follow-ups. The interview guide (Supplementary file 1) was developed by the research team based on the research objectives. Following the interviews, content analysis was performed to organize and extract symptom-related information.

Delphi expert consultation

Selection of consultation experts

The expert selection criteria were as follows: (1) possession of a master’s degree or higher, or an intermediate professional title or above; (2) for clinical experts (orthopaedic surgeons and clinical nursing specialists): direct involvement in the clinical care, surgical treatment, or perioperative management of TKA patients, with a minimum of 5 years of relevant professional experience; (3) for academic experts (nursing managers and nursing educators): a minimum of 10 years of professional experience in nursing management or nursing education, with nursing managers possessing TKA-related management experience and nursing educators demonstrating established expertise in scale development or psychometric research.

Developing the expert questionnaire

The Delphi expert questionnaire (Supplementary file 2) was constructed by integrating findings from the literature review with results from semi-structured interviews.The expert consultation questionnaire comprised three components: a letter addressed to the experts, an importance rating scale for items in the symptom cluster assessment scale for patients following total knee arthroplasty, and a basic information survey of the experts. The letter to experts outlined the research background, objectives, significance, response deadline, and contact information for the lead expert coordinator. Importance ratings utilized a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 indicated “not necessary at all”, 2 “not necessary”, 3 “neutral”, 4 “necessary”, and 5 “very necessary”. Additionally, a remarks section and an option to indicate the need for additional items within each dimension were included. The second round of expert consultation added an item relevance score to evaluate the degree of consensus among experts regarding an item’s relevance. A 4-point Likert scale was employed: 1 = not relevant, 2 = weakly relevant, 3 = strongly relevant, 4 = highly relevant. Expert basic information survey form includes name, age, gender, educational background, professional title, field of expertise, years of experience, familiarity with the scale content, and basis for judgment.

Conducting expert consultations

Prior to expert consultation, the team leader contacted experts meeting the inclusion criteria, introduced the purpose and significance of this study, and obtained their consent. Following consent, expert consultation was conducted via email, WeChat, or in-person meetings, with experts requested to respond within 7 days whenever possible. After each round of expert consultation, the research team retained or deleted items based on the following criteria: (1) Item importance: retain items with an average importance score ≥ 3.5 and coefficient of variation ≤ 0.25 [22] .(2) Item relevance (newly added in the second round): the content validity index (I-CVI) for each item is calculated based on the proportion of experts who assigned a score of 3 or 4. The I-CVI threshold for item retention is set at ≥ 0.78. (3) Expert recommendations: for items with overlapping content or similar meanings, the research team integrates and optimizes the wording based on the semantic content and clinical significance of the items. For items where expert opinions differ, the research team reaches a decision through discussion. Based on these criteria, items were deleted, modified, or added to form the initial draft of the scale.

Preliminary survey

From November to December 2024, a convenience sampling method was employed to select 20 post-TKA patients from the orthopedic department of a tertiary Grade A hospital in Hebei Province for a preliminary survey.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Aged ≥ 40 years with a confirmed diagnosis of knee osteoarthritis according to the 2024 Chinese Diagnosis and Treatment Standards for Osteoarthritis [23], and having undergone total knee arthroplasty; (2) Adequate cognitive and communication abilities to understand the study requirements and independently complete the study questionnaires; and (3) voluntary participation with written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Presence of severe major organs dysfunction (e.g., cardiac, cerebral, hepatic, or renal failure), malignant tumors, or other serious systemic conditions that could interfere with symptom reporting or questionnaire completion; (2) History of stroke, severe neurological or psychiatric disorders, cancer-related symptoms, or other comorbidities and postoperative complications that could substantially confound symptom perception or alter rehabilitation outcomes.

After reviewing the draft questionnaire, patients are requested to evaluate its acceptability, linguistic accuracy, and ease of completion. Based on patient feedback, the research team will collectively revise and refine the document to develop the clinical trial version of the total knee arthroplasty postoperative symptom cluster assessment scale.

Formal survey

Participants

From January to June 2025, patients who underwent TKA were selected as subjects from the orthopedic departments of three tertiary general hospitals in Hebei Province, China. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were consistent with those of the preliminary survey. Sample Size Calculation: The clinical test version of this study’s questionnaire comprised 27 items. Exploratory factor analysis requires a sample size of 5–10 times the number of items, while confirmatory factor analysis recommends at least 200 cases or 10–20 times the number of items [24]. Accounting for a 10% non-response rate, the calculated sample size ranged from 369 to 497 cases.

Sampling method and bias control

A convenience sampling method was employed in this study to facilitate the recruitment of eligible patients within a limited timeframe. We used the following measures to enhance sample representativeness: (1) calculation and attainment of an adequate sample size based on the number of scale items to improve statistical robustness; (2) strict adherence to uniform inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure homogeneity of the study population; and (3) the three hospitals included in this study encompass both provincial-level and municipal-level hospitals, enabling a multicenter, multi-level research design that can cover a more diverse patient population.

Survey tools and methods

The formal survey employed a baseline questionnaire designed by the research team and the clinical trial version of the Symptom Cluster Assessment Scale for Total Knee Arthroplasty Patients. Considering the variations in rehabilitation stages and symptom manifestations across different postoperative periods, this study collected data at three time points : 3–5 days (T1), 2 weeks postoperatively (T2), and 6 weeks postoperatively (T3). Specifically, T1 primarily focuses on acute symptoms such as pain and swelling during hospitalization; T2 primarily addresses early home-related symptoms or newly emerging symptoms like fatigue and fear of activity; T3 primarily assesses functional recovery-related symptoms such as joint stiffness and limited mobility. By collecting data at these phase-matched time points, we can ensure that the item pool comprehensively covers symptoms following TKA, enabling the final scale to be applicable across different time points. At T1, an on-site survey method was employed. Researchers collected data uniformly across three hospitals. After explaining the study content and obtaining informed consent from patients, researchers distributed the scales for self-assessment. For patients unable to complete the forms independently, researchers administered the survey item by item and recorded responses. After completion, researchers reviewed each item to ensure no omissions. Patients and their families were informed about the T2 and T3 surveys, and contact information was recorded. T2 and T3 were conducted via telephone within a ± 3-day window, inquiring about symptoms during the preceding week and confirming key information through repetition. The same questionnaire was used at all time points.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the Ethics Committees of The Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Approval No.: 2024-R568), The Third Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Approval No.: Ke2025-019-1), and The Third Hospital of Shijiazhuang (Approval No.: (2025) Ethics Approval No. 003). All participants provided written informed consent. To ensure patient confidentiality, all collected data were anonymized by removing personal identifiers and stored securely in a fully de-identified format on password-protected hospital servers, accessible only to authorized research team members as needed. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Data entry was performed using Excel software. Item analysis, exploratory factor analysis, and reliability testing were conducted using SPSS 25.0 software. Confirmatory factor analysis was performed using AMOS 28.0 software. See Fig. 1 for the processing of the three time data sets.

Expert consultation

The level of expert engagement was assessed by the effective response rate of the questionnaires. The authority coefficient of experts (Cr) was calculated as the average of the judgment coefficient (Ca) and familiarity coefficient (Cs), expressed as Cr = (Ca + Cs)/2. The degree of consensus among expert opinions was evaluated using Kendall’s coefficient of concordance.

Item analysis

Based on relevant literature on scale development [25, 26], we formulated four item-deletion criteria. (1) Critical ratio method: Used to test item discrimination. Respondents were ranked by total scores and divided into high- and low-score groups using the 27% cutoff method. Independent samples t-tests were conducted for each item between the two groups. Items with P > 0.05 and a decision value < 3 were deleted. (2) Correlation coefficient method: Assesses the correlation between items and the total scale score. Items with statistically insignificant correlations (P > 0.05) or correlation coefficients r < 0.4 are deleted. (3) Reliability testing method: Evaluates the internal consistency of the scale. If deleting an item increases the Cronbach’s α coefficient, that item is removed. (4) Commonality and factor loadings: Evaluating entries with high representativeness and high contribution.Principal component analysis was applied, limiting the number of extracted factors to one. Items with commonality < 0.2 or factor loadings < 0.45 were removed. Items failing any single criterion are flagged, and those meeting three or more criteria are ultimately deleted based on comprehensive assessment.

Validity testing

Content validity

Content validity was assessed using the content validity index (CVI), encompassing the item-level CVI (I-CVI) and the average scale-level CVI (S-CVI/Ave) from the scale-level CVI. As described in Sect. 2.3.3, the I-CVI was calculated based on the second-round expert correlation scores. The S-CVI/Ave is the mean of all entries’ I-CVI values.Content validity was considered optimal when I-CVI ≥ 0.78 and S-CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90 [27].

Construct validity

The construct validity of the scale was assessed using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). EFA employed principal component analysis with an orthogonal rotation maximizing variance. Factor extraction criteria [28]: (1) Eigenvalue ≥ 1; (2) Includes at least 2 symptoms; (3) Factor loading ≥ 0.4; (4) Cumulative variance explained > 60%. Intrinsic correlation was assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient to evaluate correlations among scale dimensions and between dimensions and the total score. Confirmatory factor analysis employed the following fit indices: chi-square/degrees of freedom (χ²/df), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), comparative fit index (CFI), incremental fit index (IFI), goodness-of-fit index (GFI), and normality-free index (NFI). Due to poor initial model fit, we modified the model according to the following principles [29]: (1) Allowing error term correlations only between paired items with clear clinical or theoretical relevance; (2) Releasing only the 1–2 parameters with the highest correction indices at each iteration and re-evaluating the model.

Convergent validity and discrimination validity

Convergent validity was assessed using factor loadings, average variance extracted (AVE), and composite reliability (CR) [30]. When factor loadings > 0.5, AVE > 0.5, and CR > 0.7, the scale demonstrates good convergent validity. When correlations between dimensions are less than the square root of their corresponding AVE, the scale exhibits good discriminant validity.

Reliability testing

Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s α coefficient and split-half reliability [30].

Results

Results of the scale item pool development

A total of 4,932 articles were retrieved from the database search. After removing duplicates and screening titles, abstracts, and full texts, 43 studies (19 in English and 24 in Chinese) were ultimately included. These studies were synthesized to identify common postoperative symptoms following TKA, and symptom items from TKA-specific patient-reported outcome measures were also extracted to construct a preliminary item pool of 20 items. Subsequently, semi-structured interviews with 16 TKA patients identified an additional 18 symptoms. By integrating the findings from both the literature review and semi-structured interviews, the research team developed the final initial item pool consisting of 38 symptoms across six dimensions: (1) systemic symptoms, (2) functional symptoms, (3)surgery related symptoms, (4)gastrointestinal symptoms, (5)excretion-related symptoms, and (6)sleep-psychological symptoms (Supplementary file 2).

Preliminary survey results

Patients indicated they could understand most items and their descriptive definitions on the scale. However, when selecting severity levels for symptoms such as “decreased appetite” and “difficulty falling asleep”, they reported lacking clear criteria for judgment. To enhance the accuracy of questionnaire completion, researchers refined the descriptive definitions of relevant items by consulting relevant literature [31] and prior semi-structured interview findings. Following thorough discussion within the research team, reference standards were established for each severity level. The pre-survey involved no item deletions or additions, resulting in a clinical test version comprising 27 items (Supplementary file 4).

Item analysis results

Item analysis was conducted using the critical ratio method, correlation coefficient method, reliability testing, and commonality/factor loading analysis (Supplementary file 5, Tables S3–S6). According to the predefined screening criteria, five items (“fever”, “audible sounds during knee movement”, “itching”, “abdominal distension” and “constipation”) failed to meet the requirements in three analyses and were therefore removed. Ultimately, 22 items were retained for reliability and validity testing.

Validity test results

Content validity

The I-CVI ranged from 0.824 to 1.000, while the S-CVI/Ave was 0.948.

Construct validity

Prior to exploratory factor analysis, the correlation matrix among variables was examined to confirm data suitability for factor analysis (KMO = 0.884, Bartlett’s sphericity test F = 2519.210, P < 0.001). Principal component analysis was employed to extract common factors. Based on the criterion of eigenvalues > 1, five common factors were identified, accounting for a cumulative variance contribution of 68.176%. After rotation using the maximum variance method, the factor loadings for each item ranged from 0.427 to 0.899 (Table 4). The item “Fatigue” exhibited factor loadings ≥ 0.4 on both Factor 1 (activity limitation symptom cluster) and Factor 2 (psychological symptom cluster). However, given its characteristic as a common psychosomatic response post-surgery, it was ultimately assigned to Factor 2 (psychological symptom cluster). The final scale comprises five factors named: activity limitation symptom cluster, psychological symptom cluster, surgery-related symptom cluster, gastrointestinal symptom cluster, and sleep disorder symptom cluster. Factors are named based on the EFA and the clinical significance of the entry content.

Factor 1 comprises 7 items primarily reflecting impaired lower limb motor function, termed the “activity limitation symptom cluster”: joint stiffness, dull ache and fullness in the affected limb during walking, perception of unsteadiness, limited joint mobility, abnormal gait, swelling of the affected limb, and heaviness and weakness in the affected limb.

Factor 2 comprises 6 items, concentrating on negative emotions and energy depletion, termed the “psychological symptom cluster”: fatigue, pain or stinging sensation, depressed mood, irritability, feeling worried or anxious, and fear of movement.

Factor 3 comprises 4 items representing symptoms related to local surgical trauma, termed the “surgery-related symptom cluster”: tightness around the incision, numbness around the incision, warmth around the incision, and ecchymosis of the affected limb.

Factor 4 comprises two items representing typical, interrelated gastrointestinal adverse reactions often associated with postoperative medication or stress responses, termed the “gastrointestinal symptom cluster”: decreased appetite, nausea, vomiting.

Factor 5 includes two items representing postoperative sleep disturbances in patients, termed the “sleep disorder symptom cluster”: difficulty falling asleep, frequent nocturnal awakenings.

Intrinsic correlation tests revealed correlation coefficients between each dimension and the total scale score ranging from 0.527 to 0.849 (P < 0.01), while inter-dimensional correlations ranged from 0.217 to 0.615 (P < 0.01), as shown in Table 5.

Confirmatory factor analysis was employed to test the aforementioned five-factor structure in an independent sample (n = 266). Initial model fit indices were: χ2/df = 2.244, RMSEA = 0.069, GFI = 0.867, CFI = 0.910, NFI = 0.850, TLI = 0.895. The initial model is depicted in Fig. 2. Referencing common standards (χ2/df < 3.00, RMSEA < 0.080, CFI/IFI/GFI/NFI all > 0.900) [32], the initial model failed to meet the criteria for GFI, NFI, and TLI. To refine the model, we released a few pairs of clinically relevant error term covariances based on the modified indices and the principle of clinical interpretability. For instance, the error correlation between “limited joint mobility” and “abnormal gait” was released due to their direct causal relationship; similarly, the error correlation between “swelling of the affected limb” and “heaviness and weakness in the affected limb” was released because these are often assessed together by patients as concurrent somatic sensations. These modifications aimed to reflect known clinical associations between items not fully captured by the factor structure. The adjusted model (Fig. 3) demonstrated improved fit indices: χ2/df = 1.621, RMSEA = 0.048, GFI = 0.913, CFI = 0.960, NFI = 0.903, TLI = 0.948. All indices fell within acceptable ranges, indicating good data fit for the five-factor model.

Final version scale

The final version of the Total Knee Arthroplasty Postoperative Symptom Cluster Assessment Scale comprises 5 dimensions and 22 items (Supplementary file 6). Each symptom item is assessed across three dimensions: frequency, severity, and level of distress, using a 5-point Likert scale (0–4). If a symptom has a frequency of “none”, the item is directly scored as 0. The score for each item is the mean of the three dimension scores. The total scale score is the sum of all item scores, ranging from 0 to 88 points. A higher total score indicates a greater symptom burden for the patient.

According to postoperative duration and clinical relevance, symptoms were classified into transient/self-limiting symptoms and persistent symptoms. Transient/self-limiting symptoms mainly present in the early postoperative phase (≤ 2—4 weeks) and resolve spontaneously or with standard rehabilitation. Persistent symptoms are those that persist or fail to resolve after 4—6 weeks postoperatively and are linked to compromised functional recovery or diminished quality of life, indicating a need for targeted clinical intervention. Discussion

The most important finding of this study was the successful development and psychometric validation of a symptom cluster assessment scale for patients following TKA. The final scale comprised 5 dimensions and 22 items, encompassing activity limitation–related symptoms, surgery-related symptoms, gastrointestinal symptoms, psychological symptoms, and sleep disturbances. The results demonstrated that the scale possessed good content validity, construct validity, convergent validity, discriminant validity, internal consistency, and stability. These findings indicate that the scale can comprehensively capture the symptom burden and symptom cluster characteristics of patients after TKA, providing a reliable measurement tool for postoperative symptom assessment and rehabilitation management.

This study was theoretically grounded in the Symptom Experience Model and guided by the concept of PROs. The scale was developed through a rigorous, multi-phase process encompassing a literature review, semi-structured interviews, Delphi expert consultation, and psychometric validation, thereby ensuring both a solid theoretical foundation and robust clinical applicability. Compared with existing functional assessment instruments, this scale places greater emphasis on patients’ subjective postoperative symptom experiences rather than focusing solely on joint functional recovery. Exploratory factor analysis identified five factors, with a cumulative variance contribution rate of 68.176%, while confirmatory factor analysis demonstrated a satisfactory model fit, confirming a stable factor structure with adequate explanatory power. All reliability and validity indices met recommended psychometric standards, suggesting that the scale’s ability to accurately and comprehensively assess symptom burden in patients following TKA.

A key strength of this scale is its ability to address limitations of existing assessment tools. Commonly used instruments such as the KOOS and WOMAC primarily focus on pain, joint function, and daily activities and therefore may not fully capture patients’ overall postoperative symptom experience [33]. Similarly, single-symptom measures, including the VAS, TSK-11, and HADS, assess isolated symptoms but cannot reflect interactions among symptoms. In contrast, the present scale encompasses not only pain and stiffness but also local sensory disturbances, treatment-related adverse effects, psychological symptoms, and sleep disturbances, thereby reflecting the coexistence and interplay of multiple symptoms after TKA [34]. The psychological and sleep disturbance symptom clusters identified in this study are of particular clinical value. Ten Noever de Brauw et al. [35] demonstrated that psychological factors significantly influence patients’ subjective perceptions of surgical outcomes and postoperative satisfaction. Ding et al. [36] found that patients with lower central sensitization achieve better postoperative recovery, suggesting that pain perception is associated not only with local tissue injury but also with psychological status and central nervous system regulation. Collectively, these findings indicate that TKA recovery is a complex psychosomatic adaptation process rather than mere physical function reconstruction. Therefore, comprehensive assessment based on symptom clusters facilitates a more thorough and nuanced understanding of patients’ symptom experience.

Another important value of the scale lies in its potential to support risk stratification and individualized interventions. In recent years, a growing body of studies has focused on the early prediction of adverse postoperative outcomes following TKA. Strahl et al. [37] constructed a clinical risk score to predict patient dissatisfaction at one year after TKA and highlighted the vital role of early postoperative symptoms and patient-reported outcomes in prognostic assessment. The symptom cluster assessment scale developed herein allows dynamic evaluation of symptom burden across diverse postoperative periods, facilitating effective discrimination between transient self-limiting symptoms and persistent high-burden symptoms requiring clinical intervention. For example, swelling of the affected limb within 2–3 weeks postoperatively is a transient symptom that resolves spontaneously with limb elevation and physical therapy. If swelling persists beyond 4 weeks or is accompanied by exacerbated pain, it is classified as a high burden symptom requiring immediate clinical evaluation and intervention. Such stratification capacity can prevent the overconcentration of limited medical resources on self-limiting symptoms and assist healthcare professionals in formulating personalized management strategies, thereby improving both the efficiency and effectiveness of symptom management.

Furthermore, the scale may support multidisciplinary rehabilitation management. Hoevenaars et al. [38] demonstrated that multidisciplinary interventions significantly improve functional outcomes in patients with chronic pain after TKA. Postoperative symptoms following TKA encompass pain control, functional rehabilitation, psychological adjustment, and sleep management, which cannot be fully addressed by single-discipline interventions alone. This scale enables systematic evaluation of overall symptom burden, and provides a standardized reference for orthopaedic surgeons, therapists, and nurses to develop collaborative multidisciplinary intervention strategies, thereby optimizing patients’ rehabilitation outcomes.

As a patient-reported outcome measure, the scale is concise, easy to administer, and well-suited for both routine clinical practice and digital health management applications. It can be used for symptom monitoring, early risk identification, and remote follow-up. By dynamically tracking symptom changes over time, the scale may support optimized postoperative care, enhance patient engagement in self-management, and facilitate individualized symptom management across different stages of recovery. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to determine the minimum clinically important difference (MCID) and evaluate the scale’s responsiveness to symptom changes following targeted interventions [39].

Limitations

This study is subject to several limitations. Firstly, regarding generalizability, the sample was obtained from three hospitals of varying levels in northern China through convenience sampling. As a result, the findings may not be applicable to populations in southern China or in geographically remote regions. Secondly, regarding temporal validation, the cross-sectional design utilized in this study ensures the inclusion of all pertinent cases and facilitates the comparison of symptom presentations among different patients at a consistent postoperative time point. However, the evolving nature of symptom cluster composition over time limits the capacity of longitudinal assessments to sensitively and responsively track individual symptom trajectories and variations in scale scores within the same patient cohort. Thirdly, in the context of multi-center coordination, it is acknowledged that three hospitals of differing levels may demonstrate variations in their medical treatment procedures; however, these variations remain within the parameters of established guidelines. In addition, the Delphi expert panel was relatively small and consisted exclusively of experts from China, which may introduce selection and geographical bias and limit the international generalizability of the scale. Lastly, due to the absence of a gold standard for assessing postoperative symptoms following TKA, this study was unable to evaluate criterion-related validity. Future research may consider conducting prospective, multi-center, longitudinal cohort studies to validate the scale’s generalizability across different regions, assess its progression over time, perform stratified or subgroup analyses based on specific medical treatments, and provide additional validity evidence by examining its association with clinical outcomes.

Clinical relevance

The scale developed in this study provides clinicians with a comprehensive standardized tool for assessing postoperative symptom burden in patients undergoing TKA. By systematically identifying symptom clusters and differentiating transient, self-limiting symptoms from persistent high-burden symptoms, the scale can facilitate early risk stratification, guide individualized symptom management, and inform multidisciplinary rehabilitation planning. As a concise, patient-reported outcome measure, it is well-suited for integration into routine clinical workflows, enabling continuous monitoring of symptom trajectories, supporting timely clinical decision-making, and ultimately improving the quality of patient-centered postoperative care.

Conclusion

This study developed a symptom cluster assessment scale for TKA patients based on the Symptom Experience Model. Employing literature review, semi-structured interviews, and the Delphi expert consultation method, the scale’s items were sourced and developed following standardized procedures. Validated through rigorous testing, the scale demonstrates reliability and validity meeting psychometric standards, making it suitable for assessing symptoms in TKA patients. To integrate this scale into clinical practice and research, future studies may validate its longitudinal measurement properties and cross-cultural validity across various healthcare systems and contexts.
