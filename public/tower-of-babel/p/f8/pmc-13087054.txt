U-Trans: a foundation model for seismic waveform representation and enhanced downstream earthquake tasks
Authors: Omar M Saad, Yangkang Chen, Tariq Alkhalifah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarthquake monitoring systems (EQS) play a critical role in seismic hazard assessment and tectonic studies. A complete and accurate earthquake catalog improves our understanding of fault mechanisms and supports the development of strategies to enhance public safety and the resilience of infrastructure. We propose a foundation model to improve EQS performance through a two-stage framework. Stage 1 involves training the foundation model in a self-supervised manner using a U-Trans architecture, which combines a U-Net encoder–decoder structure with a compact convolutional transformer in the bottleneck layer. At this stage, the model learns to reconstruct corrupted seismic waveforms in both the time and frequency domains, enabling it to extract well-structured and informative features in the latent space. Stage 2 focuses on downstream earthquake tasks. The latent features extracted by the encoder are flattened and concatenated as an additional input channel to the downstream models, effectively guiding them toward better performance. The U-Trans network is trained on more than 2 million three-component seismograms collected from three open-source datasets, ensuring diverse coverage and robust feature extraction. Extensive testing demonstrates that incorporating the encoder’s latent features significantly improves the performance of several key downstream tasks, including seismic phase picking, earthquake location, magnitude estimation, and P-wave polarity classification. Analysis of the latent space reveals that the extracted features strongly correspond to P- and S-wave arrival times, which are crucial for many earthquake monitoring applications.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

The earthquake monitoring system (EQS) plays a critical role in the continuous detection and characterization of seismic events, serving as the backbone for constructing a comprehensive and reliable earthquake catalog1,2. This catalog captures essential information such as event origin time, location, magnitude, and seismic phase arrivals. A complete and accurate earthquake catalog is fundamental for various downstream applications, including seismic hazard assessment, earthquake forecasting, and geophysical and tectonic studies. Furthermore, high-resolution catalogs contribute to understanding fault dynamics, assessing regional seismicity, and informing public safety and infrastructure resilience strategies.

EQS is composed of several key components, including earthquake detection and seismic phase picking, phase association, magnitude estimation, event location, and P-wave polarity classification. The first objective of the EQS is to detect seismic events from continuous waveform data by identifying characteristic P- and S-wave arrivals. Once phase arrivals are picked, the system performs seismic phase association, linking picks from multiple stations that originate from the same seismic event. Based on the associated picks and their arrival times, the event’s hypocentral location (epicenter and depth) and origin time can be determined. Subsequently, the magnitude of the event is estimated using amplitude and/or duration-based metrics. Additional classification tasks, such as P-wave first-motion polarity analysis, may also be performed to support focal mechanism solutions. Each of these blocks contributes to the generation of a complete and accurate earthquake catalog, which is vital for seismic monitoring and scientific analysis.

In recent years, deep learning (DL) has emerged as a powerful tool to automatically extract significant features from raw input data, often leading to superior performance compared to traditional methods. Its ability to model complex, nonlinear relationships makes it particularly well suited for seismic data analysis. DL models have been extensively applied across all components of the EQS. In many cases, DL-based approaches have demonstrated significant improvements in accuracy, robustness, and generalization over classical signal processing techniques, particularly in challenging scenarios involving low signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) or large-scale datasets.

For seismic event detection and phase picking, several DL models have demonstrated robust and reliable performance. The SCALODEEP network3 successfully distinguishes between earthquake signals and seismic noise by leveraging time-frequency representations as input to a U-Net architecture. Trained on data from California, SCALODEEP provides a promising tool for real-time earthquake detection. Similarly, the Generalized Phase Detection (GPD) model4 employed a convolutional neural network (CNN) to classify seismic phases, also trained on the California seismic dataset, and has shown strong capability to detect P- and S-wave arrivals. In contrast, PhaseNet5, a sample-based model built on a U-Net architecture, was trained on Japanese seismic data and has shown high precision in picking P- and S-wave arrival times. Building on these ideas, EQTransformer6 introduced a transformer-based architecture that jointly performs earthquake detection and seismic phase picking. By leveraging the attention mechanism, EQTransformer effectively captures temporal dependencies and improves phase identification, particularly in complex waveforms. Furthermore, Tokuda and Nagao7 proposed a DL model that captures both global and local features by dividing the input waveform into sub-windows while also processing the full signal. This hybrid feature extraction strategy has shown promising results in improving the accuracy of seismic phase picking7. More recently, the Earthquake Convolutional Compact Transformer (EQCCT)8 was proposed as a powerful DL model for seismic phase picking. EQCCT incorporates a compact convolutional transformer architecture designed to model long-range dependencies between extracted features. It has shown a strong generalization capability across diverse datasets and seismic environments, highlighting its potential for deployment in global-scale monitoring systems9,10.

Earthquake location and magnitude estimation can be addressed separately or jointly, depending on the application and modeling approach. Several DL models based on CNNs, long-short-term memory networks (LSTMs), or their combination have been developed to predict earthquake magnitude directly from raw seismic waveforms11–15. These models take the waveform as input and output the estimated magnitude, offering fast and data-driven alternatives to traditional amplitude- or duration-based methods. In addition, transformer-based DL models have recently been introduced for real-time magnitude estimation16, leveraging self-attention mechanisms to capture long-range dependencies in the seismic signal more effectively than conventional CNNs.

Regarding earthquake location, several DL models have been proposed to estimate the hypocenter or epicentral location from waveform data. For example, temporal convolutional networks have been used to directly estimate the latitude and longitude of seismic events from waveform inputs17. Other approaches incorporate image-conditioned CNNs to map spatial waveform patterns to epicentral locations18, or use multi-head attention mechanisms to enhance the accuracy of hypocenter determination19. Additionally, a transformer-based model, known as EQ-ConvMixer20, has been proposed to estimate earthquake locations by predicting the spatial offset between seismic stations and event sources, demonstrating strong performance in modeling the source-receiver geometry.

Beyond separate magnitude or location estimation, some DL frameworks aim to jointly characterize both attributes. These multi-output models produce both magnitude and location predictions from shared waveform input, enabling simultaneous event characterization. Notable examples include CNN-based approaches21, deep graph neural networks22,23, deep neural networks24, and transformer-based architectures25. These models highlight the growing interest in end-to-end DL solutions that streamline seismic event characterization for real-time and large-scale applications.

Furthermore, classification of P-wave first-motion polarity is a critical task for determining the focal mechanism of an earthquake source. Accurate polarity classification enables the construction of focal mechanism solutions, which are essential to understand the orientation of faults and their slip behavior. To address this task, several deep learning models have been proposed, including CNN-based approaches26–28 and more recent transformer-based architectures29, which have demonstrated improved performance and generalization in diverse seismic settings.

Although deep learning models have demonstrated promising performance across various tasks within the EQS, most existing approaches are task-specific and often lack generalization. These models are typically trained on limited datasets tailored to individual tasks, which may hinder their performance when applied to different geographic regions or alternate tasks. This limitation emphasizes the need for more generalizable models capable of operating effectively across diverse seismic datasets and applications. A promising solution to this challenge is the development of a foundation model, a large-scale model trained on diverse seismic data to extract and retain high-level, transferable features from raw waveforms. The primary objective of this study is to learn a global latent representation of seismograms using the proposed foundation model. This latent representation is then incorporated as an additional input channel into downstream EQS models during training, providing complementary information that enhances their overall predictive performance.

Unlike existing foundation models for earthquake monitoring—such as SeismoLLM30, which adapts a GPT-2 architecture pretrained on natural language data and replaces selected layers before fine-tuning for seismic tasks, or SeisLM31, which pretrains a seismic model in a self-supervised manner and fine-tunes it using multiple task-specific output heads—our proposed framework adopts a different workflow for leveraging pretrained representations. Specifically, the pretrained encoder is used as a shared feature extractor whose latent representations are integrated into independent downstream models, enabling flexible task-specific adaptation while preserving the benefits of large-scale self-supervised seismic representation learning.

In existing foundation models such as SeisLM and SeismoLLM, the pretrained model is fine-tuned to jointly predict all earthquake-related parameters using multiple task-specific heads attached to the same foundation architecture. In contrast, our method decouples representation learning from task learning: the pretrained U-Trans foundation model serves as a universal feature extractor, and its latent representations are explicitly reused as auxiliary inputs to independent downstream models, alongside the raw seismic data. This design enables efficient transfer of learned representations while allowing downstream networks to remain fully task-specific and structurally independent.

Practically, the encoder of the U-Trans foundation model is attached to the input of each downstream network, and each downstream task (e.g., phase picking, earthquake location, or magnitude estimation) is trained independently with its own architecture and objective function. During downstream optimization, the U-Trans encoder is fine-tuned jointly with the downstream model to preserve transferable feature structure while allowing task-specific adaptation. In this way, the foundation model serves as a task-agnostic physical representation that supports multiple downstream tasks, rather than a single multi-head prediction model. Moreover, U-Trans is a native seismic foundation model trained directly on waveform physics, thereby avoiding the domain-shift risks associated with adapting language-based pretrained models to geophysical signals. The primary contribution of this work lies in advancing seismic representation learning through the development of a native seismic foundation model trained in a self-supervised manner on large-scale waveform data. Rather than introducing new downstream architectures, the proposed framework focuses on learning transferable latent representations that can be reused to enhance existing earthquake monitoring workflows.

Methodology

Principle: learned representations

Foundation models are machine learning models trained on large and diverse datasets to learn transferable representations that can be adapted to multiple downstream tasks32. In seismic applications, these learned representations can be reused or fine-tuned to improve performance across earthquake monitoring tasks such as phase picking, location, magnitude estimation, and polarity classification.

Recent studies have explored foundation-model approaches for seismic representation learning33–36. These approaches highlight the importance of large-scale training data for learning robust and transferable waveform representations that generalize across different seismic conditions.

To address this limitation, we propose a foundation model designed to extract global features from three-component waveform data, with the goal of improving downstream earthquake tasks such as seismic phase picking, earthquake location, magnitude estimation, and P-polarity classification. Architecture: U-Trans

The proposed foundation model for feature extraction adopts a U-Trans architecture. It is based on a standard U-Net with five encoder and five decoder blocks; however, a CCT network is attached to the bottleneck layer to enhance the model’s ability to capture and highlight the most significant features extracted by the encoder. The input data has a shape of , representing 6000 time samples across three components. To enhance robustness and promote generalization, we employ a two-stage data corruption strategy. First, the input is transformed into the frequency domain using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), and up to 30% of the frequency components are randomly dropped. The data is then reconstructed using the inverse FFT, and we apply random temporal dropout by removing up to 30% of the time-domain samples. This dual-domain dropout strategy enables the model to learn more robust representations by forcing it to recover from missing information in both the time and frequency domains. The dual-domain corruption strategy is designed to improve representation robustness by encouraging the encoder to recover physically consistent waveform features from incomplete observations, thereby enhancing generalization and downstream task performance rather than modeling any specific noise scenario. An example of the corruption process applied to the three-channel waveforms for training the foundation U-Trans model. The corruption involves removing a portion of the frequency components and time-domain samples from the waveforms, requiring the U-Trans model to reconstruct the missing information.

Figure 2 shows the proposed U-Trans architecture. Each encoder block consists of two 1D convolutional layers, followed by a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function, and a max pooling layer to reduce the data dimensionality. In contrast, each decoder block contains three 1D convolutional layers, with a 1D upsampling layer preceding the first convolutional layer to restore the temporal resolution. Features extracted from the encoder blocks are concatenated with the corresponding features from the decoder blocks through skip connections. Specifically, the encoder features are concatenated after the first 1D convolutional layer in each decoder block, enabling the model to retain and utilize fine-grained features that may have been lost during the downsampling process. The number of feature maps in the encoder blocks increases progressively as follows: 5, 10, 20, 40, and 80. The decoder blocks use the reverse order: 80, 40, 20, 10, and 5 feature maps. The bottleneck layer between the encoder and decoder has 80 feature maps. The pooling rates for the five encoder blocks are 2, 2, 2, 2, and 5, respectively, resulting in a feature map length of 75 samples at the bottleneck layer. All 1D convolutional layers use a kernel size of 3. These architectural parameters, including the number of feature maps and pooling rates, were determined empirically through controlled trial-and-error experiments using a representative subset of the training data. Increasing the number of parameters beyond the selected configuration did not yield measurable performance improvements, while shallower configurations or fewer feature maps resulted in degraded reconstruction performance. This empirical tuning ensured an effective balance between representation capacity, model efficiency, and generalization, while avoiding unnecessary model complexity.

The + and symbols stand for point-wise addition and multiplication, respectively. During pretraining, the decoder reconstructs corrupted inputs as part of the self-supervised objective. For downstream tasks, task-specific networks replace the reconstruction decoder and operate on the pretrained encoder representations..

The CCT is placed as the bottleneck layer of the U-Trans architecture. It consists of four 1D convolutional blocks followed by four transformer encoder layers. The core component of the transformer encoder is the multi-head attention (MHA) mechanism, which assigns high attention scores to important features and lower scores to less relevant ones40. The MHA module is composed of four standard self-attention (SSA) blocks. The outputs of these blocks are concatenated to form the final representation of the MHA. Each SSA block operates by generating three vectors—value, key, and query—using fully connected layers. The attention weights are computed as the dot product between the query and key vectors, followed by a Softmax activation to normalize the scores. These attention weights are then applied to the value vector. This attention mechanism enables the model to effectively capture long-range dependencies within the sequence, thereby enhancing its ability to restore missing samples and extract significant features. Additionally, each MHA layer incorporates two stochastic depth dropout (SDD) layers to regularize training and reduce overfitting41. A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) is also employed to project the output features into a higher-dimensional space for enhanced representational capacity. Furthermore, two normalization layers are integrated to stabilize training and improve learning ability by normalizing intermediate feature maps.

Downstream applications

In this study, the term “downstream” refers specifically to task-specific deep learning models that operate on latent representations extracted by the pretrained foundation model, such as phase picking, location, magnitude estimation, and polarity classification. The features extracted from the CCT network are treated as the stored representations from the foundation model and are utilized in downstream earthquake tasks. However, we encountered a challenge when directly applying these features to certain downstream models. Specifically, some deep learning architectures, such as EQConvMixer20, a model designed for earthquake location, require long input sequences to operate effectively due to their reliance on depth-wise convolutional layers with large kernel sizes (e.g., kernel size 15). When the input window is shortened, with or without the foundation model, these models fail to perform reliably. This issue is likely to arise in other deep learning models as well.

To ensure the generalization capability of our foundation model across various downstream tasks, we propose a strategy that integrates the extracted features as an additional input channel. Specifically, features from the CCT network are first passed through three Conv blocks to reshape them into a tensor of size , as shown in Fig. 3. These Conv blocks, trained jointly with each downstream task, act as a transformation module that reshapes the latent representation of the foundation model. The resulting feature map is then flattened to a 1D shape of and concatenated with the original input waveforms as a fourth channel.

Utilization of the stored features from the U-Trans foundation model for downstream earthquake tasks. The parameters of the U-Trans model are fine-tuned, while three convolutional blocks are jointly trained with the downstream tasks to adapt the extracted features for task-specific objectives. The output of the convolutional blocks is flattened and concatenated with the original three-channel waveform input as an additional channel, resulting in a new input shape of for the downstream deep learning models.

This approach allows the downstream models to benefit from the high-level representations learned by the foundation model. By concatenating these informative features directly into the input, we guide the model toward more accurate learning. Additionally, the pre-trained foundation model is fine-tuned jointly with each downstream task to adapt to task-specific variations and further enhance performance. Figure 3 shows the framework of the proposed foundation model and its integration into downstream tasks.

The foundation model is pretrained using a fixed input window of 6000 samples, producing a compact latent representation with a temporal length of 75 samples. For downstream tasks, variable-length waveforms are handled by task-specific convolutional (Conv) blocks that rescale the latent features before flattening and concatenation with the downstream input, as shown in Fig. 3, by controlling the number of feature maps. These blocks are trained from scratch for each downstream task, allowing flexible adaptation to different window lengths and real-time monitoring constraints without modifying the pretrained architecture. The foundation encoder provides pretrained latent representations that can be reused directly or further fine-tuned during downstream training, depending on the task configuration.

Results

Self-supervised training and latent feature extraction

The proposed foundation model has two stages. Stage 1 employs a self-supervised learning scheme in which the network is trained to reconstruct seismic data from its corrupted version. Stage 2 focuses on fine-tuning the pre-trained foundation model for downstream earthquake tasks. The pretrained encoder learns general waveform representations in a self-supervised manner, while downstream fine-tuning adapts these representations to task-specific objectives without replacing the underlying transferable feature structure. To develop a comprehensive and diverse training set, we combine three open-source seismic datasets: STEAD37, TXED38, and the Instance dataset39, resulting in approximately 2.5 million three-component seismograms, each 60 seconds long, including both earthquake and noise waveforms. The combined dataset is divided into 80% for training, 5% for validation, and 15% for testing.

During training, we introduce corruption to the seismic traces in both the time and frequency domains and apply a data augmentation strategy to enhance the model’s generalization capability. The augmentation strategy include shifting seismic events within the 60-second window, adding Gaussian noise, and superimposing multiple seismic events within the same window. The model is optimized using the Adam optimizer with the mean absolute error (MAE) as the loss function, which measures the discrepancy between the ground-truth label and the reconstructed output. An early stopping criterion is applied, terminating training if the validation loss does not decrease for five consecutive epochs.

Figure 4 shows the training and validation loss curves, illustrating the model’s convergence behavior. After training, the pre-trained model is evaluated on a test set that resembles the data used in the downstream EQ tasks, without any time or frequency corruption. The model achieves an MAE of 0.392 on the training set, 0.384 on the validation set, and 0.352 on the test set, indicating stable convergence and good generalization performance. To analyze the features extracted by the model and their applicability to downstream tasks, we examine the test set after training the foundation model. Since downstream tasks use uncorrupted input data, Figs. 5 and 6 show representative test examples on the uncorrupted test set. Figure 5 shows the reconstructed seismograms of the test set alongside the corresponding ground-truth waveforms and residuals, revealing a minimal reconstruction error. These results confirm that the pre-trained model effectively captures latent features for representing and reconstructing seismograms.

Nevertheless, a slight increase in amplitude can be observed in the reconstructed waveforms compared to the ground truth. This behavior is largely attributable to the training strategy, in which the network attempts to recover the missing frequency content of the input data. When the network is trained using both time- and frequency-domain representations, it tends to reconstruct the magnitude spectrum more accurately. Consequently, the amplitudes of the dominant features, such as the P- and S-wave arrivals, can be slightly exaggerated in the reconstructed waveforms. This phenomenon reflects the network’s emphasis on features that contribute most strongly to the loss function. Importantly, this amplitude exaggeration is associated with the decoder reconstruction process and does not directly affect downstream magnitude estimation, which relies exclusively on encoder latent representations rather than reconstructed waveforms. During downstream training, the encoder is further fine-tuned jointly with the magnitude estimation network, allowing amplitude-related feature scaling to be properly adapted for the target task.

Figure 6 shows examples of the latent features extracted by the pre-trained model when applied to the uncorrupted test set. For clarity, only the vertical component is shown, along with representative latent features from the encoder corresponding to the input waveforms. The latent features highlight earthquake signals, exhibiting distinct patterns and amplitude variations near the P- and S-wave arrival times in the latent space, as indicated by colored boxes in Fig. 6. This allows the network to accurately reconstruct seismic events. In contrast, the latent representation of purely noisy waveforms reveals no distinct patterns, indicating that the network prioritizes meaningful seismic features over noise. This analysis suggests that the pre-trained model successfully learns to encode key features corresponding to P- and S-wave arrivals into its latent representation, treating them as the most significant elements of the input. Such a feature provides valuable information for downstream EQ tasks and is expected to enhance their overall performance.

Although the reconstructed waveforms exhibit slight amplitude exaggeration, as shown in Fig. 5, this does not affect the downstream tasks considered in this study, as only the encoder of the foundation model is used during inference and the decoder is discarded. The encoder primarily captures task-relevant temporal and structural features, particularly those associated with P- and S-wave arrivals (Fig. 6), while absolute amplitudes adjusted during the downstream fine-tuning.

Downstream earthquake tasks

For downstream earthquake-related tasks, we flatten the features extracted by the encoder of the proposed foundation model and concatenate them with the original three-component seismograms, forming an additional input channel for any downstream model. During the training of these downstream models, the encoder of the foundation model is fine-tuned jointly with the downstream network, allowing the latent features to further adapt to the specific task. In addition, the Conv blocks shown in Fig. 3 are trained together with the downstream task to further refine the latent features. These blocks act as a decoder network, transforming the latent representation of the foundation model into a more task-relevant form and enhancing the performance of the downstream tasks. The latent representation learned and stored by the foundation model serves as a global feature of seismic waveforms, guiding the downstream models toward more effective feature extraction and ultimately improving their predictive performance. Importantly, the extracted features can be interpreted as generalizable representations that are not limited to the training distribution of the foundation model. These representations can be combined with three-component seismograms from diverse geographic regions, including previously unseen areas, thereby promoting improved generalization, transferability, and robustness across a wide range of earthquake-related tasks.

We use two datasets to evaluate the impact of the foundation model on downstream EQ tasks. The first data set is extracted from the California region and is considered in distribution, as it originates from the STEAD data set used in stage 1 pretraining. We extracted 358,584 three-component seismograms. The second dataset is from the Tohoku region of Japan and serves as an out-of-distribution test set, since it was not included in the Stage 1 training data. During 2024, we collected 59,309 60-second three-component seismograms recorded by 57 seismic stations in the Hi-net seismic network, each containing S-wave arrivals as listed in the seismic catalog42,43. The events have depths ranging from 0.1 km to 212.31 km and local magnitudes () ranging from -0.8 to 4.7.

For the downstream tasks, we concatenate the latent features extracted from the encoder with the three-component input data before feeding them into the task-specific network. To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed foundation model, we use benchmark deep learning models for various earthquake-related tasks and compare their performance with and without the inclusion of the encoder’s latent features. During fine-tuning, the encoder’s parameters are updated jointly with those of the downstream task network to adapt the latent representations.

Seismic phase picking

For the seismic phase-picking task, we use EQCCT8 as the benchmark deep learning model and evaluate its performance with and without the inclusion of the latent representations extracted from the encoder of the foundation model. We conduct two experiments: the first using the California dataset and the second using the Japan dataset. For both datasets, we split the data into 80% for training, 5% for validation, and 15% for testing. To further challenge EQCCT and better assess the benefit of the foundation model, we use only 10% of the available California training data (i.e., 2,868 three-component seismograms) to train EQCCT. Both versions of EQCCT—with and without the inclusion of the latent representation—are trained for 50 epochs. The best-performing model, selected based on the lowest validation loss, is then used to pick seismic phases from the test set.

Note that the phase labels used as targets for the deep learning model are derived from manual picks provided in the original dataset. The seismic data are randomly split into training, validation, and test sets at the waveform level, allowing event- and station-level overlap across splits, consistent with the original benchmark protocols and baseline model training procedures, while ensuring that no duplicated waveform segments exist between sets. The foundation model is pretrained in a self-supervised manner without using phase labels, and its latent representations are incorporated only during downstream training. Importantly, the same training, validation, and test splits, as well as identical training configurations, are used for both the original EQCCT and the EQCCT augmented with foundation-model latent representations. Because both the baseline and foundation-augmented models use identical data splits and training protocols, any overlap effects are consistent across models. Therefore, the observed performance improvements reflect the contribution of the pretrained representations rather than differences in experimental setup or data partitioning.

Earthquake location

To assess the impact of the foundation model on earthquake location, we employ EQConvMixer20 as a benchmark deep learning model to predict event hypocenters using the California and Japan datasets. EQConvMixer predicts the relative latitude and longitude of the event epicenter with respect to the station, as well as the event depth, from the input waveforms. From both datasets, we select seismic events with known source locations, yielding 298,376 three-component waveforms for California and 29,903 for Japan. EQConvMixer is trained both with and without the inclusion of the latent features extracted by the foundation model’s encoder, and the resulting pre-trained models are evaluated on the respective test sets. Figure 8 shows the error histograms for event distance and depth across both datasets.

For the California test set, integrating the latent features from the foundation model significantly reduces the MAE of the event distance from 4.12 km to 3.31 km and the event depth from 2.07 km to 2.01 km. Furthermore, the standard deviation of the Location errors is consistently lower when the foundation model is incorporated.

For the Japan dataset, the task is more challenging due to the wider spatial distribution of events and the limited coverage in the training data set, which restricts the model’s ability to generalize. Nevertheless, including the foundation model improves performance, reducing the MAE from 11.17 km to 10.22 km for distance and from 7.62 km to 5.83 km for depth. Across all error histograms in Fig. 8, we observe a higher concentration of errors near zero when EQConvMixer is trained with the features of the foundation model, highlighting the ability of the foundation model to improve Location accuracy by providing latent representations relevant to the task.

Compared with other downstream tasks, earthquake location benefits less from improved waveform representations because location accuracy is primarily governed by station geometry and spatial coverage—factors that cannot be fully compensated for by representation learning alone. The larger performance gains observed for the California dataset likely reflect the higher coherence of the training data and the more favorable station distribution, which enable the downstream location model to exploit the pretrained latent representations more effectively.

Magnitude estimation

For magnitude estimation, we use a deep learning model, EQViT, as a benchmark method. The same datasets used for earthquake location are employed to train EQViT with and without the inclusion of the foundation model features. The pre-trained model is then applied to the California and Japan test sets.

Figure 9 shows a histogram of EQViT errors on both test sets, as well as scatter plots comparing predictions against ground-truth magnitudes. Incorporating the foundation model features improves EQViT’s performance, as evidenced by a decrease in the MAE from 0.142 to 0.138 for the California test set and from 0.209 to 0.179 for the Japan test set. The scatter plots demonstrate better alignment with the true magnitudes when the foundation model features are included. Standard deviation errors also decrease when the foundation model framework is integrated. Furthermore, for earthquakes with a magnitude greater than , the MAE decreases from 0.58 to 0.56 and from 0.47 to 0.45 for the California and Japan test sets, respectively. These results indicate that including the foundation model features enhances the accuracy and robustness of magnitude estimation.

P-wave polarity classification

We further evaluate the impact of the proposed foundation model on P-wave polarity classification. For this task, we utilize the TXED dataset, which includes manually labeled P-polarity (up or down). The dataset consists of 23,334 waveforms, split into 85% for training, 5% for validation, and 10% for testing. The benchmark model is EQPolarity29, which is trained both with and without the inclusion of features extracted from the foundation model, and then applied to the test set. Incorporating the foundation model features improves the test accuracy from 94.01% to 95.85%. Figure 10 shows the confusion matrices for EQPolarity with and without the foundation model features. As is evident, integrating the latent features enhances true positive, true negative, false positive, and false negative rates, highlighting the significant impact of the foundation model on downstream EQ tasks.

Discussion

Interpretation of extracted features from the foundation model

Stage 1 of the proposed foundation model, trained in a self-supervised manner, aims to extract the most significant features from the input waveforms in the latent space, enabling the decoder to effectively reconstruct the input data. As shown in Fig. 6, the foundation model highlights the P- and S-wave arrival times as the most significant information within seismic event waveforms. This behavior is particularly beneficial for downstream earthquake-related tasks, as it can enhance earthquake detection and seismic phase picking performance. Moreover, accurately capturing the location of P- and S-wave arrivals within the input waveforms is essential for downstream tasks such as earthquake location and magnitude estimation, since the S–P time difference is a key parameter for these applications.

To further assess the quality of the features learned by the foundation model, we apply t-SNE44 to reduce the dimensionality of 10,000 randomly selected samples from the test set. The resulting two-dimensional embeddings are shown in Fig. 11. We observe that features corresponding to noise waveforms are grouped closely together, forming a compact cluster, while features corresponding to earthquake waveforms form distinct, well-separated clusters. To quantitatively validate this observation, we perform K-means clustering on the t-SNE embeddings and obtain an F1-score of 89.86% for distinguishing noise from earthquake waveforms. These results demonstrate that the foundation model produces discriminative and well-structured latent representations, which effectively capture the essential characteristics of the input waveforms and provide valuable guidance for downstream earthquake-related tasks.

t-SNE visualization of latent representations extracted from the pretrained foundation encoder using 10,000 randomly selected waveform samples from the test set. Each point represents a waveform, colored according to its true class label (noise or earthquake). The clear separation between clusters suggests that the pretrained encoder learns discriminative latent representations that effectively distinguish seismic signals from noise.

Training the foundation model with a smaller dataset

To further evaluate the proposed foundation model and its capability to learn from limited data, we trained the U-Trans model in a self-supervised manner using only the TXED dataset, which contains 519,689 waveforms. After training, the pre-trained model was incorporated with EQCCT and fine-tuned for seismic phase picking on a small subset of the California dataset (10% of the original training data), which is considered out-of-distribution data. Figure 12 shows the histogram errors for P- and S-wave arrivals on the California dataset using EQCCT with and without the features extracted from the foundation model trained on TXED. We observe that both the MAE and standard deviation for P- and S-wave arrival times decrease when incorporating the latent representations extracted from the TXED-trained foundation model. Additionally, the number of pick errors near zero increases, demonstrating that the extracted features effectively improve EQCCT’s seismic phase picking performance. These results highlight the ability of the proposed foundation model to be trained on a relatively small dataset and then applied to out-of-distribution data, while still improving the performance of downstream earthquake tasks trained on limited data. This indicates the model’s strong generalization capability and its potential to enhance earthquake monitoring even in data-scarce regions.

Histogram errors for P- and S-wave arrival times on the California and Japan test sets, comparing EQCCT predictions with and without the inclusion of latent features extracted from the foundation model trained on the TXED dataset.

Evaluating downstream earthquake tasks with a frozen foundation model

All U-Trans encoder parameters are fine-tuned jointly with the downstream network, while task-specific adaptation layers are trained from scratch using a shared learning rate. To further assess the influence of the proposed foundation model, we conducted downstream task evaluations using the Japan dataset while selectively freezing the shallow layers of the U-Trans network. Specifically, all parameters from the input layer up to the second transformer encoder block were frozen, preventing them from being updated during training, while the remaining deeper layers were fine-tuned. This experiment allows us to investigate the relative contribution of different portions of the U-Trans network to the downstream tasks and to determine whether the model can still provide performance gains when only part of its parameters are trainable. Figure 13 shows the results for the downstream models integrated with the foundation model under this partial fine-tuning scheme. As observed, the performance of the downstream tasks shows a slight degradation compared to the fully fine-tuned encoder of the foundation model reported in the Results section, but it still outperforms models that do not utilize features from the foundation model. These findings suggest that even when the shallow layers are frozen, the latent features extracted by the deeper layers of the foundation model continue to provide valuable guidance for downstream tasks. More research is warranted to identify which specific blocks of the U-Trans network contribute most to the improvement of downstream tasks, with such contributions maybe task specific, which could enable a more efficient fine-tuning strategy that minimizes training time and computational cost while maintaining high performance.

Downstream earthquake task performance using a partial fine-tuning strategy, where the parameters of the shallow layers of the U-Trans network are frozen..

Fine-tuning the foundation model with smaller dataset

As demonstrated in the Results section, the proposed foundation model can be fine-tuned during the training of downstream earthquake models using a small dataset. For example, the phase-picking model was trained using only 2,868 seismic waveforms, demonstrating that the latent features extracted by the foundation model can significantly enhance the performance of downstream models, as summarized in Table 1.

To further demonstrate the effectiveness of the foundation model with limited data, we used the foundation model trained on the combined three datasets and fine-tuned it for the P-polarity classification task using only 1% of the available dataset. Figure 14 shows the confusion metrics for the EqPolarity model with and without the inclusion of latent features from the foundation model, trained on 1% of the Texas dataset. Incorporating the latent features stored by the foundation model improves performance, increasing the F1-score from 92.11% to 93.23 %.

Confusion matrices for EQPolarity with and without the inclusion of foundation model features, evaluated on the TXED dataset after training with only 1% of the Texas dataset.

These results indicate that the foundation model can enhance downstream model performance even when the available training dataset is small, particularly for tasks such as seismic phase picking and P-polarity classification. In contrast, tasks such as earthquake location and magnitude estimation remain challenging with limited data. The performance of these tasks strongly depends on the distribution of epicenter locations and the magnitude distribution, especially for larger events. Therefore, providing well-distributed training data is crucial for these downstream models to achieve robust and reliable performance.

Effects of self-supervised pretraining, CCT representation, and computational cost

For the training phase of P-wave arrival picking on the Japanese seismic dataset, incorporating the foundation encoder into EQCCT increases the per-epoch training time from 190 s to 270 s and the number of trainable parameters from approximately 200 k to 1.5 M, reflecting the additional representational capacity introduced by the foundation model.

However, for near–real-time deployment, inference efficiency is of primary consideration. During inference on a 60 s waveform window, the baseline EQCCT model requires 0.11 s, whereas the foundation-augmented model requires 0.13 s, indicating only a marginal increase in runtime. This indicates that, although the foundation model introduces additional parameters, the inference-time overhead remains minimal and does not hinder real-time applicability. All timing was obtained using an NVIDIA Quadro RTX 8000 GPU.

Furthermore, we trained the EQCCT model augmented with the foundation encoder without pretraining, in which all encoder and downstream parameters were randomly initialized, and applied it to P-wave arrival time picking using the Japanese seismic dataset. Under this configuration, for the test set, the model achieved a precision of 94.68%, a recall of 94.66%, an F1-score of 94.67%, a standard deviation of 0.058 s, and an MAE of 0.032 s.

Compared with the results reported in Table 1, this configuration performs only marginally better than the baseline EQCCT model, indicating that architectural augmentation alone provides limited performance gains. In contrast, the same architecture shows improved performance when the foundation encoder is pretrained in a self-supervised manner and subsequently fine-tuned under the same data constraints. This direct comparison suggests that the primary improvements in data-scarce settings stem from self-supervised pretraining rather than from increased model capacity or architectural modifications alone. In particular, pretraining enables the encoder to learn global waveform representations that are more effective for downstream tasks than features learned solely through task-specific supervision.

Furthermore, to explicitly isolate the contribution of the CCT module, we conducted an additional ablation experiment. Specifically, latent features were extracted from the foundation encoder before the CCT module. These features were then concatenated with EQCCT for P-wave arrival time picking using the Japanese seismic dataset. This configuration effectively removes the global contextual modeling introduced by the CCT, while preserving the high-resolution information provided by the U-NET.

Under this setting, the model achieved a precision of 94.89%, a recall of 94.84%, an F1-score of 94.87%, a standard deviation of 0.054 s, and an MAE of 0.029 s on the test set. Although this performance is comparable to the baseline results reported in Table 1, it is consistently inferior to the configuration that leverages latent features extracted after the CCT module. These results indicate that the global contextual representations learned by the CCT play an important role in achieving the full performance gains observed in the proposed framework.

Conclusion

We have proposed a self-supervised foundation model designed to extract well-represented features from seismograms by reconstructing the input data from time- and frequency-corrupted versions. Our analysis reveals that the extracted features primarily highlight the most significant components of the waveform, specifically the P- and S-wave arrival times, which are essential for downstream earthquake monitoring tasks. By leveraging more than 2 million three-component seismograms during training, the foundation model successfully learns robust latent representations that significantly improve the performance of seismic phase picking, earthquake location, magnitude estimation, and P-wave polarity classification. These latent features are flattened and concatenated as an additional input channel to downstream deep learning models, effectively guiding them toward better performance. Our evaluation on datasets both within and outside the training distribution confirms the strong generalization ability of the proposed foundation model and its ability to enhance downstream tasks across diverse testing scenarios. Future work will focus on further improving the learning capacity of the foundation model by exploring alternative training strategies, including systematic investigation of different corruption schemes and corruption strengths, experimenting with more advanced network architectures beyond U-Trans, and expanding the size and diversity of the training dataset. These efforts aim to further improve the model’s generalization ability and its effectiveness across an even broader range of earthquake monitoring applications.
