Association of pre-and postnatal mercury exposure with cardiometabolic risk factors in children: the role of the telomere length and telomerase reverse transcriptase polymorphisms

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Mercury (Hg) is a toxic heavy metal commonly found in the environment. While its neurotoxic effects are well-known, there is growing recognition of its potential role in contributing to cardiometabolic risk factors. Specifically, exposure to methylmercury (MeHg), the most toxic organic form of mercury, may worsen key risk factors for cardiometabolic risk factors (Genchi et al., 2017, Güil-Oumrait et al., 2024, Hu et al., 2021). Metabolic Syndrome (MetS) refers to a combination of specific cardiometabolic factors that elevate the likelihood of developing cardiovascular disease (CVD) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) (Alberti et al., 2009). These factors include central obesity, high blood pressure, insulin resistance, and abnormal lipid levels, affecting approximately one in four adults worldwide (Saklayen, 2018). Notably, MetS in childhood has been shown to be a strong predictor of adult MetS and T2D (DeBoer et al., 2015, Morrison et al., 2008).

The primary source of human exposure to MeHg is the consumption of contaminated fish and seafood, which, while rich in omega-3 fatty acids with cardioprotective benefits, may pose cardiometabolic risks due to Hg content (Stern, 2005). This duality complicates dietary recommendations, especially in populations with high fish consumption.

MeHg is concerning due to its ability to bioaccumulate in tissues and exert toxic effects through multiple mechanisms. It disrupts antioxidant defences by depleting key enzymes like glutathione peroxidase, leading to elevated oxidative stress and cellular damage (Maqbool et al., 2019). This oxidative imbalance promotes lipid peroxidation, which impairs membrane integrity and contributes to vascular inflammation, a well-known precursor to atherosclerosis. Additionally, MeHg interferes with glucose metabolism by impairing insulin signalling pathways, increasing insulin resistance, and disrupting pancreatic β-cell function (Pan et al., 2024). Such metabolic disturbances not only heighten the risk of T2D but can also contribute to weight gain and abnormal fat redistribution, particularly visceral adiposity (Park et al., 2017). These outcomes are critical anthropometric indicators that correlate strongly with elevated cardiometabolic risk (Jayawardene et al., 2017). The cumulative impact of MeHg exposure on metabolic and inflammatory pathways suggests its role as a significant environmental risk factor for MetS (Hu et al., 2021).

Both the prenatal and early childhood periods are critical windows during which exposure to MeHg can have significant cardiometabolic consequences. Recent studies have demonstrated that prenatal MeHg exposure is associated with decreased heart rate variability in children, indicating reduced parasympathetic modulation of cardiac autonomic function and potential disruptions in cardiovascular regulation (Chan et al., 2021). This diminished heart rate variability is often considered an early marker of increased cardiometabolic risk, as it reflects imbalances in the autonomic nervous system, which plays a crucial role in maintaining cardiac health (Valera et al., 2011). Additionally, childhood MeHg exposure has been linked to alterations in blood pressure (Gallego-Viñas et al., 2019), with evidence suggesting that early life stages are particularly susceptible to MeHg cardiometabolic effects. Elevated blood pressure in childhood can track into adulthood, increasing the risk of hypertension later in life (Farzan et al., 2021). Furthermore, higher MeHg exposure during pregnancy has been associated with a poorer metabolic profile in children, characterized by insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia, and central adiposity, factors that contribute to an increased cardiometabolic risk (Stratakis et al., 2020).

Telomere length (TL), a marker of cellular aging and oxidative stress, has emerged as a potential mediator of MeHg toxic effects. Since MeHg exposure induces oxidative stress by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), it may accelerates telomere attrition, causing a loss of cellular proliferative capacity and enhancing the risk of various diseases, including cardiometabolic conditions (Hu et al., 2021). Also, as telomeres shorten, cells become increasingly vulnerable to oxidative damage. This oxidative damage further exacerbates dysregulation of cellular mechanisms that manage inflammation and metabolism, which are critical in the development of hypertension, insulin resistance, and dyslipidaemia (Gavia-García et al., 2021, Heba et al., 2021, Sagris et al., 2022). Also, shortened telomeres have been associated with insulin resistance, inflammation, and hypertension (Gao et al., 2020, Gavia-García et al., 2021, Zhang et al., 2020, Zhang et al., 2022). However, also higher MeHg concentrations have also been associated with longer TL, potentially as a response mechanism to the oxidative stress in early childhood (Lozano et al., 2024, Stajnko et al., 2025). It should also be noted that polymorphisms in the TERT gene, which encodes the telomerase reverse transcriptase responsible for maintaining TL integrity, can significantly affect telomere dynamics, leading to either accelerated telomere shortening or extended telomere maintenance (Gavia-García et al., 2021, Heidenreich and Kumar, 2017). Thus, the presence of TERT gene polymorphisms can play a crucial role in modulating the impact of Hg exposure on TL and, by extension, influence the health outcomes associated with environmental MeHg exposure. Also, CLPTM1L (Cleft Lip and Palate Transmembrane Protein 1-Like) gene is located in close proximity and its polymorphisms have been studied because they may act as regulatory variants that influence TERT expression (McKay et al., 2008, Rafnar et al., 2009). Polymorphisms in CLPTM1L may influence TERT transcription indirectly by altering chromatin accessibility or enhancer-promoter interactions in the region (Yang et al., 2018).

The objective of this study is to investigate the association between pre- and postnatal MeHg exposure, TL and cardiometabolic risk factors (anthropometry, blood pressure, glycaemia and lipidaemia) assessed during childhood. Additionally, the study aims to evaluate the potential mediating role of TL and the effect modification of polymorphisms in TERT and CLPTM1L in the relationship between MeHg exposure and cardiometabolic risk factors.

Methods

Study population

The data utilized in this research were from the European Human Early-Life Exposome (HELIX) project. Detailed descriptions of the HELIX cohorts and their inclusion criteria can be found in prior publications (Maitre et al., 2018, Vrijheid et al., 2014). The HELIX project examines pre- and postnatal exposure to a variety of chemical and physical factors across six cohorts: BiB (Born in Bradford; United Kingdom) (Wright et al., 2013), EDEN (Étude des Déterminants pré and postnatals du développement et la santé de l’ENfant, France) (Heude et al., 2016), INMA (INfancia y Medio Ambiente) (Guxens et al., 2012), KANC (Kaunas Cohort; Lithuania) (Grazuleviciene et al., 2009), MoBa (Norwegian Mother, Father and Child Cohort Study; Norway) (Magnus et al., 2016), and Rhea (the Mother-Child Cohort in Crete, Greece) (Chatzi et al., 2017).

The present study included participant mothers and their children for whom cardiometabolic risk factors measurements at 6–12 years old were available (N = 1301 mother–child pairs). The study further focused on two subsets: (a) those with data on pre- and postnatal total blood mercury (THg) levels and fish consumption (N = 648), and (b) those with measurements of childhood TL (N = 806) (Supplemental Fig. S1). Approximately >75 % of THg is considered to represent MeHg (Llop et al., 2017, Wells et al., 2022) and has shown correlations with fish consumption in the general population (Berglund et al., 2005, Clarkson and Magos, 2006, Jenssen et al., 2012).

All participants provided informed consent at each stage of the research, and the study protocols received approval from the national medical and health research ethics committees in each participating country. This study adheres to the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Mercury measurements

THg levels (μg/L) were assessed using blood samples collected during both the prenatal and postnatal (childhood) periods. For prenatal exposure, maternal blood samples were obtained during mid-pregnancy for the MoBa cohort (average = 18.7 weeks, SD = 0.9) and the Rhea cohort (mean = 14.1 weeks, SD = 3.7), and during late pregnancy for the BiB (mean = 26.6 weeks, SD = 1.4), KANC (mean = 39.4 weeks, SD = 1.3), and EDEN cohorts (mean = 26.1 weeks, SD = 1.2). In the case of the INMA cohort, cord blood samples were collected at the time of delivery and to estimate maternal whole blood mercury concentrations, cord blood values were transformed by dividing them by 1.7 (Stern and Smith, 2003). Postnatal THg exposure was assessed using blood samples from children in all HELIX cohorts, collected between the ages of 6 and 12 years (mean age [95 % CI] = 8.2 years [6.5, 9.1]). THg levels in maternal and child blood samples from the BiB, EDEN, KANC, MoBa, and Rhea cohorts were analysed at ALS Scandinavia (Sweden), while cord blood samples from the INMA cohort were processed at the Public Health Laboratory in Alava, Spain. Strict protocols were established to harmonize procedures across cohorts. The analytical methods and quality control measures implemented in the HELIX project have been extensively detailed in previous studies (Haug et al., 2018, Llop et al., 2020).

Genetic data

DNA was extracted from children 6–12 years old following a standardized protocol with the Qiagen DNA Blood Mini Kit described elsewhere (Maitre et al., 2018). The quality and quantity of the extracted DNA were assessed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer. Only high-quality DNA samples with adequate concentration were selected for further analysis.

Relative TL was measured using a quantitative PCR (qPCR)-based method, which is a widely used and efficient technique for assessing TL in large sample sizes. The protocol used the amplification (by triplicate) of two specific sequences: telomeric repeats and a single-copy gene, typically the 36B4 gene, used as a reference to normalize the telomere signal. The telomere primers specifically amplify the telomeric regions, while the 36B4 primers target a non-repetitive gene region. The relative TL for each sample was calculated by measuring the ratio of the telomere signal to the 36B4 signal. This ratio was then compared to a standard curve generated from DNA samples of known TL to quantify the relative TL. More details can be found in Cawthon et al. (Cawthon, 2009). Relative mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) content was also measured and used for adjusting purposes in statistical models involving TL due to their previous relationship in the same population (Lozano et al., 2024). Briefly, the ratio of two mitochondrial genes (MT-ND1 and MTF3212/R3319) to a single-copy nuclear gene (RPLP0) via quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was used (Janssen et al., 2012). Measurements were done in triplicate using a 7900HT Fast Real-Time PCR System, with serial dilutions and calibrators to ensure accuracy. Relative mtDNA content expressed as mtDNA copy number (mtDNAcn) was calculated using qBase software, with a coefficient of variation of 0.72 % for mitochondrial genes and 0.43 % for the nuclear gene.

Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNP) in the chromosomal region of the telomerase-encoding gene TERT (TERT/CLPTM1L region) were obtained by means of Infinium Global Screening Array (GSA) MD version 1 (Illumina) for genome-wide genotyping at the Human Genomics Facility (HuGe-F), Erasmus MC (https://www.glimdna.org). Genotype calling and annotation were done using the GenTrain2.8 algorithm based on a custom cluster file implemented in the GenomeStudio software and the GSAMD-24v1-0_20011747-A4 manifest. SNP coordinates were reported on human reference GRCh37 and on the source strand. Details about the quality control and imputation of the genetic data has been detailed elsewhere (Bustamante et al., 2024). Genetic variants were filtered out if they had a call rate <95 %, if they were in the non-canonical pseudo-autosomal region (PAR), if they had a minor allele frequency (MAF) <1 %, and if they were not in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) at a p-value <1E−06. Twelve SNP in the TERT or CLPTM1L genotyped genes were included in this study based on their previous association in the literature with Hg, other metals and/or TL effect on health (Supplemental Table S1): rs33954691, rs10054203, rs2736100, rs2736098, rs2853669 and rs2735940 in TERT gene; rs465498, rs31489 and rs27071 in CLPTM1L gene; and rs6554759, rs27068, rs37004 proxies due to the lack of rs2455393 availability.

Cardiometabolic risk factors

Anthropometric measurements were obtained using standardized, regularly calibrated equipment. Height (in meters) was measured with a stadiometer, while weight (in kilograms) was assessed using a digital scale; participants were measured without shoes and wearing light clothing. These measurements were then used to calculate body mass index (BMI, kg/m2). Waist circumference (in cm) was measured in a standing position, at the high point of the iliac crest at the end of a gentle expiration, using a metric tape and recorded in duplicate. These measurements were expressed as age- and sex-specific z-scores based on the international reference curves provided by the World Health Organization (de Onis et al., 2007), facilitating comparability across studies.

Blood pressure (BP, in mmHg) was measured with participants in a seated position following a 5-minute rest period, using the validated oscillometric device OMRON 705-CPII. Three consecutive measurements were taken at 1-minute intervals, and the average of the second and third readings was used for analysis. To minimize potential interference, measurements were conducted near the end of the visit to ensure that children had not consumed substances such as chocolate or cola drinks within the previous hour. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure values were recorded for each measurement. An hypertensive classification was calculated as normal (<90th percentile), prehypertensive (90th to <95th percentile) and hypertensive (≥95th percentile) (Flynn et al., 2017).

Blood samples from the children were obtained following the anthropometric and blood pressure examinations, with a median fasting period of 3.3 h (ranging from the 5th to 95th percentile: 2.2–5.9 h). High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C, in mg/dL) and triglycerides (in mg/dL), adjusted for calories, were measured by homogeneous enzymatic colorimetric techniques on the MODULAR ANALYTICS system (Roche Diagnostics). Insulin concentrations (µU/mL) was assessed with the human adipokine 15-plex magnetic panel (Life Technologies) (Stratakis et al., 2020).

Outcomes were modelled as z-scores for the waist circumference, BMI, systolic and diastolic BPs, triglyceride level, HDL cholesterol level, and insulin level of children using sex and age standardization. For BP, we also standardized for height (Ahrens et al., 2014b). The triglyceride distribution was skewed and was log-transformed before analysis.

Additionally, a continuous MetS risk score was calculated based on a previously validated method for children, as established by the European Multicentre Identification and Prevention of Dietary and Lifestyle-Induced Health Effects in Children and Infants (IDEFICS) study (Ahrens et al., 2014a). All components of MetS risk score were measured using the harmonized protocol (Maitre et al., 2018). We used the calculated z-scores for all MetS components. Sociodemographic and fish consumption data

Comprehensive data were collected on maternal characteristics, including age at childbirth, smoking habits during pregnancy (smoker or non-smoker), parity (first-time mother or with more than one birth), origin (categorized as European, Pakistani/Asian, or other), and education level as the main indicator of socioeconomic status. Maternal education was classified according to the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED): low (levels 0–2, encompassing less than primary, primary, and lower secondary education), middle (levels 3–4, including upper secondary and post-secondary non-tertiary education), and high (levels 5–8, corresponding to tertiary education) (Schneider, 2013). Maternal fish consumption during pregnancy or at birth, expressed as servings per week, was assessed through food frequency questionnaires (FFQ) in all cohorts except KANC, which lacked maternal dietary data. Calories were calculated from dietary intake based on FFQ. The sex of the new-born was recorded at birth, and children’s fish consumption at the 6–12-year follow-up was evaluated using interviews and food frequency questionnaires. Children’s age during cardiometabolic variables assessment were also recorded for adjustment purposes. Both pre- and postnatal fish intake were categorized into tertiles. Detailed information about data collection has been described elsewhere (Maitre et al., 2018).

Sensitivity covariates

Information on other environmental compounds, which could potentially influence the relationship between THg and outcomes, was also gathered and included in sensitivity analyses. Specifically, concentrations of total arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), and selenium (Se) (all measured in μg/L) were analysed in urine samples obtained during the first and third trimesters of pregnancy. The mean of both measurements was calculated and log-transformed to address skewed distributions. Creatinine levels were also quantified in the same urine samples for adjustment of urinary dilution (Lozano et al., 2022). Persistent organic pollutants, including dichlorodiphenyldichloroethylene (DDE) and five polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs, IUPAC numbers 118, 138, 153, 170, 180), were measured in maternal blood samples (expressed in pg/g lipids) collected during the first trimester. All DDE and PCB measurements were corrected for lipid content, and the sum of the five PCBs was also used for analysis. The laboratory methods and quality control protocols employed for these analyses have been detailed previously (Costopoulou et al., 2024, Haug et al., 2018, Llop et al., 2010, Maitre et al., 2021).

Statistical analysis

The association between higher THg exposure and longer TL had already been evaluated in a previous study conducted by the same research group in the same population (Lozano et al., 2024). To assess potential concentration-dependencies and non-linear associations, multivariable robust quantile regression models (Koenker, 2005) and generalized additive models (GAM) were used. Segmented regressions were carried out to estimate potential break-points in the linearity (Muggeo, 2003).

Cardiovascular outcomes for this study were the z-scores for waist circumference, BMI, systolic BP, diastolic BP, HDL-C, triglycerides and insulin, as well as the hypertensive classification and the MetS score. The geometric mean (GM) and 95 % confidence intervals (95 %CI) were calculated for the untransformed variables for descriptive purposes. The ANOVA F-test was applied to compare the geometric mean (GM) of all the outcomes across cohorts, sex, ethnicities and categories of the study population’s characteristics.

THg concentrations and TL measurements were considered exposure variables and TL was also considered as a potential mediator of the THg effect on cardiometabolic outcomes. The GM and 95 %CI were calculated. Spearman correlation test was run between outcomes and exposure variables to explore potential relationships. For further analysis, the logarithmic form (base 10) of THg and TL transformed values were used.

Parsimonious outcome-exposure linear models were developed to minimize the risk of confounding while avoiding overadjustment and bias. The covariate set was informed by previous HELIX work. In particular, Stratakis et al. (Stratakis et al., 2020) developed a directed acyclic graph (DAG) to identify the relevant adjustment set for mercury–cardiometabolic associations, which was subsequently adopted in later HELIX analyses (Lozano et al., 2024). We followed the same framework here to ensure comparability and consistency across studies. Thus, all models were adjusted using a minimal set of covariates: cohort, child and maternal age, and both maternal and child fish consumption. Fish consumption was included as a covariate because, in addition to being the main source of THg exposure, it is also a source of nutrients (e.g., omega-3 fatty acids, selenium, vitamin D) with potential effects on cardiometabolic health. In HELIX, the correlation between fish intake and blood THg concentrations was moderate rather than high (Stratakis et al., 2020), reflecting variation in fish species consumed across cohorts. Other dietary variables were examined in exploratory analyses but were not associated with THg exposure or outcomes and were therefore not included. Cohort was included as a fixed effect to capture systematic population differences and meta-analysis of cohort-specific estimates showed no evidence of heterogeneity (Lozano et al., 2024). To build the models, first a core model was built for each cardiometabolic outcome by assessing the complete set of available sociodemographic covariates using a forward selection method. Only sociodemographic covariates associated with the outcome at a significance level of p < 0.1 were considered. Second, a multivariable exposure-outcome model including repeated exposure windows (prenatal and postnatal THg) was developed for each cardiometabolic outcome, incorporating both prenatal and postnatal THg concentrations (log-transformed). Additional potential confounders for the exposure-outcome relationship were evaluated from the covariates identified in the THg core model previously developed by Lozano et al. (Lozano et al., 2024) for the same population but absent in the outcome core models. These included maternal education and parity. Covariates were retained in each model only if they modified the exposure-outcome effect estimate by more than 10 % (Lee, 2014). Finally, linear regression models were constructed to assess the association between each cardiometabolic outcome and TL (log-transformed). In this case, mtDNAcn and maternal origin were evaluated as potential confounders (Lozano et al., 2024).

Causal mediation analysis was used to disentangle and quantify the TL effect through which the THg concentrations influences each outcome. For this mediation analysis, we followed the approach described by Imai et al. (2011) and further extended by Wang et al. (Imai et al., 2011) for the multiple-exposure single-mediator setting. Specifically, both prenatal and postnatal THg concentrations were simultaneously included in the mediator and outcome models, along with a common set of covariates. When estimating the mediation effect for a given exposure (prenatal or postnatal), the non-focal exposure was treated as an additional covariate. This product-of-coefficients approach (Wang et al., 2019) allowed us to decompose the total effect of each exposure into the average direct effect (ADE) and the average causal mediation effect (ACME) via TL. Confidence intervals were obtained using nonparametric bootstrapping with 1,000 simulations. The robustness of the mediation results was further evaluated by testing for potential exposure–mediator interaction and by conducting sensitivity analyses for unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding. In all mediation analyses, we included the same covariates as the exposure-outcome analysis for both the exposure-mediator and mediator-outcome associations. This analysis was conducted on participants with both pre- and postnatal THg measurements as well as TL measurements (n = 572). As a sensitivity analysis to account for potential bias due to loss to follow-up, mediation models were additionally fitted using inverse probability weights (IPW).

To assess non-linear patterns, GAM were employed. Natural cubic splines with one or two internal knots were compared using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) scores, graphical analysis, and the Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT). Segmented regression analyses were performed to identify potential breakpoints in linearity (Muggeo, 2003).

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate changes in pre- and postnatal total mercury (THg) coefficients. This was done by sequentially adding log10-transformed concentrations of additional pollutants (PCBs, DDE, As, Cd, Pb, Se) to the outcome-adjusted models. The sample size was adjusted to reflect the availability of matching sensitivity covariates among participants (Supplemental Table S2).

Effect modification by SNPs was evaluated by including an interaction term between each SNP and THg exposure in outcome-adjusted linear models. Pre- and postnatal THg effects by genotype were visualized graphically. To investigate effect modification by genetic variants, we fitted linear regression models including an interaction term between pre- and postnatal THg exposure and each SNP. SNPs were generally coded as a three-level categorical variable according to genotype. When the homozygous minor allele frequency was very low (<5 % of the sample), the homozygous minor group was merged with the heterozygotes, resulting in a two-level coding. To obtain the exposure effect estimates within each genotype category, we re-specified the reference category of the SNP in the model. This procedure uses the full sample in all cases and does not imply stratified analyses; rather, it is a reparameterization of the same interaction model that allows the THg coefficient to be interpreted as the effect among carriers of each genotype category. The overall test of interaction corresponds to a 2-df genotypic model when three categories were retained, or to a 1-df dominant model when the minor homozygotes were collapsed with heterozygotes. To account for multiple testing across models including each SNP, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied. Effect modification was considered statistically significant at an FDR-adjusted p-value <0.05, with marginal significance defined as p < 0.1. To address the possibility of overly liberal inference, we also calculated the more conservative Bonferroni and Holm adjusted p-values, which are presented as an additional sensitivity analysis.

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2017). The mediation analysis was conducted utilizing the mediation package (Tingley et al., 2014).

Cardiometabolic risk factors and metabolic syndrome score in the population

Anthropometric data from the BIB, KANC, MOBA, and RHEA cohorts fell within the normal percentiles for children aged 6–9 years based on WHO reference ranges for BMI-for-age (15.3, 16.6 kg/m2) (de Onis et al. 2007). Children from the EDEN and INMA cohorts exhibited higher BMI (GM [95 % CI] = 17.83 [17.46, 18.21] and 18.04 [17.64, 18.44] kg/m2, respectively) (see Supplemental Table S4). European participants showed higher values for both waist circumference and BMI outcomes compared with non-European children (Supplemental Table S5).

HDL cholesterol levels were within normal range (≥45 mg/dL) (Ballantyne, 2023). However, triglyceride concentrations displayed a wide distribution, with pooled values slightly exceeding the recommended maximum for children under 10 years (>75 mg/dL) (Ballantyne, 2023). Only MOBA and RHEA cohorts had triglycerides levels below the reference (73.51 [72.30, 74.79] and 73.51 [72.22, 74.80] mg/dl, respectively) (Supplemental Table S4). Fasting insulin concentrations were found at 6.97 [5.69, 6.05] µU/mL, which aligns with the normal range of 2–15 µU/mL defined by the International Society for Paediatric and Adolescent Diabetes (ISPAD) Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines 2018 (Abraham et al., 2018). Values exceeding this range may suggest insulin resistance. Blood pressure measurements were within the expected ranges for this population, with systolic values between 96 and 100 mm Hg and diastolic values between 55 and 60 mm Hg (De Jesus, 2011). Nevertheless, 4.2 % of participants exhibited prehypertensive levels (90th to < 95th percentile), and 7.6 % were classified as hypertensive (≥95th percentile). Elevated blood pressure was particularly observed in participants from the BIB, EDEN, and INMA cohorts (Supplemental Table S4), as well as among participants with non-European origin (Supplemental Table S5). Additionally, systolic blood pressure was higher in males (100.23 [99.39, 101.7] mm Hg) than in females (98.92 [98.04, 99.81] mm Hg) (Supplemental Table S6).

MetS score was significantly higher in BIB and INMA (Supplemental Table S4) cohorts and participants with Pakistani/Asian origin (Supplemental Table S5), who are predominantly (93.9 %) from the BIB cohort.

Causal mediation analysis

Fig. 1 illustrates the causal mediation analysis, quantifying: 1) the total effects of prenatal THg concentrations, postnatal THg concentrations, and TL, on each outcome (z-scores); and 2) the mediated effect of each THg on each outcome via TL (ACME).

THg and TL direct effects on each outcome and causal mediation analysis conducted in participants with all measurements available. All mediation analyses included the same covariates as the exposure-outcome analysis for both the exposure-mediator and mediator-outcome associations (see Supplemental Fig. S3).

Higher prenatal THg concentrations were associated with higher insulin levels in children (β [95 % CI], p-value = 0.285 [0.003, 0.567], 0.048). Additionally, higher prenatal THg exposure showed marginal associations with greater waist circumference (0.224 [−0.036, 0.484], 0.091), higher systolic BP (0.207 [−0.038, 0.451], 0.098), and MetS score (0.550 [−0.088, 1.189], 0.092). In contrast, higher postnatal THg concentrations were associated with lower diastolic BP (−0.208 [−0.369, −0.046], 0.012), as well as a decreased proportion of children classified as hypertensive (≥95th percentile) regarding the normal category (<90th percentile) (−0.942 [−1.749, −1.373], 0.022). Higher postnatal THg concentrations also showed marginal associations with lower waist circumference (−0.177 [−0.364, 0.010], 0.064), and lower systolic BP (−0.169 [−0.347, 0.009], 0.063).

Additionally, longer TL was associated with reductions in waist circumference (−8.193 [−14.364, −2.022], 0.006), BMI (−3.058 [−5.243, −0.872], 0.006), and both systolic (−1.053 [−1.807, −0.299], 0.006) and diastolic BP (−0.827 [−1.529, −0.125], 0.021). The decrease in the proportion of children classified in the highest hypertension category remained marginally significant (−3.514 [−7.273, 0.244], 0.067).

However, Fig. 1 also indicates that none of the significant effects of THg on the outcomes were mediated by TL (ACME p-values > 0.05). The only exception is the non-significant total prenatal THg effect on BMI (0.222 [−0.101, 0.544], 0.178), which includes a marginal mediation effect via TL, accounting for 8.17 % of the total effect (−0.029 [−0.080, 0.000], 0.086). This suggests that longer TL reduces the prenatal THg direct effect on BMI by that proportion. No evidence of exposure–mediator interaction was observed. Sensitivity analyses confirmed these findings since the ACME estimates would be reduced to zero under very modest levels of unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding, further supporting the absence of mediation by TL (Supplemental Table S7). The IPW-weighted mediation models yielded very similar estimates, with no substantial changes in ACME compared with the main analyses (Supplemental Table S8).

For sensitivity analyses, all models were re-run separately, including the log-transformed concentrations of total As, Cd, Pb, and Se, as well as the persistent organic pollutants DDE, PCB-118, PCB-138, PCB-153, PCB-170, PCB-180, and the sum of all five PCB. However, none of the pollutants altered the outcome-exposure relationships (data not shown).

Linearity assessment

Significant associations between pre- or postnatal THg and insulin, diastolic BP and the hypertensive classification followed a linear relationship. Only the significant association between the systolic BP and the TL showed a non-linear relationship. Two estimated linearity breakpoints, corresponding to relative TL of 0.853 and 1.045 after reversing the log transformation, are highlighted in the Supplemental Fig. S4.

Genetics effect modification

A summary of the most relevant findings from the genetic effect modification analyses is presented in Table 2. Several SNPs in the TERT/CLPTM1L region modified the associations between prenatal and/or postnatal THg concentrations and cardiometabolic outcomes (FDR < 0.1). Among them, TERT rs33954691 consistently interacted with postnatal THg in several examined outcomes. In the total sample, postnatal THg concentrations showed a marginally significant negative association with waist circumference but no significant association with BMI or MetS score (Fig. 1). However, these relationships varied depending on SNP genotype. For TERT rs33954691, children homozygous for the common allele GG showed a stronger significant negative association between postnatal THg and waist circumference (β [CI 95 %], p-value = -0.604 [−0.950, −0.258], 0.001) and for BMI (−0.696 [−1.132, −0.261], = 0.002) and MetS score (−1.526 [−2.368, −0.684], p-value < 0.001) (Supplemental Figs. S5–S7). Conversely, for TERT rs2853669, homozygous carriers of the common allele AA reversed the direction of the association, showing a positive relationship between postnatal THg and waist circumference (0.491 [0.013, 0.970], 0.045) and BMI (0.633 [0.032, 1.233] 0.039), while heterozygous and homozygous carriers of the alternative allele AG and GG retained the negative association with waist circumference (−0.279 [−0.552, −0.006], 0.045; and −0.257 [−0.532, 0.018], 0.067, respectively) (Supplemental Figs. S5 and S6).

However, postnatal THg exposure exhibited significant inverse associations depending on the genotype of specific SNPs: TERT rs33954691, TERT rs2736100, CLPTM1L rs465498, and CLPTM1L rs31489. Conversely, carriers of the alternative alleles followed the original positive but non-significant pattern observed in the overall sample. Notably, a significant positive association was detected exclusively among individuals homozygous for the alternative allele of rs33954691 (β = 0.304 [CI: 0.063, 0.545], p = 0.014) (Supplemental Fig. S8).

Notably, our gene–environment interaction results were robust to multiple-testing correction methods. Beyond the FDR approach used, additional Bonferroni and Holm adjustments (Table 2) confirmed that most associations of interest remained statistically significant. In particular, TERT rs33954691 consistently showed effect modification across several cardiometabolic outcomes, supporting the reliability of this SNP as a potential modifier of mercury-related effects.

Discussion

Our findings suggest distinct associations between prenatal and postnatal THg exposure and cardiometabolic outcomes in children. Prenatal THg exposure was directly linked to higher insulin levels and showed potential associations with increased body size and MetS score. In contrast, postnatal THg exposure was associated with lower blood pressure and a reduced likelihood of hypertension. These findings stress that the prenatal period is the sensitive period for cardiometabolic Hg toxicity. Longer TL was as expected related to lower body size and blood pressure, indicating a protective role against cardiometabolic risk factors. However, TL did not mediate the effects of THg exposure, except for a minor influence on the relationship between prenatal THg and BMI. Sensitivity analyses including other environmental pollutants confirmed the robustness of these findings. We also found that genetic variants in TERT and CLPTM1L genes significantly modified the impact of MeHg exposure.

Regional variations in cardiometabolic risk factors

Anthropometric and metabolic parameters in European children vary significantly by country. The prevalence of MetS in the present study differs depending on diagnostic criteria, ranging from 1.44 % to 55.8 % (Orsini et al., 2023), with Spain reporting the highest figures (2.5 %–19.6 %). MetS is more common among males (1.4–55.8 %) and children with overweight or obesity (12.9–55.8 %), with obesity as the primary cardiometabolic risk factor. Regional disparities in overweight prevalence are also evident in other studies: Southern Europe reports rates exceeding 40 %, while Northern and Western Europe remain below 10 %, with Central and Eastern Europe displaying intermediate trends (Ahrens et al., 2014c). Among children under seven, overweight prevalence ranged from 17.9 % (Garrido-Migue et al., 2019) to 28.6 % (Jones et al., 2017). The highest rates in this age group were recorded in Italy (32.4 %), Greece (29.6 %), and Portugal (26.4 %), whereas Estonia (8.3 %), France (11.0 %), and the Netherlands (13.4 %) had the lowest. The increase in childhood obesity in certain European countries may be partly due to dietary shifts toward higher consumption of animal proteins, fats, sugary and ultra-processed foods, with lower intake of complex carbohydrates and fibre (Grosso and Galvano, 2016, Monteiro et al., 2018).

Despite rising interest in cardiometabolic health, regional differences in European children remain underexplored. The European Society of Hypertension highlights that blood pressure levels in groups with different regional origins, including South Asian, sub-Saharan African, and African-Caribbean populations, as well as refugee children, are largely unstudied (Lurbe et al., 2016). However, these groups likely exhibit higher blood pressure due to a greater prevalence of hypertensive or preeclamptic pregnancies, increasing cardiometabolic risk. In the Netherlands, Turkish children had higher triglyceride levels than Dutch peers, a difference largely explained by parental socio-demographics and lifestyle factors before pregnancy (Gishti et al., 2015). Similarly, a UK-based study found that South Asian children had higher fasting insulin levels than White British children, indicating a greater predisposition to insulin resistance and metabolic disorders (Whincup et al., 2002). These disparities underscore the need for targeted public health interventions addressing ethnic-specific childhood metabolic risks.

Cardiometabolic risk factors associations and telomere length mediation analysis

Mercury effect on cardiometabolic risk factors

A previous HELIX study investigated the associations between THg exposure and metabolic health, as well as inflammatory biomarkers, in the same cohort of children aged 6 to 12 years (Stratakis et al., 2020). However, this earlier study focused exclusively on prenatal THg exposure, included a smaller sample size (805 mother–child pairs), and did not perform mediation analysis or genetic assessments. This previous study also found that higher THg concentrations were associated with increased MetS score (β [CI 95 %], p-value = 0.18 [0.01, 0.34], 0.03), along with higher waist circumference and elevated insulin levels. The direct association of Hg exposure with MetS has been reported in other countries. A cohort study in 387 Russian women (204 with metabolic syndrome and 183 without metabolic syndrome as controls) showed higher hair Hg concentrations among women with MetS (Ivanova et al., 2021). Similarly, a total of 823 participants in the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2016 showed a relationship between blood Hg level and waist measurement and triglyceride level (Park and Oh, 2021). The association of higher blood THg with higher BMI and waist circumference has also been observed in a large Korean study in 11,159 subjects (Bae et al., 2016). Proposed mechanisms include mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, insulin resistance, and pancreatic β-cell dysfunction (Moon et al., 2017). However, a study in the U.S. with 882 adolescents (Project Viva) found no independent effects of prenatal Hg on BMI or waist circumference (Smith et al., 2023). Likewise in the U.K., an analysis of 544 mother–child pairs showed no significant associations, suggesting that at low levels (median: 1.9 µg/L), the biological impact of Hg may be minimal (Dack et al., 2023).

Regarding blood pressure, results are heterogeneous. In the U.S., the NHANES study of children and adolescents aged 8 to 17 years found a negative correlation between blood THg levels and systolic blood pressure (Yao et al., 2020). However, in the New Hampshire birth cohort, Hg levels in toenails at age 3 and urine at age 5 were associated with higher diastolic and mean blood pressure (Farzan et al., 2021). Other studies in Canada (Valera et al., 2011), Spain (Castiello et al., 2020), and the U.S. (Desai et al., 2021, Fan et al., 2017, Kalish et al., 2015, Zhang et al., 2018) found no significant associations. A recent review (Hu et al., 2025) suggests that Hg may disrupt antioxidant homeostasis, promoting inflammation and vasoconstriction through the renin-angiotensin system while reducing nitric oxide bioavailability (Förstermann and Sessa, 2012). The inverse association between postnatal THg and blood pressure should be interpreted with caution. Although we adjusted for reported fish consumption, residual confounding remains possible due to measurement error in dietary questionnaires and heterogeneity in nutrient content across fish species. Long-chain n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) are plausible protective factors, as higher intakes have been associated with lower risk of hypertension (Xu et al., 2023, Xun et al., 2011). Se status has also been linked to modest reductions in blood pressure (Berthold et al., 2012), although our sensitivity analyses with prenatal Se did not change the prenatal THg estimates. Because HELIX did not include biomarker measures of postnatal Se or PUFAs, the potential contribution of these nutrients to the observed inverse associations cannot be ruled out. Future studies with objective PUFA and selenium biomarkers are needed to directly test this hypothesis.

Telomere length effect on cardiometabolic risk factors

Several studies have documented an inverse relationship between TL and adiposity. In the U.S., an analysis of 309 non-Hispanic white participants aged 8 to 80 years showed that TL was negatively correlated with BMI, waist circumference, and visceral fat (Lee et al., 2011). In China, a study on women found similar associations, except for the waist-to-hip ratio (Cui et al., 2013). In children, a trial with 158 participants aged 8 to 12 years showed that a weight control intervention increased TL (Rehkopf et al., 2023), consistent with findings in Greece (508 children, age 10) and Brazil (476 schoolchildren, ages 7 to 17) (Félix et al., 2024, Paltoglou et al., 2021). Underlying proposed mechanisms include oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular senescence (Ibraheem Shelash Al-Hawary et al., 2024, Kim et al., 2009).

The relationship between TL and cardiometabolic risk factors appears to be nonlinear in some studies, observing U-shaped relationship between TL and cardiovascular mortality (Yi et al., 2025), diabetes (Zhao et al., 2013), and risk of coronary artery disease (Ye et al., 2013). However, most studies evaluating the linearity of these relationships have not found nonlinear patterns (Cao et al., 2020, Wang et al., 2024, Xu et al., 2020). Dose-dependent effects and compensatory responses have been proposed as explanations for these inconsistencies (Zhang et al., 2003). In populations exposed to oxidative stress inducers, unexpected telomere elongation has been observed, attributed to telomerase activation and telomerase-independent mechanisms (Fernandes et al., 2021).

Telomere length mediation in the mercury effect on cardiometabolic risk factors

Emerging research suggests that environmental toxicants exposure could impact cardiometabolic health through telomere-related mechanisms. Hence, a recent study examined the mediating role of TL in the association between exposure to polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, phthalates, Pb and Cd and MetS in adults (n = 1,265). Mediation analysis revealed that TL could explain up to 17.1 % of the effect of these compounds (Tang et al., 2023). In another study, the TL mediation on the relationship between blood Pb and Cd co-exposure and blood glucose was evaluated in 600 Chinese adults. The intermediary model test results showed that TL was a mediating variable between urinary Cd level and blood glucose concentration, and the mediating effect size was 0.0192 (95 %CI: 0.0007–0.0563), with a mediation ratio of 15.57 % (Zhang et al., 2022). A recent study related these two metals with hypertension also assessing the TL as a mediator of the effect in 3,718 participants from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Higher concentrations of both metals were directly associated with higher hypertension, and longer TL with lower hypertension. The mediation proportions were 2.25 % and 4.20 % for Pb and Cd, respectively (Ouyang et al., 2024). However, the evidence is not always consistent.

Several studies have proposed compensatory mechanisms to explain why TL may not mediate the effects of cardiometabolic risk factors and metal exposure in humans. Oxidative stress and inflammation induced by metal exposure may contribute to cardiometabolic disorders through direct damage to endothelial function or mitochondrial integrity, bypassing the pathway of telomere shortening (Gao et al., 2022, Tang et al., 2023). Moreover, non-linear or threshold effects could be involved, where telomere attrition only exacerbates disease risk beyond certain exposure levels, while early damage is mitigated by compensatory responses such as telomerase activation (Das et al., 2019, Hu et al., 2025, Ng and Amini, 2021). In addition, genetic and environmental modifiers add further complexity to this relationship; for instance, polymorphisms in telomerase-related genes might independently influence susceptibility to metal toxicity and cardiometabolic risk, without TL acting as a primary mediator (Tannemann et al., 2024).

The role of genetic polymorphisms in the mercury effect on cardiometabolic risk factors

To date, this is the first study, as far as we know, to evaluate the influence of genetic polymorphisms in the TERT gene on susceptibility to Hg toxicity in relation to cardiometabolic risk factors. However, several studies have examined the impact of TERT polymorphisms on cardiometabolic outcomes. In a study of 111 Indonesian adults, alternative alleles of the TERT rs2736098 polymorphism modified the association between body fat percentage and triglyceride and HDL-cholesterol levels, revealing a positive correlation (Lestari et al., 2020). The authors suggested that individuals carrying these alternative alleles might have reduced TERT gene expression, although no transcriptomic analysis was conducted to confirm this. The TERT rs2853669 polymorphism, also examined in the present study, has previously been investigated in 140 Polish adults. That study found that individuals homozygous for the alternative CC genotype had shorter TL, which was associated with a higher incidence of type 2 diabetes (T2D) (Gutmajster et al., 2018). The authors proposed that CC homozygous for TERT rs2853669 may be linked to reduced telomerase activity, aligning with prior evidence that telomerase influences the pentose phosphate pathway, leading to altered glycogen accumulation across different genotypes. Moreover, reduced telomerase activity has been associated with hypertension, as observed in a study involving 94 adults hospitalized at a hypertension clinic in Poland (Martynowicz et al., 2020). In this context, angiotensin has been shown to regulate both the expression and activity of telomerase (Ait-Aissa et al., 2018, Durand et al., 2016).

Limitations and strengths

Our study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although we assessed THg exposure both prenatally and postnatally, each measure was based on a single biomarker sample per developmental window. This may not fully capture within-window variability or long-term cumulative exposure, although the use of biomarkers provides a more objective assessment than dietary questionnaires alone. Second, fish consumption was assessed using food-frequency questionnaires and is therefore subject to reporting error. Nevertheless, adjustment for maternal and child fish intake did not materially alter the results, and correlations between fish consumption and THg biomarkers were only moderate. Third, TL was measured in leukocytes at a single time point in childhood, which may not fully capture dynamic changes over time or across tissues. Fourth, as in all observational studies, residual confounding by unmeasured factors cannot be entirely excluded. With regard to the genetic analyses, a limitation is that we focused only on SNPs within TERT and CLPTM1L, chosen for their established role in telomere biology. Other loci potentially related to cardiometabolic outcomes were not considered, which may have restricted our ability to detect additional modifying effects. Finally, HELIX did not include biomarker measures of postnatal Se or long-chain n-3 PUFAs. Although models were adjusted for reported fish consumption, residual confounding due to measurement error in dietary questionnaires and variability in nutrient content across fish species cannot be ruled out. The lack of objective nutritional biomarkers may limit our ability to fully disentangle the role of these nutrients in the observed associations between THg exposure and blood pressure.

Despite these limitations, our study also has several strengths. It extends prior work by Stratakis et al. (2020) (Stratakis et al., 2020) and Lozano et al. (Lozano et al., 2024) in important ways: we applied formal mediation analyses with TL, explored genetic effect modification in TERT and CLPTM1L, and performed sensitivity analyses with other environmental contaminants. Importantly, previous HELIX analyses showed no evidence of between-cohort heterogeneity (Lozano et al., 2024), supporting the consistency and generalizability of our findings across European populations. In addition, the harmonized data collection across cohorts, detailed covariate information, and quality-controlled genetic and exposure data strengthen the validity of our conclusions. Finally, a key strength of our work is that the gene–environment interaction results proved robust across different multiple-testing correction methods with several SNPs, and particularly TERT rs33954691, remaining consistently significant across multiple cardiometabolic outcomes. This robustness strongly supports the reliability of our genetic findings and underlines the novelty of this contribution beyond previous HELIX studies.

Conclusions

Our findings support the cardiometabolic toxicity of MeHg, with the prenatal period emerging as a particularly vulnerable window of exposure, as indicated by associations with insulin levels and metabolic risk. The analyses indicate that these effects are largely independent of TL, suggesting alternative biological pathways. Genetic variants in TERT and CLPTM1L modified the associations, highlighting the importance of genetic susceptibility. Together, these results underscore the need to minimize early-life MeHg exposure and to further investigate gene–environment interactions and mechanisms beyond telomere dynamics. Such knowledge could inform targeted prevention strategies in vulnerable populations.
