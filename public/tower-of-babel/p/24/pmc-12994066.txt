Adjacent domains drive the emergence of misfolded intermediates in the folding pathway of the PDZ4 from MAGI1
Authors: Valeria Pennacchietti, Cosmin Marian Obreja, Dimitrios Marinidis, Sara Di Matteo, Rodolfo Ippoliti, Stefano Gianni, Francesca Malagrinò
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein misfolding in multidomain assemblies emerges from a complex interplay between intra- and interdomain interactions. Here, we dissect how neighboring domains shape the folding and misfolding of the PDZ4 domain from the scaffold protein MAGI1. Exploiting the single intrinsic tryptophan in PDZ4, we monitored its behavior in the isolated domain, in the PDZ3–4 tandem, and within the five-domain PDZ2–6 supramodule by equilibrium and kinetic (un)folding experiments over a broad pH range. Equilibrium denaturation reveals that PDZ4 displays a cooperative, two-state-like transition with similar thermodynamic stability in all constructs, indicating that domain adjacency does not appreciably affect its native state. In contrast, kinetic measurements uncover pronounced deviations from two-state behavior. PDZ4 alone populates a transient intermediate, whose population and associated kinetic slowdown are markedly amplified in the tandem and even more in the supramodular context, consistent with the formation of a misfolded ensembles stabilized by non-native interdomain contacts. Acidic pH selectively enhances the kinetic trap in PDZ3–4 and PDZ2–6, but not in PDZ4 alone, pointing to a key role of electrostatics at interdomain interfaces, potentially involving specific salt bridges. Structural inspection suggests the presence of potential specific salt bridges (Asp871/Glu901 with Arg873/Lys905), although further investigation is required. Our data show that misfolding of MAGI1 PDZ4 is a context-dependent, cooperative property of adjacent domains rather than a simple by-product of folding, and illustrate how supramodular organization encodes kinetic plasticity in scaffold proteins.Keywords: (mis)folding, PDZ domain, Multi-domain system, Kinetic trap, Kinetics

Introduction

Multidomain proteins represent 75 % of the human proteome and rely on precise interdomain communication to achieve correct folding [1,2]. Their cooperative assembly ensures functional architectures but, from a folding perspective, it also introduces additional complexity into the energy landscape, where interdomain interactions can generate competing routes and kinetic traps [[3], [4], [5]]. In particular, when multiple domains unfold simultaneously, non-native contacts may transiently appear, thereby producing misfolded intermediates that are invisible to equilibrium experiments but evident in kinetics [[6], [7], [8]]. Understanding how domain organization shapes folding and misfolding is therefore central to deciphering the design principles of multidomain proteins.

From a mechanistic perspective, transient misfolding in multidomain systems has been classically interpreted in terms of transient domain swapping, where structural elements from neighboring denatured domains interchange to form non-native but native-like topologies [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. This mechanism predicts that misfolding arises only when both contiguous domains are unfolded, while the presence of a folded neighbor protects the chain from non-productive pairing. Additionally, increase of sequence identity between contiguous domains appear to favor these phenomena [13,14]. Although this framework successfully explains some examples of tandem PDZ and immunoglobulin domains, the extent to which it generalizes to other supramodular assemblies remains unclear. In fact, non-homologous domains have also been observed to populate transient misfolded states, thereby challenging the domain swapping model [15,16]. Additionally, it is unclear whether the addition of further domains alters the probability or the stability of the misfolded trap.

The scaffold protein MAGI1 provides a suitable model to explore these questions. Its supramodular region is composed of five PDZ domains arranged linearly, each capable of autonomous folding yet coupled through weak interdomain interactions [17]. PDZ domains are small globular modules that mediate protein–protein interactions and are widely distributed in scaffold proteins. Their occurrence in tandem appears to be an evolutionary solution for expanding functional versatility [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. MAGI1 is particularly advantageous also because only one of its PDZ domains contains an intrinsic tryptophan residue, allowing selective spectroscopic observation of its folding without optical interference from the neighboring modules. Previous studies have shown that isolated PDZ domains behave as well-folded systems, whereas contiguous tandems can display signatures of transient misfolding [16,22]. In particular, the PDZ1–PDZ2 tandems of X11 and whirlin were found to populate a kinetic intermediate consistent with a misfolded ensemble stabilized by non-native electrostatic interactions [23].

Here we compare the folding and misfolding behavior of the single PDZ4 domain, the PDZ3–PDZ4 tandem, and the five-domain PDZ2–PDZ6 supramodule of MAGI1. We find that the probability and nature of the transient misfolded intermediate differ across constructs, challenging the simple domain-swapping model. Unexpectedly, the five-domain assembly populates a misfolded species distinct from that of the tandem, while the isolated domain folds without detectable traps. Moreover, the stability of the kinetic trap is modulated by pH, indicating that specific electrostatic interactions tune the transient interdomain contacts that underlie misfolding. These observations reveal that transient misfolding in MAGI1 is not a uniform property of domain adjacency but an emergent feature of supramodular organization and environmental conditions.

Material and methods

Plasmid construction

The cDNA sequences encoding for the PDZ4, PDZ3–4 and PDZ2–6 proteins were subcloned in a pET28b(+) expression vector. All constructs were verified by DNA sequencing (Eurofins Genomics).

Protein expression and purification

PDZ4, PDZ3–4 and PDZ2–6 wew transformed in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells. The cells were grown at 37 °C in 1 L of Luria broth (LB) supplemented with 30 μg/ml of kanamycin until OD600 of 0.6–0.8, and then induced with 1 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thio-galactopyranoside (IPTG). The expression was allowed to proceed overnight at 25 °C and 180 rpm.

Bacterial pellets were resuspended using a buffer containing 50 mM TrisHCl pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole and a protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (cOmplete, EDTA-free, Roche). The cells were then sonicated and centrifuged, and the supernatant was loaded in a HiTrap Chelating High- Performance column (GE Healthcare), pre-equilibrated with the same buffer used to resuspend the bacterial pellet. Then, the column was washed with 10 column volumes of a buffer containing 50 mM TrisHCl pH 7.5, 800 mM NaCl to remove any unspecific binding. The elution was performed with a gradient of 0–0.5 M imidazole through an ÄKTA-start (Cytiva) HPLC system. The imidazole was finally removed using an HiPrep desalting column (GE Healthcare), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity of all protein samples was verified through SDS-PAGE. The His-tag was not removed primarily to preserve yield; however, all constructs were expressed, purified, and analyzed under identical tagged conditions, ensuring full internal consistency of the kinetic and thermodynamic parameters.

Equilibrium (un)folding experiments

Fluorescence equilibrium (un)folding experiments were performed using a FL6500 Fluorescence Spectrophotometer (Perkin-Elmer). All proteins used were held at a constant concentration of 1 μM and experiments were performed at 25 °C in a quartz cuvette with a path length of 1 cm. Proteins were excited at 280 nm and emission spectra were recorded between 300 nm and 400 nm, at increasing GdnHCl concentrations (figure S1); under these conditions, we observed a progressive decrease in tryptophan fluorescence intensity as the denaturant concentration increased. Experiments were performed in 50 mM Formate pH 3.0–3.5, 50 mM Acetate pH 4.5–5.5, 50 mM Bis-Tris pH 6.5, 50 mM Hepes pH 7.5, 50 mM TrisHCl pH 8.5, 50 mM CHES pH 9.5–10 all supplemented with 300 mM NaCl and 10 mM DTT. All experiments were performed in buffer containing 300 mM NaCl. Lower ionic strength conditions were explored but resulted in substantially decreased protein stability, leading to poor reproducibility. We therefore used 300 mM NaCl as a compromise to ensure protein stability across measurements.

Kinetic (un)folding experiments

Rapid mixing (un)folding experiments were performed using a SX-18 single mixing stopped-flow device (Applied Photophysics). All experiments were performed at 25 °C, with a final protein concentration of 1 μM and GdnHCl as the denaturing agent. Fluorescence emissions were measured with a 320 nm cut-off glass filter, using an excitation wavelength of 280 nm. At least five individual traces were acquired and then averaged for each concentration of denaturant. Buffers used for all constructs were 50 mM 50 mM Formate pH 3.0–3.5, 50 mM Acetate pH 4.5–5.5, 50 mM Bis-Tris pH 6.5, 50 mM Hepes pH 7.5, 50 mM TrisHCl pH 8.5, 50 mM CHES pH 9.5–10, all supplemented with 300 mM NaCl and 10 mM DTT.

The protein was transferred into the experimental buffer to obtain the desired pH by dilution into the target buffer. Kinetic unfolding was initiated from native protein in denaturant-free buffer by rapid mixing with buffers containing GndHCl, yielding final GndHCl concentrations after mixing in the range of ∼2.4–5.6 M. Kinetic refolding was initiated by first denaturing the protein in 6 M GndHCl and then rapidly diluting/mixing into lower-denaturant buffers, yielding final GndHCl concentrations after mixing in the range of ∼0.3–2.6 M. The kobs values in the chevron plots are reported as a function of the final GndHCl concentration.

In a simple two-state folding mechanism, the logarithm of the folding and unfolding rate constants shows a linear dependence on the concentration of a chemical denaturant, such as guanidinium hydrochloride (GdnHCl). This relationship is typically represented by the classical two-state chevron plot, where the slopes of the folding and unfolding arms, indicated by mf and mu, describe how the rate constants for folding (kf) and unfolding (ku) vary as a function of denaturant concentration.

Results

Equilibrium unfolding of PDZ4, PDZ3–4, and PDZ2–6

A valuable approach for understanding the folding of complex multidomain systems is to compare the behavior of the domain in isolation with that in a multidomain context, which may include one or more contiguous domains [[24], [25], [26]]. Equilibrium unfolding of the PDZ4 domain, the PDZ3–4 tandem, and the PDZ2–6 supramodule of MAGI1 was monitored by intrinsic fluorescence. The protein contains a single tryptophan residue located in PDZ4, which allows the folding of this domain to be selectively observed in all constructs without interference from adjacent modules (Fig. 1). Samples were incubated at increasing concentrations of guanidinium hydrochloride in buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, and 10 mM DTT at 25 °C. The fluorescence intensity at 350 nm decreased progressively with increasing denaturant concentration, consistent with a cooperative unfolding transition. Data were well fitted by a two-state model, yielding a midpoint of 2.6 M and a ΔG°H2O of approximately 7 kcal mol⁻¹ for the isolated domain.

Measurements across pH from 3 to 10 revealed a decrease in apparent stability under acidic and basic conditions for all constructs (Fig. 3). The denaturation midpoints shifted slightly toward lower denaturant concentrations at low pH, notably, increasing the number of adjacent domains caused a small shift in the acidic pH dependence toward higher pH values (the onset of acid-induced destabilization occurs at less acidic conditions), as reported in Fig. 3B Despite these differences, the overall similarity among constructs suggested that the main effects of domain adjacency would emerge kinetically rather than at equilibrium.

Folding kinetics and the influence of domain context

Folding and unfolding kinetics were recorded by stopped-flow fluorescence monitored mixing experiments. Reactions were initiated by an eleven-fold dilution of protein equilibrated at high or low denaturant into the final refolding or unfolding buffers, maintaining identical buffer composition and ionic strength. In all cases, the fluorescence changes followed single-exponential time courses at all conditions, and the observed rate constants (kobs) were determined by exponential fitting (figure S2). The logarithm of kobs was then plotted as a function of denaturant concentration to generate the chevron plots.

For the isolated PDZ4 domain, the refolding limb of the chevron displayed a mild downward curvature at low denaturant concentration, indicating the transient population of a low-energy intermediate [27,28]. It is important to stress that, as previously noted, analysis of the chevron shape alone does not allow to unequivocally assign the intermediate to behave as on- or off-pathway species; yet, its presence confirms that PDZ4 folding deviates from a strictly two-state mechanism. The addition of a contiguous domain (PDZ3–4) returned a more pronounced curvature in the refolding limb, thus implying a stabilization of the intermediate. The kinetic partitioning constant Kmis, which quantifies the apparent equilibrium constant between the intermediate and the denatured states, increased substantially relative to the single domain. This behavior indicates that domain adjacency enhances the probability of populating a competing intermediate, consistent with non-native interdomain interactions forming during refolding.

Extending the supramodule further to five domains (PDZ2–6) accentuated these effects. The refolding rate decreased by approximately an order of magnitude, and Kmis increased accordingly. Because this additional stabilization of the detour appears only when domains other than PDZ4 are present, the most straightforward interpretation is that the slow phase arises from an misfolded ensemble stabilized by interdomain contacts. The progressive increase in curvature and slowing of refolding with domain number, reported in Fig. 2B, indicates that transient misfolding in MAGI1 is a collective phenomenon that extends beyond specific domain pairs and becomes more probable as the number of simultaneously unfolded modules increases.

pH modulates the kinetic trap in a construct-dependent manner

To examine how electrostatic interactions contribute to folding and misfolding, we measured the kinetic behavior of PDZ4, PDZ3–4, and PDZ2–6 between pH 3.0 and 10.0. Reactions were performed under the same conditions described above, using buffers appropriate to each pH range while maintaining constant ionic strength and DTT concentration. At every condition, single-exponential time courses were obtained, and the corresponding chevron plots were analyzed globally.

Fig. 4 and Table 1 summarize the results. In all constructs, both the apparent folding (kf) and unfolding (ku) rates exhibited a mild dependence on pH; however, kf display two titratable transitions centered around pH 4.5 and 8.5, whereas ku show a single titratable transition around pH 4.0. For isolated PDZ4, the curvature of the refolding limb and the extracted kinetic parameters changed little across this range, indicating that protonation events perturb the transition state but do not create or eliminate additional intermediates. In contrast, PDZ3–4 and PDZ2–6 displayed a clear stabilization of the competing kinetic phase under acidic conditions. At low pH, the refolding arm of the chevron became more depressed, and Kmis increased substantially compared to neutral pH, signifying that protonation enhances the lifetime or population of the misfolded ensemble. Moreover, we observe that at extreme pH values (both acidic and basic) the refolding amplitude progressively decreases relative to the unfolding amplitude, an effect that is most evident for the P3P4 and P2P6 constructs (data not shown). This trend is consistent with our hypothesis that, under these conditions, a fraction of the protein populates a misfolded species during refolding, thereby reducing the apparent refolding-associated signal change.

However, amplitude is a challenging parameter to interpret quantitatively, because it is strongly influenced by experimental factors such as the local microenvironment, pH-dependent effects on the signal itself, and small variations in protein concentration between experiments. We therefore base our analysis on the kinetic rate constants, which provide a more robust and quantitative framework for assessing folding/unfolding behavior across pH conditions.

The quantitative dependence is shown in Fig. 4B–D. The folding rate constant increased modestly at both low and high pH, while the unfolding rate accelerated sharply below pH 5. The largest pH sensitivity was observed for Kmis, which rose steeply under acidic conditions for PDZ3–4 and PDZ2–6 but remained essentially flat for PDZ4 alone. This observation suggests the presence of at least two ionizable groups that contribute to the stabilization/destabilization of the transition state. Fitting the experimental data with the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation we obtained a pKa of 4.4 ± 0.1 under acidic conditions and a pKa of 8.6 ± 0.2 under basic conditions. The latter can be attributed to the deprotonation of the cysteine residue located at position 882. Consistent with previous studies, cysteine residues may contribute to hydrophobic stabilization, and their deprotonation can lead to changes in local interactions that ultimately modulate the apparent kf [29]. Inspection of the Alphafold model structure (Q6RHR9-F1) reveals that there are two possible amino acids that could be responsible for this effect. Aspartic acid at position 871 and glutamic acid at position 901 may interact with arginine at position 873 and lysine at position 905, respectively (Fig. 5). This behavior demonstrates that pH modulates the interactions responsible for the off-pathway species only in multidomain contexts, consistent with a role of interdomain electrostatics in stabilizing the misfolded ensemble. Specifically, our results suggest that a protonation event with an apparent pKa of ∼4 (consistent with Asp/Glu) weakens or disrupts an intra-domain electrostatic interaction, leading to a destabilization of both the native state and the transition state, and therefore affecting both kf and ku.

In the multidomain constructs, however, we observe an additional effect: an increase in Kmis, consistent with an increased population and/or stabilization of a misfolded intermediate. This suggests an extra inter-domain contribution that is only present in the tandem architecture. We speculate that protonation (and the associated loss of charge) reduces electrostatic repulsion and/or removes polar constraints that, at neutral pH, keep the adjacent domains more separated; this may facilitate closer domain–domain approach and promote hydrophobic collapse between neighboring domains, thereby stabilizing the intermediate and increasing Kmis.

Discussion

The results presented here establish that the folding of the MAGI1 PDZ4 domain is strongly influenced by the presence of adjacent domains. Isolated PDZ4 populates a low-energy intermediate during refolding, reflected by curvature in the chevron plot, but its stability and kinetics are only weakly affected by pH. These features are typical of single PDZ domains and likely reflect transient structural rearrangements within the domain itself [30]. When neighboring domains are introduced, however, the kinetic behavior changes qualitatively: the curvature becomes more pronounced, and the refolding rate decreases in proportion to the number of domains present. These trends indicate that additional misfolded configurations become accessible when multiple domains unfold simultaneously, producing an off-pathway ensemble that competes with productive folding [7,10,13,15,16,31].

The current view of transient misfolding in multidomain proteins was first shaped by studies on titin and other Ig-like modules, where simultaneously unfolded neighbors can exchange structural elements through transient domain swapping [12,31,32]. Although this mechanism accounts for misfolding in small, well-structured tandems, more recent work on the X11 PDZ1–2 supramodule has suggested an alternative scenario in which transient misfolding arises from the collapse of the contiguous unfolded chain rather than from specific strand exchange [33]. In that model, the embryonic nucleus of one domain promotes non-specific compaction of its neighbor, generating a broad ensemble of mispaired conformations. The behavior of MAGI1 appears more consistent with this latter picture. In the five-domain construct, misfolding cannot be attributed to a single swapped topology but rather to a diffuse set of non-native contacts among multiple unfolded domains. The increased number of interfaces expands the configurational space available for such transient collapse, naturally explaining the progressive slowdown of refolding and the higher apparent Kmis observed as domain number increases. In this light, domain swapping may represent one extreme of a more general phenomenon in which the simultaneous denaturation of contiguous domains favors the transient formation of compact, non-productive interdomain arrangements that delay productive folding.

The dependence of misfolding on pH provides additional insight into its molecular origin. The fact that acidic pH stabilizes the detour only in multidomain constructs, and not in the isolated PDZ4 domain, implies that protonation events modulate interdomain rather than intradomain electrostatics. A plausible hypothesis is that acidic residues in PDZ4 capable of interacting with basic residues on neighboring domains become protonated near pH 4–5, reducing charge repulsion and transiently favoring closer association of unfolded regions. Structural inspection of the PDZ4 domain reveals several potential acidic–basic pairs that could contribute to such an effect, but at present their involvement remains speculative. In particular, structural inspection of the AlphaFold model points to Asp871 and Glu901 as plausible partners for Arg873 and Lys905, respectively, providing a mechanistic hypothesis for the observed stabilization (Fig. 5) [34]. It should be noticed, however, that since the intermediate is misfolded, its structural features and residue proximities are unlikely to mirror those of the native ensemble, making it difficult to draw firm mechanistic conclusions from analyses based solely on the native state.

Taken together, these results indicate that transient misfolding in MAGI1 arises from cooperative, context-dependent interactions between unfolded domains rather than from a fixed domain-swapping event. The single domain displays a modest kinetic curvature associated with a low-energy intermediate; the tandem can be rationalized by transient domain swapping; and the five-domain supramodule exhibits a more complex, less specific misfolded ensemble whose stability is modulated by pH. This progression demonstrates that supramodular organization amplifies the potential for non-native interdomain contacts and that these interactions can be tuned by solution conditions. In a broader context, such behavior highlights how the modular arrangement of scaffold proteins encodes not only structural adaptability but also kinetic plasticity, features that may underlie their ability to assemble and reorganize complex protein networks in the cellular environment.
