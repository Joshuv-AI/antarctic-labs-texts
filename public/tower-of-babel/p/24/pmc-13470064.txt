Djulis beverage attenuates gastric mucosal injury by modulating oxidative stress and inflammation
Authors: Fuad Sauqi Isnain, Tim Chi-Chen Lin, Hui-Yun Tsai, Nai-Chen Liao, Hsiao-Chi Wang, Pi-Jen Tsai, Yu-Kuo Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, a djulis (Chenopodium formosanum)-based beverage (DB) was developed using Taguchi optimization of enzymatic hydrolysis, and its gastroprotective effects were evaluated in an indomethacin-induced gastric injury model in rats, with an oat-based beverage (OB) as a cereal comparator. The optimized hydrolysis conditions (70 °C, pH 8, 90 min) yielded DB with enhanced protein-derived components and a richer phenolic and flavonoid profile than OB. Sensory evaluation indicated that DB exhibited a comparable appearance and texture to commercial beverages, although overall acceptability was modestly lower due to reduced sweetness and retronasal aroma. In vivo, DB pretreatment significantly attenuated gastric mucosal damage in a dose-dependent manner, as evidenced by reduced lesion area and improved histopathological features. High-dose DB (200 mg/kg) showed superior gastroprotective efficacy compared with OB at the same dose and effects comparable to omeprazole. Mechanistically, DB reduced lipid peroxidation, restored glutathione redox balance, enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, preserved prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) levels through modulation of cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), and downregulated NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signaling, including iNOS, TNF-α, and IL-6. These findings demonstrate that DB confers effective protection against NSAID-induced gastric injury through coordinated antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions, supporting its potential as a functional beverage for gastric health.

Introduction

Contemporary data indicate that gastric ulcers, a pathological condition affecting the gastric mucosa, are among the most prevalent gastrointestinal disorders worldwide, with an estimated prevalence of 5–10% of the global population1. This condition remains a persistent health concern and is associated with substantial clinical and socioeconomic burdens2. Multiple factors, including Helicobacter pylori infection, chronic psychological stress, alcohol consumption, and long-term use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), can trigger gastric lesions by disrupting mucosal defense mechanisms, such as mucus and prostaglandin synthesis, bicarbonate secretion, epithelial restitution, and adequate mucosal microcirculation3,4. Indomethacin, a widely prescribed NSAID, is well known for its ulcerogenic effects5. Excessive or prolonged indomethacin exposure reduces prostaglandin E₂ (PGE2) synthesis, promotes cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) expression via NF-κB activation, and strongly inhibits cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1), thereby compromising gastric mucosal protection6–8. In addition, indomethacin-induced gastric injury involves excessive oxidative and nitrosative stress, dysregulation of nitric oxide signaling, and activation of inflammatory responses, collectively leading to epithelial erosion, submucosal edema, and impaired mucosal repair9. These findings highlight the importance of the COX-prostaglandin pathway in maintaining gastric mucosal integrity during NSAID exposure.

In this context, functional foods rich in bioactive compounds have gained increasing attention as complementary nutritional approaches for supporting gastrointestinal health10. Djulis (Chenopodium formosanum Koidz.) is a traditional pseudocereal cultivated and consumed by indigenous populations in Taiwan, particularly in mountainous regions. It is characterized by its small, colored grains rich in betalain pigments, particularly betanin, and exhibits strong antioxidant potential. In addition, djulis is well adapted to diverse environmental conditions and has attracted increasing attention for its potential applications in sustainable agriculture and functional food development11. Djulis has been extensively studied for its nutritional value and reported functional properties, including antioxidant12, anti-inflammatory13, antihypertensive14, anticancer15, hypoglycemic16, and anti-adipogenic effects17. These biological activities are attributed to its rich composition of bioactive constituents, including essential amino acids, dietary fiber, betaine, and various phenolic compounds such as rutin, quercetin, chlorogenic acid, and saponins11,18. In addition, djulis is recognized for its high nutritional value, providing a balanced profile of macronutrients and micronutrients that further support its potential as a functional food ingredient. Recent preclinical studies further indicate that djulis extracts can mitigate oxidative damage, enhance endogenous antioxidant defenses, and attenuate inflammatory mediator activation11. Notably, our previous work demonstrated that an ethanolic extract of djulis hull significantly alleviated indomethacin-induced gastric mucosal injury in mice, accompanied by reduced ulcer area, restoration of antioxidant capacity, and modulation of inflammation-related markers, supporting the gastroprotective potential of djulis-derived components19.

Nevertheless, the biological efficacy of djulis-derived bioactive compounds is strongly influenced by their release and bioavailability, which depend on processing and formulation strategies10,20. Enzymatic hydrolysis has been reported as an effective approach to improve the accessibility and functional potency of phenolic compounds and to generate bioactive peptides with enhanced solubility, while potentially contributing to improved sensory attributes through the release of low-molecular-weight components21–24. The efficiency of enzymatic hydrolysis is affected by multiple factors, including temperature, pH, reaction time, and enzyme-substrate interactions, which can be systematically optimized through experimental design approaches. Given that NSAID-induced gastric injury is closely associated with oxidative imbalance and inflammatory responses that impair mucosal defense, the assessment of antioxidant status and inflammation-related markers provides supportive insight into the functional effects of dietary interventions25.

To date, the development of a djulis-based beverage and the evaluation of its gastroprotective effects against NSAID-induced gastric injury have not been fully explored. Therefore, the present study aimed to develop a djulis beverage (DB) and to assess its protective effects on gastric mucosal injury induced by indomethacin in a rat model. An oat-based beverage (OB), prepared under identical processing conditions, was included as a cereal-based comparator. This work seeks to provide experimental evidence supporting the potential application of a DB as a functional food for maintaining gastric mucosal health under NSAID exposure.

Results

Development and optimization of the DB

The enzymatic hydrolysis conditions for the development of the DB were optimized using a Taguchi L9 (33) orthogonal array design, with protein content selected as the response variable under a larger-the-better quality characteristic (Table 1). Dehulled djulis powder was subjected to multi-enzyme hydrolysis using α-amylase, cellulase, protease, and papain to facilitate the degradation of starch, cell wall polysaccharides, and proteins. Protein-derived components generated during hydrolysis were quantified using the Folin-phenol method. Signal-to-noise (S/N) ratios were calculated for each experimental run and further aggregated according to factor levels to evaluate the relative contribution of hydrolysis time, temperature, and pH (Table 2). The corresponding main effects plot (Fig. 1) clearly demonstrated that temperature and pH exerted a greater influence on hydrolysis efficiency than reaction time. Based on S/N analysis, the optimal hydrolysis conditions were identified as 90 min, 70 °C, and pH 8. These conditions were subsequently validated by a confirmation experiment, in which the experimentally obtained S/N ratio slightly exceeded the predicted value and remained within the expected range for a larger-the-better characteristic (Table 3), confirming the robustness and reproducibility of the optimized parameters. Accordingly, the present study fixed the proportions of djulis, water, and enzymes, while systematically optimizing the controllable processing variables. An OB was prepared under the same Taguchi-optimized conditions and served as a cereal-based comparator in subsequent analyses. The basic nutritional compositions of DB and OB were determined by an accredited laboratory using CNS- and AOAC-based standard methods and are presented in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Per 100 mL, DB contained higher protein and total fat contents than OB, whereas total carbohydrate, energy, and sodium levels were lower. These differences can be attributed to both the intrinsic nutrient composition of djulis and the effectiveness of the optimized enzymatic hydrolysis process, which likely enhanced the release of protein-derived components and reduced starch-associated fractions. To evaluate the overall abundance of phenolic compounds in DB and OB, total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC) were first determined. As shown in Supplementary Table S3, DB exhibited a significantly higher TPC than OB (p < 0.05), while TFC was readily detectable in DB but remained below the detection limit in OB. Given that flavonoids are well-recognized free-radical scavengers capable of modulating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, these results indicate that DB possesses a substantially enriched pool of bioactive phenolic constituents. To further characterize the specific phenolic and flavonoid components contributing to this enrichment, the phenolic profiles of DB and OB were subsequently analyzed by HPLC-UV. Representative chromatograms of mixed standards, DB, and OB are presented in Fig. 2. The analysis targeted major phenolic acids (gallic acid, esculetin, catechin, vanillic acid, and epicatechin) and flavonoids (vitexin and rutin). Quantitative results revealed that DB contained significantly higher levels of gallic acid and esculetin than OB (p < 0.05), while catechin, vanillic acid, epicatechin, vitexin, and rutin were detected exclusively in DB (Table 4). The combined increases in TPC, TFC, and individual phenolic compounds observed in DB suggest that the optimized enzymatic hydrolysis strategy contributed to the enhanced phenolic composition of the djulis-based beverage and may underlie its superior biological potential. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the Taguchi approach effectively identified robust enzymatic hydrolysis conditions for DB preparation, enabling the development of a djulis-based beverage with enhanced protein-derived components and a distinct phenolic profile compared to OB. Sensory acceptability of the developed DB

The sensory acceptability of the developed DB was evaluated in comparison with a commercial reference beverage using a nine-point hedonic scale. The structured sensory evaluation results are summarized in Table 5, and the overall sensory profiles are illustrated in Fig. 3. With respect to olfactory attributes, including sweetness and aroma intensity, no significant differences were observed among DB, the commercial reference beverage (commercial beverage A), and the quinoa-oat beverage (commercial beverage B) (p > 0.05). Similarly, glossiness did not differ significantly between DB and the reference product, indicating comparable surface appearance. In contrast, DB exhibited a significantly lighter color intensity than the reference beverage (p < 0.05). This difference is likely attributable to the use of black soybean milk in the commercial reference, which imparts a darker hue, whereas DB retains a lighter appearance despite similar brightness scores. Taste-related parameters revealed that both initial sweetness and swallowing sweetness of DB were not significantly different from those of the reference beverage (p > 0.05). However, DB exhibited a significantly lower aroma intensity during oral perception, suggesting that volatile release during mastication may be less pronounced than in the commercial product. Textural attributes, including viscosity and graininess, did not differ significantly among the samples, indicating that the enzymatic hydrolysis process produced a mouthfeel comparable to that of existing cereal-based beverages. Despite these similarities across multiple sensory dimensions, the overall acceptability score of DB was significantly lower than that of the commercial reference. The reduced overall acceptability of DB appears to be primarily associated with its relatively weaker retronasal aroma and lower perceived sweetness during swallowing. In this context, the lower acceptability of DB should be interpreted not as a formulation failure, but as an inherent trade-off associated with minimizing added sugars and preserving bioactive components. The radar chart (Fig. 3) further illustrates the sensory profile of DB relative to the commercial reference. DB closely matched the reference beverage in appearance-related and olfactory attributes, while divergences were mainly observed in taste perception and overall acceptance. These findings indicate that matching visual and aroma characteristics alone is insufficient to ensure consumer preference, and that sweetness perception during consumption plays a decisive role in acceptability. Importantly, the sensory performance of DB was achieved without the use of added sugars or flavor enhancers that could compromise its functional properties. Given the demonstrated gastroprotective effects of DB in vivo, as shown in subsequent sections, the present sensory evaluation provides a realistic baseline for future formulation refinement. Effects of DB supplementation on indomethacin-induced gastric mucosal injury

The gastroprotective potential of the developed DB against indomethacin-induced gastric mucosal injury was first evaluated by macroscopic examination of gastric lesions. As shown in Fig. 4, extensive hemorrhagic lesions were observed in the INDO group, whereas the Control group exhibited intact gastric mucosa with no visible damage. In contrast, rats pretreated with DB showed markedly attenuated mucosal injury, with both the low-dose (DBL) and high-dose (DBH) groups displaying smaller hemorrhagic lesion areas compared with the INDO group. Notably, DB supplementation exerted a dose-dependent protective effect. At the same administration dose (200 mg/kg), the DBH group exhibited a significantly smaller gastric lesion area than the OBH group, indicating a superior protective efficacy of the djulis-based formulation over the OB. Importantly, the observed gastroprotective effects of DB were not accompanied by adverse systemic changes. Throughout the experimental period, body weight increased steadily in all groups, with no significant differences among the Control, INDO, DBL, DBH, OBH, and OM groups (Supplementary Fig. S1). In addition, no significant differences were observed in absolute organ weights or organ-to-body weight ratios of the liver, kidney, and spleen among the experimental groups (Supplementary Table S4), indicating the absence of treatment-related organ hypertrophy or atrophy. Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels, a sensitive biomarker of hepatic injury, also remained comparable among all groups (Supplementary Fig. S2), suggesting that DB supplementation did not induce hepatotoxicity under the experimental conditions. Together, these findings confirm that the reduction in gastric lesion severity observed in DB-treated rats reflects a genuine gastroprotective effect rather than a consequence of systemic toxicity or compromised physiological status. Quantitative assessment of gastric lesion area and preventive index further confirmed these macroscopic observations (Table 6). Indomethacin administration resulted in a lesion area of 18.81 ± 2.35 mm2. This value was significantly reduced to 8.95 ± 0.85 mm2 in rats receiving DB at 100 mg/kg (DBL) and was further decreased to 3.30 ± 0.38 mm2 in the DBH group. Importantly, the lesion area in the DBH group was significantly lower than that observed in both the DBL and OBH groups, demonstrating a clear dose-response relationship and highlighting the superior gastroprotective efficacy of DB at higher doses. Correspondingly, the preventive index increased in a dose-dependent manner with DB supplementation, approaching the protective efficacy observed in the OM group. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DB supplementation effectively alleviates indomethacin-induced gastric mucosal injury in a dose-dependent manner and provides greater protection than the OB at equivalent doses. Histopathological evaluation of gastric mucosal injury

Histopathological examination of gastric tissues stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) was performed to further characterize the gastroprotective effects of the DB. Representative micrographs at low magnification (Fig. 5A) and higher magnification (Fig. 5B) illustrate the structural alterations of the gastric mucosa among experimental groups. In the control group, the gastric mucosa exhibited an intact epithelial layer with well-organized glandular architecture and no apparent pathological alterations. As shown in Fig. 5A and B, there was no evidence of epithelial cell loss, inflammatory cell infiltration, or submucosal edema, indicating preserved mucosal integrity under normal conditions. In contrast, rats administered indomethacin (INDO group) displayed pronounced histopathological damage. The INDO group exhibited extensive epithelial cell loss (Fig. 5A, B, a), accompanied by marked inflammatory cell infiltration within the mucosa (Fig. 5A, B, b) and obvious submucosal edema (Fig. 5A, B, c). These alterations reflect severe disruption of the gastric mucosal barrier and are characteristic features of NSAID-induced gastric injury. Pretreatment with DB resulted in a dose-dependent attenuation of these histopathological changes. In the low-dose DB group (DBL), partial preservation of the epithelial lining was observed, along with reduced inflammatory infiltration and less pronounced submucosal edema compared with the INDO group. More prominent protective effects were evident in the high-dose DB group (DBH), in which epithelial continuity was largely maintained, inflammatory cell infiltration was minimal, and submucosal edema was markedly alleviated. At the same dose level (200 mg/kg), the OB group also showed some improvement relative to the INDO group, particularly with respect to epithelial loss; however, residual inflammatory infiltration and edema were still apparent and were more pronounced than those observed in the DBH group. Omeprazole, used as a positive control, effectively preserved mucosal architecture, showing minimal epithelial damage and substantial suppression of inflammatory infiltration and edema. DB alleviates oxidative stress and enhances antioxidant defense in the gastric mucosa

The effect of DB on gastric mucosal oxidative stress was first evaluated by measuring malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, a well-established indicator of lipid peroxidation (Fig. 6A). Administration of indomethacin markedly increased gastric MDA levels compared with the control group, indicating enhanced oxidative damage to the gastric mucosa. In contrast, DB supplementation significantly reduced MDA levels in a dose-dependent manner (p < 0.05). The high-dose DB group (DBH) exhibited a greater reduction in MDA than the low-dose group (DBL), with values comparable to those observed in the control and OM groups. Although the OB at the same dose (OBH) also decreased MDA levels relative to the INDO group, its effect was less pronounced than that of DBH, suggesting a stronger inhibitory effect of DB on lipid peroxidation. To further assess the antioxidant capacity of DB, components of the GSH system were analyzed. As shown in Fig. 6B, indomethacin significantly depleted gastric GSH levels compared with the control group (p < 0.05). DB supplementation, particularly at the high dose, significantly restored GSH levels relative to the INDO group, exceeding those observed in the OBH group and approaching levels comparable to OM. In parallel, the GSH/GSSG ratio, an indicator of cellular redox balance, was markedly reduced in the INDO group compared with controls (Fig. 6C). Pretreatment with 200 mg/kg DB significantly improved this ratio (p < 0.05), demonstrating superior restoration of redox homeostasis compared with the same dose of OB. The activities of key antioxidant enzymes were subsequently examined. As shown in Fig. 6D, indomethacin administration significantly suppressed GSH peroxidase (GPx) activity compared with the control group (p < 0.05). DB supplementation dose-dependently enhanced GPx activity, with DBH exhibiting significantly higher activity than the INDO and OBH groups and values comparable to OM. GSH reductase (GRd) activity, which is essential for regenerating GSH from its oxidized form, was also significantly reduced following indomethacin exposure (Fig. 6E). Both low- and high-dose DB supplementation significantly increased GRd activity relative to the INDO group (p < 0.05), with high-dose treatment (DBH group) exerting a greater restorative effect than the OB at the same dose and achieving levels comparable to OM. Finally, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was evaluated as a marker of superoxide radical scavenging (Fig. 6F). Exposure to indomethacin significantly reduced SOD activity compared with the control group (p < 0.05). In contrast, DB supplementation significantly increased SOD activity in both DBL and DBH groups (p < 0.05), with DBH showing greater activity than the INDO and OBH groups and levels comparable to OM. Collectively, the results presented in Fig. 6 demonstrate that DB supplementation effectively attenuates indomethacin-induced oxidative stress by suppressing lipid peroxidation and restoring the GSH system and antioxidant enzyme activities. Discussion

Djulis is a nutrient-dense pseudocereal rich in essential amino acids, dietary fiber, phenolic compounds, minerals, and vitamins26, making it a promising raw material for the development of functional beverages. In this study, Taguchi-optimized enzymatic hydrolysis was employed to enhance protein-derived components, with hydrolysis efficiency prioritized in response to the growing interest in plant-derived bioactive peptides, which are typically composed of 2–20 amino acids and are recognized for their high stability and diverse physiological activities27. Notably, carbohydrate-degrading enzyme treatments in cereal matrices, including oats, have been reported to enhance the extractability of soluble phenolic acids and strengthen antioxidant-related activities, supporting the view that targeted enzymatic processing can effectively improve the bioactive profile of cereal-based products24. The observed influence of temperature, pH, and reaction time on hydrolysis efficiency is consistent with previous studies28. DB exhibited significantly higher phenolic and flavonoid contents than OB, with compounds such as gallic acid, catechin, epicatechin, rutin, and vitexin identified as major contributors. These phenolic compounds are well documented for their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities29–32, suggesting that the enriched phytochemical composition of DB underlies its enhanced biological effects.

The lower overall acceptability of DB appears to be primarily associated with reduced retronasal aroma and sweetness perception. This pattern is consistent with consumer studies on plant-based dairy alternatives, where overall liking is influenced by the balance between sweetness perception, aroma release, and mouthfeel33. Similarly, β-glucan fortification in quinoa milk has been shown to improve physical stability but may negatively affect sensory acceptance when viscosity becomes excessive, highlighting the importance of optimizing texture and flavor simultaneously34. Compared with commonly studied pseudocereals such as quinoa, djulis offers distinct advantages for beverage development in the present context due to its rich composition of phenolic compounds and betalain pigments17,18, its cultural relevance as a traditional Taiwanese crop, and its demonstrated suitability for formulation into a bioactive beverage. These characteristics further highlight the unique potential of djulis as a functional ingredient with added value beyond conventional cereal-based beverages. However, excessive or non-selective enzymatic hydrolysis may generate bitter peptides and off-flavors, emphasizing the need for controlled enzymatic processing. Consistent with these observations, the reduced acceptability of DB may reflect a trade-off between maintaining bioactive components and achieving desirable sensory properties. Achieving an optimal balance between nutritional value and consumer preference remains a major challenge in functional beverage development35. The sensory results also suggest that visual and aroma characteristics alone are insufficient to ensure consumer acceptance, and that sweetness perception during consumption plays a critical role. Given that DB was formulated without added sugars or flavor enhancers, its current sensory profile represents a baseline for further refinement. Strategies such as mild sweetness modulation through natural low-calorie sweeteners, aroma enhancement, or blending with complementary natural ingredients may improve palatability while preserving the integrity of bioactive constituents, including phenolic compounds. In addition, optimization of processing conditions or the incorporation of natural aroma-rich ingredients may help enhance volatile release during oral consumption and improve retronasal aroma perception. Such approaches could further increase consumer acceptance without compromising the functional value of DB.

The present study demonstrates that DB exerts a significant gastroprotective effect against indomethacin-induced gastric injury. This effect was evidenced by reduced lesion area, improved histopathological features, and a dose-dependent increase in the preventive index, consistent with previous reports on plant-derived bioactive compounds19,36. As expected, the OM group demonstrated near-complete protection against indomethacin-induced injury, consistent with its established role as a reference gastroprotective agent37. A comparable dose-dependent reduction in gastric lesion severity has been reported for djulis-derived preparations in chemically induced gastric injury models19. In particular, pretreatment with djulis hull extract significantly reduced macroscopic gastric damage and ulcer area, an effect that was closely associated with enhanced antioxidant capacity and attenuation of inflammatory responses. These findings reinforce the notion that bioactive components present in djulis contribute to gastric mucosal protection and support the dose-dependent gastroprotective effects observed for DB in the present study. Previous studies have demonstrated that bioactive peptides can attenuate chemically induced gastric lesions in a dose-dependent manner36. Given that DB is enriched with protein-derived components generated through enzymatic hydrolysis, the progressive reduction in gastric lesion severity observed with increasing DB doses is likely associated with peptide-mediated protective effects, in addition to other bioactive constituents naturally present in djulis. The superior gastroprotective effects of DB compared with OB may be attributed to its richer and more diverse phytochemical composition, particularly its phenolic constituents identified by HPLC analysis. DB contained higher levels and a broader spectrum of phenolic compounds, including gallic acid, catechin, epicatechin, rutin, and vitexin, which are known to possess strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities38. These bioactive compounds can contribute to the attenuation of oxidative stress and suppression of inflammatory signaling in gastric tissue, thereby enhancing mucosal protection. In contrast, OB exhibited a comparatively simpler phenolic profile, which may partly explain its weaker protective effect under the same experimental conditions. The enhanced efficacy of DB is therefore likely associated with the synergistic action of multiple phytochemicals, rather than a single active component. This compositional advantage highlights the functional potential of djulis-based formulations in mitigating chemically induced gastric injury. Consistent with this, similar histopathological improvements have previously been reported in djulis-based interventions, where treatment significantly alleviated epithelial disruption, inflammatory cell infiltration, and submucosal edema in indomethacin-induced gastric injury. These observations suggest that djulis-derived bioactive constituents consistently contribute to the preservation of gastric mucosal architecture under NSAID-induced stress19. These histopathological findings are consistent with previous reports describing epithelial loss, mucosal edema, and inflammatory infiltration as hallmark features of indomethacin-induced gastric injury39. Moreover, bioactive peptides and phenolic compounds have been shown to enhance epithelial restitution and suppress inflammatory responses in chemically induced gastric injury models40. Given that DB is enriched in peptide fractions generated during enzymatic hydrolysis, together with phenolic and flavonoid compounds, these bioactive constituents likely act synergistically to stabilize epithelial structure, reduce inflammatory infiltration, and limit submucosal edema.

Oxidative stress plays a central role in NSAID-induced gastric injury, leading to lipid peroxidation and impairment of antioxidant defenses. The reduction in MDA levels and restoration of antioxidant enzyme activities observed in DB-treated groups indicate that DB effectively attenuates oxidative damage. This inhibitory effect on lipid peroxidation is consistent with previous findings demonstrating that phenolic antioxidants, such as propyl gallate, markedly suppress indomethacin-induced MDA formation while restoring endogenous antioxidant systems in gastric tissue. Together, these results highlight the importance of phenolic-mediated redox regulation in mitigating NSAID-induced oxidative damage41. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that indomethacin induces oxidative stress through enhanced lipid peroxidation in gastric tissues25,42. The reduction in MDA levels following DB supplementation may be attributed to the presence of bioactive peptides, phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and betanin generated during enzymatic hydrolysis, which have been reported to effectively scavenge free radicals and attenuate oxidative damage14,40. The reduction in gastric GSH levels observed in the INDO group further supports the disruption of antioxidant defenses. This finding is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that indomethacin impairs mucosal antioxidant capacity by reducing GSH availability43. Restoration of both GSH content and the GSH/GSSG ratio further indicates that DB effectively counteracts indomethacin-induced oxidative imbalance. Given that GSH serves as a primary intracellular antioxidant in the gastric mucosa, maintenance of GSH homeostasis is critical for protecting against oxidative injury44. The restoration of GPx, GRd, and SOD activities further supports the role of DB in reinforcing gastric antioxidant defenses. This is consistent with previous reports showing that indomethacin impairs enzymatic antioxidant systems, leading to hydrogen peroxide accumulation and exacerbation of mucosal injury45. In line with this, bioactive peptides have been reported to restore GPx activity and mitigate gastric oxidative damage25,46, while diminished GRd activity is commonly associated with enhanced lipid peroxidation and impaired antioxidant recycling47. The recovery of SOD activity observed in DB-treated groups is also consistent with earlier findings demonstrating that enhancement of superoxide scavenging capacity contributes to protection against gastric mucosal injury43,48. The superior antioxidant effects of DB compared with OB may be closely associated with its enriched phytochemical composition, particularly its higher content and diversity of phenolic compounds identified by HPLC analysis. Phenolic constituents such as gallic acid, catechin, epicatechin, rutin, and vitexin are well documented to exhibit antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, including the scavenging of reactive oxygen species and the modulation of endogenous antioxidant defense systems49. These compounds may contribute to the observed restoration of the GSH system and antioxidant enzyme activities in gastric tissue. In contrast, the comparatively lower phenolic content of OB may limit its capacity to counteract oxidative stress, thereby resulting in weaker protective effects under the same experimental conditions. Collectively, these findings indicate that DB enhances antioxidant capacity and maintains redox homeostasis in the gastric mucosa, thereby contributing to its gastroprotective effects.

PGE2 plays a critical role in maintaining gastric mucosal integrity and protecting gastrointestinal function. Depletion of gastric PGE2 is a well-recognized consequence of NSAID-induced gastric injury and contributes to ulcer progression and delayed mucosal healing50. The significant reduction in PGE2 levels observed in the INDO group indicates disruption of prostaglandin-mediated mucosal defense. COX-1, a constitutively expressed “housekeeping” enzyme, is essential for maintaining gastric epithelial protection through basal prostaglandin production51. The suppression of COX-1 expression following indomethacin exposure suggests impairment of this protective pathway. In contrast, DB supplementation restored COX-1 expression, which is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that bioactive peptides can promote COX-1 expression and PGE2 synthesis in gastric injury models52. Given that DB contains enzymatically generated peptides, similar peptide-mediated mechanisms may contribute to the recovery of prostaglandin synthesis observed in the present study. In contrast, COX-2 is an inducible isoform associated with inflammatory responses and gastric tissue injury51,53. The marked increase in COX-2 expression in the INDO group is consistent with previous studies showing that indomethacin induces COX-2 as part of the inflammatory cascade54. DB supplementation effectively suppressed COX-2 overexpression, suggesting attenuation of inflammation-associated prostaglandin dysregulation. The superior effects of DB compared with OB may be attributed to its higher phenolic content, which has been reported to modulate cyclooxygenase activity and prostaglandin production. This may contribute to the restoration of COX-1 expression and suppression of COX-2 overactivation observed in the present study, thereby supporting maintenance of the COX-PGE2 axis under indomethacin challenge. Collectively, these findings indicate that DB preserves prostaglandin-mediated gastric defense through coordinated regulation of the COX-PGE2 axis, which plays a critical role in maintaining gastric mucosal integrity and contributes to protection against NSAID-induced injury.

Indomethacin-induced gastric injury is characterized by excessive inflammatory responses mediated by nitric oxide and pro-inflammatory cytokines. Elevated iNOS expression leads to increased nitric oxide production, which contributes to the formation of cytotoxic peroxynitrite radicals and delays mucosal healing55. Consistent with this, the marked increase in iNOS expression observed in the INDO group indicates enhanced inflammatory damage. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 play central roles in gastric inflammation by promoting immune cell infiltration, reducing mucosal blood flow, and impairing tissue repair45,50,56. The suppression of TNF-α and IL-6 expression by DB suggests effective attenuation of inflammatory responses. NF-κB is a key transcription factor regulating inflammatory gene expression, and its activation is a hallmark of NSAID-induced gastric injury57. The observed reduction in NF-κB expression in DB-treated groups indicates that DB modulates upstream inflammatory signaling pathways, thereby limiting downstream mediator production. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that DB effectively attenuates indomethacin-induced gastric inflammation by suppressing NF-κB activation and its downstream pro-inflammatory mediators, including iNOS, TNF-α, and IL-6. The greater inhibitory effects observed in DB compared with OB may be attributed to its higher content of bioactive constituents, particularly phenolic compounds, which have been reported to modulate NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signaling58. This anti-inflammatory action likely contributes to the preservation of gastric mucosal integrity under NSAID-induced stress.

In this study, a DB was successfully developed using a Taguchi experimental design to optimize enzymatic hydrolysis conditions. The optimal parameters (70 °C, pH 8, and 90 min) effectively enhanced protein-derived components and facilitated the release of bioactive constituents. Compared with the OB, DB exhibited higher phenolic and flavonoid contents and a more diverse phytochemical profile. In vivo, DB pretreatment significantly alleviated indomethacin-induced gastric mucosal injury, as evidenced by reduced ulcer area, improved histopathological features, and a higher preventive index. Mechanistically, these effects are likely associated with the enriched phytochemical composition of DB, particularly its phenolic constituents, which may contribute to the modulation of oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways. DB attenuated oxidative stress by reducing lipid peroxidation and restoring antioxidant defenses, preserved prostaglandin-mediated gastric protection by maintaining COX-1 expression, suppressing COX-2 induction, and increasing PGE2 levels, and inhibited inflammatory signaling through downregulation of NF-κB, iNOS, TNF-α, and IL-6. Overall, DB demonstrated superior gastroprotective efficacy compared with the OB and showed effects comparable to OM for several parameters. These findings support DB as a promising functional beverage for protecting gastric mucosal integrity under NSAID exposure, with further formulation refinement warranted to improve sensory acceptability. However, the present findings are based on an animal model, and further studies are required to confirm these effects in humans, elucidate the detailed mechanisms involved, and identify the key bioactive constituents responsible for the gastroprotective activity of DB. Future studies are also warranted to determine whether DB influences gastric acidity or intragastric pH, thereby providing a more comprehensive pharmacological profile of its gastroprotective actions.

Methods

Materials, chemicals, and reagents

Djulis used in this study was harvested from the farm of National Pingtung University of Science and Technology (Pingtung, Taiwan). Indomethacin (purity ≥ 98.5%), quercetin (purity ≥ 95%), Folin–Ciocalteu’s phenol reagent, aluminum chloride hexahydrate, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Gallic acid (purity ≥ 99%) was obtained from KM3 Scientific Corporation (New Taipei, Taiwan). Analytical-grade ethanol and methanol were supplied by Taiwan Tobacco and Liquor Corporation (Pingtung, Taiwan) and ECHO Chemical Co., Ltd. (Miaoli, Taiwan), respectively. Skim milk powder (Anchor®) used for membrane blocking in Western blot analysis was obtained from Fonterra Co., Ltd. (Auckland, New Zealand). Standard laboratory reagents for protein extraction and electrophoresis, including sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), Tris base, glycine, glycerol, ammonium persulfate (APS), and N,N,N′,N′-tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED), were obtained from BioShop Canada Inc. (Ontario, Canada). Acrylamide/bis-acrylamide solution (30%, 29:1) was purchased from Zeju Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Kaohsiung, Taiwan). Enzymes used for djulis hydrolysis, including α-amylase, cellulase, protease, and papain, were supplied by Jiuh Hsing Instrument Co., Ltd. (Kaohsiung, Taiwan). All other chemicals and reagents used in this study were of analytical grade and obtained from standard commercial suppliers unless otherwise specified.

Preparation of DB and OB

Hull-removed djulis grains were used as the primary ingredient for the preparation of the DB. Enzymatic hydrolysis was conducted by adding α-amylase (1%), cellulase (0.75%), protease (0.1%), and papain (0.1%), which were selected to target specific grain components, including starch, cellulose, and proteins. The same enzymatic hydrolysis procedure was applied to the preparation of the OB to ensure comparability between formulations. Following enzymatic treatment, the hydrolysate slurry was filtered and homogenized, and subsequently sterilized at 121 °C for 40 min to obtain the final DB and OB products. All preparation procedures were performed according to the method described by ref. 34, with minor modifications.

Each experimental run generated an S/N ratio, which was used to identify the optimal processing conditions. Proximate composition analysis

The proximate compositions of DB and OB were analyzed by an accredited laboratory using CNS- and AOAC-based Official Methods (21st ed., AOAC International, 2019). The analyses included determination of energy, protein, total fat, carbohydrates, sugars, and sodium, following standardized analytical procedures. The nutritional composition of each beverage was determined and expressed per 100 mL to describe the macronutrient profiles of the formulations. These data were used to characterize the basic nutritional properties of DB and OB and to support subsequent experimental evaluations.

Determination of TPC

The TPC of the DB and OB was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric method with minor modifications. Briefly, appropriately diluted beverage samples were mixed with Folin–Ciocalteu’s phenol reagent, followed by the addition of sodium carbonate solution. After incubation at room temperature in the dark, the absorbance was measured at 765 nm using a microplate reader (Epoch 2, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). Gallic acid was used as the calibration standard, and TPC was expressed as micrograms of gallic acid equivalents (μg GAE) per mL of beverage. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the mean values were used for subsequent statistical analysis.

Determination of TFC

The TFC was measured using the aluminum chloride colorimetric method. Diluted samples were reacted sequentially with aluminum chloride solution, and the absorbance was recorded at 415 nm. Quercetin was used as the reference standard, and TFC was expressed as micrograms of quercetin equivalents (μg QE) per mL of beverage. All assays were conducted in triplicate, and the results were expressed as mean values for subsequent statistical analysis.

Determination of phenolic compounds by HPLC

Phenolic compounds in DB and OB were analyzed by HPLC-UV. Freeze-dried samples were reconstituted in high-purity water and filtered through a 0.45 μm PTFE membrane prior to analysis. An aliquot (20 μL) of the filtrate was injected into the HPLC system. Chromatographic separation was performed using a reverse-phase Mightysil RP-18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm; Kanto Chemical, Tokyo, Japan) on an HPLC system equipped with a UV-Vis detector (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.25% formic acid and 2% methanol in ultrapure water) and solvent B (100% methanol), applied under gradient elution conditions. The flow rate was set at 1.0 mL/min, and detection was carried out at 280 nm. Phenolic compounds were identified and quantified by comparison with authentic standards, including gallic acid, esculetin, catechin, vanillic acid, epicatechin, vitexin, and rutin.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation was conducted using a nine-point hedonic scale (1 = dislike extremely; 9 = like extremely) to assess consumer acceptance. A randomized three-digit coding system was applied to ensure blinding during evaluation. Three beverage samples were evaluated: a commercial organic unsweetened red djulis-black soybean beverage (commercial beverage A), which was designated as the reference sample, the DB, and a commercial quinoa-oat beverage (commercial beverage B). Samples were served in a randomized and counterbalanced order to minimize order effects, and identical cups were used for all samples. The sensory study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Antai Tian-Sheng Memorial Hospital (IRB No. 23-107-B), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. A total of 15 healthy adult panelists (aged 20–25 years) were recruited for the evaluation. Sensory attributes assessed included aroma, appearance, taste, texture, and overall acceptability. Panelists first evaluated aroma and appearance, followed by taste, texture, and overall acceptability according to the evaluation form. Between samples, Lipton Yellow Label tea was provided as a palate cleanser to minimize carryover effects.

Animal experimental design

Six-week-old male Wistar rats (176–200 g) were obtained from BioLASCO Taiwan Co., Ltd. (Taipei, Taiwan). Animals were housed under controlled laboratory conditions (23 ± 2 °C, relative humidity 50–70%, 12 h light/12 h dark cycle) with free access to standard chow and water and were allowed to acclimate for one week prior to experimentation. After acclimation, rats were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 6 per group): (A) Control (distilled water); (B) INDO (distilled water + 100 mg/kg indomethacin); (C) DBL (100 mg/kg DB + 100 mg/kg indomethacin); (D) DBH (200 mg/kg DB + 100 mg/kg indomethacin); (E) OBH (200 mg/kg OB + 100 mg/kg indomethacin); and (F) OM (30 mg/kg OM + 100 mg/kg indomethacin). The sample size was determined based on previous studies using similar indomethacin-induced gastric injury models. All treatments were administered once daily by oral gavage for seven consecutive days, with dosing volumes adjusted according to initial body weight. On day 6, animals were fasted for 24 h with free access to water. On day 7, the final dose of each treatment was administered, followed 2 h later by oral gavage of indomethacin to all groups except the control group to induce gastric mucosal injury. Six hours after indomethacin administration, animals were euthanized by CO2 inhalation until respiratory arrest, and death was confirmed. Blood and tissue samples were collected immediately for subsequent analyses. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of National Pingtung University of Science and Technology (Approval No. NPUST-111-01). No animals or data points were excluded from the analysis.

Histopathological examination and gastric ulcer assessment

After euthanasia, the stomach was excised, gently rinsed with ice-cold saline (0.9%), and opened along the greater curvature. Gastric mucosal surfaces were photographed using a digital camera, and ulcerated areas were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). For histopathological analysis, gastric tissues were fixed in neutral-buffered formalin, processed using standard procedures, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for microscopic examination.

Antioxidant and biochemical analyses

Blood samples were collected immediately after euthanasia via cardiac puncture and allowed to clot at room temperature for 1 h. Serum was obtained by centrifugation at 1370×g for 15 min at 4 °C, followed by a second centrifugation at 16,400×g for 15 min at 4 °C to ensure clarity. Serum samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis. Serum ALT activity was measured to evaluate potential hepatotoxicity using GPT/ALT test strips (REF 77170) with an automated biochemical analyzer (SPOTCHEM EZ SP-4430, Arkray, Kyoto, Japan), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The ALT results are presented in the Supplementary Materials.

Gastric tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for biochemical analyses. Tissue homogenates were prepared under ice-cold conditions using RIPA buffer (containing Tris-HCl, NaCl, NP-40, sodium deoxycholate, and SDS) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail to minimize protein degradation. The homogenates were centrifuged at 16,400×g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were collected for biochemical analyses. Protein concentrations were determined using a Bradford-based colorimetric assay with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard. The absorbance was measured at 595 nm using the same microplate reader described above.

Total glutathione (T-GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) levels were determined using a Total Glutathione/GSSG Colorimetric Assay Kit (Elabscience, Houston, TX, USA; Cat. No. E-BC-K097-M). Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity was measured using a Glutathione Peroxidase Activity Assay Kit (Elabscience, Houston, TX, USA; Cat. No. E-BC-K096-M). Glutathione reductase (GRd) activity was determined spectrophotometrically by monitoring the oxidation of NADPH at 340 nm in the presence of GSSG. One unit of GRd activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to oxidize 1 nmol of NADPH per minute. Total SOD activity was measured using a photochemical method based on the inhibition of nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) reduction. Absorbance was recorded at 560 nm, and one unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to inhibit 50% of NBT reduction. Lipid peroxidation was assessed by measuring MDA levels using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) method. Following acid-catalyzed reaction with thiobarbituric acid and heat treatment, the resulting fluorescent adduct was extracted with n-butanol and quantified using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (excitation 515 nm, emission 555 nm). 1,1,3,3-Tetramethoxypropane (TMP) was used as the standard for MDA quantification. All antioxidant enzyme activities and MDA levels were normalized to protein content.

Determination of gastric PGE2

To evaluate alterations in the prostaglandin pathway associated with gastric mucosal protection, PGE2 levels in gastric tissue were determined. Tissue samples were homogenized in ice-cold phosphate buffer (10 mM, pH 7.4) containing indomethacin (10 μM) to prevent prostaglandin degradation, followed by centrifugation at 16,400×g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected after two consecutive centrifugation steps to obtain clarified tissue extracts. PGE2 concentrations were measured using a competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) based on the acetylcholinesterase (AChE)-Ellman method, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA; Cat. No. 500141). Absorbance was measured at 412 nm using the same microplate reader described above, and PGE2 levels were quantified using a standard curve and normalized to tissue protein concentration.

Western blot analysis

Gastric tissues were carefully excised and immediately processed for protein extraction. Homogenization was performed using ice-cold RIPA buffer (containing Tris-HCl, NaCl, NP-40, sodium deoxycholate, and SDS) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail to prevent protein degradation. The resulting lysates were centrifuged at 16,400×g for 30 min at 4 °C. The clarified supernatants were transferred to fresh tubes and subjected to a second centrifugation step under identical conditions to ensure the complete removal of residual cellular debris. The final supernatants were collected and used as total protein extracts. Total protein concentrations were determined using a Bradford-based colorimetric assay, and absorbance was measured using the same microplate reader described above. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Following electrophoretic separation, proteins were transferred onto methanol-activated polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (AmershamTM HybondTM P 0.45 μm, GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Buckinghamshire, UK) under a constant voltage of 90 V for 2 h at 4 °C. To minimize nonspecific binding, membranes were blocked with phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (PBS-T) and 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against COX-1 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; Cat. No. 4841), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2; Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. No. 12282), NF-κB (Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. No. 8242), iNOS (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA; Cat. No. 18985-1-AP), IL-6 (Proteintech; Cat. No. 26404-1-AP), and TNF-α (Proteintech; Cat. No. 17590-1-AP), with β-actin (Proteintech; Cat. No. 81115-1-RR) used as the internal loading control. After primary antibody incubation, membranes were washed with PBS-T and subsequently incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG and anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany; Cat. No. AP187P and 12–349, respectively) diluted in 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagent (AmershamTM ECL SelectTM, GE Healthcare Life Sciences). Chemiluminescent signals were captured using an iBrightTM Imaging System (Model CL750, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). The intensities of protein bands were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Differences among multiple groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for post hoc comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) or mean ± standard deviation (SD), as specified in the corresponding figure legends. Different lowercase letters indicate statistically significant differences among groups.
