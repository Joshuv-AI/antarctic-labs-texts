Introduction

Bezoars are mixed and indigestible substances that accumulate in the gastrointestinal tract especially form and get stuck in the gastrointestinal tract [[1], [2], [3]]. Based on their primary composition, bezoars are categorized into several types, including phytobezoars, trichobezoars, and others [1,3,4]. One of the rare cases are the patients who refer to medical centers for swallowing plaster [5]. One of the substances that is rarely consumed with water in a suicide attempt is plaster [6,7], which is readily available due to its increasing use in construction and low price [8]. White plaster powder, primarily composed of calcium sulfate hemihydrate (CaSO₄·0.5H₂O), undergoes an exothermic hydration reaction when mixed with water, forming gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) [9]. This reaction, represented by the equation CaSO₄·0.5H₂O + 1.5H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O [9], generates significant heat, potentially reaching temperatures up to 60 °C, which may cause thermal injury [10].

The formation of a plaster cast in the gastrointestinal tract may be asymptomatic or may present with symptoms such as abdominal pain, dysphagia, nausea, or vomiting. In severe cases, it may lead to life-threatening complication such as perforation, obstruction, and sepsis [11]. Using radiology and endoscopy to diagnose bezoars is being considered as a more precise method of diagnosis [12]. Opaque casts are visible on radiographs after plaster ingestion [7]. Treatment approaches depend on the size of the bezoar, symptoms and when the patient refers to ED [5]. Treatment includes surgical intervention for complicated cases and gastric irrigation for uncomplicated cases [4]. In more cases, longitudinal gastrotomy is required, but in few cases, gastric resection is required [13].

We present here a case of gastric bezoars due to ingestion of plaster and describe our experiences with imaging findings and the applied surgical treatments of this patient because of his obstruction symptoms and time interval from swallowing. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist [14].

Case introduction

A 47-year-old man with a known history of depressive disorder presented to the emergency department with complaints of epigastric pain, nausea, and vomiting. He had intentionally ingested a large quantity of construction plaster mixed with water two days prior to admission. Following ingestion, he experienced multiple episodes of non-bloody, non-bilious vomiting. The epigastric pain was constant. He reported two defecations in the 48 h before admission.

The patient was not receiving psychiatric treatment at the time and was on a daily dose of 10 cc of methadone. He had no history of previous abdominal surgeries.

Physical examination

On presentation, the patient appeared ill but not toxic. He was fully alert and oriented to time, place, and person. Vital signs were stable: blood pressure 130/70 mmHg, heart rate 80 bpm, and temperature 37 °C. Cardiopulmonary examination was with normal heart sounds (S1, S2) and clear lung auscultation.

On abdominal examination, a firm, non-tender, non-rebounding mass was palpated in the epigastric region.

Laboratory and imaging studies

Laboratory tests revealed leukocytosis with a white blood cell count of 15,360/mm3 and 83.8 % segmented neutrophils. Other laboratory values were within normal limits.

Abdominal and chest radiographs demonstrated radiopaque material in the gastrointestinal tract, consistent with plaster ingestion. There were no signs of perforation, and no free air was observed under the diaphragm (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

Endoscopy and surgical intervention

Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed a large, hardened plaster bezoar occupying the stomach, causing mucosal injury. The bezoar was adherent to the gastric mucosa, complicating endoscopic intervention. Due to its large size (approximately 20 × 15 × 20 cm) and the procedure was limited to diagnostic visualization. An urgent longitudinal gastrotomy was performed, revealing a solid plaster cast adherent to the gastric mucosa (Fig. 3) but without evidence of severe inflammation or scarring. The plaster cast was successfully removed, and smaller, passable fragments were observed in the intestines, which did not require further intervention.

Intraoperative photo of the removed plaster cast from the stomach.

Postoperative course

The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for three days, followed by two days in the general ward. Prophylactic antibiotics, including ceftriaxone and metronidazole, were administered before surgery. No signs of surgical site infection or postoperative complications were noted.

On postoperative day three, the patient was able to tolerate a semi-solid diet. He was discharged in good general condition on postoperative.

At a two-week follow-up, the patient was in stable condition with no reported complications. He remained asymptomatic and required no further interventions at that time.

Discussion

Foreign body ingestion in adult is more common in those with psychiatric disorders, developmental delays, alcohol intoxication and for secondary gain [15]. Ingestion of plaster in a suicidal attempt is one of the rare causes of acute formed bezoar. The hydration of calcium sulfate hemihydrate (CaSO4.0.5H2O), commonly known as plaster of Paris, leads to the crystallization of gypsum (calcium sulfate dehydrate, CaSO4.2H2O). Hydration in the paste hardens the plaster and increases its strength [9].

The clinical presentations of foreign body ingestion range from asymptomatic to epigastric pain, vomiting, dysphagia, pharyngeal discomfort, and chest pain [11]. Areas of acute angulation or physiological narrowing are where impaction, perforation, or obstruction most frequently occurs [16]. Foreign body ingestion often does not require much intervention. Based on literature, around 80 % of swallowed foreign objects can pass through the digestive system without any need for medical intervention, 10–20 % require endoscopic removal and only about 1 % require surgery [17]. The main manifestation in our patient was abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting, introducing complete gastric outlet obstruction.

Radiographs typically reveal radiopaque masses with clear borders, as seen in our case, which demonstrated a massive gastric concretion. Endoscopy further confirmed the extent of the bezoar and mucosal injury, guiding the decision for surgical management. Depending on the amount of plaster ingested, the symptoms and the time interval between ingestion and hospital arrival, our treatment approach will be different. In cases previously reported, some cases have required surgical intervention while conservative method was applied on some others.

Sanei Taheri et al. reported two cases with acute formed gastric bezoars due to plaster ingestion [4]. They were brought by their family to the emergency department in a short time after ingestion (approximately 5 h) and successfully treated by gastric irrigation. Yegane et al. reported a case presented with a large non- movable mass, with manifestations of obstructions. Therefore, gastrotomy and removal of foreign body was performed [13].

Here, we report a patient who presented to ED after ingestion of plaster for attempted suicide. During endoscopic investigation of the patient, esophagus was normal but large size of bezoar in stomach did not allow gastroenterologist to down endoscope tube further. Therefore, longitudinal gastrotomy and removal of hardened plaster was performed.

One factor that was responsible for surgical approach in this patient was the time interval between ingestion and arrival to hospital. This case presented to ED two days after ingestion. Therefore, the ingested plaster had hardened to such an extent that it was impossible to fragment and remove it. The Second factor was the size of hardened plaster that made it impossible to pass through intestine or endoscopic removal.

Our patient, with a history of untreated depression, ingested plaster as a suicidal act. Research indicates that intentional foreign body ingestion may be documented as a method of self-harm or, less commonly, a suicide attempt in individuals with mental health disorders [15]. However, this behavior is not exclusive to individuals with mental health disorders and can also be observed in other groups, such as children who accidentally ingest objects due to exploratory behavior [18], or individuals with pica, which involves the compulsive consumption of non-food substances due to various causes, including nutritional deficiencies such as iron deficiency anemia [19]. In cases where ingestion is intentional and associated with psychological distress, as observed in this patient, psychiatric evaluation and intervention following medical stabilization are essential to address underlying mental health issues and prevent recurrence.

A review of the literature (Table 1) reveals variability in outcomes based on the amount of plaster ingested, time to presentation, and treatment approach. Cases presenting early (2–6 h) often respond to gastric irrigation or observation [4,5,20], while delayed presentations frequently require surgical intervention. Notably, a case reported by Germerott et al. resulted in death due to airway obstruction from plaster, highlighting the potential lethality of this method in complex suicides [6]. These findings suggest that early recognition and intervention are critical to improving outcomes.

Conclusion

Plaster ingestion for suicidal intent is a rare but life-threatening cause of gastric bezoar formation, often requiring urgent medical or surgical intervention. This case illustrates the challenges of managing a large, hardened plaster bezoar in a patient presenting 48 h post-ingestion, where longitudinal gastrotomy was necessary due to complete gastric outlet obstruction. While conservative approaches like gastric irrigation or endoscopic removal may be adequate for early presentations, delayed cases with large or hardened bezoars frequently necessitate surgery, despite the risk of complications such as wound infection. Mental health issues in these cases show the importance of proper evaluation and follow-up.
