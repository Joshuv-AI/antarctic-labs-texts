A H2-bioreactor for mitochondrial rehabilitation in infected bone defects regeneration
Authors: Chunru Kong, Chao Si, Haofeng Liu, Yawen Wang, Jiakai Qiao, Xiaoduo Tang, Hongchen Sun, Junhu Zhang, Bei Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The repair of infected bone defects (IBDs) presents a significant clinical challenge due to recurrent infections and inflammation. Characterized by reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts and mitochondrial dysfunction, IBDs generates to a vicious oxidation-inflammation cycle that impairs bone remodeling. To address this, we developed a double-network hydrogel scaffold composed of phenylboronic acid-modified quaternary chitosan and glycidyl methacrylated poly(vinyl alcohol) (QP-P), crosslinked via dynamic boronic ester bonds and covalent bonds, loaded with magnesium microspheres (Mg spheres) (QP-P/Mg). This double-crosslinking network confers excellent injectability and tissue adhesion while integrating potent antibacterial, ROS-scavenging, pro-angiogenic, and osteogenic functions. Notably, Mg spheres enable controlled hydrolysis through a boronic ester-magnesium ion (Mg2+) complexation mechanism, providing sustained release of hydrogen (H2) and Mg2+, with H2 potently alleviating oxidative stress and restoring mitochondrial homeostasis via the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway and Mg2+ promoting vascularized bone regeneration. In a rat IBDs model, QP-P/Mg achieved substantial bone regeneration, with a BV/TV ratio of 45.32 % ± 8.22 % after 4 weeks, representing a 6.27-fold improvement over controls. These findings underscore the potential of this H2-releasing hydrogel scaffold with multifunctional properties to promote vascularized bone regeneration in IBDs through ROS elimination and mitochondrial rehabilitation, offering promising clinical translational opportunities for the treatment of IBDs.

Introduction

Infected bone defects (IBDs) are microbial-induced bone injuries characterized by chronic inflammation, impaired healing, and difficulty in treatment [1,2]. The complex anatomy and open environment of the oral and maxillofacial region predispose to recurrent infections that hinder recovery [3]. Current clinical management employs infection control through debridement and antibiotics followed by bone grafting [4], yet achieves only 60–75 % bacterial clearance with 20–30 % recurrence rates [5]. Moreover, prolonged antibiotics usage leads to the development of drug resistance and inflammatory complications, while grafting procedures increase re-infection risks and socioeconomic burdens [6,7]. IBDs therapeutic challenges stem from complex interplay of microbial invasion, immune defense, and imbalanced bone remodeling [8]. Amongst, reactive oxygen species (ROS) is one of the most detrimental factors. Invading bacteria and host immune defense cells generate a tremendous amounts of ROS through various pathways [9]. As a result, uncontrolled ROS storms initiated, which triggered vicious damage to the microenvironment. Excessive ROS disrupts mitochondrial function in multiple ways, including mitochondrial membrane damage via lipid peroxidation and the opening of mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), impairment of ATP production via tricarboxylic acid cycle inhibition, mitochondrial autophagy inducement, and mtDNA oxidative damage via the production of oxidized bases such as 8-hydroxy 2′-deoxyguanine or 5-hydroxycytosine [[10], [11], [12]]. In turn, mitochondrial dysfunction further exacerbates ROS production, resulting in a ROS-burst vicious loop.

In addition, ROS amplifies local inflammation and leads to impaired bone remodeling [13,14]. Immune cells generate ROS, which consecutively elevates inflammation by promoting NF-κB activation and release of NLRP3 inflammasome vesicles, thus creating a mutually reinforcing cycle that continuously amplifies the inflammation and oxidation levels [15]. Persistent and uncontrolled inflammation disrupts bone homeostasis by accelerating osteoclastogenesis via immune cell-derived RANKL, and by inhibiting osteoblast activity via suppressing Wnt and IGF-1 pathway and the downstream osteogenic transcription [16,17]. Moreover, inflammation disrupts angiogenesis through pro-inflammatory cytokine release, MMP-mediated extracellular matrix degradation, and endothelial dysfunction via altered NO and adhesion molecules such as P-selectin and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) [18], ultimately reducing oxygen and bio-factor supply and hindering bone regeneration [19]. Therefore, the development of efficient ROS-eliminating approach is the key to successful IBDs reparation.

To alleviate the ROS-dominated IBDs microenvironment, an “infection control-oxidative stress alleviation-tissue regeneration” therapeutic principle is proposed. Hydrogen (H2), owing to its superior penetration across the mitochondria membrane, potential to target toxic ROS, and negligible side effects, has shown potent ROS-eliminating capacity in mitochondria reactivation. For example, studies have shown that H2 promoted bone reparation via efficient oxidative stress alleviation capacity, mainly through the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) pathway, and sirtuin 1/peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ coactivator-1α pathway [20,21]. However, rapid release of H2 hinders its application. To address this, Chen et al. designed polyhydroxyalkanoate-encapsulated CaSi2 nanoparticles to slow H2 release that remodeled the aging microenvironment via antioxidation and anti-inflammation [22]. Zhao et al. doped hydrogen into TiO2 nanorods to achieve on-demand release of H2 by photocatalysis for modulating arthritic microenvironment [23].

In this work, a double-network hydrogel scaffold (QP-P/Mg) that targeted ROS-mediated IBDs regeneration was developed. It incorporates glycidyl methacrylated poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVAMA), phenylboronic acid (PBA)-modified quaternary chitosan (QCS) (QCSPBA), and magnesium microspheres (Mg spheres) via dynamic boronic ester bonding and covalent bonding, endowing it with excellent injectability, tissue adhesion, antimicrobial, antioxidant, angiogenic, and osteogenic properties. Firstly, the PBA group on QCSPBA binds to the hydroxyl group on PVAMA to form reversible dynamic boronic ester bonds, conferring on the QP-P/Mg self-healing and shear-thinning properties, hence injectability to accommodate the irregular IBDs [24]. Secondly, the double bonds between PVAMA molecules form a covalently cross-linked network, enhancing QP-P/Mg strength and adhesion. Thirdly, the Mg spheres, which hydrolysis is strictly modulated by the complexation of boronic ester bonds and magnesium ion (Mg2+), ensuring an on-demand release of H2 and Mg2+, and simultaneously avoid abrupt pH changes. The released Mg2+ not only accelerates vascularization and osteogenesis regeneration [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29]], but exerts a synergistic antibacterial effect on QCSPBA by increasing bacterial membrane permeability [30]. Collectively, this QP-P/Mg system efficiently transformed the ROS-excessive IBDs microenvironment via reconstructing mitochondrial homeostasis, and demonstrated outstanding osteogenesis effects in IBDs rat models, thus providing potential value for future clinical exploration (Scheme 1).Scheme 1Schematic representation of the design strategy and applications of QP-P/Mg for repairing IBDs.Scheme 1

Materials and methods

Materials

All reagents were obtained from commercial suppliers and used without further purification. Poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA; Mw: 13000–23000), glycidyl methacrylate (≥99.0 %), 4-carboxyphenylboronic acid (PBA; ≥98.0 %), Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; ≥99.9 %), deuterium oxide (D2O; ≥99.9 %) were purchased from Adamas-beta (China). Quaternary chitosan (QCS; 90 % degree of substitution) were purchased from Yuanye (China). Lithium phenyl(2, 4, 6-trimethylbenzoyl)phosphinate (LAP; ≥98.0 %), N-(3-(Dimethylamino)propyl)-N′-ethyl carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC; ≥98.5 %), N-hydroxysulfosuccinimide (NHS; ≥98.0 %) were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Magnesium microspheres were provided by Tangshan Wei Hao Magnesium Powder Co., Ltd.

Synthesis and characterization of PVAMA and QCSPBA

PVAMA was synthesized by modification. 2 g of PVA was completely dissolved in 18 mL deionized water, followed by the addition of 13 mL glycidyl methacrylate. The pH was adjusted to 1.5 by using concentrated HCl. The reaction was allowed to progress for 24 h at 60 °C with continuous stirring while protected from light. The polymer was precipitated with pre-cooled acetone and redissolved with deionized water. The solution was lyophilized after dialysis, and the final product PVAMA was obtained. QCSPBA was synthesized by an amide reaction. 5 g of QCS was dissolved in 300 mL of deionized water. 1.27 g PBA, 2.39 g NHS, and 3.98 g EDC were dissolved in 180 mL DMSO, and the solution was stirred for 4 h, The two solutions were then mixed with continuous stirring for 36 h at room temperature. The final product QCSPBA was obtained by lyophilization and dialysis. The chemical structures of these two hydrogels were characterized by 1H NMR and FT-IR.

Fabrication and characterization of QP-P/Mg

In the fabrication of the hydrogel scaffold, a standard equal-volume mixing strategy was adopted. Subsequent performance tests confirmed that QP-P/Mg prepared with an equal-volume mixing ratio exhibits ideal properties. First, Mg spheres were uniformly dispersed in a 10 % w/v PVAMA solution under continuous mechanical stirring, followed by mixing with an equal volume of 3 % QCSPBA solution. The mixture was then exposed to UV light (λ = 365 nm) for 5 min to prepare the QP-P/Mg. The hydrogel without Mg spheres was prepared by the same method as the control group, which was defined as QP-P. The 3D porous structure of the hydrogels was observed by SEM. The molecular structure was characterized by EDS-SEM, Raman spectra, XPS, and XRD.

Rapid gelation, injectable, and adhesive properties of QP-P/Mg

For the rapid gelation properties, the two components of the hydrogel scaffold were respectively injected into two holes of the double helix tube and then extruded for injection. The injection process was recorded. For injectable properties, the hydrogel scaffold was first prepared and loaded into the spring, and then the injection process was recorded. For the adhesive properties, the two gel solutions were mixed and adhered to the surface of pig skin, irradiated by UV light to cure it, and subjected to bending, washing, immersion.

Rheology tests of QP-P/Mg

Rheology tests of hydrogels were conducted on a rheometer (Waters Corporation, China). In the strain sweep experiments, the strain varied from 0.01 % to 100 % with a fixed angular frequency of 10 rad s−1 at 25 °C. In self-healing experiments, the strain was switched from 1.0 % (for 100 s) to 900 % (for 100 s), and 3 cycles were performed with a fixed angular frequency of 10 rad s−1 at 25 °C.

H2 release test of QP-P/Mg

Quantification of total H2 release using a hydrogen electrode probe. To test the release profile of H2 from QP-P/Mg and P/Mg in vitro, QP-P/Mg and P/Mg were placed and immediately sealed in a glass bottle containing 10 mL pure water (pH = 7.0). The gas taken at different time points was measured by a hydrogen microelectrode. The Mg spheres concentration of the gel loading was increased to 100 μM to make the results more intuitive and within the instrumental detection interval.

Visualization of H2 release using a microscope. QP-P/Mg and P/Mg were placed in 24-well plates, and microscopic images of the gels were acquired at different time points.

Antioxidant capability of QP-P/Mg

The hydroxyl radical scavenging capability of QP-P/Mg was detected using ESR. 500 μL TEMPO (100 mmol L−1), 200 μL 30 % H2O2, and 200 μL QP-P/Mg (The concentration of Mg: 1 mM, 5 mM, 10 mM) were mixed for ESR analysis.

The antioxidant capacity of QP-P/Mg was determined by the KMnO4 reduction experiment, ABTS, and DPPH assays. The KMnO4 reduction experiment was to soak the QP-P/Mg containing different concentrations of Mg spheres in 30 μg mL−1 of KMnO4 solution (3 mL) for 30 min and then recorded the absorbance at 525 nm using a UV–visible–near-infrared spectrophotometer. The ABTS assay was performed using the Total Antioxidant Capacity Assay Kit with the ABTS method, and the experiment was performed according to the protocol of the kit. For the DPPH assay, the DPPH Free Radical Scavenging Capacity Assay Kit was used according to the protocol of the kit.

In vitro antibacterial experiment

The Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus, ATCC 29213) and the Gram-negative Escherichia coli (E. coli, ATCC 25922) were used to evaluate the antibacterial effect of QP-P/Mg. The materials of each group were added to the 48-well plate, PBS was added to the control group, and then 200 μL bacterial solution (106 CFU/mL) was added to each well, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. At the end of incubation, 20 μL of diluted co-cultured bacterial suspension was placed on Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plates (Hope Bio, China), coated evenly, and cultured in an incubator for 24 h. The bacterial colony growth was observed and scanned, and the antibacterial rate of the materials in each group was calculated. The calculation formula is as follows:Antibacterialrate(%)=(Nc−Nt)/Nc×100

(Nt: Number of colonies in the experimental group, Nc: Number of colonies in the control group)

The glass slippers were put into the 24-well plate, then the materials of each group were added, and PBS was added to the control group. 500 μL bacterial solution (108 CFU/mL) was added to each well, and incubated at 37 °C for 72 h. After the biofilm was formed, the medium was removed, and the material extract of each group was added and incubated for 30 min. The bacterial biofilms were stained with equal volumes of SYTO 9 and PI, according to the protocol of the Live/Dead Bacterial Double Stain Kit (Maokang, China). The images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

500 μL bacterial solution (108 CFU/mL) was exposed to various samples and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Then the suspension was centrifugally collected and fixed with 2.5 % glutaraldehyde for 4 h. At room temperature, the bacterial samples were dehydrated with each gradient alcohol (30 %, 50 %, 70 %, 90 %, 100 %) for 15 min. The dried bacterial sample was dropped on the silicon wafer and then sprayed with gold after natural air drying. The morphology of bacteria was observed by scanning electron microscopy.

In vitro biosafety

BMSCs were extracted from the femurs of Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, using Alpha Modified Eagle Medium (α-MEM) (Gibco, China), supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin, at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 in a humidified incubator.

BMSCs were seeded onto 96-well plates and cultured with extracts of different samples for 1, 3, and 5 days. Cell viability was evaluated with Cell Counting Kit-8 (Solarbio, China).

BMSCs were seeded onto 48-well plates and cultured with extracts of hydrogel samples from each group for 1, 3, and 5 days. Live and dead cells were then stained with a Live/Dead Cell Staining Kit (Beyotime, China), and the images were captured with high-resolution fluorescence microscopy.

In vitro osteogenic properties

BMSCs were seeded in 24-well cell culture plates, and after the cells were attached to the wall, an osteogenic induction medium containing various hydrogel sample extracts was added for further culture. On day 7, ALP staining (Beyotime, China) and semi-quantitative analysis were performed. On day 14, ARS staining (OriCell, China) and quantitation were performed. Furthermore, qPCR was utilized to evaluate osteogenic gene expression levels (e.g. ALP, BMP-2, OSX, and OCN).

To assess the osteogenic properties of BMSCs under oxidative stress, H2O2 was added to the medium to simulate the osteogenic microenvironment after oxidative stress. After 7 and 21 days, ALP and ARS staining were performed to detect osteogenic activity. Furthermore, qPCR was utilized to evaluate osteogenic gene expression levels (e.g. Alp, Bmp-2, Osx, and Ocn).

RT-qPCR was used to detect the expression of Alp, Bmp-2, Osx, and Ocn. Total RNA was extracted from cells using an RNA extraction kit (Yeasen, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and RNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription from 1 μg of RNA using Hifair® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (Yeasen, China) for reverse transcription synthesis. RT-qPCR was performed using Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Primer sequences are shown in Table S1.

The assessment of protein expression levels of Runx-2 and OCN in co-cultured BMSCs was conducted using IF staining on day 7. BMSCs were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min and immersed in 0.25 % Triton X-100 for 20 min, and after being blocked with 5 % BSA for 30 min, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies to Runx2 (Proteintech, China) and OCN (Proteintech, China). Subsequently, Runx2 and OCN were incubated with FITC-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (Proteintech, China, 1:1000) for 60 min at room temperature, and Phalloidin was added for 30 min, and DAPI was added. Immunofluorescence images were observed and recorded by fluorescence microscopy, and fluorescence intensities were analyzed semi-quantitatively using ImageJ software.

In addition, protein expression levels of COL-1, RUNX-2, and OCN were assessed using Western Blot. Total cellular proteins were obtained by adding protease inhibitors to RIPA lysis buffer, and protein samples were sampled, electrophoretically separated, and then transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were closed with a Quick Blocking Solution for 20 min at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies against COL-1, RUNX-2, and OCN (Proteintech, China) overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were then incubated with secondary antibodies (Proteintech, China) at 4 °C. The samples were then incubated with a secondary antibody (Proteintech, China) for 60 min to detect protein bands.

In vitro angiogenic properties

HUVECs were inoculated into 6-well plates, and when the cell density increased to the appropriate density, the medium in each well was replaced with the extract of various hydrogel samples. Each hole was marked with a vertical line with a gun head, marking 0 h from the mark. The cell migration at different time points was recorded by an optical microscope.

The blood vessel formation ability of the composite function hydrogel was evaluated using a tube formation assay. Matrigel was added to a 96-well plate at a volume of 50 μL, and the plate was maintained at 37 °C for 30 min to allow the gel to solidify. HUVECs were then seeded into each well. The culture medium was replaced with the extract of various hydrogel samples for each group. After 6 h of incubation, the capillary network formation state was observed and recorded using an optical microscope. The tube network parameters were calculated using ImageJ software.

Measurement of mitochondrial ROS scavenging and mitochondrial function

The BMSCs with growth confluency up to 80 % were treated with 0, 100, 150, and 200 μM H2O2 for 24 h, and the cell survival was measured using CCK-8 assays. Subsequently, BMSCs were placed in 150 μM H2O2 to establish an oxidative stress model, and cell survival was measured using CCK-8 assays after co-culture with different gels. Intracellular ROS levels were measured using a DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. BMSCs were co-cultured with different gels under H2O2 conditions for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by removing the medium, configuring DCFH-DA fluorescent stain (MCE, USA, 1 μM) with PBS, incubating for 15 min at 37 °C, removing the stain and washing twice with PBS, and observing under confocal laser scanning microscope. Subsequently, the same method was used to detect the mitochondrial membrane potential of the cells using the JC-1 fluorescent probe (MCE, USA, 1 μM). Protein levels of Nrf2 (1:1000, Proteintech, China), HO-1 (1:1000, Proteintech, China), and NQO1 (1:1000, Proteintech, China) were measured using immunofluorescence and Western blotting.

Rat alveolar bone defects infection model

All animal experiments received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the College of Basic Medical Sciences, Jilin University (Ethics number: 2024544). Eight-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, sourced from the Laboratory Animal Centre at Jilin University, were used to create alveolar defects. Sixteen rats were divided into four groups (4 rats per group): control, GS (gelatin sponge), QP-P, and QP-P/Mg. After the rats were anesthetized, a rectangular defect measuring 3 mm in length, 2 mm in width, and 1 mm in depth was created on the buccal aspect of the mandible at the site of the first molar using a 1 mm diameter drill bit. 10 μL of S.aureus suspension (108 CFU/mL) was dropped into the defect site, followed by filling the cavity with each experimental material (the control group did not receive any treatment). Finally, the wound was sutured. At 4 and 6 weeks postoperatively, the rats were euthanized, and the mandibular specimens were collected for evaluation, including assessment of in vivo antibacterial activity, Micro-CT analysis, and histological examination.

At four weeks after implantation, tissue samples from the defect site were collected using sterile cotton swabs, and bacteria were isolated using ultrasound. The bacterial suspension was plated on agar and incubated overnight before being counted and photographed.

The specimens were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde and scanned using a Micro-CT scanner (Hiscan XM) with a source voltage of 80 kV, beam current of 100 μA, and resolution of 25 μm. The images were reconstructed with Hiscan Reconstruct software. CTan software was used to calculate BV/TV, BMD, Tb.N, and Tb.Th. H&E, Masson, and immunohistochemical staining (IL-1β, TNF-α, Col Ⅰ, and OCN) were conducted to assess the anti-infection and osteogenic properties of the composite hydrogels.

At four weeks, rat hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys were harvested, dehydrated, and embedded for histological examination to assess in vivo toxicity via H&E staining.

Statistical analysis

In this study, all experiments were independently conducted more than three times. All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and images shown in this article represented the typical results of 3 independent experiments. All quantitative data were collected using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software, and statistical significance among multiple groups was analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Discrepancies between groups were indicated with asterisks: #p < 0.05, ##p < 0.01, ###p < 0.001, ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001 were considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of the QP-P/Mg

An injectable double-network hydrogel scaffold, which was composed of QCSPBA and PVAMA and crosslinked via dynamic boronic ester bonds and covalent bonds, was designed and synthesized for efficient drug release and osteogenesis-promotion in IBDs regeneration.

QCS is a naturally derived cationic polymer with excellent tissue adhesion and broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity. The carboxyl group of PBA was activated using N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), followed by amide coupling with QCS to yield QCSPBA (Fig. 1a). Structural characterization by 1H NMR spectroscopy confirmed successful synthesis, with the presence of benzene ring proton resonances at 7.3–7.7 ppm in QCSPBA being consistent with the spectral features of PBA, confirming covalent grafting (Fig. S1a). Poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), a biocompatible synthetic polymer with propriate mechanical strength, was modified with glycidyl methacrylate (GMA) to introduce photo-crosslinkable vinyl moieties, resulting in the synthesis of PVAMA (Fig. 1a). To promote IBDs regeneration, sustained ROS eradication and long-lasting promotion of bone regeneration are required. To achieve this, we introduced Mg spheres that undergo hydrolysis to generate H2 with ROS-scavenging ability and simultaneously release Mg2+. The Mg spheres were homogeneously dispersed in the PVAMA solution, followed by a mixture with QCSPBA to form boronic ester bonding for rapid gelation and then double-bond crosslinking to increase the hydrogel strength, leading to the synthesis of the ultimate double-network hydrogel scaffold (Fig. 1b). Gelation occurred immediately after the components were injected and mixed through a double-tube spiral needle, which enabled easy operation and injectability for facilitated drug delivery. SEM results demonstrated a porous microstructure of QP-P/Mg and dispersed Mg spheres within the network (Fig. 1c). FT-IR analysis revealed characteristic absorption bands in the PVAMA, QCSPBA, and QP-P samples of corresponding functional groups. The stretching vibration peaks of the C=C bond were observed at 1635 cm−1 indicating the presence of unsaturated carbon-carbon bonds, the absorption peak at 1545 cm−1 confirmed the presence of a benzene ring, and the B-O absorption peak was observed at 1150-1200 cm−1, formed by the combination of the phenylboronic acid moiety of QCSPBA and the hydroxyl group of PVAMA, thus suggesting a successful synthesis of QP-P (Fig. 1d).

Rheological analysis showed that with the addition of Mg2+, the energy storage modulus (G′) of the gel was slightly improved, indicating that Mg2+ complexed with the boronic ester bonds. The double bonds curing further improved QP-P/Mg strength to match the mechanical property of bone (Fig. 1e). Meanwhile, shear stress alternation experiments proved that QP-P/Mg possessed a shear-thinning and self-healing property, endowing injectability to meet the demand of irregular IBDs (Fig. 1f). In addition, the self-healing properties of QP-P/Mg were visually demonstrated through macro-level cutting, healing, and mechanical stretching tests (Fig. 1g). Coupled with the strong bonding of QP-P/Mg to porcine skin under various mechanical stresses (Fig. 1h, Fig. S2), which guaranteed robust adhesion capacity of the scaffold to bone defects to maintain long-lasting therapeutic contact.

Due to the high corrosion potential of Mg, it rapidly corrodes, causing local pH increases and H2 accumulation, which hinders the tissue healing process [31]. To address this, Wang et al. prepared Mg/PLLA composite membranes to regulate the degradation rate of Mg, effectively eliminating these side effects and achieving long-term bone regeneration [32]. In this work, we utilized the complexation of boronic ester bonds with Mg2+ to delay the hydrolysis of Mg spheres, and assessed the performance of QP-P/Mg through compositional and degradation analyses. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS)-scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses showed that the Mg element was surrounded by C, N, and O, suggesting that the Mg spheres were homogeneously dispersed in QP-P/Mg (Fig. S3). X-ray diffraction (XRD) peaks corresponding to the (100), (002), (101), (102) and (110) lattices of monomeric magnesium (PDF:35–0821) showed that the lattices of monomeric magnesium were still present after 5 days of hydrolysis, hinting that the gel could retard the hydrolysis of Mg spheres (Fig. 1i). The Raman spectrum of QP-P/Mg showed distinct characteristic peaks at 560-580 cm−1, 640-660 cm−1, and 900-920 cm−1 (Fig. 1j), and these peaks were attributed to the complexation of Mg2+ to the boronic ester bonds, and the peaks were stable under prolonged hydrolysis conditions, confirming that the boronic ester bonds had a strong trapping capacity of Mg2+ [33]. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) results solidly proved that Mg2+ remained in QP-P/Mg after 5 days of hydrolysis. Besides, high-resolution scans of the Mg 1s region showed a distinct peak located at 1304.5 eV, indicating that even after prolonged hydrolysis, Mg2+ remained in a stable coordinated state in QP-P/Mg (Fig. 1k and l). Therefore, we successfully developed an injectable self-healing hydrogel scaffold based on a double-network of dynamic boronic ester bonding and covalent bonding. This hydrogel scaffold enabled a sustained release of Mg2+ through the complexation between Mg2+ and boronic ester bonds, which potentially satisfy the need for long-term bone tissue regeneration.

QP-P/Mg exhibits excellent antioxidant Properties and effectively scavenges free radicals

Excessive ROS impedes IBDs reparation by disrupting mitochondrial function, amplifying inflammation and inhibiting osteogenesis. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop biomaterials with potent ROS-scavenging capacity to reverse oxidative damage and recover mitochondrial functions. Traditional antioxidants such as vitamin E have low bioavailability, non-specific free radical scavenging properties, and toxic side effects like increased cancer risk and bleeding risk at high doses [34]. Meanwhile, visomitin (SKQ1), a mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant, exhibits efficient antioxidant activity, but its high-dose application may also cause side effects such as increased cell death and decreased antioxidant effect [35]. In recent years, gas therapy has gained increasing popularity in biomedical applications and clinical transitional expectation owing to outstanding advantages such as low toxicity and targeted delivery [36]. H2, as a novel therapeutic antioxidant, possesses excellent penetration across the mitochondria membrane, potential to target toxic ROS, and minimal side effects, thus holding good promise in ROS-elimination and mitochondrial dysfunction improvement.

The H2 release kinetics were evaluated through both quantitative electrochemical measurements and visualization. Quantitative analysis of H2 generation was conducted using a hydrogen electrode probe at pH 7.0 (Fig. 2a), with P/Mg releasing 91.4 μM of H2 on day 2, during which it reached its maximum production capacity, and stopped thereafter. In contrast, QP-P/Mg displayed a smoother release profile, producing 59.4 μM of H2 on day 2 and lasting until day 4. This result was further confirmed by microscopic observation of H2 generation during a 7-day hydrolysis period (Fig. S4). To evaluate the ROS-scavenging effect of QP-P/Mg, we comprehensively assessed its free radical scavenging efficiency and total antioxidant capacity. The radical scavenging ability of QP-P/Mg was evaluated using electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy analysis, 2,2′-azinobis(3-ethylbenzthiazoline-6-sulfonate) (ABTS) cationic radical scavenging assay, and 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging assay. ESR spectroscopy analysis showed that the QP-P/Mg was efficient in scavenging hydroxyl radicals that its ESR signal intensity was significantly reduced compared to that of the H2O2 group (Fig. 2b). The results of ABTS+ and DPPH assays showed that QP-P/Mg exhibited concentration-dependent free radical scavenging properties. As the concentration of Mg spheres increased, the green color of the ABTS•+ solution gradually faded, and the absorbance decreased accordingly (Fig. 2c). Similarly, as the Mg spheres content increased to 60 mM, the absorbance of DPPH at 517 nm continued to decrease until it completely disappeared (Fig. S5). In addition, the total antioxidant capacity was examined using the KMnO4 reduction assay. The results indicated that QP-P/Mg exhibited concentration-dependent antioxidant properties that the solution color gradually lightened as the Mg spheres content increased to 80 mM, when the characteristic absorption peak at 525 nm completely disappeared (Fig. 2d). These results confirmed that QP-P/Mg possessed a potent electron-supplying capacity and concentration-dependent antioxidant activity for effective attenuation of ROS-mediated tissue damage.

QP-P/Mg possesses favorable cellular Biocompatibility and outstanding antibacterial activity

Excellent biosafety is prerequisite to biomaterials for clinical translation. To assess the biosafety of QP-P/Mg, it was co-cultured bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) in vitro and examined with CCK-8 and Live/Dead staining. The CCK-8 results showed consistent cell viability at concentrations up to 10 mM (Fig. S6a), and the Live/Dead staining further confirmed the extremely low cytotoxicity of QP-P/Mg (Fig. S6b). Moreover, we examined morphologies of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) of treated rats to assess the in vivo biosafety of QP-P/Mg, and detected no pathological changes from the H&E staining results (Fig. S6c).

As the first step of the “infection control-oxidative stress alleviation-tissue regeneration” strategy, robust bacteria killing is imperative to IBDs regeneration. Biofilms are characterized by high-density bacterial communities and pose a major challenge in bone infections due to their resistance to antibiotics [[37], [38], [39]]. Thus, we next examined the antibacterial effect of QP-P/Mg via plate counting, bacterial Live/Dead staining, and SEM examination using S. aureus and E. coli as representative Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Plate counting assays demonstrated significant bacterial inhibition by individual components (Mg and QP-P), while the QP-P/Mg exhibited superior efficacy (Fig. 2e and f). Live/Dead staining also revealed markedly increased bacterial mortality in QP-P/Mg group compared to control group (Fig. 2g). SEM images showed distinct morphological damages in bacteria exposed to QP-P/Mg, including cell wall deformation and rupture (Fig. 2h), confirming the strong and effective broad-spectrum antimicrobial effect of QP-P/Mg. This effect could be attributed to multiple reasons. Previous studies have confirmed that QCS disrupts bacterial membrane through its polycationic structure, and the PBA group can target the glycans on the bacterial surface, which enhances the bactericidal activity of QCS [40]. In addition, Mg2+ increases the permeability of bacterial membrane via competitively binding with Ca2+-binding sites and reducing electrostatic repulsion [41], exerting a synergistic antibacterial effect with QCSPBA. These components functioned cooperatively and prompted potent bactericidal effects, which is crucial for breaking the vicious cycle of infection and inflammation, enabling subsequent effective oxidative stress alleviation and tissue regeneration.

QP-P/Mg alleviates mitochondrial function damage caused by oxidative stress

Excessive ROS induces mitochondrial dysfunction, which further exacerbates oxidative damage, thus forming a vicious cycle of ROS accumulation that severely weakens osteogenesis and impedes the regeneration process [43]. Therefore, targeted scavenging of ROS, restoration of redox balance, and alleviation of mitochondrial dysfunction are required to break the vicious cycle for a favorable pro-regenerative microenvironment. We systematically evaluated the mitochondrial protective efficacy of QP-P/Mg in an established H2O2-induced oxidative damage model (Fig. 4a), which was built by exposing BMSCs to gradient H2O2 concentrations (0, 100, 150, and 200 μM). CCK-8 assays showed that 150 μM H2O2 effectively induced mitochondrial damage in BMSCs, resulting in 50 % cellular death (Fig. S7a), thus it was selected as the optimal concentration for subsequent experiments. We further assessed mitochondrial function by examining mitochondrial ROS with a 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescent probe and mitochondrial membrane potential via JC-1 fluorescence staining. DCFH-DA results showed that H2O2 induced notably higher intracellular ROS level than the control group, while ROS in the QP-P group were comparable to the H2O2 group, and ROS in the QP-P/Mg group were significantly reduced in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4c, Fig. S7b). Depolarization of membrane potential represents a key indicator of oxidative stress-induced mitochondrial damage. The JC-1 images showed that the red/green fluorescence intensity ratio decreased in the H2O2 group, indicating a loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, while the QP-P/M group induced a predominant amount of red signal with minimal green signal and a higher red/green fluorescence intensity ratio (Fig. 4d, Fig. S7c).

Following the validation of QP-P/Mg in ROS-elimination and the maintenance of mitochondrial membrane potential, we further clarified the underlying molecular mechanisms. Previous studies have identified the involvement of multiple signaling pathways in the regulation of oxidative stress alleviation, including the Nrf2/HO-1, Forkhead box O (FOXO), Activator Protein-1 (AP-1), Nuclear factor κB (NF-κB), Hypoxia-inducible factor 1(HIF-1), and Sirtuins pathways [44,45]. Among these, the Nrf2/HO-1 is the most critical one that its function has been validated in the regeneration of various tissues [[46], [47], [48], [49], [50]]. In the basal state, Nrf2 is degraded by ubiquitination through binding to Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1). However, oxidative stress triggers the release of Nrf2 from Keap1, and facilitates its nuclear translocation and subsequent activation of antioxidant response element. Afterwards, the expression of key antioxidant enzymes such as HO-1 and NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1) get upregulated, which constitute the cellular defense system against oxidative damage and rehabilitate the mitochondrial function. For example, Xin et al. designed melatonin-derived carbon dots that activate the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, exerting antioxidant effects to promote osteogenesis and inhibit inflammation, thereby effectively treating periodontitis [51].

In this study, treatment with QP-P/Mg increased Nrf2 nuclear translocation as shown by the immunofluorescence images and semi-quantitative analysis, accompanied by the increased expression of its downstream effectors HO-1 and NQO1 (Fig. 4e–j). Western blotting analysis further verified this by exhibiting the consistent upregulation of the abovementioned antioxidant proteins in the QP-P/Mg group (Fig. 4k and l). Notably, H2 exhibits a selective scavenging of cytotoxic ROS unlike traditional antioxidants. Ohsawa et al. demonstrated that H2 preferentially neutralizes cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals (·OH) and peroxynitrite (ONOO−), while preserving physiologically important signaling molecules such as H2O2 [52,53]. This selectivity avoids disrupting normal cellular signaling pathways that depend on molecules such as H2O2, which may explain the Nrf2/HO-1 activation observed without impairing basic oxidative signaling. These data demonstrated that QP-P/Mg accelerated the production of antioxidant enzymes during mitochondrial biosynthesis via the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway and mitigated mitochondrial oxidative damage.

ARS staining and quantitative analysis of mineralized deposits showed that QP-P/Mg also increased the formation of mineralized nodules (Fig. 5c and d). We then employed qRT-PCR to re-examine the expression of osteogenesis-directed genes including alp, bmp-2, osx and ocn, and found that their expression levels in the H2O2 group were significantly decreased, while the QP-P/Mg group reversed this phenotype, with the gene expression levels exceeding the osteogenic induction group (Fig. 5e and f). Furthermore, we verified the expression of key osteogenesis-directed proteins (Runx-2, Col Ⅰ, and OCN) by immunofluorescence staining and western blotting. Immunofluorescence images and semi-quantitative analysis showed that QP-P/Mg distinctly recovered the synthesis of Runx2 and OCN that were downregulated by H2O2 (Fig. 5g–j). Similarly, western blotting results revealed a same trend with QP-P/Mg treatment (Fig. 5k and l). To summarize, aiming to address the complicated ROS-burst microenvironment of IBDs, we designed a QP-P/Mg double-network hydrogel scaffold with multifaceted functions. The QP-P/Mg achieves effective infection control through the synergistic antibacterial actions of QCSPBA and Mg2+, alleviates oxidative stress by leveraging the targeted scavenging of ROS using H2 controlled-release from Mg spheres, and promotes tissue regeneration via the angiogenesis and osteogenesis effects of sustained-release Mg2+. This system closely aligns with the “infection control-oxidative stress alleviation-tissue regeneration” therapeutic strategy, facilitating highly efficient bone regeneration. However, further exploration into the immune regulatory mechanisms is necessary, and validation of its long-term regeneration effects in large animal models is essential to provide robust theoretical foundations and technical support for clinical translation, thereby advancing the application of this integrated therapeutic strategy in the clinical management of IBDs.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed an Mg spheres-loaded injectable double-network hydrogel scaffold (QP-P/Mg), which integrates the functions of tissue adhesion, antimicrobial, antioxidant, angiogenesis and osteogenesis, to play a time-sequential role in the regeneration of IBDs. At the early stage, it eliminated local infection through efficient antimicrobial activity. In the meanwhile, H2 released from Mg spheres hydrolysis rehabilitated the ROS-amplifying microenvironment and recovered the mitochondrial functions. Subsequently, the sustained release of Mg2+ promoted angiogenesis and osteogenesis at a later stage (Fig. 7e) As a result, QP-P/Mg encouraged outstanding bone formation effects that a 6.27-fold increase at 4 weeks and a 5.02-fold increase at 6 weeks as shown by micro-CT analysis, confirming effective coordination of the cascade process of “infection control-oxidative stress alleviation-tissue regeneration”. This work provides an integrated solution for IBDs with both immediate treatment and long-term regeneration, and demonstrates good potential for clinical translation.
