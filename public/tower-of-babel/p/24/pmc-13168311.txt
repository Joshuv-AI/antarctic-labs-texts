Impact of formal and informal finance on environmental sustainability in developing countries
Authors: Xinwei Wu, Xianang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFormal and informal finance play a crucial role in promoting environmental sustainability, providing the necessary resources to support green projects and eco-friendly innovations. Formal finance, through banks and institutions, offers structured funding for large-scale sustainable initiatives, while informal finance helps smaller communities and entrepreneurs adopt environmentally friendly practices. Together, they drive growth towards a greener economy. Therefore, the current study contributing to the existing literature to examines the impact of formal and informal finance on environmental sustainability in 47 developing economies from 2014 to 2021. This study utilized the Generalized Method of moments (GMM), fixed effects, and robust least squares estimators. The findings show that formal finance, informal finance, and renewable energy negatively affect CO2 emissions (CO2E) while GDP, and population positively affect CO2E in developing countries. Developing country governments should expand access to formal financial institutions and services, enabling households and firms to invest in clean technologies and sustainable industries that help reduce CO2E. Policymakers in developing economies should regulate and gradually formalize informal financial channels, ensuring that their potential contribution to emission reduction is aligned with national environmental and economic goals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44704-0.Keywords: Formal, Informal finance, CO2E, Developing countries, GMMSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Environmental sustainability has become a central pillar of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), a set of international objectives to address global challenges such as climate change, and environmental degradation. The urgency of these goals is underscored by the record-high carbon dioxide emissions (CO2E) in 2022, reaching approximately 36.4 billion metric tons, an increase of about 1% from the previous year. This contradictory trend presents significant challenges to SDG 13 (Climate Action). Also, it threatens the progress of other interconnected goals that rely on environmental sustainability, such as SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production) and SDG 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy)1. Reducing CO2E is crucial for achieving Sustainable Development Goals, safeguarding the environment, combating climate change, and preserving biodiversity, all essential for building a cleaner and more resilient future2. CO2E have recently declined in developed countries, mainly due to the transition to renewable energy sources, energy efficiency improvements, and reduced industrial activity (as seen in Europe and the United States). In contrast, emissions are rising rapidly in developing countries, fueled by population growth, urbanization, and rapid industrialization (particularly in China, India, and Southeast Asia)3.

As established by previous studies, CO2E may be influenced by a variety of factors, including globalization, urbanization, FDI, Geo political risk, economic growth, energy consumption patterns, industrialization, financial development, Education, and technological innovation4–9. Financial resources and financial services can also influence CO2E. Financial development plays a significant role in shaping environmental quality by supporting the growth of enterprises and economies. Some studies suggest that financial development contributes to environmental deterioration by making it easier for businesses and households to access loans, leading to increased consumption, investment, energy use, and, ultimately, higher carbon emissions. However, others argue that financial development can help reduce CO2E by promoting the advancement of technologies that enable more environmentally friendly production10.

Formal finance includes financial inclusion, green finance, and other financial services that promote environmental sustainability and economic participation. It is crucial in funding sustainable projects, supporting innovation, and ensuring equitable access to financial resources for individuals and businesses3. Since the 2000s, inclusive finance has become a key part of financial development, focusing on providing financial services and products that are accessible, fair, convenient, accountable, and sustainable. As an essential element of financial progress, financial inclusion supports the expansion of financial sectors and institutions, promotes sustainable economic development by directing investments into high-quality and innovative industries, and improves the efficiency of capital allocation. Financial inclusion at the individual and household levels enables broader participation in economic growth and greater access to economic opportunities3.

Financial inclusion is a key policy tool for achieving various UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), fostering productivity, reducing poverty, and promoting equitable, sustainable economic growth. Microfinance, now seen as part of financial inclusion policy, has been instrumental in improving access to essential financial services for poor and rural populations, contributing to the fight against hunger, poverty, and the responsible management of ecosystems. However, the success of financial inclusion is threatened by climate change and economic globalization, which exacerbate crises in local social-ecological systems11. Financial inclusion negatively impacts CO2E, by providing access to green financing, financial inclusion facilitates investments in clean technologies and renewable energy. It also enables individuals and businesses to adopt energy-efficient practices and sustainable consumption patterns. Also, enhanced financial services can foster greater environmental awareness and support the implementation of green initiatives12.

According to Acheampong and Said13 financial systems and development significantly influence CO2E though the effect of financial development on emissions remains debated. On one hand, financial development can reduce emissions by promoting green technology, supporting renewable energy, and financing environmentally friendly projects. On the other hand, it can increase emissions by encouraging the consumption of energy-intensive goods and supporting businesses that engage in environmentally harmful activities, such as purchasing energy-heavy machinery.

Both informal and formal finance have strengths and weaknesses, suggesting that borrowers may benefit from using both simultaneously. Informal finance relies heavily on soft information, which is important for small firms lacking credit histories and collateral. However, since informal finance has limited supply and scalability, interest rates tend to rise with more significant loan amounts, making it less attractive for financing big projects. Informal lenders are often better at monitoring borrowers and ensuring repayment when legal processes are slow. Finance from family and friends can also motivate entrepreneurs through mutual trust and support14. Formal finance can expand alongside credit demand and help bridge the financing gap that informal finance cannot meet. Bank financing typically offers lower interest rates than informal sources because banks face fewer constraints in attracting deposits. Additionally, banks have a comparative advantage in managing and processing hard information, such as credit scores and collateral-based lending. Formal financial institutions can mobilize large amounts of capital, supporting investment in green technologies, research and development, and sustainable infrastructure projects. Through loans, grants, and equity investments, they help drive the creation and spread of innovative green solutions. Additionally, formal financial institutions often have broad networks, expertise, and risk assessment tools that assist in identifying promising green projects, encouraging knowledge sharing, and reducing investment risks. However, challenges remain14. Financial institutions in economies with high pollution levels tend to be risk-averse due to uncertainties surrounding green technologies, limiting financing options for green innovators, especially early-stage ventures. Furthermore, strict regulations and complex bureaucratic procedures can slow the flow of capital to green initiatives, delaying the adoption of environmentally friendly solutions. Green finance links investment and financial decisions to environmental protection and sustainable development goals, creating two main effects. First, it influences both the demand for and supply of environmental protection and sustainability. By directing where money flows, green finance encourages businesses to produce environmentally and sustainably while increasing consumer interest in green products15. In contrast, informal financing channels, which face fewer bureaucratic obstacles and offer more flexibility, may better support experimentation and adaptation to local needs16.

Developing countries must create a long-term strategies and projects to address their integrated climate and development challenges. Civic participation is crucial to building popular support for new strategies. Though still evolving, initiatives like “just energy transition partnerships” show how to map political economy issues and build consensus for change. Scaling up multiple types of finance is critical, with the right mix varying by country and project type. Rich countries should double their climate finance commitments by 2025 and improve effectiveness, while the international community must expand access to affordable financing. Issues such as high interest rates, short maturities, uncertain terms, and fragmented financing sources must be addressed. Reforming the multilateral development bank system is a recurring and urgent need identified across case studies. Developing countries should push advanced economies to take proactive steps in restoring confidence in international cooperation. Trust is vital to fostering political collaboration and reducing technical risks. Although integrated climate and development strategies carry more significant short-term financial risks, they offer greater collective benefits when all countries move together. Trust reduces the risk of being a “first mover.” Developing countries can also rebuild global solidarity by clarifying what actions they will undertake with greater international support17.

Although existing research offers valuable insights into how formal and informal finance impact CO2E, several important gaps remain. First, there has been little direct comparison between the effectiveness of formal and informal finance in promoting environmental sustainability. Earlier studies have focused on their influence on general firm innovation rather than on environmental goals. Second, even though the potential synergy between formal and informal finance is widely acknowledged, there is a lack of in-depth research on effectively integrating them. Understanding how these two types of finance can work together is essential for creating more inclusive and resilient financing models. Third, much-existing work concentrates on micro-level outcomes and overlooks broader, global-level perspectives. Finally, while some studies examine formal and informal finance on innovation, such as those by16 no study has been analyzed for developing countries. To address these gaps, this study seeks to investigate the role of formal and informal finance in supporting environmental sustainability, focusing on developing countries. Specifically, it aims to answer two key research questions:

Informal finance plays a significant role in the financial markets of developing countries, unlike in developed economies where its presence is limited. Although globally comparable data on informal finance are scarce, insights can be drawn from the “Global Findex Database 2021” (World Bank, 2022). Only 22.9% of adults in low- and middle-income countries access credit through formal channels, while 30.7% rely on family and friends. In contrast, 55.9% of adults in high-income countries use formal financial services, with just 13.7% borrowing informally. Similar patterns are observed within countries: in China, 26.4% of adults borrow from family and friends, closely followed by India (31.0%) and Indonesia (28.8%), whereas in Japan (6.4%) and Korea (13.2%), informal borrowing is much less common18.

From this perspective, our study makes several important contributions to the existing literature. First, it conducts a comparative analysis of developing countries to assess the combined effect of formal and informal finance on promoting environmental sustainability. Second, it is the first study to examine the impact of both formal and informal finance on environmental sustainability, specifically in developing economies. Third, the study enriches the analysis by incorporating important control variables, including renewable energy consumption, gross domestic product, and population growth. Fourth, we employ advanced econometric techniques such as the Generalized Method of Moments (GMM), fixed effects models, and robust least squares estimators to address potential endogeneity issues. GMM, in particular, helps address issues like endogeneity, heteroscedasticity, and autocorrelation, making it especially effective for datasets characterized by small time dimensions and extensive cross-sectional observations where traditional Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) methods may yield biased results. Lastly, the study’s findings will provide practical insights for policymakers, financial institutions, and other stakeholders aiming to enhance environmental sustainability and tackle pollution challenges in developing countries.

Literature review

Formal finance and environment nexus

The relationship between finance and CO2E is mixed, some researcher found that finance surge the CO2E, for instant Abid19 studied the impact of economic, financial, and institutional factors on CO2E in 25 Sub-Saharan African countries from 1996 to 2010. Using dynamic panel GMM. They found that financial development, Political stability, government effectiveness, democracy, and control of corruption reduced emissions, while regulatory quality and rule of law increased them. Le et al.20 examined the impact of financial inclusion (FI) on CO2E in 31 Asian countries during 2004–2014 by using the Driscoll-Kraay estimator. The study found that urbanization, FDI, and financial inclusion increased CO2E, whereas trade openness reduced CO2E. Petrović and Lobanov21 analyzed the long-run effect of financial development on CO2E in 24 countries from 1970 to 2014. They found a positive long-term effect of financial development on CO2E. Domestic credit to the private sector had indirect impacts, while loans to energy-efficient projects and low-emission technologies could mitigate emissions. Khan et al.10 investigated the effect of digital financial inclusion on CO2E in 76 emerging markets and developing economies. Using the Global Findex database and constructing a digital financial inclusion index, the study employed the dynamic two-step system GMM method. The results revealed that digital financial inclusion significantly increased CO2E, and the findings remained robust across sub-indices and traditional financial inclusion proxies. Uddin et al.22 examined the effects of energy consumption, financial development, and economic growth on the ecological footprint in 119 developed and developing countries from 2002 to 2018. Using panel ARDL models, findings showed that energy use, financial development, urbanization, FDI, and population growth increased ecological footprints in both developed and developing countries, while HDI and natural resources reduced them. Rafique23 investigated the impact of financial development, urbanization, and renewable energy consumption on CO2E in MENA countries from 2011 to 2023. Using the fixed effects regression model, the study found that financial development increases CO2E by financing carbon-intensive industries, while renewable energy reduces emissions by substituting fossil fuels. Interestingly, urbanization had a negative relationship with CO2E, reflecting the role of improved infrastructure and energy efficiency.

Some researcher found that the finance reduce the CO2E, Adedoyin et al.24 analyzed the nexus between financial development, FDI, and CO2E in Sub-Saharan Africa within the framework of the UN SDGs. Using the GMM, results indicated that economic growth increased CO2E, but financial development reduced them. The growing intersection of financial technology (FinTech), environmental sustainability, and economic development has gained significant scholarly attention, particularly in the post-COVID context. Dong et al.25 investigated the asymmetric association between Fintech advancements, clean energy consumption, climate policy, and environmental quality in Asian countries. Their study highlighted that while the region has prioritized environmental sustainability, reliance on fossil fuels continues to pose ecological challenges. Using CS-ARDL and NARDL models, they found that Fintech, climate policies, and clean energy adoption enhance both environmental sustainability and the conservation of natural resources, underscoring the importance of policy-driven and technology-enabled transitions.

Shao et al.26 investigated the effect of financial development on CO2E in the global iron and steel industry across 30 countries from 1990 to 2018. Using three-stage least squares (3SLS), results showed that financial development reduced CO2E by promoting industrial upgrading. The effect was stronger in developed than in developing countries, and the EKC hypothesis was validated in the steel sector. Policy suggestions included fostering financial support for carbon neutrality in heavy industries. Guang-Wen and Siddik27 examined the role of FinTech adoption in fostering green finance, green innovation, and environmental performance in the banking sector of Bangladesh during the COVID-19 pandemic. Employing a structural equation modeling approach, they demonstrated that FinTech adoption significantly promotes green finance and innovation, which in turn positively impact environmental performance. Their findings position FinTech as a critical enabler of sustainability-oriented financial practices in emerging markets, particularly through its indirect influence on green innovation. In the African context, Coffie et al.28 shifted the focus from financial inclusion to environmental outcomes by analyzing the impact of FinTech on carbon emissions in seven mobile money economies. Using panel data from 2009 to 2020 and FMOLS estimation, they discovered that FinTech significantly reduces carbon emissions, while GDP and trade openness tend to increase emissions. Their work reveals the dual role of FinTech as both a financial and environmental instrument, capable of reshaping development pathways in sub-Saharan Africa.

Similarly, Thangaiyarkarasi and Vanitha29 assessed the link between FinTech, economic development, and carbon emissions in mobile money economies. Using generalized linear models over 2013–2022, they confirmed that FinTech contributes to reducing CO2E while facilitating the growth of a green and low-carbon economy. Their results suggest that FinTech not only strengthens financial services but also accelerates the transition toward meeting global climate goals such as the Paris Agreement. Sun et al.12 investigated the joint role of financial inclusion and technological innovation in reducing CO2E within an N-shaped Environmental Kuznets Curve for China from 2006Q1–2022Q4. Applying ARDL, FMOLS, and DOLS, results confirmed the inverted N-shaped EKC, where FI, TI, and renewable energy reduced CO2 emissions. The study highlighted the importance of integrating financial inclusion and innovation for environmental sustainability. Ozili30 studied the effect of CO2E from gaseous fuel consumption on financial inclusion through physical financial access points in 22 countries during non-crisis years. Using median quantile regression and two-stage least squares (2SLS), the study found that higher CO2E was linked to higher financial inclusion in European, Asian, and developing countries. However, it reduced financial inclusion in African countries with strong institutions and high lending rates. The study highlighted the importance of shifting towards digital financial inclusion to mitigate CO2-related challenges.

Informal finance and environment nexus

Lian et al.16 explored the impact of formal and informal finance on green innovation across high-, middle-, and low-income economies from 2007 to 2021. Using 2SLS and GMM techniques, they found that both formal and informal finance significantly promoted green innovation. GDP, CO2E, trade openness, human capital, R&D, and digital finance were also key factors. The study suggested collaboration between formal and informal sectors to boost green innovation for sustainable development.

Theoretical review

According to Jia et al.31, the relationship between finance and CO2E is rich but remains inconclusive. One strand of research suggests that financial development stimulates economic growth, which in turn drives higher energy consumption and emissions, thereby supporting an inverted U-shaped link between finance and carbon emissions32. In contrast, another perspective emphasizes that financial development fosters technological innovation, strengthens environmental awareness, and promotes industrial restructuring, ultimately encouraging low-carbon growth33. A further body of work highlights that the impact varies across financial subsectors. For instance, Yan et al.34 demonstrate that credit scale and foreign direct investment show inverted U-shaped and U-shaped associations with emissions, respectively, while financial market expansion, competition within the sector, and more market-oriented credit allocation can help lower carbon emission intensity.

Summary and research gap

The reviewed literature reveals significant efforts to explore the relationship between financial inclusion, financial development, and environmental outcomes such as CO2E, ecological footprint, and green innovation. For instance, Le et al.20, Khan et al.10, and Sun et al.12 focused on the link between financial inclusion and CO2E, while Adedoyin et al.24, Petrović and Lobanov21, and Shao et al.26 examined the environmental effects of financial development. Similarly, Uddin et al.22 extended this discussion to the ecological footprint and institutional quality. More recently, Lian et al.16 considered the role of formal and informal finance but emphasized their impact on green innovation rather than directly on CO2E. Despite these contributions, a critical gap remains: the environmental implications of formal versus informal finance, specifically their distinct impact on CO2E, have not yet been systematically investigated. Existing studies either aggregate finance into a single dimension (financial inclusion or financial development) or examine its interaction with related variables such as FDI, energy consumption, or innovation, without disentangling the roles of formal and informal financial channels. Therefore, this fulfill the gap to examine the impact of formal and informal finance on CO2E in developing countries.

Stationarity is crucial because it ensures that the mean, variance, and Auto covariance of the series remain stable over time, which is a key requirement for conducting reliable regression analysis and making accurate forecasts. The Kao residual cointegration test examines whether a long-run equilibrium relationship exists among variables in a panel dataset. It is based on Engle-Granger’s two-step approach, testing the stationarity of residuals from a static regression. A stationary residual indicates the presence of cointegration across the panel.

Various econometric techniques are employed to achieve the objectives of this study. Our methodological approach provides a structured path to investigate how the FF and IF impact CO2E. Specifically, we apply the generalized method of moments (GMM), focusing on the system GMM estimator. Several factors motivate this choice: the panel covers 8 years (2014 to 2021), shorter than the number of cross-sectional units (47 countries). According to Baltagi38 and Roodman39, system GMM is well-suited for dynamic panel data where T < N. Alternatively, Ameer et al.40 has applied CS-ARDL methodology which comparatively better than system GMM when there exists Cross-sectional dependency (CSD) in panel time series dataset and preferably, this is most suitable methodology to apply when N (cross countries) is relatively larger than T (time span). Moreover, system GMM produces more efficient and consistent estimates than other GMM approaches41. It effectively addresses the correlation between the error term and country-specific fixed effects, which is a common issue in dynamic panels with short periods42. System GMM also mitigates potential endogeneity and heterogeneity problems. Following Bakhsh et al.43, system GMM helps address omitted variable bias and Heteroskedasticity and enhances the reliability of estimates. Furthermore, the two-step system GMM estimator is preferred as it provides more efficient results than the one-step version. Instrument validity is assessed using the Hansen test44 or the Sargan test45. System GMM is generally preferred over Difference GMM when variables are highly persistent and instruments in differences are weak. Difference GMM may suffer from a weak instruments problem, leading to biased estimates. While System GMM combines equations in levels and differences, improving efficiency. It allows using additional instruments, which strengthens identification.

Data

This study incorporates seven variables, such as CO2 emissions, formal and informal finance, renewable energy, Gross domestic product, trade, and population. The data has been obtained from the World Development Indicator (WDI) and Global Financial Development (GFD). Table 2 presents the variables and data sources. CO2 per capita is chosen over total emissions because it reflects individual responsibility and supports fairer climate negotiations by highlighting disparities in consumption and atmospheric capacity. It is also preferred over the ecological footprint, as per capita CO2 provides a more direct and policy-relevant measure of national greenhouse gas contributions, while the ecological footprint is broader, consumption-based, and less useful for targeted policy design. Before estimation, missing data were filled using linear interpolation. The Linear interpolation fills missing values by drawing a straight line between the nearest known data points. For our unbalanced panel, linear interpolation was applied to fill short gaps within countries. This method maintains the continuity of each country’s time series without imposing strong model-based assumptions. Although interpolation may introduce minor bias, the gaps are limited and trends are smooth, making it a reasonable and transparent approach compared to more complex imputations. The coefficients for FF and IF are − 0.0293 and − 0.0215, respectively. This indicates that a 1% increase in formal finance and informal finance reduces CO2 emissions by 0.0293% and 0.0215%. The negative effect of informal finance on CO2E is weaker than that of formal finance because informal finance typically supports small-scale, subsistence, or community-level activities with limited environmental impact. Formal finance, when directed toward green technologies and renewable energy, can generate larger reductions in emissions through efficiency gains and structural change. This aligns with theory: informal finance contributes modestly to sustainability, but formal finance has greater capacity to influence investment patterns and accelerate the transition toward a low-carbon economy. Moreover, formal and informal finance can help reduce CO2E by supporting green investments and promoting cleaner technologies. Informal finance, such as community lending, may fund small-scale eco-friendly projects. However, formal finance through banks, credit markets, and green bonds plays a more significant role. It provides larger, regulated funding for renewable energy, energy-efficient technologies, and sustainable industries. Formal finance also follows strict environmental standards and monitoring. Consequently, formal finance more effectively reduces CO2E compared to informal finance. Lian et al.16 analyzed the impact of FF and IF on green innovation. They found that both formal and informal funding methods significantly impact environment-related technologies in high-income and middle- and low-income countries.

Rising incomes boost demand for goods and services, further raising emissions. However, most developing nations have limited access to clean technologies, so emissions keep rising as GDP grows. Conclusion and policy recommendations

This study contributes to the existing literature to examine the impact of formal and informal finance on CO2E in 47 developing economies from 2014 to 2021. The finding showed that formal finance, informal finance, and renewable energy negatively affect CO2E while GDP, and population positively affect CO2E in developing countries. The results directly connect to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). The negative effect of formal and informal finance on CO2 emissions supports SDG 13 (Climate Action) by showing that financial systems can play a role in reducing emissions. Formal finance, by enabling renewable energy and cleaner technologies, also aligns with SDG 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy). The findings suggest that strengthening both formal and informal financial channels can reinforce global sustainability targets.

This study has several policy implications for developing economies to enhance environmental sustainability. First, Governments in developing countries should encourage formal financial institutions, such as banks and investment funds, to design specialized green finance products (e.g., green bonds, low-interest loans for clean energy projects) that make investing in sustainable technologies easier for businesses and individuals. Second, Policymakers in developing countries should recognize and support informal finance systems, including microfinance institutions, community savings groups, and cooperatives, by offering technical assistance and light regulation. This will help rural and low-income communities access funds for small-scale renewable energy projects and eco-friendly practices. Third, developing countries should integrate formal and informal finance mechanisms into their national environmental strategies. By providing incentives (like tax breaks or grants) for green investments and ensuring accessible financing across all sectors, they can boost widespread participation in environmental sustainability efforts. Fourth, Governments should encourage banks and formal financial institutions to offer green loans, green bonds, and eco-friendly investment products to support businesses and households investing in renewable energy, energy efficiency, and sustainable practices. Five, Regulators in developing countries should set clear green credit guidelines for formal financial institutions, ensuring that a portion of lending is directed toward environmentally sustainable sectors like clean energy, sustainable agriculture, and green infrastructure. Six, Policymakers should strengthen the capacity of microfinance institutions and informal lenders to provide small loans for renewable energy systems, clean cookstoves, water conservation, and other local sustainability projects in rural and underserved areas. Seven Support the establishment of community savings groups and cooperatives focused on financing local environmental initiatives, helping individuals who may not have access to formal banking systems invest in eco-friendly solutions. Eight, Governments should recognize the role of informal finance networks and incorporate them into broader national environmental sustainability strategies through training, capacity building, and incentives to ensure that grassroots initiatives contribute effectively to environmental goals.

This study has several limitations that should be addressed in future research. First, although our study examined formal and informal finance’s impact on environmental sustainability in developing countries, a more detailed investigation of developed and individual countries would provide deeper insights. Second, due to non-availability of data, this study utilized a limited dataset from 2014 to 2021. Future research could employ a larger dataset as more data become available. Third, future research should include additional factors, such as globalization, inflation, FDI, and industrialization, which also impact CO2E, but current data limitations make this difficult. Fourth, conducting country-specific comparative analyses would help clarify the varying influence of formal and informal finance on CO2E across economies. Fifth, this study has limitations in that it cannot capture micro-level or firm-level analyses, does not explicitly address the role of digital finance and mobile money, and overlooks gender-specific impacts of formal finance. Last, since all variables are not normally distributed, future studies should apply quantile regression, bootstrapping methods, or generalized linear models (GLMs) to address the issue of non-normality.
