Societal and climatic controls on swidden cultivation in the Eastern European Plain
Authors: Elena Ponomarenko, Ekaterina Ershova, Leonid Viazov, Konrad Gajewski, Pille Tomson, Alexander Alexandrovskiy, Dmitry Stashenkov, Nikita Lavrenov, Nikolay Krenke, Anna Kochkina, Julia Salova, Dmitri Antonov, Vasiliy E. Demidov, Alexander Shapovalov, Ayrat Sitdikov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Slash-and-burn cultivation was among the earliest systems of land use that allowed agriculture to expand into forested and less naturally fertile zones beyond the regions where it first developed, but the chronology of this agricultural system is debated. The development and history of slash-and-burn cultivation on the Eastern European Plain was established for the first time using a novel approach. A dataset of 120 radiocarbon dates was obtained on charcoal from swidden soil horizons in major river basins. The earliest swiddens appeared in the Bronze Age in the Dnieper basin, becoming widespread in the beginning of the Early Iron Age. Radiocarbon dates reflect four major waves of expansion of groups practicing swidden agriculture into new territories between 4000 and 500 years ago, and more numerous and asynchronous basin-scale migrations. The expansions alternated with centennial-scale breaks when swiddens became sporadic or disappeared. After the last prolonged break, 200 BCE to 100 CE, slash-and-burn cultivation rapidly expanded and persisted in the forest zone of Eastern Europe. The onset of swidden cultivation and peaks in swidden occurrence coincided either with climate cooling or with climatic transitions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41211-0.

Introduction

Slash-and-burn (SAB), or swidden cultivation, is an agricultural system based on using ash produced by in situ wood burning as a soil fertilizer1. It is not clear when SAB was invented, but it has been practiced worldwide and is still used in more than 40 tropical and extratropical countries2,3. In the Northern Hemisphere, SAB remained in use until the first decades of the twentieth century in peripheral areas of Germany, Austria, Finland, Sweden and Russia4–6,

According to historical descriptions of SAB in the northern hemisphere5,7, the technique involves several stages. A long conversion stage lasting from 1 to 24 years involves forest clearing, usually by slashing trees, felling them or letting them fall after dying, and burning them once dry. A short cropping stage with high crop yields lasts from one to five years, depending on the amount of ash released. A forest fallow stage, up to several decades long, closes the cycle7. The long length of the non-productive stages implies that all suitable patches in the swidden landscape are involved in the swidden cultivation at its various stages.

Swiddens are usually set in elevated well-drained landforms with the sandy and rocky soils primarily supporting coniferous forest, repeated cycles of SAB utilize secondary birch-dominated forests. However, there are modifications of SAB that burn sod or peat instead of wood7. These are set in sites with a more fine-textured soil and poorer drainage, such as peatlands, widely turned into swiddens in European Low Countries and Finland in the late fourteenth -seventeenth centuries8,9. In both cases, SAB utilizes parts of the landscape that are not suitable (or at least not preferred) for permanent fields.

The most important implement in the SAB system is a chopping tool, usually a socketed celt or axe, used for tree bark slashing and tree felling. No specific tillage implements are needed, as seed entrenching into ashes can be achieved by making holes with a stick or scratching the soil surface with a wooden ard or sokha, at least during the first round of sowing. The unnecessity of tillage implements and draft animals in SAB allowed for a high mobility of slash-and-burn farmers. Moreover, the SAB system of husbandry entailed mobility, as a continuous expansion of the territory involved into the SAB was needed due to a long forest fallow stage. Archaeologists suggest that utilization of swidden cultivation can be inferred from the presence and storage of cultivated crops in the absence of specific tillage implements and a short lifetime of settlements10.

Pollen analysis offers an indirect approach to reconstruction of past SAB cycles based on co-occurrence of short-term antiphase oscillations of spruce and birch curves on high temporal resolution pollen diagrams and high values of charcoal concentration in lake deposits11,12. Pollen analysis may suggest the beginning of slash-and-burn agriculture in a region, although it does not always allow the reconstruction of individual episodes, identify cultivars or localize swidden sites in the landscape13,14.

In recent years a novel approach to identifying swidden activity in paleo-soils has been developed. Soils affected by swidden cultivation have distinct morphology, charcoal assemblages, pollen and phytolith spectra15–18.

We employed the morphological and analytical indicators of SAB in the soil to localize swidden sites in the landscape and develop the first chronology of slash-and-burn cultivation in Eastern Europe based on direct radiocarbon dating of swidden horizons. In this study, we synthesize a dataset of SAB radiocarbon dates that was obtained directly on charcoal and peat from swidden layers across a wide geographical range, which reflects the spatiotemporal development of swidden agriculture in the region.

Results

Sites

Swidden horizons described in documented slash-and-burn sites in Karula (Estonia)15,19 served as a reference for the identification of swidden horizons in other areas. Other sites were selected under the assumption that large rivers were major paths of past human migrations on the Eastern European Plain and that cultural innovations, SAB among them, could be transported along the river channels. On the other hand, if there were stages with alternating land use systems and SAB was used discontinuously, time gaps in SAB utilization would reflect changes in either human populations, or their agricultural preferences. Finally, sand river terraces are ideal for swiddens, offering sandy soils with a long vegetative season, natural fire breakers for burning, and typically with coniferous forest cover that was easier to clear.

Location of swiddens in relation to soil texture

The majority of sites affected by the SAB are on sandy soil (Table S1). These are either deep sequences of alluvial and aeolian sands, or loams superposed by a thin sand veneer. However, fine-textured soils were also utilized in swidden cultivation, especially in southern and eastern regions where ca. 15% of sites were associated with silt loam soils. In several sites, swiddens were set on sandy-clay loam deposited on floodplain terraces during the Ladoga transgression. This is an exception from the overall tendency of swidden agriculturalists to avoid fine-textured soils that are more difficult to work than sandy soils. Utilization of fine-textured soils in the cycle of SAB is unexpected, as, in contrast to sands, fine-grained particles in loamy soils may weld together on high-temperature heating, greatly reducing root-inhabitable pore space in swidden horizons. However, no soil reddening was observed in any mineral swidden horizons, confirming that mainly organic soil horizons of mor- and moder humus types were affected while burning wood slash25.

Forest cover preferences

Charcoal assemblages left in swiddens after burning reflect the tree taxa that were components of the forest cover at the time of the forest clearance. Deciduous taxa that are not readily flammable as green wood were dominant in charcoal assemblages from ca. 60% of the swidden sites (Table S1).

In the earliest swiddens, dated 1700 cal BCE, charcoal fragments were mainly oak and, given the difficulty of clearing maturing oak trees, suggests that the swiddens of the Bronze Age targeted young oak stands. Pine, linden and birch were cleared in swiddens dated 700–200 cal BCE.

In the first millennium CE, the species composition of swiddens was region-specific, either conifers (pine and spruce) or broadleaved taxa (oak and linden) could be the target species during the initial burning. In the ninth - thirteenth centuries CE, oaks were commonly cleared in swidden sites in the Oka and Volga basins, sometimes in the same sites that were dominated by conifers several centuries earlier. Repeated utilization of the same sites resulted in a shift towards secondary birch, birch and linden, or birch and alder forest cover. This sequence was well known in traditional knowledge, and certain tree species could have been planted in swiddens at the transition from productive stage to fallow, as expressed in the Finnish Estonian national epic poem of Kalevala26. Overall, early successional deciduous taxa were subject to SAB more commonly than conifers.

Archaeological attribution of radiocarbon dated swiddens

The earliest swiddens, dated 2000–1750 BCE in the basins of Dnieper, Oka, and Volga rivers, fall within the Late Middle Bronze Age. This was a period of dramatic transformation of the cultural pattern in the forest zone of Eastern Europe. The Abashevo archaeological culture replaced the eastern varieties of the Corded Ware in the Dnieper basin, the Fatyanovo in the Oka basin, and the Balanovo in the Volga basin. The earliest manifestations of the Abashevo were found in the Middle Volga region and dated 2130–1900 BCE27, but it was suggested28 that at least a part of the Abashevo population originated in Central Europe and migrated eastward following the 4200 BP climatic event29.

The earliest dates within the second stage of SAB expansion, 1300–1000 BCE, appear in the Dnieper River basin and are contemporary to the Bondarikha culture of the Terminal Bronze Age that presumably was formed due to penetration of Trzciniec population from the west30. All other dates of the second expansion stage fall within the first phase of the East European Early Iron Age (EE EIA-1). In the Volga basin, this period is contemporary to the Ananyino culture, while the Don and Oka basins were populated by the Gorodets and Dyakovo cultural groups. Recent data suggest a rapid and significant increase in crops, associated with the formation of the EE EIA-1 cultural traditions, dominated by millet in Dyakovo and Gorodets sites31 and by barley and emmer in Ananyino occupation layers32,33.

In the beginning of the second phase of the EE EIA, ca. 200 BCE to CE 100, no evidence of the SAB was found in the forest and forest-steppe from the Dnieper to the Urals. However, there was no gap in the plant crop production in this period31, suggesting that the crops were grown in permanent plots. This finding correlates well with the interpretation of contemporary Dyakovo populations of Oka basin as sedentary farmers34.

A new stage of the SAB dispersal over Eastern Europe began ca. CE 100, with the earliest dates in the Dnieper and Oka basins. The archaeological attribution of the earliest swiddens of this stage in the Dnieper basin is not clear. In the Oka basin, the earliest CE swiddens were contemporaneous to the Late Dyakovo culture that differed from the Early Dyakovo in basic types of artifacts and economy. Archaeozoological finds suggest that the Late Dyakovo population increased exploitation of forest resources, relying on game for meat consumption35, whereas archaeobotanical data shows a shift from millet-dominated to barley-dominated crop production31. In the Volga basin, the earliest CE swiddens are associated with the population of the Kyiv culture, closely followed by the Imen’kovo culture18.

Reintroduction of swidden cultivation during the Late Roman Period (CE 50–369) and its subsequent expansion during the Migration Period (CE 369–680) was identified across the region and therefore did not result from local cultural development. The dispersal of the SAB coincides with major cultural transformations of the CE 50–150 in the forest zone. A set of archaeological artefacts originating in the Middle and Upper Dnieper region was distributed over the entire East-European forest-steppe zone during the period, forming the so-called post-Zarubintsy horizon. From CE 200–250 on, a new style of jewelry called the “East European barbaric champlevé enamels” appeared among the populations of the post-Zarubintsy cultural tradition36. The East European enamels were dispersed from the area of Kyiv archaeological culture, which is considered as the earliest Slavic-speaking population37. Not only the jewelry style, but also agricultural implements were introduced, such as small sickles of the Zarubintsy type and long narrow axes that could be used for tree slashing38,39. In the northeast Baltic Sea basin, the swiddens of the Staraya Ladoga cluster, dated to the end of the sixth century CE, are archaeologically attributed to the Early Slavs40.

First Medieval states began to form in Eastern Europe towards the end of the seventh century CE, and our radiocarbon dates show that swidden cultivation was practiced during their expansion. SAB was practiced at the early stage of the Khazar Khaganate, Volga Bulgaria, Kievan Rus and, later, Russian Principalities, but was largely replaced by permanent fields by the High Middle Ages, as reflected by the ongoing decrease in the swidden dates from the 8th to thirteenth centuries.

The last stage of the SAB expansion of the fourteenth -sixteenth centuries CE coincided with the progressive expansion of the Muscovite state to the north and west, into the areas with hunter-gatherer populations, and to the south and east, into the territories controlled by nomads-pastoralists since the Mongol’s invasion. In addition to the territorial changes, some lands were abandoned within the original state boundaries due to depopulation caused by the wars and epidemics of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and then cleared again using the slash-and-burn technique.

The decrease in precipitation of the late Holocene was especially pronounced between CE 1–600 (2000–600 cal BP), and most of our swidden dates fall within this accelerating cooler and driest period. A high-resolution speleothem record from the Ural Mountains, immediately east of the study region (Fig. 6d), documents a long-term trend of winter warming, corroborated by other regional evidence42. Superimposed on this trend are centennial-scale fluctuations, including an abrupt cooling at 2500–2200 BCE (4500–4200 cal BP) and again during the Little Ice Age (CE 1450–1850), the latter coinciding with the most recent peak in swidden dates.

Tree-ring series from the Alps and the Altai Mountains provide reconstructions of summer temperature43. Although lacking a persistent long-term trend, these coherent records reveal pronounced cold intervals at CE 540–660 (the Late Antique Little Ice Age, LALIA, also termed the Dark Ages Cold Period) and CE 1450–1850 (the Little Ice Age), as well as warmer conditions during the Medieval Warm Period (CE 800–1200). Büntgen et al.43 related the LALIA cooling to societal impacts across Europe. Importantly, both cold intervals coincide with the two largest peaks in swidden radiocarbon dates, whereas the Medieval Warm Period is marked by a sharp decline in swidden cultivation.

A reconstruction of the June–August Palmer Drought Severity Index based on tree-ring data45 further indicates predominantly dry conditions between CE 0 and 700, followed by increased precipitation at CE 700–800 and again at CE 1100–1200. Moist conditions generally persisted until the mid-twentieth century, with particularly wet intervals in the early 1400s and early 1600s. The dry period of the first millennium coincides with the first peak of CE swidden dates, with swiddens becoming sparse during the moist period of the seventh—eighth centuries.

Comparison of the climate reconstructions with the summed probability distribution of swidden dates suggests that at the level of short-term fluctuations, dry and cold climatic phases either closely preceded or coincided with peaks in swidden dates.

Discussion

Chronology of swidden cultivation reflects cultural transitions, migrations as well as a response to climatic variability. Swidden cultivation first appeared in Eastern Europe around 4 millennia ago, during the Bronze Age, but became widespread only in the Early Iron Age. Its introduction and expansion alternated with periods of decline, caused either by regional depopulation or by the replacement of swiddens with permanent fields. There were three major waves of swidden cultivation expansion: 1900–1700 BCE, 1200—200 BCE, and CE 100–1800, separated by multi-centennial interruptions. Over the past two millennia, the practice persisted without a complete break, but its extent fluctuated, peaking at CE 250–750 and CE 1250–1500, followed by sharp declines in the number of swidden sites both between and after these periods.

On a smaller scale, within each macro-wave of expansion, the swidden dates cluster into series spanning 100–200 years. Such series are asynchronous among the river basins, reflecting basin-specific histories of swidden expansion. In many cases, the dates of “initial burning” within these series coincide with the arrival of new populations in the basins of Dnieper, Don, Oka, and Volga Rivers and the north-eastern part of the Baltic Sea, as documented by archaeological and historical evidence. Swidden chronology thus reflects the successive arrivals of numerous populations who rapidly colonized the landscape, only to abandon it for other areas within decades to a couple of centuries.

The number of swiddens grew most rapidly between the first and eighth centuries CE, a period that coincides with increased mobility of Germanic and Slavic groups in Europe during the Late Roman and Migration Periods (2nd – seventh centuries CE)46. The series of swidden dates that postdate the Migration Period coincide with the initial expansion of budding Early Medieval states, such as the Khazar Khaganate (eighth century CE) and later, Volga Bulgaria (tenth -thirteenth centuries CE) in the Volga River basin, and Kievan Rus’ and Medieval Russian Principalities in the Dnieper and Oka basins. Finally, the increase in the swidden dates in the Volga and Dnieper River basins in the fourteenth -sixteenth centuries CE coincided with the expansion of the Russian Principalities beyond the Oka Abatis Line following the collapse of the Golden Horde and its successor states47.

According to historical sources, from the sixteenth century onward, swiddens also expanded to the north into territories inhabited by hunter-and-gatherers. This process was partly driven by the northward and westward expansion of Muscovy and the associated population movements it set in motion48. Similar migrations of Finnish swidden agriculturalists into Sámi hunting territories were encouraged in the sixteenth century by the Swedish royalty in their borderlands with Russia49.

Also in the sixteenth century, swidden agriculture spread to North European regions with a long tradition of permanent fields. In Scandinavia, fertile clay-rich soils of intermontane valleys had long supported permanent cultivation, while the rocky and sandy soils of uplands remained forested until the arrival of Forest Finns, who introduced swidden farming to these zones50,51. It is possible that different agricultural systems, crops, and ethnic groups may have similarly coexisted on different soils within the same landscape in the more remote past.

While social turmoil could trigger mass migrations and promote swidden agriculture, so did the climate. Swidden cultivation appears to have emerged and reemerged during climatic transitions, particularly from warm and wet to cold and dry phases. The observed peaks in swidden dates closely correspond to the established climatic phases for the region. The Bronze Age swidden dates coincide with the onset of neoglacial cooling 2500–1500 BCE (4500–3500 yrs BP), while the Early Iron Age swiddens appeared ca. 900 BCE (2900 yrs BP) during the cold and dry climatic Subboreal phase. At the beginning of the Roman Warm Period (250 BCE to CE 150)52, swiddens rapidly disappeared, reappearing toward the end of the period, with the highest concentration of swidden dates falling within the subsequent Dark Ages Cold Period (CE 400–765)43,53. Finally, the expansion of swidden sites at the end of the fourteenth century coincided with the transition to the Little Ice Age (CE 1450–1850)54.

Climatic transitions have frequently triggered human migrations in the past (e.g43), and the swidden system is particularly well suited for migrations, as it does not require specialized tillage tools or draft animals. In addition, the frequent relocation of cultivated patches inherent to the swidden system promoted rapid selection of crop varieties with traits adapted to the new growing conditions55.

The swidden system may have been particularly important during cold and transitional climatic phases for reasons unrelated to migrations. Cooling shortens the growing season, especially on loamy soils that have higher water-retention capacity than sandy soils. Fine-textured soils of permanent fields yield well under warm and moist conditions, but are vulnerable to early frost, late thawing, and prolonged waterlogging - factors that can reduce arable lands and cause crop failures during cooler periods. In contrast, swiddens are typically established on well-drained sandy soils, which freeze later and thaw earlier, providing a longer growth season. When seasonal length limits crop production, the combination of sandy soils and slash-and-burn cultivation offers a highly effective short-term strategy for sustaining the viability of agriculture.

Methods

Identification of swidden horizons

In temperate regions, development of soils on sand deposits under coniferous forests ultimately leads to formation of Podzols, soils with a bleached eluviated horizon underlain by a brown illuviated horizon that accumulates coloring compounds removed from the above-bedded soil mass. The humus horizon in such soils is of a mor type (peat-like) and therefore flammable.

At the preparation stage of swidden cultivation, burning and scratching transforms the original humus layer into a mechanical mixture of sand and charcoal. That results in the formation of a swidden horizon—a cinder-gray layer with numerous small charcoal fragments, evenly distributed along the surface, in contrast to patchy distribution of charcoal after forest fires. An organic component of the organo-mineral material of swidden horizons is mainly charred mass instead of the humified mass in mull- and moder-humus. In swidden horizons, the charcoal concentration is equal to or greater than one gravel-sized fragment per gram of soil mass, the fragments are isometric and commonly rounded, the median charcoal size is 4–6 mm, and a horizon thickness varies from 4 to 10 cm, depending on the cultivation depth15. The lower boundary of swidden horizons is typically dotted by constructions of sweat bees (Halictidae), forming a diagnostic transitional subhorizon between the new pyrogenic and original bleached horizons. The transitional horizon appears as an assemblage of dots and finger-like shapes that have black, white, and yellow–brown infill due to ascending transport of sand from mineral horizons by bees and descending active transport of charred mass within the bee tunnels. Despite a relatively small thickness, swidden horizons differ from other humus horizons so profoundly that they can be discerned even after fragmentation and redeposition by tree uprooting. Redeposited material of swidden horizons retains gray color, high concentration of charcoal, and isometric form of charcoal fragments16.

Sampling

We examined several archives of the slash-and-burn cultivation system: swidden horizons in situ, a material of swidden layers redeposited in local depressions, and peat layers in peatlands near the sites presumably affected by SAB in the past. Swidden layers are shallow and can be easily erased by phytoturbation, therefore soils buried under various deposits were a focus of this study. These included paleosols buried under various embankments in archaeological sites, under colluvial deposits in gullies and toe slopes of modern plough lands, and under alluvial splay deposits on floodplain terraces.

After redeposition, the material of swidden layers loses some of its morphological features, such as diagnostic trace fossils of bees, but still can be identified based on the size, sphericity, and concentration of charcoal, and aided by the phytolith and pollen analyses. Such deposits are commonly preserved beneath peat units at the edge of peatlands and in small depressions such as forest hollows. We analyzed and radiocarbon dated such redeposited swidden layers in addition to the layers preserved in situ.

The analysis of soils and mineral deposits was complemented by the investigation of peatlands. In peats, layers were identified as SAB-related if their pollen spectra showed a sharp increase in the proportion of Betula and charcoal particles with a simultaneous decrease in the proportion of late-successional forest trees such as Picea11. The presence of crop and weed pollen was important, but not instructive, as pollen and spores of most cultivars and diagnostic groundcover taxa are not deposited in peatbogs in noticeable numbers14.

While charcoal was used as a medium to radiocarbon date in situ swidden layers and their redeposited soil mass, either charcoal or peat was used to radiocarbon date SAB events in peatlands. During fieldwork, swidden horizons were identified, soil texture was described, and approximately 2 L soil samples were collected for further charcoal analysis.

Charcoal analysis

To analyze charcoal assemblages, large soil samples were air dried and then wet sieved through a 0.6 mm sieve. A whirling technique was used to separate light fractions of charcoal prior to wet sieving the mineral soil mass with remaining “heavy” charcoal. The remaining charcoal was manually picked from the mineral mass that was separated on sieves and air-dried. Charcoal fragments were identified using published manuals56,57 and online resources. If a charcoal assemblage from a layer contained several taxa, the most numerous taxa were selected for radiocarbon dating. Soil charcoal assemblages commonly contain some charcoal produced by older fire events, therefore we submitted a single charcoal fragment for radiocarbon dating whenever possible, to avoid mixing products of several fire events. A wide set of species was identified in charcoal assemblages from most layers, but only the radiocarbon dated taxa were included in the comparative analysis.

Radiocarbon dating

Charcoal samples from 75 sites were submitted for radiocarbon dating. Single charcoal fragments originating from fine fuel fractions (twigs and foliage) were selected to avoid mixing charcoal produced by multiple fires and to minimize inbuilt age associated with woody material . The majority of the samples (95 of 120) were radiocarbon dated in the AMS facilities of A.E. Lalonde Laboratory (University of Ottawa, Canada), the remainder in the AMS facilities of Keck Laboratory (University of California, USA), Livermore National Laboratory (USA), Poznań Radiocarbon Laboratory (Poland), and Tallinn University of Technology (Estonia). Eight peat and 4 charcoal samples were dated radiometrically in Kiev Radiocarbon Laboratory (Institute of Environmental Geochemistry, Ukraine).

From one to four radiocarbon dates were obtained per swidden layer in most sites. The radiocarbon dates were calibrated, and diagrams and maps were created using the OxCal 4.4 program58. The r package rcarbon version 1.5.122 was used to compute the summed probability distribution (spd) of the radiocarbon dates. The taphonomic correction follows59.
