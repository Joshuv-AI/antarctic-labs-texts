Effect of Tectonic Deformation on the Pore System and Methane Adsorption of Anthracite Coal
Authors: Mingyu Zhu, Tieya Jing, Haowei Yuan, Jian Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTectonic deformation significantly alters the physical structure of coals, holding great importance to the coal mining industry and coalbed methane. In this study, eight anthracite coal samples with varying degrees of deformation were collected to investigate the effects of tectonic deformation on the pore system and CH4 adsorption of anthracite coals. In addition, low-temperature gas adsorption (N2 and CO2), Raman spectroscopy, and CH4 isothermal experiments were performed. The results revealed that coal samples with higher degrees of deformation exhibited larger ratios of D-band intensity to G-band intensity (ID/IG), indicating increased molecular defects induced by tectonic deformation. As the deformation degree of the coal samples increased, the mesopore volume increased from 0.00044 cm3/g (primary coal) to 0.0019 cm3/g (scaly coal). Conversely, the micropore volume tended to decrease with the increasing deformation degree of the coal samples. Moreover, the impact of deformation degree on the CH4 adsorption capacity of anthracite coals was complex. With the deformation degree increasing, the Langmuir volume initially decreased from 32.0 to 24.55 cm3/g and then rose to 30.14 cm3/g. This complexity arose from the differential effects of tectonic deformation on various pore types, where micropores and mesopores collectively determined the CH4 adsorption capacity of anthracite coals. This study analyzed the influence of tectonic deformation on the pore structure and CH4 adsorption capacity at the molecular level, providing valuable insights for evaluating the in situ CH4 content in anthracite coal seams.

Introduction

Coals, predominantly composed of organic matter, are categorized as soft rocks that are susceptible to deformation induced by tectonic forces.1,2 Tectonic deformation exerts substantial impacts on key physical properties, including pore structure, mechanical properties, and gas adsorption capacity.3−5 Notably, methane (CH4) exists within the pore system in the adsorbed and free phases.6 Consequently, tectonic deformation can significantly affect in situ CH4 content, which is crucial for enhancing the safety of coal mining and advancing coalbed gas exploration.3,4 In recent years, coalbed methane geologists and coal mining engineers have increasingly focused on understanding the impacts of tectonic deformation on the physical properties of coal, leading to a substantial body of research.4,7 However, given the intricate nature of coal pore systems, additional investigations are required.

In the realm of coal mining gas burst investigations, coals subjected to tectonic influences have been denoted as “tectonic coals”.8−10 Conversely, coalbed methane geologists commonly refer to them as “tectonically deformed coals” and classify them into distinct categories based on the type and extent of deformation.1,11−13 To understand the tectonic impact on the CH4 adsorption capacity and pore system of coal, a collection of natural coal samples exhibiting varying degrees of deformation was assembled and compared.14,15 Subsequently, pore characterization methods, including low-temperature N2 and CO2 adsorption experiments, have been routinely employed to investigate the nanopore size distribution of tectonically deformed coals.16,17 Moreover, CH4 isothermal experiments represent a pivotal technique for assessing the CH4 adsorption capacity of coals.10,18 Additional investigations include the applications of Raman spectroscopy, 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (13C NMR), and HRTEM to analyze the molecular structure of tectonic coals.17,19,20 Yu et al.1 compiled a comprehensive classification of tectonically deformed coals, categorizing them into three subgroups: brittle deformation (encompassing cataclastic, mortar, and granulitic coals), ductile deformation (comprising wrinkle and mylonitic coals), and shear deformation (including schistose, flake, and scaly coals).1,13 Notably, previous studies have revealed a continuous increase in pore volume and surface area with increasing deformation levels.13,21 However, certain studies have shown that the mesopore volumes of primary anthracite coals are not significantly lower than those of tectonic coals.10 Additionally, Wang et al.22 explored the impact of tectonic deformation on the pore systems of different macerals (vitrinite and inertinite) and noted that the mesopore volume of vitrinite initially decreased and subsequently increased, whereas that of inertinite exhibited irregular changes with increasing deformation degrees.

Previous studies have reported a positive correlation between increasing deformation degree and CH4 adsorption capacity when examining the adsorption characteristics of coal samples with varying degrees of tectonic deformation. This suggests that the tectonic deformation can enhance the adsorption capacity of the coal samples.22 This observation can be attributed to the creation of new pores or the enlargement of existing pore volumes within the coal samples owing to tectonic deformation. However, other studies have indicated that with increasing deformation, the CH4 adsorption capacity initially decreases and subsequently increases.18 Numerous researchers have investigated the impact of tectonic deformation on CH4 adsorption by examining the molecular structure of coal.12 Their findings have revealed that tectonic deformation leads to increased aromaticity and reduced aliphatic chains within coal.17,23 High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) data have also illustrated that tectonic deformation results in the elongation and straightening of aromatic rings.24

Despite numerous studies dedicated to exploring the pore system and CH4 adsorption of tectonically deformed coals, debate persists regarding the extent of the impact of tectonic deformation on coal properties. Furthermore, few studies have been conducted on anthracite coal samples with varying degrees of deformation. Therefore, diverse anthracite coal specimens were collected from the Diandong coal field. A comprehensive analysis was conducted, encompassing Raman spectroscopy, low-temperature N2 adsorption experiments, low-temperature CO2 adsorption experiments, and CH4 adsorption experiments, all aimed at providing an in-depth molecular-level understanding of how tectonic deformation influences the pore structure and CH4 adsorption in anthracite coal.

Samples and Experiments

Samples

Eight coal samples were collected from the Diandong coal field located in East Yunnan, China. Due to the heterogeneity of coal seams, the deformation degree of anthracite coal samples exhibited rapid variations such that adjacent samples often displayed distinct deformation features. Therefore, we classified these Diandong anthracite coal samples based on previous studies,1 resulting in categories of primary anthracite, cataclastic, granulitic, flake, and scaly coals (Figure 1). Notably, the tectonic coal samples, particularly scaly coal and flake coal, exhibited pronounced differences in appearance compared with the primary anthracite coal samples. In cataclastic, granulitic, and flake coals, evident fractures were observed. The proximate analysis of the Diandong coal samples is presented in Table 1. The moisture content (Mad) ranged from 0.97 to 1.46%, with an average of 1.15%. The ash content in these Diandong coal samples was notably high, ranging from 15.1 to 24.61%, with an average of 20.02%. Specifically, D-GL-1 exhibited the lowest ash content, whereas D-FL-2 exhibited the highest ash content (Table 1).

Experimental Methods

Pore Structure Characterization

Low-temperature N2 and CO2 gas adsorption experiments were conducted using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020. Before experimentation, the coal samples were crushed to a particle size of 60–80 mesh. For the anthracite coals under investigation, a temperature of 110 °C was selected for dewatering and outgassing because it did not induce changes in their chemical structures. In the N2 adsorption experiment, the relative pressure range was set to 0–1. In addition, for the CO2 adsorption experiment, the relative pressure range was set to 0–0.03. These specific parameters (relative pressure) were the standards for evaluating the pore structure in coal. The pore classification utilized in this study was the classification proposed by IUPAC (1994), which categorizes nanopores into micropores (<2 nm), mesopores (2–50 nm), and macropores (>50 nm). For meso- and macropores, the pore size distribution was analyzed using low-temperature N2 adsorption data by applying the density functional theory (DFT) model.25 For the micropores, the pore size distribution and pore volume data were derived from CO2 adsorption experiments and analyzed using nonlocal density functional theory (NLDFT) models.26

Methane Adsorption Isothermal Experiment

The CH4 adsorption isotherms of Diandong anthracite coal samples were determined using the volumetric method. Before the experiments, the Diandong anthracite coals were finely ground to a particle size of 60–80 mesh and subsequently dried at 110 °C. All of the CH4 adsorption isothermal experiments were performed at a constant temperature of 20 °C. The maximum adsorption pressure applied to all Diandong anthracite coal samples was maintained at 8.0 MPa. Each coal sample underwent CH4 adsorption capacity testing, which involved the assessment of CH4 adsorption at equilibrium points within the pressure range of 9–11. Subsequently, CH4 adsorption data were analyzed using the Langmuir adsorption model.27

Raman Spectroscopy

Previous studies have demonstrated that Raman spectroscopy can effectively characterize the impact of tectonic deformation on the molecular structure of coal samples.11,20 Hence, a Raman experiment was performed to examine alterations in the coal molecule structure induced by tectonic deformation. A Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution Confocal Raman Microscope (Japan) was employed for this purpose. Before the experiment, the coal samples were meticulously reduced to a powder form. During the experimental procedure, a laser wavelength was set to 532 nm, and the Raman shift range was set within the interval of 500–4000 cm–1.

Results

Low-Temperature N2 Adsorption Experimental Results

Figure 2 shows low-temperature N2 gas adsorption and desorption curves. Notably, all Diandong anthracite coal samples exhibited prominent hysteresis patterns in their adsorption/desorption curves, which can be attributed to the presence of bottleneck and ink bottle-shaped pores within these coal samples. When considering a low-pressure range (p/p0 ≤ 0.2), the N2 adsorption was minimal, indicating a scarcity of small nanopores within the Diandong anthracite coal samples.28 Variations in the adsorption/desorption curves were noticeable among the coals with different degrees of deformation. Notably, as the pressure reached p/p0 > 0.8, there was a sharp increase in N2 adsorption, with D-SC-1 (scaly coal) displaying significantly higher adsorption than other Diandong anthracite coal samples. In the high-pressure stage (p/p0 > 0.8), the maximum N2 adsorption capacity in the D-SC-1 sample (scaly coal) reached 1.42 cm3/g, surpassing other types of Diandong deformed coal samples. Furthermore, an increase in deformation degree corresponded to a gradual rise in the maximum N2 adsorption capacity in Diandong anthracite coal. These findings also demonstrated an increase in mesopores resulting from tectonic deformation in these Diandong anthracite coal samples. Additionally, the N2 adsorption at p/p0 = 1 in the D-FL-2 (flake coal) sample was notably lower than that of the other flake coal samples (D-FL-1 and D-FL-3). This disparity may be attributed to the considerably higher ash content of the D-FL-2 sample (Table 1).

Low-temperature N2 adsorption curves of Diandong anthracite coal samples.

Utilizing data from low-temperature N2 adsorption experiments, the pore size distribution, specifically focusing on mesopores and macropores, can be obtained within these Diandong anthracite coal samples (Figure 3). Notably, the predominant mesopore size was in the range of 10–25 nm, indicating that pores within this 10–25 nm range contributed significantly to the total volume and surface area of mesopores. From Figure 3, our analysis revealed that the incremental volumes of pores sized between 2–8 nm in Diandong anthracite coals were close to 0, suggesting a scarcity of such pores within these coal samples. Additionally, the pore volume values of the coal samples were sorted in descending order, with scaly coal (D-SC-1) > flake coal (D-FL-1, D-FL-2, and D-FL-3) > granulitic coal (D-GL-1 and D-GL-2) > cataclastic coal (D-CC-1) > primary coal (D-PM-2). Figure 3 also provides insight into the formation of smaller nanopores within the coal samples as the degree of deformation increases, as exemplified by samples D-PM-2, D-CC-1, D-GL-1, D-FL-1, and D-SC-1.

Low-Temperature CO2 Adsorption Experimental Results

Low-temperature CO2 adsorption experiments were conducted to assess the presence of micropores within Diandong anthracite coals. As depicted in Figure 4, there is an increase in CO2 adsorption as the relative pressure increases for all Diandong anthracite coal samples. The CO2 adsorption amounts at p/p0 = 0.03 for Diandong anthracite coals can be ordered as follows: D-SC-1 (scaly coal) < D-GL-1 (granulitic coal) < D-PM-2 (primary coal) < D-FL-1 (flake coal) < D-CC-1 (cataclastic coal) < D-GL-2 (granulitic coal) < D-FL-2 (flake coal) < D-FL-3 (flake coal). Remarkably, despite the strong deformation experienced by the scaly coal sample, its CO2 adsorption amount at p/p0 = 0.03 (17.64 cm3/g) was notably lower than that of other coal sample types.

CO2 adsorption curves (at 0 °C) of eight Diandong anthracite coal samples.

The low-temperature CO2 adsorption experimental data, in conjunction with the DFT model, enabled the calculation of the micropore volume and micropore surface area across various pore sizes (Figures 5 and 6). An examination of Figure 5 revealed a remarkable similarity in the shapes of the micropore surface area size distribution curves among these coal samples. Notably, the highest incremental pore volume was consistently observed at approximately 0.55 nm. Moreover, distinct peaks were evident at 0.87 nm within the micropore volume size distribution curves for all Diandong anthracite coals. The overall specific surface area was mainly provided by the pores ranging in size from 0.4 to 0.9 nm, contributing to approximately 94–99% of the total micropore surface area (Figure 6).

Results of Raman Spectroscopy

Figure 7 illustrates the Raman spectra obtained from the Diandong coal samples, revealing three distinct peaks at 1352 cm–1 (D band), 1592 cm–1 (G band), and 2901 cm–1 (associated with second-order Raman modes, displaying broad peaks). Notably, the positions of the D and G bands remained consistent across the coal samples with varying degrees of deformation. This observation suggested that tectonic deformation did not induce changes in the positions of the D and G bands. Previous studies have indicated that the location of these bands is significantly affected by coal maturity.29 As the maturity levels of the Diandong coal samples were identical, their respective D- and G-band positions remained unchanged. However, a discernible disparity was evident in the ratios of the D-band intensity to the G-band intensity (ID/IG) among the Diandong anthracite coal samples. From Figure 8, the ID/IG values ranged from 0.785 to 0.868 across the coal samples, exhibiting varying degrees of deformation. Primary coal and cataclastic coal exhibited the lowest ID/IG values, whereas scaly coal exhibited the highest ID/IG ratio. In general, an increase in coal sample deformation corresponds to an increase in the ID/IG value. Because the D band is associated with disordered carbons in coal samples, tectonic deformation is inferred to augment the presence of disordered carbons within the coal matrix. This phenomenon aligned with the findings of Han et al., who identified tectonic deformation as a factor inducing defects in the aromatic structures of coal samples, with these defects categorized as disordered carbons.20

Ratio of D-band intensity to G-band intensity of the Diandong anthracite coal samples.

Methane Isothermal Adsorption Results

In Figure 9, the CH4 adsorption quantities for Diandong anthracite coals exhibited continuous increases with increasing pressure. Simultaneously, CH4 adsorption demonstrated a rapid ascent when the pressure remained below 3 MPa. Conversely, when the pressure surpassed 3 MPa, CH4 adsorption experienced a more gradual increase. For instance, when the pressure increased from 1 to 3 MPa, the CH4 adsorption in the D-PM-2 coal sample increased from 15.16 to 25.27 cm3/g, representing a 67% increase. When the pressure further increased from 3 to 8 MPa, the CH4 adsorption in the same sample increased from 25.27 to 27.98 cm3/g, displaying an 11% increment. Importantly, all of the experimental adsorption data conformed closely to the Langmuir curves, and beyond 8 MPa, CH4 adsorption continued to approach the Langmuir volume with increasing pressure. Specifically, the Langmuir volumes for D-PM-2, D-CC-1, D-FL-1, and D-SC-1 samples were recorded as 32.0, 25.5, 54.6, and 30.1 cm3/g, respectively. Significantly, among Diandong anthracite coal samples with varying degrees of deformation, there were notable differences in CH4 adsorption capacity. The CH4 adsorption capacity ranking for Diandong anthracite coals, from highest to lowest, was as follows: primary coal (D-PM-2) > scaly coal (D-SC-1) > cataclastic coal (D-CC-1) > flake coal (D-FL-1).

Discussion

Influence of Tectonic Deformation on the Pore System

Pores within the internal coal reservoir can provide storage space for CH430,31.30,31 Therefore, alterations in nanopore volume and surface area are significantly related to CH4 adsorption.32−34 From Figure 10, there is an evident and significant change in the micropore volume and surface area within Diandong anthracite coals with increasing deformation degrees. The change in the micropore volume was similar to the trends observed in the specific surface area. The change in the micropore volume within the coal samples was observed in two distinct stages with increasing deformation degree. During the first stage, spanning from primary anthracite coal to flake anthracite coal, there was a gradual increase in the micropore volume, progressing from 0.33 to 0.35 cm3/g. However, within scaly coals, there was a rapid decrease in micropore volume, plummeting to 0.29 cm3/g. Similarly, pore surface area exhibited an analogous pattern, increasing from 108.36 to 114.20 m2/g from primary coal to flake coal and subsequently decreasing to 92.09 m2/g in scaly coal. Previous studies have elucidated that coal micropores were generated within the coal macromolecule structure and were regulated by molecular composition.35,36 Therefore, tectonic deformation induced alterations in the macromolecular structure of coal samples, which was proved by Raman spectra, FT-IR spectra, and HRTEM data in previous studies.11,18,20 During the first stage, tectonic stress led to the development of molecular defects within tectonically deformed coals.11 These molecular defects subsequently manifested as micropores within the coal matrix.17 In the scaly coals, characterized by more substantial tectonic stress, the coal samples experienced further compaction, resulting in a notable reduction in micropore volume during the second stage.

Changes in micropore parameters (volume and surface area) of Diandong anthracite coals with different deformed degrees.

The formation mechanisms of mesopores differed from those of micropores, which contributed to distinct evolution patterns (Figure 11). In general, the volume and surface area of the mesopores exhibited notable augmentation with increasing deformation degree. For instance, in the primary coal (D-PM-2), the mesopore volume was 0.00044 cm3/g. In the granulitic coal, this volume increased to 0.00096 cm3/g, and in the scaly coal, it experienced a substantial increase, reaching 0.0019 cm3/g. Similarly, the surface area of mesopores followed a similar trend, initially ascending from 0.045 m2/g in primary coal to 0.11 m2/g in granulitic coal, and subsequently escalating to 0.23 m2/g in scaly coal. Tectonic stress within coal generated numerous microfractures, which created a multitude of new mesopores during the deformation process.17 From Figure 11, it can also be seen that the pore volume of D-FL-2 is much smaller than those of other flake coal samples. This is mainly because the ash content of the D-FL-2 coal sample is 24.61%, which is the highest among all of the coal samples in this study. As few nanopores in the minerals of coal and nanopores are mainly provided by the organic matter (organic pores), larger ash content means fewer nanopores.

Changes in mesopore parameters (volume and surface area) of Diandong anthracite coals with different deformed degrees.

Figure 12a,12b shows the correlations between the pore volume and ID/IG value. There was an increase in the mesopore volume as the ID/IG value increased. Conversely, a decrease in the ID/IG value corresponded to a reduction in the micropore volume. This phenomenon was primarily attributed to the formation of micropores within the basic structural units (BSUs), and mesopores predominantly arose between distinct BSUs. Figure 8 illustrates that tectonic deformation tended to elevate the ID/IG value, indicating that it altered the edge or alignment of BSUs, thereby augmenting the mesopore volume. Concurrently, there was a decrease in void spaces within BSUs.

Correlation between pore parameters and ID/IG values: (a) mesopore volume from the low-temperature N2 adsorption experiments and (b) micropore volume from the low-temperature CO2 adsorption experiments.

Adsorption Properties of Coal with Different Deformation Degrees

Langmuir pressure has a significant impact on evaluating the conditions for coalbed methane desorption, which are very important for the coalbed methane industry and coal mining safety.37 A lower Langmuir pressure indicated that a significant proportion of CH4 molecules was adsorbed within the coal at lower pressures and these CH4 molecules were difficult to be desorbed. Figure 13 illustrates the Langmuir volume and pressure characteristics for coals exhibiting varying degrees of deformation. As deformation degree increased, the Langmuir volume exhibited a decreasing trend from 32.0 cm3/g (D-PM-2) to 24.55 cm3/g (D-FL-1) before subsequently rebounding to 30.14 cm3/g (D-SC-1). Conversely, the Langmuir pressure increased from 0.9 MPa (D-PM-2) to 1.65 MPa (D-FL-1), followed by a decline to 0.91 MPa (D-SC-1). CH4 molecules are typically adsorbed on the surfaces of nanopores, encompassing both micropores and mesopores.38 Micropores in primary coal possessed a larger volume and surface area than scaly coal. As the degree of deformation increased, coal experienced fragmentation, resulting in pulverized coal that could obstruct pores.39 Hence, the CH4 adsorption capacity decreased for cataclastic and flake coals. Notably, scaly coals exhibited more mesopores than cataclastic and flake coals (Figure 11). These mesopores in scaly coals had a significantly larger surface area, providing more space for adsorption and transport of CH4 molecules. Therefore, scaly coals exhibited enhanced CH4 adsorption. These findings align with previous research.18 Wang et al. also observed a decline, followed by an increase in CH4 adsorption capacity with increasing deformation degree.18 Moreover, Li et al. suggested that strongly deformed coals displayed a greater adsorption capacity than weakly deformed coals, attributing this difference to alterations in spatial configurations and surface energy.40

Characteristics of the Langmuir volume and Langmuir pressure changed as the degree of deformation increased.

Previous studies have consistently revealed a positive correlation between the CH4 adsorption capacity and micropore volume.31,33,34 Furthermore, pore connectivity, coal molecular structure, and surface roughness have demonstrated significant impacts on the CH4 adsorption capacity.41,42 The impact of deformation on the CH4 adsorption capacity of coal samples can be complex. As elucidated earlier, tectonic deformation can induce changes in coal properties, spanning from the molecular to the micron scale. Notably, micropores and mesopores reacted diversely to changes in the coal molecular structure. The micropore volume decreased as the ID/IG value increased, and the mesopore volume increased with higher ID/IG values. Our experimental data, as well as previous research on tectonically deformed coals, collectively demonstrated that the degree of deformation significantly affected the CH4 adsorption capacity of anthracite coals.17 This degree of deformation was a pivotal factor in assessing the risk of gas outbursts, particularly within coal mines that were subjected to robust tectonic compression. Consequently, conducting a comprehensive examination of the spatial distribution, pore systems, and adsorption behavior in tectonically deformed coals were the fundamental basis for mitigating gas outburst risks. Therefore, classifying the hazard potential of coal seams in distinct regions based on pore systems and the adsorption capacities of tectonically deformed coals can be essential.

Conclusions

Eight distinct Diandong anthracite coals, characterized by varying degrees of deformation, were collected for an in-depth investigation into the impact of tectonic deformation on the nanopore system and CH4 adsorption. From this analysis, several noteworthy conclusions regarding the effects of tectonic deformation on nanopore systems and CH4 adsorption can be derived.

(1)Tectonic deformation did not alter the positions of the D and G bands. Conversely, there was an observable increase in the ratio of the D-band intensity to the G-band intensity (ID/IG) as the degree of coal sample deformation increased. This indicated that the maturity of the Diandong coal sample remained unchanged owing to tectonic deformation. However, there was an increase in the presence of disordered carbon in the coal samples.

However, there was an increase in the presence of disordered carbon in the coal samples.

(2)In the case of micropores within coal, the micropore volume exhibited an initial gradual increase from 0.33 to 0.35 cm3/g as the coal transitioned from primary anthracite to flake anthracite. However, in the case of scaly coal, there was a rapid subsequent decrease reaching 0.29 cm3/g. Conversely, the volume of mesopores in Diandong anthracite coal demonstrated a growth in proportion to the increasing degree of deformation.
