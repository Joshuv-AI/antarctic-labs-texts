Enhancing the use of routine program data for immunization decision-making: The role of coincidence analysis in localized learning and adaptation
Authors: Abdu A. Adamu, Duduzile Ndwandwe, Olalekan A. Uthman, Charles S. Wiysonge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTReal-world program data, collected through information management systems and activities like supportive supervision, are abundant in many low- and middle-income countries across the world. These data can be harnessed for systems learning to improve immunization decision-making and address disparities in vaccination coverage. However, effective systems learning relies on pragmatic causal reasoning, and the main question is: “How can these routine program data be used to support causal learning to improve equity in vaccination?” This commentary introduces coincidence analysis as a supplementary tool that can facilitate causal learning in vaccination settings using existing real-world program data. This innovative tool employs a custom-built algorithm for causal inference. It can help immunization stakeholders better understand implementation conditions across districts by using a configurational approach that identifies causal structures and chains. Such insights can guide tailored strategies for optimizing service delivery in underserved areas such as conflict zones, informal urban settlements, and remote villages.

Introduction

Vaccination coverage inequity is a serious issue in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) with global health implications.1,2 Even within the same country, significant disparities in vaccination coverage can be seen between different subnational areas.3,4 For example, Nigeria’s northern geopolitical zones, where violence, terrorism, and internal displacements are high, have a notably higher rate of unvaccinated and under-vaccinated children compared to the southern zones.3 In metropolitan areas with both formal and informal settlements, disparities in full vaccination coverage have been demonstrated by studies conducted in the Western Cape and Nairobi.5,6 When viewed through a complexity lens, these disparities are not unexpected because, in complex adaptive health systems, the interaction of factors related to individuals, health facilities, health institutions, the environment, communities, government, and policies often influences the success of interventions.7 The dynamics of these interactions can vary from one area to another, resulting in differences in program outcomes like vaccination coverage.7,8

Studies from multiple countries have shown that most children who miss out on vaccination are concentrated in underserved areas such as fragile and conflict-affected settings, informal urban settlements (i.e., slums), and remote rural villages.2,9,10 This makes vaccination coverage inequity a social justice issue that demands urgent attention from decision-makers and program implementers. If disparities in vaccination coverage remain unaddressed, its health and non-health consequences are likely to perpetuate a vicious cycle of underdevelopment that disproportionately affects marginalized populations. Although there are no easy solutions, a paradigm shift toward systems thinking in immunization programs can pave the way for greater reliance on emergent approaches at the subnational level to tackle contextual factors that hinder access to and uptake of new and existing vaccines.

Systems learning in immunization programs

One way to leverage systems thinking in immunization programs is to strengthen continuous learning, which requires data.11 Fortunately, governments in LMICs collect a substantial amount of real-world data from health and other sectors through various information management platforms.12–14 Examples include the District Health Information System (DHIS2), electronic Joint Reporting Forms (eJRF) on immunization, and the National Health Accounts, among others.13,15,16 The DHIS2 is used in over 70 countries across Africa, Asia, the Americas, and the Middle East to routinely collect data on health, logistics, education, and climate change.14,15,17 Additionally, data are collected at health facilities through activities like supportive supervision.18

These data can play a vital role in guiding ongoing decision-making to enhance immunization program performance across various subnational areas. However, effective system learning relies on pragmatic causal reasoning by stakeholders. The main question is: “How can these routine program data be used to support causal learning to improve equity in vaccination?” This commentary introduces coincidence analysis as a supplementary tool that can help facilitate causal learning in vaccination settings using existing real-world program data.19 The tool can enable immunization decision-makers, program implementers, and other stakeholders to better understand the conditions affecting the implementation of vaccination efforts across diverse subnational settings.19,20

What is coincidence analysis?

CNA is part of a family of analytical frameworks for causal inference called Configurational Comparative Methods (CCM).21,22 CCMs are especially useful for determining causality in complex health systems, which makes them popular in social sciences and, more recently, in implementation research.22,23 The theoretical foundation for CCM is derived from Mackie’s INUS theory,24 which has been refined by other scientists.25 INUS stands for Insufficient Necessary Unnecessary Sufficient.24,26 Mackie’s INUS condition is part of a larger system that encompasses many different conditions. A condition can be a factor or an intervention that affects an outcome.21 When these conditions act together, they can be sufficient but unnecessary to produce the outcome. Unnecessary means that other sets of factors can also cause the same outcome. On the other hand, a condition by itself (without others) is insufficient but necessary to produce the outcome.24,26 For example, not every zero-dose child is a consequence of residing in a conflict-affected area.10 Zero-dose children can also result from living in an informal urban settlement or caregiver vaccine hesitancy, among other factors.10,27 However, living in a conflict-affected area, where factors such as the closure of nearby fixed vaccination sites, the inability to conduct outreach vaccination, and restricted mobility of caregivers are also at play, can be sufficient for a child in that community to remain unvaccinated (a zero-dose child).4,10,28 An INUS condition in this case is residing in a conflict-affected area. In this simplified example, the causal pathway involves four interacting conditions. According to the regularity theory of causality, these factors are the “difference-makers.”25 Removing any one of them can probably alter the causal chain leading to a zero-dose child.

A popular type of CCM, known as qualitative comparative analysis (QCA), has gained a strong foothold in health service research.23 QCA is based on Boolean reasoning and designed to analyze the complex causal configurations of multiple independent causes with a single outcome.29 However, its algorithm can produce counterfactual reasoning.30 Additionally, relevant causal models can be automatically removed without any rationale due to built-in ambiguity reduction protocols.31

CNA is a newer approach to CCM that is also based on Boolean algebra.21,29 It overcomes the limitations of QCA by using a custom-built algorithm designed explicitly for investigating causal structures.29 For this reason, it can identify patterns and structures with interrelated (or dependent) conditions and multiple outcomes; the so-called multi-outcome structure.19,21,30 It can be used to determine whether multiple conditions combine to produce an outcome (conjunctivity) and to identify alternative causal pathways to the outcomes (equifinality).19 CNA employs a bottom-up process for model fitting, starting with a single condition and progressively adding more conditions until the search target is reached.29 It is during this bottom-up process that an initial analysis of sufficiency is conducted, and the results are then used to inform subsequent analyses of necessity.29

Why do we need coincidence analysis in immunization programs?

System complexity creates analytical challenges for program implementers tasked with deciphering the causal pathways between factors or interventions and vaccination coverage using routine data. Since the relationships between these factors can be interconnected in multiple ways, methods like traditional regression are less suitable.32 CNA can help immunization stakeholders make more accurate causal inferences from data collected in real-world program settings by identifying groups of relevant factors that influence vaccination coverage in specific districts. Or how different strategies across diverse settings influence vaccination coverage. This information can be used to clarify causal linkages, which can in turn guide targeted policies, inform localized practice improvements, and future research.

There has been a growing use of CNA by implementation researchers to gain better insights into the connections between contextual factors, interventions, and outcomes of interest.33–35 In a study conducted in Canada, researchers used CNA to examine the contextual elements that influence the adoption of best practices in long-term care homes, identifying three paths that led to the use of best practices among care aides.34 In the United States, CNA was employed to explore how contextual characteristics, organizational factors, and collaboration strategies affect the implementation fidelity of a behavioral intervention.35 Although evidence is scarce on the use of CNA in immunization programs, researchers have demonstrated its value through human papillomavirus vaccination catch-up data, providing a more nuanced understanding of the implementation strategies that influenced high uptake.20

Coincidence analysis in the context of other established causal inference methods

It is important to emphasize that CNA does not replace existing causal inference methods; rather, it complements them by addressing specific analytical challenges that arise in complex health system contexts.19,20 Understanding when and how to deploy CNA alongside established approaches is crucial for maximizing its utility for learning and adaptation in immunization programs. Complementing established causal inference methods.

Randomized controlled trials remain the gold standard for establishing the efficacy of interventions including policies under controlled conditions.36 Their strength lies in eliminating selection bias through randomization and providing precise estimates of average treatment effects.37 However, RCTs face significant limitations in informing immunization decision-making, particularly their substantial cost and limited external validity.38 Due to the exigencies of immunization decision-making in some situations, RCTs might not even be feasible to inform policies. For example, many countries implemented several interventions as part of their big catch-up activities after the COVID-19 pandemic.39 When immunization programs implement multiple interacting interventions simultaneously across diverse district contexts, the controlled conditions necessary for RCTs become impractical. CNA offers particular value in these situations by identifying which combinations of interventions are necessary and sufficient for achieving vaccination coverage improvements across different contextual conditions.20 Rather than seeking a single average treatment effect, CNA reveals multiple pathways to success, each applicable to specific district profiles.19

Propensity score matching represents a valuable approach for controlling selection bias in observational studies by matching treated and untreated units on observed characteristics.40 This method performs well when researchers can identify and measure the key confounding variables that influence both treatment assignment and outcomes.40,41 However, immunization programs often operate in contexts where the full set of confounders is unknown or unmeasured. District-level factors such as community social cohesion, informal leadership structures, or historical experiences with health interventions may influence both program implementation decisions and vaccination outcomes, but remain difficult to quantify in routine data systems. CNA addresses this challenge by identifying configurations of observed factors that predict outcomes without requiring complete specification of all confounding pathways.19,20 The method’s strength lies in revealing patterns in the data that suggest unmeasured confounding structures, pointing researchers toward important factors that may have been overlooked.

Difference-in-differences designs excel at evaluating policy changes by comparing trends over time between treated and control groups.41 This approach effectively controls for time-invariant confounders and has been successfully applied to evaluate large-scale immunization policy changes.42 However, the method’s core assumption of parallel trends becomes problematic when multiple interventions are introduced simultaneously or when different districts receive different combinations of interventions.42,43 Many immunization programs implement comprehensive packages that include supply chain improvements, healthcare worker training, community engagement activities, and demand generation campaigns. These multi-component interventions violate the single-treatment assumption underlying difference-in-differences designs.42,44 CNA addresses this complexity by analyzing how different combinations of interventions interact to produce outcomes, identifying which components are essential and which are redundant across different contexts.19–21

Instrumental variable approaches offer powerful solutions for addressing unobserved confounding by using external factors that influence treatment assignment but not outcomes directly.45 While theoretically elegant, valid instruments are rare in health system research,46,47 particularly at the district level, where some immunization program decisions are made. The factors that influence program implementation decisions typically also influence vaccination outcomes through multiple pathways, violating the exclusion restriction required for valid instrumental variable analysis. CNA provides an alternative approach for complex causal inference that does not rely on identifying valid instruments. Instead, it leverages patterns in configurational data to uncover causal structures, making it especially useful when traditional instrumental variable approaches are not feasible.29

The practical application of these different methods depends on specific analytical goals and data availability. When evaluating a single, well-defined intervention such as SMS reminder systems for vaccination appointments, regression discontinuity designs or randomized trials may provide the most straightforward causal inference. However, when trying to understand why some districts achieve high vaccination rates while others struggle despite similar resource allocations, CNA reveals the complex interactions between contextual factors, program components, and outcomes that determine success or failure.

CNA is especially useful for generating hypotheses in complex program settings. By spotting unexpected patterns in routine data, CNA can uncover causal links that need further testing through traditional experimental or quasi-experimental approaches. For instance, CNA might show that districts with strong community leadership and adequate cold chain infrastructure achieve high coverage regardless of healthcare worker density, whereas districts lacking these factors require more healthcare worker support to reach similar results. These insights can then help shape targeted randomized trials to test specific ideas about causal mechanisms.

The integration of CNA with established methods creates opportunities for more comprehensive causal analysis. CNA can identify relevant causal configurations from observational data, which can then be validated through experimental approaches. Alternatively, randomized trials can establish causal effects under controlled conditions, while CNA explores how these effects vary across different real-world contexts and implementation configurations.

This complementary approach acknowledges that no single analytical method can address all causal inference challenges in complex health systems. CNA fills specific gaps in the causal inference toolkit, particularly around configurational complexity and multiple pathways to outcomes, while established methods continue to provide essential capabilities for controlled evaluation and bias reduction. The optimal analytical strategy combines multiple approaches, utilizing each method’s strengths to address different aspects of the causal inference challenge inherent in evaluating and improving immunization programs. How can coincidence analysis be performed?

There are libraries of resources and training on CNA that immunization stakeholders at the national and subnational levels can consult to expand their understanding of this tool.48 It is worthwhile to integrate implementation researchers with expertise in CNA in immunization programs at all levels to institutionalize this tool for driving learning and adaptation. Whitaker and colleagues provided a step-by-step guide on conducting CNA in their 2020 article, using an example with HPV vaccination update.20 The essential first steps are to determine the quality of the data and adjust the data coding to make it suitable for CNA.20 There is also an R package called cna for performing coincidence analysis.49 Unlike traditional regression methods, the parameters for evaluating model fit in CNA are consistency and coverage.50 Consistency indicates the dependence between a configuration of factors or the entire model and the outcome.50 Coverage reflects the value of a configuration of factors or the whole model.50

Hypothetical case study: applying coincidence analysis to explore factors that contribute to children remaining unvaccinated

To illustrate the practical application of coincidence analysis in immunization programs, consider a hypothetical analysis of zero-dose children across West African districts. This example demonstrates how CNA can transform routine surveillance data into actionable program intelligence.

The analysis would utilize DHIS2 data from Nigeria, Mali, and Burkina Faso, covering 75 districts over a three-year period. The outcome variable would be high zero-dose rates, defined as districts where DTP1 coverage remains below 50%. Key conditions would include exposure to conflict, presence of urban slums, healthcare worker density, road accessibility, frequency of mobile clinics, and community engagement levels.

A CNA of this dataset might reveal two distinct causal pathways to high zero-dose rates:

Pathway 1: conflict × poor_roads × low_healthcare_workers → high_zero_dose

(Coverage: 0.85, Consistency: 0.92)

Pathway 2: urban_slums × low_engagement × infrequent_mobile_clinics → high_zero_dose

(Coverage: 0.73, Consistency: 0.88)

These results would indicate that districts experiencing the first configuration require integrated security protocols, infrastructure improvements, and healthcare worker incentives. Districts matching the second pattern would benefit from community mobilization strategies and enhanced urban outreach services.

Unlike traditional regression analysis, which might identify individual risk factors, CNA reveals that specific combinations of conditions create distinct pathways to poor outcomes for each setting. This configurational understanding enables program managers to design targeted intervention packages rather than implementing uniform strategies across all underperforming districts.

The analysis could then be validated by testing predictions against program outcomes in holdout districts, demonstrating CNA’s utility in translating complex, multidimensional challenges into concrete programmatic actions that address the root causes of vaccination inequity.

Implementation roadmap for coincidence analysis in immunization programs in resource-constrained settings

The successful integration of CNA into immunization programs in low- and middle-income countries requires careful consideration of capacity constraints, resource limitations, poor data quality, and lack of government buy-in, among other potential barriers. While the analytical potential of CNA is substantial, its implementation must be practical and sustainable within existing health system structures.

The foundational requirement for CNA implementation is analytical capacity at the appropriate organizational level. Experience from other complex analytical initiatives in LMICs suggests that having at least one data analyst with statistical software training per area (i.e., an administrative entity comprising multiple districts) is likely a suitable threshold for sustainable implementation. The strategic positioning of this capacity enables support across multiple districts while remaining close enough to local contexts to ensure a relevant interpretation of the findings. This analyst should be familiar with R statistical software and have a basic understanding of causal inference principles, although specialized CNA training can be provided subsequently.

Capacity development should follow a structured training program that balances theoretical understanding with practical application. Initial training focuses on building foundational knowledge in Boolean algebra and CNA theory, ensuring analysts understand the logical framework underlying configurational comparative methods. This theoretical grounding is essential, as CNA requires a different conceptual approach compared to traditional statistical methods commonly used in health program evaluation. The intermediate phase emphasizes hands-on experience with data preparation and quality assessment, including learning to transform routine immunization data into Boolean format, managing missing data, and ensuring dataset integrity for CNA analysis. Advanced training concentrates on model interpretation and program integration, teaching analysts how to translate CNA findings into actionable recommendations for immunization program managers and how to communicate complex configurational relationships to non-technical stakeholders.

Implementation should proceed through a carefully sequenced phased approach that allows for learning and adaptation at each stage. The initial phase focuses on developing CNA capacity at the central level while the foundational training program is underway. During this period, central-level analysts work with existing national datasets to conduct initial CNA analyses, building experience with the methodology while generating preliminary insights about national vaccination coverage patterns. This phase also involves establishing partnerships with academic institutions and international organizations that can provide technical support during the learning curve.

Success in resource-constrained settings requires strategic partnerships that leverage existing capacity while building sustainable internal expertise. Academic institutions, particularly those with established implementation science programs, can provide initial analytical support while internal capacity is being developed. These partnerships are most effective when they include explicit components for capacity transfer, ensuring that external support gradually transitions to internal capability. Collaboration with WHO country offices provides essential technical quality assurance, helping to ensure that CNA implementation aligns with broader health system strengthening initiatives. Gavi Health System Strengthening funding streams represent a natural financing mechanism for CNA capacity development, as the methodology directly supports the adaptive management approaches that the institution increasingly emphasizes in its support to countries.

The resource requirements for CNA implementation are modest compared to many health system interventions, making it particularly suitable for resource-constrained settings. The analytical software requirements center on the freely available R statistical environment and the cna package, eliminating licensing costs that often constrain adoption of commercial statistical software in LMICs. Standard desktop computers are sufficient for analyzing district-level datasets, avoiding the need for specialized hardware investments. The initial cost for training and mentorship support from CNA experts might be high since expertise is mainly in institutions in high-income countries (HICs). However, this investment in technical support is essential and worthwhile during the early years of implementation, after which LMICs would typically develop sufficient internal expertise to operate independently.

Other potential challenges, such as a lack of government buy-in and poor data quality, are also crucial and should be considered. High-level engagement of stakeholders in ministries of health at both the national and subnational levels is vital. Incompleteness of data or inconsistencies in reporting needed indicators can limit their use for CNA. Additionally, there is a need to strengthen advocacy for the quality of routine program data reporting in information systems.

This implementation roadmap recognizes that the sustainable adoption of CNA in immunization programs requires alignment with existing health system priorities, realistic capacity development timelines, addressing limitations in data quality, and fostering strategic partnerships between institutions (in HICs and LMICs) and governments (as shown in Figure 2). Conclusion

The next five years, leading up to the end of the Immunization Agenda 2030 (IA2030) and beyond, will be crucial in LMICs where vaccination coverage disparities remain high and disproportionately affect underserved communities. CNA has potential value for enhancing the use of existing real-world program data from health and other sectors for decision-making at the subnational level in learning and adaptation efforts. It can assist local immunization stakeholders in better understanding implementation conditions across districts by using a configurational approach that identifies causal structures and chains. Such insights can guide tailored strategies to optimize vaccination service delivery in underserved areas like conflict zones, informal urban settlements, and remote villages. However, human resources with the skills to perform CNA are still scarce in LMICs. Other challenges, such as funding, stakeholder buy-in, and data quality issues, among others, exist and could hinder adoption. Therefore, institutionalizing CNA in immunization programs will require coordinated investments from governments and Global Health Initiatives. It also calls for bold, innovative thinking by Expanded Program on Immunization managers within countries to leverage creative partnerships and expand local expertise in CNA, fostering ownership and sustainability.
