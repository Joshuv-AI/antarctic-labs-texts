Is stigma correlated with oral health-related quality of life in prosthodontic patients? a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zhe-Bin Yan, Rui Zhu, Xiao Yi, Ya-Peng Pei, Mei-Jun Zhan, Jia-Le Zhu, Hai-Yang Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe impact of psychological factors on oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) in prosthodontic patients should not be ignored. It is unclear about the extent to which stigma affects OHRQoL. The purpose of this study was to examine the relationship between stigma and OHRQoL in prosthodontic patients.MethodsA total of 139 patients were included. Stigma Scale for Chronic Illness (SSCI) and its subscales (enacted stigma (ES) and internalized stigma (IS)) were used to measure stigma. OHRQoL was evaluated utilizing 5-item Oral Health Impact Profile (OHIP-5), Psychosocial Impact of Dental Aesthetics Questionnaire (PIDAQ) and its subscales (Social Impact (SI), Psychological Impact (PI), Aesthetic Concern (AC) and Dental Self-Confidence (DSC)). The association between SSCI and OHIP/PIDAQ was detected employing Spearman correlation coefficients and multiple linear regression analysis.ResultsUnivariate analysis revealed that SSCI-total, ES, and IS scores were positively correlated with OHIP-5 (rs=0.350, 0.362, 0.279, P < 0.001); the first three were also positively correlated with PIDAQ-total (rs=0.625, 0.589, 0.566, P < 0.001), SI (rs=0.605, 0.548, 0.572, P < 0.001), PI (rs=0.649, 0.634, 0.551, P < 0.001), and AC (rs=0.580, 0.536, 0.534, P < 0.001), except for PIDAQ-DSC (P > 0.05). Upon controlling for confounders in multivariate analysis, SSCI-total, ES and IS maintained a strong positive correlation with OHIP-5 (B = 0.354, 0.427, 0.866, P < 0.001), PIDAQ-total (B = 2.329, 3.044, 5.138, P < 0.001), SI (B = 1.057, 1.458, 2.155, P < 0.001), PI (B = 0.813, 1.020, 1.893, P < 0.001), and AC (B = 0.423, 0.561, 0.916, P < 0.001), with the exception of PIDAQ-DSC (P > 0.05).ConclusionsStigma was negatively correlated with OHRQoL in prosthodontic patients. Higher stigma may impair OHRQoL. Interventions to alleviate the stigma in such patients is recommended to improve OHRQoL.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06935-x.Keywords: Stigma, Oral health-related quality of life, Oral health impact profile, Psychosocial impact of dental aesthetics questionnaire, Prosthodontic patient

Background

Oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL), a component of health-related quality of life, is a subjective dental patient-reported outcome that refers to the impact of oral disease states and treatment interventions on patients [1]. A commonly used tool for measuring OHRQoL is the Oral Health Impact Profile (OHIP) with 49 items [2]. The Psychosocial Impact of Dental Aesthetics Questionnaire (PIDAQ) was initially used to assess the psychosocial impact of dental appearance on orthodontic patients [3]. Recently it was adopted by Su et al. [4] to measure OHRQoL in prosthodontic patients, which focuses more on evaluating the psychosocial dimension of OHRQoL. The utilization of multiple scales allows for a more comprehensive assessment of OHRQoL, enabling a more accurate comparison of the effect sizes of the factors influencing OHRQoL, and thus the selection of factors improving it.

For prosthodontic patients, their OHRQoL is mainly jeopardized by tooth defects and loss [5, 6]. It is the prime goal of prosthodontists to fabricate high-quality prostheses aimed at rehabilitating patients’ OHRQoL and enhancing their satisfaction. Numerous studies have revealed the impact of clinical factors such as crown material [7], type and design of partial dentures [8, 9], and number of implants [10] on patients’ OHRQoL, which facilitates the screening of prosthodontic treatment options that can improve OHRQoL. Nevertheless, the influence of psychosocial factors on OHRQoL should not be neglected. It has been discovered that the socioeconomic status of older adults could affect OHRQoL [11]. Limited studies have disclosed the relationship between poor OHRQoL and certain psychological factors in prosthodontic patients, such as high neuroticism in implant patients as well as complete denture wearers [12, 13], and low self-esteem in removable partial denture wearers [14]. It is implied that in addition to clinical factors, dentists still ought to pay attention to the impact of psychosocial factors on OHRQoL in prosthodontic patients.

Stigma was first introduced by Goffman [15] as a derogatory and insulting labeling of certain individuals or groups by society. Corrigan [16] provided a further explanation, who argued that stigma could be categorized into public stigma and self-stigma, both of which included three core components: stereotype, prejudice, and discrimination. Molina et al. [17] classified stigma into enacted stigma, which referred to the negative attitudes expressed by the public towards a certain type of patients, and internalized stigma, which referred to the patient’s own endorsement of public stereotypes, prejudice, and resulting self-discrimination. Although stigma has been interpreted from multiple perspectives, it is universally acknowledged that stigma is the result of a combination of extrinsic (public) and intrinsic (patient) factors. Furthermore, measurement tools for stigma were developed, such as the 24-item Stigma Scale for Chronic Illness (SSCI) [18], and the Social Impact Scale [19]. Later the former was simplified to the 8-item SSCI (SSCI-8) [17].

Stigma, as a psychological feature, may cause several adverse effects on various types of patients, especially it may harm the quality of life (QoL). A wide range of studies have evaluated stigma in patients with neurological disorders such as epilepsy [20], mental disorders such as schizophrenia [21], and tumors such as oral cancer [22], and all have identified the association between stigma and diminished QoL or health-related QoL (HRQoL).

In recent years, stigma has begun to gain attention in the oral disease population. With the impact of notion of elite class, poor oral condition is often linked to negative labels such as poverty and low social status [23]. Interviews reported that children suffer from ridicule, prejudice, and stigmatization by their peers due to early loss of primary anterior teeth, which raises concerns among guardians about their mental health [24]. Lately, Doughty et al. [23] initially conceptualized oral health-related stigma, calling on practitioners to attend to stigma and to understand the adverse outcomes it produces for patients. However, to the best of our knowledge, few studies have quantitatively measured the degree of stigma in patients with oral diseases. Additionally, in China, it is traditionally believed that excessive interdental gaps are related to property loss. It is common to hear complaints from adult prosthodontic patients with anterior missing teeth or interdental gaps, who worry that this stereotype affects their work performance and income. Prosthodontic patients are plagued by a multitude of conditions such as unattractive tooth color, defective tooth morphology, missing teeth, and reduced vertical dimension, and are more prone to be negatively judged by others about their teeth and face compared to patients with other oral diseases. Unfortunately, however, research on the level of stigma among prosthodontic patients is also scarce. Moreover, it is unclear about the extent to which stigma affects OHRQoL for dental patients in general and prosthodontic patients in particular.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to assess the distribution of stigma among prosthodontic patients and to investigate the correlation between stigma and OHRQoL. The null hypothesis was that there would be no correlation between them in prosthodontic patients.

Methods

Subjects

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University (Approval no. WCHSIRB-D-2023-358). All patients enrolled in the study signed the informed consent.

Patients visiting Department of Prosthodontics, West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University from October 2023 to January 2024 were recruited consecutively. After the informed consent was obtained from the patients, the questionnaires were administered to them, which were comprised of demographic information, medical history information, SSCI, OHIP, and PIDAQ. Following the questionnaires received, the patients were selected for entry into this study according to the following inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria: (1) first visiting to Department of Prosthodontics of our hospital; (2) aged 18~60 years; (3) having ability to understand and fill in all the questions on questionnaires. Exclusion criteria: (1) having neurological, psychiatric or psychological disorders (epilepsy, depression, etc.); (2) having sleep disorders (insomnia, difficulty in falling asleep, etc.); (3) having headache or temporomandibular joint pain; (4) having pain in areas other than the face (neck pain, low back pain, etc.); (5) having history of head and neck trauma or surgery; (6) taking painkillers or central nervous system drugs (anti-anxiety drugs, etc.); (7) having cardiovascular diseases (hypertension, coronary artery disease, etc.); (8) having diseases of the endocrine system (diabetes mellitus, hyperthyroidism, etc.); (9) having autoimmune diseases (rheumatoid, lupus erythematosus, etc.); (10) having full edentulism; (11) having hereditary tooth defects or loss.

Questionnaires

Demographic and medical history information

Demographic information incorporated name, age, gender, and education level (secondary school and below, undergraduate, postgraduate and above). Dental and medical information included current and past history. Current history: (1) the area where the chief prosthodontic complaint of the present visit was located (anterior, chief complaint teeth included incisors/canines only; posterior, chief complaint teeth included premolars/molars only; both, chief complaint teeth included incisors/canines and premolars/molars). Past history: (1) adverse oral habits (grinding at night once or more per week, clenching once or more per day, holding or biting objects with teeth once or more per day, chewing gum once or more per day, drinking carbonated beverages once or more per day, etc.); (2) history of oral treatment (operative dentistry, endodontics, oral surgery, prosthodontics, orthodontics, etc.); and (3) history of systemic diseases.

Stigma

The SSCI-8 [17, 25] was used to evaluate stigma. The scale consisted of 8 items, divided into 2 dimensions (enacted stigma (ES) and internalized stigma (IS)). Each item was scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never; 2 = rarely; 3 = sometimes; 4 = often; 5 = always). The total score ranged from 8 to 40. A higher score indicated a greater level of stigma experienced by the patient.

OHRQoL

The overall OHRQoL was measured via the Chinese version of the 5-item OHIP (OHIP-5) [26]. The scale consisted of 5 items, each of which was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = never; 1 = rarely; 2 = sometimes; 3 = often; 4 = always). The total score of the scale ranged from 0 to 20. Higher score signified deteriorated overall OHRQoL for the patient.

The psychosocial dimension of the OHRQoL was assessed employing the Chinese version of the PIDAQ [27]. The scale contained 23 items, divided into four dimensions: Social Impact (SI), Psychological Impact (PI), Aesthetic Concern (AC), and Dental Self-Confidence (DSC). Each item was scored on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = never, 1 = rarely, 2 = sometimes, 3 = often, and 4 = always), with higher scores for SI, PI, and AC indicating a greater negative psychosocial impact of oral condition on the patient, and higher scores for DSC indicating greater self-confidence in the patient’s oral condition. When the total score was calculated, the six items of the DSC were reverse scored and then included in the total score. The total scale score ranged from 0 to 92. Higher score represented worse psychosocial dimension of OHRQoL for the patient.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated utilizing PASS software (version 21.0), with an alpha of 0.05 and a power of 0.9. The pilot research revealed a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.320 between the SSCI-total score and the OHIP-5 score, and a coefficient of 0.583 between SSCI-total and PIDAQ-total. The value of 0.320 was selected as the Pearson correlation coefficient assuming the alternative hypothesis, leading to a determination that the minimum sample size required was 116. This requirement was met by the inclusion of 139 patients in this study.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 27.0). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Cronbach’s α coefficients were calculated for SSCI and its subscales, OHIP, and PIDAQ and its subscales to analyze internal consistency reliability. For continuous data such as age and scores on each scale, Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to assess normality. It was found that all continuous data were non-normally distributed and therefore described using median and upper and lower quartiles. For categorical data such as age, gender, education, chief complaint area, adverse oral habits, and oral treatment history, number and frequency were used to describe.

Mann-Whitney U test and Kruskal-Wallis H test were used to compare SSCI, OHIP and PIDAQ scores between patients with different demographic and medical history characteristics. Spearman rank correlation coefficient was calculated for correlation analysis between SSCI, OHIP and PIDAQ scores. Multivariate linear regression analysis was utilized to explore the extent of effect of SSCI on OHIP and PIDAQ. The dependent variables were OHIP, PIDAQ-total, SI, PI, AC, and DSC. The main independent variables were SSCI-total, ES, and IS. The other adjusted covariables included age, gender, education, chief complaint area, adverse oral habits, and oral treatment history.

Results

Internal consistency reliability analysis

The Cronbach’s α coefficients for all scales exceeded 0.8 (Additional Table), indicating strong internal consistency reliability.

Descriptive statistics

A total of 139 patients were recruited in this study, which satisfied the requirement for a minimum sample size. The median age was 36 years. Slightly more females (58.3%) were recruited than males (41.7%), and the highest number of patients had undergraduate educational background (62.6%). Patients whose chief complaint area was the anterior teeth area (44.6%), who did not have adverse oral habits (75.5%), and who had a history of oral treatment (72.7%) constituted the majority of all patients. The median scores of SSCI, OHIP, and PIDAQ were 11, 4, and 40, respectively (Table 1). The most reported manifestation in the SSCI was item 1: I felt embarrassed about my illness, and the least was item 8: Some people acted as though it was my fault I have this illness (Fig. 1A). In OHIP, the most frequently reported symptom of discomfort was item 3: Uncomfortable about appearance, and the least was item 4: Difficulty doing usual jobs (Fig. 1B). In PIDAQ, the most observed performance was item 14: Wish teeth looked better, and the least was item 21: Teeth are attractive (Fig. 1C).

Multivariate analysis of OHRQoL

When OHIP was chosen as the dependent variable and SSCI-total as the main independent variable, a significant positive correlation between SSCI-total and OHIP was noted without any covariates adjusted (B=0.338, P<0.001); after adjusting for demographic variables, the correlation was still significant with the B-value decreasing to 0.335 (P<0.001); and after additional adjustment for the medical history variables, the correlation remained significant with the B-value decreasing to 0.312 (P<0.001). A similar trend in B-value was also noticed when SSCI-IS became the main independent variable. The B-value increased gradually with SSCI-ES adopted as the main independent variable (Table4).

A positive correlation between SSCI-total and PIDAQ-total was noticeable when the latter was used as the dependent variable and the former as the main independent variable, with the B-value firstly increasing and then decreasing after adjusting covariates (B=2.433, 2.439, 2.292, P<0.001). Regression analyses were performed with each PIDAQ subscale as the dependent variable. It was reported that SSCI-total, ES, and IS were positively correlated with PIDAQ-SI, PI, and AC before and after adjustment (P<0.001). No correlation among SSCI-total, ES, IS, and PIDAQ-DSC was observed in multiple models when PIDAQ-DSC was applied as the dependent variable (P>0.05, Table 5).

Discussion

This study shed light for the first time on the distribution of stigma in prosthodontic patients and revealed a negative correlation between stigma and OHRQoL, i.e. OHIP and PIDAQ scores. Consequently, the null hypothesis was rejected. Moreover, the results of the present study represent a contribution towards addressing the lack of previous studies on stigma and enhancing the understanding of stigma in a population with oral disease. It is reasonable to postulate that heavier stigma could be detrimental to OHRQoL in prosthodontic patients. This innovative finding illustrates the necessity of multifaceted strategies to reduce their stigma.

The main reason for including medical and dental history factors such as chief complaint area, adverse oral habits and oral treatment history as covariates in this study was their potential effect on OHRQoL. Regarding chief complaint area, a systematic review indicated that the impact of different sites of missing tooth on patients' OHRQoL varies [5]. For adverse oral habits, some habits, such as sleeping on hands, were associated with a negative impact on OHRQoL [28]. In terms of oral treatment history, it can be hypothesized that patients with a history of oral treatment would have high OHRQoL since a considerable number of studies have verified that treatments including implants, orthodontics, and others elevate OHRQoL [29, 30]. It is only after taking into account the role of these confounding factors that a clearer insight can be gained into the correlation between stigma and OHRQoL.

The mean SSCI-total score for prosthodontic patients was 12.47, which is analogous to the scores for patients with multiple sclerosis and Parkinson's disease (mean=12.23, 12.07) but lower than those for patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, stroke, epilepsy and chronic pain (mean=15.51, 14.75, 14.56, 17.21) [17, 31]. Similar to the systemic manifestations of neurological disorders, tooth defects and loss confined to the lower third of the face can also generate high levels of stigma. Clinical observations confirm this phenomenon, as prosthodontic patients – particularly those experiencing oral catastrophes – frequently report embarrassment and shame about their defective and missing teeth. Stigma represents a significant psychological burden in this population, requiring immediate intervention. Meanwhile, this study noticed that most demographic and medical history factors had no significant effect on SSCI-total, ES, and IS, which is in accordance with the results of Zhu et al [32]. However, an exception was the chief complaint area. Patients with tooth defects and loss in both anterior and posterior teeth area had higher SSCI-total, ES, and IS. It could be surmised that these patients are in terrible oral condition with few functioning teeth remaining, and are very susceptible to hostile comments and discrimination from others, creating a severe stigma.

The median OHIP score of 4.0 of the patients in this study is similar to that of patients with recurrent aphthous ulcers (median=4.0) [33]. This implies that, analogous to recurrent aphthous ulcers, tooth defects and loss are prolonged and repeatedly interfere with the regular eating and daily life of patients, resulting in overall OHRQoL to decline. Furthermore, worse OHRQoL was discovered in patients whose chief complaint area contained the posterior teeth area. However, more previous studies have illustrated poorer OHRQoL in patients with missing anterior teeth [8, 34]. The reason for this discrepancy could be the variation in the severity of the condition in the study population. One possible explanation could be that the most patients in this study with chief complaint area including the posterior teeth area had a very high number of defective and missing posterior teeth with a small number of existing functional teeth, which greatly affected masticatory function and even daily tasks, leading to a significant decrease in overall OHRQoL.

The mean PIDAQ-total score of the patients in this study was 42.35, which is comparable to that of patients with skeletal malocclusion (mean=44.27) [35]. In a similar manner to the latter, defective and missing teeth affect facial aesthetics and social activities, damaging the psychosocial dimension of OHRQoL. Additionally, PIDAQ is equally affected by the chief complaint area, which nevertheless has the exact opposite effect on PIDAQ as it does on OHIP. The social and psychological impact related to dental aesthetics was stronger in patients with tooth defects and loss concerning the anterior teeth area, along with a marked decline in dental self-confidence. This is in line with the results of Chen et al [36]. Tooth defects and loss in the anterior teeth area less affect the masticatory function [37], but more result in facial unattractiveness, which mostly impinges on the patient's psychosocial aspect of OHRQoL.

After adjusting for demographic and medical history confounders, higher levels of overall, enacted, and internalized stigma in prosthodontic patients independently predicted poorer OHRQoL. These stigma dimensions negatively affected both overall OHRQoL and specific domains including social functioning, psychological well-being, and aesthetic concerns related to dental aesthetics. Stigma makes patients afraid of dentists accusing them of their own neglect of oral health and avoid dental services [23], consequently leaving tooth defects and loss untreated over a long period of time, leading to more severe complications such as pulpitis and occlusal disorders, and further decreasing the overall OHRQoL. Self-discrimination against tooth defects and loss cause patients to deny their dental appearance, increase appearance anxiety, and reduce socialization, which aggravates the negative aesthetic concern, psychological impact and social impact. In similar fashion to the present work, many prior studies have uncovered connections between poorer HRQoL and heavier stigma, including enacted stigma [38] and internalized stigma [21]. Nevertheless, some discrepancies in definition exist between OHRQoL and HRQoL given that the former emphasizes the oral status whereas the latter involves the systemic condition. Additional studies will be necessary so that more adequate evidence can be provided to determine the correlation between stigma and OHRQoL.

This study did not detect a statistically significant effect of overall, enacted, and internalized stigma on dental self-confidence with control for covariates. This is not consistent with prior literature. A review summarized that stigma in patients with schizophrenia could lower their self-confidence and deteriorate their social functioning [39]. Oral health-related stigma in childhood and elderhood would lead to diminished self-esteem and impaired social functioning [40, 41]. Theoretically the presence of stigma also seems to weaken the prosthodontic patients' level of confidence in their oral condition, but the expected findings were not obtained in this study. A plausible explanation is that the present study involved prosthodontic adult patients whose dental self-confidence, as distinct from general self-confidence, may be more influenced by other unexplored psychological factors, which require further investigation.

The findings suggest that restoring OHRQoL in prosthodontic patients requires attention to both clinical factors like prosthesis design and psychological factors such as stigma. Offering reassurance to prosthodontic patients during clinical visits and referring them for psychological counseling when needed may alleviate oral disease-related shame and mitigate internalized stigma. Additionally, dentists could determine appropriate treatment plans based on the patient's degree of stigma. For example, for patients with tooth loss experiencing severe stigma, removable partial dentures may be preferable to implants as they require less treatment time and can decrease stigma more rapidly. Managing high levels of stigma in prosthodontic patients through targeted interventions is probably another new approach to enhance OHRQoL.

This study has some limitations. First, the current study was limited to patients in a single center from western China, whose distinct cultural background may lead to differences in stigma compared with other populations. This regional specificity could constrain the broader applicability of our findings. Further multicenter studies with more diverse populations are desired. Second, this is a cross-sectional study, where the causal relationship between stigma and OHRQoL has not yet been elucidated, thus further prospective studies are warranted. An attempt could be made to observe changes in OHRQoL following the intervention to alleviate stigma. Third, the OHIP-5 was selected due to its brevity. This shortened version reduces both the number of items and completion time, which improves response rates for complete questionnaires. Additionally, its reliability and validity prove comparable to those of the OHIP-14 [42]. However, its capacity to capture certain dimensions of overall OHRQoL remains limited. Unlike OHIP-14, OHIP-5 fails to provide a comprehensive assessment of patients' overall OHRQoL. This may result in a statistically significant yet weak Spearman rank correlation between OHIP-5 and SSCI (rs = 0.350), which was substantially lower than the coefficient between PIDAQ and SSCI (rs= 0.625). Future studies employing OHIP-14 might yield stronger correlations between overall OHRQoL measures and SSCI. Fourth, the number of missing teeth, the presence or absence of old dentures, and their duration of use, along with prosthesis type (fixed, removable or implant) and prosthetic material (metal, ceramics or resin), may represent confounding factors not addressed in this study. Patients with extensive tooth loss experience compromised masticatory function and may report anxiety about attracting unwanted attention during eating. Those wearing dentures for a short duration may express concern about others noticing their dental prostheses and forming negative judgments. Patients with anterior tooth loss who receive resin-based removable partial dentures may express greater dissatisfaction with the aesthetics of their prostheses and report more frequent self-discrimination than those restored with implant-supported all-ceramic crowns. These factors likely influence stigma and their correlation with stigma warrants further investigation. Furthermore, previous research demonstrated associations between tooth loss quantity and OHRQoL [43], as well as between prosthesis type and OHRQoL [9]. Future studies should consider these confounders more comprehensively.

Conclusions

Prosthodontic patients frequently experience existence of significant stigma. Their OHRQoL (OHIP and PIDAQ) is also poor, which is influenced by factors such as chief complaint area. After accounting for the role of demographic and clinical factors, stigma was negatively correlated with OHRQoL in prosthodontic patients. Heavier stigma may impair OHRQoL at the overall and psychosocial dimensions and exacerbate the negative biopsychosocial impact of oral condition. Interventions to alleviate the stigma in such patients are recommended to improve OHRQoL.
