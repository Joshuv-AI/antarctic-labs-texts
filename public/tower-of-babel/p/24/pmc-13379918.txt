Coronary Artery Aneurysms and Dilation in Children With RASopathies
Authors: Karina Javalkar, Amy E. Roberts, Annette Baker, Jesse Esch, Luis G. Quinonez, Elizabeth Pace, Jane W. Newburger, Rebecca Beroukhim, Edward Smith, Sarah D. deFerranti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRASopathies are disorders caused by variants in a gene in the RAS-mitogen-activated protein kinase (RAS-MAPK) pathway that have an association with cardiovascular anomalies, most commonly pulmonary stenosis and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. There are some reported cases of coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs) in patients with RASopathies, but there is limited understanding of CAAs in this population, particularly in younger patients and in those with varying genotypes.Case SummaryWe describe 3 pediatric cases of patients with RASopathy developing CAA or ectasia: a 3-year-old with CBL-related RASopathy with giant CAAs, one of which required surgical intervention with a coronary artery bypass graft; an 8-year-old with PTPN11-related Noonan syndrome with giant CAAs; and an 11-year-old with a clinical diagnosis of Noonan syndrome with coronary ectasia.DiscussionCAAs may be related to underlying RASopathies. Further exploration of the association and pathophysiology of CAAs in RASopathy is needed to guide screening, prevention, and management.

Kawasaki disease (KD) is the most common cause of coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs) in children.1,2 However, coronary aneurysms may also be seen in other vasculopathies or genetic syndromes and should be further explored. RASopathies are a family of disorders caused by variants in a gene in the RAS-mitogen-activated protein kinase (RAS-MAPK) pathway that have overlapping phenotypic features, including an association with cardiovascular anomalies.3,4 For example, Noonan syndrome (NS) is a complex RASopathy that is clinically and genetically heterogeneous, characterized by distinctive facial features, short stature, chest deformity, and congenital heart disease such as pulmonary stenosis. It is also associated with congenital or, less commonly, later onset hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, which necessitates lifelong cardiology follow-up and surveillance. There are case reports of CAAs in patients with RASopathies, but there is limited understanding of the relationship of RASopathies to these aneurysms, particularly in younger patients. We describe a series of 3 pediatric cases evaluated at our institution in which patients with a RASopathy developed coronary artery dilation or significant aneurysms.Take-Home Messages•Coronary artery aneurysms may be related to underlying RASopathies, particularly in the absence of a history of Kawasaki disease.•In these patients who develop aneurysms, concomitant moyamoya disease may also be considered.•Future research should consider the mechanisms of aneurysm formation in RASopathies, and dedicated surveillance for coronary artery aneurysms with echocardiography may be needed in these populations.

Case Presentations

Case 1

A surveillance echocardiogram for a 3-year-old with CBL-related RASopathy (heterozygous c.1228-2A>G, IVS8-2A>G, de novo pathogenic variant), moyamoya disease with a potentially related RNF213 heterozygous variant of uncertain significance (c.10730C>T, p.(Ser3577Phe), maternally inherited), and no prior cardiac anomalies incidentally showed a mediastinal fluid-filled sac (Figure 1). Cardiac computed tomography (CT) was obtained and showed a calcified left anterior descending (LAD) CAA (24 × 21 × 31 mm) (Figure 2). The patient was subsequently sent for cardiac catheterization for coronary angiography, which demonstrated additional right-sided CAAs (Figure 3). Notably, angiography could not sufficiently opacify the giant LAD aneurysm to delineate the distal LAD. Given the challenges in determining ischemic symptoms in a patient of this age, a dobutamine stress cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) was performed. There was a very small area of myocardial delayed enhancement in the anterior septum (suggestive of fibrosis and possible prior ischemia) without inducible ischemia, and a patent distal LAD. No etiology of the CAA was determined. The patient had had a febrile illness many months prior, but did not meet KD clinical criteria. Management akin to aneurysms in KD was pursued with anticoagulation, antiplatelet, and β-blocker therapy. Surgical intervention was not indicated at that time in the absence of inducible ischemia or coronary stenosis. Initial follow-up with serial electrocardiograms, Holter monitors, and echocardiograms was reassuring.

One year after CAA diagnosis, the patient developed symptoms of decreased exercise capacity with diaphoresis and pallor with activity. Ventricular function at rest remained normal. A repeat stress CMR demonstrated stable aneurysms but inducible ischemia with delayed myocardial enhancement (Figure 4) and regional wall motion abnormalities in the LAD territory (Video 1). Cardiac CT revealed a thin but patent distal LAD (Figure 5). The right coronary artery (RCA) aneurysms remained stable. A repeat cardiac catheterization demonstrated new severe stenosis at the entrance to the LAD aneurysm. With this constellation of symptoms and catheterization findings, the patient was referred for coronary artery bypass graft of the left internal mammary artery to distal LAD. Notably, no intervention was undertaken for the RCA aneurysms, given robust distal perfusion to the RCA territory. Of note, the patient's moyamoya disease was an important consideration during both evaluation and ultimate surgical management of the CAAs. The patient had a history of stroke and had undergone cerebral revascularization surgery previously. Because of the increased risk of stroke with moyamoya perianesthesia, a careful collaborative approach with the neurosurgical team was an important aspect of decision-making. Fortunately, neuroprotective measures with a particular focus on blood pressure goals helped to avoid cerebrovascular complications during sedated studies and through cardiopulmonary bypass.5

After the bypass graft, exercise-induced symptoms resolved. The patient continues to be managed with anticoagulation, antiplatelet, and β-blocker therapy in the setting of his bypass graft and persistent RCA aneurysms. Repeat dobutamine stress CMR 6 months after bypass graft demonstrated no inducible ischemia and no new evidence of myocardial delayed enhancement.

Case 2

An 8-year-old with mild valvar pulmonary stenosis diagnosed at 1 month of age with no intervention needed and PTPN11-related NS (heterozygous c.188A>G, p.(Tyr63Cys) pathogenic variant presented to his cardiologist at age 7 years for evaluation of palpitations and inability to keep up with peers during exercise. Echocardiogram at that time demonstrated a saccular aneurysm of the proximal RCA, as well as dilated left main coronary artery (Figure 6). Cardiac catheterization (Figure 7) and CMR demonstrated a right dominant coronary system with 3 CAAs of the RCA: a first aneurysm (9 × 9 mm), followed by a stenotic segment, a second aneurysm (5 × 8 mm), and a third aneurysm (5 × 6 mm) at the takeoff of the posterior descending artery. There was also severe stenosis and tortuosity of the LAD, with the distal LAD territory reconstituted and supplied by collaterals from the RCA. There was no significant myocardial delayed enhancement. He has now been managed medically for approximately 14 years and remains asymptomatic. Surveillance with intermittent exercise stress testing and ambulatory rhythm monitoring has been reassuring.

Case 3

An 11-year-old with moyamoya disease status post-revascularization surgery and a clinical diagnosis of NS underwent a screening echocardiogram. The echocardiogram identified mild supravalvar pulmonary stenosis as well as increased ventricular septal thickness. He was also noted to have diffuse coronary ectasia and a possible aneurysm of the distal LAD. He had no cardiovascular symptoms. He has 2 siblings also with suspected NS, both with developmental delays, short stature, and facial features of NS, and one with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy requiring myectomy. Exome sequencing detected NF1 homozygous c.6098T>A p.(Met2033Lys) variant of uncertain significance, thought to be likely benign as none of the siblings and neither parent has cutaneous findings of neurofibromatosis type 1.

CMR was performed to measure ventricular mass and to image the coronary arteries. This demonstrated mild main pulmonary artery narrowing and asymmetric left ventricular hypertrophy consistent with the echocardiogram findings. Coronary imaging showed mild fusiform dilation of the left main coronary artery (maximum dimension 4.7 mm) and mild dilation of the proximal circumflex coronary artery (maximum dimension 3.1 mm). There was no obvious myocardial delayed enhancement. A cardiac CT further demonstrated mild diffuse dilation of the left main coronary artery (4.5 × 4.3 mm), LAD coronary artery (3.9 × 3.5 mm), and proximal RCA (4.1 × 2.7 mm) (Figure 8). No discrete aneurysms were seen. He was not started on antithrombotic medication or β-blocker. Discussion

We describe a series of 3 pediatric patients at our institution with a RASopathy found to have CAAs, 2 of whom developed severe coronary stenosis. Although these 2 had a history of febrile illnesses, such a history is common in pediatrics, and neither had a definitive diagnosis of KD. Two of the patients also have a history of moyamoya disease, which is itself rare and is not known to be associated with CAAs (although it has been reported to coronary artery stenoses).6 One of these 2 had a potentially related RNF213 heterozygous c.10730C>T, p.Ser3577Phe maternally inherited variant of uncertain significance. Heterozygous variants in RNF213 have been reported in association with susceptibility to moyamoya disease.7

RASopathies are multisystem genetic disorders affecting the RAS-MAPK signal transduction pathway. In NS, the most commonly implicated gene is PTPN11, causing about half of cases, though variants in more than a dozen genes in the pathway can cause NS and related disorders. RASopathies are characterized by distinctive facial features, developmental delay, short stature, characteristic skin findings, solid tumor and hematologic cancer predisposition, and renal and lymphatic malformations. There may be vasculature complications, including cerebrovascular anomalies such as arteriovenous malformations and moyamoya disease. NS is notably the second most common syndrome associated with cardiovascular anomalies, including pulmonary stenosis, atrial septal defect, aortic stenosis, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, which is most often congenital but can develop later in life. Because of these associations, patients with RASopathies are screened for congenital heart disease and undergo periodic echocardiographic surveillance.3,4,8

Although the RASopathies have a known association with heart disease, an association with CAAs is not well codified. Coronary aneurysms in pediatric patients with RASopathies have been reported in case reports in the literature (Table 1) and predominantly in patients who are adults and who have associated structural cardiac disease such as pulmonary stenosis or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26 The current case series includes patients presenting younger than those previously reported and with a variety of underlying genetic etiologies, including a patient with CBL-related RASopathy, which has not previously been reported (Table 1). The patients in the current series also notably did not have typical structural cardiac disease of RASopathy in addition to the coronary artery findings. In addition, for the cases that offered genetic testing, this was most often single gene or RAS-MAPK pathway gene panel testing and would not have ruled out a second genetic contributor to CAA (eg, It is unknown to what extent CAAs in RASopathies share features with KD coronary aneurysms. The presence of coronary stenoses in the 2 cases in this series could suggest that CAAs in NS have the potential to evolve in malignant fashion similar to what is seen with KD. Alternatively, one could hypothesize that RASopathy may predispose to a vulnerability for KD-associated CAAs. The possibility of missed or incomplete Kawasaki in these patients must also be recognized. Although there is no evidence to support their use in this population, Kawasaki guidelines may provide a starting point for managing CAAs in the RASopathy population.2 In all 3 cases, CAAs were incidentally identified during echocardiograms obtained for other indications. Echocardiography remains the mainstay of screening for CAAs in KD and could reasonably be used to identify aneurysms in patients with NS using appropriate views. Once identified, advanced testing to identify ischemia can guide management.27 Further studies exploring the risk of CAAs in RASopathies may clarify the extent to which careful coronary surveillance is indicated.

Our series includes 2 patients with moyamoya disease. Moyamoya disease is uncommonly associated with RASopathy.4 Although CAAs have not been associated with moyamoya disease, this is an additional potential association that should be further explored. The confluence of rare findings in these patients is striking and remains unresolved. The presence of moyamoya additionally raises the risks for interventions to be considered, including mitigating an increased risk of stroke with all anesthesia exposures for testing, as well as cardiopulmonary bypass for surgical interventions.

Conclusions

We present a series of 3 patients with RASopathies and no other structural heart disease, who developed coronary artery ectasia or aneurysms. All 3 developed giant CAAs at a young age, without a clear history of KD. Although single case reports in the literature have featured RASopathies and coronary aneurysms, this series presents multiple cases from a single center. Further exploration of the association and pathophysiology of CAAs in RASopathy is needed to guide screening, prevention, and management.
