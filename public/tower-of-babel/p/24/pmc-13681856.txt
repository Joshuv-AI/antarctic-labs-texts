The effects of virtual reality on anxiety and depression in cancer patients: a systematic review with meta-analysis
Authors: Lingbei Chen, Xiaochen Ma, Feng Ding, Zhaohui Guo, Xing Wang, Min Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnxiety and depression are prevalent yet undertreated in cancer patients, impairing quality of life, treatment adherence, and outcomes. Virtual reality, with its immersive and interactive features, offers a promising non-pharmacological intervention. However, a rigorous synthesis of randomized evidence is needed to establish its efficacy and identify key moderators of effectiveness, guiding targeted clinical implementation.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted across seven electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, CNKI, WanFang, VIP) from inception to December 10, 2025. Eligible studies were RCTs involving cancer patients, comparing a VR intervention targeting emotional distress to a non-VR control. Study selection, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool were performed independently by reviewers. Primary outcomes were anxiety and depression. Data were synthesized using random-effects meta-analysis in R (meta package). Pooled effects were estimated as Standardized Mean Differences with 95% Confidence Intervals. Heterogeneity was assessed via I² and τ² statistics, and pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted for the proposed moderators. Sensitivity analyses and assessment for small-study effects were performed.ResultsEighteen RCTs (total participants: N = 1303; VR group: n = 658, Control: n = 645) met the inclusion criteria. The meta-analysis demonstrated statistically significant beneficial effects of VR interventions on anxiety (17 RCTs, n = 1287; SMD = -1.16, 95% CI: -1.60 to -0.71; P < 0.001) and depression (7 RCTs, n = 543; SMD = -1.28, 95% CI: -2.52 to -0.04; P = 0.04), with both outcomes favoring the VR groups. Substantial heterogeneity was observed. Risk of bias assessment indicated that most studies had some concerns regarding bias, with three studies rated low risk, twelve with some concerns, and three high risk. Subgroup analyses suggested variations in effect sizes across potential effect modifiers, though findings were exploratory.ConclusionsThis review suggests that VR interventions may alleviate anxiety and depression in cancer patients, with potentially clinically meaningful effects. The findings advance the field by shifting focus from overall efficacy toward contextual factors influencing outcomes, providing insights for personalized application. However, moderate-certainty evidence and substantial heterogeneity underscore the need for future large-scale, rigorous RCTs that prioritize standardized reporting, mechanistic exploration, and optimize future clinical implementation.Trial registrationPROSPERO (Registration No. CRD420251089141).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28655-9.Keywords: Virtual reality technology, Neoplasms, Cancer, Anxiety, Depression, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Background

Rationale

Cancer is a global public health issue that not only threatens individual lives but also poses immeasurable challenges and impacts on national economies and the world [1, 2]. The 4th edition of The Cancer Atlas, published by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), indicates that in 2022, an estimated 18.7 million new cancer cases and 9.7 million cancer deaths occurred globally [3]. It is projected that by 2050, global cancer cases will reach 35.3 million, with an estimated 18.5 million deaths, representing an 89.7% increase from the 9.7 million estimated in 2022 [4]. These mortality statistics underscore cancer as a severe threat to human life and health, vividly reflecting its high lethality. This suggests that the disease threat and treatment pressures stemming from high mortality are significant external triggers for anxiety and depression in cancer patients. Currently, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), one of the most authoritative global guidelines for oncology clinical practice, notes that all patients experience psychological distress related to cancer, regardless of disease stage or treatment phase [5]. Concurrently, guidelines from the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) recommend regularly screening all cancer patients for symptoms of depression and anxiety throughout the care continuum [6]. This confirms that the psychological state of cancer patients is a dimension that must be seriously addressed during treatment.

Furthermore, global epidemiological data show that anxiety and depression are highly prevalent among cancer patients across different tumor types worldwide [7] (e.g., the prevalence of depression and anxiety among breast cancer patients in the Eastern Mediterranean region is as high as 49–51% and 56%, respectively, while the prevalence of depression among digestive system cancer patients in Southeast Asia can reach 66%). Additionally, a cross-sectional study reported that 29.8% of included cancer patients reported moderate-to-severe depression, and 47.9% reported moderate-to-severe anxiety [8]. Another study covering nearly 59,000 cancer patients revealed that the prevalence of anxiety and depression among cancer patients increased significantly within one year after diagnosis, rising from approximately 11% to 24.6% and 18.3%, respectively [9]. The clinical consequences accompanying this high prevalence are equally concerning. Meta-analytic evidence has demonstrated that depression and anxiety are associated with a 21% increased risk of cancer-specific mortality and a 24% increased risk of all-cause mortality [10]. Concurrently, psychological distress contributes to sadness, fatigue, loss of motivation, and reduced treatment adherence [11–14]. At the same time, a meta-analysis of 65 studies further confirmed that depression increases the risk of mortality in cancer patients by 23–83% [15]. These detrimental effects underscore the urgent need to integrate psychological interventions into routine oncology care, shifting the clinical paradigm from epidemiological documentation toward proactive symptom management. As repeatedly emphasized in the ASCO guidelines, “depression and anxiety are common in patients with cancer and occur across the survivorship trajectory” [16]. Therefore, alleviating anxiety and depression in cancer patients has become an urgent priority. To mitigate the prevailing psychological burden commonly experienced by this population [17], it is essential to identify effective and accessible intervention approaches.

Current standard interventions for managing anxiety and depression in cancer patients primarily include pharmacological agents [18] and structured psychotherapies [19]. Although these modalities have demonstrated efficacy to varying degrees, they face substantial implementation barriers in oncology settings. Pharmacological treatments, for instance, are constrained by two core limitations. First, potential drug–drug interactions with antineoplastic agents may alter drug efficacy, increase toxicity, and ultimately lead to poorer medical outcomes and reduced quality of life [20]. Second, adverse effects of antidepressants are often difficult to distinguish from treatment-related side effects or disease symptoms, thereby complicating clinical decision-making [21]. Similarly, while psychotherapeutic approaches are effective, they are frequently limited by the scarcity of trained mental health professionals [22], high treatment costs [23], and suboptimal adherence attributable to the additional burden that intervention time commitments impose on patients’ daily lives [24]. In this context, VR emerges as a highly promising adjunctive tool with several distinctive advantages. VR immerses users in computer-generated three-dimensional environments [25]. This strong sense of “presence” can safely transfer patients’ psychological representations into virtual space [26]. It alleviates anxiety and depression through multiple synergistic mechanisms: distraction (occupying the cognitive resources of cancer patients during unpleasant procedures like chemotherapy infusions to block attention to negative stimuli) [27]; cognitive reappraisal and emotion regulation within a safe and controllable context (providing a safe environment for cancer patients to encounter and practice coping with stimuli that trigger anxiety and depression, a mechanism consistent with Gross’s model of emotion regulation) [28]; and inducing favorable regulation of the autonomic nervous system, thereby forming a positive mind-body interaction cycle [29].These advantages directly address the core limitations of traditional interventions—circumventing the issues of drug interactions and side effects, while overcoming the bottlenecks of personnel shortages and poor patient adherence. Moreover, compared with existing conventional approaches, VR offers unique value in tackling specific implementation barriers within the cancer population: for patients with limited mobility due to physical weakness, treatment constraints, or infection risks, VR can deliver high-quality intervention experiences that transcend physical space limitations [30]; and the safe, controllable virtual environment it creates is particularly suitable for exposure and desensitization to treatment-related scenarios (e.g., radiotherapy or infusion), which may be unmatched by traditional methods [31]. A recent network meta-analysis of 23,154 participants further confirmed that, compared with non-intervention controls, virtual reality therapy significantly alleviates psychological distress, depression, and anxiety [32], positioning VR as a promising therapeutic option for cancer patients.

Despite its promising prospects, evidence in this field still has limitations. First, there is a lack of exploration regarding VR intervention parameters such as device type (immersive/interactive) and dosage (frequency). Second, most studies have small sample sizes and short follow-up periods; it remains unclear whether the observed positive effects can transcend the “novelty effect” and translate into long-term clinical benefits. Furthermore, research has predominantly focused on specific cancer types like breast cancer and often treats patients as a homogeneous group. There has been insufficient in-depth investigation into the moderating effects of key patient characteristics—such as cancer type, age, and treatment stage—on the efficacy and applicability of VR interventions [33–35].

Objectives

Therefore, following preliminary exploration into the question of “Is VR potentially effective?” more nuanced clinical questions urgently require answers: Are the benefits of VR interventions clearly superior to other active interventions? Do the effects differ across various cancer types and patient characteristics? Which technical approach (type, dosage) performs best in which clinical scenario? Current literature provides insufficient exploration of these key issues, hindering the progression of VR technology towards precision and high efficiency in clinical practice.

Based on this, the present study aims, through a systematic review and meta-analysis, to move beyond the preliminary assessment of “overall effectiveness”. It seeks to integrate existing evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCT) to scientifically address the following clinically instructive questions: (1) What is the incremental benefit of VR interventions compared to usual care or other interventions in alleviating anxiety and depression in cancer patients; and (2) How patient characteristics (e.g., cancer type [36], age [37]) and intervention parameters (e.g., VR type and session frequency [38], timing of intervention [39]) moderate the intervention effects. The findings of this study will provide critical evidence and direction for future high-quality research with clear mechanisms, standardized protocols, and a focus on personalized application, as well as for the development of clinical practice guidelines.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

This study was conducted according to the PRISMA reporting guidelines for systematic reviews [40]. The review focused on anxiety and depression outcomes among cancer patients. Although the conceptual scope was informed by the ICD−11 diagnostic categories of “Anxiety” (Code: MB24.3) and “Depressed mood” (Code: MB24.5) [41, 42], study eligibility was determined based on anxiety and depression outcomes assessed using standardized instruments rather than ICD-11 diagnostic codes (e.g., validated anxiety or depression scales). The PICOS framework (Table 1) was employed to define the eligibility criteria.

Information sources

Two reviewers independently searched Embase, Web of Science, PubMed, The Cochrane Library, WanFang Database, VIP, and CNKI from database inception to December 10, 2025. Clinical trial registries, including ClinicalTrials.gov, were also searched to identify unpublished or ongoing studies, and reference lists of included studies were screened for additional eligible records. Authors of primary studies were contacted when necessary to obtain missing data.

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed and reported in strict accordance with the PRISMA-S guidelines [43]. Search queries were constructed based on the PICOS framework, with core terms including “virtual reality”, “cancer”, “depression”, “anxiety”, and their respective synonyms and controlled vocabulary. The complete search strategies for each database are provided in Appendix 1 (Supplementary Material). No other search filters were applied. The search strategy was not externally peer-reviewed.

Selection process

Records retrieved from the searches were imported into EndNote X9 for deduplication. Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and subsequently the full texts of potentially eligible records. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion to reach consensus or, when necessary, by consulting a third reviewer. Studies published in Chinese or English were considered, and no automation tools were used during screening.

Data collection

Two researchers independently extracted data from included studies using a pre-designed form. Extracted information included study characteristics, participant characteristics, intervention details, and outcome measures. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. When data were missing or unclear, the corresponding authors were contacted for additional information. Only reported data were used for synthesis, without data imputation.

Data items

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were anxiety and depression among cancer patients, measured using validated standardized scales. Data were extracted at baseline and immediately after intervention. When multiple scales were reported, all eligible validated measures were extracted, with total scores prioritized over subscale scores. No changes were made to the prespecified outcomes, and only available reported data were included in the synthesis.

Other variables

Additional variables were categorized into study, participant, and intervention characteristics. Study characteristics included publication year, country, study design, and sample size. Participant characteristics included age, sex, cancer type, and treatment stage. Intervention characteristics included VR modality (immersive or interactive VR), dosage parameters (single or multiple sessions), and control type. All variables were extracted as reported in the original studies.

Study records

EndNote X9 was used to manage search results, remove duplicates, and facilitate the screening process. Microsoft Excel 2019 was used to create the data extraction form. All process documentation—including search screenshots, deduplication records, records of consensus discussions, and full-text articles—were archived according to the respective research stage to ensure full reproducibility and verifiability of the entire review process.

Effect measures

The outcome measures were continuous variables (anxiety and depression scores from standardized scales). The primary effect measure was the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD), with 95% confidence intervals (CI) reported to indicate statistical precision.

Synthesis methods

Eligibility for synthesis

Studies were grouped for synthesis according to prespecified PICOS elements, outcomes, and intervention characteristics. Quantitative synthesis was performed only when studies showed sufficient clinical and methodological homogeneity. The primary analysis included studies reporting anxiety or depression outcomes measured by validated scales at the immediate post-intervention assessment. Subgroup analyses were conducted only when at least two independent studies were available; otherwise, findings were summarized descriptively [44]. Studies with substantial differences in intervention components or control conditions were excluded from quantitative synthesis and included in narrative synthesis.

Preparing for synthesis

Standardized preparatory procedures were applied to the included continuous outcome data to ensure their suitability for subsequent synthesis. The specific handling was as follows: First, data transformation was conducted where necessary; if a study reported only statistics such as standard error (SE) or interquartile ranges, these were converted to standard deviation (SD) using established formulas to standardize data format for effect size calculation. Second, data consistency was calibrated by verifying the scoring direction of all assessment scales; results from inversely scored scales were uniformly converted to align with a positive scoring direction.

Tabulation and graphical methods

Study characteristics and synthesis results were presented using standardized tables and graphical displays. Figures were generated using R software and the meta package. Forest plots presented pooled and subgroup SMDs with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and prediction intervals (PI). Funnel plots combined with Egger’s test were used to assess small-study effects. Heterogeneity was evaluated using I², τ², τ, and Q value. The creation of all charts and tables adhered to stratification principles (e.g., by intervention timing, cancer type), considered sample sizes, highlighted key studies, and strictly conformed to systematic review reporting standards.

Statistical synthesis methods

Meta-analysis was conducted using R software (version 5.4.2) and the meta package [45]. The pooled effect size was expressed as the standardized mean difference (SMD) with 95% CI, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. A random-effects model was applied, and the Knapp–Hartung adjustment [46] was used for pooled effect estimation. Between-study variance was estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method. Heterogeneity was assessed using I² and τ², and 95% PI was calculated to evaluate potential effect variation across future studies.Small-study effects were assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test. The trim-and-fill method was applied when Egger’s test indicated significant asymmetry (P < 0.05). This assessment was not performed when fewer than 10 studies were included [47]. All analyses and graphical outputs were performed using R software and the meta package [45].

Methods To Explore Heterogeneity.

Subgroup analysis was conducted to explore potential sources of between-study heterogeneity and to clarify effect modifiers and their direction of influence. The execution and interpretation of subgroup analyses adhered to methodological recommendations to avoid overinterpretation [48]. We prioritized investigating the prespecified moderating effects of cancer type, VR intervention timing, VR modality, and patient age on the intervention effect. It was anticipated that differences in psychological needs across treatment stages and variations in the intervention experience afforded by different VR technologies might contribute to heterogeneity in effect sizes.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analysis was performed using the leave-one-out method. Each study was sequentially removed and the meta-analysis was repeated. Changes in pooled effects (SMD and 95% CI) and heterogeneity statistics (I² and τ²) were assessed to evaluate the robustness of the findings.

Reporting bias assessment.

The risk of bias of the included randomized controlled trials was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [49], the current international standard for assessing the methodological quality of RCTs [50]. Five domains were evaluated: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result. Each domain and the overall risk of bias were rated as “low risk”, “some concerns”, or “high risk”. Two researchers independently performed the assessment, with disagreements resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Certainty assessment

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework [51, 52]. The quality of evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low based on five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Two researchers independently performed the assessment, with disagreements resolved through discussion or consultation with a third researcher.

Explanation of deviations from the protocol

Two amendments were made to the original protocol registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251089141) before data synthesis, and the updated protocol was synchronized with the PROSPERO record. First, the planned analysis software was changed from STATA 17.0 to R software (version 5.4.2) with the meta package to perform all statistical analyses, providing greater flexibility for statistical analysis and graphical presentation. Second, the originally specified outcome of “negative emotion” was refined into two separate outcomes, anxiety and depression, to improve the precision and clinical interpretability of the findings.These amendments were implemented before data synthesis to enhance methodological rigor and ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Results

Study selection

Flow of Studies

A total of 2306 records were retrieved. After removing duplicates using EndNote X9 software, 1673 records remained. Following a review of titles, abstracts, and full texts, supplemented by manual screening of reference lists, 18 studies [53–70] were ultimately included for analysis. Among these, 12 were published in English [53–63, 70] and 6 in Chinese [64–69]. The study selection process is detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of literature screening

Excluded studies

During the final stage of study selection, 18 studies were included. An additional 69 studies initially meeting some inclusion criteria were excluded after full-text review. The primary reasons for exclusion were as follows: 21 studies reported only qualitative descriptions without providing quantitative data (e.g., anxiety/depression scores), and relevant raw data could not be obtained through available channels; 19 studies were not RCTs; 15 studies, while focusing on the impact of VR on emotions in cancer patients, employed combined interventions (VR plus other therapies) where the specific VR effects could not be isolated; 7 studies were non-empirical in design; 4 studies reported outcomes not aligned with anxiety or depression; and 3 studies targeted cancer survivors or post-rehabilitation populations, which did not meet the inclusion criteria for cancer patients during active treatment or diagnosis.

Study characteristics

The 18 included studies [53–70] involved a total of 1303 participants, with 658 allocated to the intervention groups and 645 to the control groups. All participants were cancer patients. The publication years of the included studies spanned from 2019 to 2025. In all included studies, the intervention groups received VR-based interventions, while the control groups received conventional treatment (a basic intervention protocol without VR). The VR interventions were delivered in either single-session or multiple-session formats, and employed interactive or immersive modalities. The detailed characteristics of the included studies are presented in Appendix 2 (Supplementary Material).

Risk of bias in studies

The internal risk of bias of the included randomized controlled trials was methodologically assessed using the RoB 2.0 tool, as shown in Fig. 2. The evaluation revealed that among all included studies, three were rated as “low risk”, twelve raised “some concerns”, and three were rated as “high risk”. Most studies demonstrated a low risk of bias in the randomization process (Domain 1) and the completeness of outcome data (Domain 3). However, some studies presented “some concerns” or “high risk” in the domains of deviations from intended interventions (Domain 2) and measurement of the outcome (Domain 4), primarily related to factors such as inadequate allocation concealment and lack of blinding in outcome assessment. The risk regarding selective reporting of results (Domain 5) varied across studies.

Risk of bias assessment for included studies using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2.0)

Results of individual studies

For all included trials, this study extracted and summarized complete data on continuous outcomes related to the impact of VR interventions on anxiety and depression in cancer patients. For continuous indicators such as anxiety and depression scale scores, the mean, SD, and sample size for each group were collected. The effect size was uniformly calculated using the SMD, accompanied by a 95% CI to reflect statistical precision. The baseline data and effect size parameters for different emotional outcomes from each study are detailed in Appendix 3.

Results of syntheses

Characteristics of contributing studies

A total of 18 RCTs involving 1303 cancer patients (658 in intervention groups, 645 in control groups) were included in this meta-analysis. Of these, 17 studies reported anxiety outcomes and 7 reported depression outcomes. The included studies were published between 2019 and 2025. The patient populations encompassed breast cancer (6 studies), lung cancer (2 studies), head and neck cancer (1 study), acute leukemia (1 study), liver cancer (1 study), thyroid cancer (1 study), with 6 studies not specifying the cancer type. All interventions utilized VR equipment, comprising 8 studies using interactive VR and 10 studies using immersive VR. Single-session duration ranged from 2.7 to 60 min, and the total number of sessions ranged from 1 to 24. Control groups all received usual care without VR. Both anxiety and depression outcomes were assessed using validated self-report scales. Anxiety was measured using the STAI (5 studies), SAI (3 studies), SAS (3 studies), HADS (2 studies), CSAS (2 studies), CSA (1 study), and GAD-7 (1 study). Depression was measured using the SDS (3 studies), HADS (2 studies), CES-D (1 study), and BDI (1 study). All measurements were conducted pre- and post-intervention. Assessment based on the Cochrane RoB 2 tool indicated a low risk of bias concerning the randomization process, data completeness, and selective reporting. However, approximately 70% of studies were rated at high risk of bias in the measurement of the outcome domain due to the use of self-reported measures and lack of assessor blinding. Owing to the nature of the intervention, most studies also carried some risk of bias regarding adherence to the intended intervention.

Results of statistical syntheses

For the anxiety outcome, 17 studies (total sample size n = 1287; intervention n = 649, control n = 638) were included in a random-effects model meta-analysis. The pooled SMD was − 1.16 (95% CI: −1.60 to −0.71), indicating a statistically significant effect favoring the intervention group. Considerable heterogeneity was observed among studies (I² = 89.5%, τ² = 0.6592, Q = 152.83, P < 0.001). The 95% PI was [−2.93, 0.62]. The individual study effects showed SMDs less than 0 for all studies, with the 95% CIs of the majority not crossing 0, and the direction of effect was consistent with the pooled estimate (Fig. 3).

Forest plot of the pooled analysis for anxiety outcomes

For the depression outcome, 7 studies (total sample size n = 543; intervention n = 277, control n = 266) were included in a random-effects model meta-analysis. The pooled SMD was − 1.28 (95% CI: −2.52 to −0.04), indicating a statistically significant effect favoring the intervention group. Substantial heterogeneity was observed among studies (I² = 93.6%, τ² = 1.6640, Q = 94.28, P < 0.001). The 95% PI was [−4.67, 2.11]. The individual study effects showed SMDs less than 0 for all studies, with the 95% CIs of the majority not crossing 0, and the direction of effect was consistent with the pooled estimate (Fig. 4).

Forest plot of the pooled analysis for depression outcomes

Subgroup analysis of depression outcomes

Subgroup analyses showed that VR interventions significantly reduced depression in the breast cancer, lung cancer, interactive VR, during-treatment, and post-treatment subgroups, as indicated by 95% CIs excluding zero (all P < 0.05). However, the effect estimates for the immersive VR subgroup (SMD = −2.32, 95% CI: −6.04 to 1.41; P = 0.2229) and the pre-treatment subgroup (SMD = −1.78, 95% CI: −4.18 to 0.62; P = 0.1463) did not reach statistical significance, as their confidence intervals crossed the null. Heterogeneity analysis suggested that VR modality and intervention timing might be associated with the distribution of heterogeneity (e.g., I² = 98.6% for immersive VR; I² = 97.6% for pre-treatment). Nevertheless, tests for subgroup differences were not statistically significant across all stratification variables (all P > 0.05), indicating that although these factors may be related to variation in effect estimates, none were confirmed as significant sources of heterogeneity in this study.

Heterogeneity analysis revealed a polarized pattern in the between-study heterogeneity statistics for depression upon the exclusion of individual studies. When excluding any of the six studies (e.g., Ariadna et al. 2024, Bixia Zhang et al. 2024), the heterogeneity statistic I² remained extremely high (94.2% to 94.7%), and τ² fluctuated between 1.85 and 2.01. This suggests that this particular study might differ significantly from other included studies in aspects such as VR intervention details, baseline depressive characteristics of participants, or study design, making it a primary driver of the overall heterogeneity for the depression outcome. However, excluding this study did not change the significance of the pooled effect size, although it led to a reduction in the absolute magnitude of the effect.

Discussion

This comprehensive synthesis indicates that VR interventions demonstrate an overall statistically significant positive effect in reducing anxiety and depression among cancer patients, supporting its potential value as an adjunctive therapeutic tool.

This positive effect is consistent with findings from numerous previous studies [71–75] and may be attributed to the multiple synergistic mechanisms integrated into VR technology, including distraction, exposure and cognitive reappraisal within a safe context, and psychophysiological relaxation [27–29]. However, the findings must be interpreted cautiously considering the significant heterogeneity, risk of bias, and “moderate” certainty of evidence. Notably, while the pooled effect sizes based on confidence intervals consistently favored VR interventions, the PI were wide and crossed the line of no effect in the analysis. This suggests that the effect may vary substantially across different clinical settings and individual patients, and a consistent benefit cannot be guaranteed in all cases. This further indicates the existence of important moderating variables influencing the effectiveness of VR interventions. Although tests for subgroup differences did not reach statistical significance, a nuanced consideration of these subgroup variables remains crucial for rationally interpreting the high heterogeneity observed in this review and for contemplating optimized pathways for the clinical application of VR.

The substantial statistical heterogeneity observed in this meta-analysis warrants further investigation. Several interrelated factors may have collectively contributed to this variability. First, VR intervention protocols exhibited considerable heterogeneity. The included studies differed substantially in terms of devices (immersive head-mounted displays versus interactive non-immersive systems), content (e.g., natural landscape-based relaxation, gamified tasks, or structured educational programs), settings (during hospitalization/treatment periods or not explicitly reported), and intervention duration (ranging from a single 2.7-min session to 24 sessions delivered over an 8-week period). Such inconsistencies in intervention characteristics may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity. Second, patient characteristics and baseline emotional states varied considerably across studies, particularly in the subgroup analyses stratified by treatment timing, where patients were facing impending diagnosis or invasive procedures before treatment. Differences in time since diagnosis, cancer stage, urgency of treatment, and baseline severity of anxiety and/or depression may have resulted in differential responsiveness to VR interventions. Third, outcome assessment instruments were not consistent across studies. Anxiety was evaluated using seven different validated scales (STAI, SAI, SAS, HADS, CSAS, CSA, and GAD−7), whereas depression was assessed using four instruments (SDS, HADS, CES-D, and BDI). Although these scales have established psychometric validity, differences in their conceptual focus, item composition, and scoring ranges may inevitably contribute to between-study heterogeneity. Fourth, methodological considerations should also be acknowledged. Some subgroup analyses included only two or three studies; under such circumstances, the I² statistic is known to be unstable and may overestimate the true magnitude of heterogeneity, as reported in methodological literature [76]. Therefore, although the substantial heterogeneity observed in this review likely reflects genuine variations in VR interventions, patient populations, and outcome measurements, it should be interpreted cautiously. Findings from subgroup analyses based on a limited number of studies should be considered exploratory rather than conclusive. Furthermore, although subgroup analyses explored several predefined potential effect modifiers, considerable residual heterogeneity remained within multiple subgroups, suggesting that additional unmeasured factors may influence the effectiveness of VR interventions. These factors may include baseline severity of anxiety or depression, cancer stage, specific treatment regimens, as well as variations in VR content themes, degree of personalization, and implementation approaches. Despite the high heterogeneity, subgroup analyses indicated that VR interventions consistently showed a favorable direction of effect for improving anxiety and depression across several subgroups, including patients with breast cancer, lung cancer, those receiving interactive VR interventions, and those undergoing treatment or post-treatment interventions. These findings suggest that although specific intervention characteristics and patient factors may influence the magnitude of treatment effects, the overall direction of the association between VR interventions and improvements in anxiety and depression appears to be relatively robust. Therefore, substantial heterogeneity does not necessarily invalidate the potential benefits of VR interventions; rather, it highlights the context-dependent nature of their effects and provides valuable hypotheses and directions for future research to determine which VR approaches are most suitable for which patient populations.

This review found that patient characteristics appear to be important moderators of both anxiety and depression outcomes. Although the findings suggest that VR interventions may improve symptoms across different cancer types included in this review, and subgroup analyses indicate potential benefits among patients with breast and lung cancers, we note that baseline levels of anxiety and depression may vary according to cancer type. For example, patients with thyroid cancer may experience unique psychosocial stress related to societal neglect, resulting in deeper psychological conflicts, increased loneliness, and exacerbated depression and anxiety [77]. Furthermore, considering patient status and device tolerability, known side effects of VR (e.g., dizziness, nausea) have been reported in clinical applications [78], and scoping reviews have documented adverse effects associated with VR technology [79]. Therefore, cautious assessment is warranted for extremely debilitated cancer patients. Concurrently, benefits were observed across all age groups for anxiety without significant differences, indicating potentially broad age applicability of VR interventions. These findings underscore the necessity for future development of disease-specific and demographically tailored VR content.

This review also suggests that the design of VR intervention protocols may influence their effectiveness. For both anxiety and depression outcomes, VR interventions demonstrated overall effectiveness. However, the optimal dosage and timing remain unclear. Interestingly, data for the anxiety outcome suggested that a single session might be as effective as multiple sessions, which could reflect a “novelty effect” [80] or indicate that repeated exposure to homogeneous content may result in habituation [81]. These findings highlight the need for future studies to develop personalized and adaptive VR protocols capable of maintaining long-term therapeutic effects. Furthermore, although both immersive and interactive VR were effective in alleviating anxiety in patients with cancer, their optimal clinical applications may differ. Immersive VR may be better suited for distraction and relaxation during treatment, whereas interactive VR may be more appropriate for pre-treatment education or post-treatment rehabilitation.

This study also has several limitations. From the perspective of the evidence base: First, constrained by the nature of VR technology, implementing blinding was difficult. Most included studies carried a risk of bias concerning allocation concealment and blinding of outcome assessment, which may have led to some overestimation of the intervention effects. Second, outcomes primarily relied on subjective scales, and the use of different measurement instruments may have contributed to heterogeneity. Third, due to imbalanced sample sizes, the current evidence is heavily concentrated on breast cancer patients, with insufficient exploration of other cancer types or populations with specific psychosocial needs (e.g., body image concerns in head and neck cancer patients), potentially limiting the generalizability of the conclusions. Fourth, there is a lack of long-term follow-up data for VR interventions, leaving the durability of its effects unconfirmed. From the review process perspective: First, to maintain the “purity” of the intervention, we excluded 18 studies where VR was combined with other therapies (e.g., mindfulness, cognitive-behavioral therapy). This may have overlooked the actual role and synergistic effects of VR within combined psychological interventions. Second, limited by the level of detail reported in the primary studies, we were unable to perform more in-depth subgroup or meta-regression analyses on potentially important moderators (e.g., patients’ baseline level of emotional distress, specific thematic classification of VR content). Although this review focused specifically on anxiety and depression as two core psychological outcomes, rather than encompassing the full range of psychological and physical symptoms experienced by patients with cancer, this scope was predefined in the registered protocol to provide focused evidence regarding common and clinically important psychological symptoms among this population. Nevertheless, this limitation means that the broader potential value of VR interventions in cancer care may not have been fully captured. Future systematic reviews should consider incorporating additional patient-centered outcomes, including pain, fatigue, sleep disturbance, quality of life, and other relevant outcomes, while comprehensively evaluating the effects of different VR types, immersion modalities, intervention dosages, and implementation strategies to provide more comprehensive guidance for clinical practice. Despite these limitations, the prespecified subgroup analyses in this review extended beyond the preliminary question of whether VR interventions are effective and moved toward the more clinically relevant question of under what conditions and for which patients VR interventions may be most beneficial. By systematically examining cancer type, VR modality, intervention timing, and intervention frequency, this review generated preliminary hypotheses regarding the optimal application of VR across different clinical contexts. For example, the potential role of single-session VR for acute procedural anxiety versus repeated-session interventions for sustained emotional improvement, as well as variations in effects across different cancer types, provides a foundation for personalized implementation of VR interventions. Although these exploratory findings require validation in future large-scale studies, they offer preliminary clinical directions for the individualized application of VR in oncology care.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that VR offers an innovative and promising intervention approach for addressing the prevalent emotional distress of anxiety and depression in cancer patients. It represents more than just an alternative or supplement to traditional psychosocial support; it embodies a novel care paradigm capable of transcending physical limitations to provide immersive interventions on demand. Although current evidence supports its potential benefits, the high heterogeneity and unclear effect modifiers revealed in this review indicate that the effectiveness of VR is highly dependent on specific contexts and individual characteristics. Therefore, the central task of integrating VR into oncological clinical practice has shifted from proving its general efficacy towards constructing a precise framework for its application.

The key future challenge lies in exploring “how to deliver the right VR content, via the right modality, to the right patient, at the right time”. To achieve this goal, we propose the following directions: First, conduct large-scale RCTs with more rigorous methodology, employ active comparators and improve the reporting of blinding procedures. Second, establish standardized VR intervention protocols and reporting guidelines. Third, utilize individual participant data to deeply explore moderators of treatment efficacy. Fourth, implement long-term follow-up to distinguish short-term effects from sustained benefits. Only through collaborative efforts among researchers, clinicians, and policymakers can VR evolve from a “promising technology” into a validated, scalable, and evidence-based intervention component within comprehensive psychosocial care for patients with cancer.

Future broader systematic reviews could extend the scope of the present work by incorporating additional patient-centered outcomes, including pain, fatigue, sleep disturbance, quality of life, and other relevant outcomes. Such comprehensive evidence synthesis would complement the current review’s focus on psychological symptoms and provide more extensive evidence to support clinical decision-making regarding VR implementation in comprehensive cancer care. Meanwhile, establishing standardized reporting guidelines for VR interventions, including detailed descriptions of devices, content, duration, and frequency, represents an urgent priority. These efforts may help reduce between-study heterogeneity and improve comparability across future evidence.
