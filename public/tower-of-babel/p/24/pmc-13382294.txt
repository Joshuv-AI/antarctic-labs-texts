Resistant Starch Supplementation Modulates Gut Microbiota, Short-Chain Fatty Acids, and Markers of Gut Barrier Integrity in Estrogen-Deficient Mice
Authors: Femi Olawale, Sanmi E Alake, Ranjitha Chandrashekar, Proapa Islam, Brooke Sutton, Yabsira Argeta, Stanley Lightfoot, Winyoo Chowanadisai, Trina A Knotts, Brenda J Smith, Edralin A Lucas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMenopause-associated estrogen decline promotes gut dysbiosis and impaired gut health, but the effects of resistant starch (RS) under estrogen-deficient conditions remain unclear.ObjectivesHerein, we examine the effects of retrograded (RS3) and chemically modified (RS4) RS on the gut microbiota and gut barrier integrity in estrogen-deficient mice.MethodsIn this study, 3-mo-old female C57BL/6 mice received the ovotoxicant vinyl cyclohexene diepoxide (VCD, 160 mg/kg) or sesame oil (vehicle) for 30 d, followed by 16 wk of dietary intervention with control (AIN-93M), 5% RS3, or 5% RS4 (n = 15/group). Estrogen deficiency was confirmed by reduced uterine weight and increased serum follicle-stimulating hormone.Gut microbiota composition was assessed via 16S rRNA sequencing; jejunal and cecal gene expressions of inflammatory markers [tumor necrosis factor (Tnf, Il22)], tight junction proteins [Tjp1, claudin1 (Cldn1)], and antimicrobial peptides [regenerating islet-derived protein (Reg)3b, Reg3g] were evaluated by quantitative polymerase chain reaction; and fecal short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) were quantified by gas chromatography-flame ionization detector. Jejunal and colon sections were examined by histological staining. Data were analyzed using a 2-way analysis of variance with estrogen status and diet as factors, followed by post hoc tests when P < 0.05.ResultsRS supplementation induced distinct clustering in bacterial β-diversity, enriched beneficial genera including Bifidobacterium, Dubosiella, Bacteroides, and Akkermansia, and reduced proinflammatory Clostridium sensu_stricto_1 in estrogen-deficient mice. Jejunal Tnf expression was elevated by VCD in the control diet but suppressed by RS4, with no significant change in RS3. Both RS3 and RS4 enhanced Tjp1 and Cldn1 expression and elevated fecal SCFAs, whereas RS3 additionally increased Reg3g expression. Histological analysis showed no significant effects of VCD and diet on most of the parameters, except for a modest effect on gut inflammation with VCD which was improved by RS supplementation.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that RS supplementation modulates gut microbiota composition, SCFA production, and inflammatory markers, with RS4 showing stronger anti-inflammatory effects and RS3 preferentially enhancing antimicrobial peptide expression, in estrogen-deficient mice.

Introduction

Sex hormones, such as estrogen, progesterone, and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), are the primary drivers of female reproductive function [1]. Beyond reproduction, sex hormones have also been linked to extra reproductive functions such as bone remodeling, cardiovascular function, metabolism, and neurobiology [[2], [3], [4]]. During the reproductive years, these hormones fluctuate cyclically to maintain physiological homeostasis. However, with advancing age, ovarian follicular reserves progressively decline, leading to the menopausal transition (perimenopause) and ultimately menopause [5]. Among these hormonal alterations, the progressive decline in estrogen is particularly consequential and has been strongly associated with increased risk of obesity, cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, vasomotor symptoms, and metabolic dysfunction [6].

Perimenopause represents a gradual and physiologically relevant transition characterized by fluctuating yet progressively declining estrogen levels rather than abrupt hormone loss [7]. To model this transitional stage experimentally, 4-vinylcyclohexene diepoxide (VCD) is widely used to selectively deplete ovarian follicles [8]. Unlike ovariectomy models, which induce an immediate and complete loss of ovarian hormones, VCD mimics the natural progression of follicular depletion and gradual estrogen decline observed during perimenopause [9], making it particularly suitable for investigating the physiological consequences of progressive estrogen deficiency [10].

Beyond its established roles in reproductive, metabolic, and cardiovascular regulation, estrogen is increasingly recognized as a key modulator of gut physiology [6,11,12]. Estrogen contributes to gastrointestinal homeostasis by maintaining epithelial integrity, regulating immune responses, and supporting microbial balance [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. Estrogen receptor beta (ER-β), expressed in intestinal epithelial cells, regulates epithelial proliferation, mucus production, and tight junction protein (Tjp) expression, which are essential for gut barrier function [11]. Estrogen deficiency has therefore been associated with impaired barrier integrity and increased intestinal inflammation, which could exacerbate menopausal complications [11].

Concurrently, the gut microbiota modulates estrogen metabolism through the estrobolome. The “estrobolome” refers to an aggregate of bacterial genes capable of metabolizing estrogens [17]. Certain bacterial taxa produce β-glucuronidase, which deconjugates estrogen in the intestine, enabling its reabsorption and influencing systemic estrogen levels [18]. Emerging research has also introduced the concept of the "microgenderome," which addresses how sex hormones influence the gut microbiota [19]. Clinical studies have observed gut dysbiosis in menopause and menopause-associated diseases [19,20]. Consequently, gut dysbiosis has been linked to increased risks of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [21]. Together, these findings highlight the bidirectional relationship between sex hormones and the gut microbiota and suggest that estrogen depletion disrupts the hormone–microbiota axis, contributing to compromised gut barrier function and increased inflammatory risk.

Dietary strategies targeting the gut microbiota have emerged as promising approaches for the management of menopausal symptoms [22]. Under normal physiological conditions, digestible starch is hydrolyzed by α-amylases into oligosaccharides, which are further broken down by brush border enzymes into glucose for rapid absorption in the small intestine [23]. In contrast, resistant starch (RS) resists enzymatic digestion due to structural or chemical features that limit amylase accessibility, allowing it to reach the colon intact, where it undergoes microbial fermentation [24].

RS is classified into 5 subtypes (RS1–RS5) based on physicochemical characteristics, with RS3 (retrograded starch) and RS4 (chemically modified starch) receiving particular attention due to their structural stability and functional versatility [25,26]. RS3 is formed when gelatinized starch undergoes cooling, leading to retrogradation and the development of tightly packed crystalline structures that resist enzymatic hydrolysis and support sustained colonic fermentation [27]. In contrast, RS4 is generated through chemical modifications such as cross-linking or esterification, which introduce covalent bonds that alter the starch backbone and limit amylase accessibility [26]. Compared with RS1 (physically inaccessible starch) and RS2 (native ungelatinized granules), which loses its structure when heated, both RS3 and RS4 demonstrate greater resistance during food processing and digestion [28], with RS3 offering a naturally derived and consistently fermentable form, and RS4 allowing structural modification to tailor functional properties [29]. On reaching the colon, fermentation of RS3 and RS4 enhances microbial diversity and increases production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [30], including acetate, propionate, and butyrate, which serve as energy substrates for colonocytes, strengthen tight junction integrity, modulate immune responses, and exert anti-inflammatory effects [31,32]. Collectively, these properties position RS3 and RS4 as promising dietary interventions for improving gut barrier function under conditions of estrogen deficiency [33].

However, whether RS supplementation can specifically improve gut barrier integrity during progressive estrogen decline remains unclear. The present study evaluated the effects of RS3 and RS4 supplementation on gut barrier integrity and gut microbiota composition in VCD-induced estrogen-deficient mice. We hypothesized that RS3 and RS4 would enhance microbial diversity, increase SCFA production, reduce intestinal inflammation, and improve gut barrier function in estrogen-deficient mice.

Methods

Animal care and diets

Ninety 3-mo-old female C57BL/6 mice (Jackson Laboratory) were acclimated for 7 d in our animal research facility with controlled humidity and temperature and a 12-h light/dark cycle. After acclimation, mice were randomly divided into 2 groups (n = 45/group) to receive a control (CON, AIN-93M) diet and intraperitoneal injection of sesame oil (vehicle, VEH) or the ovotoxicant VCD (160 mg/kg body weight) for 30 consecutive days to induce estrogen deficiency, following previously described methods [34,35]. Estrogen deficiency was confirmed by vaginal smear cytology, and mice were then randomly assigned to 1 of 3 dietary treatments (n = 15/group): CON, RS3, or RS4 (Supplemental Figure 1). Animals had unrestricted access to dietary treatment and water for 16 wk. Mice were weighed weekly, and food intake was monitored daily. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (#IACUC 21-84-STW) of Oklahoma State University.

The CON diet was prepared based on the standard rodent diet (AIN-93M), described by Reeves et al. [36], using ingredients from Envigo Teklad. RS3 (ActiStar RT 75330) and RS4 (FYBRIN RS F100) were generously provided by Cargill Corporation and Grain Processing Corporation, respectively. The nutrient composition including dietary fiber of both RS was determined by NP Analytical Laboratories and added to the CON diet at 5% (wt/wt). The dose of RS is within the range of what has been previously reported [37,38]. All diets were adjusted to have the same macronutrient composition, total fiber, calcium, and phosphorus content (Supplemental Table 1).

Necropsy and tissue processing

Fecal samples were collected before the end of the 16 wk of dietary intervention. At the end of the study, mice were anesthetized with a mixture of ketamine hydrochloride (100 mg/kg bw) and xylazine (10 mg/kg bw). Whole body dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scans (GE Lunar PixiMus) were performed to assess body composition and mice were subsequently bled from the carotid artery. Blood samples were collected, serum separated, and stored at −80°C for later analyses. The uterus was collected, blotted, and weighed to verify the decrease in uterine weight commonly associated with estrogen deficiency. Gut tissues were harvested, flushed with cold PBS, and separated into different sections, and either stored in 10% neutral buffered formalin for histological analysis or snap-frozen for gene expression analyses. The cecum was also flushed with cold sterile PBS, and cecal contents were collected, centrifuged, decanted, and the remaining cecal content was weighed.

Serum FSH analysis

Serum FSH was analyzed using a commercially available sandwich-ELISA kit (MyBioSource) to confirm the induction of estrogen deficiency. The assay kit has a sensitivity of 0.94 ng/mL and a detection range of 1.56 to 100 ng/mL.

Gut microbiota profiling

Gut microbiota analysis was performed to evaluate the impact of RS supplementation on bacterial composition in estrogen-deficient mice. Nucleic acids were isolated from frozen cecal content samples (n = 8/group) using the Qiagen QIAamp PowerFecal DNA kit (Qiagen), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions and optimized for high-throughput sequencing. The 16S rDNA V4 region was amplified using PCR with 2 barcoded fusion primers: F341 (5'-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3') and R806 (5'-GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT-3'). The amplified 16S V4 samples were pooled, barcoded, and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform using paired-end sequencing with a 250-bp run length.

Sequencing data were processed using QIIME2 (version 2023.5.1) for 16S-based microbiota analysis [39]. Demultiplexed paired-end sequences, with barcodes and adapters removed, were analyzed using QIIME2. Quality filtering and feature prediction [amplicon sequence variants (ASVs)] were performed using DADA2 [40]. After reviewing the sequence quality, we did not trim nucleotides from the 5' end of the forward or reverse reads. Forward reads were truncated to 260 nucleotides, whereas reverse reads were truncated to 190 nucleotides based on quality scores. Representative sequences were aligned using MAFFT [41], and a phylogenetic tree of the aligned sequences was constructed using FastTree 2 [42] to infer evolutionary relationships. ASVs were taxonomically classified using a pretrained Naïve Bayes classifier based on the Silva_138_99% reference database [43]. Tables of taxonomic counts and relative frequencies were generated.

Diversity analyses were performed on the resulting feature.biom tables to evaluate both phylogenetic and nonphylogenetic alpha and beta diversity metrics [44]. Beta diversity was assessed using both phylogenetic (unweighted and weighted UniFrac distances) and nonphylogenetic (Bray–Curtis and Jaccard indices) measures. To visualize the dissimilarity in beta diversity between samples and groups, the first 2 principal coordinates were plotted with a 95% confidence interval. Pairwise analysis of β-diversity was done by permutation analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). Differential abundance of microbial taxa across groups was assessed using ANCOM-BC. Statistical comparisons were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn's post hoc test in R-studio (version 4.2).

Fecal β-glucuronidase activity

Fecal β-glucuronidase activity was assessed following established protocols with minor modifications [45,46]. Briefly, 100 mg of frozen fecal sample was homogenized in 1 mL of ice-cold PBS using a bead homogenizer for two 30-s cycles. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 2000 × g for 5 min at 4°C; supernatant was transferred to a new tube, and centrifuged again at 10,000 × g for 20 min. An aliquot of the supernatant (25 μL) was combined with PBS (50 μL) and chromogenic substrate (50 μL), 4-nitrophenyl-β-d-glucuronide (1 mM, dissolved in PBS; Sigma–Aldrich), in a microplate well. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 15 min and stop solution of 0.5N NaOH (125 μL) was added. Absorbance was measured at 405 nm using a Biotek HTX microplate reader, with PBS as the blank. Enzyme activity was quantified using a standard curve generated from serial dilutions of β-glucuronidase enzyme from Escherichia coli (Sigma–Aldrich). Enzyme activities were normalized to the total protein content, determined by bicinchoninic acid assay (Sigma–Aldrich) and expressed as U/mg protein.

Cecal content secretory IgA

Approximately 50 mg of frozen cecal content was homogenized in 1 mL of PBS and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 5 min to collect the supernatant. The resulting supernatant was then used for the quantification of mouse secretory IgA using a commercially available ELISA kit (Abcam).

SCFA analyses

Fecal samples were freeze-dried and processed in duplicates for SCFA analysis according to previously published protocol [47]. Briefly, ground, freeze-dried fecal sample (∼100 mg) was mixed with 50% (w/v) sulfuric acid (250 μL) and vortexed. Isocaproic acid (19.8 × 103 μM in diethyl ether) was used as an internal standard. Fecal samples were extracted with diethyl ether (1 mL) and centrifuged at 2500 × g for 5 min, followed by 2 successive extractions with diethyl ether, yielding a total of ∼3 mL of the organic extract. An aliquot of the resulting organic layer was transferred to a GC sample vial for analysis using an Agilent 6890N GC system equipped with a flame ionization detector. The concentrations of the SCFAs were determined by extrapolating from a 6-point calibration curve generated using standard solutions of acetic acid, propionic acid, n-butyric acid, and iso-butyric acid (Sigma-Aldrich).

Flow cytometric analysis and white blood cell count

Flow cytometry analysis was performed on lymphocytes isolated from the ileum, given the critical role of this region in host–microbe interactions and gut immune responses [48]. The ileum was opened, cut into small pieces, and incubated in complete RPMI medium (1 mM DTT, 2% FBS) as previously reported [49]. Gut lymphocytes were isolated by collagenase digestion. Cells were stained with a live/dead dye (BD Biosciences), followed by surface markers (CD3e–eFluor 450, BV421 channel, BD Biosciences; CD4–PerCP-Cy5.5 and CD25–PE-Cy7, Invitrogen; CD8–Alexa Fluor 488 (FITC channel), Invitrogen), then fixed, permeabilized, and stained for intracellular markers (FOXP3–eFluor 660 (APC channel), Invitrogen; IL-17A–PE, Invitrogen). Populations were quantified using a BD FACSAria III flow cytometer, and data were analyzed with FlowJo (v10.8).

To assess the systemic immune response, total and differential white blood cell (WBC) counts were performed. Whole blood was diluted in 1% gentian violet solution, and total WBCs were counted using a hemocytometer. For differential counts, blood smears were prepared and stained with Wright–Giemsa stain. Lymphocytes, monocytes, and neutrophils were identified and counted under a light microscope. Absolute counts were calculated and expressed as ×103 cells/μL.

RNA extraction and gene expression analyses

Samples of jejunum and cecal tissues were processed to isolate total RNA using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The relative abundance of genes encoding antimicrobial peptides [regenerating islet-derived, (Reg)3b and Reg3g], Tjps [occludin (Ocln)], Tjp1, and claudin-1 (Cldn1), mucin (Muc2), and Tnfα, intestinal alkaline phosphatase (Alpi), plasmalemma vesicle–associated protein (Plvap), estrogen receptor 2 (Esr2, and SCFA receptors [free fatty acid receptor (Ffar2 and Ffar3)] were quantified using SYBR green-based real-time PCR on an ABI 7900HT system (Applied Biosystems). The primer sequences utilized in this study are provided in Supplemental Table 2. The data are presented as relative expression, using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with the Gapdh gene serving as the housekeeping gene.

Histology

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on sections of the jejunum and colon tissues for histological examination as previously described [50]. The tissues were initially recovered from formalin, dehydrated with ethanol and toluene gradients, and embedded in paraffin blocks. A 5 μm section of the tissues was cut, stained in H&E, and photomicrographs were taken for morphometric analysis under 10× magnification. The tissue sections were independently evaluated for histopathological alterations by a board-certified pathologist who was blinded to the treatment groups. Parameters including inflammation, enterocyte injury, goblet cell loss, and muscular thickening were examined and graded using adapted scoring criteria derived from previously published methods, with scores ranging from 0 to 4 or 0 to 3 depending on the specific outcome measured [[50], [51], [52]]. In addition, Image J software was used to estimate villi height, villi width, villi area, and crypt depth from the photomicrographs.

Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2 functional prediction analysis

Functional profiles were predicted from 16S rRNA gene sequencing data using Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2 (PICRUSt2) [53]. Briefly, ASVs were placed into a reference phylogeny, and gene family abundances were inferred based on evolutionary modeling. Predicted gene families were subsequently collapsed into MetaCyc metabolic pathways to generate pathway-level functional profiles. Downstream statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using ggpicrust2 in R [54]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to assess overall differences in predicted functional pathways among experimental groups. Heatmaps were generated to visualize relative abundances of MetaCyc pathways and to compare functional patterns across samples.

Statistical analysis

The GraphPad Prism software package (version 10.1.1) was used for all the statistical analyses. For normally distributed data, a 2-factor (estrogen status and diets) analysis of variance was conducted, followed by Tukey's post hoc test when the P value for interaction or main diet effect was significant. The data are presented as means ± SEM, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Histopathological scores were evaluated using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test. Microbiome-related statistical analyses were performed in R, as outlined in the microbiome data analysis section. Pairwise correlation analysis between host gene expression and gut microbial taxa was performed in R using Spearman’s rank correlation, and the results were visualized as a correlation heatmap displaying correlation coefficients and corresponding significance levels.

Results

VCD administration reduces relative uterine weight and elevates serum FSH concentrations, whereas RS supplementation lowers serum FSH levels

VCD administration promotes increase in body weight and adiposity, whereas RS supplementation did not significantly mitigate these changes

A significant (PVCD = 0.0001) main effect of VCD, but not diet, was observed on weight gain, indicating that estrogen deficiency was associated with increased body weight, whereas RS supplementation did not significantly impact these changes (Figure 1C). Similarly, VCD-treated mice had a significant increase in fat mass (PVCD < 0.01) but not lean mass (Supplemental Figure 2), whereas a significant VCD and diet interaction effect (PVCD × diet < 0.025) was observed for both percent fat mass and percent lean mass. Post hoc analysis indicated no statistically significant differences among the treatment group means except for increased fat mass and reduced lean mass in the RS3 VCD group compared with the VEH group (Figure 1D–E).

RS supplementation enhances weights of the cecal tissue and its content, and modulates gut microbiota towards beneficial microbial population

To assess the prebiotic effects of RS supplementation in estrogen-deficient mice, we first weighed cecal tissue weight and its content. Additionally, microbial DNA was isolated from the cecal content for 16S rRNA gene analysis to evaluate changes in the gut microbiota. There was a significant main effect of diet on cecal weight (Pdiet < 0.0001), and post hoc analysis revealed that both the RS3 (P < 0.0001) and RS4 (P < 0.0001) diet groups had significantly higher cecal weights compared with the CON diet (Figure 1F). Similarly, a significant effect of diet (Pdiet = 0.0331) was observed on cecal content weight, with the RS groups showing a more pronounced increase compared with the CON diet groups (Figure 1G). No significant main effects of VCD treatment or diet and VCD interaction were observed for either cecal tissue weight or cecal content weight. These findings suggest the potential prebiotic role of RS supplementation in modulating cecum tissue weight and content irrespective of estrogen status.

Gut microbiota diversity analysis was performed to assess both alpha diversity (diversity within samples) and beta diversity (differences between communities) to determine how estrogen status and diet affect the gut microbiota (Figure 2). Shannon, Chao1, and Pielou indices were used to compare within-group diversity, and rarefaction curves were generated to assess the sequencing depth (Figure 2A–C and Supplemental Figure 3). Each of the alpha diversity metrics revealed that the RS groups (both VEH and VCD) showed a decrease in microbiota diversity compared with the CON-fed groups. The Shannon, Chao1, and Pielou indices all indicated reduced diversity, richness, and evenness in the RS groups compared with control. The Kruskal–Wallis comparison of the groups showed a statistically significant difference in alpha diversity metrics between the treatment groups (Supplemental Table 3). The Unweighted UniFrac measure of β-diversity (Supplemental Figure 4A), as assessed through principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), revealed an overlap between the CON VEH and CON VCD groups, suggesting that their microbial communities were more similar to one another. In contrast, the RS3 VEH and RS3 VCD groups exhibited greater overlap with the RS4 groups, indicating that the microbial communities in these groups shared more similarities. This pattern suggests that both RS3 and RS4 supplementation influenced microbial diversity in a similar manner. Similarly, the weighted UniFrac measure (Supplemental Figure 4B), which incorporates both the phylogenetic distances and the relative abundances of taxa, showed a clustering pattern consistent with the unweighted UniFrac analysis. However, the overlap among the RS3 and RS4 groups was more pronounced, indicating that, when accounting for abundance, these groups were even more similar to each other than was observed with the unweighted UniFrac measure.

In addition to the UniFrac measures, the Bray–Curtis distance metric also revealed important insights into microbial community structure. The PCoA analysis of Bray–Curtis distances showed a stronger overlap between the CON VEH and CON VCD groups (Figure 2D). In contrast, the RS3 and RS4 groups exhibited less overlap with the CON groups, highlighting the distinct influence of RS supplementation on microbiota composition. Similarly, the Jaccard index, which focuses solely on the presence or absence of taxa (ignoring their relative abundance), also revealed a more distinct clustering. The PCoA plot of Jaccard distances showed that the CON VEH and CON VCD clustered more closely together, whereas the RS3 and RS4 groups formed distinct clusters separate from the CON-fed groups (Figure 2E). This further emphasizes that RS supplementation led to a more unique microbial community structure that was not observed in the CON-fed groups, even when considering just the presence or absence of microbial taxa.

Pairwise PERMANOVA analysis of β-diversity showed that RS3 and RS4 supplementation induced significant changes (P < 0.05) in gut microbial composition compared with CON diet-fed groups, regardless of VEH or VCD treatment (Table 1). In contrast, β-diversity differences between CON VEH and CON VCD were largely nonsignificant, except for Jaccard matrix, indicating minimal effect of estrogen deficiency alone. Differences among RS-treated groups were generally smaller, with a few measures showing modest variation, particularly between RS3 and RS4 diets. Taken together, the results from the β-diversity metrics support the idea that RS supplementation, significantly altered the gut microbiota, resulting in distinct clustering patterns that set these groups apart from the CON diet-fed groups. The microbiota of the mice was primarily composed of the phyla Bacillota, Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, Verrucomicrobiota, and Pseudomonadota (Figure 2F). Mice on the CON diet had a higher proportion of Bacillota compared with those on the RS diet, whereas the RS diet-fed mice exhibited increased proportions of Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, and Verrucomicrobiota compared with the CON groups. Variability was also observed at lower taxonomic levels, with distinct patterns in family and genus composition between the dietary groups. At the family level, Erysipelotrichaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Muribaculaceae, and Clostridiaceae were more abundant in the CON groups compared with the RS3 and RS4 groups (Figure 2G). Notably, the CON VCD group had a higher proportion of Erysipelotrichaceae and Clostridiaceae, and lower Akkermansiaceae compared with the CON VEH and RS groups. In contrast, the RS diets resulted in higher proportions of Bifidobacteriaceae, Bacteroidaceae, Lactobacillaceae, and Akkermansiaceae compared with the CON groups.

Muc2 gene, which encodes the major secreted mucin responsible for intestinal mucus layer formation, was not affected by either VCD or diet (Figure 4J). In contrast, Alpi, a marker of enterocyte differentiation and intestinal barrier function, showed a modest interaction effect (PVCD × diet = 0.050), with RS4 VEH group having the highest gene expression (Figure 4K). VCD significantly reduced (PVCD = 0.003) Plvap gene expression compared with VEH, implicating VCD as a potential disruptor of endothelial barrier integrity (Figure 4L). ER beta (Esr2) gene expression was not affected by VCD but was significantly affected by diet (Pdiet < 0.001), with the RS4 groups showing a 2–3-fold increase in Esr2 expression relative to the CON diet (Figure 4M). This suggests that RS supplementation may influence ER activity, potentially offering a beneficial effect in estrogen deficiency.

Similar effects were observed in the cecal gene expression analysis, where both RS3 and RS4 significantly increased the expression of tight junction–related genes (Tjp1, Ocln, and Cldn1) and the gut barrier integrity marker Alpi (Supplemental Figure 6). Notably, RS4 produced the most consistent improvements in the cecal gene expression. Moreover, VCD significantly reduced (PVCD = 0.004) cecal Esr2 gene expression, whereas a significant main diet effect (Pdiet = 0.002) was also observed with RS4 significantly increasing the expression of this gene compared with the CON diet. No significant main or interaction effect was observed for cecal Tnf, Ffar3, and Reg3g (Supplemental Figure 6). Collectively, these findings indicate that RS supplementation, particularly RS4, modulates multiple markers of gut barrier integrity in estrogen-deficient mice.

Both RS3 and RS4 fed groups clustered apart from controls, which suggests substantial shifts in predicted metabolic capacity associated with RS. Separation along PC2 (23% variance) further suggested differences between RS3 and RS4, although partial overlap was observed, reflecting shared functional features between the 2 RS diets. Analysis of the top 30 MetaCyc pathways exhibiting RS × VCD interaction effects demonstrated diet-specific and treatment-dependent modulation of microbial functional potential (Figure 5B). In VCD-treated mice, pathways involved in central carbon metabolism, including glycolysis (V), the reductive and incomplete TCA cycle, and the superpathway of glycol metabolism and degradation, were consistently decreased compared with vehicle controls. VCD treatment also reduced pathways related to nucleotide metabolism, including purine and pyrimidine de novo biosynthesis and associated salvage pathways, as well as vitamin and cofactor biosynthesis pathways such as pyridoxal 5′-phosphate and thiamine diphosphate biosynthesis (Figure 5B). In contrast, RS supplementation, particularly RS4, significantly enhanced these pathways, restoring or increasing the abundance of energy metabolism, nucleotide biosynthesis, and vitamin biosynthesis functions relative to control diet groups. Notably, RS supplementation mitigated many of the VCD-associated functional deficits, resulting in distinct RS × VCD interaction effects across the top predicted microbial pathways (Figure 5B). Together, these findings indicate that RS3 and RS4 differentially influence predicted microbial functional gene profiles, with RS4 eliciting more pronounced alterations in pathways related to microbial growth and metabolic activity, and that these effects are modestly modulated by VCD treatment.

To further examine associations between gut microbial composition and intestinal integrity, correlation analyses were performed between abundant bacterial genera and diet-responsive host genes related to barrier function, inflammation, and signaling (Figure 5C). Esr2 expression was positively associated with Bacteroides (P = 0.042) and Bifidobacterium (P = 0.046), whereas a negative association was observed with Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 (P = 0.020) and Faecalibaculum (P = 0.04). The tight junction gene Tjp1 showed significant negative correlations with Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group (P = 0.002), Alistipes (P = 0.003), Muribaculaceae (P = 0.024), and Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 (P = 0.014), but a positive association with Dubosiella (P < 0.001) and Bifidobacterium (P = 0.001). Similarly, the antimicrobial peptide Reg3g was negatively correlated with Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group (P < 0.001), Alistipes (P = 0.001), Muribaculaceae (P = 0.034), and Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 (P = 0.026), while showing a positive association with Dubosiella (P < 0.001) and Bifidobacterium (P = 0.002) indicating distinct microbial signatures linked to host defense gene expression. Ocln was positively correlated with Bacteroides (P = 0.045) and Plvap negatively correlated with Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group (P < 0.022).

Associations with microbial taxa were also observed for genes involved in inflammatory and metabolic signaling. The SCFA receptor Ffar3 was positively correlated with Bacteroides (P = 0.045) and negatively correlated with Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 (P = 0.018). In contrast, the inflammatory marker Tnf was positively associated with Dubosiella (P = 0.006), while showing a negative correlation with Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group (P = 0.004) and Alistipes (P = 0.019), suggesting a nuanced role of these taxa in inflammatory regulation. No significant correlations were observed in the Reg3b, Cldn1, and Ffar2 gene.

Overall, these correlation patterns are consistent with the RS-induced enrichment of Bacteroides and Bifidobacterium and the concomitant reduction in Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 and Faecalibaculum. The positive associations of Bacteroides and Bifidobacterium with genes related to gut barrier function and estrogen signaling, together with the negative correlations observed for Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 and Dubosiella with markers of intestinal integrity, suggest that RS-mediated shifts in microbial composition may support improved gut barrier function in this model.

RS supplementation showed a modest effect on gut architecture

Histological evaluation of the jejunum revealed modest diet- and VCD-dependent alterations in tissue integrity (Supplemental Table 4 and Figure 6). Overall, enterocyte damage and ulceration were minimal across all groups, with no significant differences observed (Table 4 and Figure 6). Similarly, histological scoring of the muscularis layer revealed no significant differences in muscle thickness among experimental groups. In contrast, jejunal inflammation was markedly elevated in estrogen-deficient mice receiving the CON diet. Discussion

Menopause-associated estrogen deficiency is increasingly recognized as a critical driver of gut dysbiosis, contributing to low-grade inflammation and impaired gut barrier integrity [55]. Despite growing awareness of the gut as a target of estrogen signaling, effective and accessible nutritional strategies to counteract menopause-related gut dysfunction remain limited [56]. RS, a fermentable dietary carbohydrate known to shape microbial ecology and support epithelial health [33], represents a promising candidate; however, its capacity to mitigate estrogen deficiency–induced alterations in the gut has not been fully elucidated. Herein, we provide evidence that RS supplementation in estrogen-deficient mice improves gut barrier integrity markers.

Estrogen deficiency induced by the ovotoxicant 4-VCD significantly disrupts multiple physiological systems, including reproductive tissues, adiposity, bone parameters, and gut health [9,57,58]. In the present study, reduced relative uterine weight and elevated serum FSH after VCD administration confirm the successful induction of estrogen deficiency, consistent with previous reports [9]. Notably, RS supplementation significantly reduced serum FSH despite persistent ovarian failure, suggesting a possible gut-mediated modulation of endocrine signaling. This effect may be driven, in part, by RS-induced enrichment of estrobolome-associated bacteria [59], including Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Bacteroides, and Akkermansia [60], which possess β-glucuronidase and related enzymatic activities involved in estrogen deconjugation [61,62]. Increased β-glucuronidase activity can promote the deconjugation of estrogen metabolites in the intestine, facilitating enterohepatic recirculation and enhancing estrogen bioavailability, which may subsequently suppress pituitary FSH secretion via negative feedback mechanisms [59]. These findings are further supported by the increased expression of Esr2 observed in the jejunal and cecal tissues of RS4-treated mice, suggesting enhanced estrogen signaling, potentially mediated through RS–induced modulation of the estrobolome [[63], [64], [65]].

Previous studies have reported significant shifts in gut microbiota during menopause. In the current study, perimenopause appeared to cause modest shifts in the gut microbiota composition. These include an increase in Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, which are opportunistic pathogens [66], suggesting that these genera could drive gut dysbiosis in the perimenopausal mouse [67]. Supplementation with RS reversed these outcomes and significantly increased beneficial bacterial genera such as Bifidobacterium, Bacteroides, Akkermansia, and Lactobacillus which have been associated with increased SCFAs production and improved gut health [68]. However, RS decreased α-diversity, which is unexpected because α-diversity is generally associated with overall bacterial richness and diversity. Previous studies on RS align with the current results, and the decrease in diversity likely reflects selective enrichment of specialized fiber-fermenting taxa that outcompete less efficient microbes [[69], [70], [71], [72]]. These compositional changes were accompanied by functional shifts in microbial metabolism, as evidenced by increased fecal SCFA concentrations in RS-fed mice. Given the established roles of SCFAs in supporting epithelial energy metabolism, regulating inflammation, and reinforcing gut barrier function, these findings suggest that RS-induced microbial remodeling is functionally relevant and may contribute to improved intestinal outcomes in estrogen-deficient mice [30,73].

Furthermore, emphasizing the role of RS in gut health, we observed that although VCD treatment was associated with increased expression of the proinflammatory marker Tnf and mild inflammatory changes observed by histological analysis, RS4 supplementation mitigated these effects, as evidenced by improved histological outcomes, suggesting a potential anti-inflammatory effect of RS within the gut [74,75]. These improvements were accompanied by increased expression of Tjps, SCFA receptors (Ffar2 and Ffar3), and antimicrobial peptides, suggesting enhanced epithelial barrier function. In addition, elevated Esr2 expression in both RS3- and RS4-supplemented groups indicates that RS may modulate estrogen-responsive pathways within the gut epithelium, potentially buffering the adverse effects of estrogen deficiency on intestinal homeostasis. Esr2 has been shown to help modulate epithelial barrier function in IBD [11]. RS supplementation was also associated with reduced systemic WBC and lymphocyte counts, indicating a modest but measurable immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effect [76]. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that RS-mediated alterations in gut microbiota composition can translate into improvements in both intestinal and systemic inflammatory status in murine models [31,76,77].

Furthermore, the correlation analyses and functional predictions reinforce previously described mechanisms by which RS improves gut health, particularly under conditions of estrogen deficiency. PICRUSt2-based functional predictions indicate that VCD-treated mice show a reduction in pathways like glycolysis (V), the reductive and incomplete TCA cycle, and the superpathway of glycol metabolism and degradation, indicating a shift towards a metabolically less active gut microbiome. This reduction in microbial energy metabolism may limit fermentative output and downstream production of critical metabolites such as SCFA [78], which is important for maintaining intestinal epithelial integrity. In parallel, estrogen deficiency attenuated multiple pathways related to nucleotide biosynthesis and salvage, suggesting impaired microbial growth and community stability [79]. Also, decrease in thiamine diphosphate biosynthesis pathway by VCD is indicative of reduced activity of some vitamin-producing bacteria such as Bacteroides, which may further compromise microbial enzymatic activity and host–microbe metabolic interactions [[80], [81], [82]]. Interestingly, supplementation with RS4 largely reversed this estrogen deficiency-induced functional deficit as noted by enriched energy metabolism, biosynthesis, and vitamin production pathways, which suggest improved gut microbiota activity.

Furthermore, mechanistic insight into the impact of estrogen deficiency and RS on the gut microbiota and associated effects on gut health was obtained through correlation analysis. A negative correlation between Clostridium sensu stricto 1, which was increased in the CON VCD group, and Tjps suggests that alterations in microbial composition may directly contribute to impaired gut barrier function in the estrogen-deficient gut. In contrast, positive correlations between epithelial defense genes such as Tjp1 and Reg3g and taxa commonly associated with fermentable carbohydrate metabolism, including Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides, support a protective role for these microbes in maintaining gut barrier integrity. Importantly, the observed positive associations between Esr2 expression and multiple bacterial genera, including Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides, as well as the negative association between Esr2 and Clostridium sensu stricto 1, suggest interactions between ER and the gut microbiota activity [11]. These findings align with emerging evidence that microbial modulation of estrogen metabolism through the estrobolome can influence local ER expression within the intestine, thereby affecting epithelial homeostasis [18,83]. Furthermore, correlations between the SCFA receptors Ffar2 and Ffar3 and select bacterial taxa reinforce the role of microbial fermentation products as critical mediators linking diet, microbiota, and host intestinal signaling pathways. Collectively, these findings support a model in which estrogen deficiency disrupts key host–microbe interactions governing gut barrier integrity and hormonal signaling, whereas RS supplementation may help restore these interactions by modulating specific taxa involved in epithelial defense, SCFA signaling, and estrogen responsiveness [69,83].

Despite these insights from this study, several limitations should be considered. Estrogen deficiency was induced using 4-VCD, which, although widely used to model the menopausal transition, may exert off-target effects which may not truly reflect perimenopause complications [84]. A limitation of the present study is the relatively small sample size used for fecal sIgA analysis due to limited sample availability. Because fecal material from the same animals was utilized for multiple downstream assays, insufficient sample quantities were available for sIgA measurement in some groups. Consequently, the reduced sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle differences in mucosal immune responses, and therefore the sIgA findings should be interpreted with caution. We also acknowledge that mRNA expression data alone, in the absence of functional studies on gut barrier integrity, are insufficient to make definitive conclusions on gut barrier health. Additionally, the use of 16S rRNA sequencing limits taxonomic resolution and provides only inferred microbial function [85], and correlation analyses do not necessarily imply causation. Future studies incorporating metagenomics, direct metabolite measurements, and human trials are needed to validate and extend these findings.

In summary, RS supplementation shows promise for improving gut health under estrogen-deficient conditions by enriching beneficial SCFA- and estrobolome-associated taxa, suppressing proinflammatory bacteria, enhancing barrier function, and modulating ER activity. RS4 appeared more effective in reducing inflammation and strengthening tight junction integrity compared with RS3, whereas RS3 showed stronger enrichment of specific beneficial genera such as Bifidobacterium and Dubosiella and decrease in Bacteroides compared with RS4, highlighting subtle differences in their prebiotic effects. Overall, both RS types support gut microbial balance and changes in the expression of genes related to tight junction integrity, making them promising interventions for menopause-associated gut dysbiosis. Future studies should explore long-term effects, mechanisms, and clinical translation.
