Genotypic diversity and genotype–phenotype concordance of antimicrobial resistance in methicillin-resistant staphylococci from dogs and cats
Authors: Jeerawat Soonthornsit, Nuvee Prapasarakul, Kittitat Lugsomya, Nae Tanpradit, Sukanya Apiratwarrasakul, Nathita Phumthanakorn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Polyclonal MRSP strains were detected with ST1386, ST496, and seven novel STs reported for the first time in Thailand.•Genotype–phenotype concordance was high at 93.19% and discordance mainly due to genotypic resistance with phenotypic susceptibility.•A high number of virulence genes did not affect the clinical resolution of Staphylococcus infections.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, Cats, Dogs, Genotype, Methicillin-resistant staphylococci

Introduction

Staphylococcus spp. are one of the major opportunistic bacteria in dogs and cats. These pathogens are often isolated from various infection sites, such as the skin, ear canal, urinary tract, and others (Marsilio et al., 2018). The frequent use of antimicrobial agents in companion animals has contributed to the development of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) among bacterial pathogens. The most important resistant trait of Staphylococcus spp. is methicillin resistance, which is mediated by mecA gene located on staphylococcal cassette chromosome mec (SCCmec) (Weese & van Duijkeren, 2010). Methicillin-resistant staphylococci (MRS) are resistant to β-lactams, one of the most commonly used classes of antimicrobials, and often exhibit a multidrug resistant (MDR) phenotype, which poses a major therapeutic challenge in veterinary medicine (Nocera & De Martino, 2024).

Genotypic characterisation of MRS in dogs and cats is important, as it provides valuable insights into clonal types, resistance genes, and virulence factors, and supports the study of both local and global population structures. Ongoing surveillance is essential to monitor the emergence and dissemination of MRS, especially novel MDR strains in companion animals (Robb et al., 2024). With the growing accessibility and affordability of sequencing technologies, whole genome sequencing (WGS) is now widely used for genotypic characteristics of MRS isolated from companion animals, including strains from Thailand (Perreten et al., 2013; Phumthanakorn et al., 2021, 2022; Chanchaithong et al., 2024). However, data on the genetic traits of MRS and their associations with clinical characteristics such as clonal distribution and infection site specificity, and clinical outcomes remain limited in Thailand.

In addition to its use in research contexts, WGS has also been proposed as a routine tool in clinical diagnostic laboratories and human hospitals for predicting antimicrobial resistance through genomic analysis (Bianconi et al., 2023; Forde et al., 2023). In veterinary medicine, previous studies have explored the concordance between genotypic and phenotypic resistance in S. pseudintermedius or methicillin-resistant S. pseudintermedius (MRSP) (Morais et al., 2023; Nisa et al., 2019; Viñes et al., 2022; Wegener et al., 2018) however, other Staphylococcus species have received less attention. Additionally, variability in AMR phenotype prediction from WGS has been reported across laboratories (Doyle et al., 2020). Therefore, further research is needed to assess the utility of WGS-based prediction of phenotypic AMR across diverse Staphylococcus spp. isolated from dogs and cats.

This study aimed to investigate the genotypic diversity, clinical associations, and concordance between genotypic and phenotypic antimicrobial resistance in clinical MRS isolates from Thai dogs and cats using whole genome sequencing (WGS). The results provide insight into the population structure of clinical MRS in Thailand in comparison with global strains. In addition, resistance and virulence traits across multiple Staphylococcus species were characterized, and the emergence of novel sequence types (STs) was identified.

Materials and methods

Clinical data analyses

Complete clinical data of sick dogs and cats with MRS infection who visited a referral small animal teaching hospital, from 2020 to 2021, were included in this study. Signalment data, including age, breed, sex, and neuter status as well as information on lesions or clinical signs, sampling and infection sites, definitive diagnosis, and bacterial culture results were collected. According to the medical records, sick animals were scheduled for follow-up evaluations at the veterinary hospital, typically 7 days after the first visit, with additional appointments as required. Clinical examinations were performed at each visit, and treatment plans were adjusted based on clinical signs and laboratory results. Clinical outcomes were assessed from the first visit, at which a definitive diagnosis and initial treatment were initiated, and at the final evaluation based on the last recorded follow-up in the medical records (Bryan et al., 2012). Outcomes were categorized as either clinical improvement or no improvement for all patients with follow-up evaluations. Clinical improvement was defined as resolution of clinical signs, removal of antimicrobial drugs, and no need for further follow-up.

Bacterial identification

Bacteria were isolated from clinical specimens using routine aerobic culture methods (Markey et al., 2013) at the in-house laboratory of the Veterinary Diagnostic Center. The genus and species were identified based on colony morphology and biochemical tests, including Gram staining, catalase test, oxidase test, coagulase test (BBL™, BD Biosciences, USA), oxidative-fermentative test, glucose fermentation test, mannitol fermentation test, and susceptibility tests for polymyxin B (300 units), bacitracin (0.04 units), and novobiocin (5 μg) (Oxoid™, Thermo Scientific, UK). The biochemical media used for testing, except for the coagulase test, were prepared in-house. Clinical isolates identified as Staphylococcus spp. were kept at −80 °C for further studies in this project. Staphylococcus spp. were grown on 5% sheep blood agar (Biomedia, Thailand). Isolates were identified to the species level using unique protein profiling by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS, Bruker Daltonics, Germany).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST)

Susceptibility testing was routinely performed by disk diffusion method. The following drugs were tested: β-lactams [oxacillin (1 µg), cefoxitin (30 µg), penicillin (10 unit), cefpodoxime (10 µg)], enrofloxacin (5 µg), marbofloxacin (5 µg), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (1.25/23.75 µg), tetracycline (30 µg), doxycycline (30 µg), erythromycin (15 µg), clindamycin (2 µg), amikacin (30 µg), and gentamicin (10 µg) (Oxoid™, Thermo Scientific, UK). The inhibition zone was interpreted following CLSI VET01 guidelines (CLSI VET01, 2024). Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 was used as the reference strain. Isolates were considered multidrug-resistant (MDR) if they were not susceptible to at least one agent in at least three antimicrobial classes (Sweeney et al., 2018). The intermediates were regarded as susceptible according to the definition in the EUCAST (https://www.eucast.org) (access November 25, 2025). Isolates resistant to oxacillin or cefoxitin were included, and mecA-positive isolates were classified as methicillin-resistant following the CLSI guidelines (CLSI VET01, 2024).

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) and analysis

DNA was extracted following the recommendations of the commercial manufacturer (ZymoBIOMICS DNA Miniprep Kit, Zymo Research, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared using NEB Next Ultra II DNA library prep kit, and genomes were sequenced using Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (illumina). Raw reads were trimmed and filtered using Fastp version 0.23.2 (Chen et al., 2018). Genomes were assembled using Unicycler (Galaxy Version 0.5.0+galaxy1) (Wick et al., 2017), and annotation was done using Prokka version 1.14.6 (Seemann, 2014). Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) was performed using pubMLST (Jolley et al., 2018). SCCmec types were identified using SCCmecFinder version 1.2 (Kaya et al., 2018) on the Central of Genomic Epidemiology (CGE) website (https://www.genomicepidemiology.org) and then manually checked. The SCCmec element lacking identifiable ccr complexes or containing uncharacterized SCCmec type combinations are classified as non-typable (NT). Antimicrobial resistant genes were searched using ResFinder version 1.4 (Bortolaia et al., 2020) and the comprehensive antibiotic resistance database (CARD) (Alcock et al., 2023) and then manually checked. Mutations in the fluoroquinolone resistant genes were identified using CARD and compared with reference sequences of gyrA, gyrB, glrA, and glrB gene in S. pseudintermedius LMG22219T (accession no MLGE00000000.2), S. aureus ATCC 25213 (accession no LHUS00000000.2), S. schleiferi NCTC12218 (accession no LR962863.1), S. cohnii ATCC 29974 (accession no LT963440.1), S. haemolyticus ATCC 29970 (accession no GCA_006094395.1), and S. warneri G25 (accession no SCHD01000001.1-SCHD01000435.1). The virulence factor database (VFDB) was used to search for virulence genes common among Staphylococcus spp. (Liu et al., 2022). S. aureus was used as the reference database in VFDB.

Phylogenetic single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) tree analysis

Core genes were found using Roary (Page et al., 2015), and the SNP site version 2.5.1 (Page et al., 2016) in the Galaxy website (https://usegalaxy.eu) was used to align SNP sequences. A phylogenetic SNP tree was constructed in IQTree under maximum likelihood (Minh et al., 2020) and visualized in FigTree version 1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/Figuretree/).

Assessment of concordance between genotypic and phenotypic AMR

The AMR genes identified using WGS were compared with the routine laboratory AST results. The major error was referred to phenotypic susceptibility while demonstrating genotypic resistance. The very major error was referred to phenotypic resistance, but no genotypic resistance gene was identified. When discordance between the phenotype and genotype was detected, the isolates were repeat tested for AST. The specificity and sensitivity of concordance between genotypic and phenotypic AMR were calculated using GraphPad Prism version 10.0.2 (GraphPad software, USA) and the results were reported with 95% confidence intervals.

Results

MRS isolates from dogs and cats

A total of 312 cases of Staphylococcus spp. infection were presented to the Animal Hospital between 2020 and 2021, of which 102 individuals were infected with isolates exhibiting resistance to oxacillin or cefoxitin based on disk diffusion screening. Fifty cases had complete clinical information, whereas a follow-up was not available for the other 52 cases; therefore, these cases were excluded from further analysis. WGS and subsequent analyses showed that, among the 50 cases with complete clinical information, three isolates (two S. pseudintermedius and one S. coagulans) were mecA-negative. The remaining 47 isolates, obtained from 40 dogs and 7 cats, were confirmed as methicillin-resistant. Skin was the most common site of MRS infection (42.55%, n = 20), with lesions including pyoderma, deep wound infections, dog-bite wounds, and skin masses, followed by the ear canal (19.15%, n = 9), urinary tract (17.02%, n = 8), and surgical wounds (14.89%, n = 7) (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). The most prevalent bacterial species identified was methicillin-resistant S. pseudintermedius (MRSP) (61.70%, n = 29), followed by methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) (14.89%, n = 7), methicillin-resistant S. coagulans (MRSC) (14.89%, n = 7), methicillin-resistant S. ureilyticus (MRSU) (4.26%, n = 2), methicillin-resistant S. haemolyticus (MRSH) (2.13%, n = 1), and methicillin-resistant S. warneri (MRSW) (2.13%, n = 1).

ST distribution, SCCmec types, and AMR

MLST analysis showed that 47 MRSP had 20 STs. The most prevalent isolate belonged to ST1386 (n = 4), followed by 3 strains each from ST45, ST496, and ST433 (Fig. 1A). Seven novel STs: ST2622, ST2623, ST2624, ST2625, ST2626, ST2627, and ST2628, were observed (Fig. 1A). SCCmecV (5C2&5) was found in the ST1386 strains, SCCmecVc (5C2&5) occurred in ST496, and non-typable (NT)-SCCmec occurred in ST45 and ST433. A phylogenetic tree based on SNPs identified substantial clusters of many isolates in ST1386-ST2622-ST496-ST121 (n = 9) and ST433-ST1541-ST155-ST151 (n = 6) (Fig. 1A). Except for MRSP-ST496, all strains from the same ST had similar resistance gene profiles (Fig. 1A). Most of the seven MRSA strains were MRSA-ST398 (n = 6), which five of them carried a combination of SCCmecV(5C2) and IX(1C2) characterized by the presence of class C2 mec, ccrA1B1, and ccrC1 (Fig. 1B). Seven MRSC carried the SCCmecV. SCCmecIX (1C2) was present in the MRSU strain A434, whereas NT-SCCmec was present in the MRSU strain A779 and MRSW strain A443. SCCmecV(5C2&5) was present in the MRSH strain A841, which belonged to the novel clone ST146 (Fig. 1D).

All MRS isolates were multidrug-resistant (MDR) and exhibited high resistance to fluoroquinolones (97.9% each for enrofloxacin and marbofloxacin), tetracycline (93.52%), and doxycycline (91.5%). All strains carried blaZ and mutations in gyrA and grlA, except for a single strain each of MRSU and MRSW. All MRSC isolates showed no phenotypic resistance to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, erythromycin, or clindamycin. Single or combined presence of tet(M), tet(K), and tet(L) was identified in tetracycline-resistant strains, except for four MRSP isolates. tet(38) was specific to MRSA. MRSP isolates frequently harbored blaZ, tet(M), dfrG, erm(B), aac(6′)-Ie-aph(2′’)-Ia, aph(3′)-IIIa, and mutations in gyrA (S84L) and grlA (S80I). In addition to the tested drugs, the chloramphenicol resistance gene catpc221 was identified in 14 MRSP isolates.

Phylogenetic SNP tree of MRSP

To assess the genetic relationship with other strains, MRSP isolates from this study were compared with 20 representative global strains of the same sequence types (STs) available in the GenBank database. Additionally, previously reported Thai strains (n = 7) representing several STs were included. The MRSP-ST45 isolates in this study formed a distinct node but remained within the same cluster as other ST45 and Thai ST282 strains (Fig. 2). Strains belonging to ST181 from various countries clustered closely together. Thai ST496 strains clustered more closely with one another than with strains from South Africa and Australia.

Concordance of genotypic and phenotypic antimicrobial resistance

Concordance for the MRS surrogate drugs, oxacillin and cefoxitin, accounted for 94.0%, with a major error rate of 6.0% (Table 1). Further analysis of 47 MRS with mecA indicated that the overall concordance of genotypic and phenotypic AMR for the 10 drugs tested was 93.19%, with a sensitivity and specificity (95% confidence intervals) of 0.85 (0.74–0.92) and 0.95 (0.92–0.96), respectively (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1). Penicillin (100%) and erythromycin (100%) had the highest phenotypic–genotypic concordance rates, followed by enrofloxacin, marbofloxacin, and clindamycin, each with 97.87%. MRSW was resistant to marbofloxacin and enrofloxacin; however, its gyrA and glrA did not show any mutations. This MRSW strain had lnu(A) but was clindamycin-susceptible. Twenty-nine strains harboring dfrG or dfrE or dfrC were resistant to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (concordance 93.62%), whereas 3 susceptible strains (2 MRSA and 1 MRSU) carried dfrG (major error rate: 6.38%). None of tet(M), tet(L), or tet(K) occurred in 4 doxycycline-resistant MRSP strains, and 2 MRSP strains carrying tet(M) fell in the intermediate category under the CLSI guidelines. The concordance rate of gentamicin was 89.36%, with a predominating major error rate. In five discordant cases, aph(3′)-IIIa was observed in one MRSP strain, and aac(6′)-Ie-aph(2′')-Ia was present in three MRSC and one MRSW strain. Cefpodoxime had the lowest concordance rate at 78.72%, primarily due to major errors causing discordance. Of the 10 mecA-positive but cefpodoxime-susceptible strains, 4 MRSC and 1 MRSH strain fell in the intermediate category under the CLSI guidelines.

Assessment of STs of MRSP and MRSA in relation to sites of infection

The relationship between sequence types (STs) and sites of infection was assessed for MRSP and MRSA strains for which ST data were available. MRSP-ST1386 was found on several sites, including skin, ear canal, urinary tract, and surgical wounds. Similarly, MRSP-ST433 was isolated from skin, ear canal, and urinary tract, while ST45 was found particularly in skin infections (Fig. 1A). Four of the seven MRSA-ST398 strains were isolated from surgical wounds (Fig. 1B).

Determination of virulence genes and clinical outcomes

Overall, the virulence genes shared the unique profiles of species that carried them (Fig. 3). Clinical improvement data were analyzed to determine whether the frequency of virulence genes was related to clinical outcomes. There were 13 strains (27.7%) with no clinical improvement, including 7 MRSP, 2 MRSA, 3 MRSC, and 1 MRSW. The MRSP strains included A412-ST45, A426-ST433, B479-ST551, and strains with novel STs A561-ST2623, A1002-ST2624, B310-ST2625, and B881-ST2627. These strains harbored 10–13 virulence genes with variations in spa, sdr, and sep, which were also observed in other strains associated with clinical improvement. The MRSA strains B274-ST398 and B169-ST188 displayed distinct virulence profiles (Fig. 3). The MRSC strains A330, B299, and B449 exhibited similar virulence gene profiles. Four virulence genes hld, fbpA, sasD, and sasF were present in the MRSW strain A443.

Discussion

This study investigated genotypic diversity, clinical associations with genotypes, and the concordance with phenotypic antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in clinical MRS isolates from dogs and cats using WGS. This study identified a diverse STs of MRSP, in contrast to previous studies in Thailand, which reported that the most common clone was MRSP-ST45 (clonal complex; CC45) (Chanchaithong et al., 2014; Kadlec et al., 2016). The genetic diversity in the MRSP population may indicate a shift to a more polyclonal MRSP population, as previously reported in European countries (Bergot et al., 2018; Duim et al., 2016; Kjellman et al., 2015; Morais et al., 2023; Robb et al., 2024). To the best of our knowledge, MRSP-ST496 and ST1386 have never been identified in Thailand. They are a member of the clonal complex CC1328 together with ST121 (Kang & Hwang, 2020) and a new ST2622 that is close to them in the SNP-based phylogenetic tree. MRSP-ST496 is an emerging clone in Australia (Worthing et al., 2018) and France (Bergot et al., 2018). The findings of this study indicate that the MRSP-ST496 lineage has many resistance genes and is multidrug resistant, which corroborates findings of previous studies (Bergot et al., 2018; Kang et al., 2020; Nisa et al., 2019). However, the evolutionary pattern may differ from that of other strains, as suggested by the longer branches of the South African and Australian isolates in the SNP-based phylogenetic tree. This highlights the significance of WGS in local area surveillance of novel emerging clones and sharing information with global areas. A greater number of MRSP isolates and genomic data are needed to further verify the clone shift in Thailand.

The high resistance rates to fluoroquinolones and tetracyclines were consistent with the presence of mutations in gyrA and grlA, as well as the diversity of tet genes, and likely reflect the frequent use of these drug classes in this veterinary clinical practice. In MRSP but not in MRSA, the distribution of AMR genes and SCCmec types was associated with ST. MRSA-ST398 carried several SCCmec types and AMR genes, reflecting the high genetic diversification of this lineage. Notably, the presence of SCCmec composite elements with characteristics of both types V and IX in ST398 has previously been reported as a novel SCCmec IX variant in Thai canine isolates (Phumthanakorn et al., 2026). Similarly, as recorded in previous genomic analysis, the population of MRSC was homogenous, carrying only SCCmecV, and had a limited variety of resistance genes (Paterson, 2021). Nevertheless, little is known about the clonal lineage of MRSC distribution in different regions. Therefore, further molecular epidemiological research is required.

In the comparisons of genotypic and phenotypic AMR, an overall concordance of 93.19% was observed. The concordance rate in this study was lower than that recorded in a previous study on canine MRSP (98.3%) (Wegener et al., 2018). Variations may result from differences in AST techniques, AMR databases, software, sequence quality, and read depth (Doyle et al., 2020). The main discrepancy was observed in susceptible isolates harboring resistant genes or major error rates. The concordance rates below 90% were recorded for cefpodoxime, doxycycline, and gentamicin in this study. Low concordance to aminoglycoside (25%) was observed in S. pseudintermedius (Viñes et al., 2022), and the phenotypic gentamicin-susceptibility but genotypic resistance was previously reported in MRSP (Wegener et al., 2018). Several MRSP and MRSC carried β-lactams-, tetracycline- and gentamicin- resistance genes but fell under the intermediate category of the CLSI guidelines. Further studies to establish species-specific break points for the disk diffusion test and collect more data on gene expression and regulation associated with phenotype would be useful for improving AMR gene prediction (Nisa et al., 2019; Wegener et al., 2018).

Knowledge of predominant clones and infection sites of MRS in companion animals is currently limited. This study found that certain MRSP clones, such as ST1386 and ST433, may have the ability to colonize and infect multiple body sites; however, this observation may be influenced by the number of strains available for each ST. MRSA-ST398 was most frequently associated with post-surgical wound infections, underscoring its clinical relevance. In S. aureus, some STs have been shown to cause diverse infections and become predominant clones in specific regions (Chen et al., 2021), which may support the polyclonal shift in MRSP observed here. The recent findings also indicate that neither a high number of virulence genes nor any particular gene, species, or ST were linked to adverse clinical outcomes. Previous studies on MRSA have shown no clear consensus on the relationship between virulence factors and treatment outcomes (Otto, 2012). This study did not assess additional clinical factors that may affect clinical improvement, such as co-infections, immune status, or underlying disease. The limited number of isolates made it difficult to assess the relationship between ST and infection sites, as well as species-specific virulence traits, highlighting the need for further research with larger sample sizes.

Conclusion

This study integrated genotypic information with clinical data to investigate the genetic basis of MRS, including clonal distribution, resistance genes, and virulence factors. Genotypic concordance with phenotypic resistance was evaluated using WGS data. Although high concordance rates for several medications tested was confirmed, discordance with some tested drugs was documented suggesting a potential risk of both major and very major error in the clinical situation. The improvements in species-specific AMR data for Staphylococcus, pathogen typing, and standard setup requirements are necessary for WGS in clinical laboratories. Further research involving a larger number of isolates, which was a limitation of this study, is necessary to monitor the emergence of novel sequence types, clonal shifts, and the spread of AMR and virulence factors in Thailand.
