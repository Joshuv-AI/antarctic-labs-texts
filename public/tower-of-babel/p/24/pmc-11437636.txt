Omega-3 fatty acids protect cartilage from acute injurie by reducing the mechanical sensitivity of chondrocytes
Authors: Fan Chen, Zian Zhang, Wenzhe Wang, Chang Liu, Zhenchao Huang, Chaoqun Yu, Zhen Jia, Haining Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute cartilage injuries, such as intra-articular fractures and blunt impacts, frequently result in chondrocyte death and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, significantly elevating the risk of post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA). Despite advances in treatment, no effective therapies currently exist to fully cure PTOA or halt its progression. This study explores the protective effects of the dietary fatty acid eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) on human primary chondrocytes (HPCs) and cartilage explants exposed to mechanical overload and blunt trauma. HPCs were isolated and subjected to mechanical stretching using BioFlex six-well culture plates, while cartilage explants were subjected to impact loading via a customized drop tower. EPA was incorporated into the culture medium, followed by assays to evaluate cell viability, calcium (Ca²⁺) influx, apoptosis, reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, and collagen type II alpha (Col-2a) expression. EPA treatment markedly decreased chondrocyte mechanical sensitivity, as demonstrated by enhanced cell viability and reduced lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release. Furthermore, EPA inhibited Piezo1 activation, leading to lower intracellular Ca²⁺ concentrations, decreased apoptosis, and diminished ROS levels. In cartilage explants, EPA improved chondrocyte viability, minimized structural damage, and sustained higher Col-2a expression compared to the blunt trauma group. These results indicate that EPA effectively shields chondrocytes and cartilage explants from mechanical overload-induced damage by inhibiting Piezo1 activation and mitigating Ca²⁺ influx, apoptosis, and oxidative stress. The findings suggest that EPA supplementation could offer a promising strategy for preventing PTOA progression following acute cartilage injuries. Further research is warranted to assess the clinical applications of EPA and confirm its efficacy in larger animal models and human trials.Keywords: Posttraumatic osteoarthritis, Chondrocyte injury, Eicosapentaenoic acid, Piezo1

Introduction

Acute cartilage injuries, including intra-articular fractures and blunt impacts on joint cartilage, often result in the immediate death of chondrocytes and altered metabolic gene expression in surviving cells, which are associated with their breakdown. This process initiates local inflammatory responses, leading to progressive chondrocyte death over time [1–3]. These trauma-induced pathological mechanisms drive continuous ECM degradation, positioning cartilage injuries as a significant risk factor for the development of PTOA [4–6]. PTOA accounts for approximately 12% of all osteoarthritis cases, with 23–50% of individuals who sustain traumatic knee joint injuries progressing to PTOA, often receiving a diagnosis up to a decade earlier than those without a history of knee joint damage [7]. Despite advances in understanding PTOA, there are currently no effective methods to fully cure PTOA or to arrest the pathological progression from acute cartilage injury to PTOA [8–10]. Thus, identifying a safe and effective approach to impede PTOA development following mechanical impact-induced acute cartilage injury is of critical importance.

Piezo1, a mechanically sensitive cation channel, converts mechanical signals received by cells into electrical signals through conformational changes in the channel that result in intracellular Ca²⁺ influx [11–13]. Research suggests that Piezo1 expression in chondrocytes regulates endochondral ossification and osteoarthritis progression. As a critical channel for chondrocytes to sense mechanical stress, Piezo1 activation due to mechanical overload leads to Ca²⁺ influx, causing chondrocyte damage through Ca²⁺ overload. Recent studies indicate that mechanical stress-induced activation of Piezo1 contributes to Ca²⁺ influx, promoting ferroptosis in chondrocytes [14–17]. Furthermore, studies suggest that artemisinin can inhibit Piezo1 activation and delay osteoarthritis progression in murine models [18]. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of inhibiting Piezo1 activation as a strategy for protecting cartilage from mechanical overload-induced injuries. However, commonly used Piezo1 inhibitors, such as ruthenium red and GsMTx4, are exogenous chemical substances and peptides whose immunogenicity and limited biocompatibility constrain their clinical applications [19].

Recent research has identified that dietary fatty acids, particularly the omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid EPA, can inhibit Piezo1 function [20, 21]. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether this dietary fatty acid can regulate Piezo1 activation in chondrocytes and mitigate cartilage damage induced by mechanical overload. In this study, we investigate the potential of EPA to suppress Piezo1 activation in chondrocytes, aiming to establish a promising dietary fatty acid-based strategy for preventing PTOA development following acute cartilage injury induced by mechanical impact.

Our findings demonstrate that the dietary fatty acid EPA effectively inhibits Piezo1 function in chondrocytes and prevents mechanical overload-induced cartilage damage. In summary, this study underscores the protective role of dietary fatty acids in acute cartilage injuries caused by mechanical forces and proposes a promising approach for PTOA prevention and treatment.

Materials and methods

Isolation of human primary chondrocytes (HPCs)

Human arthritic cartilage samples were collected post-total knee arthroplasty, selecting cartilage tissue (International Cartilage Repair Society [ICRS] grade ≤ 1) was rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) in a biosafety cabinet and subsequently sectioned into small pieces using a scalpel. The cartilage fragments were digested with 0.2% collagenase for 6 h in an incubator at 37 °C, followed by filtration and centrifugation to isolate primary chondrocytes. The isolated chondrocytes were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 IU/ml penicillin/streptomycin, and cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator.

Stretching of HPCs

To apply mechanical stimulation to HPCs, well-grown chondrocytes were evenly seeded onto BioFlex six-well culture plates coated with gelatin. These plates are equipped with a flexible silicone membrane bottom capable of generating up to 33% maximum mechanical deformation (Flexcell International Corporation, USA). An enhancement of the device described by Gudipaty et al. [12], was employed, enabling the application of a single 33% overload mechanical deformation to HPCs. Following the method by Romero et al. [21], 100 µM of EPA can effectively inhibit the biological function of Piezo1. Therefore, 100 µM EPA was added to the culture medium, incubated overnight, and then subjected to stretching experiments. HPCs without mechanical force application and those subjected only to mechanical force were used as controls.

Preparation and cultivation of human cartilage explants

Under sterile conditions in a biosafety cabinet, full-thickness cartilage explants were obtained by drilling cartilage samples with a Uni-Core system hole punch (Ø = 6 mm, thickness = 2 mm). The explants were cultured individually in 24-well plates, each containing serum-enriched medium, and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. The medium was refreshed every 48 h.

Impact loading of cartilage explants

A customized drop tower, as previously described, was used to apply a 0.56 J impact to the cartilage explants under sterile conditions [22–24]. EPA was added to the culture medium the day before the impact, and the explants were incubated overnight as per the aforementioned protocol. The explants were removed from the petri dish prior to the impact experiment. Explants that were not subjected to impact and those exposed only to impact served as controls.

Cell viability detection

(1) CCK-8 assay: The cytotoxicity of EPA on primary chondrocytes was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Chondrocytes were seeded into 96-well cell culture plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells/well, and 100 µM EPA was added to the medium. Cytotoxicity was analyzed using a CCK-8 kit (Beyotime, China). At 24, 48, and 72 h, 100 µL of DMEM and 10 µL of CCK-8 solution were added to each well. After incubation at 37 °C for 1 h, the absorbance of the solution was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. (2) To evaluate the protective effect of EPA on stretched chondrocytes and impacted cartilage explants, cell viability was assessed using the Live/Dead Cell Staining Kit (MCE, USA). For chondrocytes, the staining solution was added to the wells following the manufacturer’s instructions and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Cell density per unit area was calculated at each time point using ImageJ software. For cartilage explants, the explants were rinsed three times with PBS buffer, sectioned into 0.2 mm thick slices, immersed in the staining solution, and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. Fluorescent images were captured using a BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope system (KEYENCE, Japan), and cell density per unit area was calculated using ImageJ software. (3) LDH assay: LDH release was measured using the LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). After mechanical stretching, chondrocytes were cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, 120 µL of the supernatant from each polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stretching chamber was transferred to a corresponding well of a 96-well plate. The samples were then analyzed using the LDH release reagent provided in the kit, serving as a positive control for LDH release.

Ca2+ imaging

Intracellular Ca²⁺ concentrations were measured using the Fluo-4-AM kit (Beyotime, China). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with Fluo-4-AM working solution at 37 °C for 30 min. Following incubation, fluorescence intensity was observed using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan). The excitation wavelength was set at 488 nm, and the quantification of fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software.

Flow Cytometry

Flow Cytometry: (1) Apoptosis Detection: Apoptosis in primary chondrocytes post-stretching was assessed using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Meilunbio, China). Chondrocytes on PDMS membranes were digested with EDTA-free trypsin according to the manufacturer’s protocol, followed by centrifugation at 1000 g for 5 min to collect the cells. The cells were resuspended in precooled PBS and gently washed by centrifugation, repeating this process twice. The cells were then resuspended in 1× Binding Buffer to a final concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/ml. A 100 µL aliquot of cell suspension (containing approximately 1 × 10⁵ cells) was transferred to a new tube, followed by the addition of 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 10 µL PI. The mixture was gently mixed and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min before analysis using flow cytometry. (2) ROS Detection: The ROS levels in chondrocytes post-stretching were measured using the ROS Detection Kit (Beyotime, China). Cells were treated with 10 µM DCFH-DA, diluted 1:1000 in serum-free medium, and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After digestion with EDTA-free trypsin, cells were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min at room temperature. The cells were washed twice with serum-free medium to remove excess DCFH-DA that had not entered the cells, and ROS levels were then measured using flow cytometry.

RT-qPCR

To evaluate Col-2a mRNA expression levels in primary chondrocytes after stretch treatment, RT-qPCR was conducted. Total RNA was extracted from cells using the TRIzol method and reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Kit (Takara, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the SYBR Green qRT-PCR Kit (Takara, Japan). The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, and annealing at 60 °C for 20 s. The relative expression levels of target genes (GAPDH and Col-2a) were calculated using the comparative CT (ΔΔCT) method, normalized to GAPDH as the internal reference gene. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate, and each sample was analyzed in three technical replicates. The primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Immunofluorescence staining of chondrocytes

Immunofluorescence was employed to detect collagen type II (Col-2a) expression in primary chondrocytes post-stretching. After 72 h of stretching, chondrocytes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, followed by three washes with PBS. Cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, washed three more times with PBS, and then blocked with 5% BSA blocking solution for 30 min. Primary antibodies against Col-2a (1:100 dilution; Proteintech, China) were added, and cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, cells were washed three times for 5 min each with PBS, followed by the addition of Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L Alexa Fluor® 555 (1:500 dilution; Abcam, United Kingdom) for 50 min at room temperature. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min. Fluorescence images were captured using the BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope system (KEYENCE, Japan).

Histological and immunofluorescence evaluation of cartilage explants

Cartilage explants were removed from the culture medium, rinsed three times with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, and sectioned at a thickness of 10 μm using the frozen sectioning technique. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to observe tissue morphology under a light microscope (Nikon, Japan). Immunofluorescence was also conducted to assess the effect of EPA on type II collagen expression in the impacted cartilage explants. After antigen retrieval using sodium citrate, sections were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, followed by three washes with PBS. Sections were blocked with 5% BSA blocking solution for 30 min. Primary and secondary antibodies were applied following the same protocol as in the cellular immunofluorescence section. Fluorescence images were captured using the BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope system (KEYENCE, Japan).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate under identical conditions (n = 3), and data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses and graphical presentations were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 10 software. For comparisons between two groups, the t-test was employed. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was used, followed by post-hoc analysis to evaluate intergroup differences after confirming homogeneity of variance. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Effect of stretch on the area of HPCs

HPCs demonstrated adherent growth on PDMS membranes. We randomly selected 50 cells to measure their area, finding an average of approximately 900 μm² per cell. Upon applying a 30% deformation to the PDMS membrane, the area of the adherent chondrocytes increased to about 1300 μm². However, when the PDMS membrane returned to its original shape, the average cell area significantly decreased to approximately 700 μm² (Fig. 1A). These findings suggest that mechanical stretching effectively induces deformation in chondrocytes, simulating the abnormal deformations caused by external mechanical forces.

EPA reduced Ca2+ influx induced by stretch in HPCs

Previous studies have shown that mechanical stretching deforms chondrocytes, activates the mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo1, and induces Ca²⁺ influx, leading to intracellular Ca²⁺ overload, metabolic changes, and cell death in chondrocytes. We used the Fluo-4 AM Ca²⁺ probe to detect intracellular Ca²⁺ concentrations in each group of chondrocytes. Compared to chondrocytes without mechanical stretching, stretched chondrocytes exhibited a significant increase in intracellular Ca²⁺ concentration. However, chondrocytes pre-treated with 100 µM EPA before stretching showed no significant increase in intracellular Ca²⁺ concentration (Fig. 1B, C). This indicates that pre-treatment with EPA effectively reduces the activation of Piezo1 and decreases intracellular Ca²⁺ levels, consistent with findings reported by Romero et al. [21].

EPA reduced the injury of HPCs induced by stretch

Mechanical stretching of chondrocytes leads to LDH release, an important indicator of cellular injury. Our results indicated that stretched chondrocytes released more LDH compared to unstretched controls. While LDH release also significantly increased in EPA-treated chondrocytes after stretching, the levels were markedly lower than in the untreated stretched group (Fig. 1F).

We further evaluated the protective effect of EPA on chondrocytes subjected to mechanical stretching using live/dead cell staining. Chondrocytes that were not subjected to mechanical stretching exhibited significantly higher viability compared to both the untreated stretched group and the EPA-treated stretched group. Despite notable cell death in EPA-treated chondrocytes post-stretching, their survival rate was significantly higher than in chondrocytes subjected to stretching alone (Fig. 2A, B). These results indicate that EPA improves the survival rate of chondrocytes following mechanical stretching.

Excessive mechanical stretching can induce chondrocyte apoptosis, a process mediated by Ca²⁺ overload. To determine whether EPA inhibits Piezo1 and reduces apoptosis induced by mechanical force and Ca²⁺ overload, we used flow cytometry to assess apoptosis levels in each group of chondrocytes. Stretched chondrocytes showed significantly higher levels of apoptosis compared to unstretched controls, with more mechanical damage evident. Although EPA-treated chondrocytes also exhibited increased apoptosis compared to unstretched controls, the apoptosis level was significantly reduced compared to chondrocytes subjected to mechanical stretching alone (Fig. 2C, D). These findings demonstrate that EPA effectively reduces apoptosis induced by mechanical stretching.

Mechanical stretching also elevates reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in chondrocytes, leading to cellular damage. Given EPA’s known antioxidant properties as an unsaturated fatty acid, we utilized a ROS detection kit to examine EPA’s inhibitory effect on intracellular ROS levels caused by excessive mechanical stress. Our results showed that stretched chondrocytes exhibited significantly higher ROS levels compared to unstretched controls. However, EPA-treated chondrocytes did not display a significant increase in ROS levels compared to unstretched controls (Fig. 2E, F). Notably, a high ROS content was observed in chondrocytes isolated from osteoarthritis (OA) patients, even without mechanical stretching, likely due to pre-existing high ROS levels in these cells.

EPA alleviated the phenotypic changes of HPCs induced by stretch

Col-2a is a primary component of the extracellular matrix in hyaline cartilage, with its synthesis and degradation regulated by chondrocytes. Previous studies have reported that abnormal mechanical forces can lead to metabolic changes in chondrocytes, including a significant reduction in Col-2a synthesis. To evaluate the protective effect of EPA on chondrocyte synthetic metabolism, we measured Col-2a mRNA levels using RT-qPCR and assessed Col-2a expression levels via immunofluorescence. Compared to chondrocytes that did not undergo mechanical stretching, the mRNA levels and expression of Col-2a were significantly reduced in the mechanically stretched group. However, EPA-treated chondrocytes exhibited a significant increase in Col-2a mRNA levels and expression compared to the untreated stretched group (Fig. 3A, B, C). These results suggest that EPA effectively maintains the synthetic metabolism of chondrocytes under mechanical stress.

The addition of EPA reduced impact-induced HPCs death

Blunt trauma is a significant contributor to post-traumatic osteoarthritis, as it can cause immediate chondrocyte death, initiate apoptosis, and disrupt synthetic metabolism. To investigate the protective effects of EPA against blunt trauma, we employed a drop tower to induce blunt trauma in cartilage explants (Fig. 4A) and subsequently cultured these explants in medium containing 100 µM EPA for 14 days. Chondrocyte viability was assessed using a live/dead staining kit. The results indicated a significant decrease in cell survival in cartilage explants subjected to blunt trauma compared to those that did not experience trauma, on both the first and fourteenth days. However, cartilage explants treated with EPA showed a significantly higher cell survival rate than those subjected to blunt trauma alone (Fig. 4B, C).

The addition of EPA alleviated the cartilage destruction caused by impact

Blunt trauma leads to structural damage in cartilage and alters chondrocyte metabolism. Cartilage explants cultured for fourteen days were cryosectioned and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E). Compared to the control group, cartilage explants subjected to blunt trauma exhibited notable surface compression and structural damage. Cartilage explants without EPA treatment post-injury showed extensive cartilage damage. In contrast, while explants treated with EPA after injury also exhibited surface compression, the extent of cartilage structural damage was significantly reduced compared to the blunt trauma-only group (Fig. 5A). Additionally, immunofluorescence analysis revealed a significant loss of Col-2a in cartilage explants subjected to blunt trauma compared to non-injured controls. Although the expression of Col-2a in EPA-treated cartilage explants post-injury was lower than in non-injured explants, it was notably higher than in the blunt trauma-only group (Fig. 5B, C).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the protective effects of the dietary fatty acid EPA on chondrocytes and cartilage explants subjected to mechanical overload and blunt trauma. EPA, a polyunsaturated fatty acid known for its various health benefits, including its role in anti-inflammatory processes and regulation of cell membrane stiffness [25, 26]. Some studies have already demonstrated that EPA can alleviate the progression of osteoarthritis, but the mechanisms are not yet fully understood [27–29]. Our findings demonstrate that EPA can effectively reduce mechanical sensitivity, inhibit Piezo1 activation, and mitigate the damage induced by mechanical stress in chondrocytes. Additionally, EPA was shown to protect cartilage explants from impact-induced damage, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent for preventing PTOA.

The activation of the mechanically sensitive ion channel Piezo1 is influenced by mechanical deformation of the cell membrane. Additionally, the fatty acid composition of the cell membrane affects its mechanical properties [30–33]. The study by Romero et al. [21], indicates that EPA inhibits Piezo1 function by accelerating its inactivation time, which effectively reduces Piezo1-mediated calcium influx induced by mechanical stimulation. The results indicate that EPA treatment significantly reduces the mechanical sensitivity of chondrocytes. This is evident from the improved cell viability and reduced LDH release observed in EPA-treated chondrocytes subjected to mechanical stretching. The decrease in intracellular Ca2+ concentration further supports the notion that EPA inhibits Piezo1 activation.

In addition to reducing Ca2+ influx, EPA also decreases apoptosis and ROS levels in chondrocytes. Flow cytometry analysis revealed significantly lower apoptosis rates and ROS levels in EPA-treated chondrocytes compared to those subjected to mechanical force alone. This highlights EPA’s potential in reducing cellular stress and damage associated with mechanical overload.

Our study also shows that EPA protects cartilage explants from impact-induced damage. EPA supplementation in the culture medium improved chondrocyte viability and reduced structural damage in cartilage explants subjected to blunt trauma. Histological analysis revealed that EPA-treated explants exhibited less structural damage and higher Col-2a expression compared to the trauma-only group. These results suggest that EPA can mitigate the adverse effects of mechanical impact on cartilage, potentially lowering the risk of PTOA development.

The protective effects of EPA observed in this study are attributed to its ability to inhibit Piezo1 activation, thereby reducing Ca2+ influx, apoptosis, and oxidative stress in chondrocytes. By maintaining chondrocyte structural integrity and metabolic function, EPA helps preserve the extracellular matrix and overall cartilage health. These findings underscore the potential of EPA as a dietary therapeutic strategy for preventing and managing PTOA following acute cartilage injuries.

Despite these promising results, several limitations must be addressed. First, the experiments were conducted in vitro, and results may not fully translate to in vivo conditions. Future research should explore the effects of EPA in animal models of acute cartilage injury and PTOA to validate these findings. Additionally, the long-term impact of EPA supplementation on cartilage health and PTOA progression needs further investigation. Determining the optimal dosage and administration route of EPA will be crucial for its clinical application.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study provides evidence that EPA can effectively protect chondrocytes and cartilage explants from mechanical overload-induced damage. The inhibition of Piezo1 activation and the reduction of Ca2+overload, apoptosis, and oxidative stress are key mechanisms underlying EPA’s protective effects. These findings suggest that dietary supplementation with EPA may offer a promising approach for preventing the progression of PTOA following acute cartilage injuries. Further research is needed to explore the clinical applications of EPA and to validate its efficacy in larger animal models and human trials.
