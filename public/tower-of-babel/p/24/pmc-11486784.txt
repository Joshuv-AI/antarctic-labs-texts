A Rubik’s Cube-Inspired Pedagogical Tool for Teaching and Learning Engineering Ethics
Authors: Yuqi Peng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo facilitate engineering students’ understanding of engineering ethics and support instructors in developing course content, this study introduces an innovative educational tool drawing inspiration from the Rubik’s Cube metaphor. This Engineering Ethics Knowledge Rubik’s Cube (EEKRC) integrates six key aspects—ethical theories, codes of ethics, ethical issues, engineering disciplines, stakeholders, and life cycle—identified through an analysis of engineering ethics textbooks and courses across the United States, Singapore, and China. This analysis underpins the selection of the six aspects, reflecting the shared and unique elements of engineering ethics education in these regions. In an engineering ethics course, the EEKRC serves multiple functions: it provides visual support for grasping engineering ethics concepts, acts as a pedagogical guide for both experienced and inexperienced educators in course design, offers a complementary assessment method for evaluating students learning outcomes, and assists as a reference for students engaging in ethical analysis.Keywords: Engineering ethics knowledge, Pedagogical tool, Learning assessment, Rubik’s cube

Introduction

One primary goal of engineering ethics education is to enhance students’ acquisition of ethical knowledge, as highlighted by Harris et al. (1996), Newberry (2004), and Davis (2005). Despite these efforts, a study by Harding et al., (2013) indicates little improvement in the ethical knowledge levels among engineering students. This stagnation is attributed to issues such as unclear coordination of ethics content, which makes it difficult for students to comprehend the subject and for instructors to organize course materials effectively. In addressing these challenges, this paper presents the theoretical conception of a novel pedagogical tool for engineering ethics education, inspired by the Rubik’s Cube metaphor,1 named the Engineering Ethics Knowledge Rubik’s Cube (EEKRC).

The EEKRC synthesizes six critical aspects of engineering ethics: Ethical Theories lay the philosophical groundwork for ethical analysis, while the Codes of Ethics operationalize these theories into professional guidelines, grounding ethical conduct within engineering. Ethical Issues, encompassing both micro and macro dimensions, facilitate a thorough investigation of ethical dilemmas, whereas Engineering Disciplines address these ethical challenges across specific fields. Stakeholders highlight the impact of engineering decisions on various affected individuals or groups, and the Life Cycle aspect ensures continuous ethical consideration across all project stages. This model, informed by an analysis of engineering ethics textbooks and courses across the United States, Singapore, and China, aims to align with engineering students’ learning preferences and organize the broadly referenced yet previously ambiguous categorized content.

These six aspects have been organized within a Rubik’s Cube-like structure (see Fig. 1). The Rubik’s Cube is chosen as the model due to its popularity as a puzzle among engineering students, proven to enhance their interest in learning (Cornock, 2015). The six identified aspects of engineering ethics knowledge correspond to the six colored faces of the Rubik’s Cube. The mechanism of the Rubik’s Cube, which allows for the arrangement of differently colored modules through twisting, metaphorically emphasizes the importance of examining and interrelating various aspects of knowledge when comprehending ethical issues in engineering.

Three-dimensional Engineering Ethics Knowledge Rubik’s Cube

The EEKRC can be adapted into two-dimensional (2-D), three-dimensional (3-D), or digital formats,2 depending on the resources available to instructors. The simplest form is a 2-D diagram of six 3 × 3 squares, illustrated in Fig. 2.3 This paper, focusing on the 2-D model, employs its static layout, indicating that the rearrangement of segments does not affect its classroom application. It outlines the process of constructing the EEKRC, including the motivations for its creation, the selection rationale for the six aspects, and the specific content of each square. Furthermore, this paper explores the EEKRC’s application in engineering ethics courses, highlighting its utility in aiding instructors with course planning, assisting students in ethical analyses, and serving as an assessment tool for students learning. Additionally, the suitability and potential obstacles of using the Rubik’s Cube model to organize content and enhance instructional methods in engineering ethics will be discussed.

Two-dimensional Engineering Ethics Knowledge Rubik’s Cube

Background

To enhance engineering students’ understanding of ethics in their fields and tackle associated challenges, the EEKRC is proposed.

A Key Learning Objective in Engineering Ethics Education

Amid ongoing discussions, scholars have identified multiple objectives for engineering ethics education (see Martin et al., 2021). Harris et al. (1996) outline comprehensive goals, including: stimulating students’ ethical imagination, recognizing ethical issues, analyzing ethical concepts and principles, managing ambiguity, taking ethics seriously, enhancing sensitivity to ethical issues, broadening knowledge of relevant standards, improving ethical judgment, and bolstering ethical determination. Newberry (2004) categorizes these objectives into three themes: emotional engagement, intellectual engagement, and specific knowledge. Davis (2005) identifies the objectives of engineering ethics education as enhancing students’ sensitivity to professional standards, improving knowledge for ethical issue resolution, enriching judgment, and bolstering commitment to professional standards.

The acquisition of engineering ethics knowledge is a central theme in these educational objectives, essential for grounding engineering students in ethical decision-making and behavior. Despite its significance, its realization is often insufficient. Many institutions worldwide still have not incorporated engineering ethics into their curricula (Clancy, 2020), and those that have face challenges in fulfilling this objective. Harding et al. (2013) underscore this challenge, revealing through a two-round Survey of Engineering Ethical Development (SEED) involving 450 engineering undergraduates across 16 institutions that students’ ethical knowledge showed little progress between their freshman/sophomore years and their junior/senior years. Moreover, instances of cheating increased, indicating a need for more effective ethics education within engineering programs.

Barriers to Ethical Knowledge Acquisition Among Engineering Students

(1) Challenges in Engineering Ethics Memorization for Students

Harding et al. (2013) highlight that a possible cause for the lack of progress in engineering ethics proficiency among students could be their difficulty in retaining knowledge. To address this, the EEKRC acts as a taxonomy to organize and visually represent engineering ethics knowledge, aiding in memorization. Knowledge classification, a method widely used in engineering (El-Diraby et al., 2005; Zeng & Chan, 2004), offers benefits like standardized terminology, improved understanding of knowledge interrelations, and gap identification within a domain (Usman et al., 2017; Vegas et al., 2009). The EEKRC is designed to leverage these advantages, helping students categorize and remember engineering ethics knowledge effectively.

Comparable efforts in other fields, such as Seedhouse’s (1998) Ethics Grid for healthcare and its application in educational research by Stutchbury and Fox (2009), have shown the efficacy of frameworks in facilitating ethical reasoning and integrity. Unlike these models, which focus on ethical decision-making, the EEKRC uniquely emphasizes the integration of engineering and ethics knowledge, crucial for engineering students to improve their grasp and application of ethical knowledge in their discipline.

(2) Lack of Interest in Engineering Ethics Among Students

Engineering students’ engagement with ethical knowledge often suffers due to a predominant focus on technical skills, leading many to overlook the importance of ethics (Sax, 2004; Colby & Sullivan, 2008; Cech, 2014). Newberry (2004) finds that students frequently perceive ethical issues as irrelevant and uninteresting, with some choosing engineering to avoid such topics. The abstract nature of ethical theories and their perceived lack of alignment with practical engineering further alienate students (Herkert, 2000). Voss (2013) points out that, for students accustomed to laboratory work, quantitative analysis, and clear objectives, engineering ethics may be considered a “messy subject,” difficult to capture their interest.

To counteract this disinterest, the EEKRC acts as a relatable visual heuristic, strengthening the ethics-engineering nexus. The Rubik’s Cube successfully used in STEM education (Valmari, 2006; Zeng et al., 2018), has been shown to enhance enjoyment and understanding of complex theories (Cornock, 2015). Visual aids, preferred by engineering students for simplifying learning (Fang et al., 2017; Felder & Silverman, 1988; Felder & Spurlin, 2005), also help ensure the completeness and coherence of knowledge (Bobek & Tversky, 2016). By employing the Rubik’s Cube as a model, the goal is to increase students’ ability to engage with and analyze engineering ethics, boosting their interest and self-efficacy in the subject.

(3) Issues with Instructions and Assessments in Engineering Ethics

Bairaktarova and Woodcock (2017) argue that the lack of student progress in learning engineering ethics stems from instructors not using effective teaching and assessment methods. A wealth of literature and initiatives exists on pedagogical strategies in engineering ethics, including case-based studies, teaching by ethical codes, and active learning (Colby & Sullivan, 2008; Hess & Fore, 2018; Wilson, 2013), though some methods have been deemed suboptimal (Kreiner et al., 2004; Lawlor, 2007).4 The EEKRC is proposed as a complementary approach to course instruction and assessment, with Sect. "Guiding Students in Ethical Analysis" detailing its integration with case studies for students’ ethical analysis.

Traditional assessment methods like the Defining Issues Test (DIT) (Rest, 1979) and its iterations DIT-2 (Rest et al., 1999), along with SEED and the Engineering and Science Issues Test (ESIT) (Borenstein et al., 2010), are often used only for pre- and/or post-course evaluations, not throughout the course (Hess & Fore, 2018). Martin et al. (2021) identify key assessment issues in engineering ethics education: (1) ethical components are often omitted or simplified into pass/fail judgments, with some instructors overlooking evaluation activities (Keefer et al., 2014); (2) instructors may avoid standardized assessment methods due to unfamiliarity with ethics education, time constraints, and questionable relevance to diverse student populations (Bielefeldt et al., 2016; Davis & Feinerman, 2012); (3) standardized tests, primarily Western-focused, fail to account for the diversity in culture, gender, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status (Zhu et al., 2014), and miss feminist and value-based perspectives. While the EEKRC may not resolve all identified issues, it offers a flexible and adaptable approach to supplement standardized methods. By enabling continuous, nuanced assessments that incorporate diverse perspectives, as discussed in Sect. "Real-Time Assessment of Student Learning Outcomes", it aims to enhance students’ ethical understanding and accommodate varied demographics.

(4) Inadequacies in Instructors’ Proficiency in Engineering Ethics Education

Instructors’ proficiency in delivering engineering ethics significantly influences student learning, yet challenges persist. Martin et al. (2021) identify a discrepancy between the depth of ethics coverage by instructors and students’ perceptions, often seen as oversimplified by the latter. This issue may stem from instructors’ limited training in ethics, complicating their ability to present complex ethical discussions effectively and integrate them with technical content (Barry & Herkert, 2014). The shortage of pedagogical support and the extensive preparation time further complicate teaching ethics (Harding et al., 2009; Walczak et al., 2010). Collaborative teaching with experts from philosophy or social sciences is suggested as a solution, though it requires substantial resources and coordination (Bombaerts et al., 2021). The EEKRC enhances instructional methodologies by providing a structured model and streamlines ethics integration into engineering curricula. It helps instructors of varying experience levels in course preparation and execution, as detailed in Sect. "Facilitating Instructors in Course Planning".

What Should be Taught in Engineering Ethics Course?

Determining the content for engineering ethics education remains a critical yet unresolved issue. While ethical theories and codes of ethics play a foundational role in shaping the courses, focusing solely on them is insufficient for a comprehensive ethical education. Effective curricula should equip students with the knowledge and skills to address contemporary ethical challenges, incorporating specific disciplined topics and promoting cultural awareness in a diverse global context (Li & Fu, 2012).

Professional responsibility, a key component of engineering ethics education in the United States, covers a broad spectrum of issues from public safety to bribery (Herkert, 2000; Rabins, 1998). However, conventional education often prioritizes individual moral dilemmas over wider societal concerns, such as public policy, cultural diversity, and sustainable development (Herkert, 2000; Hess & Fore, 2018; Mitcham & Englehardt, 2019). Increasingly, there is agreement on the importance of situating engineering ethics within the broader contexts of science, technology, and society (Herkert, 2001, 2005; Martin et al., 2021).

Cao’s (2015) comparative study on engineering ethics education in the US and China, involving online surveys across 26 universities, reveals shared ethical values like natural ethics between humans and nature; discourse ethics among engineers, colleagues, employers, and clients; and responsible ethics between engineers and society. They both recognize the diversity of normative ethics: deontological, functional, and environmental ethics. Despite commonalities, educational emphases differ; the US focuses on professional virtues and behavior, guided by codes from NSPE and other professional societies such as IEEE, whereas China leans on its philosophical traditions and moral principles, integrating cultural values and historical insights (Cao, 2015; Zhang & Zhu, 2021). This variation highlights the need for engineering ethics education to consider different cultural and professional settings.

In essence, engineering ethics courses should strive for a curriculum that balances ethical theories, professional codes, and practical applications, while being mindful of cultural diversity and the ethical challenges posed by technological advancements. Despite ongoing debates about standardizing course content, this study advocates for the EEKRC as an effective tool that simplifies instructional preparation and enhances students’ comprehension of engineering ethics knowledge.

The Six Aspects of the Engineering Ethics Knowledge Rubik’s Cube

The six aspects of EEKRC are conceptualized based on an examination of engineering ethics textbooks, relevant scholarly literature, and informal expert input, tailored to engineering students’ study habits and content familiarity. This study analyzes the latest editions of six leading engineering ethics textbooks,5 evenly divided between the US (US1-3) and China (CN1-3), along with textbooks (US4 and CN4) used in specific American and Chinese engineering ethics courses. This examination aims to identify both common themes and cultural differences in engineering ethics education. The analysis of these textbooks indicates the importance and comprehensiveness of the EEKRC’s six aspects. Ethical theories, codes of ethics, ethical issues, and engineering disciplines are notably emphasized across all textbooks. Stakeholders and life cycle aspects receive comparatively less focus but are nonetheless incorporated into the textbooks. Simultaneously, not all content in these textbooks is covered by EEKRC. For instance, the emphasis on professionalism in the initial chapters of American textbooks, as well as the discussions on the concepts of engineering and ethics in the early chapters of Chinese textbooks, are not explicitly included in the EEKRC. This indicates that the EEKRC captures the majority, but not all, of the elements frequently referenced in these textbooks. Furthermore, the six aspects of the EEKRC are not predetermined and subsequently forced to align with each chapter of the textbooks. Selection Rationale and Content of the EEKRC’s Six Aspects

Ethical Theories

Most of the eight textbooks acknowledge ethical theory as a crucial tool or framework for analyzing ethical dilemmas, often supported by ethical values. American textbooks typically dedicate entire chapters to ethical theories, while Chinese textbooks introduce these ideas more briefly, often in subsections. Common theories include deontology, utilitarianism, and virtue ethics, with justice theory, care ethics, social contract theory, duty ethics, and rights ethics also mentioned. Topics like egoism, anthropocentrism, responsibility ethics, and moral relativism are discussed to help students navigate ethical issues in engineering. Notably, while Chinese textbooks focus on concepts like Confucian ethics, US1 and US3 also explore non-Western philosophical ideas. The diverse and comprehensive treatment of ethical theory reflects its essential role in ethics education.

The inclusion and effectiveness of ethical theory in education are debated, particularly regarding its understanding and application by engineering students as mentioned. This paper stands for the importance of ethical theory in the curriculum as a foundational component for ethical analysis and judgment, and suggests exploring effective teaching methods to improve student understanding. For example, US4 uses abundant case studies for each theory, and CN4 compares several theories in one scenario, enhancing student insight. An alternative approach teaches values derived from these theories, such as humanity, autonomy, utility, virtue, care, and justice, as seen in the textbooks. For instance, CN1 emphasizes humanitarianism and the analysis of ethical values through ends and means, without focusing solely on conventional theories.

Codes of Ethics

Codes of ethics, essential for engineering ethics education, outline the profession’s rights, duties, and responsibilities, promoting ethical unity (Colby & Sullivan, 2008; Li & Fu, 2012). The most frequently cited codes come from organizations like NSPE, IEEE, ASME, and ASCE, with US1 also referencing AIChE and US4 mentioning the ACM code. US textbooks often incorporate brief references to these codes throughout the text and append them in full, whereas Chinese textbooks integrate excerpts or summaries directly into relevant chapters.

While crucial, codes of ethics alone cannot ensure ethical competence or replace thorough ethics education. An effective response involves analyzing codes of ethics in-depth and incorporating them with various elements of ethical content. US1 and CN4 offer profound discussions on codes of ethics, exploring their application, contradictions, and resolutions across various professional and geographical contexts. CN4 specifically focuses on codes of ethics for civil engineers, while CN2 addresses codes related to research and business in engineering. Several textbooks (US2, US3, CN3) and courses emphasize both local and international codes of ethics, for example, US1 presents the code of ethics from the Japanese Society of Civil Engineers.

Ethical Issues

Table 1 shows extensive coverage of ethical issues in engineering across textbooks from micro and macro perspectives, often discussed simultaneously.6 For example, the concept of responsibility is examined at both the individual and community levels in engineering. This dual approach is highlighted in US4, where Part 3 (Chapters 7–12) blends micro and macro perspectives on six key ethical issues, while the latter part, “Engineering and Society” (Chapters 13–16), focuses mainly on a macro perspective.

Through these textbooks, specific ethical issues that are often discussed are summarized: responsibility, risk and uncertainty, engineers in organizations and workplace, conflicts of interest, whistleblowing, cost–benefit analysis, the problem of many hands, privacy, trust and reliability, honesty, justice in engineering, engineering leadership, technology assessment (technology as a social experiment; new technology), attitudes towards technology, ethics of artifacts, engineering and the environment, and global engineering. Several textbooks (US4, CN2, CN4) initiate discussions on other topics like the integration of engineering with ethics, the role of engineers in their profession, and the historical backdrop of engineering ethics. The selection of ethical topics for course content is influenced by course duration, context, and the backgrounds of both students and instructors. Contents of the Engineering Ethics Knowledge Rubik’s Cube

Engineering Disciplines

Ethical challenges in engineering range from universal issues, like the problem of many hands, to discipline-specific concerns, such as data privacy in computer science and engineering. Including a variety of engineering disciplines in the ethics curriculum illustrates both widespread and specialized ethical dilemmas. This approach aims to equip students for ethical decision-making within their specific fields and in broader contexts, taking into account codes of ethics, stakeholders and life cycle pertinent to various engineering industries.

The ethical issues presented in each textbook are relevant to particular engineering specializations, as evidenced by the case studies or examples. Certain textbooks feature dedicated chapters discussing specific engineering disciplines, which are labeled in Table 1. Notably, CN2 and CN3 allocate chapters to explore issues unique to specific engineering disciplines, including civil, hydraulic, chemical, nuclear, information and computer, environmental, biomedical, and genetic engineering. This approach reflects a feature of China’s education system where students specialize early in their undergraduate studies, unlike the US’s initial general engineering focus. CN4 and its associated course focus exclusively on civil engineering. Ethical research and education tailored to specific engineering disciplines already exist, but they are not yet widespread. The curriculum’s emphasis varies by country, with China notably focusing on hydraulic engineering. Recent textbooks or editions (US2, CN3) increasingly discuss computing, and big data ethics, and advocate for a multidisciplinary approach, highlighting evolving priorities in engineering ethics education.

Stakeholders

Textbook reviews indicate that stakeholder and life cycle concepts are underemphasized compared to other ethical topics. This paper argues for a heightened focus on these concepts in engineering ethics education, suggesting their integration into course content and ethical analysis. With engineering management and related courses emphasizing stakeholder and life cycle analysis, students become adept at considering projects from stakeholders’ perspectives and through all stages. Merging these concepts with other ethical topics could foster deeper consideration of ethical impacts on stakeholders throughout a project.

CN2 identifies stakeholders in engineering activities as including project investors, undertakers, organizers, designers, constructors, and product users. US3 introduces stakeholder theory, highlighting stakeholders such as employees, customers, dealers, suppliers, local communities, and the general public. Additionally, Chinese textbooks spotlight the engineering community concept, which is also recognized by Zhang and Zhu (2021), encompassing workers, engineers, investors, managers, and community residents. Concurrently, the conception of stakeholders in ethical discourse is evolving; contemporary ethical frameworks increasingly acknowledge the interests of future generations. All these textbooks address considerations for future generations within discussions on environmental ethics, distributive justice, and intergenerational justice. US1 and US2 reference IEEE and NSPE Codes of Ethics provisions, respectively, highlighting engineers’ responsibility to safeguard the environment for future generations. US3 addresses the challenge of securing informed consent from all those affected by engineering decisions, especially future generations. Engineers are encouraged not only to consider direct stakeholders but also to be cognizant of indirect or remote groups, such as those outside the immediate project area. This is particularly pertinent in the context of global disparities.

Other sections across various textbooks explore stakeholder involvement in contexts such as the engineer-manager relationship, conflicts of interest, collective responsibility within the engineering profession, and public participation. CN4 delves into the impact of codes of ethics on stakeholders, the ethical duties engineers owe to various stakeholders, and the ethical responsibilities that stakeholders undertake. This paper encourages exploring diverse perspectives on stakeholder considerations in engineering ethics, as presented in these textbooks, rather than identifying exclusive stakeholders or suggesting that every ethical issue necessitates stakeholder consideration.

Life Cycle

Ethical challenges may arise at any stage of an engineering project’s life cycle, encompassing planning, design, development, operation, maintenance, and disposal. Both US3 and CN2 enumerate ethical issues spanning from conceptual design to disassembly and recycling, including patent infringements during the design phase and safety and environmental concerns during operation and demolition. These textbooks frequently reference the life cycle within the context of sustainable development, particularly emphasizing recycling. US2 and US3 include subsections on life cycle analysis, a systematic evaluation of environmental impacts throughout a product’s or activity’s entire life cycle.

In contrast to US textbooks, Chinese textbooks place greater emphasis on the life cycle concept and stakeholder involvement, engaging in comprehensive discussions. For example, CN1 explores various impacts of engineering projects, categorizing them as continuous or temporary, explicit or implicit, direct or indirect, avoidable or unavoidable, and repairable or irreparable, in relation to different project stages. CN2 examines the interactions and ethical dilemmas among stakeholders during the decision-making and construction phases. Meanwhile, CN4 proposes a novel management model for civil engineering that encompasses the entire life cycle of buildings, from planning and surveying to design, construction, usage, demolition, and the recycling and safe disposal of building materials, thereby addressing the multifaceted ethical considerations inherent in engineering projects.

In practical application, instructors and students are encouraged to modify the content to align with their contextual needs.

Application Strategy of the Engineering Ethics Knowledge Rubik’s Cube in the Course

Beyond its role in structuring and presenting engineering ethics knowledge, EEKRC is integrated into various phases of engineering ethics instruction. Specifically, the model is employed by instructors for course design before the commencement of classes. During the course, students leverage the EEKRC under instructors’ guidance for conducting case studies. At the course’s conclusion, both students and instructors collaboratively refine the EEKRC, enhancing the depth of analysis for final assignments and prospective projects. Moreover, the EEKRC is utilized at each of these stages as a tool to evaluate students’ comprehension of engineering ethics knowledge.

Facilitating Instructors in Course Planning

The EEKRC can be utilized across various types of engineering ethics courses to construct teaching content.7 It is essential to determine the relevant engineering specialties according to the course nature and student demographics. Additionally, the selection of ethical issues for discussion should take into account the geographic and cultural context, and the instructor’s expertise, integrating stakeholder analysis and life cycle considerations. The choice of suitable ethical theories and codes of ethics to facilitate these discussions is also crucial. The emphasis within each type of course will vary based on its specific nature and duration.

The three courses listed in Table 2 provide a framework for constructing content in similar courses. They incorporate the six aspects of EEKRC to different degrees, with most of them successfully integrating these aspects. The lessons are tailored to the geographical and student contexts, incorporating regionally relevant cases. For instance, the Civil Engineering Ethics course at CSU is a stand-alone course for part-time professional postgraduates in this discipline. It centers on five topics of significant interest and relevance to engineering practitioners, ensuring in-depth exploration of each topic to enhance these students’ comprehension. The course integrates EEKRC aspects under main themes, particularly from stakeholder and life cycle perspectives, to facilitate engineers’ understanding of ethics in engineering practice.

The Engineers in Society course at NTU, an STS-type offering for first-year engineering students, focuses more on the macro-ethical implications of engineering professions and technologies on society, humanity, and the environment. The integration of EEKRC aspects within each unit is moderate, typically encompassing two aspects to ensure clarity and comprehensibility for first-year cohort. Ethical theories and codes of ethics are discussed in limited depth, through one or two items followed by case studies. It introduces ethical, social, and legal issues across engineering disciplines from historical and future perspectives, with brief mentions of stakeholders and life cycle concept.

The Ethics and Engineering course at TAMU, a stand-alone course designed for junior or senior undergraduates, includes detailed explorations of several ethical theories and codes of ethics across various industries in global and local contexts. These components are closely integrated into class discussions on ethical issues at both micro and macro levels which are tailored to students’ backgrounds, like specific discussions on computer and petroleum engineering. Additionally, stakeholder and life cycle considerations are seamlessly incorporated into the instruction.

Instructors can design their courses by referencing the content structures of the above courses or by mapping EEKRC content to other courses. This helps identify any additional topics or components needed to meet the course objectives.

Guiding Students in Ethical Analysis

A common teaching method in engineering ethics involves coupling ethical analysis with case studies to improve students’ understanding and application of knowledge in this area. Yet, students frequently struggle to initiate ethical analysis, especially when it involves integrating ethical theories. Introducing students to analyzing ethical cases through the EEKRC, followed by having them use it for final assignments and potentially in their future projects, aims to facilitate their proficiency in engineering ethics.

Using the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster8 as a case study and referencing the EEKRC in Fig. 3, instructors can detail the incident and then guide students to identify ethical issues across the EEKRC’s six aspects, highlighting critical factors of the event. The analysis further delves into specific topics, like aerospace engineers’ responsibilities. Through relevant codes of ethics and ethical theories such as deontology and utilitarianism, this approach examines engineers’ duties to stakeholders, their pivotal roles or oversights at various project phases, and the wider engineering community’s obligations. Students are also prompted to consider broader topics like the sustainability of mega-scale projects.

Furthermore, the EEKRC aids students in conducting ethical analyses for their final assignments, acting not as a constraint but as a foundation for topic selection and outline development by following a sequence: selecting engineering field(s), identifying moral issues within the field(s), choosing the ones student is interested in, considering applicable ethical theories and codes of ethics, and focusing on specific stakeholders and life cycle stages. If students already have a topic, either self-chosen or assigned, they can use the EEKRC to ensure comprehensiveness, following a similar approach but with an emphasis on uncovering any overlooked aspects.

Instructors can revisit the EEKRC at the end of the course to reinforce students’ understanding, by encouraging students to inquire about specific ethical knowledge within the EEKRC. Additionally, instructors can motivate students to construct their own EEKRCs, tailored to their professional backgrounds and interests, for application in future project planning and analysis. In this context, the EEKRC serves as a tool for stimulating thought and raising awareness of ethical issues, applied in a manner akin to the previously described approach.

Real-Time Assessment of Student Learning Outcomes

The EEKRC serves as an assessment tool for students learning across the pre-course, in-course, and post-course phases. It involves prompting students to fill in the subcomponents of six aspects of the EEKRC, thereby gauging their comprehension of engineering ethics knowledge. This assessment can be conducted during lectures or tutorials, with the support of teaching assistants (TAs). By reviewing the students’ completed EEKRCs, both instructors and TAs can track students’ mastery of the course material.

In all three courses mentioned, TAs’ involvements varied in degree. Notably, in the courses at NTU and TAMU, tutorials complemented the main lectures, where TAs assisted students in comprehending course content and facilitated activities such as group presentations. Utilizing the EEKRC for assessing teaching effectiveness in tutorials offers multiple benefits: Firstly, since tutorials are largely independently and flexibly structured by TAs, they offer a conducive environment for conducting learning assessments without impacting the continuity of the lectures. Secondly, tutorials facilitated by TAs offer a more direct and immediate interaction with students compared to lectures. Should the EEKRC assessment highlight specific learning patterns or challenges among students, TAs are well-positioned to provide prompt clarifications or make necessary adjustments. Furthermore, it is imperative to equip TAs with specialized guidance on how to effectively teach engineering ethics (Peng et al., 2024), for which the EEKRC can serve as an aid.

In a preliminary study of the EEKRC application within the course at TAMU, the author allocated 5 min to introduce the EEKRC during a tutorial class at the beginning and mid of the semester, followed by a 20 min period for students to complete it. It took approximately 30 min to review the EEKRCs completed by 30 students. The assessment indicated that students initially possessed limited knowledge of engineering ethics, but exhibited improvement during the course, gaining a better grasp of every aspect of EEKRC. Particularly, students frequently mentioned codes of ethics in local areas and the university, underscoring the relevance of content related to their own identities. The EEKRC also highlighted students’ misconceptions about ethical theories, providing the TA with immediate feedback and opportunities for corrective instruction. A comprehensive evaluation of student learning at the course’s conclusion is currently being planned. This assessment will evaluate students’ improvement in knowledge mastery, their preference and attention to specific knowledge areas, their ability to construct a cohesive body of knowledge in this subject, and their propensity to apply engineering ethics. Throughout the assessment process in the course, both instructors and students gain a timely understanding of student’s learning progress and performance, thereby boosting their confidence in mastering ethics.

Given the acceptable time and human resources costs associated with implementing the EEKRC, this approach could be integrated into the main lectures of engineering ethics programs, even those without tutorials. If necessary, it can be combined with other assessment methods to enhance evaluation accuracy and comprehensiveness.

Discussion

The EEKRC is designed to be flexible, allowing for modifications and updates to its six aspects and sub-contents to address evolving needs. For researchers and educators who view engineering ethics content as extending beyond these six aspects, there is the flexibility to modify, add, or remove content as needed. They might also choose different polyhedral models (such as tetrahedral, octahedral, and dodecahedral Rubik’s Cubes) to construct customized pedagogical models.9 The potential of adapting this model to other fields, like business ethics, suggests its versatility, although its effectiveness in non-engineering contexts requires additional exploration.

The Rubik’s Cube model is selected for its appeal to engineering students and its capacity to visually and graphically represent complex ethical concepts. Meanwhile, it is crucial to assess if certain features of the Rubik’s Cube might not align with teaching engineering ethics. For instance, while traditional Rubik’s Cube involves aligning same-colored blocks to solve the puzzle, the EEKRC does not neatly apply to resolving engineering ethical issues which cannot be resolved simply by aligning corresponding ethical elements. Furthermore, many such issues may not have definitive resolutions, such as the ongoing challenge of balancing technological progress with environmental sustainability. Nonetheless, the concept of “restoring the Rubik’s Cube to its original state” serves as a metaphor for striving to harmonize and balance various elements in ethical analyses.

This paper focuses on a 2-D Rubik’s Cube model. Considering the 3-D EEKRC, additional challenges arise regarding the model’s adaptability. For example, the difficulty of solving a Rubik’s Cube stems from the challenge of accurately moving a specific square to a targeted position. Analogously, within a 3-D EEKRC, it is equally challenging to deliberately rotate elements such as utilitarianism, NSPE codes of ethics, responsibility, aerospace engineering, engineers, managers, design phrase, testing phrase, and others onto the same side to construct an analytical framework. To mitigate this, a hybrid approach combining both 3-D and 2-D models is suggested. Initially, students can use the 2-D model to identify relevant ethical elements, then explore more complex scenarios by twisting the 3-D model randomly to discover nuanced issues. For example, aligning aerospace engineering with future generations on the Rubik’s Cube could lead to unique perspectives.

Conclusion

This paper presents the Engineering Ethics Knowledge Rubik’s Cube theoretical model to improve engineering ethics education by categorizing knowledge for better retention, supporting ethical analysis, and enhancing lesson planning and assessment. The Rubik’s Cube model is selected as the visual heuristic due to its appeal to engineering students and its metaphorical representation of integrating various ethical perspectives. The EEKRC is structured around six aspects: ethical theories, codes of ethics, ethical issues, engineering disciplines, stakeholders, and life cycle, tailored to students’ backgrounds and learning preferences, and aims to systematize the ambiguous domain of engineering ethics. While the EEKRC demonstrates systematic coherence and broad applicability in diverse engineering ethics courses, it does not claim to provide an exhaustive account of engineering ethics pedagogy, nor does it imply that engineering ethics is mandated by these six aspects. It serves as a preliminary guide for exploring engineering ethics and structuring course content, allowing for customization by instructors and students to meet specific educational needs and professional focuses.

The effectiveness of the EEKRC in teaching and learning engineering ethics is currently undergoing empirical evaluation. This includes comparing the learning outcomes of students who use the model with those who do not, while controlling for other variables. Feedback from students and instructors on the use of the EEKRC will be collected and analyzed. Future research will explore potential applications of the Rubik’s Cube model in various fields, including science and technology ethics, research ethics, medical ethics, and business ethics. This may lead to the development of specialized tools, such as the Business Ethics Knowledge Rubik’s Cube. An in-depth discussion on 3-D and digital versions of the EEKRC will also be a focus of future research.
