Mammary simple adenoma of the right rear udder in a 15-month-old Holstein heifer
Authors: Mikuya IWANAGA, Momoka SUZUKI, Hayate NISHIURA, Susumu IWAIDE, Tomoyuki SHIBAHARA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 15-month-old Holstein heifer developed progressive swelling of the right rear udder 3 month. The heifer was euthanized to investigate the cause. Postmortem examination revealed a solid mass measuring approximately 10 × 10 × 10 cm in the lactiferous sinus of the right rear udder. Histopathologically, the mass consisted of glandular and ductal proliferation of epithelial-like cells with slight atypia. Immunohistochemically, the cells were strongly positive for pan-cytokeratin (CK) and CK7, and approximately 25% of the cells were strongly positive for estrogen receptor. The Ki-67 proliferation index of tumor cells was 4.53%. No neoplastic proliferation of myoepithelial cells or fibrous connective tissue was observed. The mass was diagnosed as a simple adenoma. To our knowledge, this is the first report of mammary simple adenoma in a Holstein heifer, and in such a young heifer without a history of artificial insemination or pregnancy.Keywords: heifer, Holstein, lactiferous sinus, mammary gland, simple adenoma

Mammary tumors are common in dogs and cats, but extremely rare in ruminants [3, 5, 14]. Previous reports include fibroadenoma [7], papillary cystadenoma [10], and adenocarcinoma [9] in Holstein cattle; fibroadenoma in a lamb [6]; and carcinoma arising from an adenoma in a ewe [8]. Older literature sources have varying degrees of value because histologically definitive diagnoses had not been established [3]. The present study reports a rare case of a mammary simple adenoma that formed in the right rear udder of a 15-month-old Holstein heifer.

In July 2025, a 15-month-old heifer in Fukushima Prefecture, Japan, presented with marked swelling of the right rear udder that had been present for 3 months. Palpation revealed a lump in the udder, with yellow-brown serosanguineous discharge expressed from the right rear udder. The heifer was treated with penicillin for suspected mastitis. However, the reduction in tumor size was not observed. No bacteria were isolated from culture of the expressed discharge. Subsequently, within 2 months, the swelling had expanded to encompass the entire right rear udder. To investigate the cause, the heifer was moved to the Fukushima Prefectural Chuo Livestock Hygiene Service Center and euthanized later that month.

Clinical examination before necropsy revealed a fluctuation in the cranial portion of the right rear udder (at the border with the right fore udder), whereas the caudal portion was firm and indurated (Fig. 1a). Ultrasonography revealed solid tissue consistent with a mass, papillary structures, and mammary ducts dilated in a mesh-like pattern within the mammary glands (Fig. 1b). No fever was observed, and the mammary glands exhibited no increased temperature. At necropsy, the mammary glands weighed 5.8 kg. The right mammary gland was swollen to approximately 20 × 30 × 30 cm, compared with approximately 10 × 15 × 20 cm for the left gland. The posterior right mammary section showed a well-defined, pedunculated, cauliflower-like papillary tumor measuring approximately 10 × 10 × 10 cm within the lactiferous sinus (Fig. 1c). The tumor sections were yellow-orange, solid and water-rich. The tumor was connected to the mammary gland tissue at a distal point within the lactiferous sinus. Macroscopically, edema and hemorrhage were observed at the periphery of the mass sections. The surrounding mammary gland tissue was spongy, dilated, and contained the same yellow-brown, serosanguineous discharge observed during clinical examination. A corpus luteum 15 mm in diameter was observed in the right ovary. No significant lesions were observed in other organs.

The mammary mass and lymph nodes, liver, spleen, kidneys, heart, lungs, and ovaries were sampled and fixed in 20% neutral-buffered formalin. The fixed tissues were then embedded in paraffin, sectioned (approximately 3 μm), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological examination. Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) reaction, Alcian blue pH2.5 (AB), AB and PAS double, toluidine blue (TB), and Azan staining were also performed on mammary mass sections. Histopathologically, mild structural atypia was observed in the tumor. Specifically, the tumor tissue was compressing the surrounding mammary gland tissue (Fig. 2a), and the border region connected to the mammary gland tissue was clearly demarcated by fibrous connective tissue (Fig. 2b). In addition, glandular epithelial-like cells proliferated in acinar, tubular, or ductal patterns (Fig. 2c), and in some areas, in cystic or papillary patterns. Most proliferating cells were well differentiated, with a few mitotic figures (<1/50 high power field (HPF), field number 26.5/40× objective), and showed mild atypia. Myoepithelial cells were also observed at the margins of the glandular structures (Fig. 2d, arrowheads). Eosinophilic serous fluid, showing strong PAS reaction (Fig. 3) but not AB or TB staining, was retained within the lumina. In contrast, no similar PAS-reacted secretions were observed in the mammary lobules surrounding the tumor. At the periphery of the tumor, edema, hemorrhage, and inflammatory cell infiltration, primarily composed of neutrophils, were observed. The fibrous connective tissue formed distinct borders and was stained blue by Azan staining. No histopathological abnormalities were observed in ovaries. The pituitary gland could not be examined due to failure during the tissue sampling.

For immunohistochemical examination, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded sections of the mammary mass were deparaffinized and incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol to suppress endogenous peroxidase activity. Antigens were retrieved using pH9 Target Retrieval Solution (Dako, Santa Clara, CA, USA) in a microwave for 4 min. After adding 10% normal goat serum to block nonspecific reactions, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies for 30 min at 20–25°C, as summarized in Table 1. Next, the sections were incubated with secondary antibody (Histofine Simple Stain MAX-PO Multi; Nichirei Bioscience Inc., Tokyo, Japan) for 30 min at 20–25°C and then treated with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate solution (Histofine Simple Stain DAB solution; Nichirei Bioscience Inc.) at 20–25°C. Finally, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Ki-67 proliferation index was recorded based on the proportion of positive tumor cells in HPF (field number 26.5/40× objective). Immunohistochemical staining revealed that glandular epithelial-like cells were strongly positive for pan-cytokeratin (CK) (Fig. 4a) and CK7, and negative for CK5/6, desmin, and thyroid transcription factor-1. Approximately 25% of the cells were strongly positive for estrogen receptor (ER) (Fig. 4b). Myoepithelial cells were positively stained for pan-CK, CK5/6 (Fig. 4c), α-smooth muscle actin (SMA), and vimentin at the periphery of the glandular structures. Vimentin was also expressed in the tumor stroma. The Ki-67 proliferation index was 4.53% (68/1,500) (Fig. 4d). No progesterone receptor (PR) cross-reactivity was observed.

Using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid-treated whole blood from the heifer, concentrations of estradiol (E2) and progesterone (P4) in plasma were measured using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) by ASKA Pharma Medical Co., Ltd. (Fujisawa, Japan). The E2 and P4 concentrations were 0.86 and 232.2 pg/mL, respectively. In addition, the lactoferrin (LF) concentration was measured in a serosanguineous discharge sample from the present heifer using a Bovine Lactoferrin Latex Test Kit (Biolinks Co., Ltd., Yokohama, Japan). For comparison, serum concentrations were also measured. The procedure was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and measurements were obtained after diluting the serosanguineous discharge to 1:1,000 and the serum to 1:10, with the values subsequently corrected. The results were 1,049 μg/mL for the discharge and 3.01 μg/mL for the serum, respectively.

The mammary neoplasm contained mainly glandular, epithelial-like cells. These cells were well differentiated with slight atypia, and no metastasis to the mammary lymph nodes was observed. According to the classification of canine mammary tumors [4, 13], the mammary tumor was diagnosed as a simple adenoma. Other differential diagnoses included complex adenoma and fibroadenoma [7]. However, complex adenoma was excluded because no neoplastic proliferation of myoepithelial cells was observed, whereas fibroadenomas were excluded because of the scant proliferation of fibrous connective tissue within the tumor stroma.

Based on the PAS reaction, the serous fluid retained within the lumina of the neoplastic glandular epithelial cells was considered to contain glycoproteins. Measurements of LF, one of the major glycoproteins in milk, revealed a significantly higher concentration in the serosanguineous discharge than in the serum, and also higher than the concentration measured in the colostrum of healthy cows (201 μg/mL) [1]. Therefore, the yellow-brown serosanguineous discharge observed in the present case was presumed to be secreted by neoplastic cells that retained their secretory function. Alternatively, localized circulatory impairment caused by the tumor may have led to exudate accumulation.

In humans, dogs, and cats, imbalances of female hormones such as estrogen and progesterone are strongly implicated as risk factors affecting mammary gland tumors. In cows, repeated pregnancy and calving may suppress mammary gland tumor development by maintaining the balance of these hormones [2, 11]. Only one report in the literature has described such tumors in nulliparous animals; in that case, a 15-year-old nulliparous cow kept in a zoo was diagnosed with papillary cystadenoma [10].

In the present study, the etiology of the tumor was considered idiopathic, with possible causes including hormonal stimulation or hormone-independent factors such as genetic factors and development secondary to inflammation. The first estrus of the heifer was confirmed during monitoring of the udder swelling, and a corpus luteum was present in the right ovary. Thus, the tumor may be formed immediately after its first estrus and possibly associated with hormonal stimulation during sexual maturation. However, no macroscopic abnormalities were observed in the central organs secreting sex hormones, including the pituitary gland and ovaries. Additionally, the reported E2 and P4 concentrations during the normal bovine estrus cycle are approximately 1.0–11.2 pg/mL and 1,000–7,000 pg/mL, respectively [12]. Consequently, the concentrations of these hormones in the heifer in the present case may have been comparatively low (E2: 0.86 pg/mL, P4: 232.2 pg/mL). Since concentrations were extremely low and can vary depending on the measurement method, species differences, and individual variation, the influence of hormones on tumor formation in the present case remains unclear. Histopathologically, the ER expression in neoplastic glandular epithelial cells suggested that the ER expression may be influenced by low concentrations of E2 and that the ER sensitivity was heightened. High sensitivity to E2 during the first estrus may have been an incidental factor. One limitation of the current hypothesis regarding the pathogenesis is that hormone levels were measured only once. To elucidate the relationship between mammary tumors and hormones in cattle, it is important to accumulate a larger number of cases.

In conclusion, we report a case of simple adenoma identified in the right rear udder of a 15-month-old heifer. No previous reports have described mammary gland tumors occurring at such a young age in cattle, or mammary simple adenoma in cattle. Thus, this study provides important insights into the characteristics of simple adenomas in cows.
