Evaluation of the effect of virtual reality hypnosis on pain through attentional diversion during prostate biopsies under local anesthesia (HYPNOSURG-VR): a study protocol
Authors: Maximilien Delay, Assilah Bouzit, Joris Giai, Jean-Alexandre Long, Jean-Luc Descotes, Gaëlle Fiard, Clement Sarrazin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Prostate cancer is the second most commonly diagnosed cancer in men worldwide. Prostate biopsies are essential for diagnosis but often induce significant pain and anxiety despite local anesthesia. Virtual reality (VR) has emerged as a promising non-pharmacological tool for pain management during medical procedures. This study aims to assess the effect of virtual reality hypnosis (VRH) as an adjunct to standard local anesthesia during prostate biopsies.Methods:This single-center, prospective, randomized, controlled, parallel-group, single-blind trial will compare standard prostate biopsies under local anesthesia with and without VRH. A total of 130 patients will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either standard care with local anesthesia alone or standard care plus VRH. The VRH will be initiated 15 minutes pre-procedure and continue throughout. The primary outcome is the maximum pain assessed using a 0–10 Numerical Pain Rating Scale immediately post-procedure by a blinded assessor. Secondary outcomes include post-procedure pain, anxiety, physiological stress response, immersion level, patient satisfaction, procedural success, additional analgesia needs, procedure duration, and adverse events. Follow-up will occur at 1 month.Discussion:This trial will provide high-quality evidence regarding VRH effectiveness as a non-pharmacological adjunct for pain management during prostate biopsies. By targeting both anticipatory anxiety and procedural pain, this approach may significantly improve patient comfort. If effective, this technique could transform the standard of care for prostate biopsies and potentially extend to other invasive urological procedures.

Background

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second most commonly diagnosed cancer in men, with 1.4 million diagnoses worldwide in 2020[1]. Prostate biopsies are the cornerstone of diagnosis for PCa, representing one of the most common urological procedures with over 1 million procedures performed annually in Europe and the United States[2]. Transrectal biopsies (TRB) and transperineal biopsies (TPB) are both conducted under real-time transrectal ultrasound guidance, typically in an office-based setting[3]. Despite being essential for diagnosis, prostate biopsies are often associated with pain and frequently induce significant anxiety in patients[4]. The European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines[3], informed by evolving clinical evidence and research standards[5-7], recommend ultrasound-guided periprostatic nerve block for pain management[8]. Data regarding pain for each approach have been inconsistent; some studies suggest that TPB may be more painful than TRB[9], while recent randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with appropriate local anesthesia protocols found either non-clinically significant differences[10] or no difference[11] in pain between these approaches, with both procedures generally causing only mild discomfort.

In recent years, virtual reality (VR) has emerged as a promising non-pharmacological and non-invasive tool for pain management during medical procedures[12]. This technology mitigates the cognitive perception of pain by engaging patients in an immersive virtual world that diverts attention from the procedure[13].

VR has demonstrated efficacy in decreasing pain and anxiety during various urological procedures, including rigid[14,15] and flexible[16,17] cystoscopy and shockwave lithotripsy[18,19]. For prostate biopsies specifically, a previous RCT by Genç et al[20] compared VR to stress balls and standard of care for TRB, reporting positive outcomes. However, methodological concerns were noted, including inadequate details about allocation concealment, imprecise comparator definition, absence of a reported local anesthesia protocol, potential bias in outcome assessment due to lack of blinding, and no clear pre-specified analysis plan. Similarly, a 2-arm prospective cohort study (VR vs standard of care) with 153 men undergoing magnetic resonance imaging-guided TRB found that VR in addition to a periprostatic nerve block significantly reduced pain in a biopsy-naïve cohort[21]. Notably, neither of these trials evaluated the transperineal approach.

Even with recommended periprostatic blocks, the most painful procedural steps are often probe insertion and anesthetic administration, not the biopsies themselves[22]. Thus, VR could serve as an adjunct before and at the time local anesthesia is applied. VR could also alleviate anxiety, as studies showed that higher anxiety levels led to increased pain perception during the procedure[23]. VR may therefore offer meaningful benefits for both pain management during prostate biopsies and overall procedural tolerance.

A recent systematic review of VR applications in urological procedures provides important insights about the current evidence[24]. While most studies achieved reasonable sample sizes (7 of 10 studies included >90 participants, ranging from 30 to 270), the review identified key limitations. Technical heterogeneity across studies is substantial, with VR systems ranging from simple smartphone-based setups to sophisticated standalone devices, and content varying from static nature images to interactive gaming environments. This variability complicates efforts to identify optimal VR parameters. The effectiveness of VR varies considerably by procedure type, showing greater pain reduction in more invasive procedures (prostate biopsy and rigid cystoscopy) compared to less invasive interventions (flexible cystoscopy), where effects were primarily on anxiety rather than pain. For less invasive procedures, the smaller absolute effect sizes mean larger samples are needed to detect statistically significant differences. Some procedures, such as vasectomy, showed no benefit despite an adequate sample size (n = 140). The review concludes that VR effectiveness depends on multiple factors, including procedure invasiveness, patient anxiety levels, and VR technology specifications. This heterogeneity underscores the need for adequately powered trials with standardized VR protocols.

While most VR applications rely on simple distraction, emerging evidence suggests that VR combined with hypnotic content – including guided relaxation, breathing techniques, and dissociative suggestions – may provide enhanced analgesic effects[25]. This combination, termed virtual reality hypnosis (VRH), integrates immersive visual environments with therapeutic hypnotic suggestions.

In this context, we designed the HYPNOSURG-VR study, a prospective, randomized, and controlled trial, which aims to compare the pain experienced by patients undergoing prostatic biopsies, with and without distraction by VRH.

Methods

Protocol overview

This study is designed as a single-center, prospective, randomized, controlled, parallel-group, single-blind (outcomes assessor) trial. Participants will be randomized in a 1:1 allocation ratio to undergo standard prostate biopsies under local anesthesia either with or without VRH using a Hypno-VR headset. Ethical considerations

The study will be conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The protocol has received approval from the institutional ethics committee. The trial has been registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Patients will be informed that they may withdraw from the study at any time without affecting their medical care. Results will be published in peer-reviewed journals regardless of outcome.

Primary and secondary endpoints

Primary endpoint

To compare the maximum perioperative pain intensity, measured using a 0–10 Numerical Pain Rating Scale (NPRS), experienced during targeted prostate biopsies under local anesthesia between patients receiving VRH as an adjunct and those receiving standard care with local anesthesia alone.

Secondary endpoints

Our secondary objectives encompass multiple domains of the patient experience and procedural outcomes:

Pain and Comfort Measures: We will evaluate post-discharge pain intensity (at hospital discharge and day 1), procedural anxiety levels, and requirements for additional analgesia between the VRH and control groups.

Physiological and Psychological Responses: Beyond subjective pain assessment, we will measure physiological stress responses through heart rate variability monitoring using a clinical smartwatch. In the VRH group, we will specifically assess immersion level during the hypnosis experience to understand the relationship between immersion depth and analgesic effect.

Procedural Performance Metrics: The study will compare critical procedural parameters, including success rates of targeted biopsies, procedure duration, and recovery time between groups, to ensure VRH does not compromise clinical efficiency.

Patient-Centered Outcomes: We will assess overall patient satisfaction with the procedure and conduct qualitative semi-directed interviews to gain deeper insights into differences in patient experience between the intervention approaches.

Safety Assessment: Finally, we will compare the incidence and severity of all procedure-related adverse events during the 1-month follow-up period to ensure that VRH implementation maintains or improves the safety profile of prostate biopsies.

Study population

This study will enroll adult male patients (≥18 years) scheduled for ultrasound-guided prostate biopsies under local anesthesia (TPB or TRB) who can provide written informed consent.

Assessment of baseline characteristics

Baseline patient characteristics will be collected during the diagnostic work-up, including demographic data (age), family history of PCa, most recent serum Prostate Specific Antigen (PSA) values and PSA density, digital rectal examination findings and clinical tumor stage, prostate volume measured by mpMRI, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings including Prostate Imaging-Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) scores, and previous prostate biopsy history.

Interventions

Control group

Patients allocated to the control group will receive standard care for prostate biopsies under local anesthesia according to current clinical guidelines[3]. This protocol includes patient positioning in lithotomy position, ultrasound-guided periprostatic nerve block (administered transperineally or transrectally depending on the biopsy approach), intrarectal lidocaine gel application, and standard ultrasound-guided TPB or TRB based on clinical indications. All biopsies will be software-assisted MRI-fusion biopsies combining targeted sampling (three cores per PI-RADS 4–5 lesion and maximum two lesions) and systematic sampling (six cores per lobe for transrectal approach; six cores per lobe in the peripheral zone following the MUSIC template for transperineal approach).

Intervention group

Patients in the intervention group will receive identical standard care as described for the control group, with the addition of VRH. A VR headset will be placed while the patient is in the pre-procedure holding area, 15 minutes before the biopsy. Multiple independent studies have converged on a 10–15 minutes pre-procedure window for non-pharmacological interventions: hypnotherapy[26] administered 10 minutes or diaphragmatic breathing taught 15 minutes[27] before prostate biopsy, and the same HypnoVR device started 5–10 minutes before external shockwave lithotripsy (ESWL)[19] or minimally invasive functional urological interventions[28] (detrusor botulinum toxin injections, bulking agents, and sacral neuromodulation). This timing appears to reflect an optimal balance between efficacy and feasibility, ensuring adequate immersion and relaxation response establishment before the most painful procedural elements without disrupting clinical workflow. The HypnoVR device (Strasbourg, France) provides six dynamic, immersive 360-degree environments: tropical beach, underwater diving, mountain forest walk, space travel, zen garden journey through four seasons, and winter mountain traverse. These moving visual narratives are designed to capture and maintain attention while promoting deep relaxation. Patients select their preferred environment and choose between male or female voice guidance. The audio combines relaxing music with hypnotic suggestions focusing on breathing adaptation, progressive relaxation, and dissociation from physical sensations. For this protocol, all patients will receive the full immersion modality combining both visual and audio components. The specific hypnotic scripts are proprietary to the commercial device but incorporate established hypnotic techniques as validated in previous urological applications[19,28]. Healthcare providers maintain the ability to communicate with patients during the procedure through the tablet control interface. VRH will continue throughout the entire biopsy procedure.

Randomization and blinding

Patients will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio into one of the two study groups. Randomization will be performed on the day of the procedure using a computer-generated random sequence with permuted blocks of variable size. Stratification will be performed based on prostate biopsy approach (TPB or TRB). Owing to the nature of the intervention, patients and treating physicians cannot be blinded to group allocation. However, outcome assessors will be blinded to the randomization arm.

Study procedures

Randomization will occur on the day of the procedure, allocating patients to one of the two study groups. Immediately following the procedure, the primary outcome of maximum pain intensity will be assessed by a research associate or coordinating nurse blinded to treatment allocation using a 0–10 NPRS. Additional assessments conducted during this visit will include anxiety levels, physiological stress response via heart rate variability (HRV) monitoring, and a semi-structured qualitative interview to capture patient experience. All participants will attend a follow-up visit 1 month post-procedure to evaluate any delayed complications, adverse events, and long-term outcomes related to the intervention.

Outcome measurements

Primary outcome measurement

Maximum pain intensity will be assessed using a 0–10 NPRS immediately after the procedure, where 0 represents “no pain” and 10 represents “worst pain imaginable.” Assessment will be performed by an outcome assessor blinded to treatment allocation.

Secondary outcome measurements

Follow-up

All patients will attend a single follow-up visit 1 month after the procedure to review biopsy results, assess for delayed complications, and collect the completed patient diaries that were distributed at discharge.

Sample size calculation

The sample size calculation is based on detecting a clinically meaningful difference between the two groups. On the 0–10 NPRS, a difference of 1.3–1.6 points is widely accepted as the minimal clinically important difference[29,30]. We therefore aim to detect a difference of 1.5 points between the groups, with an estimated mean pain score of 4.0 in the control group[10,11,23]. Assuming a standard deviation of 2.5, with 80% power and a 2-sided significance level of 5%, a sample size of 58 patients per group is required. Accounting for a potential 10% dropout rate, a total of 130 patients (65 per group) will be enrolled.

Statistical analysis

Data will be analyzed according to the intention-to-treat principle. The primary outcome (maximum pain scores) will be compared between groups using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the distribution of the data. For secondary outcomes, appropriate statistical tests will be applied based on the type and distribution of the variables. A P-value <0.05 will be considered statistically significant. Multivariable regression analysis will be performed to adjust for potential confounding variables. Multiple imputation of missing data on the main criterion will be performed if necessary, following the recommendations from Jakobsen et al[31]. Secondary criteria and covariates will not be imputed.

Discussion

This RCT will provide high-quality evidence regarding the effectiveness of VRH as a non-pharmacological adjunct for pain management during prostate biopsies. To our knowledge, this will be the first methodologically rigorous RCT specifically designed to evaluate VRH during prostate biopsies. Our study incorporates several critical methodological strengths: clear allocation concealment through computerized randomization, well-defined intervention and comparator groups, standardized local anesthesia protocols across both arms, blinded outcome assessment, and a pre-specified analysis plan. Furthermore, our trial is unique in that it includes both TPB and TRB with appropriate stratification, considers both biopsy-naïve and repeat-biopsy patients, and employs a comprehensive assessment of both subjective and objective outcomes.

The timing of our VRH intervention is specifically designed to address the most painful aspects of the procedure identified in previous research[22], including probe insertion and local anesthesia administration – elements often overlooked in conventional pain management approaches. By initiating VRH in the preparation area 15 minutes before the procedure, we target not only the biopsy-associated pain but also the anticipatory anxiety that influences pain perception[23].

Despite these methodological strengths, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the single-center design at a tertiary academic hospital may limit the generalizability of our findings to other healthcare settings with different patient populations, institutional cultures, or procedural practices. Second, although we standardized the biopsy protocol, individual variations in operator technique, communication style, and procedural efficiency among urologists may influence patient pain and anxiety levels. These operator-dependent factors represent potential sources of unmeasured variability that could affect our outcomes. Future multicenter trials with larger samples and systematic assessment of operator-related variables would help validate our findings and enhance their external validity. Additionally, our results are specific to the HypnoVR device and its proprietary content; different VR systems with alternative visual environments or hypnotic approaches may yield different outcomes.

If proven effective, this approach could significantly transform the standard of care for prostate biopsies by providing a non-pharmacological complement to local anesthesia. The potential benefits extend beyond pain reduction to include decreased procedural anxiety, improved patient experience, and potentially increased compliance with diagnostic procedures. Given the considerable number of prostate biopsies performed, even modest improvements in patient comfort could have substantial public health implications.

These findings may extend to other invasive procedures, potentially reducing pharmacological intervention needs and associated side effects.

While our study evaluated standardized VR hypnosis content, recent advances suggest potential for artificial intelligence (AI)-enhanced personalization of VR interventions. Bulduk et al[32] demonstrated that AI-assisted VR applications significantly reduced anxiety and improved physiological parameters in children undergoing endoscopy, with AI enabling age-appropriate content customization and real-time adaptation of visual and auditory elements. Applied to prostate biopsy procedures, similar AI integration could enable personalized VR content based on patient characteristics such as anxiety levels, previous biopsy experience, or biopsy approach (transrectal vs transperineal). Future research could explore whether AI-driven personalization of VR hypnosis content enhances pain reduction beyond standardized protocols, potentially through machine learning algorithms that predict optimal VR parameters for individual patients or adaptive content that responds to real-time physiological feedback. However, establishing the efficacy of standardized VR hypnosis through rigorous controlled trials, as in the present study, remains an essential foundation before investigating such technological enhancements.

Several implementation challenges may arise for the real-world application of VR hypnosis in clinical practice. These include the equipment costs (approximately €2500 per year for software, equipment, and license), the need for staff training in device operation and patient guidance, additional procedure time (estimated 15 minutes), and space requirements for equipment storage and patient preparation. Additionally, certain patient populations cannot use VR technology due to sensory impairments, severe psychiatric conditions, or physical constraints, which should be considered when estimating the proportion of patients who could benefit from this intervention in routine practice. Healthcare systems considering implementation should evaluate these factors against potential benefits, including reduced medication use, improved patient satisfaction, and possible increases in procedure compliance.

In conclusion, this trial protocol advances prostate biopsy analgesia research by: (1) first rigorous RCT design for VRH covering both biopsy approaches; (2) standardized local anesthesia protocols with blinded outcome assessment, addressing prior methodological limitations; (3) evidence-based intervention timing targeting anticipatory anxiety; (4) comprehensive outcomes including physiological markers; and (5) pragmatic commercial technology use.
