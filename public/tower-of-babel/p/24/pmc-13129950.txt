Digital tools for cognitive healthcare: Exploring perceptions of an everyday function app among midlife and older adults
Authors: Abigail T. Stephan, Allison Walton, Hye Won Chai, Vanessa Martinez, Milan Lewis‐Harris, Charlotte Weaver, Tate Steele, Juliet Skrelja, Ava McVey, Margo Courtney, Christine B. Phillips, Alyssa A. Gamaldo, Lesley A. Ross
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly detection of cognitive decline may be effective in reducing the adverse impacts of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD). Given that functional declines precede ADRD evaluation and diagnosis, regular assessments of everyday function are an avenue for detecting cognitive performance changes. While app‐based measures of everyday function and cognition are promising tools for early detection, perceptions of these tools' value remain unexamined. This study explored perceptions of an app‐based measure of everyday function (i.e., comfort with sharing performance data and perceived utility in healthcare) with community‐dwelling midlife and older adults in South Carolina, United States (N = 131, Mage = 67.08 years). Participants completed daily tasks through a mobile app objectively measuring everyday function then shared their feedback through a semi‐structured interview. Our thematic analysis found that interest and confidence in utilizing this technology was connected to beliefs around the value of having real‐time information about one's cognitive performance, experiences with healthcare providers, and trust in technology security and accuracy. Additionally, some adults have not thought critically about the role of these technologies in their healthcare. As health‐tracking technology expands in cognitive healthcare, researchers and practitioners must be aware of midlife and older adults' perceptions and educate users on its potential function.

INTRODUCTION

The number of adults living with Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) in the United States is projected to surpass seven million in the coming years (Alzheimer's Association, 2025). Without a cure for ADRD, nor treatments that effectively prevent cognitive decline for all persons with ADRD, prevention efforts are among the most promising option for supporting cognitive health (Yost et al., 2024). This includes initiatives that emphasize the early detection of declines in cognitive function, such as regular screening during doctor's visits. However, prevention and early detection can be challenging if cognitive performance and related metrics, such as one's ability to engage in instrumental activities of daily living (i.e., everyday function), are not monitored regularly throughout midlife and older adulthood (Isaacson & Saif, 2020; Vinze et al., 2023). Barriers, including insurance and financial constraints, finding time to visit the doctor, transportation challenges, fear of clinical assessment and diagnosis, and lack of awareness, may limit regular monitoring through traditional assessments with healthcare providers. Thus, there is a need to implement easy‐to‐administer measures for early detection of ADRD that can be used within individuals' daily lives, with results that are transferrable to cognitive healthcare systems (Hilsabeck et al., 2022). At the same time, exploring whether adult users see value in utilizing novel technologies—such as those that track everyday function and other indicators of cognitive health—as early detectors of cognitive impairment in their healthcare is an important next step. Likewise, gauging possible user concerns is critical in understanding the viability and acceptability of these tools in older adults' cognitive healthcare. Therefore, this study explores midlife and older adults' comfort with, and perceived utility of, an app‐based everyday function assessment tool in relation to their cognitive healthcare.

Midlife and older adults' approaches to cognitive healthcare: Theoretical grounding

Embracing the complex and multifaceted considerations to effectively address issues within cognitive healthcare, we draw upon three interwoven frameworks to guide our investigation: Health Empowerment Theory, the Health Belief Model (HBM), and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology. Cognitive healthcare is critical for midlife and older adults to prevent or mitigate the negative effects of cognitive impairment or ADRD. This can be achieved through improved health behaviors—such as maintaining a healthy diet, acquiring adequate sleep, and engaging in regular physical and cognitively stimulating activities—as well as through early intervention via medication or other behavior modifications ascertained through regular and open dialogue with healthcare providers. The Health Empowerment Theory (Shearer, 2009) posits that empowerment, which entails supporting individuals to make informed decisions about their health, arises through personal (e.g., confidence, resilience, self‐capacity) and social‐contextual (e.g., support from healthcare systems, community services, and social networks) resources. This dynamic approach involves actively engaging with one's environment, recognizing personal patterns, and drawing on inner strengths to improve well‐being. This is important for older adults living with chronic conditions such as ADRD, as they frequently experience numerous unmet social and healthcare needs (Bolnick, 2004). This theory informs our broader conceptual model by emphasizing the interplay between one's social environment and perceptions of self in relation to their cognitive health, with health empowerment serving as the ultimate outcome of interest.

At the same time, the HBM seeks to explain and predict health‐related behaviors by examining individuals' beliefs and attitudes toward health (Vincenzo et al., 2022). Grounded in the expectancy‐value framework, the HBM emphasizes decisional balance, which entails comparing perceived benefits to perceived barriers when determining the level of engagement in particular health behaviors (Rejeski & Fanning, 2019). The model consists of six key constructs: perceived susceptibility to a health condition, perceived severity of that condition, perceived benefits of taking preventative action, perceived barriers to taking that action, cues to prompt health‐promoting behaviors, and self‐efficacy to follow through with those behaviors (Janz & Becker, 1984). As one of the most widely applied frameworks in health behavior research, the HBM was initially developed to understand how individuals respond to treatments for acute and chronic illnesses (Champion & Skinner, 2008). It has since become instrumental in analyzing and shaping health‐related decision‐making among older adults across diverse settings (e.g., Gustavsson & Beckman, 2024; Vincenzo et al., 2022). Within the context of our overarching model, this lens describes older adults' internal processes that inform their health decisions and is represented as their personal beliefs and attitudes. Within the current study, this includes personal beliefs and attitudes directly related to health as well as technology as a tool for engaging in and tracking health behaviors.

Activities that support cognitive health can be carried out and tracked through technology. The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), a framework that examines users' acceptance and utilization of health technology (Venkatesh et al., 2003), provides a helpful guide when thinking about technology use and adoption in the context of cognitive healthcare. Widely applied to technology adoption studies among older adults and even to health‐tracking tools specifically with older adult populations (e.g., Wu et al., 2025), the UTAUT model identifies four core constructs: performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions. These constructs interact to shape user intentions and behaviors (Hsu et al., 2021) and have been used to understand mobile device and app adoption in older populations (Gao et al., 2015). Encapsulated within personal beliefs and attitudes as perceived technology competence and utility, this conceptual framing situates technology as a tool for health empowerment that is dependent on one's experiences, self‐efficacy, anticipated benefit, and expected barriers.

Through the integration of these conceptual models (Figure 1), we posit that midlife and older adults engage in complex calculations when making decisions around health behaviors and care decisions for cognitive health. They may evaluate personal and social‐contextual resources and weigh costs and benefits when making decisions about their healthcare. Given the increasing prevalence of technology use among middle‐aged and older adults, considerations of how technology can be used to support and inform these processes is central to promoting cognitive health. Emerging research shows that older adults typically refrain from speaking with their healthcare provider about their cognitive health due to the stigma surrounding an ADRD diagnosis and concerns over losing autonomy (Hill et al., 2023). However, there has not been extensive research on older adults' perceptions and feelings toward communicating with their healthcare providers about their cognitive health, and existing work has not included midlife adults nor their perspectives on app‐based cognitive tracking.

Theoretical framework.

Midlife and older adults' views toward health tracking

As a promising approach for taking ownership of one's cognitive healthcare, the use of and views toward health‐tracking technologies by midlife and older adults have been at the forefront of growing research. Special focus has been given to the increasing role that health tracking plays in preventative care and disease management (Yost et al., 2024). While older adults generally appear curious and motivated to use health‐tracking technologies (Vaportzis et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2025), their perceptions toward technology‐based health trackers are heavily influenced by various factors. These factors, at times functioning as barriers, include prior experience or exposure to digital tools, technology anxiety, accessibility and usability challenges, educational gaps, lack of social support, and resistance to change (Ehn et al., 2018; Godfrey, 2017; Lee et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). However, research in this space has mainly considered how health‐tracking technologies can be used to address older adults' physical needs, with a general lack of studies exploring older adults' perceptions of technologies that address mental and cognitive health or instrumental activities of daily living (Fowe & Boot, 2022).

Health‐tracking tools for everyday and cognitive function

Although functional impairments are a hallmark of later ADRD stages, increasing evidence demonstrates that everyday function declines gradually over the years preceding a clinical diagnosis; subtle changes in complex instrumental activities of daily living, such as managing finances, driving or using transportation services, and making decisions around health affairs, can indicate future cognitive decline and heightened ADRD risk (Dubbelman et al., 2020; Farias et al., 2019; Phillips et al., 2024). Therefore, our team is treating everyday function performance as a measure of cognitive health, an approach that has been suggested by other scholars employing digital assessment and tracking (Bernstein et al., 2022; Yost et al., 2024).

Existing technological tools for measuring everyday function and cognitive performance are becoming increasingly ubiquitous. Accordingly, researchers and clinicians are finding new ways to use technology to help older adults maintain their cognitive health as interest in this area grows in healthcare settings. While technologies that measure everyday function objectively have not been distributed for widespread use, multiple technologies have been identified for monitoring cognitive performance changes, including eye‐trackers, wearable sensors, keystroke patterns, and near‐infrared spectroscopy (Asiri et al., 2023). Among these technologies, the most cost‐effective, practical, and enjoyable for older adults are often those that can be used in everyday life, such as mobile apps, smartwatches, or assistive smartphone technologies that track performance over time, encourage regular engagement, and provide insight into multiple health domains (Addae et al., 2024; Puri et al., 2017). Using mobile, app‐based technology in monitoring and assessing cognitive and everyday function within a real‐world context provides multiple benefits, including real‐time information regarding changes in performance (Hilsabeck et al., 2022). In addition, older adults can use the data collected to communicate with their healthcare providers regarding their health status (Öhman et al., 2021). However, while research shows that older adults are capable of using technology in preventing and monitoring the progression or development of cognitive impairment, there is a lack of studies exploring the willingness of older adults to use this technology in managing their cognitive healthcare (Fowe & Boot, 2022; Leung et al., 2022). Also, despite mobile tracking technologies being intended for older users, few tools have been tested for ongoing use and acceptability among older adults (Hilsabeck et al., 2022). Finally, as noted in the previous sections, there is an absence of research on midlife adults' perceptions.

The present study

Despite advancements in health‐tracking technologies, there is little available research regarding the views of midlife and older adults on utilizing these technologies for cognitive healthcare. This study moves beyond examining the ease of use of health‐tracking technologies to consider utility and value. Hearing from the perspective of midlife and older adults—both their excitement around the potential of these tools and their concerns regarding the utilization of these tools in healthcare—is needed to promote more effective prevention efforts that could mitigate the deleterious effects and associated costs of ADRD (Hilsabeck et al., 2022). Thus, the primary aim of this study was to examine midlife and older adults' acceptability and perceived value of an app‐based everyday function assessment tool in relation to their cognitive healthcare. We expected the findings of our exploratory inquiry to reveal multiple factors, attitudes, and dispositions that midlife and older adults hold when considering current and future cognitive healthcare needs and their relation to existing and emerging health‐tracking technologies. As a secondary aim, we also examined whether perceptions differed for midlife and older adults. The specific research questions guiding these aims include:How do midlife and older adults perceive the acceptability and value of an app‐based everyday function assessment tool in the context of cognitive healthcare?To what extent do perceptions of the acceptability and value of said tool vary between midlife and older adults?

METHOD

For this study, our team drew on interview data from a larger study, the Tracking Real‐World Activities in Life Study (TRAiLS; MPI: Ross, Phillips), collected in November 2022 through August 2023 in South Carolina. TRAiLS was a pilot study that (a) explored connections between cognitive, physical, and everyday function among midlife and older adults ranging in area deprivation score and cognitive status and (b) designed and tested a mobile app to objectively measure everyday function. The institutional review board of Clemson University provided approval in October 2022 (IRB2022‐0418).

Sample

Community‐dwelling midlife and older adults (N = 131; Mage = 67.08 years) from South Carolina and the surrounding area (i.e., North Carolina, Georgia) participated (Table 1). Our team recruited participants using multiple methods including: a registry of individuals interested in being involved in research; flyers and handouts shared at community events, local community centers, libraries, and grocery stores; Facebook advertisements; and word of mouth. Eligibility requirements included regular use of a smartphone or tablet (i.e., daily, weekly, biweekly), ability to speak and read English fluently, absence of serious illness or neurological condition (i.e., stroke, brain tumor, Alzheimer's disease, congestive heart failure; self‐reported during telephone screening), and a Modified Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS‐M; Cook et al., 2009) score above 24. For age eligibility requirements, participants were between 40 and 60 years (midlife; Lachman et al., 2015) or 65+ years (older adult; National Institutes of Health, 2025) in age. Falling outside of these groups, individuals between 61 and 64 years were not eligible given the larger study aims, which sought to detect functional differences between midlife and older adults. Limited resources also dictated strategic recruitment of those solidly in midlife or older adulthood.

Participant characteristics.

Procedure

Interested individuals completed a telephone screening conducted by study personnel to ensure eligibility. If eligible, participants were scheduled for their first of two in‐person study visits and were sent both the informed consent document and remote questionnaires—either through email or by mail based on their preference. If participants did not have any questions about the study, they returned both the signed informed consent and completed questionnaires before their first visit. Staff members were available by phone or in person to answer any questions. During the first visit, participants completed assessments of physical, cognitive, and everyday functioning (~150–180 min). At the end of this visit, participants were given a study‐provided Android smartphone installed with the Everyday Function app, which the study team developed. Both the smartphone and app were linked to each participant's study ID and did not require or collect identifiable information. Participants could opt in to have location data collected. A team member demonstrated how to use the device and app and scheduled the participant for the second in‐lab visit 2 weeks later.

Every day across the 2‐week period, participants completed three daily tasks in the app. The tasks fell within everyday function domains of finance, medical, shopping, transportation, and entertainment and were aimed at mirroring tasks that individuals might encounter in their everyday routines. For example, a participant might be asked to transfer $20 from their fictional Checking Account to John Doe (finance), view test results (medical), order five bananas for delivery (shopping), schedule a rideshare from 101 Main Street to 102 University Drive (transportation), or tune in to 100.1 FM radio station (entertainment) in the app portal. The three daily tasks were randomly assigned to be completed at specified, randomly‐generated times within a window participants provided and our team configured on their behalf (typically between 9 a.m. and 7 p.m.). Participants were instructed to log into the app every morning to check their assigned times; they did not receive an auditory signal (i.e., “beep” or “ring”) when the assigned times approached. Participants attended a second in‐person lab visit after 2 weeks to return the smartphone, then debrief their experience using the app and share perceptions of its utility through a brief evaluation survey (~5 min) and a follow‐up semi‐structured interview conducted by a trained study team member (~20 min). With participants' consent, the interviews were audio‐recorded.

Measures

To address the current study's aims, we drew on responses to two prompts in the semi‐structured interview protocol: Would you feel comfortable with your doctor or healthcare provider having access to your task performance in the app? and Do you think it would be useful for your doctor or healthcare provider to have access to this information? Why or why not? Participants provided verbal responses. When appropriate, interviewers asked follow‐up questions for participants to clarify their responses or provide additional information; for example, interviewers might have asked, What do you mean by that? or Could you provide more detail? as appropriate.

Analytic approach

Our team prepared the data through verbatim transcriptions of the audio‐recorded interviews. Given the current study's focused aims, the first author then transferred responses to the two items of interest to an Excel spreadsheet where our team completed each round of coding. The coding team consisted of the first author (holding 10 years of qualitative research experience) and five trained research assistants, ranging from novices to intermediate beginners in qualitative inquiry. Our team also benefited from two co‐authors (one faculty member and one graduate student involved in data collection for this study) who did not participate in coding but provided critical feedback on big‐picture concepts in each round of coding.

Our analysis was informed by principles of phenomenological and thematic analysis, which allow findings to emerge from data that are based in the experiences and beliefs of individuals (Braun & Clarke, 2006). The coding process involved three stages and was guided by a protocol developed by the first author and adapted iteratively in consultation with team members. Throughout the coding process, our team discussed progress, questions, and roadblocks during weekly meetings.

Our first round included descriptive coding, where coders indicated a word or short phrase to encapsulate the sentiment and ideas conveyed through a participant's response (Saldaña, 2025). The protocol included a list of 12 codes determined by the first author, though coders were encouraged to develop additional codes as needed to best fit the data; this process resulted in 21 Round 1 codes. Coders could indicate as many codes as needed to capture what was conveyed through a response. Of the 262 distinct responses coded, half (n = 131) had more than one code assigned to describe what was being explained through the participant's response. Each response was coded independently by at least two members of the coding team; we had five Round 1 coders, so two were assigned to code data from participants with identification numbers ending in even numbers and three were assigned to code data from participants with identification numbers ending in odd numbers. A high number of responses (n = 157 responses, 59.92%) had complete intercoder agreement on all codes. Out of a total of 496 codes assigned (given that a response could have more than one corresponding code), all coders were in agreement on 51.81% (n = 257). At least two coders agreed on an additional 20.16% (n = 100) of codes, meaning there was majority agreement on 71.97% of assigned codes during the first round. The first author reviewed discrepancies and noted recommended code assignments based on past coding norms in this process. Our team discussed discrepancies in weekly meetings and resolved issues with 100% agreement from the team.

Our second round consisted of pattern coding, where codes were arranged and categorized to create themes (Saldaña, 2025). Five coders reviewed the Round 1 codes independently and generated groupings that incorporated the existing codes and represented the essence of the data. This resulted in configurations with two to five themes. The first author then reviewed each configuration and used pattern coding and awareness of the data to develop a meta‐configuration. This meta‐configuration included four themes that were presented, discussed, and agreed upon by the research team.

The third round of the coding process was not intended to generate or consolidate codes but rather to verify the themes developed through Round 2. In this verification process, five coders reviewed the raw data, indicating which, if any, of the four themes captured the ideas conveyed through the participant's response. While a response could align with multiple themes, coders selected the theme that best represented the response. This process gave us insight into whether the themes we generated fully encapsulated the data or if additional or modified themes were needed. Our team determined that for any given response within the entirety of the data, at least one of the themes was applicable.

As a hallmark of ensuring the rigor of qualitative inquiry, the four criteria for trustworthiness—credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability—were incorporated within our investigation (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). To bolster credibility, we employed peer debriefing during our weekly meetings, and more often as needed. While we have already mentioned the qualitative expertise levels of our coding team, it should be noted that our research group included a combination of those involved in the data collection and those who solely supported the analysis; no one from our team was actively working in healthcare. Past experience with the research method, prior engagement with these data, and current and future interests inform the lens through which we approach the analysis. Transferability, comparable to external validity, is supported by the inclusion of interview responses from a relatively large number of participants by qualitative research standards. We ensured dependability through a living coding protocol document, as well as clear organization of files providing an audit trail. Our third round of analysis supported confirmability, where we verified the generated themes.

To address our secondary aim, we also conducted chi‐square tests of independence using SPSS Version 28 to examine whether the rate of endorsement for each identified theme differed between midlife and older adults.

Health management

Among all responses, around one third (n = 83, 31.68%) aligned with the theme of health management. This theme includes responses that discuss activities and sentiments supporting long‐term health promotion, awareness of disease progression, and proactive health management. Participants with responses in this category generally perceived the app as a valuable tool for the early detection of cognitive decline. As one female, midlife participant described, “I think that in being able to get that information, you would be able to track or see if there's any kind of abnormal activity, train of thought, or patterns that are not quite lining up with where my mental state or brain health should be.” This quote illustrates the perceived utility of regularly monitoring everyday and cognitive function to identify changes over time.

Around two thirds of responses falling within this theme emphasized the app's potential role in facilitating timely and targeted interventions. One female, older adult participant noted, “He's [physician] the general that coordinates everything else, and if he starts seeing some changes, then he would help me make the next steps to make sure that I'm on board with whatever measures are proving to be helpful in slowing things down.” Another participant (female, midlife) stated, “I think it would be useful if I was at risk for dementia so they would see kind of a decline, and if a decline was happening maybe we could prescribe medication or do other things.” These responses suggest trust in the app's ability to provide detailed information to health providers to promote more effective health management.

A smaller subset of participants (around 25%) with responses corresponding to the health management theme also recognized the potential of the app to support behavior change and cognitive engagement by providing detailed information about one's performance. As one female, older adult respondent shared, “When you're a senior, it's important [to track your cognitive performance]. I think a lot of people, especially people who live alone, don't even know when they're going downhill. And I think doing something like this keeps someone focused…I think it's good for you.”

Healthcare relationship

Regarding participants' views on healthcare relationships, 28.63% (n = 75) of responses fell within this theme. This theme captures the perceived quality and frequency of participants' interactions with their healthcare providers. While some responses in this theme (around 40%) explicitly described these relationships as positive and collaborative, roughly one in five expressed skepticism, mistrust, or concern about autonomy and data sharing. Others still expressed ambivalence, suggesting that it would largely depend on the doctor, the quality of the relationship, and the circumstances. One participant (female, older adult) summarized this notion by stating, “No to some, yes to some. Access with dialogue and permission would be okay.”

Within the former categorization, participants typically emphasized a sense of trust, respect, and appreciation for the attentiveness of their providers. One male, older adult participant stated, “Especially at my age, the more people watching out for me is great. That's their job, and he [healthcare provider] does his job or she does her job better the more stuff, and the more current stuff, they know.” Another participant (female, older adult) agreed, pointing out that having a familiar provider involved in ongoing care is especially beneficial: “I think the analytical summary could come definitely from a physician who watches me on a number of issues that my internalist, my main care provider, would be a perfect person to talk to me about that.” This statement reflects a common sentiment shared by participants that providers who know their health history are better equipped to guide their care.

In contrast, around 20% of responses in the health relationship theme conveyed participants' uncertainty or skepticism about the intentions and understanding of their healthcare providers and larger healthcare systems. As one male, older adult participant stated, “It [having information from the app shared with a provider] could be [useful], but I don't know how well the doctor and all would understand what they were getting from it.” Responses in this area also revealed concerns about control over health data and the boundaries around provider access or implications for insurance, especially when the relationship with providers was not perceived as trusting or positive. One such response illustrating this point was from a female participant in midlife: “It's none of their [clinicians'] business … I mean it's just not. I think there's too much information shared. I think it should be my option if I choose to share it, not just a given.”

Technology capabilities

Participants expressed varying degrees of concern about current technology capabilities. Twenty‐three responses (8.78%) fell within this theme, which highlights a lack of perceived efficacy or acknowledgment of potential unknowns regarding the capabilities of current technology, both within and beyond the healthcare setting. Around 35% of responses within this group saw the app for its limitations, utilizing descriptive terms such as “bugs” or “faulty,” but felt the technology held promise if not for these deterrents. For instance, one female, older adult participant noted, “It could be helpful. But you need to get the bugs out first because I don't think it's showing what I'm able to do at all.”

Other responses (roughly 65%) focused on the technology's assessment abilities, often emphasizing the need for interpretation and context to be provided to healthcare providers along with the resulting data from the app. Representative quotes for this particular subcategory include: “They may get some skewed information that they think, 'well there's a problem' but maybe they just didn't want to deal with it or mess with it or try too hard with it or anything like that. So, it could lead to some false information” (male, midlife), “I think it could be useful, if the app were truly providing the right data. Because I know that there are times that the way you answer a question or the way you do things can skew the data” (female, midlife), and “things can be interpreted in a million ways. It's a computer, it's not reality” (female, older adult). Overall, these sentiments convey that the app could provide more beneficial, trustworthy information if further developed and refined.

Nonelaborate responses

Just under one third of responses (30.92%, n = 81) fell into the nonelaborate, or nondescriptive, theme. This category captures responses that did not go into additional detail, indicating an unexamined or unexplored view of their comfort with sharing everyday and cognitive function performance data from the app or how the app might be helpful in their cognitive healthcare. This often took the form of taking the app at face value without providing additional context for their responses through short phrases. In most cases (around 90%) where this theme was noted, participants expressed an affirmative response, using language like “sure,” “yeah,” or “absolutely” when asked about their comfort with and perceived utility of the app. Around 10% of responses in this theme were more hesitant, but participants—most of whom were from our midlife subsample—could not directly point to where their concerns lay when asked to provide more information. Representative quotes from participants in this subcategory include: “I think it asked that question over there [on survey taken at computer at start of visit], too, and I guess I was undecided on that” (female, midlife), “I think so…yes” (this phrase was stated by female, older adult and male, older adult), “I don't know about that…I don't know why I feel that way” (male, midlife), and “I'm kind of neutral on that” (female, midlife). Only one participant (male, midlife) indicated they would not be comfortable with their healthcare provider having access to their performance without providing additional explanation, stating: “Not…no, but I mean not directly related to this. No.”

DISCUSSION

This study examined midlife and older adults' acceptability and perceived value of an app‐based everyday function assessment tool in the context of healthcare through an exploratory, primarily qualitative approach. Our analysis revealed an often nuanced perspective on utilizing technology for tracking and monitoring cognitive health across midlife and older adulthood, with over 80% of participant responses indicating that access to and use of this form of technology would be beneficial. Several factors influenced midlife and older adults' interest and confidence in this technology, including beliefs around the value to themselves and their healthcare providers of having access to real‐time information about their cognitive performance (mentioned in roughly 32% of responses), relationship quality with their healthcare providers (mentioned in roughly 29% of responses), and skepticism around the veracity of the data generated from engaging with the technology (mentioned in around 9% of responses). We observed similar rates of endorsement in these areas for both midlife and older adults, indicating that the two age groups may share similar overall perceptions toward utilizing mobile technology in healthcare.

Midlife and older adults in our study generally demonstrated a commitment to long‐term health promotion, awareness of disease progression, and proactive health management that could be accomplished through an app‐based tracking tool. These findings align with past work suggesting that older adults appreciate and are motivated by enhanced awareness of their health provided by tracking technologies (Ehn et al., 2018) and provide promise for preventive ADRD interventions that incorporate health‐tracking technology. Responses within the theme of health management demonstrated that adults consider several components of the Health Belief Model when identifying their comfort with and perceived utility of using an app that measures everyday function for health‐tracking purposes, including suspected susceptibility to Alzheimer's disease or dementia, perceived benefits of taking preventative action, and cues to prompt health‐promoting behavior (Janz & Becker, 1984). While researchers and clinicians have advocated for the use of these technologies to track health outcomes (e.g., Hilsabeck et al., 2022; Öhman et al., 2021), this study is among the first that examines the willingness of older adults to use this technology in managing their cognitive healthcare (Fowe & Boot, 2022; Leung et al., 2022).

A salient theme from this investigation was the importance of healthcare system perceptions and relationships with healthcare providers. Findings in this area align with a central component of Health Empowerment Theory as well as past research around relational aspects of health technology engagement, which suggests that older adults' positive relationships and social circles have a large influence on their willingness to engage with the technology and share their information with their healthcare providers (Fowe & Boot, 2022; Lee et al., 2025). This reinforces the assumption within the Health Empowerment Theory that social‐contextual resources, such as utilization of healthcare resources and positive interactions with providers, play an important role in health management (Shearer, 2009). While relationships with healthcare professionals were noted consistently in responses, our team did not observe that other social influences, such as the engagement of family or friends in these behaviors, were a stated factor connected to comfort with using the app or perceptions of the app's utility, as has been observed in other studies (e.g., Zhang et al., 2024). However, it is important to note that our interview questions were focused on sharing performance data specifically with a healthcare provider. Future research should explore comfort and perceived utility in sharing with other entities, such as spouses, children, or friends.

In line with past research, a portion of our sample (around 20%) perceived barriers to engaging with the technology, including a lack of understanding of the benefits, trepidation surrounding data usage, and lack of trust in healthcare providers taking the time to review and appropriately utilize the data (Godfrey, 2017; Hill et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). With health‐tracking technology continuing to evolve, both midlife and older adults in the present sample were also wary of utilizing the app to monitor their cognition due to potential data privacy concerns. At the same time, most participants (roughly 91%) did not express concerns about data security or privacy, mirroring one recent study that asked older adults to share their perspectives of health‐supporting technologies and cognitive health (Fowe & Boot, 2022). Regardless, strong descriptions of security measures, along with education and awareness around the exact processes involved in data collection, storage, and sharing, and instruction on the effective use of technology, could be used to bolster older users' likelihood of accepting such technology (Addae et al., 2024; Hsu et al., 2021; Vaportzis et al., 2017). Aligning with our conceptual framing and the UTAUT, continued research on older adults' perceived technology utility and use behaviors should emphasize technology use in relation to cognitive health.

A relatively large proportion of midlife and older adults provided responses that fell within the emergent theme labeled “Non‐Elaborate Responses.” Our team conceptualized responses in this category as cursory answers without a deeper explanation of the “why” behind an affirmative, negative, ambivalent, or uncertain reaction. While we generally concluded that these responses were characterized by a lack of additional consideration of the topic, it is possible that other factors led participants to respond in this way. For example, participants might have felt indifferent, not uncritical. Future work should probe participant responses to better understand the underlying mechanisms that lead to nonelaborate responses.

The secondary aim exploring differences in theme endorsement by age did not yield significant results. This may indicate that other factors that were relatively consistent across our sample, such as education level, smartphone/tablet use frequency, and geographic region, are more salient in shaping perceptions toward utilizing mobile technology in healthcare than age. Though most research drawing upon the Health Empowerment Theory and Health Belief Model considers a single age group, recent studies assessing potential age group differences have brought forth conflicting findings. Some suggest that midlife and older adults share similar perspectives about health behaviors (e.g., Bechard et al., 2021), while others find differences in health empowerment and confidence in digital health literacy (e.g., Papp‐Zipernovszky et al., 2021). Additional research is needed with larger, more heterogeneous samples to better understand which characteristics are most influential in the formation of perceptions around digital healthcare and supporting health behaviors.

Taken together, these findings suggest that midlife and older adults are willing to engage with technology in a healthcare setting to encourage proactive care and consideration of long‐term cognitive health needs. Additionally, their relationship with healthcare providers and confidence in the technology determined their willingness to engage and share the collected data with their healthcare providers, which has meaningful implications for the development of prevention‐focused ADRD interventions. Our findings conveyed views of the app as a tool to assist individuals, their families, and their healthcare providers in augmenting precision and quality of cognitive healthcare, while also revealing concerns around potential weaponization of medical data collected via app‐based technologies through use without patient permission or being taken out of context. At the same time, a contingent of participants had not thought deeply about this, leading to unexamined or unexplored views of the app and taking it at face value. In this case, it is possible that this finding reflects participants lacking sufficient awareness of the potential benefits and disadvantages of mobile health applications to feel comfortable evaluating and expressing their views. Future research and practice should consider placing a greater emphasis on curriculum development and educational offerings that can assist midlife and older adults falling within this group in better understanding the pros and cons of mobile health applications.

Strengths and limitations

Among the strengths of this study, the sample size and structure are at the forefront. The analytic sample for this study (N = 131) is relatively large for qualitative research, which allows for a greater number of voices to substantiate the themes identified through our analysis. Additionally, the data collection method allowed interviewers to immediately follow up with the participants in instances where their response was unclear or could be further expounded upon. Given that many participants held a nuanced perspective or had not yet grappled with considerations of the app's utility and their comfort with the data being used as a health‐tracking tool, having an open dialogue and space to reflect allowed participants to share their thoughts in real‐time, many times as they were developing. The integration of multiple theoretical perspectives is another innovative feature of this work. Given the incorporation of technological tools into healthcare practice and management, grounding research in integrative frameworks across disciplines that allow for a nuanced and multifaceted understanding provides promise for addressing complex challenges.

One of the core limitations of this study relates to our inclusion criteria, which were necessary to accomplish the aims of this work in a timely manner. These include the sole inclusion of participants who (1) were willing to attend study visits in a hospital setting given our lab's location and (2) regularly used a smartphone or tablet. This may skew our findings, especially those related to healthcare relationship and technology perceptions, as the sample may have held more positive beliefs of healthcare efficacy, positive provider relationships, and comfort with technology than the general public. Also, while participants' responses often transcended the specific app used in this study, the questions asked were not about perceptions of health technology in general. Additionally, our findings may not translate to other geographic locations and more gender diverse populations. Participants in this study resided in one region of the southeastern United States and were majority female. The proportion of endorsed themes, and even the themes themselves that emerged within this sample, may not hold among the broader population. Moreover, our sample includes cognitively healthy older adults who reported being relatively digitally active, which limits the generalizability of our findings for populations actively experiencing cognitive impairment or those who do not use technology regularly; though intended as a preventative tool, the health‐tracking technology could also be useful for detection at any stage of cognitive decline, thus making it relevant to assess acceptability of the tool for those exhibiting cognitive decline in future work. Finally, the larger study dictated that individuals between 61 and 64 years of age were not included in the study; with individuals in this age range being on the precipice of older adulthood, it is possible that they may be more receptive to health tracking as a form of early detection, and this should be explored in future studies. Given these limitations, the results presented above are likely not to be transferrable to midlife and older adults' perceptions of all types of health technologies.

Implications

Based on our findings from midlife and older adults who engaged with our study's mobile app, we recommend that healthcare systems prioritize three interrelated factors when implementing health technologies for aging populations: (1) responsiveness to long‐term health needs through proactive management, (2) the quality of patient–provider relationships, and (3) addressing low confidence in current technologies. As the proportion of older adults in society continues to grow and technology becomes increasingly integrated into everyday life, these considerations are critical in enhancing age‐related cognitive healthcare. Addressing these elements can improve health technology adoption among midlife and older adults, resulting in improvement in individual health outcomes and supporting healthcare efficiency and care. One meaningful avenue for advancing efforts in the three areas listed above is through increased education surrounding cognitive health, which has the potential to increase older adults' willingness to engage in activities that reduce the risk of developing ADRD (Khalil et al., 2025). Educational programming and resources should convey why early detection and prevention efforts are critical and how tracking—and subsequently sharing that information with healthcare providers—can promote positive long‐term health outcomes.

Future research should emphasize longitudinal assessments of real‐world use of the health‐tracking technologies in healthcare settings, moving beyond stated intentions to examine actual engagement, utilization, and sustained behavior over time. Additionally, understanding how engagement varies among vulnerable populations, including individuals with mild cognitive impairment (Czaja et al., 2006), will be essential for designing inclusive digital health interventions. Finally, it is critical to assess obstacles related to the use of health‐tracking technologies. Factors such as financial costs and limited access to internet may create barriers to use and exacerbate existing health disparities. Additionally, privacy laws and regulations need to be considered, which can be a complex issue given the rapidly developing state of digital healthcare tools and regulations. Incorporating these insights can guide the development of targeted, practical technologies that enhance adherence to health regimens and ultimately benefit individuals and the broader healthcare system.

CONCLUSION

This study explored midlife and older adults' acceptability with and perceived value of an app‐based everyday function assessment in the context of healthcare. Our exploratory inquiry found that responses touched on the meaningful role of the healthcare relationship, consideration of long‐term health needs and proactive health management, a lack of confidence in current technology capabilities, and an unelaborated view of these issues. Our findings suggest most adults are generally open to the use of digital technologies for cognitive health tracking. However, routine use of these tools in everyday life and integration within healthcare decision‐making must be aligned with midlife and older adults' health goals and values. Additional education for the public regarding the capabilities and limitations of these technologies is needed. This education should be offered in conjunction with training for healthcare providers on how to (a) continue building strong relationships with patients and (b) interpret and discuss data from tracking devices, as the patient‐clinician relationship was highlighted as a salient factor in trusting and utilizing these technologies for cognitive healthcare. Healthcare systems may integrate data generated from these technologies into electronic health record clinical workflows, whereby patients can allow data to flow directly into their chart for review by their practitioners. As tools for tracking everyday function and cognitive performance in daily life become more prevalent, examining the nuanced perspectives of midlife and older adults toward these tracking technologies will be essential for ensuring these technologies are not only effective in supporting early detection efforts but are also user‐centered. Technological development projects can address data safety and utility issues by strictly adhering to federal regulations for storing and transmitting Electronic Protected Health Information (ePHI) and testing technology efficiency with the midlife and older adult population to ensure accessibility. Both researchers and practitioners should continue to explore the intersection of day‐to‐day cognitive performance, accessible health‐tracking technology, and healthcare communication to support ADRD prevention and greater well‐being in older adulthood.
