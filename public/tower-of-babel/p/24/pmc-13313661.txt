Effects of Mulligan sustained natural apophyseal glide mobilizations on pain, mobility, and lumbar-related disability in chronic non-specific low back pain: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Kogilavani Shager, Moganavatany Murugiah, Tham Sze Kei, Harikrishnan Ranganathan, Mohd Azzuan Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Chronic nonspecific low back pain (CNSLBP) is a major cause of disability worldwide. Mulligan sustained natural apophyseal glides (SNAGs) are commonly used by physiotherapists; however, evidence regarding their effectiveness remains inconclusive because of inconsistent findings across randomized controlled trials (RCTs). This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the effects of Mulligan SNAGs on pain, lumbar mobility, and disability in adults with CNSLBP.Methods:A comprehensive search of 7 databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, MEDLINE, Embase, PEDro, and CINAHL) was conducted up to June 2025. Eligible studies were RCTs on SNAGs in CNSLBP, comparators including sham, standard physiotherapy, or other manual therapies, and outcomes on pain, lumbar range of motion (ROM), or disability. Study quality was assessed using the PEDro scale and the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias tool. Meta-analyses used random-effects models, reporting standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals.Results:Seventeen RCTs involving 716 participants, published between 2015 and 2024, met the inclusion criteria. Methodological quality ranged from fair to good (PEDro scores 4–9; mean = 6.1), with an overall low-to-moderate risk of bias, primarily related to allocation concealment and blinding. Qualitative synthesis demonstrated consistent within-group improvements in pain, mobility, and disability following SNAG interventions. Meta-analysis showed significant effects on pain intensity (SMD = −0.78; P < .001; I2 = 92%) and functional disability (SMD = −0.60; P < .001; I2 = 83%), with larger effects when disability was measured by the Roland–Morris Disability Questionnaire. SNAGs also improved lumbar extension ROM (SMD = 0.75; P < .001; I2 = 93%), although results for flexion ROM were not significant. Considerable heterogeneity was observed across analyses, likely reflecting differences in intervention protocols, comparator treatments, and follow-up durations. Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the findings. Certainty of evidence was moderate for pain and low for disability and mobility.Conclusion:Mulligan SNAGs were associated with improvements in pain, disability, and lumbar extension mobility in adults with CNSLBP. However, substantial heterogeneity, low-to-moderate certainty of evidence, and limited long-term follow-up warrant cautious interpretation. Further high-quality RCTs are needed to confirm these findings and determine their long-term effectiveness.

1. Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is one of the most prevalent and disabling musculoskeletal disorders worldwide,[1] with nearly all individuals experiencing at least 1 episode during their lifetime.[2] The Global Burden of Disease reports consistently rank LBP as the leading cause of disability across populations, surpassing other chronic conditions in terms of functional impairment and socioeconomic impact.[3] Beyond the global perspective, the economic cost of LBP is substantial, straining healthcare systems and productivity through increased medical utilization and reduced work participation.[1,2] Clinically, LBP is categorized as either specific, when a clear pathological cause such as fracture, infection, or disc herniation can be identified, or nonspecific, when symptoms cannot be attributed to a specific structural pathology.[4,5]

Chronic nonspecific low back pain (CNSLBP) is defined as pain persisting for more than twelve weeks without identifiable structural pathology.[4] Its management remains a significant challenge.[6,7] Pharmacological approaches often provide limited sustained benefits and are associated with adverse effects and risk of dependency, particularly with prolonged opioid use.[7,8] As a result, contemporary clinical guidelines emphasize conservative and noninvasive interventions that target both physical and neurophysiological dimensions of pain.[6] Physiotherapy interventions, particularly those grounded in the biopsychosocial model, have become central to CNSLBP management. Manual therapy (MT) is frequently incorporated within multimodal rehabilitation programs to restore mobility, modulate pain, and enhance function.[9,10] In addition to biomechanical benefits, MT may activate neurophysiological mechanisms at both spinal and supraspinal levels, contributing to pain modulation, improved proprioception, and reduced maladaptive behaviors such as fear avoidance.[11,12]

Among MT approaches, the Mulligan Concept is one of the most widely practiced methods, developed on the principle that minor positional faults of joints contribute to pain and restricted mobility.[13] Within this concept, Sustained Natural Apophyseal Glides (SNAGs) are a hallmark technique, involving the application of a sustained accessory glide to spinal facet joints while the patient performs an active movement.[13] SNAGs are considered to combine mechanical correction of joint dysfunction with immediate symptom relief, while also promoting active patient participation.[13] Evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) suggests that SNAGs may improve pain intensity, lumbar range of motion (ROM), and disability scores measured by validated tools such as the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), Numeric Pain Rating Scale (NPRS), Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), and Roland–Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ).[14-16] Moreover, SNAGs are generally safe and well-tolerated, with few reported adverse events, making them an accessible option in physiotherapy practice.[17]

Despite growing clinical application, the evidence for the effectiveness of SNAGs in CNSLBP remains inconclusive.[18] Several RCTs have reported inconsistent results, with variability in treatment protocols, therapist expertise, outcome measures, and follow-up periods.[19-22] Previous systematic reviews frequently combined acute, subacute, and chronic populations,[23,24] thereby diluting conclusions specific to CNSLBP. In addition, earlier reviews often lacked methodological rigor, failed to consistently apply standardized effect size reporting, and did not perform subgroup analyses to explain heterogeneity.[25] Consequently, current clinical practice guidelines for the management of nonspecific LBP do not provide specific recommendations regarding Mulligan SNAG mobilizations, largely because high-quality, condition-specific evidence remains limited.[18,26] Nevertheless, SNAGs may be considered within broader recommendations supporting MT as part of a multimodal rehabilitation approach for selected patients with nonspecific LBP.[27]

To address these limitations, the present systematic review and meta-analysis focused exclusively on RCTs involving adults with CNSLBP. By synthesizing evidence on pain intensity, lumbar ROM, and disability outcomes, this study aims to provide a more specific and methodologically robust evaluation of SNAGs. Additionally, this review critically appraises risk of bias and methodological quality while incorporating subgroup and sensitivity analyses to explore sources of heterogeneity. By narrowing the scope and strengthening methodological transparency, this review seeks to provide clinicians and policymakers with clearer evidence regarding the role of SNAGs in evidence-based rehabilitation for CNSLBP.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.[28] The study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with registration ID CRD42025623131. Although this review involved the secondary analysis of previously published studies and did not require separate ethical approval or informed consent, it was conducted as part of a broader research project that received ethical approval from the Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia Research Ethics Committee (JEPUKM; Approval No. JEP-2025-384). The review question was formulated using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome (PICO) framework:[29] “Are Mulligan SNAGs effective in reducing pain intensity, improving lumbar mobility, and reducing lumbar-related disability among adults with CNSLBP?”

2.2. Search strategy and data sources

A comprehensive electronic search was conducted across 7 major databases: PubMed, Web of Science (WOS), MEDLINE, Embase, PEDro, Scopus, and CINAHL. Records from inception to June 2025 were considered. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), free-text keywords, and Boolean operators (Table 1). Search terms included “low back pain,” “lumbar pain,” “chronic nonspecific low back pain,” “CNSLBP,” “SNAG,” “Mulligan mobilization,” “manual therapy,” “joint mobilization,” and “sustained natural apophyseal glide.” Boolean operators (AND, OR) and database-specific syntax adjustments were used to optimize results. Filters were applied to limit results to studies involving humans, RCTs, and English-language publications. In addition, reference lists of eligible articles and relevant reviews were screened manually to capture additional studies. Grey literature and unpublished data were not included.

2.3. Study selection criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: RCTs that investigated the effects of SNAGs in adults with CNSLBP, defined as pain persisting for more than twelve weeks without an identifiable pathological cause; participants aged 18 years or older, irrespective of sex or geographical setting; SNAGs administered as the primary intervention, either as a standalone approach or in combination with other physiotherapy techniques; comparator interventions including placebo or sham techniques, standard physiotherapy, McKenzie exercises, Maitland mobilizations, or electrotherapy modalities such as ultrasound or transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS); and at least 1 reported outcome measure related to pain intensity, lumbar mobility, or lumbar-related disability. Studies were included regardless of year of publication and restricted to English-language publications to ensure accurate interpretation of methodology and results. Studies were excluded if they were non-randomized, observational in design, case reports, reviews, editorials, or conference abstracts. Trials were also excluded if they involved acute or subacute LBP, surgical interventions, or pharmacological treatments.

2.4. Study screening, selection, and data extraction

All search results were imported into reference management software (EndNote), and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining records to identify potentially eligible studies. Full-text articles were then reviewed in detail against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. The study selection process was documented and visualized using a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram, outlining the number of records identified, screened, excluded, and finally included. A supplementary table provides details of excluded full-text studies with reasons for exclusion.

Data extraction was conducted independently by 2 reviewers using a standardized form to ensure consistency and accuracy. The data extraction form was pilot tested by the reviewers and refined for accuracy and uniformity prior to full data collection. Extracted information included study characteristics (author, year, and country), participant demographics, diagnostic criteria, details of the SNAG intervention (frequency, duration, targeted spinal level), comparator interventions, outcome measures (e.g., VAS, ODI, RMDQ), follow-up duration, and any reported adverse events. Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved by consensus or adjudication from a third reviewer.

2.5. Risk of bias and quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included RCTs was evaluated using the Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro) scale, which examines criteria such as random allocation, allocation concealment, baseline comparability, blinding, and the application of intention-to-treat analysis.[30] Studies scoring 6 or higher were classified as good to high quality, those with scores of 4 to 5 as fair quality, and scores of 3 or below as poor quality.[31] Risk of bias was further assessed using the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias (RoB) 2.0 tool, which evaluates 5 domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results.[30] Assessments were conducted independently by 2 reviewers, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion to ensure consensus.

2.6. Data synthesis and meta-analysis

Both qualitative synthesis and quantitative meta-analysis were undertaken in this review. Narrative synthesis was used to describe SNAG intervention protocols, participant characteristics, comparators, outcome measures, and reported findings across included studies. Quantitative meta-analyses were conducted using Review Manager software to pool treatment effects for pain intensity, lumbar mobility, and functional disability. Mean differences (MD) were calculated when studies employed the same outcome instrument, whereas standardized mean differences (SMDs) were applied when outcomes were measured using different instruments. To aid clinical interpretation, commonly reported minimum clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds for chronic LBP outcomes were considered alongside statistical significance.[32,33] Previous studies have suggested that a reduction of approximately 15 to 20 mm on a 100-mm VAS, or approximately 1.5 to 2.0 points on NPRS, represents a clinically important improvement in pain intensity.[32,33] For disability outcomes, an improvement of approximately 10 points on the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) has been proposed as a clinically meaningful change.[34] All pooled analyses were performed using a random-effects model to account for methodological and clinical variability, including differences in SNAG protocols, comparator interventions, and follow-up durations. Effect estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Where necessary, continuous outcomes were standardized to a uniform scale (e.g., VAS rescaled to 0–10) to enable synthesis. If studies reported multiple time points, the primary endpoint was the earliest post-intervention measure, while follow-up data were analyzed separately. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, with thresholds above 50% considered substantial.[35] Where high heterogeneity was identified, subgroup analyses were performed. For disability outcomes, analyses were stratified by the assessment tool (ODI, modified Oswestry disability index [MODI], or RMDQ). Additional subgroup comparisons examined treatment duration and the use of SNAGs alone or in combination with exercise-based interventions. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding trials with a high RoB or those contributing outlier effect sizes. Preplanned sensitivity analyses included exclusion of studies with PEDro scores < 6 and trials with unclear allocation concealment.[35] Forest plots were generated to present the meta-analysis results, providing a visual summary of study-specific estimates, confidence intervals, and study weights. Publication bias was assessed when ≥10 studies were available for an outcome using funnel plots.[35] The certainty of evidence for each outcome was appraised using the GRADE approach, which considers study limitations (RoB) and inconsistency.[36] Based on these domains, the quality of evidence was categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low.[36]

3. Results

3.1. Study selection process

A total of 1040 records were retrieved through comprehensive searches across 7 electronic databases: PubMed (n = 149), Scopus (n = 266), Web of Science (n = 30), PEDro (n = 12), MEDLINE (n = 52), Embase (n = 506), and CINAHL (n = 25). After removing 194 duplicates, 846 records remained. In addition, 3 eligible studies were identified through cross-reference searching, bringing the total number of records screened to 849. Of these, 799 records were excluded for not meeting the eligibility criteria, including studies that were not RCTs, those unrelated to SNAG interventions, reviews, letters to the editor, and studies focusing on conditions other than nonspecific LBP. A further 19 records were excluded after screening the title and abstract. This resulted in 31 full-text articles being assessed for eligibility (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1). Fourteen studies were excluded at this stage: 1 due to an unavailable full text and 13 that did not specify MT as the intervention. Ultimately, 17 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the final review. The selection process adhered to PRISMA 2020 guidelines and is illustrated in Figure 1. Data extraction was carried out using a standardized form that captured study design, participant characteristics, intervention protocols, comparator details, outcome measures, and key findings. All data were independently extracted and cross-checked by 2 reviewers to ensure accuracy.

3.2. Characteristics of included studies

Seventeen RCTs with a total of 716 participants were included. Sample sizes ranged from 18 to 84, and participants were aged between 18 and 60 years. The studies were conducted across multiple countries, including India, Turkey, Egypt, Pakistan, Belgium, and Saudi Arabia, and were published between 2015,[37] and 2024.[38] All studies investigated Mulligan SNAGs, either as a standalone intervention or in combination with other physiotherapy modalities. Comparators included sham SNAGs,[15,19,20,37] McKenzie exercises,[39-41] Maitland mobilizations,[42-44] myofascial release,[14] low-level laser therapy,[45] and electrotherapy techniques such as TENS and ultrasound.[46,47] A detailed overview of each study is presented in Table 2.

3.3. Outcome measures and assessment tools

Pain intensity was reported in 15 trials. Twelve studies used the VAS,[14,15,19,20,37,38,41,44-46,48,49] and 3 used the NPRS.[40,42,47] Lumbar ROM or spinal flexibility was evaluated in 14 studies using measurement tools such as a goniometer,[15,38,40,44,47,49] the Modified-Modified Schober Test,[42,45] or a digital inclinometer.[14,41,43] Functional disability was reported in all except 2 studies.[43,48] Twelve trials employed the ODI or its modified version (MODI),[14,15,19,37-41,44,47-49] while 1 study used the Oswestry Low Back Pain Disability Questionnaire (OLPDQ).[42] Two studies used the RMDQ.[45,46]

3.4. Methodological quality and risk of bias assessment

The 17 studies included in this review and meta-analysis demonstrated methodological quality ranging from fair (42%, n = 4) to good (76.5%, n = 13), with PEDro scores between 4 and 9 and a mean score of 6.1 as shown in Figure 2. Four studies were double-blinded,[19,37,39,43] four were single-blinded[14,20,42,46] and 9 studies did not specify the blinding method.[15,38,40,41,44,45,47-49] The methodological quality of all included RCTs was assessed using the Cochrane ROB 2.0 tool. Figure 3 provides visual summaries of the judgments across studies for each domain. Overall, the included studies demonstrated varying levels of methodological rigor. Most trials showed a low risk of bias in random sequence generation, with 11 out of 17 studies explicitly describing appropriate randomization procedures.[15,19,20,37-40,42,43,46,47]

Allocation concealment was clearly reported in some trials, while others were rated as unclear[15,38,40,42,46] or high risk,[14,41,45,47-49] indicating a potential for selection bias. Blinding of participants and personnel was consistently marked as high risk across nearly all included trials, reflecting the inherent difficulty of implementing participant blinding in physiotherapy interventions. Only 1 study[37] achieved a low-risk rating in this domain.

Blinding of outcome assessors showed variable reporting. Several studies were judged as unclear due to limited information on whether outcome assessments were blinded,[38,41,47,49] raising concerns about detection bias. In terms of incomplete outcome data, most studies demonstrated a low risk of bias, suggesting adequate handling of missing data and participant attrition. Only a few trials lacked sufficient reporting in this area, though no consistent pattern of concern was observed. Selective reporting was generally well managed, with most studies judged to be at low risk. Most outcomes reported in the methods section were also presented in the results, supporting transparency in outcome reporting. Overall, the risk of bias across included studies was moderate, with most concerns arising from the lack of blinding and inconsistent allocation concealment procedures.

3.5. Synthesis of findings

3.5.1. Pain intensity

Pain intensity was assessed in 15 out of the 17 included RCTs using validated instruments such as the VAS and NPRS.[14,15,19,20,37,38,40-42,44-49] Two studies did not assess pain-related outcomes.[39,43] Most studies reported statistically significant within-group reductions in pain following SNAGs interventions. Improvements were observed both at short-term endpoints and sustained follow-up periods. Between-group comparisons showed that SNAGs consistently outperformed control interventions, including sham techniques,[15,19,20,37] conventional physiotherapy,[38,48,49] and passive modalities such as TENS and ultrasound.[46,47] In several trials, SNAGs were also applied in combination with active interventions such as McKenzie extension exercises,[39-41] Maitland mobilizations,[42,49] and myofascial release,[14] where they demonstrated comparable or higher pain-relieving effects.

3.5.2. Lumbar mobility

Fourteen studies reported changes in lumbar ROM, primarily focusing on flexion and extension.[14,15,37,38,40-49] SNAGs consistently led to significant within-group improvements in lumbar flexion and extension. Some studies also reported positive changes in lateral flexion and rotation, although these were less consistent.[40,41,44,47-49] Between-group comparisons revealed mixed results. Certain trials demonstrated higher gains in ROM for the SNAGs group compared to control or active treatments,[38,46-49] while others showed more modest or comparable effects.[14,15,37]

3.5.3. Lumbar-related disability

Fifteen trials assessed disability outcomes using the ODI, MODI, or RMDQ.[14,15,19,37-42,44-49] Most studies demonstrated significant within-group reductions in disability scores following SNAGs interventions.[14,15,37-42,44-49] However, between-group findings were less consistent. Several studies reported greater improvements with SNAGs compared to sham or standard care,[38,48] while others reported no significant difference when SNAGs were compared to other active approaches, such as Maitland mobilizations or electrotherapy.[37,42]

3.6. Meta-analysis of pain intensity outcomes (n = 14 studies)

A total of 14 RCTs involving 583 participants (295 in the experimental group and 288 in the control group) evaluated pain intensity using validated scales such as the VAS and NPRS.[14,19,20,37,38,40-42,44-49] The SMD was calculated due to variability in pain assessment tools across studies. The pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in pain favoring the SNAGs intervention (SMD = −0.78, 95% CI: −1.43 to −0.13; P = .02; Fig. 4). Although statistically significant, the pooled effect was lower than the commonly cited MCID threshold of approximately 1.5 to 2.0 points for pain improvement in chronic LBP; therefore, its clinical importance should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, substantial heterogeneity was observed among the included studies (P < .0001; I2 = 92%), indicating variability in participant characteristics, SNAG protocols, comparator interventions, and follow-up periods. A sensitivity analysis excluding studies that used the NPRS and including only VAS-based studies revealed a larger pooled effect favoring Mulligan SNAGs (SMD = −1.23, 95% CI: −1.86 to −0.59). This finding suggests that the pain-relieving effect remained statistically significant when restricted to studies using a common pain assessment tool. The variation in pain assessment instruments may have contributed to differences in the pooled estimates, as reflected by the change in effect size. However, this sensitivity analysis should be interpreted with caution because of the reduced number of included studies.

3.7. Meta-analysis of lumbar mobility (flexion) outcomes (n = 8 studies)

Seven RCTs involving 318 participants (160 in the experimental group and 158 in the control group) evaluated lumbar flexion ROM as a primary outcome following Mulligan SNAGs intervention.[14,38,41,44,47,49] The SMD was determined due to differences in assessment methods among studies. The pooled analysis showed a significant difference between the 2 groups post-intervention, favoring the experimental intervention (SMD = 0.66, 95% CI: −0.07 to 1.39; P = .08) as shown in Figure 5. However, significant heterogeneity was found among the included studies (P < .0001; I2 = 89%), reflecting differences in participant characteristics, intervention protocols, comparator interventions, and follow-up periods.

3.8. Meta-analysis of lumbar mobility (extension) outcomes (n = 8 studies)

Seven RCTs involving 318 participants (160 in the experimental group and 158 in the control group) evaluated lumbar flexion ROM as a primary outcome following Mulligan SNAGs intervention.[14,38,41,44,47,49] The pooled analysis showed a significant difference between the 2 groups post-intervention, favoring the experimental intervention (SMD = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.50–1.00; P = .0001; Fig. 6). However, significant heterogeneity was found among the included studies (P < .0001; I2 = 93%), reflecting differences in participant characteristics, intervention protocols, comparator interventions, and follow-up periods.

3.9. Meta-analysis of lumbar-related disability outcomes (n = 14 studies)

A total of 14 RCTs involving 599 participants (304 in the experimental group and 295 in the control group) evaluated functional disability using validated instruments, including the ODI, MODI, and RMDQ. The overall pooled effect showed a statistically significant reduction in disability favoring the Mulligan SNAGs intervention (SMD = −0.60, 95% CI: −1.02 to −0.18; P = .005; Fig. 7). Although statistically significant, the pooled effect should be interpreted cautiously when considered alongside commonly reported MCID thresholds for disability, including an improvement of approximately 10 points on the ODI. Furthermore, substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (P < .001; I2 = 83%). To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, a subgroup analysis was conducted according to the disability outcome measure used. Twelve studies using the ODI or MODI (n = 530) demonstrated a statistically significant but smaller effect size (SMD = −0.47, 95% CI: −0.92 to −0.02; P = .04; I2 = 84%). In contrast, 2 studies using the RMDQ (n = 69) showed a larger and more consistent treatment effect with no observed heterogeneity (SMD = −1.41, 95% CI: −1.95 to −0.88; P < .001; I2 = 0%).

A funnel plot including 14 RCTs evaluating pain intensity demonstrated a relatively symmetrical distribution of effect sizes around the pooled estimate, suggesting no significant small-study effects or publication bias (Fig. 8). Although some asymmetry was observed at the lower precision range, overall findings did not indicate systematic bias.

3.11. Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was evaluated using the GRADE approach. Pain outcomes were rated as moderate certainty, downgraded for risk of bias related to allocation concealment[14,41,44,45,47-49] and blinding.[14,19,20,38,40-42,44-49] Disability outcomes were rated as low certainty, reflecting substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 83%), risk of bias due to allocation concealment and blinding issues, and imprecision across disability measures such as ODI, MODI, and RMDQ. Lumbar mobility outcomes were also rated as low certainty because of high heterogeneity (I2 > 89%), risk of bias from allocation concealment and blinding concerns, and variability (goniometer and Modified-Modified Schober Test) in assessment tools.

4. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis suggest that Mulligan SNAGs may provide short-term benefits in reducing pain, improving lumbar mobility, and decreasing disability in adults with CNSLBP. Across the 17 included RCTs, the overall direction of effect generally favored SNAGs. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the certainty of evidence ranged from moderate to low, and substantial heterogeneity was observed across several pooled outcomes. High heterogeneity was identified for pain intensity, lumbar mobility, and disability outcomes, indicating considerable between-study variability in treatment effects. Therefore, while the pooled findings support the potential role of SNAGs within multimodal physiotherapy management, they do not imply consistent benefits across all patient populations, clinical settings, or intervention protocols. Publication bias assessment for pain outcomes did not indicate substantial asymmetry, suggesting that major small-study effects were unlikely, although some risk cannot be completely excluded. Furthermore, the GRADE assessment indicated moderate certainty of evidence for pain outcomes and low certainty for disability and mobility outcomes, primarily due to methodological limitations and inconsistency across studies.

4.1. Meta-analytic findings and clarification of inconsistent evidence

One of the main findings of this review was the statistically significant reduction in pain intensity associated with SNAG interventions. The pooled analysis demonstrated a moderate effect favoring SNAGs (SMD = −0.78; P = .02). Potential mechanisms include the restoration of spinal arthrokinematics, stimulation of joint mechanoreceptors,[13] and modulation of central pain processing pathways.[50,51] Emerging evidence suggests that MT may also influence cortical excitability, pain perception, and autonomic nervous system activity, providing additional neurophysiological explanations for the observed analgesic effects.[12] These mechanisms are consistent with contemporary models of MT for chronic musculoskeletal pain.[52,53] From a clinical perspective, pain reduction may facilitate participation in rehabilitation and improve engagement with active treatment strategies.[7,54]

To aid clinical interpretation, the findings should be considered alongside commonly reported MCID thresholds for chronic LBP outcomes.[32,33,55] Although the pooled analyses demonstrated statistically significant improvements favoring SNAGs, the clinical importance of these effects remains uncertain because the pooled estimates did not directly evaluate MCID achievement, and substantial heterogeneity was observed across several outcomes. Therefore, the magnitude of benefit should be interpreted cautiously despite the favorable direction of effect.

Improvements in lumbar ROM, particularly extension, were also observed, with pooled analyses demonstrating a significant effect (SMD = 0.75; P < .001). Enhanced lumbar mobility is an important therapeutic target in CNSLBP because movement restrictions are commonly associated with disability and reduced quality of life.[9] The observed improvements may reflect reductions in joint stiffness, restoration of movement patterns, and enhanced proprioceptive feedback.[25,51] Previous reviews examining Mulligan techniques in related lumbar conditions, including sciatica, have similarly reported beneficial effects on pain and mobility.[24] These findings suggest that the movement-based and neurodynamic characteristics of Mulligan mobilizations may contribute to improved functional movement across different lumbar disorders. Nevertheless, the substantial heterogeneity observed in the mobility analyses highlights the influence of methodological differences, including measurement tools, therapist expertise, targeted spinal levels, and treatment protocols.[42,47,49]

Functional disability outcomes also favored SNAGs, with a pooled SMD of−0.60 (P = .005). Subgroup analyses indicated that disability improvements were more pronounced when measured using the RMDQ (SMD = −1.41; I2 = 0%) compared with the ODI or MODI. This difference may reflect variations in responsiveness between disability instruments, with the RMDQ potentially being more sensitive to changes in activity limitations among individuals with CNSLBP.[56] Similar improvements in functional outcomes have been reported in studies investigating Mulligan mobilization techniques in related lumbar conditions.[24] Collectively, these findings suggest that SNAGs may contribute to functional improvements that are relevant to daily activities and participation.

The considerable residual heterogeneity observed in this review likely reflects both clinical and methodological diversity among the included trials. Potential contributors include differences in SNAG dosage and repetition, spinal levels treated, therapist experience, comparator interventions, adjunctive therapies, participant characteristics, and follow-up duration. Although subgroup analysis based on disability outcome measures partially explained some variability, heterogeneity remained substantial across several pooled analyses, indicating that differences in measurement tools alone do not fully account for the observed variation. Meta-regression was considered but was not undertaken because the number of studies contributing to individual pooled outcomes was limited, and the included trials were clinically heterogeneous, making such analyses underpowered and potentially unreliable. Consequently, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were considered more appropriate approaches for exploring heterogeneity in this review.

4.2. Clinical Implications

This review builds upon previous evidence syntheses that reported inconclusive findings regarding the effectiveness of Mulligan mobilization techniques. Chitale et al reported positive effects of Mulligan mobilization for chronic LBP but highlighted the limited availability of rigorous studies focused specifically on CNSLBP.[23] Similarly, Hussein et al reported beneficial effects of Mulligan mobilizations in individuals with sciatica while emphasizing the need for higher-quality trials.[24] The present review extends these findings by focusing exclusively on adults with CNSLBP and synthesizing evidence from RCTs using contemporary systematic review methods. The findings are broadly consistent with clinical practice guidelines that support the use of MT as part of a multimodal rehabilitation approach for nonspecific LBP.[6,57] However, most guidelines do not specifically endorse Mulligan SNAG mobilizations and instead recommend MT in combination with exercise and active rehabilitation strategies.[18] Consequently, the current findings should not be interpreted as evidence supporting SNAGs as a standalone intervention but rather as evidence suggesting that they may have a role within a broader rehabilitation framework.

SNAGs may represent a practical component of multimodal physiotherapy management because they are typically performed within a pain-free range of movement and may facilitate patient participation in active rehabilitation strategies.[13] In addition, self-SNAG techniques can potentially support self-management and reinforce adherence to home-based exercise programs.[13] These characteristics align with contemporary rehabilitation models that emphasize active engagement, movement confidence, and self-management in individuals with persistent musculoskeletal pain.[11] Nevertheless, most included studies evaluated outcomes immediately following treatment or over relatively short follow-up periods. Therefore, the current evidence primarily supports short-term improvements in pain, mobility, and disability. Evidence regarding sustained benefits remains limited, and the long-term effectiveness of SNAG interventions remains uncertain. Furthermore, the substantial heterogeneity and low-to-moderate certainty of evidence reduce confidence in the magnitude and generalizability of the observed treatment effects. Accordingly, the clinical implications of these findings should be interpreted cautiously until more robust evidence becomes available.

4.3. Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths, including adherence to PRISMA 2020 guidelines, prospective registration in PROSPERO, comprehensive database searches, and rigorous quality assessment using the PEDro scale and RoB 2.0 tool. The exclusive focus on adults with CNSLBP addresses an important gap in the literature and provides more condition-specific evidence regarding the potential role of SNAGs in physiotherapy practice. Furthermore, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed to explore sources of heterogeneity and assess the robustness of the findings.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The GRADE assessment indicated moderate certainty of evidence for pain outcomes but low certainty for disability and lumbar mobility outcomes, primarily because of methodological limitations, inconsistency, and imprecision across studies.[36] Performance and detection bias were common because participant, therapist, and assessor blinding are inherently challenging in MT research.[58] In addition, several studies reported unclear allocation concealment, increasing the risk of selection bias. Consequently, some of the observed treatment effects may have been influenced by methodological bias.

Another important limitation is that most included studies evaluated outcomes immediately after treatment or over relatively short follow-up periods. Therefore, current evidence primarily supports short-term benefits, while the sustainability of treatment effects remains uncertain. Future research should prioritize well-designed RCTs with standardized SNAG protocols, improved methodological rigor, and longer follow-up periods. Comparative effectiveness studies against other guideline-supported interventions and investigations of broader patient-centered outcomes, including psychological well-being and quality of life, would further clarify the role of SNAGs in the management of CNSLBP.

5. Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis suggest that Mulligan SNAGs may improve pain, lumbar mobility, and disability in adults with CNSLBP, particularly in the short term. However, substantial heterogeneity, methodological limitations within the included trials, and low-to-moderate certainty of evidence warrant cautious interpretation of these findings. While SNAGs may be incorporated as part of a multimodal physiotherapy approach, further high-quality, standardized RCTs with longer follow-up periods are required to confirm the sustainability, generalizability, and clinical relevance of the observed effects.
