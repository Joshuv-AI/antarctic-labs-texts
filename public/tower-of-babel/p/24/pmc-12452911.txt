From molecular damage and viscoelasticity to interfacial fracture in soft polymer networks: Insights from mechanochemistry
Authors: Anthony Arrowood, Jackson Frazier, Matteo Ciccotti, Gabriel E. Sanoja
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceUnraveling the interplay between time-dependent material properties and the dynamic process of crack propagation is essential for developing a fundamental understanding of fracture in soft polymer networks. Such knowledge is critical for predicting and preventing catastrophic failures across engineering and biological applications and for enabling the rational design of advanced materials with sufficient toughness and predictable service lifetime under diverse loading and environmental conditions. This study illustrates the relationship between loading rate, temperature, and molecular-level damage in soft polymer networks, highlighting a nonmonotonic coupling between chain friction and breakage in dissipating energy ahead of an interfacial crack.

Numerous soft and tough materials have recently emerged (1–4), enabling advances in healthcare, energy, and manufacturing technologies such as artificial prosthetics (5, 6), wearable electronics (7), and soft robotics (8, 9). However, understanding the interfacial fracture behavior of these materials remains an utter challenge due to the critical role played by multiple length and time scales in the energy dissipation mechanisms ahead of cracks (10, 11); for example, damage from chain scission and viscoelasticity from molecular friction.

This work aims to provide a multiscale understanding of interfacial fracture by unraveling the interplay between viscoelasticity and damage ahead of interfacial cracks. It focuses on a specific class of soft materials, adhesives, which are three-dimensional polymer networks thermally equilibrated at temperatures well above the glass transition temperature, T≫Tg (10, 12–14). Unlike metals, ceramics, and glasses, these materials do not form a well-defined process zone ahead of an interfacial defect or crack, (10, 11) making it challenging to i) pinpoint the exact moment and location where polymer chains break to nucleate a crack, and ii) develop predictive fracture models that treat the process zone as more than just an energy sink or a zero-thickness cohesive zone (15–20). Experiments that reveal the relationship between network chain scission, energy dissipation, and crack nucleation and growth could significantly enhance the ability of state-of-the-art models to predict interfacial fracture.

Mechanochemistry is an example of such an experiment, offering insight into stress or damage fields ahead of cracks (21–25). This technique relies on detection and quantification of the activation of mechanophores—molecular probes that change the bulk photophysical properties in response to an applied force (24, 26, 27)—in regions of high stress concentration, such as crack fronts subjected to continuous, cyclic, static, and hydrostatic loads (23, 28–37). For example, Slootman et al. crosslinked a series of poly(methyl acrylate) elastomers (Tg=15°C) with a fluorogenic mechanophore and examined the interplay between viscoelasticity and damage ahead of a mode-I cohesive crack (23). By fracturing prenotched specimens in single-edge notch tests and imaging the regions next to the fracture surfaces with a confocal microscope, Slootman et al. demonstrated that conditions favoring viscoelastic dissipation, such as high rates and low temperatures, lead to increased mechanophore activation and molecular damage. Their work revealed a coupling between viscoelasticity and damage ahead of cracks, challenging longstanding viscoelastic fracture theories that treat these energy dissipation mechanisms separately (17, 19, 38).

Indeed, due to the lack of molecular insights into the dissipation mechanisms ahead of cracks, the fracture of soft viscoelastic materials has historically been described using an empirical expression, Γ(vcrack)=Γ0·[1+f(aTvcrack)] (16, 17, 19, 38–40), which treats the rate-independent dissipation due to damage, Γ0, separate from the rate-dependent dissipation due to viscoelasticity, Γ0·f(aTvcrack). In this expression, aT and vcrack represent the linear viscoelastic shift factor and the crack propagation velocity, respectively. However, using linear viscoelasticity to describe Γ(vcrack) leads to two paradoxes in the fracture behavior of soft viscoelastic solids (11, 18, 41). First, linear viscoelastic fracture theories lack an inherent length or energy scale, predicting a local energy release rate independent of crack propagation velocity, vcrack—inconsistent with experiments (42). Second, resolution of this issue with a cohesive zone results in a finite length scale, on the order of a few Å, which is unphysically small and inconsistent with physical expectations (15, 16, 19, 43).

Understanding time-dependent fracture thus requires the use of nonlinear viscoelastic models to address the paradoxes of linear viscoelasticity, like that of Guo et al. (41). In this model, localized viscoelastic processes, such as chain friction, reduce stress concentrations ahead of cracks, giving rise to a well-defined dissipation zone that depends on strain, strain history, and damage.

With these considerations in mind, this work examines the relationship between viscoelasticity and damage ahead of an interfacial crack; complementing Slootman et al. by focusing on adhesive rather than cohesive fracture, and Guo et al. by experimentally mapping and quantifying localized damage. In this geometry, a polymer network bonds to a stiff, flexible backing layer and a stiff, rigid substrate, and the energy required to propagate an interfacial crack depends not only on the network stiffness (i.e., crosslink density) and viscoelasticity but also on the density and strength of the interfacial bonds. In 1975, Ahagon et al. elegantly illustrated this point (39, 44), studying the impact of rate, temperature, and surface density of vinyl bonds on the adherence energy of uncrosslinked and crosslinked poly(butadiene) networks. They found that i) the adherence energy, Γ, of crosslinked networks increases with the substrate density of vinyl bonds under near-equilibrium conditions—at low rates and high temperatures, ii) the adherence energy, Γ, of crosslinked networks becomes comparable to the cohesive energy at high substrate density of vinyl bonds, and iii) the adherence energy, Γ, of polymer networks is relatively insensitive to both crosslink density and substrate density of vinyl bonds at high rates and low temperatures. Together, these results led Ahagon et. al. to postulate that interfacial fracture involves energy dissipation ahead of an interfacial crack through viscoelasticity and damage, much like in cohesive fracture.

However, the picture painted by Ahagon et al. raises several questions that remain unresolved, particularly regarding the rate- and temperature-dependence of the adherence energy. Both viscoelasticity and damage contribute to energy dissipation under loading conditions; however, feature distinct activation barriers. Specifically, damage due to chain scission follows the Bell–Evans–Polanyi theory of force-induced chemical reactions, whereas viscoelasticity due to chain friction obeys the Williams–Landel–Ferry equation for dynamics of polymer chains. Thus, the relative contributions of viscoelasticity and damage to energy dissipation—and, thus, to the adherence energy—should depend on both the peeling rate and temperature—a hypothesis.

This work addresses this hypothesis by answering two key questions.

In addressing these questions, this work integrates recent advances in polymer mechanochemistry with more conventional mechanical testing and confocal microscopy (Fig. 1), considering the relationship between viscoelasticity and damage in poly(methyl acrylate-co-ethyl hexyl acrylate) adhesives labeled with the same fluorogenic mechanophore used by Slootman et al. (23) and bonded to a PET substrate via physical, noncovalent bonds—mainly van der Waals interactions. These adhesives, containing methyl acrylate fractions of 80, 90, and 100 wt%, were debonded in a T-peel geometry across a range of peeling rates and temperatures, and their damage was quantified postmortem using a confocal microscope. The results reveal a more intricate coupling between viscoelasticity and damage than that observed by Slootman et al. for cohesive fracture (23); with damage contributing most significantly to energy dissipation at deformation timescales comparable to the network relaxation time and exhibiting two distinct regimes. Overall, these findings refine the current understanding of interfacial fracture, informing the molecular design of polymer networks for increasingly demanding adhesive applications.

Materials and Methods

Materials.

Unless otherwise specified, all chemicals were used as received. Methyl acrylate (MA), 2-ethylhexyl acrylate (EHA), and 1,4-butanediol diacrylate (BDA) were sourced from TCI America; 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenone (HMP), anhydrous THF, triethylamine, acryloyl chloride, and hydroquinone from Millipore-Sigma; basic aluminum oxide, dichloromethane (DCM), sodium chloride, sodium bicarbonate, and anhydrous DMSO from VWR; and the π-extended anthracene-maleimide adduct, diol precursor of the fluorogenic mechanophore (DACL), from Concept Life Sciences.

MA (≈50 mL), EHA (≈20 mL), and BDA (≈20 mL) were purified by elution over basic aluminum oxide. Purified MA, EHA, BDA, and as-received HMP, were placed in dry septum-sealed bottles, sparged with N2 for 45 min, and transferred into a N2-filled glovebox.

PET sheets of 0.005” thickness were sourced from McMaster-Carr (Item No. 8567K52) and used as received.

Synthesis of Fluorogenic Mechanophore.

The fluorogenic mechanophore (DACL) was synthesized following the procedure reported by Göstl et al. (45) Briefly, the diol precursor (50 mg, 0.11 mmol) was dissolved in anhydrous THF (2 mL) and activated with excess triethylamine (160 μL, 1.1 mmol). Separately, acryloyl chloride (90 μL, 1.1 mmol) was dissolved in anhydrous THF (3 mL), previously cooled in an ice bath and equilibrated to approximately 0 °C. The diol solution was added dropwise to the acryloyl chloride solution, and the reaction was allowed to proceed overnight at room temperature.

The following day, DI water (25 mL) was added to the reaction mixture, and the reaction product was extracted with DCM (25 mL). The organic layer was sequentially washed with saturated sodium bicarbonate (3 × 25 mL) and saturated sodium chloride (3 × 25 mL), dried with magnesium sulfate, filtered, and stabilized with hydroquinone (1 mg, 0.01 mmol). The DCM was then evaporated under vacuum, yielding the mechanophore crosslinker, DACL, as a pale-yellow solid. This mechanophore was dried overnight at room temperature in a vacuum oven (see 1H NMR in SI Appendix, Fig. S1) and used without further purification.

Synthesis of Polymer Networks.

Polymer networks were synthesized in a N2-filled glovebox, following the procedure reported by Slootman et al. (23). Briefly, monomers MA and EHA (98.8 mol%), crosslinkers BDA (0.19 mol%) and DACL (0.01 mol%), and photoinitiator HMP (1 mol%) were mixed in a scintillation vial. This solution was then injected into a mold consisting of two glass plates coated with PET and separated and sealed by a silicone rubber spacer, approximately 0.8 mm thick. The copolymerizations were initiated with UV light (365 nm, 0.30 mW.cm−2) and allowed to proceed for 2 h, resulting in PET-polymer network-PET bonds, which were then cut into tapes (1 cm wide and ≈5 cm length) or flat cylindrical specimens (8 mm diameter) for mechanical testing.

Rheology.

Polymer networks were punch-cut into cylindrical specimens of 8 mm diameter, and their rheological properties were evaluated in an Anton-Paar MCR302e rheometer equipped with stainless steel flat plates of the same diameter. Frequency sweeps from 0.01 to 100 rad.s−1 at temperatures from 23 to 100 °C were performed within the linear viscoelastic regime at a strain of 1%. Master curves for the storage, μ’, and loss, μ”, moduli were constructed by Time-Temperature-Superposition (TTS), using a reference temperature of 23 °C and horizontal, aT, and vertical, bT, shift factors (SI Appendix, Figs. S2 and S3).

T-Peel Tests.

T-peel tests were used to characterize the adherence energy, Γ. For these tests, the free regions of the PET backing layers were clamped to an Instron 34TM5 tensile tester equipped with a 100 N load cell and an oven. The adhesives were then equilibrated at a specified temperature for 30 min before testing. Finally, the backing layers were separated at a constant displacement rate while the force was measured.

Force–displacement data were recorded continuously during the peeling tests. From each force–displacement trace, the middle 50% of the steady-state peeling region was identified, and the corresponding forces were averaged to obtain the mean peel force, F, along with its SD. The adherence energy, Γ, was then calculated from the energy release rate, G, using the relation G=2Fb=Γ, where b is the tape width (1 cm). All values of Γ are reported as mean ± SD.

To measure the adhesive strain, vertical lines were drawn before peeling on the exposed ends of the adhesive using a permanent marker. T-peel tests were then conducted on a custom-built tensile tester, which enabled high-resolution optical imaging of the peel front during steady-state debonding (Fig. 1B).

The average strain at the peel front was then estimated using an Equivalent Fibril Model (46), according to ε=L−L0L0, where L0 is the initial adhesive thickness, and L is the maximum stretched length at the peel front. These lengths were measured by monitoring the vertical marker lines on the tape and analyzing side-view images using Inkscape. For each peel test, multiple measurements were taken during steady-state peeling to calculate the mean strain, ε and its SD.

T-peel tests were conducted across a range of peeling rates and temperatures to examine the impact of viscoelasticity and damage on both the adherence energy, Γ, and the average strain at the peel front, ε. However, side-view imaging was limited to room temperature due to difficulties in placing the custom-built tensile tester inside the oven.

Confocal Microscopy.

Peeled specimens were rinsed with ethanol and placed flat on a glass slide, positioned upside down under the lens of a confocal microscope. The imaging path passed through the glass slide, ensuring alignment between the imaging and fracture (i.e., peeled surface) planes. Fluorescent images were acquired using a Nikon AXR Confocal System (Galvano and Resonant Scanner), equipped with a CFI60 Plan Apochromat Lambda D 20× objective lens (N.A. 0.80, W.D. 0.80 mm, F.O.V. 25 mm, DIC, spring-loaded).

π-Extended anthracene fluorophores were excited using a 405-nm laser, and their fluorescent emission was recorded in the 424 to 524 nm range in resonant mode. The optical magnification was set to 10×, resulting in 2,048 × 2,048 raw images with a spatial resolution of 0.84 μm.pixel−1. 3D scans were collected from the specimen surface (plane of maximum intensity) through a depth of 100 μm, with a step size of 2 μm.

Damage Quantification.

The fluorescence intensity, I(x,y,z,) was used to estimate the number of activated mechanophores, following the procedure introduced by Slootman et al. (23). First, the background intensity I0 was determined from the average fluorescence in the undamaged regions (i.e., beyond 100 μm from the peeled surface). Then, each voxel intensity was used to determine the number density of activated mechanophores, ϕ(x,y,z), according to:[1]ϕ(x,y,z)=I(x,y,z)−I0αc0,

where α is a linear coefficient relating the fluorescence intensity to the activated mechanophore concentration, and c0 is the mechanophore concentration in the adhesive network (≈1.0 mol.m−3). This density was assumed to represent the number of broken chains per unit volume, as in Slootman et al. (23) [see the representative calibration curve in SI Appendix, Fig. S11, and detailed discussion on the representativity assumption in Appendices 6 and 7 of Slootman et al. (23), as well as in ref. 47).

The number density of activated mechanophores was averaged in each imaged plane, z, according to ϕ(z)=1LxLy∫0Lx∫0Lyϕ(x,y)dxdy, where Lx=1.7 mm and Ly=1.7 mm are the dimensions of the imaged plane along the tape width, x, and length, y, respectively (see representative images in Fig. 1C).

The average density ϕ(z) was then integrated along the first 100 μm of the adhesive thickness, Lz = 100 μm, since beyond this depth the adhesives were not fluorescent. This integral was used to estimate the number of broken network chains per unit area of propagated crack, Σ, according to Σ=νx∫0Lzϕ(z)dz, where νx is the elastic chain density—evaluated from the plateau modulus, μp′, according to νx=μp′/kBT. This quantification procedure was conducted in at least four images, distributed along the adhesive length and near the midpoint of its width, to estimate the average and SD of Σ.

To compare damage measurements with previous reports on cohesive fracture, Σ was normalized by the areal density of elastic chains, Σ0, as estimated from the Lake and Thomas theory of fracture (48). This normalization offered the number of elastic chain monolayers that break upon interfacial fracture, herein referred to as Σ¯.[2]Σ0=12νxR022=l0μp′ρC∞NA2M0kBT1/2,

where R022 is the average distance between crosslinks, μp′ is the plateau modulus, l0 is the length of a C–C bond (l0≈0.154 nm), ρ is the density of pol(methyl acrylate) (1.2 g.cm−3), C∞ is the characteristic ratio (C∞≈ 8.1), NA is Avogadro’s number, and M0 is the molecular weight of methyl acrylate (M0 = 86 g.mol−1).

Results

Viscoelasticity Governs the Adherence Energy, as Well as the Average Strain at the Peel Front.

In line with the viscoelasticity of the networks, the adherence energy, Γ, varied with both rate and temperature. At 23 °C, Γ increased from 0.23 kJ.m−2 at 0.002 s−1 to 4.2 kJ.m−2 at 6.25 s−1; conversely, at 1.25 s−1, Γ decreased from 3.7 kJ.m−2 at 23 °C to 0.042 kJ.m−2 at 70 °C (see representative peel curves in SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Dimensional analysis on these results revealed a power-law relationship, Γ∼Wiα, where α=0.47±0.06. Here, Wi=ε˙τ is a nondimensional Weissenberg number defined by the average strain rate at the peel front, ε˙≃vcrackL0, and the network relaxation time, τ; and L0 is the undeformed adhesive thickness (see Fig. 3, and details on the fitting protocol in SI Appendix, section 7). This scaling is similar to that reported by Gent and coworkers for other soft viscoelastic networks (16) and underpins linear viscoelastic fracture theories, which describe the adhesion energy as Γ(vcrack)=Γ0·[1+f(aTvcrack)]. In these theories, Γ0 represents the intrinsic, rate-independent fracture energy, Γ0f(aTvcrack) accounts for viscoelastic rate-dependent dissipation, and aT is the linear viscoelastic shift factor. However, defining the Weissenberg number using only the average strain rate, ε˙, oversimplifies the true deformation field by neglecting strain concentrations at the peel front and assuming uniform strain rates throughout the network. In reality, these concentrations lead to much higher local strain rates and correspondingly larger local Weissenberg numbers, Wi,local=ε˙localτ, which are difficult to measure with standard techniques such as Digital Image Correlation (DIC).

As opposed to the adherence energy, Γ, which monotonically increased with Wi, the average strain at the peel front, ε, exhibited a transition in its rate- and temperature-dependence at a critical Weissenberg number Wi,c≈0.3 (Fig. 4). In the Wi<Wi,c regime, the strain followed a power-law scaling of the form ε∼Wiα, where α=0.39±0.15, likely because the networks had sufficient time to relax stress and deform during peeling. In contrast, in the Wi>Wi,c regime, the strain had a very weak negative slope (ε∼Wiβ, where β=−0.07±0.09) with average ε≈1.3±0.2, presumably because of hindered chain dynamics. These observations suggest that the enhancement in adherence energy, Γ∼Wi0.47, stems from two distinct dissipation mechanisms. First, increased dissipation due to large deformations at the peel front—that is, polymer chain elongation and breakage. Second, increased dissipation because of hindered chain dynamics and enhanced stiffness—that is, chain friction. Both dissipation mechanisms contribute to the adherence energy measured in interfacial fracture, with their relative importance dictated by Wi.

Notably, at the highest Weissenberg number probed, Wi≈102, interfacial fracture occurred through a macroscopic stick–slip mechanism (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), consistent with peeling near the upper limit of the rubbery regime (50). However, this stick–slip behavior lies outside the scope of this investigation.

Mechanochemistry Unravels a Complex Interplay Between Viscoelasticity and Damage Ahead of the Peel Front.

Under confocal microscopy (λex=405 nm), the peeled specimens exhibited fluorescence with intensity peaking at the peeled surface and extending approximately 20 Âµm into the network (SI Appendix, Figs. S12 and S13). This observation indicates mechanophore activation ahead of the peel front via force-induced retro-Diels–Alder reaction, in line with the cohesive fracture study of Slootman et al. (23). Control experiments further supported this result. First, as-synthesized (i.e., unpeeled) MA networks showed no fluorescence, consistent with the absence of damage prior to peeling. Second, unlabeled MA networks remained nonfluorescent after T-peel tests, confirming that the observed fluorescence indeed stems from mechanophore activation alone (SI Appendix, Fig. S15).

Fluorogenic mechanochemistry thus provides an unprecedented direct measure of the damage zone, which is mesoscopic, approximately 20 Âµm, and considerably larger than the network mesh size, ξ≈(kBTμp′)1/3≈2.7±0.14 nm. In addition, the technique suggests that strain concentrations at the peel front can be sufficient to induce irreversible damage—despite the networks adhering to the PET substrate primarily via physical, noncovalent bonds—van der Waals interactions. This result is noteworthy because such bonds require significantly less energy—and force—to break than is needed to stretch a polymer chain and activate the mechanophore (i.e., ≈1 kJ/mol vs. ≈100 kJ/mol)—or, in other words, because the surface energy is considerably lower than the adherence energy, Γ (10).

The rate- and temperature-dependence of the damage further revealed that, contrary to the adherence energy, Γ, which increased monotonically with Wi, the damage, Σ exhibited two distinct regimes: first, an increasing regime where Σ∼Wiα, where α=0.47±0.14, and then, a decreasing regime where Σ∼Wiβ, where β=−0.61±0.23 (Fig. 5). These two regimes crossed at the same Wi observed for the peel front strain, Wi,c≈0.3, which also corresponded to a maximum in damage of Σmax≈5·1017—or Σ¯≈Σ/Σ0≈3 in terms of Lake and Thomas monolayers. Last, in the limits of Wi→10−3 and Wi→10, the damage was negligible, Σ≈1.5·1016 m−2 or Σ¯≈0.1. Note, however, that normalizing by the Lake and Thomas limit serves only as a reference for comparison with the cohesive fracture measurements of Slootman et al. (23); in adhesive cracks, covalent bonds do not need to break for the peel front to fracture.

Overall, these observations mark a sharp departure from linear viscoelastic fracture theories (18, 42), highlighting a coupling between viscoelasticity and damage ahead of interfacial cracks. Specifically, they reveal that the contribution of damage to the adherence energy depends on the crack propagation velocity, Γ0(vcrack), as Γ0∼Σ within the Lake and Thomas model (48).

In addition, these results suggest that the molecular mechanisms governing the interplay between viscoelasticity and damage—namely, polymer chain elongation and breakage along with chain friction—influence the observed strain at the peel front, ε (see further discussion below).

Finally, these mechanochemistry experiments unveil a more complex relationship between viscoelasticity and damage than that reported by Slootman et al. for cohesive fracture (23). In interfacial fracture, damage does not simply increase with Wi but, instead, peaks at Wi,c≈0.3; likely due to the weaker intermolecular interactions between the adhesive network and the PET substrate compared to the covalent bonds ahead of a cohesive crack (SI Appendix, Fig. S16). After all, in cohesive fracture, covalent bonds must break for the crack to propagate, whereas, in interfacial fracture, only scission of the noncovalent PET-network interactions is needed.

Molecular Interpretation of Interfacial Fracture.

The multiscale picture that emerges from these observations is that of an interfacial crack propagating through the scission of both physical bonds at the interface and chemical bonds in the adjacent polymer network, as dictated by the ability of the polymer chains to rearrange under a given peeling rate and temperature (see schematic in Fig. 6). In the limit of Wi→10−3, the networks are fully rubbery, the chains experience negligible intermolecular interactions, and the crosslinks bear most of the load. The damage zone is molecular in size—as the load is too low to activate the mechanophore and the strain at the peel front is insufficient to dissipate significant energy—fracture occurs primarily via scission of weak interfacial bonds, and the adherence energy is very low, Γ≈42 J.m2.

Molecular picture of interfacial fracture. For Wi<0.3, the networks become increasingly viscoelastic with Wi, leading to larger strains in both the bulk and the strain-concentrated region, while also dissipating more energy and inducing greater damage. For Wi>0.3, the networks become progressively stiffer with Wi, dissipating more energy in the bulk, localizing stresses at the peel front, and reducing both strain concentration and damage. Finally, at Wi≈0.3, the effects of enhanced dissipation from increased strains and stress localization balance out, resulting in maximum damage. The blue region depicts the dissipation zone, evaluated from the adherence energy, Γ, and the work-to-fracture under uniaxial tension, Wf, following Chen et al. and Long et al. (11, 51). The intensity of the orange and blue colors illustrates the extent of damage or dissipation. The mechanophore-labeled polymer networks show coexisting chemical crosslinks and entanglements, in line with the stress-softening observed in the stress-stretch curves at low-rates and high-temperatures (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Dashed lines indicate weak interfacial bonds—van der Waals interactions—between the PET substrate and the adhesive network.

In the regime where 10−3<Wi<0.3, strain and damage scale as ε∼Wi0.39, and Σ∼Wi0.47, respectively. The networks remain rubbery—with a constant storage modulus, μ′—but the peel front starts to develop a strain concentration, which gives rise to highly localized strain rates, ε˙local, and local Weissenberg numbers, Wi,local that exceed the nominal Wi. The networks become progressively more dissipative with increasing Wi—with an increase in loss modulus, μ″—storing more recoverable elastic energy and dissipating more energy as they sustain larger strains in the bulk and at the peel front. In addition, the strain concentrations at the peel front become sufficiently high to locally stretch and break network chains, thereby activating the mechanophore. In this regime, the damage zone is mesoscopic in size, ≈20 μm, and both viscoelastic chain friction and molecular-level damage work in concert to dissipate energy and increase Γ—much like in cohesive fracture (23).

Instead, in the regime where 0.3<Wi<10, the strain plateaus at ε≈1.3 while the damage scales as Σ∼Wi−0.61. The networks begin to transition away from the rubbery state—with an increase in both the storage modulus, μ′, and loss modulus, μ″—and the chains start to experience significant molecular friction. The networks become stiffer and more dissipative as Wi increases, storing more recoverable elastic energy and dissipating more energy as they sustain higher stresses both in the bulk and at the peel front.

In this regime, two contrasting effects emerge. First, a notable increase in both bulk and local stress, which enhances elastic energy storage and viscous energy dissipation, and raises the driving force for overloading and breaking the weak interfacial bonds. Second, a decrease in strain concentration at the peel front, which reduces the probability of locally stretching and breaking network chains, thereby limiting mechanophore activation. The damage zone remains mesoscopic, ≈20 μm, but viscoelastic chain friction and molecular-level damage no longer act synergistically to delay fracture—in stark contrast to cohesive fracture (23).

These opposing trends help explain the observed increase in adherence energy, Γ∼Wi0.47, and the concurrent decrease in damage, Σ∼Wi−0.61. The adherence energy, Γ, can increase with Wi due to network stiffening—increase in μ′ and μ″ above aTω≈0.3aTωc—higher stresses in the bulk, and enhanced elastic energy storage and viscous energy dissipation. In contrast, damage, Σ, can decrease with Wi because of more significant network stiffening within the strain-concentrated region ahead of the peel front, and the emergence of a local stress that presumably exceeds the interfacial strength before the network chains can elongate, break, and activate the mechanophore.

These competing effects can also explain the observed plateau in peeling strain, ε: The networks cannot deform further without the local stress exceeding the interfacial strength and causing fracture—a consequence of the coexistence of weak physical bonds at the network–substrate interface and strong covalent bonds at the crosslinks.

Conclusions

This work combined polymer mechanochemistry and T-peel tests to investigate the relationship between viscoelasticity and damage in a series of soft polymer networks, which were interfacially fractured from a stiff substrate via scission of both physical and chemical bonds. Quantitative mapping of molecular damage using confocal microscopy revealed a mesoscopic damage zone—approximately 20 μm in size—and its nonmonotonic evolution with the debonding rate and viscoelastic character of the networks, as measured by the Weissenberg number, Wi. The results challenge longstanding assumptions underlying linear viscoelastic fracture theories on adhesive fracture, highlighting both the presence of molecular damage at the peel front and its rate- and temperature-dependence.

At low Weissenberg number, Wi<0.3, damage increases with the viscoelasticity of the network—mirroring the behavior observed in cohesive fracture (23). The peel front progressively sustains larger strains, increasing the probability for polymer chains to elongate and break, thereby activating the mechanophore. In contrast, at high Weissenberg number, Wi>0.3, damage decreases with the viscoelastic character of the network—a stark departure from cohesive fracture (23). In this regime, the molecular friction between segments bears most of the load, and the enhancement in adherence energy, Γ, results from increased stiffness and viscoelastic dissipation rather than from larger strains and molecular damage. This high-Wi regime is unique to interfacial fracture and emerges from i) load localization at the weak interfacial bonds before the chains can rearrange, elongate, and break, and ii) increased stiffness and dissipative losses ahead of the peel front.

The results have several implications for both polymer physics and fracture mechanics. First, they highlight the importance of molecular friction and polymer chain dynamics for interfacial fracture, even when the bulk of the networks appears rubbery based on rheology. In this regime, the large strains at the peel front can induce sufficient strain concentrations and localized strain rates, allowing polymer chains to fully elongate, bear high loads, and ultimately undergo irreversible breakage. Second, these findings provide direct experimental evidence of the impact of viscoelasticity on damage ahead of the peel front, thereby underscoring the limitations of linear viscoelastic fracture theories in describing the peeling behavior of soft polymer networks and unveiling an alternative paradigm. These theories typically consider viscoelasticity and damage as independent processes and neglect mesoscopic-scale network damage to the adherence energy. Finally, this work demonstrates the power of mechanochemistry for examining interfacial fracture, leveraging the transition at Wi,c, to uncover how polymer chain elongation and molecular friction influence the strain, stress, and energy dissipation fields ahead of an interfacial crack.
