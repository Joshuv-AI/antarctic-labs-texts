Factors influencing the health behaviors of patients with ischemic stroke from a social-ecological perspective: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Min-Xiu Wang, Gui-Zhen Weng, Jin-Hua Liao, Xin-Yan Chen, Yan-Juan Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough numerous studies have investigated factors associated with health behaviors in patients with first-onset ischemic stroke, the variables examined have varied substantially. Moreover, most analyses have employed ordinary linear regression models that treat variables across different levels as equivalent, thereby overlooking the nested structure of influencing factors. To address this methodological limitation, the present study adopts the Social-Ecological Model as a theoretical framework and employs hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) as a statistical approach. This framework enables systematic stratification of factors across individual, interpersonal, community, and societal levels, and facilitates the identification of key level-specific factors associated with health behaviors in this patient population.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 350 patients with first-onset ischemic stroke who were assessed via structured telephone interviews three months after hospital discharge. Univariate analysis, correlation analysis, stratified multivariate analysis, and hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) were conducted to identify key factors associated with patients’ health behaviors.ResultsStratified multivariate analysis identified educational level, physical functioning, stroke-related knowledge, and health beliefs as individual-level factors; per capita monthly household income and perceived social support as interpersonal-level factors; and community health environment, distance to the hospital, and place of residence as community- and societal-level factors associated with patients’ health behaviors. Further analysis using hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) revealed stroke-related knowledge, health beliefs, and perceived social support as the most salient associated factors across levels.ConclusionsHealth behaviors among patients with ischemic stroke were shaped by a hierarchically structured set of factors spanning the individual, interpersonal, community, and societal levels. The present study offers a theoretical foundation for informing the development of targeted, multilevel strategies aimed at promoting health behavior improvement in this population.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Socio-ecological model, Hierarchical linear model, Secondary prevention, Health behavior, Influencing factor

Introduction

Ischemic stroke (IS) is the most prevalent type of stroke among the Chinese population, accounting for 80% of all cases [1]. It is characterized by high incidence, disability, and recurrence rates [2], with recurrent stroke increasing mortality by 2.67 times and disability by 9.4 times [3]. Health behaviors, such as adherence to secondary prevention medication, maintaining a balanced diet, smoking cessation and alcohol limitation, engaging in regular physical activity, and controlling blood pressure, lipids, and glucose levels, as well as sustaining good psychological well-being, are critical in reducing recurrence risk [4]. However, studies indicate that IS patients generally exhibit poor health behaviors[5–9], which are influenced by multiple factors5. Although previous studies have examined the determinants of health behaviors in IS patients, they have primarily focused on individual-level factors, such as baseline characteristics and disease status, while systematically identifying multiple variables. However, community- and societal-level influences have received comparatively less attention, limiting a comprehensive understanding of broader contextual determinants. Moreover, some studies have produced conflicting conclusions, further complicating the interpretation of influencing factors [10, 11]. This inconsistency may stem from the lack of explicit differentiation between individual factors and environmental influences, as some studies have treated them as a single level of analysis, potentially leading to statistical misinterpretations.

The Social-Ecological Model (SEM) is a theoretical framework that emphasizes the dynamic interactions between individuals and environmental factors [12]. According to ecological theory, determinants of health behaviors exhibit a hierarchical nested structure and interact across multiple levels. SEM categorizes influencing factors into five levels: individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and societa, [13]providing a systematic approach to analyzing the complexity and multilevel interactions of health behavior determinants. However, research applying SEM to the health behaviors of stroke patients remains limited, with a predominance of qualitative studies. [14]Existing quantitative studies primarily [15] employ single-level regression models (e.g., multiple linear regression, logistic regression, structural equation modeling). By treating variables from different levels as equivalent, these models fail o capture the nested relationships inherent in SEM. This limitation restricts a deeper understanding of cross-level interactions and has led to contradictory conclusions in some [10, 11]cases. Hierarchical Linear Modeling (HLM) provides an appropriate statistical framework for analyzing nested data structures [16] (multilevel data). It accounts for hierarchical dependencies, estimates the effects of variables across different levels, and mitigates statistical misinterpretations arising from treating multilevel variables as belonging to a single analytical layer.

This study integrates the Social-Ecological Model (SEM) [17]as the theoretical framework with Hierarchical Linear Modeling (HLM) as the statistical approach to systematically examine multilevel factors associated with health behaviors among patients with ischemic stroke (IS). Drawing on the SEM framework, relevant variables are stratified across ecological levels, and HLM is subsequently employed to analyze their multilevel associations with health behaviors. This analytic strategy is designed to ensure methodological rigor and yield/generate robust and reliable quantitative insights. The identified factors provide a theoretical basis for secondary prevention, health management, targeted interventions, health education, and early rehabilitation among patients with ischemic stroke. This analytic framework may provide a transferable approach for integrating theory and statistical modeling in the analysis of other contextualized variables.

Methods

Participants

Patients with first-onset IS who visited the Department of Neurology at a tertiary general hospital in Fujian Province between May 2020 and March 2023 were selected. The inclusion criteria were (1) having IS and meeting the diagnostic criteria of the Chinese Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute Ischemic Stroke 2018 [18]; (2) being at least 18 years old; (3) being conscious at discharge with stable vital signs and capable of independent medication management; and (4) facing no obstacle in communicating with others. The exclusion criteria were: (1) comorbidity with severe heart, liver, kidney insufficiency or other diseases; (2) experiencing transient ischemic attacks; and (3) comorbidity with malignant tumors.

Multilevel model analysis was used to estimate sample size. We followed Kline[19], who suggested that the sample size should be 10–20 times the number of parameters to be estimated. As the number of independent variables explored in this study was 20, the minimum sample size was 200 cases. Accounting for an estimated 20% dropout rate, 250 participants were required. Consequently, the final sample size of 350 ensured adequacy.

Data collection and analysis

Measures

A structured questionnaire was developed based on previously identified factors significantly associated with health behaviors in patients with ischemic stroke. It included sociodemographic characteristics and multiple validated measurement scales. A total of 21 research variables, including the dependent variable of health behavior and 20 associated factors, were selected for analysis.

Health behavior, identified as the dependent variable in this study, was assessed using the Health Behavior Scale for Stroke Patients (HBS-SP)[20], which served as the primary outcome measure. The HBS-SP comprises 25 items across six dimensions: physical activity, medication adherence, adherence to medical instructions, nutrition, health responsibility, and smoking and alcohol avoidance. Each item is rated on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 4 (regularly), with reverse scoring applied to the smoking, alcohol, and medication dimensions. Higher total scores indicate healthier behaviors. The scale has demonstrated good psychometric properties, with content validity, internal consistency, split-half reliability, and test–retest reliability values of 0.850, 0.878, 0.801, and 0.845, respectively, supporting its suitability for assessing health behaviors among stroke patients. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the six subscales in this study were 0.935, 0.899, 0.919, 0.894, 0.828, and 0.813, indicating high internal consistency.

The sociodemographic characteristics assessed in this study included 14 variables: gender, age, occupational status, marital status, educational level, number of chronic comorbidities, per capita monthly household income, household composition, rehabilitation equipment, place of residence, medical expense payment method, distance to the nearest clinic, distance to the nearest hospital, and medical resources to meet the demand for consultation.

Additionally, the questionnaire incorporates six validated and widely recognized measurement scales: the Modified Rankin Scale (mRS), the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), the Stroke-Related Knowledge Questionnaire, the Champion’s Health Belief Model Scale (CHBMS), the Chinese version of the Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS), the Chinese version of the Community Health Environment Scan Survey (CHESS).

The Modified Rankin Scale (mRS)[21], used in this study to represent the variable of physical function, assesses post-stroke disability on a scale from 0 (no symptoms) to 6 (death). As deceased patients were excluded, only scores of 0–5 were used. Higher scores indicate greater disability, with scores of 3–5 reflecting moderate to severe post-stroke disability.

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)[22] includes two subscales: anxiety (HADS-A) and depression (HADS-D), each comprising seven items, for a total of 14 items. Responses were rated on a four-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms (0–7 = asymptomatic; 8–10 = borderline abnormal; 11–21 = abnormal). The overall Cronbach’s alpha for the HADS was 0.879[23], demonstrating good internal consistency. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha values for the HADS-A and HADS-D subscales were 0.905 and 0.903, respectively.

The Stroke-Related Knowledge Questionnaire[24], which is based on the secondary prevention guidelines for stroke, was used to determine patients’ knowledge level. It contains 31 questions, including risk factors related to stroke, taking medication, desired lifestyles, and early warning signals of stroke. Responses were rated on a three-point Likert scale (0 = do not know; 1 = not sure; and 2 = know), with higher scores indicating better knowledge. Cronbach’s alpha of the questionnaire was 0.791, and the scale content validity index (S-CVI) was 0.98, indicating good reliability and validity. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for risk factors, taking medication, desired lifestyles, and warning signals were 0.953, 0.848, 0.936, and 0.919, respectively.

The Champion’s Health Belief Model Scale (CHBMS)[25] was used to measure patients’ health beliefs. The CHBMS comprises 34 items in six dimensions: perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, health motivation, self-efficacy, perceived benefits, and perceived barriers. Responses were rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating better health beliefs about stroke prevention among patients. Cronbach’s alpha for measuring health beliefs among patients who suffered a stroke to prevent stroke recurrence was 0.89, and the content validity was 0.80. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the six dimensions of perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, health motivation, and self-efficacy were 0.889, 0.907, 0.94, 0.943, 0.922, and 0.905, respectively.

The Chinese version of the Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS)[26, 27] was used to measures the degree to which an individual perceives support from various sources of social support such as family, friends and others. The scale includes 12 items in three dimensions: family, friends, and other support. Responses were rated on a seven-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 7 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater social support. The Chinese version of the PSSS had a retest reliability of 0.81 and an internal consistency reliability of 0.85. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for family, friends, and other support were 0.903, 0.94, and 0.923, respectively.

The Chinese version of the Community Health Environment Scan Survey (CHESS)[28]assessed the health environment related to the three major risk factors of chronic diseases: unhealthy diet, physical inactivity, and smoking. Consistency between the early-stage data collected by the research group using Baidu maps, online shopping applications, and interviews and those collected during the field survey was 88.5%. This reduced the investigators’ workload and accelerated data collection, indicating that CHESS data could be used to replace field survey data collection with a good consistency of assessment methods. The item-level content validity index in this study was 0.82-1.00, the S-CVI was 0.96, and the correlation coefficients of the retest reliability of the nine dimensions were all greater than 0.85, indicating that the questionnaire had good reliability and validity.

Variable taxonomy in SEM

Originally, according to the Social-Ecological Model[17], variables were classified into five levels: Individual, Interpersonal, Organizational, Community, and Policy. However, in this study, the organizational level was integrated into the interpersonal and community levels, as organizational structures are inherently shaped by the cultural norms and regulations of interpersonal and community networks. Additionally, patients’ living environments and their interpersonal and community interactions exhibit significant overlap, making a clear distinction between these domains challenging. Therefore, organizational factors were subsumed under the interpersonal and community levels, resulting in a four-level classification: individual, interpersonal, community, and social. Based on the existing literature and practical work experience, this study preliminarily identified 20 factors influencing health behaviors. These factors were categorized according to the social-ecological model, and their impact on health behaviors was analyzed at different levels (as visually summarized Fig. 1).

Data collection methods

General and clinical data were collected before the patients were discharged. CHESS was collected and assessed using Baidu maps (Fig. 2), online shopping applications, and interviews. Moreover, the above scales were used to collect data by telephone follow-up three months after discharge.

CHESS data was collected and assessed using Baidu maps

Ethical considerations

The hospital ethics committee (approval number 2020KY038) approved this study. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Statistical analysis methods

Descriptive statistics, univariate analyses, correlation analyses, and stratified multivariate analyses were performed using SPSS 29.0. Hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) was conducted using Mplus 8.4. Descriptive statistics (means ± standard deviations for continuous variables; frequencies and percentages for categorical variables) were calculated for demographic characteristics. Univariate analyses were performed using independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA (F-tests). Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated to assess bivariate associations between continuous variables. Separate multiple linear regression models were fitted for each level of the Social-Ecological Model (individual, interpersonal, community, societal) to identify level-specific factors associated with health behaviors. Finally, hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) was employed to integrate significant level-specific factors identified in the previous step and estimate their unique, simultaneous contributions to health behaviors within the multilevel framework. A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was applied for all non-special labels.

Results

Data collection was conducted among patients with first-onset ischemic stroke (IS) who met the inclusion criteria. A total of 380 patients were initially contacted; however, 5 could not be reached due to incorrect phone numbers, 17 did not respond to the calls, and 8 were unable to complete the questionnaire due to physical fatigue. Consequently, 350 valid questionnaires were obtained, yielding a response rate of 92.11%.

Sample characteristics

A total of 350 patients were interviewed, of whom 234 (66.9%) were male and 116 (33.1%) were female. Participants’ ages ranged from 36 to 90 years, with a mean age of 66.75 ± 11.28 years. At three months post-stroke, the mean HBS-SP score among patients with ischemic stroke was 2.911 ± 0.530, with subscale scores as follows: health responsibility (1.830 ± 0.821), physical activity (2.450 ± 0.853), adherence to medical instructions (2.841 ± 0.728), nutrition (3.168 ± 0.618), smoking and alcohol avoidance (3.686 ± 0.564), and medication adherence (3.710 ± 0.446).

Based on the results of univariate and correlation analyses, factors significantly associated with HBS-SP were included in multivariate analysis. Variables were stratified according to the Social-Ecological Model, and multivariate analyses were conducted separately at different levels to mitigate statistical bias arising from the inclusion of variables from multiple levels within a single analysis.

Individual level

HBS-SP was higher among participants with high school or college and above education than those with primary school and below (P < 0.05). Physical function had a negative effect on HBS-SP (β=−0.109, P < 0.05). Stroke-related knowledge and health beliefs positively affected HBS-SP (P < 0.01; Table 3).

Socio-community level

Since the community health environment was the sole factor at the community level, and due to the conceptual overlap between the community and societal levels, the societal-level variables—place of residence and distance to the nearest hospital—were integrated with the community-level variable into a unified construct, termed ‘socio-community level factors’, to enhance model parsimony and provide a more comprehensive assessment of their collective impact.

The results showed that the participants with a distance to the nearest hospital of 0.5–1 km, 1–3 km, and more than 3 km had lower HBS-SP scores than those with a distance to the hospital of < 0.5 km (P < 0.05). Furthermore, patients in urban areas had higher HBS-SP scores than those in rural areas (P < 0.05). The community health environment variable positively affected the HBS-SP (P < 0.001; Table 5).

Hierarchical linear modeling

Level-specific multivariate analyses were conducted to identify variables associated with health behaviors among patients with ischemic stroke. The variables found to be significant at each level were subsequently incorporated into a multilevel linear model for further examination. This approach is grounded in the core premise of the Social-Ecological Model, which posits that factors operating at distinct levels exert influence primarily within their respective domains. This conceptual alignment supports the application of Hierarchical Linear Modeling (HLM) as a theoretically coherent and methodologically appropriate framework for analyzing nested data structures[16].

Based on the results of multivariate analysis at the community and social levels, both distance to the nearest hospital and place of residence exhibited significant effects on HBS-SP, indicating that the health behaviors of ischemic stroke patients are hierarchically structured within these two categorical variables. Consequently, participants were classified into 3 × 4 = 12 distinct groups (Table 6), stratified by distance to the nearest hospital (four categories) and place of residence (three categories). These classifications collectively define a newly constructed variable, environmental aspects, which encapsulates the nested relationships among participants within the analytical framework.

Discussion

Ischemic stroke is the most significant and severe type of stroke. Numerous studies have examined the factors influencing the health behaviors of ischemic stroke patients, yet the variables included in analyses vary, sometimes yielding contradictory conclusions. One potential reason for this discrepancy is the misclassification of variables from different levels as belonging to the same category. This study first conducted a literature review to identify factors with a significant impact on health behaviors from previous research. Using the social-ecological model, these variables were systematically categorized. A progressive analytical approach was employed, integrating univariate analysis, correlation analysis, stratified multiple linear regression, and multilevel linear modeling, ultimately identifying the key determinants of health behaviors in ischemic stroke patients.

Descriptive results showed that patients’ health behaviors with IS three months after discharge were intermediate, consistent with previous findings[29]. After conducting univariate analysis and correlation analysis, multiple linear regression analyses were respectively performed at the individual, interpersonal, community and societal levels within the social-ecological framework, The results suggest that the health behaviors of patients with ischemic stroke (IS) are shaped by factors at the individual, interpersonal, community, and societal levels. At the personal level, a higher educational level was associated with better health behaviors; weaker physical function was associated with worse health behaviors; greater knowledge of the disease was associated with better health behaviors, and stronger beliefs in health were associated with better health behaviors. At the interpersonal level, greater support from family, friends, and society was associated with better health behavior. At the Socio-Community level, a better community health environment was associated with better health behavior, living closer to the hospital was associated with better health behavior, possibly due to facilitated follow-up care and rehabilitation access, and urban patients had better health behavior than rural ones. Significant variables identified in multiple linear regression analysis for health behaviors in ischemic stroke (IS) patients were incorporated into the multilevel linear model[16], the final results suggest that disease knowledge and health beliefs at the individual level and perceived social support at the environmental level promoted health behaviors.

Greater disease knowledge was positively associated with improved health behaviors, aligning with previous studies[30, 31]. Poor medication adherence in IS patients often stems from insufficient understanding of dosage, action, duration, and discontinuation guidelines. Oshio and Kan (2019)[32] identified fear of stroke recurrence and trust in physicians as primary facilitators of adherence to secondary prevention, while lack of medication information was a key barrier. Providing tailored information enhances adherence. Brouwer-Goossensen et al. (2021)[33] emphasized that the optimal window for health behavior change is within three months post-stroke, making this period critical for health education. Healthcare professionals should capitalize on this phase, tailoring interventions to patients’ knowledge, understanding, and motivation[30]. Diversifying education methods pamphlets, videos, demonstrations, and patient testimonies enhances comprehension, ensuring effective communication of stroke-related knowledge to emphasize secondary prevention. Stronger health beliefs correlate with better health behaviors, as knowledge, beliefs, and behaviors are interconnected, serving as the foundation, driving force, and reinforcement of behavioral change[34]. Patients who internalize disease-related knowledge through positive thinking develop stronger health beliefs, leading to sustained behavioral improvements[15]. Healthcare professionals should clearly explain stroke pathogenesis, highlight high recurrence, disability, and mortality risks, and emphasize secondary prevention’s importance. By demonstrating disease risks and benefits of preventive measures, they can enhance patient acceptance of interventions[35], ultimately promoting health behaviors. Higher comprehension support correlated with improved health behaviors, consistent with previous findings[36]. IS patients often experience sequelae such as aphasia, hemiplegia, and movement disorders, which limit social participation and contribute to adverse emotions like anxiety and depression[37, 38]. Support from family, friends, and the community fosters recovery confidence, encouraging patients to engage in rehabilitation and adopt healthier behaviors. Della Vecchia et al.[39] found that stroke patients shape their disease identity throughout acute and chronic phases. Limited medication knowledge and low perceived control over recurrence, due to inadequate or poorly timed information from healthcare providers, contribute to anxiety and disengagement. Support from relatives and medical professionals enhances rehabilitation and behavior change. Healthcare providers and families should offer early care, introduce successful rehabilitation cases, and provide emotional encouragement to reinforce patients’ confidence and engagement in recovery. Expanding social support networks, improving external environments, and maintaining close communication can help patients manage their condition, actively participate in rehabilitation, and sustain healthy behaviors. Enhancing the health behaviors of IS patients requires coordinated efforts across multiple sectors and disciplines. Patients should proactively manage their condition, regulate emotions, strengthen health beliefs, and engage in rehabilitation. Caregivers must provide personalized care, tailored education, and structured follow-up, particularly within the critical three-month period post-stroke, while family members offer continuous support and encouragement. Governments should invest in health-promoting infrastructure, including exercise facilities, nutrition programs, and smoking cessation initiatives, and improve healthcare accessibility, especially in rural areas. Hospitals, families, and communities should collaborate to reinforce health behaviors through education and social support, fostering confidence, adherence, and recurrence prevention.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, all participants were selected from a single source. Multicenter surveys that expand the width and breadth of the samples should be conducted to reduce geographical limitations. Second, the multilevel model was constructed based on the patients’ residence and distance from the hospital but not strictly in accordance with the four levels of the social-ecological model: personal, interpersonal, community, and social. Subsequent studies should collect information on patient locations to categorize patients’ living environments more accurately and construct a precise multilevel model.Third, while we employed the Modified Rankin Scale for functional assessment, more comprehensive evaluation incorporating cognitive measures like MoCA or MMSE might provide additional insights.

Despite these limitations, our findings have important clinical and public health implications. They suggest that effective interventions for improving health behaviors in stroke patients should adopt a multilevel approach, simultaneously addressing individual knowledge and beliefs, strengthening social support networks, enhancing community health resources, and optimizing healthcare accessibility. Such comprehensive strategies may prove more effective than single-level interventions in promoting sustainable behavior change and reducing stroke recurrence.

Conclusions

The overall health behavior scores of patients with IS three months after discharge were moderate, with low scores on the responsibility and exercise dimensions, indicating the need for attention. Health behaviors were influenced by individual, interpersonal, community, and societal factors, with the most significant and positive influences from disease-related knowledge, health beliefs, and perceived social support.

Relevance to clinical practice

Our findings indicated that healthcare professionals should focus on influencing factors and provide targeted interventions to improve patients’ disease-related knowledge, help them establish correct health beliefs, and mobilize the support of family, friends, and society to improve patients’ health behaviors and prevent disease recurrence.
