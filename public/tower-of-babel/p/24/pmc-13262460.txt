Psychological difficulties in refugee adolescents with war-related experiences and forced displacement: a community-based comparison with non-refugee peers
Authors: Ilknur Ucuz, Ayla Uzun Cicek, Onur Burak Dursun, Rabia Olucak, Mekiye Demir, Esma Kesriklioglu, Elif Abanoz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe number of refugee and immigrant children and adolescents is rapidly increasing worldwide. Early identification of psychological risk factors is essential, as childhood mental health significantly influences outcomes in adulthood. This study examined psychological difficulties associated with refugee status, war-related experiences, and forced displacement in refugee adolescents, with a particular focus on emotion regulation, dissociation, somatization, and self-esteem.MethodsNinety-two adolescents (12–18 years) who migrated to Turkey due to the Syrian war were included in the study. A control group of 120 age- and gender-matched adolescents without refugee experience was also recruited. Sociodemographic information was collected, and participants completed the Children’s Somatization Inventory (CSI-24), Adolescent Dissociative Experiences Scale (A-DES), Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES), and Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS).ResultsRefugee adolescents had significantly higher total DERS scores than controls (103.98 ± 15.47 vs. 91.09 ± 23.74, p < 0.001), as well as higher dissociation (A-DES: 4.27 ± 1.62 vs. 2.93 ± 1.98, p < 0.001), somatization (CSI-24: 21.20 ± 11.86 vs. 14.95 ± 9.14, p < 0.001), and higher RSES scores indicating lower self-esteem (3.93 ± 1.25 vs. 1.93 ± 1.60, p < 0.001). All DERS subscales were significantly higher in refugees except the goals subscale, which was higher in controls. In adjusted linear regression analyses controlling for age, gender, parental separation, parental loss, and school non-attendance, refugee status remained independently associated with higher DERS, A-DES, CSI-24, and RSES scores (all p < 0.001). Significant positive correlations were observed among emotion dysregulation, dissociation, somatization, and lower self-esteem.ConclusionsThese findings highlight a broad pattern of psychological difficulties in refugee adolescents and support the importance of trauma-informed assessment and mental health support in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04845-3.Keywords: Refugee adolescents, Mental health, Emotion regulation, Dissociation, Somatization, Self-esteem

Introduction

The International Organization for Migration (IOM) reported there are approximately 281 million international migrants worldwide as of 2024, which is three times higher than 50 years ago [1]. Syria and Afghanistan are the top countries of origin [2, 3]. Since 2014, Turkey has hosted the world’s largest population of people under temporary and international protection. By 2024, approximately 3.1 million Syrians were registered under temporary protection, with children accounting for nearly half of this population and women and girls making up about 48% [4]. Migration is driven by various factors, including education, employment, natural disasters, and wars. This situation confronts migrant and refugee children with many biopsychosocial consequences, including physical, developmental, behavioral, and mental problems [5, 6]. Living in a war zone can lead to various problems, including violence, food shortage, epidemics, increased exposure to traumatic experiences, and inadequate health and education services [7]. War and migration are significant risk factors for the development of many mental disorders [8–10]. The prevalence of depression and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) has increased significantly in war-affected regions [11, 12]. Studies that have clinically assessed the prevalence of mental disorders in refugee children and adolescents have reported rates of 23% for PTSD, 14% for depression, and 16% for anxiety disorders. These rates are higher than the general prevalence of PTSD, depression, and anxiety in children and adolescents [8]. It is crucial to assess the level, duration, and areas of post-war psychological effects in order to determine appropriate intervention programs.

Emotion regulation is the process of managing emotions, including the ability to recognize, experience, and respond appropriately to them. It has been suggested that challenges in emotion regulation, known as emotion dysregulation, could predict several psychiatric disorders, including PTSD, depressive disorders, personality disorders, and anxiety disorders [13–16]. In a study investigating the frequency of PTSD and emotional dysregulation among Syrian refugee children and adolescents who settled in Lebanon and Jordan, the frequency of PTSD and emotional dysregulation was found to be highest in cases of greater exposure to war atrocities [17].

Self-esteem is an important factor in protecting mental health and mental resilience in individuals [18, 19]. It reduces perceived threat through the selection and use of appropriate and effective coping strategies [18, 19]. In a study evaluating predictors of psychological risk and resilience among Syrian refugee children, self-esteem was found to be a protective feature of mental health [20].

Dissociation, defined as disruptions in the integration of consciousness, memory, and identity, is frequently observed in individuals exposed to chronic stress and trauma, including refugee populations. Such experiences may present as depersonalization, derealization, or memory gaps and are often interpreted as coping mechanisms developed in response to overwhelming stressors [21, 22]. Similarly, somatization reflects the tendency to communicate psychological distress through physical symptoms rather than verbal expression. This phenomenon is particularly pronounced among children with limited emotional coping resources and in sociocultural contexts where psychological suffering is stigmatized, making the open expression of emotions difficult. Empirical research indicates that somatization occurs at elevated rates among populations exposed to traumatic experiences, notably refugee and immigrant children [8, 9, 23].

Refugee children and adolescents exposed to war and forced displacement frequently experience multiple and co-occurring psychological difficulties, including emotion dysregulation, somatization, dissociation, and low self-esteem [3, 7–9]. These domains are not independent and may be linked through shared psychological mechanisms related to chronic stress and trauma. Emotion dysregulation may represent a transdiagnostic vulnerability that contributes to maladaptive responses to trauma and may increase susceptibility to dissociative experiences and somatic expressions of distress [13, 24]. Dissociation may emerge as a response to overwhelming or insufficiently integrated emotional experiences, whereas somatization may reflect the expression of psychological distress through bodily symptoms, particularly in youth with limited emotional coping resources or in contexts where emotional suffering is difficult to verbalize [8, 9, 21, 23]. In parallel, self-esteem may function as an important resilience-related construct, as chronic adversity and trauma may undermine self-worth while also interacting with emotional regulation capacities and broader trauma-related psychopathology [18, 20, 25]. Examining these domains together may therefore provide a more integrated understanding of psychological functioning in refugee adolescents than considering each construct in isolation. The purpose of this study was to examine emotion dysregulation, dissociation, somatization, and self-esteem in refugee adolescents in relation to refugee status, war-related experiences, and forced displacement, and to compare these psychological outcomes with those observed in non-refugee peers. We hypothesized that refugee adolescents would show greater difficulties in emotion regulation, higher levels of dissociative experiences and somatic symptoms, and lower self-esteem than non-refugee peers. We also hypothesized that emotion dysregulation would be significantly associated with dissociation, somatization, and lower self-esteem within the refugee group.

Method

Study design and sample selection

This was a cross-sectional study. The study was approved by the local ethics committee and conducted in accordance with good clinical practice and the current revision of the Declaration of Helsinki (ethics committee number: 2020 − 513). Permission was obtained from the Turkish Red Crescent and the Republic of Türkiye Ministry of Interior Presidency of Migration Management, in addition to the approval of the local ethics committee. Written parental consent and verbal assent from the adolescents were obtained for participation in both groups. The study included adolescents aged 12 to 18 years who had migrated to Turkey with their families and/or relatives due to the Syrian civil war that began in 2011 and who were being followed at Turkish Red Crescent Community Centers. Refugee adolescents were recruited from Turkish Red Crescent Community Centers. All participating families were receiving support from the Turkish Red Crescent, a major humanitarian organization. To reduce heterogeneity and limit major confounding related to country of origin, the study exclusively included adolescents from Syria, one of the most common refugee communities in the world [2, 3]. The main inclusion criterion was being born in Syria and migrating to Turkey after the civil war. Language abilities were documented using the sociodemographic data form, which included questions regarding participants’ ability to speak, read, write, and understand Turkish and Arabic. Only adolescents with sufficient Turkish language ability to complete the questionnaires reliably were included in the study. Accordingly, all participants completed the validated Turkish versions of the study instruments, and Arabic-language versions were not used. No forward-back translation procedure was performed within the study, because the questionnaires were not retranslated or administered in Arabic. The questionnaires were completed in a self-report format. Bilingual psychologists affiliated with the Turkish Red Crescent were present only when participants required clarification regarding the meaning of a specific Turkish item. Their role was limited to standardized, non-directive clarification of item content and did not involve verbatim translation of the full instruments, interpretation of responses, or assistance that could influence participants’ answers. The same administration procedure was followed across study sites to maximize consistency and standardization. Adolescents with known chronic medical conditions (such as epilepsy, diabetes, or cardiovascular diseases), medication use, or war-related organ loss (e.g., arm, leg) were excluded from the study, because these factors could independently affect psychological functioning. Adolescents receiving active psychiatric treatment or using psychotropic medication were also excluded to avoid treatment-related confounders. Individuals with war-related organ loss were excluded due to the additional psychological burden associated with physical disability, which could disproportionately influence self-esteem and somatization scores and reduce the internal consistency of the sample. The study also included a control group of adolescents with no known history of migration, war exposure, or medical illness, matched to the refugee group by age and sex. Control participants were recruited from a community-based, non-clinical sample on a voluntary basis and were selected to be broadly comparable to the refugee group in terms of socioeconomic background. A total of 117 refugee adolescents and 143 control adolescents were approached for participation. Among the refugee adolescents, 14 declined to participate, 9 were excluded because of incomplete questionnaire data, and 2 met an exclusion criterion due to war-related organ loss. Among the control adolescents, 17 declined to participate and 6 were excluded because of incomplete questionnaire data. These individuals were not included in the final study sample. Both the case and control groups completed sociodemographic data forms, the Child Somatization Inventory, the Adolescent Dissociative Experiences Scale, the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale, and the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale.

Screening tools

Sociodemographic data form

The Sociodemographic Data Form was prepared by the researchers for the study and included questions about the participants’ age, gender, place of birth, level of education, history of psychiatric diagnosis and treatment, age and employment status of parents with whom they lived at home, length of stay in Turkey, and ability to speak, read, write and understand Turkish and Arabic. In addition, the questionnaire included questions about the experience of losing family members during the war (event 1), witnessing the death of a beloved one or friend (event 2), abduction or hostage-taking of family members or friends (event 3), exposure to or witnessing violence during the war (event 4), serious personal injury during the war (event 5), and witnessing a visual or auditory explosion (event 6).

Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS)

The DERS was developed by Gratz and Roemer (2004) to assess difficulties in emotion regulation across six subcategories (nonacceptance of emotional responses, difficulty in engaging in goal-directed behavior, difficulty in controlling impulses, lack of emotional awareness, limited access to emotion regulation strategies, and lack of emotional clarity [26]. Higher scores indicate greater difficulties with emotion regulation. The scale was initially translated into Turkish by Ruganci and Gençöz and later validated by Sarıtaş et al. on a sample of adolescents, demonstrating its applicability to this age group [27, 28].

Children’s Somatization Inventory (CSI-24)

The Child Somatization Inventory (CSI) was developed by Walker et al. in 1991 to screen for somatic complaints. It was subsequently rearranged and adapted for children, as a 24-item form [23]. The Turkish validity and reliability of the Likert-type scale, which was performed by Kadıoğlu et al. in 2012, requires the participant to measure and score the distress caused by each symptom based on the last 2 weeks. The higher the score, the more intense the somatic symptoms [29].

Adolescent Dissociative Experiences Scale (A-DES)

This is a self-report scale developed by Armstrong et al. to screen for dissociative experiences in children and adolescents aged 10–21 years [30]. Zoroglu et al. conducted a validity and reliability study of the Turkish scale, which is used to screen for dissociative experiences rather than diagnose them [31]. If the mean score is 3.7 or higher, dissociative psychopathology should be assessed.

Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES)

The Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale was developed by Morris Rosenberg in 1965 as a measure of self-esteem in adolescents [32]. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Çuhadaroğlu in 1986 [33]. High self-esteem is defined as 0–1 points, medium as 2–4 points, and low as 5–6 points.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test together with distributional characteristics. Because several key continuous variables showed deviation from normality, non-parametric analyses were used for the primary bivariate comparisons and correlation analyses. Internal consistency of the psychometric instruments in the current dataset was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. Given the number of event-specific comparisons, these analyses were considered exploratory and were interpreted cautiously. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), median (min-max), or number (percentage), as appropriate. Comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-squared test for categorical variables and the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. Correlations between scale scores were assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficients. In addition, multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted for DERS total score, A-DES score, CSI-24 score, and RSES score to examine whether refugee status remained independently associated with the psychological outcomes after adjustment for potential confounders. In these models, refugee status was entered as the main independent variable, while age, gender, parental separation, parental loss, and school non-attendance were included as covariates. Unstandardized regression coefficients (B), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p values were reported. A two-tailed p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

The study analyzed 92 Syrian refugee adolescents who were born in Syria and migrated to Turkey after the civil war (case group) and 120 adolescents living in Turkey who did not witness the war (control group). The mean age of the refugee adolescents was 15.07 ± 2.23 years, while the mean age of the control group was 15.3 ± 2.07 years. Of the adolescents in the case group, 29 (31.5%) were male, while in the control group, 38 (31.7%) were male. There were no significant differences in gender and age distribution between the case and control groups (p > 0.05). The percentage of refugees who did not attend school was 5.4% (n = 5), while this rate was 0% in the control group. There was a significant difference in family structure between the two groups, with a significantly higher rate of parental loss in the refugee group (9.8% vs. 1.7%). Internal consistency coefficients were calculated for the principal instruments used in the present study. Comparison of scale scores between refugee adolescents and control group

When comparing the scale scores of the refugee adolescents with those of the control group, all subscale and total DERS scores, except for the DERS-goals scores, were significantly higher in the refugee group than in the control group (all p values < 0.05). The DERS-goals scores were significantly higher (p = 0.007) in the control group. Additionally, CSI-24 and A-DES scores were significantly higher in refugee adolescents than in control adolescents (all p values < 0.001). Adolescents in the refugee group had significantly higher RSES scores than the control group, indicating lower self-esteem (p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Multivariable analyses of psychological outcomes

To assess whether the observed associations remained significant after adjustment for selected sociodemographic confounders, multivariable linear regression analyses were performed for DERS total score, A-DES score, CSI-24 score, and RSES score. Refugee status remained an independent predictor across all models. For DERS total score, the model explained 11.6% of the variance (R² = 0.116, adjusted R² = 0.090), with refugee status significantly associated with higher scores after adjustment (B = 12.227, 95% CI 6.457–17.997, p < 0.001). For A-DES score, the model explained 12.9% of the variance (R² = 0.129, adjusted R² = 0.103), and refugee status remained significantly associated with higher dissociation scores (B = 1.287, 95% CI 0.763–1.811, p < 0.001). For CSI-24 score, the model explained 11.8% of the variance (R² = 0.118, adjusted R² = 0.092), and refugee status remained independently associated with higher somatization scores (B = 5.740, 95% CI 2.802–8.679, p < 0.001); younger age was also significantly associated with higher CSI-24 scores (B = -0.901, 95% CI -1.717 to -0.085, p = 0.031). For RSES score, the model explained 33.8% of the variance (R² = 0.338, adjusted R² = 0.319), and refugee status remained an independent predictor of higher RSES scores, indicating lower self-esteem (B = 2.037, 95% CI 1.622–2.452, p < 0.001). Female gender was also independently associated with higher RSES scores, indicating lower self-esteem (B = 0.553, 95% CI 0.114–0.991, p = 0.014).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to assess emotion dysregulation, dissociation, somatization, and self-esteem among refugee adolescents with a history of war-related experiences and forced displacement, in comparison with non-refugee peers drawn from a community sample. Identifying potential mental health difficulties in refugee children is crucial for designing effective intervention programs. The study showed that refugee adolescents had higher levels of emotion regulation difficulties, somatic and dissociative experiences, and lower self-esteem than non-refugee adolescents. Additionally, the findings suggest that refugee adolescents showed a broad pattern of psychological difficulties that was not fully explained by the specific war-related event categories examined in this study. Emotion dysregulation was also significantly associated with somatic complaints, dissociative experiences, and lower self-esteem, suggesting that these domains may reflect interconnected aspects of psychological distress rather than entirely separate symptom domains. Interventions focusing on emotion regulation, trauma processing, and adaptive coping, such as trauma-focused cognitive behavioral approaches, as well as interventions incorporating emotion regulation and mindfulness-based strategies, may be particularly relevant for refugee adolescents presenting with this pattern of psychological difficulties [34–37]. Importantly, the associations between refugee status and higher levels of emotion dysregulation, dissociation, somatization, and lower self-esteem remained significant after adjustment for selected sociodemographic confounders, supporting the robustness of the overall pattern observed in the bivariate analyses. This study may contribute to the literature by focusing on a community sample of refugee adolescents born in a war-affected setting who were later displaced, and by incorporating adolescents’ self-reported psychological experiences.

An important consideration is that the refugee and control groups differed not only in war exposure, but also in broader family-related and social characteristics. In our sample, parental loss was more common and school non-attendance was more frequent among refugee adolescents. Because these factors may independently contribute to psychological distress, they were treated as potential confounders in the adjusted analyses. Notably, these associations remained significant after adjustment. These findings suggest that the observed group differences were not explained solely by measured family disruption or school non-attendance, although residual confounding by unmeasured contextual factors cannot be excluded.

The event-specific analyses should also be interpreted cautiously. After stratification by individual traumatic events, subgroup sizes became smaller, reducing statistical power. In addition, war-related experiences were coded dichotomously and likely overlapped with one another; therefore, these variables did not capture the severity, chronicity, timing, or cumulative burden of trauma. For this reason, the largely non-significant event-specific findings should not be interpreted as evidence that psychological distress was unrelated to traumatic event characteristics. Rather, they suggest that the psychological burden in refugee adolescents may be better understood at the level of the overall trauma-displacement context than through isolated yes/no event categories.

Although a significant proportion of the world’s refugees are children and adolescents [3, 38, 39], research on refugee mental health has primarily focused on adults. However, because children are still developing, they may be particularly vulnerable to traumatic experiences, and studies in this area remain relatively limited. In addition to PTSD, mental health problems observed in refugee children and adolescents include depression, anxiety, sleep disturbances, somatic complaints such as headaches, dizziness, and nausea, as well as emotional and behavioral difficulties. These problems are also evident in the preschool years [3, 40–43]. Longitudinal studies have shown that some groups experience a gradual decrease in mental health symptoms over time, whereas others remain symptomatic and develop more chronic difficulties [44, 45]. Against this background, it is important to examine how war-related experiences, forced displacement, and related adversities are associated with emotion regulation and self-esteem. At the same time, these outcomes are unlikely to reflect pre-migration war exposure alone. In refugee adolescents who have lived in the host country for several years, psychological functioning is more likely to be shaped by the combined burden of pre-migration trauma, displacement-related adversity, and post-migration stressors such as interrupted education, family burden, bereavement, and social disadvantage.

Studies on the mental health of migrant and refugee children, whether based on self-report or clinical assessment, consistently report high levels of psychological difficulties [8, 9]. In our study, dissociation and somatization scores were significantly higher in refugee adolescents than in healthy controls, and the mean dissociation score in the refugee group exceeded the commonly suggested cut-off value of 3.7 (4.27 ± 1.62). This finding suggests that dissociative experiences may represent a clinically meaningful concern in refugee adolescents, rather than merely a statistical difference between groups, and may warrant further psychological assessment when indicated. Furthermore, no significant associations were observed between most specific war-related traumatic events and emotion dysregulation or somatization scores. Among the traumatic events examined, higher dissociation scores were observed only in adolescents who had witnessed an explosion, whereas lower self-esteem was found in those who had experienced serious injury during the war. These findings suggest that psychological difficulties in refugee adolescents may extend across multiple domains and may not be fully explained by any single type of traumatic event. One possible interpretation is that chronic trauma exposure and prolonged adversity may disrupt emotional processing and regulatory capacities, thereby increasing vulnerability to both dissociative experiences and somatic expressions of distress. In this context, emotion dysregulation may be considered one potentially relevant correlate of broader psychological distress in refugee adolescents, rather than a directly established mechanism in the present study. These event-specific findings should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. Psychological outcomes in refugee adolescents may be shaped more by cumulative trauma burden, chronic stress, and post-migration adversities than by any single traumatic event considered in isolation, particularly because different war-related experiences often co-occur and may reduce the distinct observable effect of individual event types. Within this broader context, the association between witnessing explosions and higher dissociation scores may reflect the particularly overwhelming and acute nature of this exposure, whereas the association between serious injury and lower self-esteem may indicate the enduring effects of bodily harm on self-image and psychosocial functioning. The higher somatization scores observed in refugee adolescents are also clinically important and should be interpreted within a broader cultural and trauma-related context. In refugee youth, psychological distress may be expressed not only through emotional symptoms but also through bodily complaints; thus, elevated somatization may represent a meaningful manifestation of war-related and displacement-related distress rather than merely an accompanying symptom. This interpretation is consistent with prior literature indicating that refugee populations frequently report elevated somatic symptoms and that mental health outcomes in refugee youth may include somatic symptom presentations in the context of trauma and displacement-related stress [46, 47]. Similarly, a study conducted among adolescents who migrated to Turkey after the Syrian war and were living under temporary protection in Turkey examined the relationship between mental health problems and psychological resilience. The study found that 47.2% of adolescents scored above the cut-off for dissociation and that 61.3% of adolescents with PTSD had dissociative symptoms [48]. Consistent with our findings, Uysal et al. highlighted that dissociation is a particularly salient problem among refugee adolescents and emphasized the role of resilience in mitigating the psychological burden of trauma. Taken together, these findings further support the view that refugee adolescents may be particularly vulnerable to dissociation, somatization, and emotion regulation difficulties [48].

Carter et al. suggested that emotion regulation is crucial in shaping positive and negative emotional responses and that addressing emotion regulation difficulties may represent an important intervention target in childhood trauma [49]. It has also been argued that emotion dysregulation plays an important role in psychosocial maladaptation and is associated with symptom severity in PTSD [50]. Individuals with PTSD often struggle to manage negative emotions such as fear, guilt, shame, and anger [24]. They may also rely on maladaptive emotion regulation strategies, which can contribute to aggression and dissociation [24]. Accordingly, effective treatment for PTSD should aim to improve maladaptive emotional responses while addressing emotion dysregulation, which may complicate treatment [24]. Our study showed that refugee adolescents reported greater difficulties in emotion regulation than their peers without refugee status or war-related trauma exposure. Additionally, we observed significant positive correlations between emotion dysregulation and dissociation, somatization, and lower self-esteem, with the exception of the DERS subscale assessing difficulties in engaging in goal-directed behavior. An exception to the overall pattern was observed for the DERS subscale assessing difficulties in engaging in goal-directed behavior, which was significantly higher in the control group. This was an unexpected finding and should be interpreted with caution. The present study was not designed to determine why this pattern emerged, and several untested contextual and developmental factors may have contributed to it, including differences in academic expectations, structured daily responsibilities, future-oriented demands, disrupted educational trajectories, sociocultural response tendencies, or possible item-level comprehension and psychometric issues. Accordingly, this isolated result should not be interpreted as indicating better overall emotion regulation in the refugee group.

Self-esteem is closely related to a range of psychiatric disorders. Pohl et al. reported that individuals with borderline personality disorder, which is closely linked to childhood trauma, had lower levels of self-esteem and self-compassion than healthy controls [18]. Evidence suggests that low self-esteem is more likely to contribute to the development of depression than to result from depression [51]. A study of 237 refugee children and adolescents from Southeast Asia aged 6 to 17 years found that positive self-esteem was associated with greater academic success, whereas low self-esteem was associated with a higher risk of depression [25]. It is important to better understand how self-esteem is related to large-scale traumatic contexts such as war and forced displacement. In our study, refugee adolescents showed lower self-esteem, and this pattern was particularly pronounced among those who had sustained serious war-related injury. We also found that lower self-esteem was associated with emotion dysregulation, somatization, and dissociation, all of which represent clinically relevant dimensions of psychological functioning.

This study focused on refugee adolescents who migrated to Turkey with their families and/or relatives due to the Syrian war and examined a community sample rather than a treatment-seeking population, thereby reducing the likelihood that the findings reflect only the most symptomatic adolescents. The study assessed multiple dimensions of psychological distress, including emotion regulation, somatization, dissociation, and self-esteem. These findings may have practical implications for trauma-informed psychosocial support in refugee adolescents. In particular, interventions targeting emotion regulation, trauma-related distress, and adaptive coping may be especially relevant, including trauma-focused cognitive behavioral approaches and other structured supportive interventions tailored to the needs of refugee youth.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the inclusion of only Syrian refugee adolescents from a specific region in Turkey may limit the generalizability of the findings to other refugee populations and settings; accordingly, the findings should not be interpreted as fully representative of the broader refugee adolescent population. Second, although the control group was matched for age and sex and selected to be broadly comparable in socioeconomic background, it was not comprehensively characterized with respect to other contextual factors relevant to adolescent mental health, including parental education, parental mental health, and exposure to other adversities. Although additional multivariable analyses adjusted for age, gender, parental separation, parental loss, and school non-attendance, residual confounding cannot be excluded; the findings should therefore be interpreted as associations within a cross-sectional design rather than as evidence of a direct causal effect of war exposure. In addition, the exclusion of adolescents receiving active psychiatric treatment, using psychotropic medication, or living with war-related physical disability may have reduced the representativeness of the sample and may therefore have led to underestimation of the true burden of psychological distress in refugee adolescents. Third, the modest sample size may have limited statistical power, particularly in the event-specific analyses, which were based on binary indicators and did not capture the severity, chronicity, timing, overlap, or cumulative burden of traumatic experiences. In addition, the study did not include formal tests of measurement invariance or latent mean comparisons across groups; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as observed-score differences rather than firm evidence of between-group differences at the latent construct level. Internal consistency coefficients were low to modest for some measures in the refugee subgroup, particularly for the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale, and these findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. Finally, all measures were self-reported and may be subject to reporting bias. Future research would benefit from longitudinal designs, multi-informant and clinician-administered assessments, more comprehensive characterization of pre-migration, migration-related, and post-migration stressors, and larger, theory-driven analytic approaches that could help clarify whether emotion dysregulation plays a mediating role in relation to dissociation, somatization, and self-esteem in refugee adolescents.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study, refugee status, war-related experiences, forced displacement, and related psychosocial adversities were associated with a broad pattern of psychological difficulties in refugee adolescents, underscoring the importance of trauma-informed assessment and support in this population. These findings also highlight the importance of considering the interrelationship among emotion dysregulation, dissociation, somatization, and self-esteem when assessing and supporting adolescents with refugee backgrounds. The results may help inform future research involving refugee children and adolescents across diverse cultural and geographic settings.
