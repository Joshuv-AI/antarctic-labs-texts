Psychedelics and neurostimulation for treatment resistant psychiatric disorders
Authors: Filip Blazek, Klara Latalova, David Krahulik, Lumir Hrabalek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsychedelic-assisted therapy and neurostimulation represent converging approaches to modulating dysfunctional brain circuits in treatment-resistant psychiatric disorders. This Perspective outlines shared mechanisms involving neuroplasticity and network reconfiguration, and examines ethical, regulatory, and infrastructural barriers to their translation. Bridging neuroscience, psychiatry, and policy, it proposes strategies for integrating these innovative interventions into evidence-based, circuit-focused models of mental health care.

Introduction

Mental disorders affect a substantial portion of the global population, with major depressive disorder (MDD), anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), schizophrenia, and others contributing significantly to the global burden of disease [1]. Despite advances in understanding the neurobiological bases of these conditions, the current standard of care often relies on therapeutic approaches—both pharmacological and psychological—that do not achieve remission for all patients, and can take weeks or months to exert a clinically meaningful effect [2]. Alarmingly, it is estimated that up to one-third of patients with mental disorders exhibit treatment resistance, failing to respond to multiple standard therapies [3]. This treatment-resistant population not only faces a heightened risk of prolonged functional impairment and reduced quality of life but also places a significant burden on healthcare systems due to escalating costs associated with their care.

Recent years have witnessed a resurgence of interest in innovative treatments for mental disorders, driven by the urgent need to address these shortcomings. Particularly noteworthy are psychedelics—such as psilocybin, LSD, and MDMA—as well as neurostimulation therapies, including transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and deep brain stimulation (DBS) [4–7]. Early clinical trials of psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression and MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD have demonstrated rapid improvement in symptoms and, in some cases, durable responses after a limited number of dosing sessions delivered within a broader therapeutic program that typically includes structured screening, preparatory sessions, monitored dosing, and post-session integration [4, 6, 8, 13, 15]. Similarly, TMS and DBS have shown promise in cases of severe depression and obsessive–compulsive disorder [5, 9–11]. Given these findings, these methods represent a promising but still developing opportunity to enhance the standard of care through faster-acting, targeted interventions that may help those most at risk of prolonged disability, suicide, and escalating treatment costs [12].

However, the clinical adoption of such innovative methods faces multiple challenges. Regulatory concerns, ethical and social considerations, limited infrastructure, and the scarcity of practitioners trained in these interventions can hinder their widespread acceptance. Bridging the gap between promising research findings and real-world implementation therefore requires a multi-faceted approach that includes policy changes, further high-quality clinical studies, and the development of robust training programs for clinicians and care teams.

This article is intended as a selective Perspective rather than a systematic or comprehensive review. Its aim is to examine how psychedelic-assisted interventions and neurostimulation converge at the level of neural circuit modulation, neuroplasticity, and clinical translation, with emphasis on barriers relevant to implementation in contemporary psychiatric practice. To keep the discussion focused and clinically interpretable, we use major depressive disorder, treatment-resistant depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, and obsessive–compulsive disorder as representative use-cases, because these indications currently provide some of the clearest translational signals across these treatment domains. The evidence discussed is therefore illustrative rather than exhaustive, and the included tables are intended as concise orienting summaries rather than comprehensive evidence syntheses.

Emerging therapies and their promise

Psychedelics

The use of psychedelics in psychiatric treatment has a complex history, dating back to the mid-twentieth century, when agents like LSD were studied for their potential to catalyze psychotherapeutic breakthroughs [6, 13]. Renewed scientific interest in these compounds has been fueled by encouraging findings suggesting that psilocybin and MDMA can induce transformative psychological experiences leading to rapid and sustained symptomatic relief [4, 8]. In treatment-resistant depression, for example, remission rates in small clinical trials have compared favorably with some standard antidepressant approaches, and benefits may persist for months after one or two supervised dosing sessions; however, these effects occur within a treatment model that also includes careful screening, structured preparation, psychological support during dosing, and integration sessions afterward [4, 7, 15, 28, 29].

Standards and therapeutic context

Psychedelic trials share consistent best-practice elements: comprehensive medical/psychiatric screening, exclusion of psychosis/bipolar I, structured preparation, monitored dosing with two trained facilitators, and integration sessions to translate insights into behavior change [30, 33]. In psychedelic research, “set and setting” refer to two specific modulators of the acute experience: “set” denotes the participant’s mindset, expectations, and psychological state; setting denotes the external environment and contextual conditions during dosing (e.g., room design, music, therapeutic presence). Set and setting do not encompass the entire therapeutic protocol. By contrast, integration—a distinct, complementary process—refers to structured post-session psychotherapy that helps translate insights from the experience into durable behavioral change. [13, 15]

The proposed mechanisms of action include enhanced neuroplasticity [19], acute modulation of the serotonin 5-HT2A receptor, and altered functional connectivity within key brain networks [8, 14, 18]. Many patients report facilitated emotional processing, insights into personal trauma, and a sense of psychological “reset.” Although these reports are promising, larger Phase III trials are needed to confirm efficacy, identify predictors of response, and ensure the safety of these interventions in broader clinical populations—including those with multiple comorbidities or long-standing treatment resistance. Across trials, several broad best-practice elements recur, including comprehensive medical and psychiatric screening, exclusion of individuals at elevated risk of psychosis or mania, monitored dosing in a controlled environment, and planned post-session integration [13, 15, 33]. At the same time, important heterogeneity remains in psychotherapy models, therapist training, treatment manuals, dosing-day procedures, and especially the assessment and reporting of adverse events. Recent systematic work suggests that side-effect reporting in psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy trials remains inconsistent, often incomplete, and at meaningful risk of bias, underscoring the need for more standardized safety frameworks before routine clinical scaling [30, 49]. Psychedelics constitute a heterogeneous group with distinct core mechanisms: classical psychedelics act primarily via 5-HT2A agonism with large-scale network reconfiguration; MDMA (empathogen) is a monoamine-releasing agent that modulates fear circuitry and affiliative processes [20, 21]; ketamine/esketamine (dissociative) produces NMDA antagonism with downstream AMPA throughput and synaptogenic signalling [22, 23]. Because ketamine-based interventions are already incorporated into parts of mainstream psychiatric practice, they may also function as a pragmatic bridge between psychedelic psychiatry and interventional neurostimulation. Their rapid effects on glutamatergic signaling, cortical excitability, and synaptic plasticity have led to growing interest in whether ketamine-induced neuroplastic states could be paired with rTMS or related stimulation paradigms, although optimal timing, patient selection, and safety boundaries remain unsettled.

From an implementation perspective, these interventions also require careful attention to patient selection, therapeutic readiness, and trauma-informed practice. This is particularly relevant in populations with PTSD, complex trauma, dissociation, suicidality, or unstable psychosocial circumstances, where the intensity of acute experience or post-session destabilization may have practical consequences beyond symptom scales alone. In real-world settings, safe delivery therefore depends not only on protocol fidelity but also on the availability of specialized outpatient or referral pathways, longitudinal follow-up, and teams able to respond to complications or incomplete response.

Conceptually, three partially overlapping phases may be distinguished: the acute experiential phase during dosing, the early integrative phase in the hours to days that follow, and the longer adaptive phase in which behavioral and network-level changes may consolidate over time. If future multimodal protocols prove effective, neuromodulation might be timed either to shape acute or early post-acute plasticity, or later to stabilize gains and reduce relapse; however, this sequencing remains theoretical and should not yet be interpreted as an evidence-based clinical algorithm.

Neurostimulation procedures

Neurostimulation therapies, including TMS, DBS, and emerging modalities such as transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), aim to target specific neural circuits implicated in psychiatric symptoms [9, 10]. TMS, for instance, delivers focused magnetic pulses to the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and has been shown to improve depressive symptoms in patients who do not respond to first-line treatments [9]. This method is noninvasive and typically well-tolerated, though the requirement for daily sessions over several weeks may pose challenges in outpatient settings, even if this is less limiting in structured inpatient or day hospital care [34, 35, 39, 40].

DBS involves surgically implanting electrodes in deeper structures such as the subcallosal cingulate or nucleus accumbens, with electrical impulses modulating activity in circuits that govern mood and motivation [5, 11]. Though DBS is more invasive and requires sophisticated clinical infrastructure, it has yielded significant symptom improvement in patients with highly refractory depression, potentially offering a life-changing option for those who have exhausted other avenues [5, 10, 26, 27, 41–44]. While noninvasive modalities like TMS generally spare systemic side effects associated with pharmacotherapies, more invasive methods such as DBS carry distinct risks, including the possibility of neurological and psychiatric complications. Obsessive–compulsive disorder deserves specific mention as an important translational bridge within this field. Compared with several other psychiatric indications, OCD has relatively strong support for neurostimulation, particularly for DBS in highly refractory cases and for selected TMS protocols, whereas psychedelic approaches for OCD remain preliminary and investigational. This asymmetry is informative: it highlights that the translational maturity of psychedelic and neurostimulation approaches differs across diagnoses, even when both are framed within circuit-based psychiatry [50].

Mechanisms and circuit engagement

Noninvasive stimulation (rTMS/iTBS) can induce LTP/LTD-like plasticity depending on frequency, pattern, and cortical state, with downstream modulation of fronto-limbic circuits; target engagement and dosing schedules shape durability of effects [24, 25]. In contrast, DBS modulates activity within defined white-matter pathways (e.g., subcallosal cingulate; ventral capsule/ventral striatum/nucleus accumbens), with clinical response depending on target selection and parameter optimization [9–11, 37, 38].

Challenges to clinical implementation

Regulatory and ethical considerations

Despite increasing evidence for their efficacy, many psychedelic compounds remain categorized as Schedule I substances in several jurisdictions, reflecting a legacy of strict legal constraints [7, 15]. Such regulation complicates large-scale clinical research, imposes high costs on trial protocols, and creates barriers to training healthcare professionals in psychedelic-assisted therapy. Shifting these legal frameworks will likely require sustained efforts by clinicians, researchers, patient advocacy groups, and policymakers to legitimize and standardize the use of psychedelic-assisted therapy.

Neurostimulation therapies confront their own set of ethical and regulatory challenges. TMS is now FDA-cleared for treatment-resistant depression, but reimbursements and access to specialized clinics can still vary [9]. DBS, in particular, sparks ethical debates over the invasiveness of implanting electrodes in the brain, the potential for personality changes, and the need for lifelong device management [11, 16]. Ongoing dialogue around informed consent, responsible marketing, and transparent risk–benefit communication is essential to ensure ethical deployment of these cutting-edge interventions [36].

In August 2024, the FDA issued a Complete Response Letter declining approval of Lykos Therapeutics’ MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD and requested an additional Phase 3 trial, underscoring that regulatory adoption may proceed more slowly than anticipated despite positive Phase-3 efficacy findings [31, 32, 45–48].

As ongoing research continues, additional psychiatric and neurological conditions may be identified as viable targets for psychedelic and neurostimulation-based therapies. These innovative approaches hold promise for reshaping modern psychiatric care and providing alternative treatment options for individuals with refractory mental health conditions. These examples are intended to illustrate the translational landscape rather than to catalogue all current or emerging indications.

Personalized and combined approaches

One of the most promising directions for future research involves precision psychiatry—tailoring treatment according to individual biological, clinical, and cognitive characteristics rather than relying solely on diagnosis-level categories [14, 17]. In this context, biomarkers may have several potential roles: identifying patients most likely to benefit from a given modality, clarifying whether a circuit-based or trauma-processing model is more clinically relevant, and helping determine when rapid-acting interventions should be followed by maintenance neuromodulation, psychotherapy, or both. Neuroimaging, electrophysiology, and symptom-domain profiling may eventually support individualized treatment sequencing, although this goal remains aspirational and requires prospective validation before routine use.

Moreover, there is growing interest in sequential or combined interventional models. One conceptual framework is that rapid-acting pharmacological interventions may transiently increase emotional or synaptic plasticity, thereby creating a window during which neuromodulation or structured psychotherapy could have amplified effects. In practice, however, the evidence base is uneven across modalities. The interface between ketamine and rTMS has been discussed more explicitly in the recent literature [4, 14], including proposed synergistic effects on cortical excitability, network regulation, and neuroplasticity, but currently available studies remain heterogeneous and are not yet sufficient to define standard sequencing protocols. Comparable psychedelic-neuromodulation combinations remain even more preliminary. Accordingly, combined strategies should presently be viewed as hypothesis-generating translational models rather than established clinical pathways.

Roadmap for future research and policy

Long-term efficacy and safety

Although early findings are encouraging, especially for treatment-resistant populations, data on long-term outcomes and safety remain incomplete. Future trials should include extended follow-up periods to ascertain whether initial benefits are sustained, whether booster sessions are necessary, and which subgroups of patients respond most consistently [4, 8]. Large-scale registries tracking real-world results of psychedelics or neurostimulation therapies will be vital to refining guidelines and identifying rare but important adverse effects.

Refining mechanistic understanding

Further mechanistic clarification may help refine patient selection and treatment sequencing. Classical psychedelics are thought to act primarily through 5-HT2A-mediated changes in large-scale network dynamics, increased neural signal diversity, and downstream plasticity-related processes; MDMA appears more closely linked to modulation of fear circuitry, social-affiliative processing, and trauma-related emotional learning [14, 18–21]. Noninvasive stimulation techniques such as rTMS and iTBS can induce state- and protocol-dependent plasticity effects within cortical and cortico-limbic networks, whereas DBS acts through sustained modulation of activity within specific distributed circuits and white-matter pathways, with response depending strongly on target selection, connectivity, and parameter optimization [11, 24–27, 37, 38, 41–44]. Better mechanistic resolution could ultimately support more individualized dosing schedules, stimulation parameters, and multimodal treatment strategies.

Policy and access

Regulatory agencies have shown increasing receptivity to reevaluating psychedelic and neuromodulatory treatments, as evidenced by the FDA granting “breakthrough therapy” status for psilocybin in treating depression [15].

However, ensuring equitable access will require addressing reimbursement issues, scaling up workforce training, and expanding specialized facilities. Constructing national or international standards for psychedelic and neurostimulation treatment protocols could facilitate consistent, high-quality care and promote global adoption where appropriate.

An important international example is Australia, where the Therapeutic Goods Administration allows authorized psychiatrists to prescribe MDMA for post-traumatic stress disorder and psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression under tightly controlled pathways. However, this regulatory opening has not eliminated practical barriers, including limited numbers of trained clinicians, high treatment costs, restricted referral pathways, and the need for specialized multidisciplinary services. The Australian experience therefore illustrates that regulatory change, while important, is only one component of successful clinical translation.

Collaborative frameworks

Successful translation of psychedelics and neurostimulation from research to widespread clinical practice calls for multidisciplinary collaboration among neuroscientists, psychiatrists, psychologists, policymakers, industry leaders, and patient advocacy groups. Joint consortia and public–private partnerships can accelerate large-scale clinical trials, foster shared data repositories, and develop consensus statements that shape emerging standards of care. Engaging patients and caregivers as stakeholders will ensure that these treatments address real-world needs and ethical imperatives.

In practice, both psychedelic-assisted treatment and neurostimulation depend on multidisciplinary delivery models rather than single-procedure interventions. Appropriate implementation requires coordinated input from psychiatrists, psychologists or psychotherapists, nursing staff, neuromodulation specialists, and where relevant anesthetic or surgical teams, together with structured follow-up care capable of monitoring delayed adverse effects, relapse, medication changes, and psychosocial reintegration. These real-world service requirements are central to equitable access and should be considered part of the treatment model rather than external logistical details.

Conclusion

The emergence of psychedelics and neurostimulation therapies marks an important development in contemporary psychiatry, offering the possibility of offering fast-acting, and, in some cases, durable interventions that contrast with the slow onset and partial efficacy of many conventional treatments. By targeting core neural mechanisms of psychopathology, these approaches open the door to novel paradigms in precision psychiatry, potentially reshaping how we treat patients who have long struggled with insufficient relief.

Across patient populations, the strongest efficacy signals currently include MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD, psilocybin for MDD/TRD (short-term outcomes with ongoing work on durability and subgroups), rTMS/iTBS for TRD, and DBS under HDE for OCD (depression indications remain investigational). These signals should be interpreted alongside evolving standards, long-term safety, and regulatory context [4, 8, 9, 11, 12, 15]. This Perspective has intentionally focused on selected exemplar indications and translational themes rather than providing a comprehensive review of all psychiatric applications.

Nevertheless, translating this promise into everyday practice entails navigating regulatory hurdles, expanding infrastructure, and mitigating stigma. Policymakers, educators, and clinical leaders must work collectively to embed these breakthroughs within a framework that safeguards patient safety, promotes equity, and aligns with robust ethical guidelines. With continued high-quality trials, standardized protocols (including set/setting and integration), and careful post-marketing surveillance where applicable, psychedelics and neurostimulation may—over time—assume a meaningful, evidence-based role in psychiatric care; for now, their promise should be balanced with cautious interpretation of heterogeneous data, evolving standards, and regulatory pace.
