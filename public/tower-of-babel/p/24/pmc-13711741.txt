Mental health damage risk, help-seeking behavior and coping strategies among young college students under different types of network media violence: a comprehensive study based on qualitative phenomenological research methodology
Authors: Jinan Gou, Qiancheng Zhou, Lianchao An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the widespread adoption of network technology and the increasing online engagement among young college students, network media violence has become a prevalent issue threatening their physical and mental health. Different types of network violence exhibit distinct patterns and impacts on students, yet existing research lacks targeted studies on their differentiated characteristics and corresponding countermeasures. This study takes Husserl’s phenomenology ‘returning to the thing itself’ as the core essence, and adopts qualitative phenomenological research methods, including literature data method, case analysis method and phenomenon combing method, to carry out in-depth analysis, so as to restore the essential characteristics of the phenomenon. This paper categorizes network media violence into four types: explicit verbal violence, privacy violation violence, implicit ridicule exclusion violence, and disinformation and defamation violence, delineating their distinct characteristics. It further reveals how different forms of network violence pose differentiated mental health risks to college students across four dimensions: emotional experience, self-worth, interpersonal adaptation, and psychological adaptation. This study elucidates the distinct impacts of various forms of network media violence on college students’ willingness to seek help, their preferred sources of assistance, the methods they employ, and the challenges they encounter during the help-seeking process. Based on these findings, targeted strategies for addressing different types of network media violence and self-protection recommendations are proposed. The research enriches the qualitative literature on network media violence and the mental health of young college students, addressing previous gaps in insufficient attention to implicit violence and the lack of focused analysis. It provides actionable insights for enhancing students’ self-protection capabilities, improving university initiatives for network media violence prevention and mental health education, and strengthening cyberspace governance by relevant authorities.

Introduction

Digital spaces serve as core learning and social venues for college students, yet anonymity and instant spread fuel network media violence, severely endangering the mental development of adolescents with immature self-cognition [1]. Network media violence transcends the temporal and spatial constraints of traditional violence, permeating students’ online lives through various forms: from malicious insults and defamation to personal data breaches and targeted attacks; from group assaults to subtle forms of exclusion [2]. Different types of network violence exhibit distinct patterns of harm, leading to significant variations in psychological impacts and behavioral responses. College students are at a critical stage of self-awareness development and psychological resilience building; their cognitive maturity remains limited, and their emotional regulation and crisis management skills are still evolving [3]. Consequently, they are more susceptible to psychological imbalance and behavioral misconduct when exposed to malicious cyberattacks. This issue has become a pressing challenge that requires urgent attention in university mental health education and cyberspace governance [4].

Currently, the governance of network media violence has become a crucial component of legal system development and social governance in most countries. To date, the “Guiding Opinions on Punishing network media violence Offenses in Accordance with the Law,” jointly issued by China’s Supreme People’s Court, Supreme People’s Procuratorate, and Ministry of Public Security, has clarified the applicable criminal charges for network media violence and established specific provisions under criminal law for acts such as “doxxing” and defamation, demonstrating the nation’s firm commitment to combating network media violence [5]. However, existing research predominantly focuses on the overall hazards of network media violence, legal regulations, or quantitative prevalence analyses, lacking detailed typological classifications of network media violence. Moreover, it overlooks the differential mechanisms by which various forms of network media violence impair college students’ mental health, as well as the decision-making logic and challenges faced by students when seeking assistance against different types of network media violence [6]. Additionally, current studies seldom adopt a qualitative phenomenological perspective to thoroughly examine the authentic experiences and psychological impacts of network media violence on college students, making it difficult to accurately identify the latent damages and profound effects of different network media violence forms on mental health or provide targeted coping strategies for students.

The qualitative phenomenological research method centers on “reducing the essence of phenomena and listening to subjective experiences.” Through in-depth analysis of existing cases, literature, and typical phenomena, it accurately captures the genuine psychological states and behavioral patterns of the subjects of study, aligning perfectly with the core objective of this research: “focusing on the differentiated impacts of various forms of network media violence.” This study adopts a qualitative phenomenological research perspective, systematically examining cases of network media violence, relevant literature, and the online behavior patterns of college students, with a focus on the youth college student population [7]. The research centers on four core dimensions: First, it scientifically categorizes types of network media violence based on its current manifestations, clarifying the core characteristics and forms of expression for each type. Second, it conducts an in-depth analysis of the differential risks of psychological harm posed by various forms of network media violence to college students, revealing the associated mental health issues and their underlying mechanisms [8]. Third, it investigates the impact of different types of network media violence on students’ help-seeking behaviors, examining variations in their willingness to seek assistance, preferred help-seeking targets, and chosen assistance methods, along with the underlying influencing factors. Fourth, based on students’ actual needs and the typological features of network media violence, the study proposes targeted and actionable coping strategies to provide practical guidance for students in addressing network media violence and maintaining their mental health, while also offering theoretical insights for universities to enhance mental health education and improve network media violence prevention mechanisms [9].

As the primary user group of the internet, college students’ mental health not only impacts their personal growth and development but also affects social harmony, stability, and the nation’s future prospects. Currently, forms of network media violence are continuously evolving, with emerging tactics such as using deep synthesis technology to disseminate illegal content and organizing “water armies” for coordinated cyberattacks posing increasingly complex threats to students’ mental well-being [10]. Research indicates that college students exhibit higher rates of mental health impairment among online violence victims, with significantly greater prevalence of negative emotions like depression, anxiety, and stress compared to non-victims. Some students also suffer from diminished self-esteem and academic concentration issues. However, due to insufficient awareness of network media violence, inadequate coping skills, limited access to support channels, and an underdeveloped social support system, many victims tend to remain silent or avoid seeking help, making it difficult for them to receive effective assistance and support—thereby exacerbating their psychological distress [11].

Therefore, this study holds significant theoretical and practical value. At the theoretical level, employing qualitative phenomenological methods, it systematically categorizes forms of online media violence, thoroughly analyzes their distinct impacts on college students ‘mental health and seeking-help behaviors, thereby enriching existing research in this field [12]. Practically, while identifying the harms associated with various types of online media violence, the study proposes actionable strategies to enhance students’ awareness and coping abilities, guiding them to adopt scientific approaches for self-protection and seeking assistance when encountering such incidents. Additionally, this work provides practical recommendations for universities to optimize mental health education programs and establish comprehensive prevention mechanisms against online media violence, offers valuable insights for cyberspace governance and youth rights protection, and contributes to fostering a healthy, civilized, and secure digital environment that supports the holistic development of young adults [13].

Literature review

With the growing prominence of network media violence, scholars worldwide have conducted extensive research on its types, harms, influencing factors, and coping strategies, yielding substantial academic findings. Focusing on core themes, this paper systematically reviews existing literature across four dimensions: typological studies of network media violence, its detrimental effects on the mental health of college students, its impact on students’ help-seeking behaviors, and coping strategies for such violence. The analysis clarifies current research achievements and limitations, providing theoretical foundations and insights for this qualitative phenomenological study.

Typological research on network media violence

In the field of typological research on network media violence, existing studies have yet to establish a unified classification framework. Scholars have proposed diverse classification methods based on different research perspectives [14, 15]. International scholars primarily categorize network media violence according to its manifestations into three types: verbal violence (including malicious insults and defamation), visual violence (such as malicious image editing and dissemination of inappropriate photos), and relational violence (manifested as online isolation or group exclusion) [16]. Domestic research aligns more closely with China’s specific digital environment, with some scholars classifying network media violence into insult-based, defamatory, privacy-infringing, and harassment categories while clarifying the core characteristics of each type [17, 18]. Other researchers differentiate violence by dissemination platforms, such as social media violence, short-video platform violence, and forum violence [19]. However, current typological frameworks remain significantly inadequate: most studies focus solely on overt network media violence, neglecting covert forms like implicit sarcasm, malicious guidance, and cyber cold violence, and fail to tailor classifications to the online behavior patterns of college students—a critical gap that drives the focus of this study [20, 21].

Research on the impact of network media violence on the mental health of young college students

Research on the detrimental effects of network media violence on the mental health of young college students represents a focal area in existing studies, with scholars elucidating the relationship between network media violence and student psychological well-being from various perspectives [22, 23]. Current research consistently indicates that online violence exerts significant negative impacts on college students’ mental health, with core impairments primarily manifesting at three levels: emotional, cognitive, and self-perception [24]. At the emotional level, students subjected to network media violence are prone to negative emotions such as depression, anxiety, anger, and fear, with the duration of these emotions positively correlated with the intensity and persistence of the bullying [25]. At the cognitive level, some students develop cognitive distortions due to network media violence, exhibiting symptoms like hypersensitivity, self-doubt, social anxiety, and even distrust toward their surroundings [26]. At the self-perception level, malicious evaluations stemming from network media violence severely undermine students’ self-confidence and self-esteem, diminishing their sense of self-worth; some may also exhibit behaviors such as social withdrawal and reality avoidance [27]. However, most existing studies focus on the overarching detrimental effects of network media violence, employing quantitative methods to analyze incidence rates while paying insufficient attention to the differentiated mental health impairments caused by various forms of network media violence [28, 29]. Moreover, there is a lack of in-depth analysis of the underlying mechanisms, hindering the revelation of the latent hazards posed by different types of network media violence to college students’ mental health [30].

A study on the impact of network media violence on college students’ seeking help behavior

Current research on the impact of network media violence on college students’ help-seeking behaviors remains relatively limited, with studies often adopting narrow perspectives [31]. Through case analyses, scholars have identified distinct patterns in students’ response behaviors following network media violence experiences: their willingness to seek help correlates closely with the type and severity of the violence as well as the students’ individual personality traits [32, 33]. Generally, when facing overt network media violence—such as personal attacks or privacy breaches—students exhibit stronger help-seeking intentions, typically turning to classmates, friends, or family members, while some may contact platform support or their universities [34, 35]. In contrast, covert network media violence, characterized by its concealed nature, often leaves students uncertain about the nature of the violence, resulting in weaker help-seeking tendencies and a preference for silence or avoidance. Additionally, existing research highlights barriers such as inadequate support channels, high assistance costs, and concerns about secondary harm as significant factors hindering students’ willingness to seek help [36]. However, existing studies predominantly rely on cumbersome quantitative methods such as questionnaires and interviews, lacking a qualitative perspective to thoroughly examine the decision-making logic and psychological dilemmas behind college students’ seeking-help behaviors [37, 38]. The analysis of variations in seeking-help behaviors across different types of network media violence remains insufficiently detailed, and the relevance and practical applicability of research findings need further enhancement [39].

Research on strategies for young college students to respond to network media violence

In the research on strategies for addressing network media violence among college students, existing studies have primarily proposed solutions at three levels: individual, institutional, and societal [40]. At the individual level, scholars recommend enhancing students’ digital literacy, emotional regulation skills, and self-protection awareness to guide them toward rational responses to network media violence [41]. At the institutional level, universities are advised to improve their mental health education systems, introduce specialized courses on network media violence response, and establish support and intervention mechanisms [42]. At the societal level, emphasis is placed on strengthening cyberspace governance, refining relevant laws and regulations, intensifying efforts to combat network media violence, and fostering a healthy and civilized online environment [43]. However, most existing strategies remain generic recommendations that fail to address the specific characteristics of different types of network media violence or account for individual differences among students, making them inadequate for meeting diverse practical needs [44, 45]. Additionally, current research predominantly employs quantitative methods, lacking attention to students’ actual experiences, resulting in strategies that are disconnected from real-world demands and requiring further improvement in practical applicability [46].

Summary of the literature review

In summary, existing research provides a theoretical foundation and reference for this study, yet significant limitations remain: First, the classification of network media violence lacks sufficient detail, with insufficient attention paid to implicit forms of network media violence that fail to align with the characteristics of college students; second, research on the distinct mental health impacts and seeking-help behaviors induced by different types of network media violence is relatively scarce; third, current coping strategies lack specificity and practicality, relying predominantly on quantitative methods without in-depth analysis from alternative methodological perspectives. Addressing these gaps, this study employs a qualitative phenomenological approach to examine the differential effects of various forms of network media violence on young college students, delve into their authentic experiences, and propose targeted coping strategies. This approach addresses the shortcomings of prior research and offers new perspectives for studies on preventing and mitigating network media violence among college students.

Research methods and design

Selection of research methods

This study focuses on the mental health impairment risks, seeking-help behaviors, and coping strategies among young college students exposed to various forms of network media violence. Its core objective is to clarify the authentic experiences, psychological perceptions, and behavioral patterns associated with network media violence among university students, thereby uncovering the underlying mechanisms of this phenomenon. Consequently, the qualitative phenomenological research method proves most appropriate for this study. Grounded in Husserl’s phenomenology principle of “returning to the thing itself,” this approach emphasizes starting from the subjective experiences of the subjects under study and, through in-depth analysis of representative cases and empirical data, reconstructs the essential characteristics of the phenomenon.

Unlike quantitative research that focuses on data quantification, qualitative phenomenological studies emphasize interpreting the subjective experiences of subjects. They accurately capture the latent harms of various forms of network media violence to college students’ mental health, the dilemmas in seeking help, and their genuine needs during coping processes, thereby overcoming the limitation of quantitative research that “emphasizes data over experience.” Furthermore, by systematically reviewing existing cases, literature, and publicly shared accounts of college students’ network media violence experiences, this approach ensures the authenticity and relevance of research findings, aligning with Husserl’s phenomenological principle of “returning to the thing itself.”

Definition of the study subjects

The subjects of this study are young college students who have experienced network media violence. The inclusion criteria are as follows: first, they must be aged 18–24, falling within the typical age range for Chinese college students and covering students at both undergraduate and associate degree levels; second, they must have a documented history of network media violence exposure, with clear accounts—including public sharing or case records—that detail the types and processes of the violence, along with their psychological experiences, seeking help behaviors, and coping attempts; third, the incidents must have occurred within the past five years to ensure the timeliness of the research data and its relevance to current forms of network media violence and the coping strategies employed by college students.

To ensure the comprehensiveness and representativeness of the study, the subjects included college students of diverse genders, academic backgrounds, and internet usage frequencies, covering individuals who have experienced various forms of network media violence. This encompasses both victims of overt network media violence (such as verbal abuse, rumor-mongering, and privacy breaches) and those subjected to covert network media violence (including emotional coldness, implicit sarcasm, and malicious guidance). This approach prevents one-sided conclusions arising from a homogeneous sample and enables a thorough analysis of the distinct impacts of different types of network media violence on college students.

Research tools and data collection

This study employed a combined approach of literature review, case analysis, and phenomenon mapping to collect and organize research data, ensuring the authenticity, relevance, and comprehensiveness of the materials.

The first step involves literature collection: systematically reviewing domestic and international publications on network media violence, college students’ mental health, seeking-help behaviors, and coping strategies—including academic journal articles, dissertations, monographs, policy documents, and industry reports. The focus is on synthesizing research findings regarding the classification of network media violence types, mechanisms underlying mental health impairment, and influencing factors of seeking-help behaviors, thereby providing theoretical support for the study while avoiding redundant research and clarifying its research focus and innovative contributions.

The second step involves collecting case materials, selecting publicly available typical cases of network media violence among college students as the core research source. Case sources include news reports, publicly disclosed cases from university mental health centers, personal accounts shared by students on social media platforms (such as Weibo, Zhihu, and Douban), and publicly available court rulings related to network media violence. Case selection adheres to the principles of typicality and representativeness, covering various types of network media violence to ensure each category has corresponding representative cases. Additionally, the cases are comprehensive, clearly illustrating the progression of network media violence incidents, students’ psychological reactions, their attempts to seek help, and the outcomes of their responses.

The third method is the phenomenon analysis approach. By browsing channels such as social media platforms, university forums, and short-video platforms, this method identifies common manifestations, psychological experiences, and behavioral tendencies exhibited by college students after experiencing network media violence. It collects unstructured experiential data—including student comments, shared posts, and emotional expressions—to address gaps in case materials, thereby enriching research resources and ensuring a comprehensive reconstruction of the actual circumstances faced by college students under network media violence.

Fourth, the inclusion of material standardization criteria. This study establishes standardized guidelines for data inclusion and screening, primarily manifested in two aspects: quantitative criteria for case material selection and screening rules for social media phenomenon materials. First, regarding data inclusion criteria: participants must be college students aged 18–24; cyberbullying incidents must comprehensively document causes, perpetrator behavior, victims’ psychological experiences, and assistance-seeking actions; materials should have been published within the past five years (2021–2026); information must be complete and traceable (media sources specified in news reports, case numbers indicated in legal cases, original screenshots preserved from social sharing). Non-compliant materials are excluded if: participant ages do not match; descriptions are fragmented without full event details; information is ambiguous and unverifiable; cyberbullying behaviors lack differentiation in types; or cases exhibit repetition or high homogeneity. Second, social media material screening rules: only first-person subjective accounts from college students are retained; third-party objective narratives, promotional content, and emotional comments unrelated to personal experiences are excluded; all texts containing emotional, social, or self-awareness-related expressions are uniformly extracted as raw coding materials.

Research process design

This study strictly adhered to the core methodology of qualitative phenomenological research. Tailored to the research topic and characteristics of data collection, the following research protocol was designed to ensure systematic and logically rigorous implementation:

The first step involves literature collection: systematically reviewing domestic and international publications on network media violence, college students’ mental health, seeking-help behaviors, and coping strategies—including academic journal articles, dissertations, monographs, policy documents, and industry reports. The focus is on synthesizing research findings regarding the typological classification of network media violence, the mechanisms underlying mental health impairment, and the influencing factors of seeking-help behaviors, thereby providing theoretical support for the study while avoiding redundant research and clarifying its research focus and innovative contributions.

The second step involves collecting case materials: selected typical cases of network media violence against college students that are publicly available serve as the core research source. Case sources include news reports, publicly disclosed cases from university mental health centers, personal accounts shared by students on social media platforms (e.g., Weibo, Zhihu, Douban), and publicly available court rulings related to network media violence. Case selection adheres to the principles of typicality and representativeness, covering various types of network media violence to ensure each category has corresponding representative cases. Additionally, the cases are comprehensive, clearly illustrating the occurrence process of network media violence, students’ psychological reactions, their seeking-of-help behaviors, and the outcomes of their responses.

This study employs a three-tier coding approach combined with phenomenological reduction to systematically analyze raw data. The comprehensive analytical process comprises the following steps: First, open coding: All selected cases and social interaction texts were analyzed sentence by sentence to extract free nodes, yielding initial codes—for example, classifying “repeated use of offensive language in private messages” as “direct verbal personal attacks” and “deliberate disregard of individual contributions in class groups” as “online group exclusion.” Second, axial coding: Initial codes were clustered based on patterns of harm and affected entities, identifying subcategories that ultimately formed four major categories representing distinct forms of online media violence. Additionally, four secondary damage categories—emotional distress, self-worth perception, interpersonal adaptation, and psychological adjustment—were derived from victims ‘subjective experiences. Third, selective coding: The core category “different types of online media violence” was established, establishing a logical framework linking violence types, manifestations of psychological harm, and victim-seeking behaviors. Throughout this process, phenomenological reduction was applied: existing academic classifications of cyberbullying were temporarily set aside; irrelevant contextual information was removed; only objective records of violent acts and authentic victims’ psychological experiences remained; and the authentic nature of various cyberharassment behaviors was reconstructed. Ultimately, these refined phenomena enabled precise definition and characterization of the four categories of online media violence.

The third method is the phenomenon analysis approach. By examining channels such as social media platforms, university forums, and short-video platforms, this method identifies common manifestations, psychological experiences, and behavioral tendencies among college students affected by network media violence. It collects unstructured experiential data—including student comments, shared posts, and emotional expressions—to address gaps in case materials, thereby enriching research resources and ensuring a comprehensive reconstruction of the actual experiences of college students subjected to network media violence.

This study comprehensively documents the data screening criteria, operational guidelines for three-level coding, and steps for phenomenological reduction. All original coding records and case screening logs have been archived, enabling other researchers to adopt identical inclusion/exclusion criteria, coding logic, and phenomenological reduction procedures. By utilizing publicly available cases of online violence among college students and social media sharing materials, the analysis can be repeated with high reproducibility.

Types of network media violence

Building upon the systematic organization of raw data through standardized material screening, three-level coding analysis, and phenomenological reduction procedures, this study integrates the characteristics of online victimization experiences among college students and adopts a qualitative phenomenological approach. By incorporating existing case materials, analyses of college students ‘online behaviors, and the actual manifestations of cybermedia violence, it focuses on common scenarios of cybermedia violence faced by young university students. The research categorizes cybermedia violence into four types: explicit verbal violence, privacy-violation violence, implicit ridicule-exclusion violence, and disinformation and defamation violence. This classification not only accounts for the diverse forms of cybermedia violence but also aligns with the realities of college students’ online lives, accurately reflecting the core harmful features of each category.

Explicit verbal violence

Explicit verbal violence is the most prevalent form of network media violence faced by college students. Its defining characteristic lies in the use of direct, malicious verbal attacks that insult or abuse students’ personalities, appearances, and abilities. This form of violence demonstrates clear intent, employs direct communication methods, and exhibits high levels of aggression and humiliation. It typically occurs in high-traffic online platforms such as social media, short-video comment sections, and university forums, often triggered by differing opinions, unintentional offenses, or malicious provocations. The content spreads rapidly and easily incites herd mentality attacks.

Specific manifestations include: using offensive language and vulgar insults against college students in comment sections or private messages; engaging in malicious ridicule and personal attacks targeting students’ appearance, physique, family background, or academic abilities; conducting persistent verbal harassment through flooding messages or tagging others to force responses; employing aggressive language in public discussions to dismiss students’ viewpoints and values, or even engaging in personal defamation. For instance, when college students share their academic achievements or daily lives on social platforms, they are often subjected to malicious remarks such as “useless,” “absolutely ugly,” or “showing off despite having no talent.” Such violent behavior is direct and intense, capable of instantly triggering negative emotions among students.

Privacy violation violence

Privacy violation violence refers to a form of network media violence (network media violence) characterized by the unauthorized disclosure or dissemination of college students’ personal privacy information without their consent, or the malicious use of such information to harass or threaten them. Its core violation involves infringing upon students’ right to privacy, with effects that are concealed, persistent, and potentially extending into real life, posing a serious threat to their personal safety. Perpetrators of this type of network media violence are typically individuals familiar with the students (such as classmates or friends), though they may also be strangers online; they obtain and maliciously spread private information to exert psychological pressure and threats against the students.

Specific manifestations include: unauthorized disclosure of college students’ personal basic information such as names, student IDs, contact details, home addresses, and ID numbers; dissemination of private content including photos, videos, chat records, and diaries, or even malicious photo editing and alteration of such information before sharing; threatening students with privacy breaches to force them to comply with unreasonable demands like money transfers, apologies, or relinquishing certain rights; and collecting various private details through methods like “doxxing” and publicly exposing them online, thereby sparking public scrutiny and discussion. Such violent acts not only instill profound insecurity among students but may also lead to real-life harassment, adversely affecting their academic and daily lives.

Implicit ridicule exclusion violence

Implicit ridicule exclusion violence is a highly concealed form of network media violence (network media violence). Its core characteristic lies in employing subtle tactics—such as implicit ridicule, malicious guidance, and online isolation—to psychologically marginalize and suppress college students without direct verbal aggression. The violent intent remains concealed and difficult to detect, yet it inflicts lasting harm to students’ mental health. This type of network media violence predominantly occurs within college students’ social circles (e.g., class groups, dormitory groups, interest groups), with perpetrators often being fellow students or friends who isolate victims through covert means, thereby undermining their social relationships.

Specific manifestations include: in group chats and social platforms, using sarcastic language to mock college students’ behaviors and opinions, such as “You’re so impressive—you’re the only one who truly understands” or “No wonder you’re the one; you always come up with something unique”; maliciously guiding others to isolate college students by privately spreading negative remarks about them, preventing interactions, or deliberately ignoring their posts and refusing engagement online; indirectly amplifying negative perceptions through reposting or liking negative comments, thereby creating subtle public pressure; and in team collaborations or online activities, intentionally excluding or neglecting college students while denying their contributions and value. Although these violent behaviors are not direct attacks, they cause college students to feel isolated and self-doubting, severely impacting their social confidence and mental well-being over time.

Disinformation and defamation violence

Disinformation and defamation violence refers to a form of network media violence (network media violence) that involves fabricating false information and distorting facts to defame and tarnish the reputation and image of college students. Its primary infringement targets the students’ right to reputation, with the harm stemming from the rapid dissemination and wide reach of false information, which can cause irreversible damage to their personal image, academic progress, and social lives. Perpetrators of this type of network media violence typically act out of jealousy, retaliation, or malicious hype, fabricating false information and widely spreading it online to induce misunderstandings and negative evaluations.

The specific manifestations include: fabricating negative incidents involving college students—such as academic fraud, misconduct, or violations of university regulations—and disseminating them online; distorting students’ statements and actions by taking them out of context and publishing them, thereby misleading public perception of college students; forging evidence like chat records, images, and videos to fabricate students’ “faults” and engage in defamation; and publishing false reports against students online, hindering their academic and personal development. For example, spreading widespread false information about students cheating on exams or engaging in campus bullying can lead to misunderstandings by peers and faculty, damage their reputation, and even affect critical opportunities such as awards, honors, and employment.

The risk of mental health damage caused by different network media violence

From the original nodes representing victims ‘subjective experiences extracted through open coding, four major dimensions of mental health impairment—emotional experience, self-worth, interpersonal adaptation, and psychological adaptation—were identified via principal component analysis. The following sections elaborate on distinct risk patterns associated with each category of cyberbullying. When confronted with different forms of online media violence, college students experience markedly differentiated mental health risks due to variations in its characteristics, transmission methods, and impact scope. Building upon the four categories of cyberbullying defined earlier and supported by collected case studies and students’ subjective feedback, this paper systematically analyzes the specific mental health risks posed by various types of cyberbullying across these four core dimensions: emotional experience, self-worth, interpersonal adaptation, and psychological adaptation.

The risk of mental health impairment caused by explicit verbal violence

Explicit verbal violence centers on direct and malicious linguistic attacks. Its aggressive nature and immediate transmission can instantly trigger negative emotions in college students, easily leading to herd-like aggressive behaviors. This type of violence exhibits immediate and overt effects on students’ mental health; prolonged exposure may solidify negative emotions and result in persistent psychological issues.

From the perspective of emotional experience, college students who encounter explicit verbal violence instantly experience negative emotions such as anger, resentment, and fear; some sensitive students may even exhibit emotional breakdowns, irritability, or restlessness, severely impacting their daily emotional well-being. In terms of self-worth, prolonged malicious insults and personal attacks severely undermine students’ self-confidence and self-esteem, leading to self-denial, self-doubt, and even negative beliefs like “I’m not good enough” or “I’m worthless.” Regarding interpersonal adaptation, the public nature of explicit verbal violence causes students to feel ashamed and embarrassed, prompting them to avoid both online and real-life social interactions due to fear of repeated attacks, ultimately resulting in social withdrawal behaviors. From the perspective of psychological adaptation, the negative emotions induced by explicit verbal violence can distract college students, leading to reduced learning efficiency and poor concentration. Some students may also experience sleep disorders such as insomnia and excessive dreaming. Over time, this can result in impaired psychological adaptation, disrupting normal academic and daily routines. The specific risk profile is detailed in Table 2.

The risk of psychological health impairment caused by privacy violation violence

The core of privacy violation violence lies in infringing upon college students’ right to privacy. Its harm is characterized by concealment and persistence, and may extend into real-life contexts, severely undermining students’ psychological security. Compared to explicit verbal violence, its impact on mental health is more profound and enduring, often leading to long-term psychological trauma.

The covert nature of privacy violation violence makes it difficult for college students to take preventive measures in advance. Once personal privacy is leaked or disseminated, it triggers intense feelings of insecurity and panic, with concerns about further spread of the breach or even threats to personal safety in real life. From the perspective of self-worth, malicious dissemination or alteration of privacy makes students feel offended and disrespected, leading to emotions such as inferiority and shame, and a denial of their own value. In terms of interpersonal adaptation, privacy breaches cause students to fear gossip from classmates and friends, prompting them to avoid social interactions and resulting in strained relationships or a sense of isolation. Regarding psychological adaptation, prolonged exposure to privacy invasion fosters persistent anxiety, heightened sensitivity, and suspicion, sometimes manifesting as compulsive phone checking or avoidance of online activities, severely impairing psychological resilience. The impact of different types of network media violence on college students’ help-seeking behavior

Help-seeking behavior refers to the proactive coping strategies adopted by college students after experiencing network media violence (network media violence) to mitigate psychological harm and escape the abusive situation. The rationale behind these choices and their manifestations are directly influenced by the type and severity of the online violence, as well as the students’ own psychological state. This paper systematically analyzes help-seeking behavior across four core dimensions: the intensity of help-seeking willingness, the selection of help-seeking targets, preferences for help-seeking methods, and the dilemmas encountered during the help-seeking process.

The impact of explicit verbal violence on college students’ help-seeking Bbehavior

Explicit verbal violence centers on direct verbal attacks, characterized by overt aggression and clear violent intent. It predominantly occurs in public online settings such as comment sections and university forums, easily triggering herd mentality attacks and exerting immediate emotional impact on college students. These features result in students exhibiting a “moderate to high intensity” help-seeking willingness upon encountering such violence. Their assistance-seeking behaviors are immediate and targeted, primarily aimed at “halting the aggression and alleviating emotions.” However, influenced by their personality traits and social concerns, the individuals they seek help from and the methods they employ remain relatively limited.

Regarding help-seeking willingness, the majority of college students exhibit clear intention to seek assistance after experiencing explicit verbal violence. Students with extroverted personalities and strong self-protection awareness demonstrate stronger willingness to seek help, whereas introverted and sensitive students, despite having such willingness, often hesitate due to concerns about “involvement” or “fear of further ridicule.” In terms of assistance recipients, students primarily turn to familiar individuals (classmates, friends, family), followed by online customer service platforms, with fewer choices including university teachers, counselors, or relevant departments. The core reason is that “acquaintances are more empathetic and communication costs are lower,” while they worry that seeking help from the university might “amplify the incident’s impact.” Regarding assistance methods, “online communication” predominates—such as messaging friends for advice or submitting reports to platform customer service—while offline or legal channels are less frequently used. Assistance-seeking behaviors are predominantly concentrated within 1–3 days after the violence occurs, reflecting an immediate nature. Regarding help-seeking dilemmas, the primary challenges include “slow platform response,” “inability to effectively resolve harm through acquaintances,” and “fear of retaliation from the perpetrator after seeking help.” Detailed impacts are presented in Table 7.

The harm caused by this form of violence is gradual and insidious, making it challenging for students to recognize whether they are experiencing network media violence. Consequently, their willingness to seek help is generally low to moderate, with their assistance-seeking behaviors being passive and ambiguous; many students even choose to remain silent or avoid help altogether. Such seeking behaviors are passive, typically occurring only when psychological pressure becomes overwhelming and self-regulation proves ineffective, with individuals confiding in close acquaintances rather than actively seeking concrete solutions. From the perspective of help-seeking dilemmas, students primarily face challenges such as “difficulty defining violent behavior and inability to provide evidence,” “lack of understanding after seeking help,” and “the risk of secondary exclusion within their social circles.” These factors lead many students to abandon seeking help despite their willingness to do so. The impact of disinformation and defamation violence on college students’ help-seeking behavior

Disinformation and defamation violence centers on fabricating false information and distorting facts, infringing upon college students’ right to reputation. It spreads rapidly and has a wide reach, causing irreversible damage to students’ personal image, academic development, and social lives, and even triggering severe psychological crises. The consequences of such violence are the most severe; consequently, college students exhibit a “high intensity” in their willingness to seek help, with their assistance-seeking behaviors aimed primarily at “discrediting rumors, protecting their reputation, and holding parties accountable.” The targets and methods of assistance-seeking are more diverse and proactive, and the process is continuous, yet the associated dilemmas are also more complex.

Regarding help-seeking willingness, the vast majority of college students exhibit strong willingness to seek assistance after experiencing disinformation and defamation-related violence. The primary reason is that “rumors severely damage personal reputation, academic performance, and future prospects”; even introverted students proactively seek help to protect their rights. In terms of assistance sources, students utilize multiple channels: prioritizing online platforms (for rumor removal) and university faculty/counselors (for clarification and support), followed by family and friends (for emotional support). When rumors have severe impacts, some students turn to public security authorities or legal channels to hold perpetrators accountable, addressing the core needs for rumor clarification and rights protection. Regarding assistance methods, the predominant approach combines platform reporting, rumor clarification, and evidence preservation, supplemented by offline interventions (such as consulting teachers or reporting to authorities) and legal consultations. This proactive and sustained approach ensures continuous monitoring of rumor removal and clarification progress until the rumors are completely eradicated. Regarding help-seeking dilemmas, the primary challenges include: “rapid spread of rumors that are difficult to thoroughly dispel,” “difficulty in preserving evidence and holding perpetrators accountable,” “slow platform response,” and “negative impacts persist even after rumor clarification.” Some students also experience psychological stress due to negative evaluations stemming from rumors, which hinders their ability to seek help effectively. The dual structural and cultural barriers that hinder college students from seeking help through formal channels

As previously discussed, college students face distinct difficulties in accessing assistance when encountering four types of online media violence. Their reluctance to utilize formal channels such as universities, public security authorities, or legal systems stems from both soft barriers at the cultural level and structural barriers at the institutional level. Among these, the constraints posed by implicit mocking and exclusionary violence and privacy-infringing violence are particularly prominent, and they exhibit significant gaps compared to existing university support systems and the legal framework governing cyberbullying.

Cultural barriers hindering formal recourse mechanisms. First, a culture of stigma and shame in privacy violation scenarios. Both campus and society often hold biased perceptions, attributing personal data breaches and private image dissemination solely to victims themselves. College students fear being labeled negatively by teachers or peers after disclosing their experiences to counselors or authorities, leading to intense shame and avoidance of formal complaint channels. Even when privacy leaks threaten personal safety, most students prefer confiding privately with family or friends rather than seeking institutional assistance. Second, a campus social culture emphasizing “conflict avoidance” under implicit violence within familiar circles. Hidden mockery and exclusion frequently occur in offline-based online social networks like classrooms and dormitories. A prevailing consensus exists that “one should not initiate conflicts,” with students fearing group criticism for being “sensitive or overreacting” when seeking school support—a practice that exacerbates dual isolation both online and offline, prompting them to abandon formal assistance from counselors or university support centers. Third, insufficient awareness of cyberbullying creates conceptual gaps. Media coverage and university outreach primarily focus on overt forms of cyberbullying such as explicit insults and public exposure of private information, while lacking education about covert forms like psychological harassment. Most students fail to recognize sarcastic remarks or online isolation as violations of rights, subjectively deeming their experiences unwarranting formal help-seeking, which further reduces their willingness to seek assistance.

Structural institutional barriers hinder students from seeking formal assistance. These obstacles stem from inherent limitations in universities ‘existing support systems, governance rules of online platforms, and practical implementation procedures of current laws, creating substantial barriers to formal help-seeking. Firstly, there are structural gaps in university support systems for addressing cyberbullying. Current mental health centers and student complaint channels primarily handle overt incidents such as verbal abuse, widespread privacy breaches, and malicious rumors, but lack standardized intervention protocols for covert forms of mockery, exclusion, or online isolation. Additionally, there is no sustained collaboration mechanism with online platforms or law enforcement authorities; when students face covert privacy violations, institutions cannot assist with evidence collection, account tracing, or reporting procedures, increasing time and resource costs for formal assistance. Secondly, complaint handling mechanisms on online platforms exhibit structural deficiencies. Reporting systems fail to distinguish between overt violence and covert relational violence, lacking dedicated channels for evidencing covert mockery or online isolation, while defining textual evidence of covert violence remains challenging. In cases of mass privacy content sharing, platforms lack bulk deletion capabilities or traceability tools for dissemination chains, only processing individual reports individually—making it impossible to effectively curb privacy leaks and undermining students’ confidence in seeking legal protection online. Thirdly, the legal framework governing cyberbullying faces systemic barriers at the operational level. The “Guiding Opinions on Punishing Cyberbullying Offenses in Accordance with the Law” explicitly regulate human flesh searches and acts of insult or defamation; however, judicial practice faces practical challenges such as difficulties in providing evidence and determining the perpetrators’ identities. For covert forms of mockery and exclusion, the extent of harm often fails to meet the criteria for civil liability or criminal prosecution, while legal remedies are excessively burdensome, leading students to develop the misconception that “minor covert online harms cannot be addressed through proper legal channels” and consequently refrain from seeking judicial assistance.

Decision-making strategies and self-protection recommendations

Resolution strategies and self-protection recommendations for explicit verbal violence

The core approach to addressing explicit verbal violence lies in “timely damage mitigation, rational response, and proactive rights protection,” balancing emotional regulation with preventing further harm. First, college students should remain rational, avoiding arguments or confrontations with perpetrators to prevent escalation and secondary attacks [47]. Immediately capture screenshots of malicious comments or private messages—including the perpetrator’s account details, publication dates, and content—to support subsequent rights protection efforts. Second, take proactive measures to mitigate harm: block the perpetrator’s account, close comment sections or private messaging functions to avoid continuous exposure to harmful content and reduce negative emotional impact; simultaneously submit a formal report to the platform, detailing the violent behavior, and urge prompt removal of malicious content and punishment of the perpetrator. Finally, prioritize emotional regulation and psychological counseling—share feelings with classmates or friends to alleviate negative emotions and avoid self-denial [48]. If emotional regulation proves challenging, seek assistance from the university’s mental health center; if violent behavior persists and disrupts daily life, consult campus counselors or law enforcement authorities to legally protect one’s rights and cultivate self-protection awareness, avoiding collapse under malicious rhetoric.

Resolution strategies and self-protection recommendations for privacy violation violence

The core strategy against privacy violation violence lies in “halting dissemination, preserving evidence, and seeking legal redress,” with a focus on preventing further leakage of personal data. First, college students should immediately take preventive measures by submitting privacy deletion requests to online platforms, demanding the removal of leaked content and blocking related sharing links, while contacting the individuals responsible to cease dissemination and delete relevant materials. Second, comprehensively preserve evidence—including original leaked content, sharing records, and platform response logs—with screen recordings and notarization when necessary to provide solid evidence for holding perpetrators accountable [49]. Third, strengthen self-protection awareness by promptly changing account passwords, concealing sensitive personal information, and preventing further leaks; if privacy breaches threaten personal safety, immediately inform family members, school counselors, and file a report with authorities for professional assistance. Additionally, overcome feelings of shame, seek help proactively, avoid isolating oneself due to privacy breaches, and receive psychological counseling when needed to alleviate negative emotions like panic and anxiety, preventing worsening psychological harm.

Resolution strategies and self-protection recommendations for ridicule exclusion violence

To address implicit ridicule and exclusion violence, the core strategies involve “clear definition, proactive avoidance, and self-acceptance” to overcome the challenges posed by covert online harassment. First, college students should recognize that they are experiencing implicit network media violence, abandon the misconception that “it’s just a matter of personal sensitivity,” avoid self-denial, objectively evaluate the perpetrator’s actions, and strengthen their self-worth. Second, actively avoid harm by exiting social groups or discussion forums where implicit ridicule and exclusion occur, minimize online interactions with perpetrators, and refrain from continuous exposure to negative information. Simultaneously, cultivate positive social relationships and communicate more with individuals who understand and support them to alleviate feelings of loneliness and depression [50]. Third, learn self-management techniques—such as engaging in physical activities or hobbies—to release psychological stress and enhance mental resilience, preventing oneself from being overwhelmed by the negative emotions of implicit violence. If psychological pressure becomes overwhelming and self-regulation proves difficult, seek professional help from family, close friends, or campus mental health centers for emotional support and coping guidance. When necessary, retain evidence of ridicule or exclusion and report it to the relevant platform or institution to prevent the continuation of violent behavior.

Resolution strategies and self-protection recommendations for disinformation and defamation violence

The core strategy for combating disinformation and defamation-related violence lies in “rapid clarification, evidence preservation, and accountability,” aiming to minimize the negative impact of rumors. First, college students should promptly issue clarification statements detailing the false claims supported by facts and relevant evidence, published on major rumor-sharing platforms to guide public understanding and curb further dissemination. Second, comprehensively document evidence—including rumor content, dissemination records, and the publication of clarification statements [51]. If rumors involve serious offenses such as academic fraud or misconduct, submit supporting materials to university authorities to prove innocence. Third, pursue legal remedies through multiple channels: report rumor-spreading accounts to platforms to demand content removal and punish perpetrators; consult lawyers for civil or criminal liability if rumors have widespread impact. Additionally, maintain psychological resilience by avoiding negative evaluations from rumors, seeking support from family, teachers, and friends, and seeking professional counseling when necessary to safeguard mental health and prevent severe psychological crises.

Institutional optimization strategies targeting universities, platforms, and regulatory authorities

At the university level, it is essential to enhance a tiered and categorized support system for addressing cyberbullying to mitigate structural shortcomings. Special procedures should be established for handling covert forms of cyberbullying, with counselors conducting relevant training and establishing collaborative intervention mechanisms between psychological centers and student affairs offices. A one-stop campus rights protection channel should be created to provide evidence notarization, assistance in reporting incidents, and legal consultation services for students affected by privacy breaches. Regular public awareness campaigns should be conducted to eliminate misconceptions about covert violence and dispel the campus bias that views seeking help as shameful. At the platform level, reporting mechanisms should be optimized by categorizing cyberbullying reports specifically and adding dedicated channels for reporting “covert sarcasm” and “online social isolation.” Technical capabilities for batch tracing of privacy leaks and automated post deletion should be improved to reduce the burden of evidence collection for students seeking redress. In terms of legal and regulatory frameworks, detailed implementation guidelines should be developed: specific criteria for identifying damages related to covert relational cyberbullying should be established to lower evidentiary thresholds in civil cases; real-name account tracing mechanisms should be refined to streamline reporting and evidence collection processes for personal data infringement cases.

Research conclusion

First, employing phenomenological reduction and coding analysis, this study categorizes network media violence into four distinct types based on the fundamental patterns of harmful behaviors identified from case repositories and experiential texts, each exhibiting unique characteristics in both mechanisms of harm and manifestations (see Table 1 for comparison): Explicit verbal violence is characterized by overt malicious insults and public defamation of personal dignity, demonstrating immediacy and visibility; Privacy violation violence involves data breaches and privacy threats, reflecting covertness, persistence, and potential real-world extension, with victims often reporting persistent distress; Implicit ridicule-exclusion violence stems from sarcastic remarks and deliberate isolation tactics, featuring strong concealment, difficulty in quantification or verification, and typically manifesting as forms of subtle violence within campus social circles. Disinformation and defamation violence is grounded phenomenologically in core mechanisms such as fabricating negative narratives and falsifying evidence; its typical consequences include rapid dissemination of false information, extensive social impact, and severe, often irreversible damage.

Second, there are significant differences in the risk of harm to college students ‘mental health across different types of online media violence. These differences stem from core categories derived from the initial coding of victims’ experiences—such as direct insults, privacy violations on social platforms, sarcastic remarks within groups, and rumor dissemination—and are specifically reflected in four analytical dimensions: emotional experience, self-worth, interpersonal adaptation, and psychological adaptation. This differentiated pattern of psychological harm manifests as follows: Explicit verbal violence causes immediate emotional distress and short-term social withdrawal (typical feedback includes “bursting into tears upon hearing abusive remarks, unable to sleep for days,” see Table 2); Privacy violation violence severely compromises psychological security and triggers chronic anxiety and panic (typical feedback: “experiencing daily panic and low mood after private photos were shared,” see Table 3); Implicit ridicule exclusion violence is characterized by progressive psychological suppression and persistent erosion of self-esteem, with common feedback being “gradual decline in speechability and self-confidence” (see Table 4); Disinformation and defamation violence entails comprehensive psychological damage affecting emotions, self-worth, interpersonal relationships, and behavior, often accompanied by extreme crisis scenarios such as suicidal ideation (see Table 5), indicating its high propensity to induce severe psychological crises.

Third, different types of online media violence directly shape distinct patterns of help-seeking behavior among college students. The characteristics of the four types of violence, including the intensity of the need for assistance, the primary individuals seeking help, the main methods used to seek assistance, and the core difficulties encountered, were derived from a detailed analysis of the corresponding tables in Chap. 6 (Tables 7, 8, 9 and 10) and the coding of victims’ original assistance-seeking behaviors (e.g., “immediately taking screenshots and sharing them with friends,” “daily following up on platform handling,” “only complaining to close friends but avoiding teachers,” “immediately requesting the platform to delete posts and consulting counselors for clarification”). Specifically: explicit verbal violence (Table 7) exhibits a “moderate-to-high intensity” help-seeking tendency due to its overt nature, primarily manifesting as immediate reporting to acquaintances or platforms for resolution; privacy violation violence (Table 8) demonstrates the strongest help-seeking motivation driven by fear of information leakage, with behaviors centered on requesting post removal and systematic evidence preservation; implicit ridicule exclusion violence (Table 9), due to its ambiguous definition, shows weak help-seeking intent and passive, limited responses, confined to emotional venting through private confessions to close friends. Disinformation and defamation violence (see Table 10) exhibits the strongest and most proactive willingness to seek assistance due to its severe consequences, with the most complex assistance-seeking behaviors that involve multi-platform reporting, comprehensive evidence preservation, and multi-channel efforts to obtain clarification and accountability.

Fourth, given the distinct characteristics of the four types of network media violence, college students should adopt targeted strategies: for explicit verbal violence, they should promptly mitigate losses and protect their rights rationally; for privacy violation violence, they should block its spread and seek legal redress; for implicit ridicule and exclusion violence, they should actively avoid it and embrace themselves; for disinformation and defamation violence, they should swiftly clarify the facts and pursue accountability through multiple channels—all while emphasizing psychological adjustment and self-protection.

Fifth, college students often abandon formal assistance channels such as schools, public security authorities, and legal systems, while facing dual structural barriers stemming from cultural perceptions and the constraints imposed by universities, digital platforms, and legal frameworks. The most pronounced issues arise in scenarios involving implicit ridicule, exclusion, and privacy violations, where existing campus support mechanisms and practical legal regulations fail to adequately address the rights protection needs arising from these two forms of covert cyberbullying. Institutional optimization must be advanced through multi-dimensional approaches encompassing campus governance, platform regulation, and legal framework development.

Research discussion

Research contributions and significance

Theoretically, this study focuses on the college student population and provides a practical typological classification of online media violence, addressing the shortcomings of existing research—particularly its lack of detailed categorization and neglect of implicit forms of violence. Furthermore, it systematically analyzes the distinct impacts of different types of online media violence on college students’ mental health and seeking-help behaviors, revealing the intrinsic link between such violence and help-seeking behaviors. This work enriches qualitative research in the field of online media violence and college student mental health, enhances the theoretical framework of related studies, and offers both theoretical support and new perspectives for future research.

In terms of practical significance, the targeted response strategies and self-protection recommendations proposed in this study align closely with the actual needs of college students. They help students clearly identify different types of cyberbullying, enhance their awareness of self-protection and coping skills, effectively avoid harm, mitigate psychological distress, and safeguard their legitimate rights and interests. The findings provide practical references for universities to optimize mental health education programs and establish comprehensive prevention and control systems against cyberbullying, while also supporting institutions in delivering tailored psychological counseling and guidance. Furthermore, they offer valuable insights for online platforms to improve their violence governance mechanisms and for relevant authorities to strengthen cyberspace regulation, contributing to the creation of a healthy, civilized, and safe digital environment that promotes the holistic well-being of young college students.

Limitations and future research directions

The limitations of this study are primarily manifested in three aspects: First, the limitation of research materials; the study relies mainly on publicly available cases, literature, and experiential data shared by college students, lacking in-depth, dynamic tracking of students’ experiences with cyberbullying (network media violence), and the collection of evidence regarding certain forms of covert violence is insufficient, potentially compromising the comprehensiveness of the findings. Second, the limitation of the research perspective; the study focuses solely on college students and does not examine the roles of perpetrators, online platforms, universities, or other stakeholders, leaving gaps in understanding the mechanisms underlying cyberbullying and strategies for collaborative prevention. Third, the limitation of the research methodology; qualitative phenomenological research emphasizes subjective experience analysis and lacks quantitative data support, making the generalizability of the conclusions subject to further verification.

Given these limitations, future research can be pursued along three key directions: First, enriching research materials and methodologies by conducting longitudinal tracking studies of college students experiencing cyberbullying to gather comprehensive dynamic data, while incorporating quantitative methods to supplement numerical evidence and enhance the robustness and generalizability of findings; Second, broadening the research perspective to include perpetrators, online platforms, universities, and families, investigating the mechanisms underlying cyberbullying and analyzing the roles of various stakeholders in prevention efforts to establish a collaborative prevention framework; Third, focusing on emerging forms of cyberbullying and leveraging advancements in internet technology to develop more timely and targeted countermeasures, while examining differences in coping strategies across different academic years and disciplines to improve research relevance and practical applicability.
