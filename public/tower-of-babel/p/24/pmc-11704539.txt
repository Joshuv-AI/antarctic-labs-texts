Presenter: Dr Michael Kindo

Dr Jessica Brodt(Stanford, Calif). Professor Kindo, thank you for such important work. I really am grateful you had to review your paper in depth ahead of this. It seems like you did 2 massive pivots. Not only did you do a complete overhaul of your opioid management strategy, but you also implemented a really excellent enhanced recovery after cardiac surgery (ERAS) program in your patients in 1 fell swoop. And I think every program would be lucky to have done the work that you've done. I have a question about the use of ketamine rather than dexmedetomidine or another postoperative sedation. Can you comment on that? And when did you stop ketamine postoperatively?

Dr Michael Kindo(Strasbourg, France). Thank you for your question. We choose ketamine because we think that ketamine has better analgesic effect and, of course, has less risk of bradycardia or hypotension compared with dexmedetomidine. Usually, the ketamine is stopped on the first postoperative day, just after the removal of the chest tube. We have a specific protocol of chest tube removal on the morning of the first postoperative day. Seventy percent of our patients have this chest tube removed on the first postoperative day. On postoperative day one, following chest tube removal, the ketamine infusion is discontinued, and the patient is transferred out of the ICU in the department.

Dr Brodt. So, you kind of extubated early sleep overnight on a little bit of ketamine.

Dr Kindo. Exactly. All our patients must be extubated <6 hours after the surgery.

Dr Brodt. That's great. Another question was regarding the blocks that you're doing there. Some concern with the parasternal, whether you call it the transversus thoracis, superficial, or deep parasternal, and the proximity to the internal thoracic artery, of course. With really strict implant technique, you can always stay away from the deep plane, but you're doing coronary patients, and so how did you approach that, how many patients got blocks, and also how many patients needed the rescue blocks postoperatively? You have data on that?

Dr Kindo. Yes. Thank you for your question. Of course, when the anesthesiologist says to me that they are going to start to perform some regional pain block, I was really afraid about the potential risk of internal thoracic artery injury or infection. Nevertheless, we decided to use the transverse thoracic pain block because it offers the best analgesic effect coverage of the left anterior wall. So, we use, of course, ultrasound guidance for the performance of regional plane block. And with this ultrasound guidance, we have no internal thoracic artery injury in our experience with more than 5000 patients. Regarding the rescue block, it was not very often performed on the morning of the first postoperative day. As you can see in my presentation, 98% of the patients have no postoperative pain. So, the block was performed only in 4.7% of the patients in the ERAS group on the first postoperative day.

Dr Brodt. Thank you. I think we have time for 1 more question. The previous panel placed a big emphasis on a lot of really important points. One that stood out to me was compliance. Since you've completed your study, can you comment on your ongoing compliance and integration, I mean, the sustainability of the work that you've done, and what are your next steps?

Dr Kindo. Of course, now our ERAS program regarding our result and the result of the other team ERAS program, it is the standard of care. So, there's no difference between all our patients. Currently, all of our patients are enrolled in the ERAS program. Since 2022, our ERAS program has continued to evolve, specifically with the implementation of a preoperative Patient Blood Management (PBM) protocol, which includes screening for and correction of iron-deficiency anemia. We have also implemented protocol for early rehabilitation at home with telemonitoring. Additionally, we have also incorporated a postoperative PBM protocol that includes the administration of erythropoietin in the presence of postoperative anemia to optimize recovery and enhance postoperative rehabilitation.
