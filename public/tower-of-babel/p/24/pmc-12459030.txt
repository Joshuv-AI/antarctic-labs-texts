Phenotypic characteristics and carbapenemase genes in Klebsiella pneumoniae from patients at Upendra Devkota Memorial National Institute of Neurological and Allied Sciences, Kathmandu, Nepal
Authors: Babita Neupane, Madhu Dixit Devkota, Bharat Mani Pokhrel, Sandesh Rimal, Megha Raj Banjara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) has emerged as a critical nosocomial pathogen, associated with a substantial rise in morbidity and mortality. Phenotypic and molecular characterization of key carbapenemase genes in K. pneumoniae isolates is essential for understanding resistance mechanisms, guiding effective antibiotic stewardship and infection control strategies.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted from November 2023 to April 2024 at Upendra Devkota Memorial National Institute of Neurological and Allied Sciences, Kathmandu. Various types of clinical specimens were collected and processed for bacterial isolation and identification. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed, isolates were screened for β-lactamase production. Carbapenem-resistant isolates were subjected to mCIM and eCIM tests to detect carbapenemase. The presence of blaKPC, blaNDM-1, blaVIM, blaIMP and blaOXA-48-like genes was confirmed by polymerase chain reaction. Data were analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS).ResultsOut of total 812 samples, 21.2% (172/812) were culture positive. K. pneumoniae accounted for 28.5% (49/172) of isolates. More than half of the K. pneumoniae isolates were resistant to carbapenems, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, nitrofurantoin and cotrimoxazole. Multidrug resistance (MDR) was observed in 73.5% (36/49) of isolates. ESBL and AmpC production were detected in 73.5% (36/49) and 44.9% (22/49) of isolates, respectively. Among 29 CRKP isolates, 62% (18/29) and 34.5% (10/29) were MBL and KPC producers respectively. Carbapenemase production was confirmed in 58.6% (17/29), while metallo-carbapenemase activity was seen in 55.1% (16/29) of CRKP isolates. The prevalence of blaNDM-1, blaVIM and blaOXA-48-like were 58.6%, 10.3% and 6.9% in respective CRKP isolates. Co-existence of blaNDM-1and blaVIM was observed in 6.9% (2/29), while blaNDM-1 and blaOXA-48-like in 3.4% (1/29) of the isolates.ConclusionsThe study highlights the concerning prevalence of carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae in clinical settings, underscoring its role as a significant public health threat. Strengthening antimicrobial stewardship and implementing strict infection control measures are crucial to curb the spread of CRKP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11530-0.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the ten major global public health challenges that undermine the effectiveness of antibiotics, leading to increased morbidity, mortality and healthcare costs. In 2019, the bacterial AMR directly caused 1.27 million deaths worldwide and contributed to an additional 4.95 million deaths [1]. By 2050, it is estimated to cause 10 million deaths worldwide by AMR associated problems [2].

Klebsiella pneumoniae is a part of normal microbiota of human gastrointestinal tract, skin and nasopharynx. However, it can also behave as opportunistic pathogen and tends to cause severe community and nosocomial infections [3]. About 33% of hospital acquired Gram-negative infections are due to K. pneumoniae [4]. Further, it is one of the ‘ESKAPE pathogens’ which shows high resistance to various class of first line to last resort antibacterial drugs [5]. Consequently, K. pneumoniae has been prioritized as a critical priority pathogen for AMR due to its high levels of resistance, association with severe infections and potential for global dissemination [1].

K. pneumoniae produces extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) which confer resistance to broad range of beta-lactam antibiotics [6]. Carbapenems such as, meropenem, ertapenem and imipenem are used for the treatment of ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae. However, the over and misuse of these antibiotics has led to the emergence of carbapenem resistant strains which has disseminated globally with substantial increase in morbidity and mortality [7]. The most clinically significant carbapenemases found in K. pneumoniae are class A KPC type enzymes, class B MBLs like NDM, VIM, IMP and class D OXA-48-like enzymes [8]. In addition to production of carbapenemase enzymes, multiple mechanisms are responsible for conferring resistance to carbapenem which may include mutation in porin genes reducing drug entry, upregulation of efflux pumps and changes in penicillin binding proteins [9]. The problem is exacerbated when multiple mechanisms are concomitantly seen.

The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified eight pathogens as reportable pathogen for AMR surveillance [10]. However, individual countries have their own specific lists of reportable pathogens based on their epidemiological situations. In case of Nepal, 12 AMR priority pathogens have been identified, including K. pneumoniae which overlaps with the WHO list [11]. Carbapenems are last resort of β-lactam antibiotics and they are being used extensively for the treatment of infections nowadays [12]. Due to extensive use, there might be high pressure of these antibiotics on bacteria and fitness cost to these antibiotics may be high [13, 14]. Identifying carbapenemase producers early is now imperative, as their detection is pivotal in controlling the dissemination of bacteria that produce carbapenemases. The incidence of carbapenemase genes in clinical bacterial isolates is mostly underreported in Nepal since there aren’t enough investigations on the identification and characterization of carbapenemase employing both phenotypic and molecular methods. Therefore, we sought to address this crucial information need by determining the prevalence rate of K. pneumoniae and their antibiotic susceptibility patterns along with the frequency of carbapenemase encoding genes using both phenotypic and molecular methods. These findings could be beneficial in infection control, treatment and prevention, ultimately improving patient treatment outcomes.

Material and methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted at Upendra Devkota Memorial National Institute of Neurological and Allied Sciences located in Kathmandu, Nepal from November 2023 to April 2024. Clinical specimens were collected and processed in the laboratory of the same hospital. Molecular work for detection of carbapenemase genes was carried out at Central Department of Microbiology, Tribhuvan University.

Sample collection and transportation

Clinical specimens such as urine, blood, sputum, CSF, pus, tracheal aspirates and catheter tips were collected aseptically adhering to standard protocols [15]. Repeated samples and samples suspected of contamination were excluded from the study. Clean-catch mid-stream urine samples (10–20 ml) were collected in a sterile, dry, wide mouth and leak proof container. Blood was collected using a sterile needle and syringe or a vacutainer system with appropriate tubes and appropriate volume based on tests ordered. Deeply coughed sputum from lower respiratory tract was collected in a sterile, dry, wide-mouth and leak proof container minimizing the contamination. Pus was collected using a sterile syringe and needle, aspirating from the site with less contamination. CSF was collected by trained physicians by lumber puncture in a sterile collection tube. All the samples were labeled with patient information, date and time of collection. The samples were transported to the laboratory promptly and processed as soon as possible.

Bacterial culture and identification

Aseptically collected specimens were inoculated on MacConkey agar, Blood agar and Chocolate agar, while Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth was used for blood samples. All the inoculated media was incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Identification of the isolates was based on colony morphology, Gram staining and biochemical tests: catalase, oxidase, MR-VP, SIM, TSIA, Urease and Citrate [15].

Antibiotic susceptibility test

Antibiotic susceptibility test (AST) was performed by the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method as recommended by CLSI guidelines [16]. The antibiotics disks used were ampicillin/clavulanic acid (30 μg), piperacillin/tazobactam (100/10 μg), ceftriaxone (30 ug), ceftazidime (30 μg), cefotaxime (30 μg), cefoperazone/sulbactam (30 μg), ofloxacin (30 μg), levofloxacin (5 μg), ciprofloxacin (5 μg), imipenem (10 μg), meropenem (10 μg), gentamycin (30 ug), tobramycin (10 μg), azithromycin (15 μg), tigecycline (15 μg), nitrofurantoin (300 μg), cotrimoxazole (25 μg), and Chloramphenicol (30 ug) (HiMedia India Pvt. Ltd., Bengaluru, India). Nitrofurantoin was used only for urine and catheter tips. Isolates showing resistance to three or more different classes of antibiotics have been described as multi-drug resistant (MDR) [17].

Screening and confirmation of ESBL, MBL, KPC, carbapenemase and AmpC producers

All the bacterial isolates were screened for ESBL production using cefotaxime (30 μg) and ceftazidime (30 μg). Phenotypic confirmation was done using combined disc method with the difference of ≥ 5 mm zone diameter in presence of clavulanic acid than cephalosporin alone [16]. Those isolates resistant to carbapenem i.e. imipenem (10 μg) or meropenem (10 μg), in disk diffusion method, were suspected as MBL producer. Phenotypic confirmation of isolates were done by imipenem-EDTA disk diffusion method [18]. Isolates having zone diameter of imipenem-EDTA ≥ 7 mm than that of imipenem alone were confirmed as MBL producers. Isolates identified as carbapenem resistant was screened for possible phenotypic KPC production employing the meropenem-phenyl boronic acid (PBA) disk diffusion method as described by [19]. The isolates having the zone of inhibition ≥ 5 mm in meropenem-phenyl boronic acid than that of meropenem alone, was considered as KPC producer. In AST plate, bacterial isolates showing resistance to imipenem and meropenem disks was considered as carbapenemase producers. The phenotypic confirmation of carbapenemase producers was done using eCIM and mCIM tests [20]. Isolates showing resistant or intermediate to cefoxitin was screened as AmpC producers which was further confirmed phenotypically using an inhibition-based method [21]. The isolates were confirmed as AmpC producers when the zone of inhibition in alone disk was ≥ 5 mm.

DNA extraction and molecular characterization of carbapenemase genes

Bacterial DNA was extracted employing boiling method [22]. DNA quantification and quality assessment was performed using nano-drop (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA).

All the carbapenem-resistant isolates were tested for presence of blaKPC, blaNDM-1, blaVIM, blaIMP and blaOXA-48-like genes by conventional PCR technique using the different primers sets (Supplementary Table 1) [23]. The final volume (25 μl) of PCR mixture consisting of 5 μl master mix (5X), 1 μl of forward and 1 μl of reverse primer (10 pM), 2 μl of DNA template, and nuclease free water (16 μl) was prepared. The amplification reactions were carried out in the BioRad T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, California, USA) under the following conditions: initial denaturation 10 min at 94 °C, followed by 35 cycles of amplifications with denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing (blaKPC: at 55 °C for 40 s, blaNDM-1: at 52 °C for 40 s, blaVIM: at 58 °C for 40 s, blaIMP: at 57 °C for 40 s and blaOXA-48-like at 55 °C for 40 s), extension at 72 °C for 45 s and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The amplified PCR products were then analyzed using gel electrophoresis (80 V, 60 min) in 1.5% agarose gel with a 100 bp DNA ladder (Solis Biodyne, Tartu, Estonia) [23].

Quality control

All the experiments were performed by adhering standard sterile procedures and CLSI guidelines. Control strains of E. coli (ATCC 25922) and K. pneumoniae (ATCC 700603) were used for quality control of AST. During PCR, quality control was ensured using the K. pneumoniae isolates harboring the genes under question. Distilled deionized water was used as negative control. All these control samples were included in each set of PCR reactions.

Statistical analysis

The data were entered and analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25. Frequency and percentages were calculated. Chi-square test was employed for inferential analysis. P-value < 5% was considered statistically significant.

Co-occurrence of ESBL, MBL and KPC among K. pneumoniae isolates

Out of 49 total K. pneumoniae isolates, 36 (73.4%) were identified as ESBL producers. Of the total 29 carbapenem resistant isolates, 18 (62.1%) were MBL producer and 10 (34.5%) were KPC producer. Nine K. pneumoniae isolates produced both ESBL and MBL. Similarly, combination of ESBL, MBL and KPC production were also detected in nine isolates. Only one isolate was found to be ESBL and KPC producer.

Among 29 carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae, blaNDM-1, blaVIM and blaOXA-48-like genes were detected in 17 (58.6%), 3 (10.3%) and 2 (6.9%) isolates respectively. The blaIMP and blaKPC genes were not detected in any of the CRKP isolates. Discussion

The majority of K. pneumoniae isolates in our study displayed multidrug resistance. MDR in K. pneumoniae was found to be highly variable in Nepal, with resistance rate varying from 3 to 100% [24–29]. The production of multiple—β-lactamases is the primary factor responsible for the MDR bacterial profile [30]. The rising incidence of MDR may be related to misuse of antibiotics in Nepal, including the non-empirical use, taking antibiotics in insufficient doses and unrestricted access to antibiotics without a medical prescription [31]. Further, lack of antibiotic stewardship and poor hygiene practices can also contribute to the spread of resistant bacteria.

More than half of the K. pneumoniae isolates were found to be carbapenem resistant in our study. The high rates of resistance observed in this study align with global trends indicating an increasing prevalence of carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae [32]. An increase in resistance to carbapenems is accountable to various mechanisms such as, production of β-lactamases, increased efflux pumps or alteration of outer membrane porins and inhibiting the entry of these antibiotics [33]. All carbapenemase producing K. pneumoniae isolates were found to be MDR. This high prevalence of carbapenemase production among MDR isolates may be attributed to the co-selection of resistance genes through horizontal gene transfer and the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics [34].The early identification of carbapenemase producers can inform appropriate antibiotic therapy, potentially improving patient outcomes and curbing the spread of resistance.

Maximum isolates of K. pneumoniae were resistant to fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, macrolides, nitrofurantoin and cotrimoxazole. Mutations in the target genes gyrA and parC, and increased efflux are the primary cause of resistance to fluoroquinolones [35]. On the other hand, a significant proportion of K. pneumoniae isolates exhibited resistance to second and third-generation cephalosporins (cefotaxime, ceftriaxone and ceftazidime). Previous studies have also documented a rise in resistance to third-generation cephalosporins [24, 26, 29, 36]. The growing resistance to these antibiotics may be explained by the extensive use of cephalosporin antibiotics without understanding the severity of infection and the presence of ESBL enzymes encoded on plasmids within K. pneumoniae isolates [37]. Pathogen resistance is propagated through the horizontal transfer of resistant bacterial clones within hospital and nursing homes which is further aided by migration and international travel [38]. Therefore, nowadays multiple drug resistant isolates are very common in healthcare settings illustrating a critical situation.

The patients from semi-ICU (Intermediate Care Unit) and ICU had more K. pneumoniae infections. Similar to this study, several other studies had found significantly higher K. pneumoniae infections among ICU patients [39–41]. Invasive procedures, immune-compromised or critically ill patients, longer hospital stay and frequent use of antibiotics could be the reason for higher incidence of K. pneumoniae infection in ICU patients [39, 42]. Further, the inpatient department had a higher number of isolates than the outpatient department. This could be explained by the fact that K. pneumoniae is commonly associated with hospital-acquired infections [43].

This study showed the much higher prevalence (73.46%) of ESBL production along with co-occurrence of multiple β-lactamases (ESBL, MBL and KPC) in K. pneumoniae isolates. Although the co-production of ESBL with either MBL or KPC enzymes does not typically alter clinical management, the detection of such co-occurrence remains epidemiologically significant. The simultaneous presence of multiple β-lactamase genes within a single K. pneumoniae isolate may reflect horizontal gene transfer and selective antibiotic pressure. Monitoring these genes contributes to a better understanding of the evolving resistance patterns and supports infection control and surveillance strategies. It is frequently observed that ESBL-producing isolates exhibit co-resistance to multiple classes of antibiotics [44]. The co-production of multiple resistance mechanisms further limits treatment option and is associated with increased mortality and morbidity [45]. These bacteria are often make troublesome when associated with risk factors such as old age, extended hospital stay, previous exposure to antibiotics and use of invasive medical devices [46]. Thus, judicious use of antibiotics and the development of novel therapeutic strategies is necessary to combat these formidable pathogens.

Our study detected carbapenemases from both class B (NDM-1 and VIM) and class D (OXA-48-like). blaNDM-1 gene was the most frequently detected gene among CRKP isolates followed by blaVIM and blaOXA-48-like. While blaKPC and blaIMP genes were not identified in any of the CRKP isolates. Even though few isolates were KPC positive, neither of them harbored blaKPC gene. Previous studies from Iran have shown similar results regarding the prevalence of KPC and IMP carbapenemases among CRKP isolates [47, 48]. The sensitivity and specificity of mCIM for detecting blaNDM-1 was found to be 82.4% and 75% respectively. Similarly, the sensitivity and specificity for eCIM was 81.3% and 93.7% respectively. This suggest that, mCIM/eCIM test was not 100% reliable for confirmation of carbapenemase gene detection. In this study, 14 (77.8%) MBL producing isolates harbored blaNDM-1 gene while 3 (27.3%) non-MBL producing isolates were also observed to have blaNDM-1 gene. Similarly, blaVIM was detected in two MBL producers and in one non-MBL producer. There may be a possibility that these genes are expressed in isolates even though they are not phenotypically expressed. Additionally, in certain phenotypically resistant strains, it may not be expressed [49]. We also found co-existence of blaNDM-1 with blaVIM and blaOXA-48-like. Prevalence of multiple genes in K. pneumoniae isolates in this study may be due to prolonged use of antibiotics, particularly those with broader spectrum, as patients often referred from medical centers or hospitals where antimicrobial treatment had already been administered. The high frequency of these genes as well as the co-occurrence is concerning, as it limits the treatment options and contributes to the spread of carbapenem resistance [47].

Besides enzymatic resistance mechanism, carbapenem resistance can also be attributed to non-enzymatic mechanisms, such as alterations in porins and efflux pumps. In order to develop strategies to combat carbapenem resistance infections effectively, it is crucial to understand the complete spectrum of resistance mechanisms [50]. In addition, the potential existence of new carbapenemase gene in isolates cannot be ignored, suggesting the necessity of a full genome analysis of the isolates in the future.

This study has few limitations, such as the omission of carbapenemase gene sequencing. All carbapenemase genes were not detected in this study; only selected genes were identified. Our study included a limited number of carbapenem-resistant isolates. Increasing the sample size could enhance the positivity rate in identifying genes. Additionally, this study did not examine other mechanisms of carbapenem resistance, such as the expression of efflux pumps and the porin profiles of the isolates.

Conclusion

This study reported a high rate of MDR among K. pneumoniae isolates. ESBL was the most frequently identified β-lactamases followed by AmpC, MBL and KPC. Carbapenem resistance K. pneumoniae isolates are more than half. blaNDM-1 was the predominant gene followed by blaVIM and blaOXA-48-like gene. The blaIMP and blaKPC genes were not detected in any of the CRKP isolates. Routine surveillance of antibiotic resistance patterns and the periodic detection of carbapenemase production can guide appropriate antibiotic treatment and preventing the further spread of resistance.
