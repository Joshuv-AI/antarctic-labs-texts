Cost-effectiveness of eCPR: accounting for organ donation
Authors: Sasa Rajsic, Robert Breitkopf
Published: 2025

Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (eCPR) is a highly resource-intensive procedure, and its cost-effectiveness remains a subject of debate [1]. Hospital-based eCPR for refractory out-of-hospital cardiac arrest has been shown to have a low probability of cost-effectiveness compared to conventional cardiopulmonary resuscitation (cCPR) [2]. A recent cost-effectiveness analysis reported an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of €121,643 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained for eCPR. This places eCPR in the cost-effectiveness plane quadrant characterized by higher costs and greater effectiveness. At a willingness-to-pay threshold of €80,000 per QALY, the probability of eCPR being cost-effective was estimated at only 36% [2].

However, the potential benefit of organ donation from patients with unfavorable eCPR outcomes is an important factor often overlooked in cost-effectiveness analyses of eCPR. Such analyses should account for the additional value derived from otherwise unattainable organ donations facilitated by eCPR. This omission may lead to a significant underestimation of its overall cost-benefit profile. The contribution of organ transplantation to improving long-term quality of life and healthcare efficiency is well-established [3–5].

Numerous cost-effectiveness studies have consistently demonstrated that organ transplantation offers significant economic and clinical benefits compared to ongoing organ replacement therapies. For patients with end-stage renal disease, kidney transplantation is widely regarded as more cost-effective than long-term dialysis, providing improved survival and quality of life at a lower overall healthcare cost [6, 7]. Similarly, liver transplantation has been shown to be cost-effective relative to non-transplant therapies for end-stage liver disease and hepatocellular carcinoma, contributing to enhanced patient outcomes and reduced healthcare expenditures [8]. In the case of end-stage heart failure, heart transplantation prolongs life and is cost effective in eligible patients [9]. These findings underscore the value of organ donation not only in extending life expectancy but also in optimizing healthcare resource utilization.

Although organ donation should be regarded as a secondary outcome of eCPR, it remains a critical factor in assessing the broader societal and economic value of the intervention. We encourage authors of future cost-effectiveness studies on eCPR to incorporate data on potential organ procurement, including quality-of-life outcomes for both survivors and organ recipients. Such comprehensive analyses would support a more accurate and evidence-based assessment of the overall impact of eCPR.
