RHOBTB2 enhances cell proliferation of acute myeloid leukemia by modulating Hippo-YAP1 signaling and dependent of KLHL13
Authors: Yao Liu, Fanghui Zhou, Lianjie Wang, Yanming Xue, Wei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a heterogeneous hematologic malignancy associated with poor clinical outcomes. RHOBTB2, an atypical member of the Rho-GTPase family, contains a conserved GTPase domain at its N-terminus. While previous studies have implicated RHOBTB2 in AML progression, its underlying mechanisms remain inadequately defined. In this study, bioinformatics analyses revealed that RHOBTB2 is markedly upregulated in AML and correlates with unfavorable prognosis. To elucidate its functional role, we overexpressed or silenced RHOBTB2 in human AML cell lines KG-1 and MOLM-13. Functional assays, including CCK-8, Transwell, and Annexin V/PI staining, demonstrated that RHOBTB2 overexpression enhanced proliferation and migration, suppressed apoptosis, and shortened G0/G1 phase. Conversely, RHOBTB2 silencing exerted opposing effects. In addition, a direct interaction between RHOBTB2 and KLHL13 was identified through STRING database predictions and validated by co-immunoprecipitation. Western blot analysis confirmed that RHOBTB2 upregulates KLHL13 protein expression levels. Notably, KLHL13 downregulation induced by RHOBTB2 knockdown was reversed upon treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132, indicating that RHOBTB2 stabilizes KLHL13 by inhibiting its proteasomal degradation. Collectively, our findings highlight the RHOBTB2/KLHL13/Hippo pathway as a critical regulatory mechanism in AML malignancy and suggest RHOBTB2 as a potential therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20492-x.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) encompasses a varied array of primary hematological cancers that arise from myeloid progenitor cells. Nearly half of the patients fail to attain remission following their initial therapeutic intervention, subsequently advancing to a state of refractory AML1,2. In the past, individuals diagnosed with AML faced restricted therapeutic choices, typically relying on the combination of cytarabine and anthracycline (known as 7 + 3) for intensive chemotherapy, along with hypomethylating agents3,4. Thus, further efforts are needed to uncover novel mechanisms underlying the progression of AML, with the ultimate goal of enhancing the survival outcomes for patients.

The RHOBTB proteins, a distinct subgroup of unconventional Rho GTPases, are present in mammals as RHOBTB1, RHOBTB2, and RHOBTB35,6. Distinguished by a conserved Rho-GTPase domain located at the N-terminus, which is succeeded by two BTB domains that facilitate protein-protein interactions7,8. Rho GTPases, recognized as pivotal molecular regulators, are now elucidated to play essential roles in diverse cellular functions such as cellular movement, orientation, attachment, progression through the cell cycle, and regulation of the cytoskeleton9,10. Numerous investigations have underscored the significance of RHOBTB2 in the progression of AML11,12, however, its exact regulation mechanism remains unclear.

The Hippo signaling pathway, together with its downstream effectors—namely the transcriptional co-activators Yes-associated protein (YAP) and the transcriptional co-activator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ)—is fundamental to the processes of tissue renewal, regeneration, and cellular proliferation13,14. YAP/TAZ exhibit hyperactivation in human cancers, with sustained activation of these proteins leading to cancer initiation in mouse models15,16. The activation of YAP1 accelerates the development of AML17,18. Aberrant YAP1 expression is strongly linked to cancer progression and resistance to chemotherapy in AML19,20.

In this study, we observed that knockdown of RHOBTB2 in two human AML cell lines, KG-1 and MOLM13, suppressed cell proliferation, whereas overexpression of RHOBTB2 promoting cell proliferation. Utilizing the STRING database, we predicted proteins that interact with RHOBTB2 and identified KLHL13. We further demonstrated that RHOBTB2 regulates the YAP signaling pathway and cell proliferation mediated by KLHL13.

Materials and methods

Antibodies and reagents

The antibodies used were anti-RHOBTB2 (A18432, ABclonal), anti-YAP (13584-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-TAZ (23306-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-phospho-YAP (AP0489, ABclonal), anti-KLHL13 (15612-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-Bcl-2 (48496, Signalway Antibody), anti-Bax (WL01637, Wanleibio), anti-β-actin (AC026, ABclonal), anti-P53 (A19585, ABclonal), anti-P21 (A19094, ABcloanl), Cyclin D1 (A19038, ABclonal), anti-CDK4 (A11136, ABclonal).

Cell culture, treatment and stable transfections

Human AML cell lines KG-1 and MOLM13 were obtained from Hycyte Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China) and cultured in IMEM Medium (PM150510, Procell) supplemented with 20% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, 10099141, Gibco) and 100 units/mL of a penicillin/streptomycin mixture (15140122, Gibco). The cells were maintained in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2. SiRNA oligonucleotides targeting the human RHOBTB2 gene (si-RHOBTB2: 5’-UGACAUGGAUUAUGAAAGGUU-3’) and a negative control (si-NC: 5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’) were synthesized by Sangon (Shanghai, China). An overexpression construct of pcDNA3.1-RHOBTB2 was generated by inserting the coding sequence (CDS) of RHOBTB2 into the pcDNA3.1 vector through the process of ligation by General Biol (Anhui, China). The vector alone (pcDNA3.1) served as a negative control. Transfection was executed following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer of Lipofectamine™ 2000 (Invitrogen, California, USA). The cells were collected 48 h after the transfection procedure.

GEPIA2

The GEPIA database (accessible at http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/) serves as an online resource designed for the analysis of gene expression profiles, offering interactive visualizations of RNA sequencing data derived from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) as well as the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) initiatives21,22. The “Expression Analysis” module in this tool utilizes data from TCGA-LAML tumor samples (n = 173) and adjacent normal tissues (n = 70) to analyze the expression profile of RHOBTB2, using Student’s t-test for the analysis of gene expression. The “Survival” module displays the relationship between the gene and overall survival rates in TCGA-LAML, with survival analysis results shown through Kaplan–Meier curves indicating P-values obtained from the log-rank test. The criterion for statistical significance is established at a P-value of less than 0.05.

STRING

The STRING database (accessible at https://string-db.org/) serves as a thorough repository for protein-protein interaction networks, offering a wealth of information regarding protein interactions along with detailed functional annotation data23,24. we can use the STRING database to search for interactions between proteins and explore protein functions and pathway information. By using the “protein name” module, we predict proteins associated with RHOBTB2.

Immunofluorescence staining

KG-1 and MOLM13 cells (5 × 105) were cytospun onto glass slides and fixed in cold methanol at − 20 °C for 20 min. The slides were washed twice with PBS, blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, and subsequently incubated overnight with anti-RHOBT2 and anti-KLHL13 antibodies. The following day, Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:500, Invitrogen, USA) was applied for 1 h, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Leica, Germany).

Western blot

Cells were subjected to lysis using ice-cold RIPA buffer (R0010, Solarbio) that included protease inhibitor (1:1000; Sigma-Aldrich). After centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min, the concentrations of proteins were quantified utilizing a BCA assay provided by Bio-Rad. Equal quantities of protein samples were subjected to separation via SDS-PAGE, followed by transfer onto PVDF membranes. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated for 1 h with 5% BSA for blocking purposes, and then exposed to primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following this, the membranes underwent an incubation period at room temperature for one hour with secondary antibodies, specifically HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG. The detection of the blots was carried out utilizing Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad, CA, USA).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

For Co-IP, cell lysis was carried out using Protein lysis buffer (N8032, Solarbio) enriched with a comprehensive proteinase inhibitor. Antibodies directed against RHOBTB2 (sc-398774, Santa) were introduced to the lysates in conjunction with protein A/G beads (sc-2003, Santa) and permitted to incubate overnight at 4 °C. Following centrifugation, the precipitate was collected and subjected to three washes using cold lysis buffer. The sample was reconstituted in 2X loading buffer, subjected to heating at 95 °C for a duration of 5 min, and subsequently centrifuged once more. The supernatant obtained was used for western blot.

qRT-PCR assay

Total RNA in each cell group were extracted using TRIzol. Next, reverse RNA into cDNA using the HiScript III RT SuperMix Kit (R323, Vazyme) following the instructions provided. Finally, amplify the cDNA using SYBR Green dye in qRT-PCR. The sequences and details of the qRT-PCR primers are listed in Table 1.

Cell activity determination by cell counting kit-8

Cell viability was assessed using CCK8 assay. KG-1 and MOLM13 cell lines were cultivated in a 96-well plate until they achieved approximately 70% confluence. Following this, the cells were subjected to transfection with either si-RHOBTB2 or a plasmid designed for the overexpression of RHOBTB2. After 48 h post-transfection, cell viability was evaluated.

Cell cycle determination by flow cytometry

The assessment of the cell cycle was conducted using a detection kit (C1052, Beyotime). The prepared cells were collected and resuspended to a concentration of 2 × 105 cells/mL, then centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 minutes. After washing with cold PBS, the cells were fixed with pre-chilled 70% ethanol at 4 °C for 30 min. Following another centrifugation at 1000 g for 5 minutes and PBS wash, the cells were incubated with PI staining solution at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. The samples were then analyzed using flow cytometer (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Cell migration determination by transwell assay

Cell migration was evaluated through 8 μm transwell insert (3422, Corning). KG-1 and MOLM13 cells were seeded in the uper chambers of transwell plate at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. Medium with 20% FBS was added to the lower chambers. Then, the cells were cultured in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 48 h. The cells in lower chambers were collected and counted. More specifically, cells were harvested and resuspended in an appropriate volume of medium. A 10 µL aliquot of the cell suspension was mixed with an equal volume of trypan blue solution and loaded onto a hemocytometer. The number of viable cells was counted under a light microscope, and the cell concentration was calculated based on the average number of cells per quadrant.

Cell apoptosis analysis

Apoptosis was assessed using flow cytometry. Cells were harvested and incubated for apoptosis detection (40302ES, YEASEN) with flow cytometry. After performing two washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the cells were resuspended in 100 µL of a 1 X binding buffer supplemented with 2.5 µL of Annexin-V–FITC and 2.5 µL of 7-AAD staining solution. This mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for a duration of 15 min. Following incubation, an additional 400 µL of binding buffer was introduced, and the cells were subsequently analyzed for apoptotic activity utilizing a flow cytometer (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). The assessment of apoptosis was conducted by examining the populations in Q2 (early apoptosis) and Q3 (late apoptosis) to ascertain the overall rate of apoptosis.

Statistical analysis

The statistical evaluation was performed utilizing Prism 9.0 software. Data that were normally distributed are expressed as Mean ± SD, and the unpaired Student’s t-test was utilized to assess the significance between the two groups. To evaluate differences across several groups, either one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA was employed. A p-value less than 0.05 was deemed to signify statistical significance.

Results

RHOBTB2 expression is prominently upregulated in AML patients

Based on the analysis of the GEPIA2 database, an analysis encompassing various cancer types regarding the expression of RHOBTB2 was performed, revealing that RHOBTB2 is upregulated in various cancers, including Lymphoid Neoplasm Diffuse Large B-cell Lymphoma (DLBC), Esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), AML, Pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), Skin Cutaneous Melanoma (SKCM), and Thymoma (THYM) (Sup. Figure 1). In order to assess the significance of RHOBTB2 in the progression of AML, we initially analyzed the expression levels of RHOBTB2 in a cohort comprising 173 AML patients alongside 70 control samples, utilizing the GEPIA2 database. Subsequently, we explored the relationship between RHOBTB2 expression and overall survival rates of AML patients as recorded in the TCGA datasets. The findings indicated a notable increase in RHOBTB2 expression among patients with AML (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, those AML patients exhibiting elevated levels of RHOBTB2 experienced a reduced overall survival rate (Fig. 1B). The investigation in vitro was also carried out to validate the relationship between RHOBTB2 and tumor progression. RHOBTB2 overexpression and knockdown were illustrated successfully in KG-1 and MOLM13 cells, respectively (Fig. 1C,D).

To move on, we assessed the effect of RHOBTB2 on cell viability in KG-1 and MOLM13 cells. The results revealed RHOBTB2 knockdown inhibited cell viability, while overexpression of RHOBTB2 improved cell viability (Fig. 2A,B). Moreover, we further analyzed the cell cycle at 48 h using flow cytometry. The results showed that RHOBTB2 knockdown led to an increase in the proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase and a decrease in the S phase population. Conversely, RHOBTB2 overexpression produced the opposite effect (Fig. 2C,D). In addition, Western blot analysis of cell cycle-related proteins revealed that RHOBTB2 knockdown reduced the expression of Cyclin D1 and CDK4, while upregulating P53 and P21. In contrast, overexpression of RHOBTB2 increased Cyclin D1 and CDK4 levels and suppressed P53 and P21 expression (Fig. 2E,F). These findings suggest that RHOBTB2 knockdown inhibits AML cell proliferation, whereas RHOBTB2 overexpression promotes it. Migration assays demonstrated that RHOBTB2 knockdown suppressed the migration capacity of KG-1 and MOLM13 cells, whereas RHOBTB2 overexpression enhanced cell migration (Fig. 2G-H). Finally, we examined the effect of RHOBTB2 overexpression or knockdown on apoptosis in KG-1 cells. The results confirmed that knockdown of RHOBTB2 promoted apoptosis, whereas overexpression of RHOBTB2 inhibited apoptosis. The results obtained align with the conclusions reached from the assays measuring cell proliferation (Fig. 2I,J).

RHOBTB2 interacts with KLHL13 and inhibits proteasomal degradation of KLHL13

To explore the underlying mechanisms through RHOBTB2 regulates cell proliferation, RHOBTB2-associated proteins were predicted based on the STRING database (Fig. 3A). Through Co-IP, we found that RHOBTB2 interacts with KLHL13 in both KG-1 and MOLM13 cells (Fig. 3B). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated that RHOBT2 and KLHL13 were co-localized in KG-1 and MOLM13 cells (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, KLHL13 was expressed lowly in cells with RHOBTB2 knockdown, while it was elevated in cells with RHOBTB2 overexpression (Fig. 3D,E). Subsequently, we assessed the possible involvement of RHOBTB2 in the synthesis and degradation of KLHL13. MG-132, a proteasome inhibitor, was able to shield KLHL13 from degradation induced by RHOBTB2 knockdown (Fig. 4A). Conversely, protein synthesis inhibitor Chloramphenicol (CHX) did not influence the RNA or protein expression levels of KLHL13 following overexpression RHOBTB2 (Fig. 4B,C). These results demonstrated that RHOBTB2 knockdown accelerated KLHL13 degradation.

RHOBTB2 regulates YAP activation by Inhibition of Hippo signaling

The Hippo signaling pathway has been reportedly associated with the advancement of tumors and has been shown to be associated with RHOBTB225,26. To clarify the regulatory mechanisms responsible for the increased cell proliferation induced by the overexpression of RHOBTB2, we selected Hippo pathway for further validation. Immunoblotting analysis revealed that YAP and downstream gene TAZ were downregulated, while YAP phosphorylation level was upregulated in RHOBTB2 knockdown KG-1 and MOLM13 cells (Fig. 5A). In opposition, the elevated expression levels of RHOBTB2 in KG-1 and MOLM13 cells resulted in an elevation of YAP and TAZ expression, accompanied by a reduction in the phosphorylation status of YAP (Fig. 5B). These results indicated that RHOBTB2 may enhance Hippo signaling and accordingly induce aggressive cell proliferation.

RHOBTB2 recruits KLHL13 to inhibit Hippo pathway and cell proliferation

Given our above observation that RHOBTB2 regulated KLHL13 expression and YAP activation, we want to test whether RHOBTB2 modulated YAP activity in a KLHL13-dependent manner. To this end, we further explored the relationship between KLHL13 and YAP pathway, and the results displayed KLHL13 overexpression increased the ubiquitination levels of PYAP, suggesting that it may reduce PYAP expression by enhancing its ubiquitin-mediated degradation pathway in both KG-1 and MOLM13 cells (Fig. 6A). Next, we overexpressed KLHL13 in RHOBTB2-knockdown KG-1 and MOLM13 cells. Immunoblotting analysis discovered that overexpression of KLHL13 in RHOBTB2-knockdown cells restored the suppressed activity of YAP and its downstream gene TAZ, while also decreasing the phosphorylation levels of YAP (Fig. 6B). To determine whether KLHL13 overexpression in RHOBTB2-knockdown cells was responsible for the enhanced cell proliferation observed above, we identified that overexpression of KLHL13 in RHOBTB2-knockdown KG-1 and MOLM13 cells could rescue the cell viability (Fig. 6C). The cell cycle analysis showed that overexpression of KLHL13 rescued the reduction in S-phase cell population caused by RHOBTB2 knockdown (Fig. 6D). Correspondingly, it also alleviated the increase in P53 and P21 levels and the decrease in Cyclin D1 and CDK4 expression (Fig. 6E). Transwell assay revealed that KLHL13 overexpression mitigated the suppressive effect of RHOBTB2 silencing on KG-1 and MOLM13 cell migration (Fig. 6F). Furthermore, knockdown of RHOBTB2 resulted in a significant upregulation of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax, however, overexpression of KLHL13 in RHOBTB2 knockdown KG-1 and MOLM13 (Fig. 6B) cells led to a partial restoration of Bax levels. Conversely, the level of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 exhibited an opposite trend (Fig. 6B). Similarly, Annexin V/PI staining experiments demonstrated that the overexpression of KLHL13 partially reversed the increased apoptosis induced by RHOBTB2 knockdown in KG-1 cells (Fig. 6G). In summary, these findings indicate that RHOBTB2 facilitates AML cell proliferation and suppresses apoptosis by modulating the Hippo pathway via regulation of KLHL13.

Discussion

RHOBTB are a typical member of the Rho GTPase family, comprising three subtypes: RHOBTB1, RHOBTB2, and RHOBTB327. Among them, RHOBTB2 has been widely recognized as a tumor suppressor in various cancers. Studies have shown that RHOBTB2 expression is downregulated in various malignancies, and its upregulation inhibits tumor initiation and progression28. For instance, one study reported that E2F1 enhances RHOBTB2 transcription, leading to elevated RHOBTB2 expression and increased apoptosis in H1299 non-small cell lung cancer cells29. Furthermore, another study demonstrated that RHOBTB2 overexpression in breast cancer cells suppresses cellular proliferation, hinders colony formation, and induces apoptosis30. Additionally, RHOBTB2 has been shown to induce apoptosis in breast cancer cells by facilitating the proteasomal degradation of MSI225. Contrary to its role in solid tumors, recent studies have identified a distinct function for RHOBTB2 in AML. Two independent reports demonstrated that RHOBTB2 expression is significantly upregulated in AML, suggesting a potential oncogenic role11,12. In the present study, we first analyzed RHOBTB2 expression in the TCGA-LAML dataset through bioinformatics. Consistent with prior findings, RHOBTB2 levels were markedly elevated in AML samples. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis further indicated that high RHOBTB2 expression correlates with poor overall survival in AML patients. To validate these observations, we assessed RHOBTB2 expression in clinical peripheral blood samples and AML cell lines, confirming its upregulation in AML. Functional assays revealed that RHOBTB2 knockdown significantly impaired cell proliferation and promoted apoptosis. Furthermore, RHOBTB2 silencing also induced G1 phase cell cycle arrest in AML cells. Collectively, these results demonstrate that, in contrast to its tumor-suppressive function in solid cancers, RHOBTB2 acts as a pro-oncogenic factor in AML.

RHOBTB2 is classified as an atypical Rho-GTPase, distinguished by a conserved Rho-GTPase domain located at its N-terminal region. This domain is followed by two BTB domains, which are likely involved in mediating protein interactions31,32. Hence, utilizing the STRING database, we identified potential proteins associated with RHOBTB2. Among these, KLHL13, which belongs to the Kelch-like (KLHL) protein family, has been shown in studies to interact with Cul3-scaffold E3 ligase, functioning in lung cancer therapy33,34 and mitotic progression35,36.

A growing body of evidence has highlighted that the dysregulation of the Hippo-YAP1 signaling pathway contributes to the development of tumors37,38. CircSH3BGRL3 increases upregulates YAP1 expression, leading to activation of the Hippo signaling pathway implicated in AML39,40. Furthermore, the Hippo/YAP1 axis is linked to therapy resistance in AML41. This study validates the upregulation of RHOBTB2 associated with cell proliferation and apoptosis in vitro, suggesting a potential correlation with YAP activity. These findings introduce novel concepts for the personalized therapy of AML patients.

Nevertheless, this study has some limitations. Firstly, although we have demonstrated the correlation between RHOBTB2 and cell proliferation in in vitro experiments, its clinical relevance remains to be established. Future prospective studies involving clinical samples are needed to evaluate the association between RHOBTB2 expression and patient outcomes, which will help clarify its potential as a diagnostic or prognostic biomarker in AML. Secondly, further experiments are required to explore the mechanisms associated with relevant molecules in AML. In conclusion, these findings require additional validation through comprehensive experiments investigating the regulation of RHOBTB2 in AML, as well as clinical studies.

In this study, we confirmed that RHOBTB2 promotes cell proliferation and YAP pathway activation in vitro. Second, the interaction between RHOBTB2 and KLHL13 was identified, and it was demonstrated that RHOBTB2 inhibited the proteasomal degradation of KLHL13. Finally, it was confirmed that RHOBTB2 activated the YAP pathway by recruiting KLHL13, thereby promoting cell proliferation.
