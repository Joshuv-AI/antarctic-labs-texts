Effects of reduced blood pressure dipping on the progression of chronic kidney disease in children
Authors: Li Huarong, Chen Chaoying, Tu Juan, Lin Tiantian, Wang Nannan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension is a common complication of chronic kidney disease (CKD) in children. It is related to the progression of CKD. However, current guidelines for hypertension management in CKD patients mainly focus on managing patients with above-target blood pressure (BP). The effects of reduced nocturnal BP dipping on CKD progression in children are unclear.MethodsThis single-center, retrospective cohort study analyzed the clinical data of children with stage 2–5 non-dialysis CKD undergoing ambulatory BP monitoring. Clinical characteristics and laboratory parameters of children with and without hypertension and/or reduced nocturnal BP dipping were analyzed.ResultsNinety-four children were included (median age: 10 years; interquartile range [IQR]: 6–12 years; males: 65 [69.1%]). The median estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was 41 ml/min/1.73 m2, and the median 24-h proteinuria level was 637 mg/24 h. There were 41 (43.6%) cases of ambulatory BP at target and 53 (56.4%) cases of ambulatory hypertension. Nocturnal dipping without ambulatory hypertension was found in 10 cases (10.6%), nocturnal non-dipping without ambulatory hypertension in 31 cases (33.0%), nocturnal dipping with ambulatory hypertension in 5 cases (5.3%), and nocturnal non-dipping with ambulatory hypertension in 48 cases (51.1%). The nocturnal non-dipping with ambulatory hypertension group had the lowest hemoglobin level (F = 2.798, P = 0.045), eGFR (F = 3.228, P = 0.026), and 25 (OH) D3 level (F = 3.428, P = 0.023). During a 15-month median follow-up (IQR: 7–31 months), 27 patients (28.7%) showed a decrease in eGFR of > 3 ml/min/1.73m2/year. Further, 21 patients (22.3%) progressed to end-stage kidney disease requiring kidney replacement therapy. The nocturnal non-dipping with ambulatory hypertension group had the highest renal progression risk (hazard ratio [HR] = 3.634; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.866–15.258), while the nocturnal dipping with ambulatory hypertension group (HR = 1.945; 95% CI, 0.273–13.865) had similar risks to the nocturnal non-dipping without ambulatory hypertension group (HR = 1.584; 95% CI, 0.350–7.171). Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that persistent proteinuria and reduced nocturnal BP dipping were associated with renal outcomes.ConclusionRegardless of whether ambulatory BP was normal, reduced BP dipping was correlated with the risk of renal progression in children with CKD.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, Children, Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, Blood pressure dipping, Progression

Background

Hypertension is a common complication of chronic kidney disease (CKD) in children, with an incidence ranging from 50 to 80%, depending on the disease stage [1], which is far higher than the 4% prevalence of hypertension in all children [2]. It has been well-established that hypertension can damage the kidney through the activation of the endothelial system, as well as the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system. Hypertension has also been classified as an independent risk factor for CKD [3, 4] and can predict adult hypertension, which is related to cardiovascular events, such as left ventricular hypertrophy and atherosclerosis [5]. As such, controlling hypertension can help to delay the progression of CKD, and minimize increases in the incidence and mortality rates of related cardiovascular events.

However, studies measuring office blood pressure (BP) have shown that hypertension is grossly underestimated and undertreated [6, 7], with more than 50% of children with CKD showing abnormal BP during 24-h ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM) [8]. Further, 50% of children with CKD have nocturnal hypertension, which is difficult to identify using office BP measurements [7]. The European Society of Hypertension and the American Heart Association (AHA) both recommended performing routine ABPM for children with CKD as early as 2016 and 2017, respectively, to assess parameters that could not be measured in office BP. These include the BP load, nocturnal BP, diurnal BP pattern, and BP variability. As such, ABPM is considered to be the gold standard for diagnosing “true” hypertension status, and the only tool for assessing abnormal diurnal BP patterns.

Nevertheless, studies using ABPM to assess the prognosis of patients with CKD have predominantly focused on independent situations of nocturnal non-dipping and ambulatory hypertension. Current guidelines for hypertension management, including for CKD patients, have predominantly focused on controlling office hypertension and ambulatory hypertension, and management of patients with non-dipping in nocturnal BP is difficult. The effect of nocturnal non-dipping on renal prognosis, particularly in children with CKD, has rarely been investigated. In 2022, AHA released an updated Scientific Statement on ABPM in children and adolescents [9], which removed the concept of dipping and indicated that isolated blunted nocturnal BP dipping in the absence of elevated mean BP is not classified as a form of masked hypertension. However, this phenomenon appears to be more common in patients with CKD. Therefore, this statement is still recommended to identify and monitor nocturnal hypertension in children with CKD. In this study, children with stage 2–5 CKD admitted to our hospital between January 2018 and December 2023 were systematically evaluated, stratified, and compared, according to the characteristics of ambulatory BP. The influencing factors of different ambulatory BP parameters on the progression of CKD were further evaluated.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective single-center observational study enrolled 94 children with stage 2–5 non-dialysis CKD undergoing ABPM who were admitted to the Department of Nephrology, Capital Center For Children’s Health, Capital Medical University (Beijing, China) between January 2018 and December 2023. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Capital Center For Children’s Health, Capital Medical University, [SHERLL2023008]. The parents of all patients provided informed consent prior to inclusion in the study, which was conducted in strict accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 5–17 years and (2) children with stage 2–5 non-dialysis CKD, diagnosis of CKD according to the KDIGO guidelines [10].

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) underwent kidney replacement therapy (KRT) at the start of the study and (2) incomplete clinical data.

Observation and evaluation indicators

The following baseline data were collected: General information, including age, sex, height, weight, body mass index, CKD stage, CKD etiology; and laboratory data, including serum creatinine, serum urea nitrogen, eGFR according to the Schwartz formula [11], urinary protein/creatinine, 24-h proteinuria, hemoglobin, blood glucose, albumin, triglyceride, cholesterol, low density lipoprotein cholesterol, serum uric acid, calcium, phosphorus, total parathyroid hormone, 25 (OH) D3 level, and echocardiography.

BP measurement

BP was measured according to the following requirements: the width of the cuff should be at least 40% of the circumference of the middle upper arm, while the length should be at least 80% of the circumference of the middle upper arm. Office BP and ABPM were measured in a consulting room on the same day.

Office BP was measured using auscultation as follows: after the patients rested for 5 min, the measurement was repeated at intervals of 1–2 min, and the average value of the two measurements was recorded as the office BP.

ABPM was conducted using a Spacelabs device during hospitalization. The 24-h period was divided into waking and sleeping periods; the waking period was measured every 30 min from 07:00 to 21:00, and the sleeping period was measured every 60 min from 21:00 to 07:00. Strenuous activities were avoided during the measurements, and daily activities and waking and sleeping times were recorded. The ABPM data should contain at least 70% valid [5]. During the ABPM monitoring, we reminded the children to avoid vigorous exercise, punctures, and examinations and to place the monitor under a pillow to protect it and minimize vibrations at night. At least one successful measurement was obtained every hour. BP characteristics collected by ABPM included the 24-h systolic pressure load, 24-h diastolic pressure load, 24-h mean systolic pressure, 24-h mean diastolic pressure, daytime mean systolic pressure, daytime mean diastolic pressure, nighttime mean systolic pressure, nighttime mean diastolic pressure, and nighttime BP dipping rate.

Patient grouping

According to the nocturnal dipping status and ambulatory BP, the patients were divided into four groups: nocturnal dipping without ambulatory hypertension, nocturnal non-dipping without ambulatory hypertension, nocturnal dipping with ambulatory hypertension, and nocturnal non-dipping with ambulatory hypertension.

Follow-up visit

Patients were followed up for at least 6 months, until they started KRT or until death.

The following outcome measures were assessed: follow-up time, CKD stage, eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m2), urinary protein/creatinine, annual eGFR decline slope (mL/min/1.73 m2/year) calculated based on eGFR change and observation time.

The endpoint events were as follows: renal combined outcomes included progression to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) requiring KRT, or rapid decline of eGFR (≥ 3 mL/min/1.73 m2/year).

Definitions

The following definitions were used for separate parameters.

Office hypertension: BP ≥ 95th percentile of the same sex, age and height (age < 13 years) or 130/80 mmHg (age ≥ 13 years) [9].

ABPM hypertension: For children aged 5–12 years, 24-h mean systolic or diastolic BP ≥ 95th percentile for gender and height; For children aged ≥ 13 years, 24-h, daytime, or nighttime mean BP ≥ 125/75 mmHg, 130/80 mmHg, or 110/65 mmHg, respectively [9].

Ambulatory hypertension: meeting both office hypertension and ABPM hypertension criteria [9].

Decreased nocturnal BP: dipping in mean nocturnal systolic or diastolic BP < 10% on ABPM [12].

Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH): using echocardiography to assess the diagnostic criteria for LVH [5], with a left ventricular mass index (LVMI) > 51 g/m2.7. Echocardiography measured left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVDd), ventricular septal thickness (IVST), and left ventricular posterior wall thickness (LVPWT). According to the Devereux formula, left ventricular mass (LVM) = 1.04 × 0.8X[(LVDd + IVST + LVPWT)3-LVDd3] + 0.6), LVMI = LVM/height2.7.

Statistical methods

SPSS 26.0 statistical software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for data processing. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was applied to evaluate the normality of data distribution. Normally distributed variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s), and differences between groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Non-normally distributed measurement data are expressed as the median (interquartile range [IQR]), and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between groups. Count data are presented as the use cases (%). The χ2 test or Fisher’s exact probability method were used for inter-group comparisons. The threshold for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. A multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was tested and applied to estimate the risk of renal outcomes (hazard ratio [HR] and 95% confidence interval [CI]) in the four groups based on nocturnal dipping status and ambulatory hypertension (nocturnal dipping and non-ambulatory hypertension were used as reference groups).

Results

General characteristics of the study participants

A total of 94 children were enrolled, with a median age of 10 years (IQR 6–12 years), including 65 males (69.1%). The most common causes of CKD were congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT) in 52 patients (55.3%), glomerular disease in 21 patients (22.3%), tubular disease in 10 patients (10.6%), thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in 4 patients (4.3%), cystic nephropathy in 3 patients (3.2%), and other in 4 patients (4.3%). Overall, 29 (30.8%), 31 (33.0%), 9 (9.6%), and 25 (26.6%) patients had stage 2, 3, 4, and 5 of CKD, respectively.

Baseline laboratory measurements were as follows: median eGFR 41 ml/min/1.73 m2 (IQR 16–65 ml/min/1.73 m2) and a median 24-h proteinuria of 637 mg/24 h (IQR 168–1714 mg/24 h).

The baseline BP characteristics were as follows: there were 47 patients (50%) with office hypertension, with incidence rates ranging from 44.8% of patients in CKD the stage 2 of CKD to 72.0% of patients in the stage 5 of CKD. Further, there were 56 patients with ABPM hypertension (60.0%), 53 with ambulatory hypertension (56.4%), and 79 (84.0%) with reduced nocturnal BP dipping.

In the entire cohort, the baseline median office systolic BP was 117 mmHg, median office diastolic BP was 73 mmHg, median 24-h systolic load was 46%, median 24-h diastolic load was 29%, median 24-h systolic BP was 119 mmHg, and median 24-h diastolic BP was 70 mmHg. Overall, the median nocturnal systolic and diastolic BP decreased by 6.2% and 12.0%, respectively.

Patients were stratified according to ambulatory BP and nocturnal dipping status, as follows: 10 patients (10.6%) had nocturnal dipping without ambulatory hypertension, 31 patients (33.0%) had nocturnal non-dipping without ambulatory hypertension, 5 patients (5.3%) had nocturnal dipping with ambulatory hypertension, and 48 patients (51.1%) had nocturnal non-dipping with ambulatory hypertension.

Comparison of ambulatory BP and other baseline clinical features in children with CKD revealed no differences in the distribution of the etiology and stage of CKD among the four groups. The lowest hemoglobin (F = 2.798, P = 0.045), eGFR (F = 3.228, P = 0.026), and 25 (OH) D3 (F = 3.428, P = 0.023) levels were found in the nocturnal non-dipping with ambulatory hypertension group, all of which showed statistical differences. There were no significant differences in albumin, serum uric acid, cholesterol, triglyceride, low density lipoprotein cholesterol, calcium, phosphorus and parathyroid hormone across the four groups. Comparison of ambulatory BP with LVH revealed no statistically significant difference in the distribution of LVH among the four groups. The LVMI in the nocturnal non-dipping with ambulatory hypertension group was significantly higher than that in the other three groups, but there was no statistical difference (Tables 1 and 2). Regarding antihypertensive drugs, 42 patients (44.7%) received calcium channel blockers, and 19 (20.2%) received angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers. The use of antihypertensive drugs in the four groups was not significantly different.

Renal outcomes

At a median follow-up of 15 months (IQR 7–31 months), 27 patients (28.7%) had an eGFR decline > 3 ml/min.1.73 m2/year, 21 (22.3%) progressed to ESKD requiring KRT, and 2 died. The children who died had nocturnal non-dipping with ambulatory hypertension accompanied by LVH, and refractory hypertension, treated with three and four different antihypertensive drugs, respectively. The incidences of renal endpoint events in the four groups are presented in Fig. 1. The highest risk of this outcome was observed in the nocturnal non-dipping with ambulatory hypertension group (HR = 3.634; 95% CI, 0.866–15.258), while the nocturnal dipping with ambulatory hypertension group (HR = 1.945; 95% CI, 0.273–13.865) had similar risks to the nocturnal non-dipping without ambulatory hypertension group (HR = 1.584; 95% CI, 0.350–7.171; Table 3). In the univariate regression analysis, significant difference was found between nocturnal non-dipping status, hemoglobin, serum creatinine, eGFR, persistent proteinuria, and renal outcomes. However, multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that only persistent proteinuria and nocturnal non-dipping were associated with renal outcomes.

Discussion

The results of this retrospective study indicate that nocturnal non-dipping with ambulatory hypertension is the most common pattern of hypertension in children with CKD and is associated with the highest risk of renal outcomes. Interestingly, even when ambulatory hypertension is not present, nocturnal non-dipping status alone is an important predictor of renal disease progression.

Hypertension is a common complication of CKD in children, as well as an independent risk factor for the progression of CKD [13–15]. Controlling BP can effectively delay CKD [16]. Studies have shown that ambulatory hypertension and nocturnal non-dipping status are associated with a high risk of renal events. This coexisting state of hypertension is the most common in clinical practice, accounting for 51.1% of the cases in this study, which is consistent with previous data collected from adult patients with CKD [17]. However, the predictive values of ambulatory and nocturnal hypertension alone have been limited in childhood CKD studies. Therefore, in this study, we combined the characteristics of ambulatory BP and nocturnal dipping status in children with CKD to assess their independent prognostic effects on renal outcomes.

Among hypertension with nocturnal non-dipping in adult CKD patients, the detection rate was 70% in an Italian study, and 83–86% in multiple Japanese studies [17, 18]. However, pediatric data are lacking in previous studies. In the CKiD cohort study, 41% of children with CKD did not have an available nocturnal dipping status [19], while 84.0% of children with CKD had a nocturnal non-dipping status in the present study. Even in the group without ambulatory hypertension, 76.6% of children had nocturnal non-dipping, which indicated that nocturnal hypertension is common in children with CKD. The pathogenesis of nocturnal hypertension is thought to be primarily related to sympathetic overdrive, endothelial dysfunction, and activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system [20, 21]. Circadian rhythm disturbances in urine sodium excretion, particularly in patients with CKD in whom salt-sensitive hypertension is more common, occurs in the kidneys at night, with a stress natriuretic effect decreasing nocturnal BP, which is one of the primary mechanisms underlying nocturnal hypertension [22]. Therefore, nocturnal hypertension is more likely to occur in patients with severe kidney injury.

In 2022, an updated Scientific Statement on ABPM in children and adolescents from the AHA indicated that isolated blunted nocturnal BP dipping in the absence of elevated mean BP is not classified as a form of masked hypertension [9]. However, some adult data suggest that nighttime hypertension, including isolated nighttime hypertension, is also associated with a faster rate of CKD progression and target organ damage. In a cohort study of CKD in adults conducted by Borrelli et al. [17], the stratified group with nocturnal non-dipping with ambulatory hypertension had the highest risk of cardiovascular events and renal outcomes. even in those with normal ambulatory BP, the risk of cardiovascular endpoints (HR = 2.06; 95% CI, 1.15–3.68) and renal outcomes (HR = 1.82; 95% CI, 1.17–2.82) was increased in those with nocturnal non-dipping. In a prospective observational study, Wang et al. [23] found that non-dipping in BP at night in adult CKD was associated with worse renal outcomes but not with 24-h mean systolic BP. These results indicate that nocturnal non-dipping is an independent risk factor of adverse events in patients with CKD. In our study, these BP parameters were not related to LVH, which was not entirely consistent with prior research in adults. The same results were also found in the CKiD cohort, and there was no statistically significant association between dipping and LVMI [19]. It may be that many adult patients with CKD often have comorbid diseases such as diabetes and hyperlipidemia, which can increase the risk of LVH. However, we found that more than half of the children with ambulatory hypertension above the target level and without dipping had the highest risk of renal progression. Similarly, we observed that, even if the ambulatory BP was normal, the risk of renal outcomes in the non-dipping group was higher than that in the dipping group. Along with persistent proteinuria, nighttime hypertension, including isolated nighttime hypertension, is the most significant factor in CKD progression. Another study of pediatric CKD from Deja et al. [24] confirmed that nocturnal hypertension can serve as a predictive indicator of a rapid decrease in eGFR (> 3 mL/min/1.73 m2/year). Regarding the potential mechanisms, increased glomerular vascular resistance and glomerular structural pressure load may accelerate the progression of residual nephron damage in CKD, leading to an accelerated decrease in eGFR [17]. However, contradictory conclusions have also been drawn. For example, two studies in Japan [25, 26] both showed that in the group with ambulatory hypertension, non-dipping in nighttime BP was not associated with the risk of renal events. This discrepancy may be due to the study design, definition of the goal of ambulatory BP, and differences in population and ethnicity [27]. The updated Scientific Statement on ABPM in children and adolescents in 2022 removed the concept of dipping that was present in the 2014 guidelines [9]. Because of the high incidence of nocturnal hypertension in patients with CKD and the extent to which BP autoregulation and circadian rhythms degrade as CKD progresses [28], the importance of assessing circadian BP rhythms in children with CKD is clear, even in children with early CKD. We believe that nocturnal hypertension plays as big of a role as daytime hypertension [29]. Prospective observational and interventional studies are needed to evaluate the effects of nocturnal BP normalization on the progression of CKD in children.

This study had some limitations. First, the sample size was limited and there was a lack of long-term follow-up data. Further, it should be noted that hypertension in children with CKD may result from multiple factors, including diet, drugs, and other concomitant diseases. No stratified analysis was conducted on children with white coat hypertension and masked hypertension. Moreover, this was a retrospective study to assess the BP status of patients with CKD at a single time point during hospitalization. which may have led to white coat hypertension, and sleep disturbances caused by nighttime monitoring may have reduced their accuracy. In the future, multicenter and large-sample prospective studies on children with CKD should be conducted to fully evaluate the prognostic value of ABPM in pediatric patients with CKD using high-quality evidence, including studies with long-term follow-up, guided by ABPM and comprehensive and in-depth assessment of its impact on target organ damage.

In conclusion, this retrospective, single-center study analyzed the prognostic value of ABPM for renal progression in children with CKD. In our cohort, nocturnal non-dipping BP was common in children with CKD, which could not be detected by office BP measurements, thereby underscoring the superiority of ABPM as a diagnostic tool for hypertension in children with CKD. In addition to proteinuria, CKD progression is dependent on BP, and independent of ambulatory hypertension, while nocturnal non-dipping BP is also a risk predictor of renal progression. Therefore, nighttime and diurnal BP characteristics should be closely monitored when assessing BP in children with CKD.
