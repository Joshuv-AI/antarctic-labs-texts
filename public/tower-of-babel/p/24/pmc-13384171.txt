Nationwide, multicentre, prospective periprosthetic joint infection cohort study: study protocol of the Swiss Revision Cohort (REVCO)
Authors: Micol Pallanza, Atesch Ateschrang, Anders Boyd, Anna Conen, Antonios Kritikos, Jonathan Laurençon, Emanuel Francis Liechti, Sylvain Meylan, Truong-Thanh Pham, Sylvain Steinmetz, Domizio Suva, Maria C Thurnheer, Nora Renz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDiagnostic and therapeutic management of patients with periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) remains challenging. This national cohort aims to describe the epidemiology, clinical phenotypes, management strategies and outcomes of patients with PJI.Methods and analysisThis is a national, multicentre, prospective, longitudinal, observational cohort study of patients who underwent revision for PJI of the hip, knee, shoulder, elbow or ankle. Participating centres are university or non-university tertiary healthcare facilities located in Switzerland. Episodes include likely and confirmed PJI, as defined by the European Bone and Joint Infection Society criteria. During routine clinical visits around revision surgery, data are collected and patients are followed-up thereafter for a minimum of 2 years. The primary objective is infection outcome and secondary objectives include functional outcome and patient satisfaction and evaluation of host, infection, microbiological and treatment factors associated with treatment failure and poor functional outcomes, both in the overall population and across subgroups. A minimum of 329 patients would be needed to have sufficient statistical power to carry out these analyses.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval has been granted by the Research Ethics Committee under protocol number 2021-01791. All patients provide written informed consent for inclusion in the study. Study findings will be disseminated through publications in peer-reviewed journals and presented at national and international conferences in the fields of orthopaedics, infectious diseases and microbiology. We plan to leverage the platform within the cohort to conduct future studies and interventional trials.Trial registration numberNCT07473089.

Introduction

Total joint arthroplasty (TJA) is the current standard treatment for degenerative, inflammatory and posttraumatic osteoarthritis. Over the past decade, the use of TJA has increased steadily, and the most recent Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) Health Report ranks Switzerland among the countries with the highest rates of hip and knee replacement in 2023.13 With the rising life expectancy of the general population, and advancements in surgical methods, materials and postoperative care, joint prosthesis implantation is expected to increase substantially by 2030.4 5 Although surgical adverse events are uncommon, one of the high-impact complications of prosthetic joint replacement is periprosthetic joint infection (PJI). While rare, it requires complex surgical intervention and prolonged antibiotic therapy and is associated with significant morbidity and substantial healthcare costs.6 The reported prevalence of postoperative PJI ranges between <1% for shoulder replacements, 0.5% to 1.0% for hip replacements and 0.5% to 2% for knee replacements.7 However, carriers of joint prostheses remain at lifelong risk for haematogenous infection in the setting of bacteraemia.

Successful management of PJI requires revision surgery of the prosthetic joint.8 The Swiss National Joint Registry (SIRIS) reported 2-year revision rates of 2.5% for total hip arthroplasty and 3.3% for total knee arthroplasty in 2023, with infection being the most frequent cause of early revision (27.9% for hips and 20.6% for knees).9 Despite published guidelines, the management of PJI remains insufficiently standardised, primarily due to heterogeneous clinical presentations and limited availability of rigorous data from prospective studies and randomised controlled trials (RCTs) comparing interventions to improve outcomes.10 The presence of foreign material with biofilm formation further complicates the diagnosis and treatment of PJI, posing a significant challenge for clinicians necessitating close collaboration among specialists from various medical fields, including orthopaedic surgeons, microbiologists and infectious disease experts.11 A recent observational study suggests that a multidisciplinary team approach is associated with improved diagnostics, fewer surgeries, reduced length of hospital stay and higher success rates.12

Globally, only a limited number of prospective trials and cohort studies have been conducted to evaluate and define the most effective treatment strategies for PJI, including the preferred surgical approaches, optimal duration of antimicrobial therapy and comparisons between different antimicrobial agents. One notable example is the Prosthetic joint Infection in Australia and New Zealand, Observational (PIANO) study, a multicentre, prospective, observational cohort study, which included 783 patients in 27 centres and has provided valuable insights into the clinical characteristics, aetiology and initial management strategies of PJI.13 Despite Switzerland’s high volume of TJA procedures and existence of national implant registries, such as SIRIS, comprehensive data on incidence, treatment modalities, outcomes and long-term results of PJI across different joint types remain scarce.14 As a result, infectious diseases and orthopaedic clinicians in Switzerland collaborated to launch a prospective, multicentre cohort study (Revision Cohort (REVCO)) to collect comprehensive clinical, diagnostic, therapeutic and outcome data from patients who underwent prosthetic joint revision surgery for PJI.

Methods and analysis

Study design

The REVCO study is a national, multicentre, prospective, longitudinal, observational cohort study that aims to describe characteristics and treatment outcomes of patients with PJI in Switzerland. Participants who undergo revision surgery of a prosthetic hip, knee, shoulder, ankle or elbow joint for suspected PJI will be consecutively recruited on hospital admission. After inclusion, a patient may contribute one or more episodes to the study. An episode is defined as a condition leading to revision surgery for a suspected or confirmed PJI. This means that a single patient may be included multiple times, either due to infections affecting different joints at the same time (eg, multifocal haematogenous PJI) or due to subsequent infections of the same joint at different times (including infection failure) or new infections of other joints. Each episode is analysed separately.

Recruitment started on 23 September 2022 at the first participating centre. Currently, three Swiss universities (Inselspital Bern, Centre Hospitalier Universitaire Vaudois, Geneva University Hospitals) and two non-university tertiary care centres (Kantonsspital Aarau, Hôpital Cantonal de Fribourg) are recruiting patients, while two additional tertiary care centres are planning to begin recruitment in 2026.

A study steering committee (SSC) has been established to oversee study progress, provide strategic direction, guide data analyses and the development of manuscripts arising from emerging study data. Each centre has assigned at least one dedicated principal investigator from the Infectious Diseases specialty and one from Orthopaedics specialty to be part of the SSC. All proposed substudies, amendments to the study protocol and publications are reviewed for approval by the SSC.

Definitions

PJI: Each episode is analysed separately and classified according to EBJIS criteria, which combine clinical, laboratory and intraoperative findings.15 Based on these criteria, episodes are categorised as either periprosthetic joint infection (likely or confirmed) or aseptic failure, where infection is unlikely.

Acuity: Acute PJI is defined as the presence of symptoms for less than 4 weeks after the most recent surgery in early postoperative infections, or less than 4 weeks of symptom duration in patients with haematogenous infections with late presentation after the last surgery.

Microbiology: Based on microbiological results, PJI episodes are categorised as monomicrobial (one clinically significant organism isolated), polymicrobial (more than one clinically significant organism isolated) or without isolated pathogen (no organisms isolated from microbiological samples including culture and molecular tests.

Route of infection: Haematogenous PJI is defined by acute onset of symptoms after a pain-free period 1 month or more after arthroplasty or last revision, and the presence of at least one of the following four criteria, as previously published: (1) positive blood cultures and evidence of a distant infectious focus consistent with the pathogen; (2) positive prosthetic site culture and evidence of a distant infectious focus consistent with the pathogen; (3) onset of symptoms 2 years or more after TJA or revision surgery and evidence of a distant infectious focus or positive blood cultures and (4) or judged as haematogenous PJI by the infectious diseases physician using clinical and chronological features and microbiological findings.16 Contiguous (‘per continuitatem’) PJI are infections with a confirmed infection source in the tissue adjacent to the affected prosthetic joint. Postoperative or postinterventional PJI episodes are defined as infections with clinical, laboratory, microbiological and chronological features consistent with pathogen introduction during a preceding surgery or intervention (eg, infiltration or arthrocentesis).

Infection failure is defined as the presence of PJI (according to EBJIS criteria) at time of follow-up or infection-related death. Types of failure include (1) persistent or recurrent PJI with the same pathogen or no detected pathogen (ie, treatment failure) and (2) a new infection caused by a pathogen different from the index episode.

Study procedures

Consecutive patients are screened and recruited through the local project leader or other members of the dedicated study team (ie, study nurse or clinical/research fellows). Patients undergoing prosthetic revision surgery are screened for inclusion during a preceding outpatient visit or on hospital admission. The responsible orthopaedic surgeon or a member of the study team informs the patient about the research project. The patient is then given 24 hours to consider participation, after which a co-investigator or a study nurse qualified to perform delegated study tasks is available to answer questions and ask the individual if they wish to participate. If they agree and no exclusion criteria are present, they are asked to sign an informed consent prior to inclusion.

After obtaining consent, participants are then followed during routine clinical visits and hospitalisations, and data are collected at visits occurring 6 weeks, 12 and 24 months (and optionally at 3 and 6 months) after the last surgery (figure 1). No additional examinations beyond clinical care or for the purposes of this study are performed. Participants are asked to fill out a routine care questionnaire assessing patient satisfaction and activities of daily life (EQ-5D-5L).17 During follow-up visits, functional, imaging and laboratory tests are performed as part of clinical routine care. Participants are followed until withdrawal of consent, last routine visit, date of administrative censoring of the cohort (to be determined at the time of each analysis) and death, whichever occurs first. Any unscheduled clinical contact related to issues with the affected joint, including those occurring after the routine 24-month follow-up, is recorded. In the event of another PJI of the same joint, the preceding episode is classified as an infection failure and a new episode—with a new record number but the same patient number—is included in the cohort. For individuals who chose to discontinue follow-up or cannot continue follow-up, reasons for discontinuation will be recorded. In the event of consent withdrawal, participants will continue to receive the standard of care. In the case where a participant refuses to attend a planned clinical follow-up visit at 24 months, telephone contact will be attempted, and documentation of any prosthesis-related medical events will be obtained from other institutions, where applicable.

Statistical analysis plan

A descriptive analysis of the characteristics will be carried out. Depending on the distribution of data, the results will be expressed as mean (SD) or median (IQR) for continuous variables or counts (percent) for categorical variables, respectively. If characteristics between groups are to be compared, their distributions will be compared using Student’s t-test or Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables or Pearson’s χ2 or Fisher’s Exact test for categorical variables, respectively.

Baseline is defined as the date of revision surgery for PJI. Follow-up will begin at the time of most recent surgery and continue until withdrawal of consent, last routine visit, date of administrative censoring of the cohort (to be determined at the time of each analysis) and death, whichever occurs first. Patients are allowed to be included in the analysis multiple times in the event of treatment failure requiring repeat revision surgeries.

The survival analysis of the primary outcome (ie, infection failure) will be graphically depicted using Kaplan-Meier methods. These curves will be stratified by exposures of interest and compared using the log-rank test. Time to infection failure will be modelled using a Cox proportional hazards model. Covariates representing the exposures of interest will be included and will be used to calculate the HRs and 95% CIs comparing the hazards across levels of determinants. Variance estimation will account for the repeated measurements within patients and multiple episodes per patient. Multivariable models will be constructed either using an a priori selection of variables based on current evidence or backwards selection, depending on the research question.

Sample size calculation

The sample size is based on the Cox proportional hazards model to evaluate the primary endpoint. This calculation used the method proposed by Latouche et al.18 with the R package ‘powerSurvEpi’. The minimum desired HR is 2.0 to distinguish a statistically significant difference. Assuming a two-tailed type I error of 0.05, power at 80%, the outcome occurring at a rate of 10% per year, an exposure occurring at a probability of 0.5, an additional binary covariable with 0.01 correlation, a total of 329 patients will be needed to detect a significant difference.

Data management and storage

Data are collected using paper or electronic case report forms and entered in coded form into a secure REDCap database. Data quality is ensured through source data verification and adherence to principles of International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) guidelines for Good Clinical Practice (GCP). Source data are derived from routine clinical records. All data handling complies with Swiss data protection legislation. Access to study data is restricted to authorised personnel, with data securely backed up and archived for at least 10 years after study completion.

Data monitoring

This is a prospective, observational study with a minimum follow-up of 2 years. While no formal real-time data monitoring is in place, data collection and quality are regularly reviewed by the local principal investigator throughout the study period to ensure accuracy, consistency and completeness of the dataset. No independent data monitoring committee will be established, as the study is non-interventional and does not involve any investigational treatments or procedures. Oversight is provided by the principal investigators and the institutional research board, as appropriate.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical considerations and informed consent

This study is conducted in accordance with the protocol, the Declaration of Helsinki,19 the principles of ICH guidelines for GCP.20 All included participants provide written informed consent. The research sponsor is the Inselspital (Bern, Switzerland). The study has been approved by the Ethikkommission Bern (ID: 2021-01791).

Risk–benefit assessment

There is no benefit or risk attributable to the study participation for the individual, as treatment is provided as part of standard medical practice and not according to the requirements of a trial protocol. The risk of incidental findings is not increased due to study participation, as no additional tests outside the clinical routine are performed.

The possible benefit of the participant is warranty of best clinical care by adherence of close follow-up visits, which allows for early detection of potential infection persistence or relapse. Obtaining insights into current clinical practices of institutions and comparing them between centres facilitates quality control assessment of the institutional approaches to a highly complex entity and enables to adapt or improve treatment algorithms for future patients.

Dissemination plans

Results will be published in peer-reviewed medical journals. Preliminary and definitive results of the study may be presented at national and international scientific meetings in the fields of infectious diseases, orthopaedics and clinical microbiology, depending on the content and relevance of the findings. Patients will not be individually informed about the study results, as the data will be analysed and reported at a group level and do not directly impact individual care. Dissemination to other stakeholders, including healthcare professionals, hospital leadership and research collaborators, will depend on the nature and significance of the results. If appropriate, findings may be shared through institutional reports, academic publications or internal presentations to support improvements in clinical practice or inform future research initiatives.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research project. The study was designed and conducted by clinical and academic investigators based on scientific and clinical priorities.

Discussion

This national, multicentre, prospective longitudinal cohort study will generate a comprehensive dataset on PJI, leveraging clinical data and routinely collected samples from multiple university and non-university centres in Switzerland. These data should allow assessment of failure rates following treatment of PJI and investigation of the underlying cause of failure, thereby enabling quality control of institutional management strategies. Ultimately, these results should support clinicians in refining and optimising management algorithms for future patients and contribute to current national recommendations.

Several cohort studies have been previously initiated to address the topic of PJI. For example, the Spanish multicentre cohort (2003–2012) has provided valuable insights into temporal trends in the aetiology of PJI across numerous hospitals including a large case volume. However, as a ambidirectional observational study, with both prospective and retospective data collection, it is subject to inherent limitations, including potential selection and information biases. Its main strength lies in its broad temporal coverage and large, heterogeneous dataset.21 The Australian–New Zealand PIANO cohort (2014–2017) has provided prospective, multicentre data on several hundred PJI cases, substantially contributing to our understanding of disease presentation and outcomes. However, interpretiation of the results of these cohorts is limited by the heterogeneity in definitions, classification systems and inclusion criteria. Definitions of PJI vary across studies, with some adopting Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) criteria, modified Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) criteria, or EBJIS criteria, limiting direct comparisons and comlicating the pooled interpretation of results.

There are several important features adding to the significance of our cohort. First, patient data are prospectively collected, which allows us to establish the sequence of various interventions and outcomes and hence minimises temporal bias. Second, the broad range of data, particularly when treatment failure occurs, will provide a more comprehensive understanding of which aspects of current interventions might give rise to failure. In Switzerland, there is no standardised approach to the therapy and management of PJI. The fact that we have included many different clinical centres in Switzerland means that the heterogeneity of certain interventions can be examined while preserving the generalisability of these centres. Third, the multicentric design allows each centre to contribute to a sufficiently large sample size for answering clinically meaningful research questions. This is particularly important for PJI subgroups known to be associated with poor outcomes and major clinical challenges with unresolved questions—such as infections caused by specific pathogens (eg, streptococci) or those arising via haematogenous route—the multicentre design enables the accumulation of sufficiently large case numbers.2225 This cohort is particularly well suited for the analysis of haematogenous PJI, as it applies a specific and comprehensive definition for this distinct entity, which has been lacking in many previous studies. In addition, we aim to contribute to the refinement of diagnostic standards, through comprehensive analysis of synovial fluid and standardised assessment of histopathological changes in periprosthetic tissue.

Naturally, RCTs provide a higher level of evidence than cohort studies. However, RCTs involve highly selected populations, which limits generalisability, and they may not fully reflect routine clinical practice. In contrast, this prospective cohort will evaluate the effectiveness of current management strategies in real-world clinical settings, including the frail and most vulnerable patients, who are often excluded from RCTs. Furthermore, the dataset will allow exploration of potential biases in treatment indication and differential loss to follow-up, both of which can be addressed using methods that emulate RCTs.26

Study limitations

Several limitations of this cohort study should be acknowledged. First, all participating centres are tertiary care hospitals, which may bias the study population towards more complex cases (eg, due to referral of difficult cases to tertiary centres). In Switzerland, many smaller or peripheral hospitals manage uncomplicated PJI locally, whereas patients are typically referred to tertiary care centres following treatment failure or when more complex surgical procedures are required. As a result, standard or uncomplicated cases, such as chronic low-grade infections, are possibly underrepresented in tertiary care settings. Second, as we rely on routinely collected data, most outcome measures are obtained from electronic medical records or are patient-reported. This may introduce measurement or validity bias, which requires consideration when interpreting results. Moreover, due to financial constraints, no biobanking initiative was undertaken within this study, limiting the potential for future biomolecular analyses or other analyses involving stored biological samples. Finally, given the relatively small number of individuals undergoing revision surgery for PJI, statistical power for more nuanced analyses may be limited, underscoring the need for broader international cohort collaborations.

Future perspectives

In the future, an expansion of the cohort is planned to include aseptic revision cases. In light of this anticipated expansion the project has been named REVCO, encompassing all revision surgeries irrespective of the underlying cause. This addition will provide an appropriate control group for comparative analyses of outcomes, such as infection rates, functional recovery, complication rates and patient-reported satisfaction and quality of life measures, thereby enhancing the scientific value of the project. Moreover, inclusion of aseptic failures will allow validation of newly proposed PJI definition criteria and support the development of future definitions.

Furthermore, to improve generalisability, an expansion of the cohort to non-tertiary centres will be envisioned once the cohort is well established and validated. In the meantime, including statistical weighting based on key demographic variables such as age and sex will be used to approximate the distribution of the broader Swiss PJI population.

Although joint-specific PROMs were not included in the current cohort design, their incorporation may be considered in future expansions of the cohort once they are more consistently collected as part of routine clinical care across participating institutions.
