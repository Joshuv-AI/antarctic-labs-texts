Influence of Seasonal and Chronobiological Factors on Driver Behavior and Traffic Accident Risk: A Scoping Review
Authors: Eber Pinheiro Assis, Ana Amélia Benedito Silva, Claudia R. C. Moreno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The current is a scoping review of the possible link between seasonal patterns in traffic accidents and chronobiological aspects. Although this relationship has been widely investigated in different contexts, the present study performs an in-depth analysis of the existing literature on the topic. The main objectives are to map and synthesize the scientific evidence and to identify knowledge gaps. To ensure methodological rigor and transparent reporting, the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) update was employed as the framework the development of the present article. The review confirmed the seasonal pattern in the occurrence of traffic accidents and highlighted the deficit in melatonin production, which can lead to drowsiness and, consequently, impair driving performance. Seasonal sensitivity, common in winter, can affect psychological well-being, posing a risk to drivers' mental health. Daylight saving time (DST) increases driver fatigue and drowsiness, while adverse winter conditions increase the risk of collision. These biological disruptions follow a seasonal pattern and may contribute to the higher rates of crashes observed at certain times of the year.

Introduction

According to the Global Status Report by the World Health Organization (WHO), 1 there has been a steady rise in deaths involving road-traffic crashes (1.35 million/year in 2016) worldwide. Among these deaths, a higher incidence has been observed in young people aged under 29 years. Approximately 3/4 (73%) deaths related to traffic collisions involve male individuals younger than 25 years, who have an almost 3 times greater chance of death compared to young female individuals. 1 2 Road-traffic crashes cost most countries 3% of their gross domestic product, not only affecting the economy, but also representing a serious public health problem. 1

According to Kamel and Sayed, 3 seasonality in the context of traffic crashes refers to the periodic and predictable variations in crash occurrence over time, resulting from correlations among crashes that occur in close temporal proximity and, therefore, share unobserved characteristics that systematically influence traffic exposure and crash risk. 4 5 Using data from cyclist-vehicle crashes and traffic volume from 134 traffic-analysis zones in Vancouver, the authors 3 showed that seasonal and weather-related variations, such as rainfall and periods associated with holidays and school schedules, systematically influence traffic exposure and crash risk. By explicitly accounting for these seasonal patterns in the analysis, the study demonstrated that crash risk is not constant over time, and that models incorporating temporal variation provide a more accurate representation of traffic-safety dynamics. Furthermore, Fritz et al. 6 emphasize that seasonal changes in photoperiod may intensify driver sleepiness, thereby increasing vulnerability and the likelihood of crashes. Thus, understanding seasonal patterns is essential to develop more effective preventive strategies. 4 7 8 9 10 11

Seasonality can promote changes in hormones, such as thyroid hormones, cortisol, and melatonin, 12 13 leading to mismatches in circadian rhythm. 14 Driver drowsiness may be related to alterations or disorders of the circadian rhythm, 10 which is responsible for regulating several biological and behavioral functions, such as hormone secretion, body temperature, sleep-wake patterns, and social interaction throughout the 24-hour cycle. 13

Seasonal variations affect human behavior and physiology through multifactorial mechanisms. According to Levin et al., 8 seasonality is a terrestrial phenomenon influencing human activities and natural cycles across species. Seasonal rhythms are a type of infradian rhythm, with periods longer than 24 hours and therefore considered low-frequency rhythms. Environmental variations related to seasonality, particularly in daylight duration, such as the shorter photoperiod observed in winter, can, in turn, impact circadian rhythms, resulting in changes in biological and behavioral patterns. The circadian timing system plays a crucial role in hormonal regulation and can significantly affect driving performance. 3 4 6 15 Melatonin, for example, regulates the sleep-wake cycle, and its production occurs during periods of reduced light, such as in winter, potentially leading to increased daytime sleepiness. 12 These physiological changes can alter the driver's alertness or behavior, which can increase the risk of traffic crashes. 16 17

This circadian timing system is intrinsic to the organism, but it is influenced by internal and external factors, including the local photoperiod, habitual sleep and wake times, and other conditions that affect sleep duration and quality. 14 Seasonal variation in endogenous biological rhythms can affect an individual's cognitive and physical functions. 12 The photoperiod is the one of the main seasonal determinants, which varies according to latitude and season. Individuals exposed to extreme photoperiod variations throughout the year may experience changes in behavior and physiology. 18 These alterations can disrupt circadian rhythmicity, leading to deficits in melatonin production and impacting sleep duration and quality, 6 19 which affects an individual's cognitive and physical functions. 12

Cortisol, commonly referred to as the stress hormone, is also modulated by seasonal influences. In humans, physiological rhythms are organized hierarchically and interact with behavioral and cognitive functions, such as alertness, vigilance, and decision-making, which are sensitive to environmental conditions. 16 Analyses of large-scale medical records 12 comprising millions of hormone measurements show that cortisol and other effector hormones display clear seasonal set-points, with peak levels occurring predominantly during winter and early spring. Elevated cortisol levels during colder months, potentially intensified by cold exposure and reduced daylight, may negatively affect cognitive control, emotional regulation, and risk assessment. These neuroendocrine changes can promote more impulsive and less cautious driving behaviors, thereby contributing to the higher incidence of traffic crashes observed during winter. 12 16

The regulation of sleep and wakefulness is based on the interaction of two main biological processes: a homeostatic process (process S) and a circadian process (process C). 14 16 Process S represents the accumulation of sleep pressure during wakefulness, which dissipates during sleep, reflecting the physiological need for rest. 13 16 Process C, governed by the circadian pacemaker, which is located in the suprachiasmatic nuclei, exhibits an approximately 24-hour rhythm that modulates alertness and the thresholds for sleep onset. 13 14 Seasonal variations may disrupt both processes; a reduced photoperiod in winter, for example, may desynchronize process C and alter melatonin production, while season-related behavioral changes may impair sleep quality, thereby increasing the homeostatic pressure of process S. 6 13 20 The interaction between high sleep pressure and a circadian signal that promotes sleepiness creates periods of maximal vulnerability, significantly increasing the risk of sleepiness-related driving crashes. 21 22

The suprachiasmatic nuclei (SCN) play an essential role in synchronizing biological rhythms with environmental signals, such as light, and disruptions in these rhythms have a significant impact on mood disorders. 17 The SCN, located in the hypothalamus, are considered the primary endogenous temporal marker of the organism, responsible for regulating the circadian cycle and coordinating various physiological functions in response to environmental variations. 13 15 Additionally, dysfunctions in melatonin production and genetic predispositions to affective disorders highlight the complexity of seasonal effects, potentially compromising drivers' performance. 13 23

A study conducted in Switzerland 24 assessed the prevalence of excessive daytime sleepiness among drivers undergoing driving fitness evaluations, including private and professional drivers. The investigation used questionnaires such as the Epworth Sleepiness Scale and the Berlin Questionnaire. Of the 435 questionnaires distributed, 128 were returned (29%), with a mean participant age of 42.5 years. The results showed that 9% of drivers had probable excessive daytime sleepiness and the same proportion had a high risk of obstructive sleep apnea syndrome; additionally, 16% reported involuntary nodding off while driving, which was associated with significantly-higher Epworth Sleepiness Scale scores, while 8% reported sleepiness-related crashes, and another 8% reported near-miss events. 24

The indirect costs of sleepy driving extend beyond traffic crashes and include substantial losses related to premature mortality. Each year, approximately 1.3 million deaths worldwide are attributed to road traffic injuries, of which about 400 thousand occur among individuals under 25 years of age, and another 400 thousand among those older than 65 years. When standard economic valuations of human life (e.g., the Value of a Statistical Life) are applied, deaths attributable to sleepiness at the wheel correspond to an indirect societal cost of approximately US$ 2.37 trillion per year, demonstrating the scale of the human and economic burden associated with insufficient driver alertness. In addition to fatal outcomes, sleepy driving contributes to many injuries and long-term disabilities, resulting in high indirect costs due to loss of quality of life, reduced functional capacity, and productivity losses arising from impaired performance and operational delays in transportation and logistics activities. 25

According to Pilkington-Cheney and McNicholas, 26 sleep problems among workers are linked to poorer occupational performance, a higher risk of adverse events, and substantial economic impacts for employers and society. The estimated cost of poor sleep ranges from US$ 322 to US$ 1,967 per employee, and, in Australia, the total economic burden reached US$ 45.21 billion in 2016 to 2017, including healthcare costs, productivity losses, and reduced well-being. Regarding road safety, driver sleepiness is a major risk factor for traffic crashes, with annual direct costs estimated at US$ 139.4 to 152 billion in the United States, and €43 to 337 billion in Europe, while indirect costs related to the loss of human lives from sleepiness-related crashes are estimated at approximately US$ 2.37 trillion worldwide.

Sleepy driving represents a significant occupational and public safety risk among professional drivers. In Brazil, a survey of professional truck drivers 22 identified sleepiness while driving in 31.5% of the participants, with driving distances exceeding 1 thousand km and productivity-based remuneration independently associated with increased risk. In Sweden, 26 shift-working bus drivers frequently report difficulty maintaining alertness, particularly under split-shift schedules, indicating a direct effect of circadian disruption on driving safety. Additionally, assessments conducted in different countries 25 show that drivers assign lower perceived risk to sleepy driving compared with other hazardous behaviors, such as speeding, indicating insufficient recognition of circadian-related vulnerability during the afternoon and early morning hours. Together, these findings support the need for interventions focused on sleep health, work schedules, and circadian alignment to reduce crashes associated with driver sleepiness. 25

Another study, conducted by the European Sleep Research Society, 22 analyzed 12,434 questionnaires from 19 countries to estimate the occurrence of falling asleep at the wheel, its determinants, and consequences. The average prevalence during the 2 years investigated was of 17% and, among drivers who reported this event, 7.0% were involved in sleep-related crashes, of which 13.2% required hospital care and 3.6% resulted in fatalities. Results obtained from multivariate logistic regression analyses showed a higher likelihood of falling asleep at the wheel in the Netherlands (odds ratio [OR] = 3.55; 95%CI: 1.97–6.39) and Austria (OR = 2.34; 95%CI: 1.75–3.13), followed by Belgium (OR = 1.52), Portugal (OR = 1.34), Poland (OR = 1.22), and France (OR = 1.20), while lower odds were observed in Croatia (OR = 0.36), Slovenia (OR = 0.62), and Italy (OR = 0.65). The main individual determinants included younger age, male sex (OR = 1.79), driving ≥ 20 thousand km per year (OR = 2.02), higher daytime sleepiness (OR = 7.49), and a high risk of obstructive sleep apnea syndrome in men (OR = 3.48), highlighting the magnitude and heterogeneity of drowsy driving risk. 22

In the general population, drivers' sleep patterns are predominantly characterized by daytime wakefulness and nighttime sleep, with physiological peaks of sleepiness occurring at night and in the early afternoon, reflecting the interaction between sleep duration, chronotype, and circadian rhythms. 27 This pattern, however, is not uniform across the life course, as age influences preferred driving times due to physiological changes associated with aging, such as reduced melatonin production, which compromises synchronization with the light–dark cycle and may negatively affect alertness. These changes, together with the higher prevalence of visual impairments among older adults, tend to restrict driving to daytime hours. 28

Moreover, inadequate exposure to natural or artificial light, combined with irregular sleep-wake schedules and insufficient sleep duration, may result in impaired melatonin production, promoting circadian misalignment, excessive daytime sleepiness, and reduced functional performance while driving. 13 29 The circadian clock interacts with the environment through circadian synchronizers known as zeitgebers , such as lighting, which induce circadian variations in the organism, generating changes in cognition and behavior. 30 31 This temporal adaptation consists of the harmonization between biological rhythmicity and environmental cycles. 30

This scenario is particularly critical among shift workers, especially night workers, for whom insufficient sleep, circadian variability, and long working hours substantially increase the risk of commuting crashes, particularly after leaving work. 22 32 33 In this context, evidence indicates that nocturnal sleep deprivation intensifies sleepiness and reduces alertness during vehicle operation, thereby compromising traffic safety. 34

In addition to the biological factors contributing to crashes, road conditions often demand increased attention and control from drivers. Issues such as potholes, inadequate signage, and the absence of shoulders heighten the vulnerability to adverse events in urban and highway traffic. When physiological changes further impair driver performance, the risk of crashes is exacerbated. 35 Chronobiological imbalance may lead to excessive sleepiness, difficulty concentrating, and irritability, all of which impair the decision-making ability while driving. Additionally, seasonal weather conditions, such as heavy rain, fog, or icy roads, further intensify these challenges, creating an environment in which physiological and environmental factors interact negatively, significantly increasing the risk of traffic crashes. 21 36 37 38 39

In order to comprehensively explore the relationship between seasonality and traffic collisions urban areas and on highways, the current review includes studies on the topic and promotes a discussion based on them. Thus, the hypothesis that the interaction between seasonal factors and endogenous circadian mechanisms represents a fundamental framework to understand the impact of temporal fluctuations on the risk of traffic crashes. To our knowledge, the present is the first scoping review integrating seasonality, seasonal affective mechanisms, chronobiology, and daylight saving time (DST) within a unified framework for traffic crash risk.

Materials and Methods

Type of Study

The current is a scoping review, a study design crafted to comprehensively explore the existing literature, delving into its depth and breadth. The primary objectives are mapping and synthesizing scientific evidence related to a specific topic, along with pinpointing knowledge gaps within a designated field. To ensure methodological rigor and transparent reporting, the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) update was employed as the framework.

Search Strategy

A search was performed in the PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane, and LILACS databases. The search strategy was formulated using the controlled vocabulary of the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), Embase Subject Headings (Emtree), and Health Sciences Descriptors (Descritores em Ciências da Saúde, DeCS, in Portuguese) databases, up to November 21, 2023, and limited to the English language ( Table 1 ).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Observational (cross-sectional, cohort, case-control) studies and experimental (randomized clinical trials, RCTs) studies, as well as systematic reviews, were used as a guide to define the inclusion criteria. Articles not published in English, gray literature, opinion articles, editorials, and those published more than 10 years ago were excluded.

Data Extraction Methods

Three independent researchers systematically reviewed citations, assessing relevance based on titles and abstracts. Citations meeting the inclusion criteria or with unclear titles/abstracts underwent full-text screening. Consensus was achieved to settle any inconsistencies. Full articles were subsequently reviewed by at least two of the authors, and content was extracted in a standardized form and inserted into a database for comprehensive analysis. The standardized data elements included study type (descriptive, review, cohort, case-control, cross-sectional, RCT, non-RCT), population studied, sample size, chronobiological factors, seasonal physiological responses, sleep quality among drivers, and other outcomes such as cognitive or physical performance measures.

Justification for Database Selection

The database selection was designed to comprehensively capture studies investigating seasonal patterns associated with chronobiological aspects of driver behavior and traffic accidents. The PubMed/MEDLINE database was included as the primary biomedical database, since it is widely recognized for its extensive indexing of research on sleep, circadian rhythms, and related health outcomes. Embase was selected to complement the PubMed/MEDLINE search by expanding coverage of international journals and studies in epidemiology, public health, and transportation safety. The Cochrane Library was included to identify high-quality evidence and previously-published reviews relevant to the topic. Additionally, LILACS was incorporated to capture studies from Latin America and the Caribbean, thereby reducing regional publication bias and ensuring broader geographic representation. Together, these databases provide a robust and complementary search strategy, consistent with the recommendations for scoping reviews and aligned with the PRISMA-ScR framework.

Synthesis Methods

Initially, we quantified the number of publications addressing various types of seasonal factors related to traffic accidents, circadian alterations, accident risk, and changes in drivers' performance. Additionally, we evaluated the overall quality of the studies published in these diverse domains. Then, employing a more detailed qualitative approach, we thematically organized the content of the studies, enabling the identification of results specifically referring to seasonal increases or decreases in traffic accident rates and consistent associations between sleep disturbances and adverse driving outcomes, exploring the associations involving several physiological indicators and outcomes of interest. The analysis began with an examination of the seasonality of traffic accidents, followed by an investigation into the sleep patterns of drivers, and it concluded with an exploration of the factors associated with seasonality and chronobiology in the impairment of drivers' performance and the increase in traffic accidents.

With respect to drivers' sleep patterns, the synthesis focused on information describing drivers' sleep-wake characteristics, including sleep duration, sleep efficiency, daytime sleepiness, circadian misalignment, and drivers' decline in performance. In addition, the analysis included evidence on night-shift work and commuting accidents, particularly in the context of sleep deprivation and circadian-rhythm disturbances associated with seasonal changes.

With regard to the physiological indicators considered in the analysis criteria, we primarily included variables related to circadian rhythm 14 and alertness, 10 such as sleep duration and efficiency, 13 14 self-reported daytime sleepiness, chronotype, psychomotor reaction time, 10 hormonal variations associated with the sleep-wake cycle (such as melatonin and cortisol levels), 12 13 and physiological parameters derived from activity recordings (actigraphy and polysomnography). 20 These indicators were selected for their relevance in understanding the biological mechanisms that may mediate the influence of seasonal factors on the occurrence of traffic accidents and on drivers' health.

Results

This section presents the findings of the scoping review, structured to reflect the study selection process and the thematic synthesis of the included evidence. First, the identification and screening of studies are described according to the PRISMA–ScR framework, followed by an overview of the main characteristics of the selected sources. The results are then organized thematically to summarize the evidence, including environmental, biological, and contextual determinants. Finally, specific aspects, such as seasonal sensitivity and the impact of DST, are addressed, highlighting their potential influence on circadian regulation, alertness, and accident risk.

Study Identification

As shown in Fig. 1 , the search encompassed 1,758 records across all databases. The selection process adhered to the PRISMA–ScR guidelines. Following the removal of duplicates, 1,634 records remained for the evaluation of titles and abstracts. Approximately 103 articles underwent full-text reading, and 40 studies were chosen for synthesis. It should be noted that 31 articles were excluded ( Fig. 1 ) during the full-text screening because they only addressed seasonality or chronobiology in relation to traffic accidents and driver behavior, rather than examining both factors together as central risk elements. Accordingly, nine articles were included in the review because they jointly addressed seasonality and chronobiology in relation to traffic accidents or driver behavior ( Fig. 1 ).

In addition, 14 further works—including reviews, narrative articles, and expert opinion papers—were incorporated into the review to strengthen the conceptual framework and theoretical understanding of seasonal and chronobiological influences, even though they do not present statistical analyses or empirical data on traffic accidents or driver behavior. This categorization enabled a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence and facilitated the identification of thematic patterns relevant to the research objectives.

Seasonality of Urban and Road Traffic Accidents

Impact of School Holidays and Seasonal Tourism

Several studies indicate that traffic accidents tend to increase during periods of school holidays and seasonal tourism, when changes in driving patterns, higher traffic volume, and increased driver fatigue and drowsiness adversely affect driving behavior. Algaahtany 46 analyzed 5,830 medical records of patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI) secondary to traffic accidents in Aseer province, Saudi Arabia, and found that 37% of the cases occurred during the summer months of June, July, and August, coinciding with the holiday season and greater tourism activity. Similarly, another study, 47 from the United States, reported a higher prevalence of accidents during the summer period, which is associated with favorable weather conditions for socialization and leisure, as well as the school vacation period. In both studies, drowsiness was identified as a key biological factor contributing to impaired driver alertness and reduced vigilance while driving, increasing the likelihood of traffic accidents, although the direct relationship with circadian rhythms was not detailed.

Seasonal Trends and Accident Statistics

Large-scale analyses provide further evidence of seasonal patterns in traffic accidents. Pawłowski et al. 45 reported 475,591 traffic accidents in Poland between 2004 and 2014, resulting in 50,217 deaths. Accident frequency was lowest in January and February (winter) and highest in July and August (summer), periods associated with increased traffic exposure and behavioral changes in drivers, including higher driving speeds and reduced attentional control. The primary causes were excessive speed relative to road conditions and failure to respect the right of way. Standard school holiday periods (June–August and December–January) and long celebratory holidays such as Christmas, New Year's, and Easter were associated with higher traffic volumes, which may further elevate the accident risk. 45 47 In Norway, Pokorny et al. 30 found that collisions occurring in winter resulted in less severe injuries compared to other seasons, while summer conditions may encourage riskier driving behavior, particularly higher driving speeds and reduced hazard perception, contributing to more severe traffic accidents..

Contextual and Multifactorial Determinants

Beyond seasonality, other contextual factors contribute to the high incidence of accidents by shaping driver behavior, influencing risk perception, compliance with traffic rules, and the ability to respond effectively to road hazards. Kourouma et al. 31 highlighted the role of limited road safety legislation enforcement, poor road infrastructure, irregular vehicle maintenance, limited access to health care, and lower quality of life as additional determinants of traffic accidents. These findings underscore that, while seasonal and social factors affect accident patterns, multifactorial contextual elements also play a critical role.

Configuration of Roads and Climatic Variations

Chen et al. 36 identified that factors such as road surface conditions, lane width, road alignment, visibility, lighting, and season significantly contributed to the occurrence of traffic accidents in China. They found that adverse seasonal weather conditions require drivers to possess greater skill in maintaining vehicle control under challenging traffic circumstances. 12 14 16 36

Mao et al. 37 highlighted that rear-end collisions, collisions with lamp posts, and rollovers are more likely to occur during the winter due to poor weather conditions such as rain, snow, fog, and hail. Traffic density and peak hours are additional factors that influence the likelihood of accidents.

Although factors such as road infrastructure and traffic-law violations play a major role in the occurrence and severity of crashes, seasonality introduces climatic variations that can further elevate the risk of road accidents. Seasonal changes often bring abrupt shifts in weather, including increased rainfall, fog, snow, or ice, all of which reduce road traction and impair visibility, thereby increasing the danger of driving. 37

Sudden temperature fluctuations can also induce physiological stress, leading to fatigue, slower reaction times, and reduced alertness in drivers. Extreme cold, for instance, may impair cognitive function due to the seasonal sensitivity experienced by some individuals in winter.

Regarding high summer temperatures, Zhang and Hassan 48 found that increasing ambient temperatures correlated with a rise in traffic accidents. Similarly, Wang 49 observed that motor and cognitive performance are negatively impacted by higher temperatures while driving. Extreme heat can induce fatigue and irritability, making drivers less attentive and slower to react.

These environmental factors, coupled with circadian misalignment due to shorter daylight hours, may further increase the risk of accidents by impairing cognitive functions and delaying responses to difficult traffic situations. 6 12 14 16

Age, Drowsiness, and Accidents

Pack et al. 47 have shown that most drivers who stay awake during the day and sleep at night have peak sleepiness at night and in the afternoon. However, age group may influence the preferred time to drive the vehicle. This can be explained by hormonal changes that occur during senescence, such as a decrease in melatonin production. Although the circadian profile of melatonin is preserved in healthy elderly individuals, nighttime levels of this hormone undergo a significant reduction in individuals over 75 years of age. 27 These age-related endocrine changes are accompanied by alterations in sleep architecture, a phase advance in the circadian rhythm, and a diminished circadian modulation of alertness across the 24-hour cycle, which collectively impair sustained attention, vigilance, and reaction time. 28 These changes common to aging can affect sleep and wakefulness patterns, also impacting driver behavior. Furthermore, it is common for them to develop visual disturbances that can limit their vision when driving vehicles at night. 30

For Chen et al, 36 rural or highway roads are more dangerous, in which0 the severity of injuries tends to be greater compared with urban roads. The researchers also noted a higher proportion of deaths caused by speeding, drowsiness while driving, and by novice drivers with fewer than 3 years of experience. Additionally, the predictors of age, season, visibility, lighting conditions, road alignment, and terrain significantly influenced crash outcomes. Moreover, studies focusing on driver characteristics 36 37 suggest that younger and less experienced drivers are particularly vulnerable during periods characterized by reduced physiological alertness and increased sleep propensity, such as late-night and early-morning hours, often engaging in riskier behaviors under conditions of sleep loss and circadian misalignment. This increased vulnerability among younger drivers may be partly explained by limited development of attentional regulation, impulse control, and risk assessment abilities, combined with a greater tendency to underestimate the functional impact of sleepiness when driving under elevated circadian and homeostatic sleep pressure. 36 37 50 51

According to Golombek and Rosenstein, 14 elderly people present sleep-wake cycles in a polyphasic pattern, so that they feel sleepy more than once a day. This pattern can be interpreted as a possible weakening of the circadian timing system, which becomes less efficient in maintaining a regular sleep-wake cycle. Within the framework of the two-process model of sleep regulation, aging has been associated with a reduced effectiveness of the circadian component (process C) in sustaining daytime alertness, together with alterations in sleep continuity that modify the dynamics of the homeostatic sleep drive (process S). 52 In addition to the impairment in endogenous timing mechanisms, the sensory channels and effector pathways of this system are also compromised, further affecting the rhythmicity of the sleep-wake cycle. In general, the elderly population has different sleep patterns compared to an adult, as they sleep early, wake up at dawn, doze off easily, and present an advance in the sleep-wake cycle phase during the aging process. 53

Disruptions in this synchronization process may further compromise the alignment between internal circadian timing and externally-imposed activity schedules, thereby intensifying declines in alertness, cognitive performance, and driving capacity at specific times of day. 27 This temporal adaptation refers to the dynamic adjustment of the endogenous circadian system to external light-dark cycles, enabling the stabilization of sleep-wake timing and daily patterns of alertness and performance. 30

Seasonal Sensitivity

The circadian temporal system is profoundly influenced by environmental factors such as natural light exposure and geographical location. Individuals living in the western portions of a time zone are exposed to later sunrise times compared to those in the eastern portions, which can lead to greater circadian misalignment due to a mismatch between biological rhythms and social schedules. Within the same time zone, individuals living in eastern regions experience earlier sunrise times, which increases the misalignment between solar time and socially-imposed schedules when compared to western regions, a phenomenon commonly described as social jetlag . 54 This circadian misalignment is associated with insufficient sleep duration and impaired sleep-wake regulation, negatively affecting alertness, cognitive performance, and engagement in daily activities. 54 In the context of road safety, reduced alertness represents a critical risk factor for traffic accidents. These aspects can lead to changes in the driver's sleep-wake cycle, compromising their alertness while operating a vehicle. According to Alvarado et al., 15 high-latitude regions, such as southern Chile and northern Germany, may trigger seasonal sensitivity in individuals as a result of seasonal changes in natural. 6 15

In this context, Meesters and Gordijn 9 assert that there is a seasonal pattern of recurrent episodes of depression during autumn and winter, with symptoms spontaneously improving in spring and summer, termed seasonal affective disorder (SAD). Individuals with SAD are more susceptible to the effects of seasonal transitions and suffer from reduced exposure to daylight, which is common in winter, although further studies are needed to fully elucidate all the causes involved.

Seasonal variation in the duration of natural-light exposure, particularly at high latitudes, has been consistently associated with mood dysregulation. 15 Although the term seasonal sensitivity has been used descriptively to refer to individual vulnerability to seasonal light variation, it is not widely adopted in clinical research. Instead, the concept of subsyndromal SAD (S-SAD) is well established in the literature and refers to otherwise healthy individuals who experience mild seasonal depressive symptoms without fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder. 55 56 57 Subsyndromal SAD is considered a marker of vulnerability, identifying populations at increased risk for the development of full affective episodes under seasonal light deprivation. 56

Both S-SAD and SAD are intrinsically linked to reduced availability of natural daylight during winter months, which triggers neuroendocrine dysregulation. This dysregulation is characterized by alterations in melatonin secretion, affecting sleep timing and quality, and serotonin regulation, which is closely related to depressive symptomatology. 9 Epidemiological evidence 58 59 60 indicates that the prevalence of SAD and S-SAD increases significantly with latitude, reinforcing the role of seasonal photoperiod variation in the pathogenesis of these mood disorders.

Impact of seasonality on accidents.

Effect of Cognitive Fatigue and Drowsiness on the Risk of Accidents

Circadian disruptions induced by seasonal factors are increasingly recognized as core features of mood episodes and as critical mechanisms underlying transitions between affective states. 59 60 In the context of driving, cognitive fatigue and excessive daytime sleepiness resulting from circadian dysfunction compromise sustained attention, reaction time, and the ability to respond to unexpected events, thereby increasing drivers' susceptibility to traffic accidents. 61

Kivelä et al. 62 observed a seasonal worsening in mood accompanied by a delay in the sleep phase. These mood changes were correlated with the winter period and were more frequent in individuals with an evening chronotype. The authors argued that evening people are more vulnerable to sleep disorders and are more likely to develop depression.

Bernardi et al. 61 identified that individuals exposed to sleep deprivation or restriction experienced a decrease in brain electrical activity during wakefulness. This finding was associated with symptoms of cognitive fatigue, as well as episodes of impulsivity and/or behavioral apathy. In the most extreme cases, changes in the participants' functional performance were observed. Circadian changes can give rise to various health disorders, including metabolic, musculoskeletal, and cardiovascular diseases. Furthermore, cognitive performance is impaired, reducing concentration and the ability to respond to unexpected events, which can increase drivers' susceptibility to accidents. 10 29 34 63 64 65

Environmental and physiological variations marked at different times of the year guide how seasonal factors affect drivers' abilities and states of attention, and what are the causes associated with seasonality and traffic accidents. 47 66 Despite consistent evidence 61 linking seasonal light variation, circadian disruption, and impaired cognitive functioning, the direct causal relationship between seasonality, circadian misalignment, and the occurrence of traffic accidents remains insufficiently established and requires further investigation.

Daylight Saving Time and Traffic Accidents

In some countries that adopt DST, such as the United States, a significant increase in traffic accidents was identified 6 in the week following the switch over to the new time. This may be related to the earlier wake-up time, as experienced by day-shift workers, who need to wake up an hour earlier than usual.

The results found by Fritz et al. 6 showed that the transition to DST increases the risk of fatal traffic accidents by approximately 6% in the first week after the time transition. This increased prevalence of events was observed predominantly in the morning, given the data pertain to time zones in western regions. These findings can be partially attributed to sleep deprivation and circadian mismatch. Similarly, Robb and Barnes 67 observed that traffic accidents increased in the first few days after the change from standard time to DST. The authors found that traffic accidents in New Zealand increased by over 16% between 2005 and 2016.

The analysis of the impact of DST on road safety has deepened the distinction between the effects of sleep deprivation and the reorganization of ambient light conditions. 68 In a comprehensive study in the United States, Woods et al. 69 observed that the transition to DST in the spring resulted in a 12% increase in fatal crashes involving motor-vehicle occupants, but, conversely, a 24% reduction in fatal crashes involving pedestrians and cyclists. The opposite effect was observed in the autumn transition. The authors 69 concluded that these differential effects depend on the type of road user and are strongly linked to ambient light conditions at the time of the crash, with the light mechanism being more prominent for pedestrians and cyclists. Corroborating this perspective, Laliotis et al., 70 in a study in Greece, also supported the ambient light mechanism, which reduces serious accidents in the spring transition and increases minor accidents in the autumn transition, with the effects concentrated in the time intervals most affected by the change.

Despite the complexity of the mechanisms, driving drowsiness remains a crucial risk factor. 71 72 Orsini et al. 71 conducted a pilot study with a driving simulator and demonstrated a considerable rise in driving-fatigue levels in young male drivers in the week after the spring transition to DST. This fatigue was objectively documented by a worsening in vehicle lateral control and an increase in eyelid closure, even though participants did not perceive the decrease in their state of alertness. 71 In a study in Chile, Gillmore 68 found suggestive evidence that the sleep-disruption effect increases automobile accidents by 6% in the first week after the transition to DST, although the overall effect of the DST regime in the country was a 2.7% reduction in accidents. 68

According to Roenneberg et al., 73 the first few days after the sudden change to DST in spring have acute effects, including shorter sleep duration, increased cardiovascular disorders when compared to other times, and higher risk of accidents. Chronic effects, however, can last for months, for social clocks (set by human socialization) and biological clocks (set by exposure to sunlight) can remain out of step with each other for many people.

Circadian-rhythm changes may occur due to the amount of time people are exposed to natural or artificial light. 11 Delays in sleep onset, for example, can occur with increased exposure to light in the afternoon and less exposure in the morning. The opposite can also occur, when more light in the morning and less in the afternoon promote an advance in the sleep phase. 74 The disruption caused by the springtime transition is particularly severe because it forces a phase advance of the circadian rhythm. In occupational health and shift-work studies, 75 76 77 forward rotation (moving schedules to a later time) is generally better tolerated by the human biological clock, which has a natural endogenous period slightly longer than 24 hours. By contrast, the start of DST imposes a backward or counter-clockwise shift, requiring individuals to wake up earlier than their internal clocks dictate. This forced phase advance leads to significant circadian misalignment and acute sleep debt, which are primary contributors to the observed spike in traffic accidents during the first week of the transition. 73 75

When DST starts, most of the population is forced to go to bed earlier while still following their daily routine, such as always waking up and going to work at the same time. Otherwise, people would lose an hour of their usual sleep at the start of DST. 78 Thus, a portion of the population starts getting up before sunrise, reducing the duration of exposure to natural light in the morning. This situation can trigger a mismatch in the sleep-wake cycle and alter the production of melatonin in the body, leading to sleep deprivation and a state of drowsiness in the first few weeks following DST. 73 79

Daylight saving time increases the discrepancy between the solar clock and the social clock; therefore, it will also increase the discrepancy between the biological clock and the social clock. This can generate mood disorders, or personality disorders, also increasing the risk of metabolic disorders, such as obesity and metabolic syndrome. 73 79

For Johnson and Malow, 80 the neuropsychological effects on memory, learning, judgment, attention, and other processes have important impacts that can cause DST to worsen people's cognitive performance. These researchers found that DST is associated with circadian disruption, which affects people in the short term after the time transitions and in the long term during DST. Sleep loss and/or poor sleep quality occur after the transition to DST and persist throughout this period.

In the study by Orsini et al., 19 which was conducted in Italy, drivers underwent performance tests in the week before and after DST. The authors found negative short-term effects in the week after the start of DST, such as increased drowsiness and fatigue in the drivers. The increase in fatigue while driving persisted in the fourth week after the transition with the same magnitude, indicating that the negative effects after the start of DST may persist in the long term.

When resynchronization fails to occur effectively, the individual may manifest changes in circadian rhythms or sleep disorders. Thus, drowsiness increases driver vulnerability to commuting accidents, as workers are deprived of the ideal sleep time and/or quality of sleep for their circadian rhythm. 32 66 81 82

Drowsiness in drivers or anyone else may be related to changes or disturbances in the circadian rhythm, triggered by irregularities in the sleep-wake pattern and socialization. 83 84 These endogenous variations can occur in response to sudden changes in waking up and/or working times, impacting biological rhythms and influencing sleep time and quality. 33 62

Discussion

The findings synthesized in the current scoping review, as summarized in Tables 2 3 , indicate that traffic accidents are not randomly distributed over time, but are strongly modulated by seasonal and chronobiological factors that influence driver behavior, alertness, and physiological functioning. Evidence 6 7 12 15 19 36 37 44 45 demonstrates that variations in photoperiod, circadian alignment, and sleep duration and quality play a central role in shaping accident risk throughout the year and across the 24-hour cycle.

A prominent topic emerging from the reviewed literature is the impact of DST transitions on driver performance and accident risk. A population-based cohort study 6 reported a significant increase in fatal traffic accidents following the spring transition to DST, a period characterized by acute sleep loss and circadian misalignment. Complementarily, experimental and observational studies 19 44 have demonstrated increased driving fatigue and impaired performance in the weeks following DST transitions, with effects persisting beyond the immediate posttransition period. This body of evidence 6 19 44 aligns with chronobiological research, 12 44 indicating that even modest shifts in the sleep-wake schedule can have disproportionate effects on alertness, reaction time, and risk-related driving behavior, particularly among individuals with evening chronotypes.

Cruz et al. 85 concluded that the ability of the human circadian timing system to adjust to seasonal oscillation was disrupted by the DST-related 1-hour loss in spring. According to the authors, people with an evening chronotype—those who prefer to go to bed at a later time than most of the population and who also wake up later—may be affected to a greater degree. For these individuals, it is much more difficult to wake up 1 hour earlier than usual, and resynchronization may take longer due to their behavioral profile. Conversely, people with a morning chronotype are less affected, as they normally fall asleep and wake up earlier, and their body adapts better to earlier wake-up times.

Corroborating this information, Alencar et al., 44 in a study involving 12,467 volunteers in Brazil, found that 25% of these individuals, most with an evening chronotype, reported discomfort or changes in sleep throughout DST. The researchers concluded that DST in low-latitude locations can be disruptive to circadian rhythms. Additionally, they noted that seasonal fluctuations in sunrise, common in countries far from the equator, have a greater impact on sleep duration and quality.

Seasonality also emerges as a critical determinant of accident patterns. Epidemiological studies 37 45 have reported higher accident frequencies during summer months, likely reflecting increased traffic volume, extended daylight exposure, and seasonal changes in social behavior. In contrast, observational studies conducted in colder climates 7 36 have indicated that winter conditions are associated with greater accident severity, largely due to adverse weather, reduced visibility, and seasonal physiological changes. At the biological level, experimental and cohort studies 7 12 have provided evidence that seasonal fluctuations in hormones such as melatonin, cortisol, and thyroid hormones contribute to circadian instability, sleep disturbances, and reduced cognitive performance, thereby indirectly increasing driving risk.

Chronobiological vulnerability related to time of day is another recurring finding across the literature. Longitudinal and cross-sectional studies 36 37 45 have demonstrated an increased risk of traffic accidents during evening and nighttime hours, particularly between late evening and early morning, when circadian sleep propensity peaks and alertness declines. Empirical evidence 12 19 37 has consistently identified drowsy driving, sleep deprivation, and accumulated sleep debt as major contributors to accident risk, especially among shift workers and individuals exposed to irregular work schedules.

Notably, despite the growing body of literature addressing seasonality and chronobiology independently, there remains a clear scarcity of studies explicitly integrating these two dimensions within a unified analytical framework. Most investigations focus either on seasonal patterns (such as, month, season, or daylight variation) or on chronobiological factors (such as, time of day, sleep deprivation, or circadian misalignment), but few studies 36 45 simultaneously examine how seasonal changes in photoperiod and environmental exposure interact with endogenous circadian processes to amplify accident risk. This limited integration constrains the ability to identify periods of compounded vulnerability—such as specific seasons combined with circadian low points—, thereby restricting a more precise characterization of high-risk temporal windows for traffic accidents.

Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of integrating chronobiological principles into traffic safety research and policy. Evidence from intervention-focused studies 6 19 indicates that strategies addressing only infrastructure or enforcement may be insufficient if temporal vulnerabilities related to sleep, circadian rhythms, and seasonal transitions are not simultaneously addressed. Research findings 12 19 support the implementation of targeted awareness campaigns during high-risk periods—such as DST transitions, winter months, and nighttime hours—, as well as policies that account for work schedules, shift work, and commuting patterns, particularly for professional drivers. The findings herein synthesized have important implications for traffic safety and prevention strategies, particularly those addressing temporal risk factors. Rather than adopting uniform, year-round interventions, the evidence supports the implementation of time-sensitive and seasonally-tailored measures. Targeted public awareness campaigns, for example, could be intensified during high-risk periods, such as the weeks following DST transitions, winter months with reduced photoperiod, and nighttime hours characterized by elevated circadian sleep pressure. 86 87

From an occupational health perspective, adjusting work schedules, rest periods, and shift rotations—especially for professional drivers and shift workers—may help mitigate circadian misalignment and accumulated sleep debt. Infrastructure-based interventions, such as enhanced road lighting during winter months and early morning hours, as well as increased enforcement during identified temporal-vulnerability windows, may further reduce the accident risk. Importantly, integrating chronobiological principles into driver education programs could increase awareness of drowsy driving and promote adaptive behaviors, such as strategic napping and avoidance of high-risk driving times. 88 89

Despite the overall robustness of the evidence, there are limitations that must be acknowledged when interpreting the findings herein synthesized. First, a substantial proportion of the included studies were based on cross-sectional or ecological designs, which limits causal inference regarding the temporal relationship involving seasonal factors, circadian disruption, and traffic accidents. Second, considerable heterogeneity was observed in the operationalization of key exposures, including seasonality (such as season, month, or photoperiod), circadian rhythm-related variables (such as time of day, chronotype, or sleep duration), and outcome definitions (such as the total of accidents, fatal accidents, or injury severity), which limits direct comparability among studies. Furthermore, evidence 36 45 shows that relatively few investigations simultaneously integrate environmental, biological, and behavioral factors into a single analytical framework, and that most available data originate from high-income countries, limiting generalizability to other contexts.

Additionally, many investigations lacked objective sleep or circadian markers, relying instead on self-reported measures that are susceptible to recall bias. The geographic concentration of studies in high-income countries further limits the generalizability of the findings to low- and middle-income settings, where traffic systems, work schedules, and environmental exposures may differ substantially. Finally, few studies explicitly modeled interactions between seasonal and circadian dimensions, restricting the ability to identify periods of compounded temporal vulnerability.

In summary, the overall body of evidence synthesized in the present scoping review 6 7 12 15 19 36 37 44 45 supports a multifactorial and temporally-sensitive model of traffic-accident risk, in which seasonality, circadian rhythms, sleep-wake patterns, and social timing jointly influence driver behavior and safety outcomes. The findings 6 7 12 15 19 36 37 44 45 highlight the need for future longitudinal studies using standardized chronobiological measures and integrative analytical models capable of capturing the complex interactions involving biological timing, environmental exposure, and human behavior in traffic systems.

Beyond synthesizing existing evidence, the current scoping review provides added value by explicitly framing traffic accidents within an integrated seasonal and chronobiological perspective. Unlike previous reviews that address either seasonality or circadian factors in isolation, the present study highlights how environmental timing (such as photoperiod, season, and daylight transitions) and endogenous biological rhythms jointly shape driver alertness, behavior, and the accident risk. This integrative temporal framework enbables the identification of periods of compounded vulnerability that may not be evident when these dimensions are examined separately.

By organizing and critically appraising the literature across biological, environmental, and behavioral domains, the present review offers a conceptual foundation for future longitudinal and intervention-based research. Furthermore, it provides actionable insights for policymakers, traffic-safety authorities, and occupational health practitioners seeking to design prevention strategies that account for human biological timing. In this sense, the study advances the field by moving beyond descriptive seasonality analyses toward a more mechanistic and temporally-informed understanding of traffic accident risk.

Final Remarks

A preliminary survey of scientific publications revealed evidence supporting the study hypothesis, that is, that seasonal patterns disrupt biological rhythms and affect driver performance. This effect can exert a significant influence on the occurrence of traffic crashes, in urban areas and on highways.

People tend to engage in different activities depending on season. During the summer, for example, the population tends to go out more for outdoor activities, vacation trips, parties, in addition to other specific events. Thus, the high flow of vehicles can increase the chance of collisions. In winter, however, people are less exposed to natural light, which can promote circadian changes, causing drowsiness when driving a vehicle. These factors can lead to different risks and, consequently, increase the probability of crashes.

Understanding the seasonality associated with traffic crashes plays a crucial role in providing valuable information for the implementation of more effective prevention strategies and for proper allocation of government resources. In agreement with the study by Chen et al., 36 the present investigation highlights the importance of basing crash-prevention measures on the characteristics that affect fatal crashes, particularly the specific seasonal pattern of each region.

Furthermore, it is essential to consider the particularities of each location and adapt prevention strategies according to local traffic conditions. This approach can help reduce the number of traffic crashes and their devastating consequences, such as deaths and serious injuries, while promoting safer traffic conditions and protecting public health.
