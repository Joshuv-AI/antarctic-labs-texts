The Moderating Effect of Physical Activity Between IQOS Smoking and Ventricular Repolarization Parameters in Lebanese University Students
Authors: Jean Khalil, Lucia Helou, Maria Ibrahim, Nicolas Nakhle, Mikhael Kossaify, Talal Harb, Souheil Hallit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCigarette smoking is a major modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular morbidity and sudden cardiac death, in part through abnormalities in ventricular repolarization. However, little data are available on other tobacco products and alternative tobacco use delivery devices, such as heated tobacco products, as well as the modifying impact of physical activity, on repolarization among young adults.ObjectiveTo assess the association between dependence on cigarettes, waterpipe and heated tobacco products and indices of ventricular repolarization among a sample of Lebanese university students and to examine the moderating effect of physical activity on such an association.MethodsEighty‐one Lebanese university students participated in this observational cross‐sectional study (mean age 21.3 ± 1.7 years). Tobacco, waterpipe and heated tobacco product dependence were assessed with the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND), the Lebanese Waterpipe Dependence Scale (LWDS), and the Heated Tobacco Products Addiction Questionnaire (HeaTPAQ) respectively. Physical activity were also evaluated using the physical activity index (PAI). Standard resting 12‐lead electrocardiograms were recorded, and Tp‐e interval, Tp‐e/QT and Tp‐e/QTc ratios were measured. Bivariate correlations and multivariable linear regression models, as well as moderation analyses were performed.ResultsDependence on cigarettes, waterpipe and heated tobacco products were significantly associated with higher Tp‐e interval values and higher Tp‐e/QT and Tp‐e/QTc ratios. Male sex was associated with higher repolarization indices, while female participants had lower values. Physical activity significantly moderated the association between dependence on heated tobacco products and repolarization indices in moderation analyses. Higher dependence on heated tobacco products was associated with higher Tp‐e based indices at low levels of physical activity. This association was no longer significant at moderate and high levels of physical activity. No significant differences were found in QT or QTc intervals.ConclusionDependence on cigarettes, waterpipe and heated tobacco products is associated with higher ventricular repolarization heterogeneity in young adults, even in the absence of conventional QT abnormalities. Physical activity may attenuate the negative association between dependence on heated tobacco products and repolarization indices. Such findings may indicate the early presence of subclinical electrophysiologic risk and suggest that Tp‐e based indices should be further assessed and used in future studies, while also highlighting the role of both nicotine dependence and lifestyle behaviors in young populations.Keywords: QT interval, smoking, Tpe interval, ventricular repolarization

Introduction

Tobacco use is among the leading preventable causes of morbidity and premature mortality worldwide, largely through cardiovascular and cardiometabolic mechanisms [1]. In general, nicotine and other toxicants in combustible and non‐combustible tobacco products appear to accelerate various pathophysiologic processes linked to atherosclerosis, such as endothelial dysfunction, autonomic imbalance, oxidative stress, inflammation, and pro‐thrombotic processes [2]. As combustible cigarette smoking has been the main focus of past cardiovascular‐oriented tobacco research, the marketplace has become more complex and diverse in recent years [3, 4]. Waterpipe (hookah/shisha) and heated tobacco products (HTPs) represet growing alternatives to cigarettes which now attract young adults and adolescents in several areas including the Eastern Mediterranean [4, 5, 6, 7]. However, product diversification has sometimes obscured the overall risk profile because many users, including young users, perceive non‐cigarette products as inherently safer or less harmful than cigarettes despite often being exposed to nicotine and other hazardous constituents [8]. In Lebanon, previous research suggests high levels of tobacco use among youth and young adults, with waterpipe use especially common and sometimes socially normalized [9]. The initiation of tobacco use at an early age is a particular concern, given that patterns of product use frequently persist into adulthood and increase cumulative lifetime exposure [10]. Heated tobacco products such as IQOS represent a newer generation of products that heat processed tobacco sticks to generate an inhalable aerosol without the high‐temperature combustion of cigarettes [11]. While HTPs such as IQOS may have reduced some combustion‐derived toxicants compared with cigarettes, their aerosol is not without nicotine or other constituents with potential cardiovascular consequences [12]. Experimental data have demonstrated that IQOS exposure can induce short‐term cardiovascular effects (tachycardia, hypertension, etc.), vascular endothelial function impairment and arterial stiffness [13, 14]. [14] Due to efficient nicotine delivery, dependence can develop and could be a driver of frequent use, dual use, or escalation over time [15]. In situations where IQOS uptake is emerging in the youth, characterizing cardiovascular electrophysiologic correlates is becoming of great interest [16].

Cardiac electrophysiology could provide noninvasive markers that may be sensitive to early changes in myocardial function that could be present even in otherwise healthy and young populations [17]. The surface electrocardiogram (ECG) is a reflection of global depolarization and repolarization processes, and specific intervals could be used as surrogates for ventricular repolarization heterogeneity [18]. The QT interval duration represents the time elapsed from ventricular depolarization onset to the end of repolarization, with several modulators (heart rate, autonomic tone, etc.) affecting its length [19]. Because QT duration is heart rate‐dependent, the corrected QT (QTc) is usually employed to standardize repolarization assessment between individuals and conditions [20]. Beyond QT/QTc, more recently studied indices have been focusing on the transmural dispersion of repolarization, which is pathophysiologically implicated in ventricular arrhythmogenesis [21]. The Tpeak–Tend (Tp‐e) interval duration, measured from the peak of the T wave to its end, has been proposed as an ECG marker of repolarization dispersion [22]. Prolonged Tp‐e has been associated with increased arrhythmic vulnerability in several clinical settings, including individuals with structurally normal hearts and those with disease [23]. Tp‐e/QT and Tp‐e/QTc ratios are used to contextualize Tp‐e with respect to overall repolarization duration and could be more useful in capturing dispersion that is independent of baseline QT length [24]. These repolarization indices could be of interest in young tobacco users because subclinical autonomic and vascular function changes could translate into detectable electrophysiologic abnormalities [25]. Detecting repolarization differences early could be important for primary prevention, especially in situations where exposure prevalence is high.

Ventricular repolarization parameters could vary from one individual to another due to a wide range of biological, developmental, and contextual factors [26]. Age could influence QT/QTc and related indices throughout adolescence and young adulthood [27]. Sex differences are well‐described for QT/QTc, with females demonstrating longer QTc than males after puberty, potentially related to hormonal effects on cardiac ion channels [28]. Body composition and anthropometric factors such as body mass index (BMI) have also been associated with repolarization measures through their potential effects on autonomic balance, inflammation, and metabolic signaling [29]. Socioeconomic and educational factors could indirectly relate to repolarization through differences in lifestyle behaviors, stress exposure, sleep patterns, and cardiometabolic health [30, 31]. Therefore, most electrophysiologic studies usually consider sociodemographic and clinical covariates to reduce confounding and improve interpretability of tobacco–ECG associations [32].

A growing body of literature suggests that tobacco exposure could be associated with altered cardiac autonomic regulation and measurable ECG changes [33]. Cigarette smoking enhances sympathetic activity and inflammatory pathways, which can alter heart rate, disrupt ventricular repolarization, and contribute to electrophysiologic instability over time [34, 35]. In adolescents and young adults, smoking has been linked to prolonged Tp‐e intervals and higher Tp‐e/QT ratios, indicating increased repolarization heterogeneity despite the absence of overt cardiovascular disease [24]. Such findings are clinically relevant because markers of repolarization dispersion have been associated with ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death risk in diverse populations [24]. Waterpipe smoking has also been associated with acute hemodynamic changes and adverse cardiometabolic profiles, which could plausibly extend to electrophysiologic effects [36]. However, evidence directly examining waterpipe use and ventricular repolarization indices in young, generally healthy cohorts remains comparatively limited. For HTPs, the evidence base is newer and still emerging, with early studies pointing to cardiovascular responses that could resemble those from cigarettes in the short term [37]. Because IQOS use could occur alone or in addition to other tobacco products, separating HTP electrophysiologic effects from dual‐use patterns is an important methodological consideration [38]. Overall, existing studies support a plausible association between tobacco exposure and repolarization markers, but gaps remain in product‐specific effects, dependence‐related gradients, and contextual modifiers.

Alongside smoking, physical activity is a major lifestyle determinant of cardiovascular function and warrants specific consideration. It encompasses any skeletal muscle movement that increases energy expenditure and includes aerobic, resistance, lifestyle, and sports activities, each associated with distinct physiological adaptations [39, 40, 41]. In many studies, physically active individuals demonstrate ECG patterns consistent with enhanced parasympathetic modulation, though adaptations could depend on training load and individual characteristics [42]. Physical activity could relate to QT/QTc and repolarization dispersion through effects on ion channel expression, myocardial remodeling, autonomic regulation, and metabolic health [43]. Interestingly, there is some evidence that fitness might be associated with a more stable repolarization profile and therefore a decreased arrhythmia susceptibility in certain settings [44]. However, it is important to note that extreme endurance training or even an underlying predisposition might play a role for arrhythmia risk in specific subsets as well [45]. In young adult populations, the net effect of habitual moderate physical activity is generally considered cardioprotective and could buffer physiologic stressors that are linked to arrhythmogenesis [46].

University students represent a critical population for tobacco research because they are in an “emerging adulthood” period marked by identity formation, social influence, and health behaviors adoption [47, 48]. Within this age range, experimentation with cigarettes, waterpipe, and newer products such as IQOS could increase due to peer networks, marketing exposure, and perceived harm reduction narratives [4, 49]. Lebanon provides a particularly relevant context given reported high prevalence of tobacco use and the cultural acceptability of waterpipe in social settings [7, 48, 50]. Studying Lebanese university students could therefore capture contemporary patterns of single and dual product use, including dependence features that may not be well represented in older cohorts. Because many university students have few diagnosed comorbidities, any observed ECG differences may reflect early subclinical effects rather than advanced disease. This population also allows for the examination of lifestyle moderators such as physical activity and stress, which are central in student life and could influence autonomic tone [51]. Thus, Lebanese university students offer a valuable opportunity to examine early electrophysiologic correlates of tobacco product dependence in a real‐world, high‐exposure environment.

Dependence severity could act as a proxy for intensity and frequency of nicotine exposure, which could in turn drive physiologic changes that are relevant to repolarization [52]. Cumulative exposures in nicotine‐containing products could further promote sympathetic activation and imbalance of autonomic tone, which has the potential to further prolong repolarization heterogeneity [53]. Simultaneously, physical activity can influence autonomic function, vascular health, and inflammatory status, which have all been implicated in repolarization stability [54]. It is therefore plausible that physical activity modifies the association between IQOS dependence and repolarization indices by buffering adverse autonomic or vascular effects of nicotine exposure. Testing physical activity as a moderator provides a clinically meaningful framing because it is a modifiable exposure that could inform prevention strategies even when tobacco cessation is not immediately achieved.

In light of the increasing rates of waterpipe and new IQOS use in the Lebanese adolescent population, the need to study their impact, as well as that of cigarette smoking, on repolarization indices is of high importance. The current study will assess Tp‐e interval and Tp‐e/QT and Tp‐e/QTc ratios in Lebanese adolescent smokers (cigarettes, waterpipe or IQOS) versus non‐smokers to see if early electrophysiological changes that can predispose to future cardiovascular events can be identified and evaluates whether physical activity moderates the relationship between IQOS dependence and repolarization factors.

Methods

Study Design and Ethical Considerations

This observational cross‐sectional study was conducted among Lebanese university students between October and November 2025. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Holy Spirit University of Kaslik. Participation in the study was completely voluntary, and all participants provided electronic informed consent prior to enrollment. Confidentiality and anonymity of all participants were strictly ensured. The study population included healthy Lebanese university students aged 18 years old and older. Inclusion criteria for study enrollment were being a Lebanese university student and providing digital informed consent for participation.

Non‐smokers were defined as participants who had never smoked cigarettes, waterpipe, heated tobacco products, or other nicotine‐containing products. Tobacco users were defined as participants with current use of at least one tobacco product with a minimum use duration of 1 year prior to enrolment in the study. Cigarette smokers were defined as participants who smoked more than three cigarettes per day for at least 1 year, as defined in previous studies [24].

Exclusion criteria were the presence of a history of congenital or acquired heart disease, rheumatic valvular heart disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, thyroid dysfunction, or any known systemic disease. In addition, participants were excluded if they were currently taking any medication that could affect cardiac conduction or ventricular repolarization (e.g., antidepressants, macrolide antibiotics, antihistamines, or antiarrhythmic agents). Finally, to reduce the acute effects of nicotine on cardiac electrophysiology, all participants were asked to refrain from cigarette smoking, waterpipe use, and heated tobacco product use for at least 30 min before ECG recording.

Minimal Sample Size Calculation and Population

The minimal sample size needed to detect an expected effect was estimated a priori. Sample size calculations were performed with G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). The estimates were based on detecting differences in ventricular repolarization indices between smokers and non‐smokers, with means and standard deviations used in a previously published study that evaluated the impact of cigarette smoking on ventricular repolarization in adolescents by Kayalı and Demir [24]. In that study, the Tp‐e interval was significantly higher in smokers than non‐smokers.

Using these parameters, a paired t‐test model was used with a two‐tailed hypothesis, alpha of 0.05, and statistical power of 90%. Based on these parameters, the estimated effect size was 1.97, with a minimum required total sample size of 14 participants. Given that this was an observational study design with the inclusion of multiple tobacco products, adjustment for potential confounders, and the use of moderation analyses, a total of 81 participants were ultimately included in this study.

Questionnaire and Data Collection

Data were collected using a self‐administered online questionnaire, which was prepared in Arabic and administered using Google Forms. The questionnaire took approximately 5–10 min to complete and included questions assessing sociodemographic characteristics, tobacco use and dependence (cigarettes, waterpipe, and heated tobacco products), alcohol consumption, physical activity, and psychological stress. Content of the questionnaire is provided below.

Sociodemographic Characteristics

Participants provided information on age, sex, marital status, academic specialization (scientific or non‐scientific), and household characteristics. Household crowding index was calculated as the number of persons living in the household divided by the number of rooms (excluding kitchens and bathrooms) and was used as a proxy indicator of socioeconomic status [55].

A composite physical activity index (PAI), validated in Arabic, was used to assess the intensity, duration, and frequency of physical activity. Participants were asked to choose the answer that best represented their usual activity level for each of the three dimensions of the PAI [56].

Activity intensity was assessed from light activity to vigorous activity that caused heavy breathing and sweating. Duration was measured by the average length of time for each activity, from less than 10 min to more than 30 min. Frequency was assessed by how often participants performed physical activity, from less than once a month to daily/almost daily activity. Responses for each of the three dimensions were multiplied to generate a continuous physical activity index with higher scores representing higher habitual physical activity [56].

Cigarette Smoking and Nicotine Dependence

Cigarette smoking status was assessed by asking participants if they currently smoked cigarettes. Current smokers were also asked to report their average daily number of cigarettes smoked, age of smoking initiation, and duration of smoking in years. Time to first cigarette after waking was also assessed.

Nicotine dependence among current smokers was assessed using the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND), validated in Arabic, which consists of six items assessing time to first cigarette, number of cigarettes smoked per day, difficulty refraining from smoking in places where smoking is forbidden, the cigarette most difficult to give up, smoking if ill and having cough and cold, and smoking pattern throughout the day [57, 58]. Items are summed to yield a total FTND score, with higher scores indicating higher nicotine dependence.

Waterpipe Smoking and Dependence

Waterpipe smoking status was assessed by asking participants if they currently smoked waterpipe. Frequency of use was assessed by the number of waterpipe sessions per week, duration of waterpipe smoking in years, and the average duration of each session in hours.

Dependence on waterpipe smoking was assessed using the Lebanese Waterpipe Dependence Scale (LWDS) [59]. The LWDS contains 11 items that assess both behavioral and psychological dependence through items related to smoking alone, smoking when ill or bedridden, difficulty abstaining, time to first craving, failed quit attempts, financial sacrifice, and motivation for smoking (e.g., pleasure, stress relief, social reasons) [59]. Responses were scored according to standard LWDS scoring guidelines, with higher scores indicating higher waterpipe dependence.

Heated Tobacco Products and Electronic Cigarette Dependence

Participants were asked if they used heated tobacco products or electronic cigarettes. If the answer was yes, participants were asked to report daily consumption and duration of use in years.

Dependence on heated tobacco products was measured using the Heated Tobacco Products Addiction Questionnaire (HeaTPAQ), which contains 13 items that evaluate craving, tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, loss of control, continued use despite health or social problems, difficulty abstaining in restricted places, and unsuccessful quit attempts [4]. Responses were recorded on a Likert scale with responses ranging from strong disagreement to strong agreement and total scores were calculated, with higher scores indicating higher dependence.

Electrocardiographic Recording and Analysis

A standard 12‐lead electrocardiogram (ECG) was recorded for each participant under resting conditions in a calm and quiet environment on USEK Kaslik campus. Electrocardiograms were recorded in the supine position with the use of ECG machine (Model No: GE HEALTHCARE MAC 1200). The speed and voltage of all recordings were set at 25 mm/s and 10 mm/mV, respectively. Electrocardiograms were digitized and interpreted manually by a PGY‐2 resident specializing in cardiology, who was blinded to the tobacco use status of study participants to minimize inter‐observer bias. The PR interval was measured from the start of the P wave to the beginning of the QRS complex. The QT interval was measured from the beginning of the QRS complex to the end of the T wave. The Tp‐e interval was measured as the distance from the peak of the T wave to the point where the downslope of the T wave intersects the isoelectric line. All measurements were made in lead V5, and the mean value of three consecutive cardiac cycles was used for analysis. The QT interval was corrected for heart rate using Bazett's formula (QTc = QT/√RR). The Tp‐e/QT and Tp‐e/QTc ratios were calculated as indices of ventricular repolarization heterogeneity.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS v.27 was used for the statistical analysis. The normality of the electrical parameters was considered normally distributed since the skewness and kurtosis values varied between ‐1.96 and +1.96. The Student t test was used to compare two means, and the Pearson's test to correlate two continuous variables. Linear regressions were conducted to assess factors associated with each electrical parameter. The moderation analysis was performed using PROCESS MACRO v.4.2 Model 1. Interaction term between HeaTPAQ dependence and each electric parameter was examined at –1 SD, the mean and +1 SD of the moderator (physical activity). Factors showing p values < 0.250 were taken as confounders in the multivariable models. Significance was considered for a p value < 0.05.

Results

The study population consisted of 81 Lebanese university students. The sample comprised 60 males (74.1%) and 21 females (25.9%), with a mean age of 21.30 ± 1.74 years. Most participants were single (98.8%). Of the study population, 38 were non smokers, 37 were current cigarette smokers (45.7%), 18 were current waterpipe smokers (22.2%) and 16 were current heated tobacco products users (19.8%).

Discussion

In the present study, ventricular repolarization indices were found to be closely associated with tobacco product dependence and lifestyle factors in Lebanese university students. The results revealed higher Tp‐e interval and increased Tp‐e/QT and Tp‐e/QTc ratios were observed in association with greater dependence on cigarettes, waterpipe, and heated tobacco products, while no significant differences were detected in conventional QT or QTc intervals. Importantly, physical activity moderated the relationship between heated tobacco product dependence and ventricular repolarization, such that higher dependence was associated with adverse repolarization indices at low levels of physical activity, but not at moderate or high levels of activity.

A central finding of this study is the strong positive correlation between Tp‐e interval, Tp‐e/QT, and Tp‐e/QTc ratios and dependence scores across multiple tobacco products, including waterpipe (LWDS), and heated tobacco products (HeaTPAQ). These findings suggest that greater dependence severity, rather than product use alone, is an important determinant of electrophysiologic changes. The observed association between smoking‐related dependence and increased repolarization dispersion is consistent with prior studies in adolescents and young adults demonstrating prolonged Tp‐e interval and elevated Tp‐e/QT and Tp‐e/QTc ratios among cigarette smokers compared with non‐smokers [24]. These repolarization markers have been linked to a higher risk of ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death, even in the absence of structural heart disease [60]. Extending the current literature, the present study suggests that a similar electrophysiologic profile may be present in waterpipe and heated tobacco product users and that, therefore, alternative tobacco products may not be free from electrophysiologic cardiovascular adverse effects. Notably, the strong and highly significant relationship between repolarization indices and HeaTPAQ dependence underlines the relevance of such heated tobacco products as IQOS. The current findings add to this emerging body of literature by suggesting that greater dependence on heated tobacco products is associated with electrophysiologic indices of repolarization heterogeneity, even in young adults, and may represent an early and subclinical effect.

Our results showed that BMI was not associated with ventricular repolarization indices. In contrast, prior research has demonstrated that systemic cardiovascular risk factors have an impact on ventricular repolarization characteristics. According to some research, obese patients had noticeably longer Tp–e intervals, QTcs, and elevated Tp–e/QT ratios, which suggests a higher risk of arrhythmia and repolarization heterogeneity [61]. Similar ECG alterations have been described in lone obesity, where increased P‐wave duration and dispersion suggest early electrical remodeling in the absence of overt cardiovascular disease [62]. However, another study did not find a significant correlation between the severity of coronary artery disease as measured by the SYNTAX score and ventricular repolarization indices. This suggests that functional or systemic factors, rather than angiographic burden, may be more responsible for repolarization abnormalities [63]. The non‐significant results in our study may be attributed to the low number of participants recruited, with a normal BMI mean value (25.00 kg/m2).

One of the most clinically relevant findings of this study is that physical activity moderated the relationship between IQOS dependence and ventricular repolarization indices. Specifically, higher IQOS dependence was associated with increased Tp‐e interval and Tp‐e/QT and Tp‐e/QTc ratios only among individuals with low levels of physical activity, whereas this association was no longer significant at moderate or high levels of activity. This finding supports the hypothesis that physical activity may exert a protective or buffering effect against nicotine‐related electrophysiologic alterations.

The absence of a significant association between IQOS dependence and repolarization indices at moderate and high activity levels suggests that physical activity may mitigate, but not necessarily eliminate, the electrophysiologic impact of heated tobacco product use. Importantly, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that physical activity renders IQOS use safe. Rather, it suggests that lifestyle factors such as physical activity may influence the extent to which nicotine‐related exposures translate into measurable electrophysiologic changes.

Clinical Implications

The present findings are of high relevance in the Lebanese setting, in which tobacco use, including waterpipe and increasingly heated tobacco products, is highly prevalent among young adults. University students represent a population in which nicotine dependence and physical inactivity may co‐exist, potentially compounding cardiovascular risk. The presence of subclinical repolarization abnormalities in this population highlights the importance of early prevention and targeted health promotion interventions. The findings from the present study have a number of potential implications for clinical practice and public health policy. From a public health perspective, they suggest that interventions promoting physical activity may have a role in reducing early electrophysiologic risk among tobacco‐using young adults. However, such interventions should not replace primary prevention and cessation interventions aimed at reducing or eliminating nicotine dependence. Clinically, Tp‐e‐based indices could be further investigated as potential early markers of cardiovascular risk in young tobacco users in high exposure settings.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths, including the assessment of dependence on multiple tobacco products, the use of validated tobacco dependence scales, and the assessment of physical activity as a moderator rather than as a simple covariate. The focus on a relatively healthy young adult population enables the detection of early changes before the development of clinical disease. The main limitations of this study include the cross‐sectional design, which limits causal inference and does not allow for the exclusion of reverse causation. Self‐reported measures of tobacco use and physical activity are subject to recall and social desirability bias. Longitudinal data are needed to determine whether repolarization abnormalities predict long‐term cardiovascular risk. Another limitation is that the study did not assess long‐term cardiovascular outcomes or arrhythmic events, so the clinical significance of the repolarization changes is uncertain. However, these data are critical to establish whether the observed subclinical changes are associated with increased risk of adverse events over time. The present findings are based on a single resting ECG recording and may not capture within‐subject variability or changes over time.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that cigarette, waterpipe, and heated tobacco product dependence are associated with early alterations in ventricular repolarization among young adults, even in the absence of clinical cardiovascular disease or traditional QT abnormalities. The use of Tp‐e‐based indices may provide sensitive indicators of subclinical electrophysiologic vulnerability in this population. Notably, higher physical activity levels seem to mitigate the negative impact of heated tobacco product dependence on ventricular repolarization, suggesting physical activity as a potentially modifiable protective factor. These findings highlight the importance of early prevention strategies that target both nicotine dependence and lifestyle behaviors in young adults, particularly in regions with high tobacco use prevalence.
