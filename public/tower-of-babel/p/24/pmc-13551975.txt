Ribosome biogenesis in osteoarthritis: mechanisms and therapeutic potential
Authors: Zewen Xu, Zhiwei Pei, Haobo Yang, Shanjie Guo, Qiang Li, Liuchang Jiang, Jing Wang, Leipeng Wang, Dexin Liu, Xuanyin Chen, Baoxin Zhang, Ting Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRibosomes are the core machinery for cellular protein synthesis, and defects in their biogenesis have been confirmed to be closely associated with various diseases. Osteoarthritis (OA), one of the most common joint diseases worldwide, has a complex pathogenesis that remains incompletely elucidated. Traditional research has primarily focused on metabolic imbalances in articular cartilage and inflammatory responses. However, growing evidence suggests that dysregulated ribosome biogenesis may play a critical role in the pathogenesis of OA. Impaired ribosome biogenesis limits the protein translation capacity of chondrocytes and may participate in the initiation and progression of OA by modulating cellular metabolism and stress responses, indicating that OA could be considered an acquired ribosomopathy.Main bodyThis review systematically summarizes the various factors involved in ribosome biogenesis and their impact on chondrocyte function and joint tissue homeostasis, with a particular focus on key molecules such as non-coding RNAs, ribosomal proteins, and translation regulators. By integrating the latest findings from in vivo and in vitro models, this work evaluates the potential therapeutic value of targeting ribosome biogenesis-related molecules for the treatment of OA.ConclusionIn summary, in-depth investigation of the role of ribosome biogenesis in OA may not only provide new perspectives for elucidating its pathological mechanisms but also offer a theoretical foundation and potential targets for developing therapeutic strategies that modify the disease course.Keywords: Ribosome biogenesis, Osteoarthritis, Chondrocytes, Protein translation, Ribosomopathies

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) affects approximately 250 million people globally, and severely impacts patients’ quality of life. Beyond classical biomechanical and inflammatory determinants, metabolic and nutritional factors have also been implicated in OA progression. For example, in a 2 year prospective study of 392 individuals with symptomatic knee OA, higher dietary potassium intake was associated with reduced WOMAC scores, improved quality of life, and greater lowerlimb muscle strength, although no significant links were observed between dietary potassium and structural MRI outcomes such as cartilage volume or bone marrow lesions 1. While such macroelementlevel associations operate largely at the symptomatic rather than structural level, they underscore that OA pathogenesis integrates metabolic cues alongside the proteinsynthesis and nucleolar stress deficits discussed below. The mechanism behind the disease is multifactorial, accounting for the maintenance of articular cartilage degeneration, subchondral bone sclerosis, synovial inflammation and osteophyte formation, and thereby joint pain and dysfunction [2]. Most of the OA management is related to symptomatic and functional relief with the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs that include physical therapy and surgery. Nevertheless, there has yet to be any disease-modifying treatment, one that alters the natural progression of OA [3, 4], Despite the promise of new approaches such as regenerative medicine and cell therapy [5, 6], their efficacy and mechanisms require further validation, indicating a clear need for something novel at the level of basic mechanistic studies.

Ribosome biogenesis is one of the most important processes in the cell. This means the production of ribosomes which forms a crucial area of functioning in all eukaryotic cells. The processes involved in ribosome biogenesis include rRNA transcription, processing, modification along with the synthesis and assembly of ribosomal proteins [7, 8], Further, this process is significantly complex and requires a lot of regulation. There are various factors and regulatory processes which control ribosome biogenesis. These include rRNA modifications by small nucleolar R [9, 10] Proper ribosome biogenesis is essential for the maintenance of stable cellular protein synthesis capacity similar to that of metabolic homeostasis [11], There is increasing evidence indicating improper functioning of ribosome biogenesis leading to diseases. This improper functionality is affecting the overall size of unbalanced cellular proliferation in cancers [11, 12].

The recent investigations have presented a new angle towards OA pathogenesis. It outlines how ribosomal biogenesis abnormalities and their controlling factors may lead to impaired protein synthesis as well as stress responses in chondrocytes [13, 14], However, recent studies suggest that abnormalities in ribosome biogenesis and its associated regulatory factors may contribute to OA pathogenesis by impairing protein synthesis and stress responses in chondrocytes, thereby accelerating cartilage degradation and joint structural damage [15, 16]. For instance, U3 snoRNA which is a non-coding RNA shows downregulated expression in chondrocytes. This leads to lower levels of rRNA and compromised translational capacity. All these impact the chondrocyte phenotype and function negatively [17], Similarly, eIF6 which is a ribosomal protein showcases ribosome biogenesis mediation agonistic manner in RA. This promotes synovial cell inflammation and invasiveness showcasing its broad-spectrum action [18].

There is a growing amount of evidence that OA may represent an acquired ribosomopathy as opposed to just being a joint breakdown disease [16], This new concept is not just a metabolic imbalance and affirms ribosome biogenesis dysfunction is integral to OA. Elucidation of key molecular mechanisms in ribosome biogenesis may lead to the identification of novel therapeutic targets and disease-modifying drugs. In addition, the interrelationship of ribosome biogenesis, cellular stress responses, metabolic regulation, and inflammatory signaling networks provide a multi-disciplinary perspective for multi-dimensional OA research [11, 19], The relationship between ribosome biogenesis and OA will be systematically reviewed including their underlying molecular mechanisms and potential therapeutic options (Fig. 1), which can inspire new ideas for early diagnosis and precision therapy for OA.

Basic mechanisms of ribosome biogenesis and its role in chondrocytes

Molecular processes of ribosome biogenesis

Ribosome biogenesis refers to the synthesis and assembly of ribosomes. The process of ribosomogenesis includes transcription, processing modification, maturation of rRNA, synthesis assembly of RPs, and regulation of translation initiation factors. This process is important for the normal synthesis of proteins in cells and it is associated with cell growth, differentiation, and disease development. The essential molecular mechanisms are discussed below.

The key steps are rRNA transcription, processing and maturation. In eukaryotic cells, RNA polymerase I (Pol I) transcribes major of the rRNA as a large 45S precursor rRNA (pre-rRNA) which is subsequently cleaved, modified and folded to give the 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNAs. RNA polymerase III is responsible for the transcription of 5S rRNA. According to recent studies, RNA polymerase II (Pol II) also has a role in ribosome biogenesis via R-loop formation in the nucleolus that regulates rRNA gene expression, prevents the synthesis of non-coding RNAs that disrupt nucleolar architecture, and thus promotes ribosome biogenesis stability [20], rRNA processing is mediated by small nucleolar ribonucleoproteins (snoRNPs). The early processing of pre-rRNA is guided by U3 snoRNA. The binding of U3 snoRNA to specific sites on pre-rRNA aids its cleavage and folding to help make 18S rRNA, which forms the small (40S) subunit. 18S rRNA undergoes chemical modification by snoRNPs [21]. Moreover, the snoRNPs also mediate the pseudouridylation and 2’-O-methylation of rRNA. Both these chemical modifications increase the stability and function of rRNA [21].

The ribosomal proteins must be synthesized and assembled in conjunction with rRNA. RPs, synthesized by cytoplasmic ribosomes, return to the nucleolus via nuclear pore complex to engage in pre-rRNA assembly. The hierarchy and cooperativity of RPs forming ribosomal subunits are the topic of investigation. The small subunit protein uS5 is assembly is regulated by several auxiliary factors [22]. Assembly factors, such as the WDR74 module consisting of WDR74, RPF1, MAK16, and RRP1, regulate pre-rRNA cleavage and maturation for the 60 S large subunit, a critical step in its biogenesis [23]. Certain GTPases, such as Era, GTPBP7, and GTPBP10, have catalytic and regulatory roles in RP assembly and rRNA folding [24, 25]. Ribosomal proteins (RPs) in bacteria interact with rRNA and assist it with the correct formation of the rRNA tertiary structure, ensuring proper assembly of the subunits [26].

Translation initiation factors regulate the function of ribosome eIF5B, a highly conserved translation initiation factor, participates in 40S subunit maturation and in 18S rRNA 3’ end processing, coordinating small ribosomal subunit maturation with translation initiation [27], Other translation initiation factors involved ribosome biogenesis that also contribute to the assembly quality control of the ribosomal subunits, ensuring the functional integrity of mature ribosomes. Certain viral proteins, such as SARS-CoV-2 Nsp1, can inhibit ribosome biogenesis by targeting rRNA processing to block host [28].

In summary, ribosome biogenesis is a complex, multi-step molecular process. Furthermore, this process requires the concerted actions of many factors which includes rRNA transcription/processing/maturation and snoRNP-regulated assembly. In addition, there is also the RP synthesis/assembly and translation initiation factor function modulation. This process is evolutionarily conserved, as well as having organism-specific features. The association of plasma cell neoplasms with dysregulated ribosome biogenesis speaks to the potential for developing therapies that target these processes in the disease [29–32].

Regulatory characteristics of ribosome biogenesis in chondrocytes

Chondrocytes are important cells for cartilage function. Chondrocyte development has a particular demand for protein synthesis, which engages ribosome biogenesis regulation. Ribosome biogenesis regulation is central to protein synthesis intracellularly and it is critical for chondrocyte phenotype and function. In osteoarthritic (OA) chondrocytes, ribosome biogenesis undergoes modifications in response to disease and cellular environment changes. Research shows that OA chondrocytes, or cells in the cartilage, have an impaired ability to synthesize proteins. This is partly due to the expression of ribosome biogenesis-related genes and non-coding RNAs. For instance, BMP7 enhances the transcription of the 47 S pre-rRNA via an NKX3-2-dependent mechanism thereby increasing translational capacity [33], Molecular signalling pathways are intimately linked to ribosome biosynthesis; TGF-β2 stimulation alters ribosome activity and composition in chondrocytes, while promoting cap dependent protein synthesis, it suppresses IRES mediated translation, which is associated with epitranscriptomic modification of rRNA [34]. Collectively, these findings suggest that chondrocytes adapt the demand for protein synthesis through modulation of ribosome biogenesis and translational mechanisms under OA pathological settings, which in turn affects cellular homeostasis and disease progression.

Chondrocytes in OA reflect altered expression in genes related to ribosome biogenesis and elevated levels of non-coding RNA. Reduced expression of other small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs) with significantly down-regulated U3 snoRNA, which negatively affects rRNA maturation and the formation of the ribosome. Decreased expression of U3 snoRNA, an important non-coding RNA for pre-18S rRNA processing, reduced rRNA levels and translational capacity. These changes occur not only at the transcriptional level and also affect global translational regulation and lead to phenotypic changes and functional impairment in chondrocytes [17], Further, in OA chondrocytes, the differential regulation of epigenetic modification including 2’-O-methylation of rRNA modulates the selective translational activity of ribosome and affects the synthesis of proteins, for instance increased expression of COL1A1 and it highlights the significance of ribosome epitranscriptomic in the OA progression [35].

The downregulation of the U3 snoRNA complex has mechanistic significance for levels of rRNAs and protein synthesis. According to a study, U3 snoRNA promoter activity is inhibited by factors present in the synovial fluid of OA, while BMP7 and other signalling molecules upregulate this activity. The expression of U3 snoRNA that is damaged slows down the processing of pre-rRNA as well as decreases the levels of mature rRNA and weakens the capacity for protein synthesis. The aberration in ribosome biogenesis also causes phenotypic changes in chondrocytes, more specifically their differentiation status and activation of inflammatory pathways, thus causing OA [17]. U3 snoRNA is a non-coding RNA that is critical in ribosome assembly. Findings from this study show that U3 snoRNA is a molecular basis for disrupted protein synthesis and chondrocyte dysfunction due to OA. U3 snoRNA is likely a viable target for therapy.

While the association between U3 snoRNA downregulation and the OA chondrocyte phenotype is now well documented across independent human cartilage cohorts and ex vivo/in vitro models, the strength and completeness of functional validation warrant explicit stratification (Table 1). To date, causal inference rests predominantly on loss-of-function (antisense/knockdown) and gain-of-function (plasmid-driven U3 re-expression/promoter rescue) experiments in human articular chondrocytes and SW1353 chondrocytic cells, coupled with promoter-reporter assays demonstrating that U3 suppression is driven, at least in part, by OA synovial fluid-mediated transcriptional repression. In vivo, reduced U3 snoRNA has been reported in cartilage from the DMM (destabilisation of medial meniscus) murine OA model, but a definitive in vivo rescue experiment— wherein U3 snoRNA restoration in the joint environment reverses cartilage degeneration — remains unpublished and represents the principal evidence gap. The chondrocyte paradox: high anabolic demand vs. limited biosynthetic capacity

Ribosome biogenesis is a universal cellular process, yet its impairment appears to have catastrophic consequences specifically for articular chondrocytes. To understand why chondrocytes are uniquely susceptible to ribosomopathy, one must analyze the “Demand vs. Capacity” balance.

Association between abnormal ribosome biogenesis and osteoarthritis pathological mechanisms

Manifestations of ribosomal dysfunction in OA cartilage degeneration

As cartilage degenerates in osteoarthritis (OA), it experiences ribosomal dysfunction manifesting as defects in various stages of ribosome biogenesis, including rRNA transcription and processing, ribosome assembly, and changes in translation efficiency. Recent studies of OsteoChondral Unit (OCU)-on-Chip have successfully recreated the strain gradient environment in vivo at the cartilage–mineralized subchondral bone interface and shown the effect of mechanical overload on ribosome biogenesis. In this model of excessive mechanical stress on cartilage, the presence of altered ribosome biogenesis pathways was observed, which were identified due to suppressed rRNA transcription and assembly, thereby reducing the overall capability to synthesize protein. The chondrocytes under aberrant mechanical conditions were reported to show ribosomal dysfunction and a programmed cell death activation of ribosome biogenesis and apoptosis-related signalling pathways owing to mechanically induced cellular stress [36].

Ribosome abnormalities contribute toward an imbalance in the synthesis of protein and the expression of chondrocyte matrix proteins and regulatory factors. This can enhance cartilage matrix degeneration and inflammatory response. When ribosomes are not functioning properly, the overall efficiency of protein synthesis becomes low. Particularly, the translation of important regulatory proteins such as transcription factors and matrix synthesis initiator proteins mainly affects the ribosomal mechanisms. Impaired protein synthesis can prevent chondrocytes from maintaining tissue homeostasis and promotes the abnormal expression of inflammatory mediators, causing cartilage degeneration. Similarly, in rheumatoid arthritis, high expression of eIF6 (a key regulator of ribosome biogenesis) potentiates the inflammatory cell proliferation and invasion through modulation of the translation efficiency of selected mRNAs, thus enhancing inflammation. The above findings highlight the implication of ribosome biogenesis imbalance in inflammatory joint diseases and the essential involvement of ribosomal dysfunction in OA cartilage degeneration [18].

In conclusion, dysregulation at multiple levels of ribosome biogenesis occurs due to ribosomal dysfunction in OA cartilage, which will alter protein synthesis and lead to destruction of cartilage matrix as well as inflammatory activation (Fig. 2). New approaches and treatment targets may emerge for OA through targeting ribosome biogenesis pathways.

Differential impact of nucleolar stress on transcript translation in OA chondrocytes

First, transcripts encoding ECM components (e.g., COL2A1, ACAN) typically possess long, highly structured 5’ Untranslated Regions (5’UTRs) with stable secondary structures (e.g., hairpins). Efficient translation of these messages is heavily dependent on cap-dependent scanning and high initiation factor (eIF4F) activity. During nucleolar stress, cells often prioritize the translation of short, unstructured mRNAs (e.g., stress-response genes like ATF4) via internal ribosome entry sites (IRES) or leaky scanning, while long-structured mRNAs like those of ECM are selectively filtered out. Our previous work noted that TGF-β2 stimulation suppresses IRES-mediated translation while promoting cap-dependent synthesis, highlighting the sensitivity of ECM translation to these switches.

Second, the concept of the “ribosome code” posits that variations in ribosomal protein composition or rRNA modifications can confer functional specificity. In OA chondrocytes, dysregulated ribosome biogenesis leads to altered rRNA 2’-O-methylation patterns [35]. These epitranscriptomic changes can modulate the ribosome’s affinity for specific mRNA subsets. For instance, specific ribosomal proteins (e.g., RPL38, RACK1) have been shown in other systems to act as “specialized ribosomes” that selectively translate mRNAs governing cell fate and tissue homeostasis. Although direct evidence in chondrocytes is nascent, it is plausible that the U3 snoRNA deficiency observed in OA not only reduces total ribosome numbers but also alters the composition of the remaining ribosomes, shifting the translatome away from anabolic ECM production toward catabolic and inflammatory outputs.

Altered rRNA epitranscriptomics and selective translation in OA

While the quantitative loss of ribosomes (nucleolar stress) undoubtedly contributes to OA, emerging evidence suggests that qualitative changes in ribosome composition—specifically rRNA modifications—play an equally critical role in driving selective translational programs.

rRNA modifications as regulators of translational fidelity and efficiency

Ribosomal RNA undergoes over 200 site-specific chemical modifications, including 2’-O-methylation and pseudouridylation, predominantly guided by small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs). Traditionally viewed as stabilizing the ribosome, these modifications are now recognized as key regulators of translation dynamics. For instance, 2’-O-methylation at specific sites in the decoding center (helix 44 of 18 S rRNA) can alter the ribosome’s affinity for certain codons.

Evidence in osteoarthritis

A landmark study demonstrated that OA chondrocytes exhibit a distinct rRNA epitranscriptomic signature compared to healthy controls [35]. Specifically, OA is associated with site-specific changes in 2’-O-methylation. These modifications can lead to ribosome heterogeneity:

Impaired ECM Translation: Certain modifications appear to be optimized for the efficient translation of G + C rich mRNAs, such as those encoding type II collagen (COL2A1). Loss of these specific modifications in OA creates “defective ribosomes” that are less efficient at translating these structurally demanding transcripts, even if overall ribosome numbers were sufficient.

Stress Response Bias: Conversely, altered modifications may favor the translation of mRNAs with simpler secondary structures (e.g., ATF4, HIF1A), effectively shifting the cell from an anabolic to a stress-survival state.

Link to snoRNA dysregulation

The downregulation of U3 snoRNA, as discussed previously, initiates this cascade. However, other snoRNAs (e.g., C/D box snoRNAs guiding 2’-O-methylation) are also dysregulated in OA. Since snoRNAs guide the enzymatic machinery to specific rRNA residues, their depletion directly results in site-specific hypo-modification. This provides a mechanistic explanation for why simply restoring total ribosome numbers might fail to rescue the ECM phenotype; the newly formed ribosomes may still lack the correct “epitranscriptomic fingerprint” required for specialized translation.

In summary, nucleolar stress in OA alters the quality of the translational apparatus through aberrant rRNA modification, contributing to the selective loss of ECM proteins independent of global translation rates.

Temporal dynamics and causality: nucleolar stress vs. inflammation

A central challenge in understanding the role of ribosome biogenesis (RiBi) in OA is resolving the temporal relationship between nucleolar stress and the inflammatory/catabolic milieu. Does impaired RiBi initiate the disease, or is it merely a bystander effect of chronic IL-1β/TNF-α signaling?

Evidence Supporting RiBi Impairment as an Early Event.Longitudinal studies in the destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) mouse model provide a timeline of events. Notably, reductions in key RiBi components, such as U3 snoRNA and specific ribosomal proteins, are detectable as early as 2–4 weeks post-surgery, preceding overt histological signs of proteoglycan loss or cartilage erosion (typically observed at 8–12 weeks) [16, 17] Similarly, in vitrokinetic studies demonstrate that exposing healthy chondrocytes to IL-1β induces a rapid (within 24 h) suppression of Pol I transcription and U3 snoRNA expression, occurring simultaneously with or slightly before the peak induction of catabolic enzymes like ADAMTS5. These findings suggest that RiBi shutdown is an early cellular response to stress, rather than a late-stage consequence of tissue breakdown.

Downstream: Defective RiBi leads to the accumulation of free ribosomal proteins (e.g., RPL5, RPL11) that are not incorporated into mature ribosomes. These free RPs bind to MDM2, stabilizing p53 and activating the p53-p21 axis, which further suppresses Pol I transcription. Crucially, nucleolar stress also activates the NF-κB pathway independently, creating a self-sustaining inflammatory loop [9, 16].

Reconciling mTORC1 hyperactivation with impaired ribosome biogenesis

The mTORC1 pathway is canonically recognized as the master positive regulator of ribosome biogenesis (RiBi), promoting rRNA transcription and ribosomal protein translation. Paradoxically, numerous studies report mTORC1 hyperactivation in OA chondrocytes, concurrent with the RiBi impairment described herein. This apparent biochemical contradiction can be reconciled by considering the context-dependent uncoupling of mTORC1 signaling from its biosynthetic outputs.

The Glutamine Deprivation Hypothesis.mTORC1 activation alone is insufficient to drive RiBi; it requires substrates. Ribosome biogenesis is an energetically expensive process requiring nucleotides and amino acids. In OA, chronic inflammation and defective autophagy lead to glutamine depletion. Glutamine is not only a nitrogen donor for nucleotide biosynthesis but also a key regulator of the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, which fuels Pol I transcription. Thus, even with hyperactive mTORC1, the lack of metabolic substrates (glutamine) creates a “starvation in the midst of plenty” scenario, stalling rRNA synthesis [11, 16].

Regulatory role of RNA‑Binding Proteins (RBPs) in OA

Through RNA-binding proteins as post-transcriptional regulators, osteoarthritis affects chondrocyte functioning. Each RBP regulates mRNA subcellular localization, stability, and translation efficiency. All these together modulate the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis. Numerous studies in recent years have shown alterations in RBP expression profiles in OA cartilage and possibly the development of OA.

Preliminary analysis suggest significant changes in the expression pattern of RBPs in OA cartilage. A notable example is constituted by TIPARP, an RBP with implications in the formation and construction of ribosomes, which is highly expressed and enriched in cartilage that is healthy, but has undergone significant down-regulation in cartilage that constitutes OA. This decrease may disrupt ribosome production in cells, which will affect protein creation and cellular reactions, jeopardising the normal functioning of chondrocytes and allowing for the onset of OA pathology. According to single-cell RNA sequencing data, the TIPARP level was lower in “pathological cluster” of OA cartilage but not in the normal cluster, suggesting its importance for OA [38].

Inhibiting the translation of TLR4 mRNA’s 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR), PUM1 a downstream RNA binding protein (RBP), can affect NF-κB signalling pathway and delay chondrocyte senescence. Studies have shown that the expression of PUM1 declines as OA progresses. In addition, PUM1 overexpression can protect mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) from the damages of inflammatory factor-induced damage and maintain their chondrogenic potential. The PUM1–TLR4 axis of OA may be a therapeutic target [39]. In contrast, heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein D (HNRNPD) is up‑regulated in OA cartilage, promoting mitochondrial dysfunction and cellular senescence in chondrocytes. Its overexpression exacerbates OA progression, a mechanism mediated through up‑regulation of FOXM1 [40].

In terms of regulatory mechanisms, RBPs facilitate ribosome biogenesis and contribute to mRNA translation through different pathways. The presence of RNA-binding activity in addition to chaperone activity by Heat shock protein beta-1 (HSPB1) and influence on osteoarthritic (OA) genes like epidermal growth factor receptor gene (EGFR) and COL5A1 through AU-rich elements, coding region and 3UTR of mRNA. The reduction of HSPB1 in the cartilage of osteoarthritis shows that it is not doing its job, according to research [41]. In addition, SND1 binds to 3′UTR of HSPA5 mRNA to promote HSPA5 mRNA instability and decrease HSPA5 expression to regulate GPX4 and promote ferroptosis of chondrocyte, which is involved in OA-related inflammation and extracellular matrix degradation [42].

RBPs also modulate the alternative splicing of OA-associated genes. The alternative splicing of the apoptosis‑related gene XAF1, which alters chondrocyte survival and apoptosis, is modulated by the RBP CUL4B. This highlights the diverse complexity of RBPs’ regulatory roles during OA development [43].

In short, the expression and function of RBPs is altered in OA cartilage. Through the regulation of post-transcriptional processes at multiple steps such as ribosome biogenesis, stability of mRNA, translation and splicing, they influence chondrocyte metabolism, inflammation, apoptosis and senescence (Fig. 4). To the end, the identification and elucidation of these RBPs can contribute to the understanding of OA pathogenesis and provide a theoretical basis for developing new types of molecularly targeted therapeutic strategies [44–46].

Interplay between ribosome biogenesis and mechanical stress in OA

Impact of mechanical overload on ribosome biogenesis

The key pathological factor in osteoarthritis that affects cartilage and subchondral bone through mechanical overload is the transcriptional regulation of ribosome biogenesis pathways. The capacity of a cell to synthesise proteins depends on the ribosome biogenesis. It involves: RDNA transcriptionRRNA processingRibosomal protein assemblyThey are highly dependent and regulated by the cellular microenvironment and external stimuli. The stress causes of the cartilage mechanical force, the protein synthesis capacity of chondrocytes by regulating ribosome-related genes and their upstream signaling pathways, and cartilage metabolism and tissue homeostasis.

Mechanical stress can modulate the activation of transcription factors and signalling molecules in chondrocytes and subchondral tissues. Skeletal muscles can have their AKT/mTOR signalling activated by mechanical loading to promote ribosome biogenesis and thus increase rDNA transcriptional activity and ribosome production [47]. A similar regulatory mechanism is likely present in cartilage tissue and its dysregulation can impair protein synthesis and maintenance of chondrocyte function. Moreover, mechanical stress can also induce epigenetic modifications that affect ribosome biogenesis, such as DNA methylation changes in rDNA regions. Research reveals that mechanical loading can modify methylation patterns in the rDNA enhancer and MYC-associated regions, modulating rDNA transcription activity and ribosome biogenesis [48].

Conversely, under mechanical overload conditions, the aberrant expression of ribosome-related genes contributes to cartilage degeneration. The overload stress in chondrocytes impairs the biogenesis of ribosomes, which disrupts protein synthesis. Such an impairment may aggravate cartilage matrix destruction and cell apoptosis. Overload-associated oxidative stress, for instance, can induce the abnormality of ribosome biogenesis, trigger nucleolar stress and the activation of the p53 signaling pathway that leads to cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [49, 50]. Furthermore, inhibition of ribosome biogenesis caused by overload is closely related to the abnormal expression of ribosomal proteins. It interferes with the assembly of ribosomes and causes the activation of apoptotic pathways through signaling mediation by ribosomal proteins [51]. These mechanisms together cause progressive loss of chondrocyte function and lead to OA progression.

To summarize, multi-level and multi-mechanistic pathways underlie the regulation of ribosome biogenesis in cartilage and subchondral tissues by mechanical stress It promotes ribosome biogenesis to support tissue metabolism and repair under physiological loading conditions. Nevertheless, when put under mechanical overload, it modulates the ribosome biogenesis pathway, resulting in aberrant expression of ribosome-related genes while promoting degeneration and apoptosis of chondrocytes thereby establishing. Findings suggest that focusing on important nodes involved in ribosome biogenesis under mechanical stress may be a new way to treat OA that needs to be explored further and clinically validated.

In vitro and in vivo models for integrative studies of ribosome biogenesis and mechanical signaling

Ribosome biogenesis refers to the multi-layered regulatory roles in mechanical signalling, a major Cellular process taking signals from an external environment and deciding the fate of the cell. Researchers have made advances to new in vitro models. These models include the organ-on-a-chip technology. Organ-on-a-chip technology can use to study the effect of mechanical stress on ribosome biogenesis. Furthermore, it can study the effect of mechanical stress on cell fate. Organ-on-a-chip systems are employed to simulate the dynamic mechanical stimuli and three-dimensional microenvironment experienced by cells. The purpose is to help researchers study how mechanical signals regulate ribosome biogenesis and its downstream cellular signalling. Mechanical stress affects ribosome assembly and activity via modulation of translational machinery components, including ribosome‑associated proteins and small nucleolar RNAs, which subsequently regulate cell fate decisions like proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. The study of these models provides insight into the pathogenic alterations that occur in chondrocytes as a result of mechanical-load-related diseases, such as OA and so on. In addition, these findings provide proof of a principle for intervention that targets ribosome biogenesis in order to achieve an efficacious outcome.

The dynamic measurement of ribosome biogenesis and protein synthesis in vivo requires stable isotope labeling techniques. Researchers can quantitatively measure the age-related changes in ribosome synthesis rates and protein translation activities in cartilage using isotopically labelled amino acids in conjunction with high-precision analytical techniques such as mass spectrometry. Research shows that with age, chondrocytes’ ability to carry out ribosomal biogenesis diminishes, limiting their protein synthesizing capacity, which ultimately impacts the maintenance and repair of the cartilage matrix. The dynamic monitoring detects early molecular events in OA and provides theoretical evidence for interventions against cartilage degeneration delay. Combining stable isotope labeling with in vitro organ-on-a-chip models may allow a fuller exploration of how mechanical signals are integrated into the regulation of ribosome biogenesis [52, 53]. This could further the understanding of OA pathological mechanisms and the optimization of targeted therapies.

Potential of ribosome biogenesis‑related factors as therapeutic targets in osteoarthritis

Role and interventional value of translation initiation factor eIF6 in joint diseases

The translation initiation factor eIF6 plays a vital role in the regulation of ribosome biogenesis and protein translation process. It shows altered expression in the pathogenesis of joint diseases like osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis. Genetic studies reveal the EIF6 gene is strongly associated with OA. Gene expression regulatory loci analyses suggest that EIF6 is located in the 20q11.22 chromosomal region with other OA risk genes such as GDF5 and UQCC1, indicating that it could be one of the genetic risk factors for OA [54]. Furthermore, as an anti-association factor removed during ribosomal 60 S subunit maturation, the eIF6 expression level directly influences ribosome assembly efficiency and translation initiation that affect cellular function and metabolic state [55], In the OA environment, eIF6 abnormal expression may lead to imbalanced protein synthesis in bone and cartilage cells that drive disease progression and cartilage degeneration.

eIF6 expression shows notable increase in rheumatoid arthritis. Research examining fibroblast‑like synoviocytes from RA joints has shown that eIF6 supports ribosome biogenesis, augments the translation of specific proteins such as transcription factors that include SP1, and promotes the proliferation, migration, and inflammatory response of FLS, indicating that it is a key effector in RA pathology [18]. Utilizing RNA interference to inhibit eIF6 expression inhibits the inflammatory behaviour and invasive behaviour of FLS and reduces the production of inflammatory mediators. Furthermore, eIF6 knockdown significantly alleviates joint injury in a collagen‑induced arthritis rat model, indicating that eIF6 is a therapeutic target for RA. By clarifying the mechanism, our comprehension of synovial inflammation in RA is deepened and it also provides a theoretical basis for intervention strategies aimed at ribosome biogenesis.

The natural flavonoid apigenin modulates the expression of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 6 and improves chondrocyte function in OA therapeutics research. The upregulation of eIF6 expression by apigenin effectively alleviates hydrogen peroxide-induced chondrocyte senescence, restores cell viability, and displays potential for slowing OA progression [56]. While targeting eIF6 in cells reflects not only on synthesis but also on potential OA pathological development for cellular senescence and metabolic state.

To summarize eIF6, a key factor in ribosome biogenesis, plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of RA and OA (Fig. 5). It facilitates the migration, proliferation, and inflammatory responses of inflammatory cells and alters joint tissue homeostasis by modulating protein translation efficiency. Strategies to inhibit eIF6 showed efficacy in animal models, and the action of natural products such as apigenin establishes its therapeutic potential. Future studies can investigate the molecular regulatory network of eIF6 and its signaling pathways involved in joint diseases to develop more specific and potent therapies [18, 54, 56].

Therapeutic prospects of non-coding RNA regulation of ribosome biogenesis

In the recent year, the elucidation of the function of non coding RNAs (ncRNAs) in ribosome biogenesis regulation has been proved to be useful in diseases like osteoarthritis (OA). The process of ribosome biogenesis is essential for protein synthesis in cells. Furthermore, the dysregulation of this process is associated with OA, or osteoarthritis. Non-coding RNAs manipulate the cellular capacity to synthesise proteins and the inflammatory status via mechanisms that affect ribosome assembly and function. These include snoRNAs, lncRNAs and miRNAs.

The U3 snoRNA is a pivotal snoRNA which is mainly responsible for the processing of pre-rRNA for the making of mature 18 S rRNA and is essential for the formation of the small ribosomal subunit. Research indicates that the expression of U3 snoRNA is significantly lower in OA cartilage and chondrocytes, resulting in a reduced rRNA level and protein synthesis capacity [17]. Moreover, the downregulation of U3 snoRNA weakens the protein synthesis of chondrocytes that governs the normal functionality of the chondrocytes and also causes changes in the inflammatory microenvironment of cartilage by regulating inflammation-related signalling pathways. Reduced expression of U3 snoRNA disrupts overall protein translation, leading to dysfunctional cellular properties and altered expression of inflammatory factors, thereby promoting the development of OA. In addition, changing U3 snoRNA expression from outside can reverse these changes, raise rRNA and protein synthesis levels and lessen inflammatory responses, thus making it a promising therapeutic target [17].

The U3 snoRNA regulatory role has direct effects on ribosome biogenesis but appears to also be involved in the regulation of chondrocyte differentiation to maintain homeostasis. The presence of bone morphogenetic protein 7(BMP7), a soluble factor related to OA, can restrict U3 snoRNA expression and influence the chondrocyte phenotype. This indicates that the ribosome biogenesis mechanism linked to snoRNAs can be regulated by external signals [17]. By controlling U3 snoRNA expression, the protein synthesis ability of chondrocytes may be restored. This is expected to alleviate OA-related inflammation and tissue degeneration.

Due to the significant regulatory role of ncRNAs in the ribosome biogenesis and the development of the RNA interference (RNAi) approach, novel strategies will help to restore the ribosomal dysfunctional diseases. Antisense oligonucleotides, siRNAs, miRNA mimics or inhibitors fall under the umbrella of RNAi, which can effectively modulate different ncRNA expression levels.

For snoRNAs whose expression is downregulated, like U3 snoRNA, we can use synthetic RNA molecules to restore snoRNA function to promote rRNA processing and ribosome assembly to further increase protein synthesis capacity in chondrocytes and alleviate OA pathological features [17], For lncRNAs and miRNAs that influence ribosome biogenesis, RNAi can also be used to regulate their expression and affect post‑transcriptional regulation of ribosome‑related genes. Some lncRNAs can affect the overall ribosome assembly and function by regulating the stability or translation efficiency of mRNAs of ribosomal proteins [57, 58], The regulation of these lncRNAs by the RNAi technology may restore ribosome biogenesis function and chondrocyte function.

RNAi technology used along with nanocarrier or targeted delivery systems can enhance the stability and specificity of therapeutic molecules into the joint while having minimal systemic side effects. Current studies are also investigating whether ncRNA‑mediated ribosome biogenesis pathways could be targeted to modulate cellular proliferation and inflammation, offering novel insights into OA disease‑modifying therapy [59, 60].

Non-coding RNAs that regulate ribosome biogenesis can act as novel targets to treat arthritis. Changing the expression of important ncRNAs like U3 snoRNA can help bring back normal ribosome biogenesis function. This can increase protein synthesis and anti-inflammatory power. As a result, chondrocytes can be not only protected but also slow down OA processes. Future precise therapeutic strategies for OA patients that combine RNAi technology will offer more effective disease-modifying treatment alternatives.

RNA‑binding protein‑targeted therapeutic strategies

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) play a crucial role in RNA metabolism and are implicated in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis (OA). RBPs play a crucial role in RNA splicing, stability, transport, and translation efficiency that influences cellular function and diseases. In recent years, as studies surrounding the role of RNA binding proteins (RBPs) in OA development deepens, TIPARP (Tetrachlorodibenzodioxin Inducible Poly (ADP‑Ribose) Polymerase), a key RBP, has been identified as a potential novel therapeutic target. TIPARP is highly expressed and cartilage-specific enriched in healthy cartilage but downregulated in OA-affected cartilage with a strong decrease in “pathological cell populations” [38]. The expression level of TIPARP closely associates with pathological status of OA and regulatory mechanisms of TIPARP may involve certain influences on ribosome biogenesis and splicing. As such, restoring normal TIPARP expression or modulating its function may represent a new therapeutic strategy for OA. Other RBPs, such as HSPB1, PUM1, and HNRNPD, have also been reported to regulate OA‑related cellular senescence, apoptosis, and metabolic abnormalities. For instance, small heat shock protein HSPB1 possesses RNA-binding ability; HSPB1 binds to mRNAs related to osteoarthritis (OA) such as EGFR and COL5A1, regulating the expression of related genes and participating in the pathology of OA [41]. PUM1 slows the senescence of MSCs and protects cartilage tissue by inhibiting TLR4 mRNA translation and regulating the NF-κB signaling pathway. According to various studies, many other RBP(s) can also be a target for OA treatment. Firstly, PUM1-TLR4-axis-based gene therapy has been reported to protect articular cartilage in an animal model [39], In addition, upregulation of FOXM1 by HNRNPD promotes chondrocyte senescence and accelerates OA progression across various disease models [40].

Medicine and gene-editing tools offer new ways of controlling the activity of RBPs. Small-molecule inhibitors or agonists that target the function domains of specific RBPs can regulate the ability of RBPs to RNA bind or affect their stability, thereby impacting the downstream signaling pathways. One option is to generate functional restorers or activators for TIPARP so that it might reverse characteristic changes caused by downregulation in OA. Additionally, similar to FABP4, small‑molecule inhibitors like andrographolide have exhibited the ability to ameliorate inflammation and cartilage destruction through their capacity to inhibit FABP4 activity, further proving that it is feasible to develop small molecules that target OA‑related proteins [61], Gene‑editing technologies such as CRISPR/Cas9 provide powerful tools for the molecular therapy of OA by enabling precise regulation of RBP gene expression. Lentivirus-mediated overexpression of the PUM1 gene has cartilage-protective effects in a mouse OA model [39], while RNA interference technology and antisense oligonucleotides could be used to specifically downregulate the expression of disease-promoting RBPs. A further example is inhibiting HNRNPD or SND1 expression, which may restrain the senescence and ferroptosis of chondrocytes, thus potentially alleviating OA [42, 62]. In conclusion, therapeutic strategies targeting RBPs, which combine small-molecule drugs with gene-editing technologies, may enable precise regulation of important molecules in the OA pathological process, and this also opens new windows for OA treatment.

Overall, RBPs are important molecules in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis with intricate and diverse regulatory networks. Through the identification of key RBPs, including TIPARP, and the elucidation of their regulatory mechanisms, new biological insights may emerge for OA. The prospects of broad applications on small-molecule drugs and gene-editing technology to modulate RBP function indicate that they can be an important approach for targeted OA therapy in the future. Future OA research should clarify the interactive mechanisms between RBPs and OA-related signaling pathways; and optimize the design of RBP-targeting drugs and gene-editing strategies for clinical translation and effective and safe intervention of OA.

Risks, limitations, and translational challenges

While targeting ribosome biogenesis offers promising disease-modifying avenues for OA, the translation of these strategies from bench to bedside faces substantial hurdles. Unlike systemic malignancies where broad cytotoxicity is sometimes the goal, OA therapy demands chondroprotection—requiring extreme specificity to preserve normal cellular physiology while halting degeneration. Three major challenges must be addressed: systemic toxicity, delivery barriers in avascular cartilage, and off-target effects.

Systemic toxicity and the “Ribosome Paradox”

Ribosomes are fundamental to every eukaryotic cell. Systemically inhibiting ribosome biogenesis (e.g., targeting eIF6 or snoRNAs) carries a high risk of bone marrow suppression, gastrointestinal toxicity, and hepatic dysfunction, as seen with traditional chemotherapeutics like 5-fluorouracil. Even “pro-chondrogenic” factors like BMP7, which upregulate U3 snoRNA and translation [33], can induce heterotopic ossification or uncontrolled osteophyte formation if dosed improperly. Therefore, any systemic intervention must maintain a narrow therapeutic window to modulate chondrocyte translation without crippling global protein synthesis.

Delivery barriers in the avascular cartilage

The dense, avascular, and negatively charged extracellular matrix (ECM) of articular cartilage acts as a formidable barrier to macromolecular drugs.

RNAi/ASO delivery: Negatively charged nucleic acids (siRNA, ASOs for U3 snoRNA or TIPARP) face repulsion from the anionic proteoglycan matrix and poor diffusion into deep zones.

Gene editing: Viral vectors (AAV) show tropism for chondrocytes but struggle to penetrate the full thickness of adult human cartilage.

Small molecules: While superior in diffusion, small molecules targeting specific protein-protein interactions (e.g., eIF6 inhibitors) often suffer from poor solubility and rapid clearance from the synovial fluid. Nanocarrier systems (liposomes, exosomes) are promising but currently lack robust clinical validation in OA [59, 60].

Off-target effects and ribosome heterogeneity

The concept of “ribosome specialization” implies that altering ribosome biogenesis components may have unintended consequences.

snoRNA pleiotropy: U3 snoRNA is essential for 18 S rRNA processing, but snoRNAs often share sequence motifs with other non-coding RNAs. Manipulating U3 levels via RNAi could inadvertently affect other snoRNPs or trigger interferon responses.

RBPs and splicing: Targeting RBPs like TIPARP or HNRNPD for gene editing (CRISPR/Cas9) risks disrupting their non-canonical functions in RNA splicing or DNA repair, potentially triggering genomic instability in chondrocytes.

In conclusion, while the molecular rationale is compelling, successful clinical translation will depend on localized delivery strategies (e.g., smart hydrogels, intra-articular nanoparticles) combined with highly specific targeting to minimize systemic exposure and off-target toxicity (Table 3).

Physical and Mechanical Methods.

Simple intra-articular injection often results in rapid “washout.” Combining injection with viscosupplementation (injecting therapeutics alongside high-molecular-weight HA) or using thermo-sensitive hydrogels that solidify at body temperature can significantly extend the residence time of ribosome-targeted drugs (e.g., eIF6 inhibitors) within the joint space.

For ribosome biogenesis-targeted therapies, we propose that HA-conjugated ASOs or CPP-modified LNP-siRNA targeting U3 snoRNA or TIPARP represent the most viable near-term strategy, as they leverage the physicochemical properties of cartilage to achieve localized, sustained efficacy while minimizing systemic exposure.

Future research directions and challenges

Dynamic changes and mechanism analysis of ribosome biogenesis in different stages of OA

The dynamic changes and regulatory mechanism of ribosome biogenesis in the occurrence and development of OA are gradually being paid more attention. Osteoarthritis, fundamentally characterized by an imbalance between the synthesis and degradation of proteins, occurs in the joint tissues. Despite earlier studies focusing on an imbalance in the metabolism of the tissue surrounding the joint, OA may be an acquired ribosomopathy in which a disturbance in ribosome biogenesis is participating in its pathogenic process [16], More specifically, changes from rRNA transcription and processing through to maturation along with altered ribosomal protein expression and functional state may be closely related to OA. Changes in ribosome biogenesis signal the onset of OA. This may lead to alterations in ribosome formation and maturation, decreased protein translational capacity, and compromise normal chondrocyte function and phenotype maintenance.

Ribosome biogenesis regulation is spatiotemporally specific at various OA stages, indicating complex dynamic changes in the context of various cell heterogeneity. According to experts, as the disease progresses, chondrocytes show a drop in the expression of ncRNAs involved in ribosome biogenesis, such as U3 snoRNA, which negatively affects rRNA levels and the protein translational capacity of chondrocytes, impairing their functionality and phenotype, a condition that fits the characteristics of the OA pathological state [17]. As age influences cartilage ribosome biogenesis.

The use of single‑cell sequencing technology has also revealed differences in ribosome‑related gene expression in different cell subpopulations. In osteoarthritis (OA) cartilage, the presence of different cell subpopulations can cause them to display differences in ribosome biogenesis and protein translation activity, providing a perspective for understanding functional differentiation and heterogeneity of chondrocytes during the OA pathological process [36], or for example, by applying single‑cell RNA sequencing in a mechanically overload‑induced OA model, it was found that the activity of ribosome biogenesis pathways is closely associated with apoptosis and inflammatory signaling. The synthesis of proteins is impacted by ribosomal dysfunction but is also involved in regulating cell fate decisions mention. Furthermore, RBPs are markers for ribosome biogenesis and mRNA translation.RBPs are largely expressed in healthy cartilage but downregulated in OA condition which affects ribosome and protein synthesis regulatory network [38].

Taken together, the ribosome biogenesis exhibits significant dynamic changes at different stages of OA. A range of factors influences cellular heterogeneity, including the age of the cells, mechanical load, and inflammation. The next major advances in molecular understanding of OA with new therapeutic targets made possible with ribosomal gene expression insights from single-cell sequencing are indicative of future major successes in drug development. The development of future interventions to correct aberrant ribosome biogenesis offers attractive new avenues for OA disease-modifying therapy.

Application of multi‑omics integration and systems biology approaches

Recent development of multi-omics and system biology methods will probably play an increasing key role in the study of the regulatory network of ribosome biogenesis with a broad application prospect to elucidating OA mechanisms and precision treatment. A comprehensive analysis of proteomics, transcriptomics, and epigenetics could reveal the key regulatory factors and interactions involved in the ribosome biogenesis process, constructing an OA‑related regulatory network. Single-cell RNA sequencing technology can provide insightful details about the transcriptional changes of ribosome biogenesis in chondrocytes and subchondral bone tissues. This type of analysis has shown ribosome biogenesis-related gene expression dysregulation to be associated with pathologic apoptosis caused by mechanical overloading [36], In this study, the development of an osteochondral unit-on-a-chip (OCU-on-Chip) platform mimicking the mechanical strain gradient at the cartilage–subchondral bone interface successfully captured abnormities in ribosome biogenesis and activation of cellular transcriptional machinery. This experimental design can serve as a powerful system for integrating multi-omics data and studying experimental mechanotransduction. The expression and post-translational modification states of key regulatory proteins can be validated through proteomics technology, whereas epigenetics methods can reveal the regulatory mechanisms of chromatin remodeling and histone modifications in ribosome biogenesis. This cross-level data integration enables comprehensive construction of ribosome biogenesis regulatory networks and identification of potential therapeutic targets.

Drawing from the above multiomics data, a systematic model for ribosome biogenesis abnormalities in OA may help formulate precision treatment based on systems biology methods. Models that incorporate cell signaling, gene regulation and metabolic pathways have been used to study the effects of abnormal ribosome biogenesis activities on chondrocyte functionality and joint tissue homeostasis. Studies in rheumatoid arthritis (RA), for example, show that eukaryotic translation initiation factor 6 (eIF6) is upregulated in synovial fibroblastlike cells, regulating ribosome biogenesis and promoting inflammatory and invasive phenotype [18]. Therefore, this mechanism shows that ribosome biogenesis abnormalities are not only present in cartilage lesions in OA but could also contribute significantly to synovial inflammation. Using modeling of transduction and signalization through systems biology, it will be possible to make predictions regarding the regulatory network of eIF6 and its downstream expression factor SP1. This result has great application potential aware building either upon the design of inhibitors on small-molecule bases, or also on siRNAs. In addition, the predictions of the systems model can be tested experimentally using advanced in vitro models such as OCU-on-Chip to refine the treatment direction. In summary, the integration of multi-omics with systems biology enables a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms of ribosome biogenesis abnormalities in OA and lays the foundation for the development of individualized precision treatment strategies, with high value for clinical transformation.

Key issues in clinical translation and drug development

Ribosome biogenesis is the fundamental cellular process for the synthesis of proteins. Abnormalities in these processes are closely associated with the occurrence and development of osteoarthritis (OA). Nonetheless, there are still a number of important hurdles that will need to be overcome to translate the basic research findings associated with ribosome biogenesis into clinical applications in drug safety and specificity, in vivo delivery systems and targeting strategy optimization, and clinical validation of biomarkers.

Evaluating the safety and specificity of drugs targeting ribosome biogenesis‑related factors presents the greatest obstacle to their clinical translation. Ribosomes are protein synthesis machines found in all cells and have varied functions. Thus, targeting ribosome biogenesis may affect the physiological functioning of normal cells and cause toxic side effects. Expressional changes in essential factors that control rRNA transcription, processing, and maturation have a direct impact on protein-synthesis capacity and phenotypic changes of chondrocytes. For example, U3 snoRNA and others considerably effect it. To avoid harming the function of normal chondrocytes, targeting this approach requires a careful design [17]. Besides, there has to be a coordinated action of many proteins and RNA in the different steps of ribosome biogenesis. Research investigating the function of eIF6 in rheumatoid arthritis, for example, action of eIF, regulates ribosome biogenesis and influences inflammation. While eIF6 shows promise as a therapeutic target, it is important to evaluate the effect on other cell types [18], eIF6-targeted drugs should ultimately achieve safe and efficacious therapeutic benefit, helping abnormal ribosome biogenesis with high specificity.

Ensuring that drugs exert their desired effects on target cells is possible through optimizing in vivo delivery systems and targeting strategies. The complex pathology of OA (osteoarthritis) is mainly characterised by low vascularity of cartilage tissue and dense extracellular matrix which limits drug penetration and targeted delivery. Most current studies have not yet achieved mature technologies to deliver drugs that regulate ribosome biogenesis precisely in humans with minimized systemic side effects, mostly using in vitro or animal models. Nanocarriers, local injections, and intra-articular routes are novel strategies that need to be designed based on the spatial distribution characteristics of the abnormality of ribosome biogenesis [36], for instance, stimulus-responsive release system design based on the stress and mechanical signaling properties at the cartilage-bone junction may increase drug accumulation and prolong drug action time at the lesion site, thus improving efficacy and reducing systemic exposure.

The use of biomarker validating ribosome biogenesis indicators in clinical samples should be the first step for advancing ribosome biogenesis‑related therapies into the clinic. Research findings indicated that ribosome biogenesis RNA and protein levels in OA cartilage are significantly abnormal, and these features have strong correlations with disease progression stages, with rRNA transcription levels and protein translational activity displaying stage-specific alterations [16]. Additionally, RNA-binding proteins such as TIPARP are highly expressed in healthy cartilage, but significantly down-regulated in OA and have strong connections with pathological cell populations making them useful biomarkers and drug targets [38]. Most of these indicators are still in the basic and preclinical research stages, and they lack systematic validation in large-scale clinical samples and standards of detection. Future multi-centre, multi-stage clinical studies with high-throughput sequencing and proteomics technology will be developed diagnostic and prognostic evaluation system of ribosome biogenesis-related biomarkers and personalised therapy formulation.

In summary, clinical translation and drug development of ribosome biogenesis require concerted efforts to tackle issues related to safety and specificity of drugs, fine-tuning of delivery systems, and clinical identification of biomarkers. The need for interdisciplinary study of ribosome biogenesis-targeting strategy application in these subjects requires the latest developments in molecular biology, medicinal chemistry as well as clinical medicine. Furthermore, the construction of experimental models that more closely replicate human pathophysiological conditions is required [16, 17, 36, 38].

Limitations of the current study

While this review synthesizes the burgeoning evidence linking ribosome biogenesis to osteoarthritis (OA), it is imperative to acknowledge the limitations inherent in the current body of literature. A balanced perspective reveals significant gaps that must be bridged before ribosome-targeted therapies can transition to the clinic.

Methodological and Model Limitations.The majority of mechanistic insights into OA ribosome biogenesis are derived from in vitrotwo-dimensional (2D) monolayer cultures of chondrocytes or immortalized cell lines (e.g., SW1353). These systems fail to recapitulate the zonal architecture, mechanical loading, and complex nutrient gradients of native articular cartilage. Although organ-on-a-chip models (OCU-on-Chip) represent a significant advance [36], they remain simplified representations of the osteochondral unit. Furthermore, much of the human tissue data is cross-sectional and derived from end-stage OA patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty. This design limits our ability to infer temporal causality—whether ribosome dysfunction is a primary driver of early OA or a consequence of end-stage tissue degeneration.

Translational and Therapeutic Challenges.

Several translational hurdles remain:

Delivery Specificity: delivering nucleic acids (siRNA/ASOs for U3 or RBPs) to the deep zones of avascular cartilage is inefficient.

Safety Window: Ribosomes are essential for all cells. Systemic targeting of eIF6 or snoRNAs carries unacceptable risks of hematological toxicity. The therapeutic window for modulating a housekeeping process like translation is likely very narrow.

Biomarker Validation: While molecules like TIPARP and U3 snoRNA are proposed biomarkers [38], they lack validation in large, multi-center, longitudinal human cohorts. Their utility in predicting OA progression or treatment response remains unproven.

Addressing these limitations through the development of better in vivomodels, rigorous causal validation, and safer delivery systems will be the focus of the next generation of OA research.

Conclusion

Ribosome biogenesis is an essential process for protein synthesis. Growing evidence indicates it plays an important role in the pathogenicity of osteoarthritis (OA). This suggests that studies addressing ribosome biogenesis and OA have taken a big leap forward. Due to an expert opinion on ribosome biogenesis abnormalities in chondrocytes, they are not merely metabolic unbalance but they also regulate inflammatory response and fate. This provides a new angle to understand the OA complexity. Historically OA has generally been regarded as a degenerative disease due to mechanical wear and tear and metabolic disorders. By redefining it as an “acquired ribosomopathy,” the conventional approach to the disease is broken and researchers are encouraged to re-explore the molecular basis of the disease.

This new insight could enrich OA pathophysiology theory and open wide avenues for therapeutic strategy development. Ribosome biogenesis regulatory factors like noncoding RNAs and protein factors that mediate translation initiation are central nodes regulating protein synthesis while providing potential highly site-specific drug action for any disease processes. Recent advances have made gains at the molecular mechanism level. However, specific regulatory factors and their mechanisms of action have been subjected to ongoing debates across various studies. This is in light of OA heterogeneity. Future research will centre on balancing and integrating such findings and other diverse data. The multiomics techniques enable observing dynamic alterations of ribosome biogenesis at the genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic levels. Furthermore, metabolomics assists in deciphering the specific mode of action in OA.

Both in vivo and in vitro disease models are continuously improving and they have powerful potential to better study ribosome biogenesis function. This will allow more theoretical investigation to be better linked to clinical investigation. In replicating the pathological environment of OA, therapeutic evaluation and safety prediction for ribosome biogenesis regulatory factors can be conducted, thereby promoting the transformation from basic research to clinical application. In future, with the incorporation of the concept of precision medicine, it may be possible to develop diseasemodifying drugs targeting ribosome biogenesis abnormalities with actual efficacy to relieve OA symptoms and slow disease progression.

Ribosome biogenesis is crucial in the development of OA. In-depth studies on the regulatory mechanism of ribosome biogenesis can not only shed light on the pathology of OA, it can also offer hope for diagnosis and treatment of the same. By using interdisciplinary approaches and combining molecular biology, clinical medicine, and bioinformatics, it can be revealed that ribosome biogenesis abnormalities are fundamentally connected to OA, which in turn can lead to effective and accurate therapeutic interventions. By continuing down this path, OA research will rise a notch, and a myriad of patients will benefit.
