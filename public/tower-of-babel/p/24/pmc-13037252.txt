Systemic inflammatory mediators and neurodevelopmental outcomes in term infants: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ning Xu, Huijing Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the relationship between systemic inflammatory mediators and early neurodevelopmental outcomes in term infants following invasive bacterial infection.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed neonates admitted to a single center from March 2016 to February 2019. Clinical data from birth to 120 days were reviewed. Fifty term infants with clinically diagnosed invasive bacterial infection (the vast majority with culture confirmation) constituted the exposure group, matched with fifty term infants without any inflammatory history. Neurodevelopment was assessed at 18–22 months using the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development-IV (BSID-IV) as the primary comprehensive tool and the Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ) to derive a developmental quotient (DQ) and incorporate parent-reported measures, providing a multi-faceted evaluation. Serum levels of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, MMP-2, and MMP-9 were measured during the acute infection phase.ResultsThe infection group demonstrated significantly lower BSID-IV composite scores (82.19 ± 1.55 vs. 89.59 ± 1.52, P < 0.001) and ASQ-derived DQs (80.25 ± 4.85 vs. 89.14 ± 5.83, P < 0.001) compared to controls. These infants also exhibited markedly elevated levels of all inflammatory mediators (all P < 0.001), with IL-6 showing the most pronounced elevation (157.89 ± 11.17 pg/mL vs. 5.16 ± 0.58 pg/mL). Furthermore, a history of invasive infection remained a significant independent predictor of poorer developmental outcomes in multivariable analysis (β = -6.92 for BSID, P < 0.001). All inflammatory markers showed significant but weak-to-moderate inverse correlations with neurodevelopmental scores.ConclusionIn term infants, invasive bacterial infection and its associated acute systemic inflammatory response are strongly associated with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes at 18–22 months, independent of key demographic confounders. Integrating early cytokine profiling with routine developmental screening may aid in the early recognition of infants at potential risk, though further research is needed to determine if these associations are modifiable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06577-0.Keywords: Inflammatory factors, Neurodevelopment, Cytokines, Bayley scales

Neurodevelopmental (neurobehavioral) disorders comprise a spectrum of behavioral and cognitive deficits that usually surface in the first years of life and impede children’s progress in intellectual, motor, language and social domains [1]. The spectrum spans issues such as speech- or language-based communication impairments, autistic-spectrum traits, hyperactivity–attention dysregulation, learning difficulties, and motor-coordination conditions exemplified by developmental coordination disorder. Collectively, these disorders affect approximately 5–20% of the population [2, 3]. Their aetiology is multifactorial and remains unidentified for many individuals [4]. Clinical signs emerge early yet often persist into adulthood [5], and subtle presentations can postpone recognition, diagnosis and intervention [6].

The developing brain is particularly vulnerable during critical periods of rapid growth and differentiation, where insults such as inflammation can disrupt neurogenesis, migration, and synaptogenesis, leading to long-term neurodevelopmental impairments [7, 8]. For instance, perinatal inflammation has been shown to alter microglial function and induce excitotoxicity in immature neural circuits [9]. The immature brain’s vulnerability is heightened by an underdeveloped blood-brain barrier and ongoing processes like myelination and synaptic pruning, making it susceptible to systemic inflammatory signals that can trigger microglial activation, oxidative stress, and neuronal apoptosis, potentially contributing to disorders such as autism spectrum disorder and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder [10, 11]. Mounting evidence further implicates pro-inflammatory cytokines in the immune–neuroendocrine cascade activated after brain injury, regulating the synthesis and actions of other cytokines and thereby shaping downstream neural outcomes [12].

Interleukin family peptides—exemplified by IL−6 and IL−1β—display wide-ranging bioactivity. IL−6, a central pro-inflammatory mediator, orchestrates downstream cytokine cascades while regulating neuronal proliferation, maturation, repair and turnover across both central and peripheral nervous tissues [13]. During intra-uterine infection, surging IL−6 magnifies maternal–fetal inflammatory load and is strongly linked to chorioamnionitis, premature delivery and a threefold rise in periventricular white-matter injury among very-pre-term neonates [14]. Excess placental TNF-α likewise marks heightened inflammation and predicts less favorable neurological trajectories in newborns [15]. Among matrix metalloproteinases, neuronal MMP−9 fine-tunes dendritic spine architecture and synaptic efficacy; when dysregulated, it has been implicated in epilepsy, psychotic disorders, autism-spectrum traits, traumatic and ischemic brain injury, neurodegenerative conditions, chronic pain syndromes and intracranial tumors—effects largely traced to its immunomodulatory actions [16]. MMP−2 performs complementary roles, underpinning cognitive and motor performance and proving indispensable for neural growth, adaptation and repair [17]. Because early brain maturation is extraordinarily sensitive to immune-related cues, clarifying how these mediators steer developmental trajectories—and quantifying their ties to standardized developmental metrics—holds considerable clinical relevance.

Participants and methods

Participants

This study employed a retrospective cohort design with prospective follow-up. Data were retrospectively collected from medical records of full-term newborns admitted from March 2016 to February 2019, covering clinical information from birth to 120 days after birth. Neurodevelopmental assessments were then conducted prospectively at 18–22 months of age. Fifty infants with at least one episode of laboratory-confirmed invasive bacterial infection were selected as the infection group, and fifty infants without any history of inflammatory infection served as the control group. Invasive bacterial infection was operationally defined as the presence of bacterial pathogens isolated from normally sterile body sites, including blood (bacteremia/sepsis), cerebrospinal fluid (meningitis), urine obtained by suprapubic aspiration or catheterization (urinary tract infection), or other sterile sites (e.g., joint fluid, pleural fluid), accompanied by clinical signs of systemic infection such as fever, hypothermia, respiratory distress, hemodynamic instability, or altered mental status [18, 19]. All cases required microbiological confirmation by positive culture from the sterile site and were treated with intravenous antibiotics for a minimum of 7 days. Infants with clinical sepsis (suspected infection with negative cultures) were excluded from the infection group to ensure diagnostic specificity.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Qingtian County People’s Hospital (No: ) on September 15, 2020]. For the retrospective component involving the collection and analysis of pre-existing clinical data and archived serum samples, the IRB granted a waiver of informed consent. For the prospective neurodevelopmental follow-up assessments conducted at 18–22 months of age, written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participating infants prior to the assessment. All eligible parents agreed to participate; there were no refusals. Inclusion criteria were as follows: children within the early childhood age range of 0–2 years and availability of detailed inflammation-related factor level testing and neurodevelopmental assessment results. Exclusion criteria were as follows: children outside the age range of 0–2 years and studies lacking detailed inflammation-related factor level testing and neurodevelopmental assessment results.

The sample size of 50 participants per group was determined by the availability of eligible cases in this retrospective study and was justified by an a priori power calculation assuming a clinically meaningful mean difference of 10 points in Bayley composite scores between groups (standard deviation = 15, consistent with Bayley scale norms and prior research on neurodevelopmental impacts of neonatal infection [20, 21]. Using a two-sided independent samples t-test with alpha = 0.05 and 80% power, the estimated required sample size was approximately 37 per group; our cohort of 50 per group exceeds this threshold, providing sufficient statistical power to detect the observed differences while accounting for potential variability or minor attrition in follow-up data (Fig. 1).

Method

Data collection

Clinical data were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records, covering the period from birth to 120 days of age. For all infants, the following data were extracted: demographic characteristics (sex, gestational age, ethnicity), perinatal parameters (birth weight, mode of delivery), and routine clinical measures (weight, height). For infants in the infection group, detailed information regarding the invasive infection episode was collected, including: infection type (e.g., sepsis, meningitis, pneumonia, pyelonephritis), time of onset (postnatal age at diagnosis), microbiological culture results (pathogen identification and antibiotic susceptibility), C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, white blood cell count (WBC) and differential, duration of antibiotic therapy, and documented risk factors (e.g., maternal Group B Streptococcus colonization, premature rupture of membranes > 18 h, small for gestational age status). For infants in the control group, the reason for the initial neonatal admission was recorded. Long-term complications occurring during the follow-up period (e.g., sensorineural hearing loss, epilepsy, bronchopulmonary dysplasia) were also documented from follow-up clinical records for the infection group.

Diagnostic criteria and classification of invasive infections

All invasive bacterial infections were classified according to the site of infection and microbiological findings. Sepsis was defined as bacteremia with systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) criteria adapted for neonates [22], including temperature instability (> 38.5 °C or < 36 °C), tachycardia or bradycardia, tachypnea, and abnormal white blood cell count. Meningitis was confirmed by positive cerebrospinal fluid culture and/or pleocytosis (> 20 white blood cells/µL in term infants) with elevated protein and decreased glucose, consistent with established diagnostic criteria [23]. Urinary tract infection required > 10⁵ colony-forming units per milliliter from a catheterized or suprapubic specimen with accompanying pyuria, following American Academy of Pediatrics guidelines [24]. Pneumonia was diagnosed by radiographic infiltrates with positive blood culture or bronchoalveolar lavage culture in ventilated infants. The severity of infection was documented using clinical parameters including need for intensive care admission, mechanical ventilation, vasopressor support, and duration of hospitalization. Pathogen identification included Gram-positive organisms (e.g., Group B Streptococcus, Staphylococcus aureus), Gram-negative organisms (e.g., Escherichia coli, Klebsiella species), and other bacterial species, with antibiotic susceptibility patterns recorded.

Laboratory assessments and sample collection

Venous blood samples (approximately 3 mL) were collected from all participants in the infection group during the acute phase of the first documented invasive infection episode (within 24 h of hospital admission for that episode), and during a routine well-baby visit for the control group. For the infection group, the timing of sample collection was chosen to capture the peak inflammatory response associated with the diagnosed invasive infection. Serum was separated by centrifugation and stored at−80 °C until batch analysis. Serum concentrations of principal immune mediators—IL−6, IL−1β, tumor-necrosis-factor-α (TNF-α)—and the activities of matrix metalloproteinase−2 (MMP−2) and−9 (MMP−9) were quantified using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits in accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols. Specifically, the following kits from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA) were used: Human IL−6 (cat. no. ab178013; detection range: 7.8 pg/mL–500 pg/mL), Human TNF-α (cat. no. ab181421; detection range: 15.6 pg/mL–1000 pg/mL), Human IL−1β (cat. no. ab214025; detection range: 1.1 pg/mL–70 pg/mL), Human MMP−2 (cat. no. ab100606; detection range: 0.1 ng/mL–10 ng/mL), and Human MMP−9 (cat. no. ab100610; detection range: 0.16 ng/mL–10 ng/mL). All samples were analyzed in duplicate, and any samples from the infection group with concentrations exceeding the standard curve range were diluted 1:10 with the provided diluent buffer and re-assayed. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation for all kits were below 10% and 12%, respectively. Routine inflammatory indices, including C-reactive protein (CRP) and full blood count (FBC) with differential white blood cell count, were measured simultaneously using standard clinical laboratory techniques. Microbiological culture results (e.g., from blood, cerebrospinal fluid) were recorded for the infection group to confirm the etiology and focus of invasive infection.

To address the timing of sample collection, the exact postnatal age (in days) at the time of blood draw was recorded for all infants in both groups. For the infection group, this corresponded to the age at diagnosis of the invasive infection. For the control group, this corresponded to the age at the routine well-baby visit when the research blood sample was obtained.

Neurodevelopmental assessment

Neurodevelopment was assessed using two complementary tools. The primary and comprehensive assessment was conducted using the fourth edition of the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development (BSID-IV) [20], a clinician-administered evaluation that profiles five functional domains—cognition, language, motor performance, social-emotional interaction, and adaptive behavior. All BSID-IV assessments were performed by two trained pediatric developmental specialists who had completed standardized BSID-IV certification training and had at least 3 years of experience in administering developmental assessments. To ensure inter-rater reliability, both evaluators independently assessed 10 pilot cases (not included in the study cohort) prior to study commencement, achieving an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.92 for composite scores, indicating excellent agreement. To minimize assessment bias, evaluators were blinded to the infant’s group assignment (infection vs. control), medical history, and inflammatory biomarker results at the time of assessment. Clinical records were reviewed only after completion of all neurodevelopmental testing. Blinding was maintained by having a research coordinator schedule appointments and provide only the child’s name, age, and contact information to the evaluators. Parents were instructed not to discuss their child’s medical history during the assessment session. All assessments were conducted in a standardized testing environment at the hospital’s developmental follow-up clinic. The assessment scores were combined to yield a composite score: <70 indicates developmental delay, 70–84 indicates mild impairment or risk of developmental delay, and ≥ 85 indicates normal development.

Complementary assessment and developmental quotient calculation

Additionally, the Ages and Stages Questionnaires (ASQ) for children aged 0–6 years [25] was employed as a parent-reported screening tool. This dual-instrument approach allowed for the integration of professional assessment with caregiver perspectives, enhancing the robustness of follow-up tracking [26, 27]. The results from both instruments were analyzed independently and then compared for consistency. The ASQ covers five domains: gross motor skills, fine motor skills, communication, problem-solving, and personal-social. To calculate the developmental quotient (DQ), an adapted protocol was followed to determine the developmental age [28]. Beginning with the age-appropriate ASQ questionnaire, if a child’s score in any domain fell below the cutoff (more than 2 standard deviations below the mean), lower age-interval questionnaires were sequentially administered until an appropriate developmental level was identified. Developmental age equivalents were assigned for each domain based on the child’s scores, and an overall developmental age was computed by averaging these equivalents across the five domains. The developmental quotient was then calculated as (overall developmental age / chronological age) × 100. A DQ ≥ 130 indicates excellent development, 115–129 indicates above-average development, 85–114 indicates normal development, 70–84 indicates below-average development, and ≤ 69 indicates developmental delay.

Socioeconomic status assessment

Socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed using paternal education level as a proxy measure, which has been validated as a reliable indicator of family SES in Chinese populations [29]. Paternal education was categorized into three ordinal levels: (1) middle school or below (≤ 9 years of education), (2) high school or vocational training (10–12 years), and (3) college or above (≥ 13 years). This information was obtained from hospital admission records at the time of birth. In cases where paternal education data were missing from the birth record (n = 3 in the infection group, n = 2 in the control group), maternal education level was used as a substitute, applying the same categorical coding.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 21.0). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using independent samples t-tests, with results reported as mean differences, 95% confidence intervals, Cohen’s d effect sizes, t-values, and P-values. For BSID-IV scaled scores, which are designed to be normally distributed, normality was confirmed by Shapiro-Wilk test prior to parametric testing. Continuous variables with non-normal distribution were expressed as median with interquartile range. For routine inflammatory parameters and ASQ domain scores, between-group comparisons were performed using Mann-Whitney U test, with median differences and 95% confidence intervals estimated using the Hodges-Lehmann method. For inflammatory cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases, which typically exhibit right-skewed distributions, natural log-transformation was applied prior to analysis using independent samples t-test with Welch’s correction for unequal variances. Results for these biomarkers are presented as median with interquartile range in their original scale, alongside back-transformed geometric mean ratios comparing infection to control groups with 95% confidence intervals. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages and compared using Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Correlations between inflammatory marker levels and neurodevelopmental scores were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient due to non-normal distribution of biomarkers, with results reported as rho values and P-values. To adjust for potential confounding factors, separate multivariable linear regression models were constructed with BSID-IV composite score and developmental quotient as dependent variables. Independent variables included group assignment, sex, birth weight, gestational age, and socioeconomic status measured by paternal education level. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, with VIF values below 2.0 considered acceptable. Regression results are presented as unstandardized coefficients, standard errors, standardized coefficients, 95% confidence intervals, and P-values. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding meningitis cases to assess robustness of findings across infection severity spectrum. Additionally, an exploratory composite inflammatory index was created by summing standardized z-scores of all five log-transformed inflammatory mediators measured during acute illness, and its association with neurodevelopmental outcomes was examined. To account for multiple comparisons, P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction where applicable. For between-group comparisons of inflammatory parameters, cytokines, and neurodevelopmental domains, FDR correction was applied within each table. For correlation analyses, FDR correction was applied across all tested associations (5 cytokines × 2 outcomes = 10 comparisons). Adjusted P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant after FDR correction. All five cases with substituted data were flagged, and sensitivity analyses confirmed that their inclusion did not materially affect the regression results. No other missing data were encountered for the primary variables of interest (group assignment, neurodevelopmental outcomes, inflammatory biomarkers), as these were required for study inclusion. The threshold for statistical significance was set at a two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 for all analyses.

Detailed clinical characterization of the invasive bacterial infection group

The invasive bacterial infection group comprised 50 term infants diagnosed with at least one episode of culture-proven invasive infection between day 1 and 120 of life. The detailed clinical characteristics of this cohort are presented in Table 2. The median age at the onset of the first documented infection was 18 days (IQR: 5–45 days). The most common infection type was late-onset sepsis (LOS, occurring after 72 h of life), diagnosed in 26 infants (52%), followed by pneumonia (18%), bacteremia without a focal source (14%), meningitis (10%), and pyelonephritis (6%). A subset of infants (12%) was diagnosed with early-onset sepsis (EOS). Regarding risk factors, 8% of infants were small for gestational age (SGA), 28% were born to mothers with prolonged rupture of membranes (PROM > 18 h), and 18% had mothers with documented Group B Streptococcus (GBS) colonization. Importantly, 6 infants (12%) experienced more than one episode of invasive infection during the study period. Pathogenic bacteria were identified in 46 cases (92% culture positivity), with the most prevalent pathogens being Escherichia coli (34%), Staphylococcus aureus (28%), and Group B Streptococcus (16%). The median duration of antibiotic treatment for the initial infection episode was 10 days (IQR: 7–14 days). Clinical chorioamnionitis was histologically confirmed in the placentas of 8% of the mothers in the infection group.

Comparison of routine inflammatory parameters between the groups

As detailed in Table 3, the infection group exhibited a profound and statistically significant systemic inflammatory response at the time of diagnosis compared to the healthy controls. Both CRP and WBC were markedly elevated in the infection group. The median CRP level in the infection group was 35.2 mg/L (IQR: 28.5–43.8), which was drastically higher than the 3.1 mg/L (IQR: 2.5–4.9) observed in the control group, with a median difference of 32.1 mg/L (95% CI: 28.9 to 35.6, P < 0.001). Similarly, the median WBC count was significantly higher in the infection group (18.5 × 10⁹/L, IQR: 15.8–22.3) compared to the control group (10.2 × 10⁹/L, IQR: 9.1–13.5), with a median difference of 8.3 × 10⁹/L (95% CI: 6.5 to 10.2, P < 0.001).

Comparison of overall neurodevelopmental status between the groups

Comprehensive neurodevelopmental assessment at 18–22 months of age revealed significantly poorer outcomes in the infection group compared to the control group, as detailed in Table 4. Infants in the infection group had substantially lower scores on both the BSID-IV composite score (mean ± SD: 82.19 ± 1.55 vs. 89.59 ± 1.52, mean difference:−7.40, 95% CI:−8.01 to−6.79, P < 0.001) and the DQ calculated from the ASQ (80.25 ± 4.85 vs. 89.14 ± 5.83, mean difference:−8.89, 95% CI:−11.02 to−6.76, P < 0.001). Independent samples t-tests were used for these comparisons after confirming normal distribution of both outcome measures using the Shapiro-Wilk test (P > 0.05). The magnitude of these differences was very large, with effect sizes (Cohen’s d) of 4.83 for the BSID-IV composite score and 1.68 for the DQ, unequivocally demonstrating a strong association between a history of invasive bacterial infection in infancy and adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in early childhood.

Fine-grained analysis of neurodevelopmental domains

A domain-specific analysis of neurodevelopment revealed a pervasive pattern of deficits across multiple functional areas in the infection group compared to the control group, as detailed in Table 5. On the clinician-administered BSID-IV, which uses scaled scores designed to be normally distributed, the infection group scored significantly lower in all five domains assessed by independent samples t-tests (all P < 0.001). The most pronounced deficits, indicated by the largest effect sizes, were observed in the language domain (mean ± SD: 81.5 ± 7.3 vs. 95.8 ± 6.5, Cohen’s d = 2.01) and motor domain (83.8 ± 6.9 vs. 96.1 ± 6.2, Cohen’s d = 1.92), followed by adaptive ehavior (84.6 ± 6.0 vs. 91.9 ± 5.7, Cohen’s d = 1.25), cognitive (85.2 ± 6.1 vs. 92.5 ± 5.8, Cohen’s d = 1.22), and social-emotional domains (87.1 ± 5.5 vs. 93.4 ± 5.2, Cohen’s d = 1.17).

Long-term complications and control group information

During the follow-up period, additional long-term complications were documented in a subset of the infection group. Specifically, sensorineural hearing loss was identified in 3 infants (6%), all of whom had a history of bacterial meningitis. One infant (2%) who recovered from meningitis developed post-infectious epilepsy. Additionally, 4 infants (8%) were diagnosed with bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) following severe sepsis and pneumonia requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation. No other major organ system sequelae were recorded. Regarding the control group, the initial hospital admissions during the neonatal period were for transient and non-severe conditions that resolved without sequelae and were not associated with systemic inflammation or known neurological risk. The reasons for admission included physiological jaundice requiring phototherapy (n = 28), transient tachypnea of the newborn (n = 12), brief observation for mild perinatal asphyxia (Apgar score of 7 at 5 min, n = 6), and feeding difficulties requiring short-term support (n = 4). All control infants were healthy and developing normally at the time of enrollment into the study.

Sensitivity analysis: neurodevelopmental outcomes in an age-matched subset

To further mitigate any potential residual confounding from sampling age, we performed a sensitivity analysis. We identified a strict age-matched subset of 35 pairs of infants (n = 70), where each infant in the infection group was matched to a control infant within a 3-day window of the sampling age. In this matched subset, the infection group continued to demonstrate significantly lower BSID-IV composite scores (mean difference:−7.15, 95% CI:−8.02 to−6.28, P < 0.001) and Developmental Quotients (mean difference:−8.41, 95% CI:−11.80 to−5.02, P < 0.001) compared to their age-matched controls. These results are consistent with the primary analysis and reinforce that the association between invasive infection and adverse neurodevelopment is robust and not an artifact of sampling timing.

Sensitivity analyses and multicollinearity diagnostics

To ensure the robustness of our findings and address potential confounding by infection severity, we conducted sensitivity analyses excluding the 12 cases with meningitis, the most severe infection subtype in our cohort. As detailed in Supplementary Table S1, group differences in both BSID-IV composite scores and DQ remained highly statistically significant with large effect sizes when comparing non-meningitis infections to controls, though meningitis cases demonstrated even more pronounced impairments. This confirms that neurodevelopmental deficits are present across the spectrum of invasive bacterial infection severity, not solely driven by the most severe cases.

Multicollinearity diagnostics revealed all variance inflation factors in our regression models were below 2.0, indicating no problematic collinearity among predictors. To explore whether a composite measure of inflammatory burden might better predict neurodevelopmental outcomes than individual biomarkers, we created a standardized inflammatory index by summing z-scores of all five log-transformed mediators. As shown in Supplementary Table S2, 3, this composite index showed a weak non-significant correlation with BSID-IV scores and DQ, and did not add significant predictive value beyond group assignment in multivariable models. This reinforces our finding that the relationship between acute inflammatory magnitude and long-term neurodevelopmental trajectories is complex and non-linear.

Multivariable regression analysis of factors associated with neurodevelopmental outcomes

To determine whether the association between invasive bacterial infection and adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes was independent of potential confounding factors, we performed multivariable linear regression analyses. After adjusting for sex, birth weight, gestational age, and family socioeconomic status (SES, as represented by paternal education level), a history of invasive infection remained a strong and statistically significant independent predictor of both a lower BSID-IV composite score (β =−6.92, P < 0.001) and a lower developmental quotient (β =−8.15, P < 0.001), as detailed in Table 8. Among the covariates, higher family socioeconomic status was significantly associated with better outcomes in both models (BSID: β = 1.05, P = 0.012; DQ: β = 1.22, P = 0.008). Neither sex, birth weight, nor gestational age were significant independent predictors in the final models. These results confirm that the robust negative association between early-life invasive infection and subsequent neurodevelopment is not explained by these baseline demographic and perinatal factors.

Discussion

This study found that term infants with a history of invasive bacterial infection in early life exhibited significantly poorer neurodevelopmental outcomes at 18–22 months compared to matched controls without such infections. The baseline characteristics, including sex, age at assessment, weight, height, gestational age, and ethnicity, showed no significant differences between the groups, which supports the comparability of the cohorts and strengthens the attribution of observed differences to the infection exposure. This matching minimizes potential biases from demographic factors, consistent with similar cohort studies where balanced baselines allowed for clearer isolation of infection effects on development [30]. The infection group was characterized by a median onset age of 18 days, with late-onset sepsis as the most common type, followed by pneumonia and other focal infections, and predominant pathogens like Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus. These clinical features align with epidemiological patterns in term neonates, where gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria dominate invasive infections [31], though our cohort had a higher proportion of multiple episodes than some reports, potentially amplifying inflammatory exposure.

Routine inflammatory parameters, such as CRP and WBC, were markedly elevated in the infection group, with large effect sizes indicating a robust systemic response at diagnosis. This elevation reflects the acute phase reaction typical in bacterial invasions, and our findings echo those in recent cohorts where high CRP levels during neonatal sepsis correlated with prolonged hospital stays and increased complication risks in term infants. The overall neurodevelopmental status was substantially worse in the infection group, with lower BSID-IV composite scores and ASQ developmental quotients, accompanied by very large effect sizes. These results suggest a strong link between early infection and developmental delays, similar to systematic reviews showing neonatal sepsis associates with impaired cognitive and motor outcomes in term and near-term infants [32, 33], though differences arise in the magnitude, possibly due to varying follow-up durations. Our innovation lies in focusing exclusively on term infants, a less studied group compared to preterms, highlighting that even in this population, infection poses significant risks without the confounding prematurity factors common in other research [34].

A detailed domain analysis revealed deficits across all BSID-IV and ASQ areas in the infection group, with the most pronounced impairments in language/communication and motor domains, as evidenced by the largest effect sizes. This pattern indicates selective vulnerability, differing from some preterm-focused studies where cognitive domains predominate [35, 36], but aligning with reports on term infants where motor and language delays persist post-sepsis, possibly reflecting infection timing during critical myelination and synaptic pruning periods. Serum levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and MMP-2, MMP-9 were dramatically higher in the infection group, with extreme effect sizes underscoring profound inflammation. Elevated levels of these mediators are correlated with known pathways of neurotoxicity, such as blood-brain barrier disruption and oxidative stress, as supported by animal models and human studies linking elevated IL-6 and TNF-α to white matter injury in neonatal brains. Including MMPs adds novelty, as few clinical studies in term infants measure these alongside cytokines, revealing their role in extracellular matrix remodeling that may exacerbate neuronal damage during infection [37].

Correlation analyses revealed that only IL-1β demonstrated a statistically significant weak negative correlation with BSID-IV scores, while other inflammatory mediators showed no significant associations. These findings suggest that although invasive bacterial infection is clearly associated with both elevated inflammatory markers and impaired neurodevelopmental outcomes, the direct relationship between individual cytokine concentrations during acute illness and subsequent developmental performance is complex and not consistently linear. This is consistent with integrative reviews noting that cytokine elevations predict neuroimpairment in infants, yet strength varies with measurement timing and confounders like infection severity [38]. The selective significance of IL-1β warrants further investigation into the specific neurotoxic mechanisms of this cytokine in the developing brain. The modest correlations suggest that acute inflammatory burden, while contributory, is not the sole determinant, likely reflecting multifactorial mechanisms encompassing direct cytokine neurotoxicity, cerebrovascular injury, and metabolic derangements. Long-term complications in the infection group included sensorineural hearing loss in those with meningitis, post-infectious epilepsy, and bronchopulmonary dysplasia, aligning with cohort data showing neonatal meningitis increases risks of auditory impairment and epilepsy [39], though our rates were lower possibly due to prompt antibiotic therapy and term status reducing vulnerability. The control group’s transient admissions without inflammation further validate the contrast.

Multivariable regression confirmed that invasive infection independently predicted lower BSID and DQ scores after adjusting for sex, birth weight, gestational age, and socioeconomic status, with higher SES linked to better outcomes. This independence mirrors findings in preterm cohorts but innovates by demonstrating it in term infants, where adjustments often overlook SES, emphasizing infection’s direct role beyond perinatal factors [40, 41]. From a mechanistic perspective, our observations are consistent with experimental models in which cytokine elevations are associated with microglial activation and apoptosis in developing brain regions, particularly affecting motor and language pathways, as inflammation disrupts neurogenesis and connectivity in vulnerable periods.

Our sensitivity analyses excluding meningitis cases confirmed that neurodevelopmental impairments persist across the spectrum of infection severity, not solely in the most critically ill patients, suggesting that even moderate systemic bacterial infections in early infancy carry neurodevelopmental risks. The failure of a composite inflammatory index to substantially improve outcome prediction beyond individual biomarkers or group assignment alone highlights the mechanistic complexity of infection-related brain injury, which likely involves not only inflammatory mediator concentrations but also temporal dynamics, blood-brain barrier integrity, pathogen-specific factors, and individual host susceptibility.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The sample size of 50 per group, while powered for primary outcomes, remains modest and may limit generalizability, potentially underestimating rare effects or interactions. The single-center retrospective design introduces selection bias and restricts applicability to diverse populations or settings with varying infection management protocols. Although we adjusted for key confounders in regression, unmeasured variables like maternal nutrition or genetic factors could influence results. Using two developmental tools, BSID-IV as primary and ASQ as complementary, adds depth but may introduce variability from clinician versus parent reports, though correlations supported consistency. The weak cytokine-outcome correlations highlight that associations are modest, possibly diluted by single-timepoint measurements or residual confounding. Causality cannot be inferred due to the observational nature, and follow-up at 18–22 months may miss later-emerging deficits.

Future research should involve larger, multicenter prospective studies with serial cytokine profiling and advanced neuroimaging to elucidate mechanisms and identify biomarkers for early intervention. Exploring targeted monitoring for high-risk domains like language and motor could inform tailored therapies, while investigating modifiable factors such as SES might guide preventive strategies. Overall, these findings provide evidence of an association between early invasive bacterial infections in term infants and adverse neurodevelopment, underscoring the importance of monitoring and further study of potential long-term outcomes.
