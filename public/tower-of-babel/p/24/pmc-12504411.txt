Association of unhealthy lifestyle and the risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus with arthritis in middle-aged and elderly Chinese individuals: evidence from the CHARLS
Authors: KaiJian Zhang, Hui Li, JingHan Chu, Nan Chen, HongLi Yan, BuChun Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultimorbidity is an emerging global public health concern. However, the relationship between a healthy lifestyle, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and arthritis remains unclear. Our study aimed to evaluate the impact of a healthy lifestyle on the comorbidity of T2DM and arthritis in the Chinese population.MethodsThe data originated from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and comprised 4,706 participants aged 45 years or over. The healthy lifestyle score (0–5) was determined by factors including smoking, drinking, physical activity, sleep and body mass index (BMI). T2DM was assessed using a self-reported physician diagnosis, fasting blood glucose levels ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or a history of taking hypoglycaemic medication. Arthritis was evaluated using a self-reported physician diagnosis at baseline. Multivariate logistic regression models and stratified analyses were used to estimate the relationship between healthy lifestyles and the risk of T2DM in patients with arthritis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were analysed to investigate the association between an unhealthy lifestyle, T2DM and arthritis risk.ResultsCompared with healthy lifestyle participants, those with unhealthy lifestyles had an increased risk of diabetes (OR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.01–1.95) and comorbid diabetes‒arthritis (OR = 1.73, 95% CI: 1.01–2.96), but no significant associations with arthritis were detected. In the stratified analysis, stronger associations were found between an unhealthy lifestyle and diabetes-arthritis comorbidity in people aged 45–65 years and in those who were married. ROC analysis revealed high diagnostic accuracy of unhealthy lifestyle for both diabetes (AUC = 0.894) and diabetes-arthritis comorbidity (AUC = 0.894).ConclusionAn unhealthy lifestyle was significantly associated with diabetes-arthritis comorbidity among participants ≥ 45 years of age in China, which suggesting that promoting healthy lifestyle may be important for reducing the burden of chronic disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03196-0.Keywords: Unhealthy lifestyle, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Arthritis, China health and retirement longitudinal study, Cohort study

Introduction

The growing prevalence of chronic diseases among middle-aged and older adults is a major global public health concern. Two of the most common chronic diseases are diabetes and arthritis, which significantly affect quality of life, healthcare costs and mortality [1]. Lifestyle factors, including physical activity, diet, smoking and alcohol consumption, play a crucial role in the development and progression of these diseases [2]. However, the relationship between a healthy lifestyle and the co-occurrence of diabetes and arthritis is not well understood, particularly in ageing populations.

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) provides a valuable opportunity to investigate these associations within a nationally representative sample of middle-aged and older Chinese adults [3]. While previous studies have identified individual risk factors for diabetes and arthritis [4], few have examined how a healthy lifestyle influences their prevalence and comorbidity. Understanding these relationships is critical to developing targeted prevention strategies given China’s ageing population and increasing burden of chronic disease.

This study examined the associations between healthy lifestyle behaviours and the prevalence of diabetes and arthritis in middle-aged and older adults using data from the CHARLS. By analysing key lifestyle factors such as physical activity, diet, smoking and alcohol consumption, we sought to establish whether healthier lifestyles are associated with a lower likelihood of developing these conditions. Our findings could inform evidence-based recommendations for the prevention of chronic diseases and healthy ageing in China and similar populations.

Methods

Data sources and study design

Our population-based cross-sectional investigation used data from the 2013–2015 CHARLS, which is a nationally representative survey that used multistage probability sampling in 28 provinces and 450 villages [5]. The CHARLS protocol systematically collects longitudinal data on sociodemographic characteristics, health behaviours, and chronic disease diagnoses through standardised questionnaires and physical measurements. The eligibility criteria restricted participation to individuals aged ≥ 45 years with complete data on lifestyle exposures (e.g. smoking, alcohol consumption, sleep duration and BMI), outcome variables (e.g. diabetes and arthritis diagnoses) and core covariates. After excluding 1,203 participants due to missing data or age < 45 years, the final analytical cohort comprised 4,706 individuals (Fig. 1). The study participants were classified into four distinct groups based on their health status, with no overlap between categories: (1) no disease (n = 3,169), (2) diabetes (n = 315), (3) arthritis (n = 1,120), and (4) diabetes‒arthritis comorbidity (n = 102). Ethical approval was granted by the Peking University Biomedical Ethics Committee (IRB00001052-11015), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants enrolled in the study.

Flowchart of participant selection

Assessment of lifestyle factors

A composite health lifestyle score (range: 0–5) was constructed using five modifiable behavioural and physiological indicators, each of which was dichotomised into ‘healthy’ (1 point) or ‘unhealthy’ (0 points) categories [6, 7]. Sleep duration was categorised as either optimal (≥ 6 h per night, awarded 1 point) or suboptimal (< 6 h per night, awarded 0 points). BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2), with trained staff taking measurements via standardised protocols. Participants with a BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 kg/m² were classified as having a healthy weight. Information on smoking status and alcohol consumption was gathered via a self-report questionnaire. Participants were required to record their smoking history and alcohol consumption habits in the questionnaire survey.

Assessment of outcomes

The primary outcomes of interest were T2DM and arthritis. Diabetes status was determined using the following criteria: FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) levels ≥ 6.5%, current use of antidiabetic medication, or self-reported diagnosis(answering “Yes” to the question “Have you been diagnosed with diabetes or hyperglycaemia by a doctor?“) [8].

The arthritis diagnosis was based on self-reported data. Those who answered “Yes” to the question, “Have you been diagnosed with arthritis by a doctor?” were classified as arthritis patients.

Covariate adjustment

Covariates were chosen based on prior studies and clinical expertise [9]. The following covariates were included: age (stratified as 45–65 and > 65 years), sex (male/female), marital status (married/unmarried), and residence (rural and urban). Health behaviours included alcohol consumption and joint pain severity categorised by site and frequency. Psychosocial factors included health and life satisfaction. Clinical covariates included physician-diagnosed comorbidities such as heart disease, stroke, liver disease, lung disease, stomach disease, mental illness, asthma and kidney disease. Anthropometric and metabolic parameters included height, weight, waist circumference, fasting glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), C-reactive protein (CRP), HbA1c, uric acid (UA), creatinine and BMI (Table S1).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between groups using chi-square tests for categorical variables and ANOVA or t-tests for continuous measures. The associations between healthy lifestyle factors and disease risk were assessed using multivariable logistic regression models, which were constructed in three stages: Model 1 (unadjusted); Model 2 (adjusted for age, household income, and physical pain); and Model 3 (fully adjusted for all covariates). The results are expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05 (two-tailed). Stratified analyses examined heterogeneity by sex, age, marital status and history of heart disease, with interaction terms tested. The ability of the lifestyle scores to discriminate was assessed via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. An area under the curve (AUC) exceeding 0.7 was considered to be indicative of clinically meaningful diagnostic value. Several sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robustness of our findings, with further adjustment for different covariates. All the statistical analyses were conducted via R statistical software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

In this prospective cohort of the CHARLS, 4706 participants were enrolled and stratified into four mutually exclusive groups (Table 1). The prevalence of diabetes was 6.69%, that of arthritis was 23.8%, and that of diabetes-arthritis comorbidity was 2.17%. The majority of people in the middle-aged group (45–65 years) were in all disease categories (52.4% had diabetes, 63.6% had arthritis, and 59.8% had diabetes-arthritis comorbidity), with a notable female predominance in the arthritis (56.6%) and diabetes-arthritis comorbidity (66.7%) groups. BMI was higher in patients with diabetes (25.55 ± 3.60) and diabetes-arthritis comorbidity (24.77 ± 3.60) than in those with no disease (23.93 ± 3.89, P < 0.001). Similar trends were observed for wrist circumference, with diabetes (92.18 ± 10.51) and diabetes-arthritis (89.46 ± 10.17) comorbidities exceeding those with no disease (86.63 ± 10.49, P < 0.001). HbA1c levels were higher in patients with diabetes (7.59 ± 1.91) and comorbid diabetes-arthritis (7.36 ± 1.94) than in patients with no disease (5.86 ± 0.68, P < 0.001). Triglyceride levels were higher in the diabetes (173.19 ± 102.62) and diabetes-arthritis comorbidity (173.03 ± 116.28) subgroups than in the subgroup with no disease (141.54 ± 88.41, P < 0.001). Patients with comorbid diabetes and arthritis had a significantly higher prevalence of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease (34.3% had heart disease and 4.9% had experienced a stroke), as well as liver disease (12.7%) and kidney disease (23.5%). Satisfaction with health varied between groups: 56.8% of patients with diabetes and 66.4% of patients with arthritis reported satisfaction. However, dissatisfaction was prevalent in the group with both diabetes and arthritis (52.0%).

Associations between lifestyle factors and diabetes‒arthritis comorbidity

Logistic regression analyses revealed that an unhealthy lifestyle was significantly associated with an increased risk of diabetes and diabetes-arthritis comorbidity, but not arthritis alone. In the fully adjusted model (with all possible factors taken into account), individuals with an unhealthy lifestyle were found to be at significantly greater risk of both diabetes (OR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.01–1.95, P = 0.042) and diabetes-arthritis comorbidity (OR = 1.73, 95% CI: 1.01–2.96, P = 0.035), compared to those with a healthier lifestyle. Notably, no significant associations were detected between lifestyle factors and arthritis in isolation (Table 2, Tables S2-S3).

Stratified analysis

Stratified analyses were performed for several demographic and clinical variables, including sex (male/female), age group (45–65 years vs. >65 years), marital status (married vs. unmarried), and the presence of heart disease (yes/no), as shown in Fig. 2. The link between an unhealthy lifestyle and an increased risk of diabetes was stronger in men (OR = 2.21, P < 0.001) and in individuals aged ≥ 65 years (OR = 1.83, P < 0.001). By contrast, an increased risk of arthritis was mainly associated with females (OR = 1.48, P < 0.05) and married individuals (OR = 1.2, P = 0.016). The risk of comorbidity between diabetes and arthritis was particularly high in individuals aged 45–65 years (OR = 2.03, P = 0.014) and in married individuals (OR = 2.06, P = 0.004).

Diagnostic accuracy of lifestyle factors

ROC curve analysis revealed that indicators of an unhealthy lifestyle had high diagnostic value for diabetes and diabetes-arthritis comorbidity (both AUC = 0.894), whereas diagnostic accuracy for arthritis was limited (AUC = 0.665) (Fig. 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were systematically conducted to ensure the integrity of our findings. After adjusting for potential confounders, an unhealthy lifestyle was found to be significantly associated with an elevated risk of diabetes (Model 2: OR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.35–2.28, P < 0.001; Model 3: OR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.02–1.97, P = 0.036) and diabetes-arthritis comorbidity (Model 2: OR = 1.70, 95% CI: 1.08–2.69, P = 0.022; Model 3: OR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.03–3.01, P = 0.040) (Table 3, Table S4-S5). Additionally, the presence of multiple types of joint pain was significantly inversely associated with diabetes risk (model 2: OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.40–0.66, P < 0.001; model 3: OR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.36–0.69, P < 0.001) and diabetes‒arthritis comorbidity (model 2: OR = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.22–0.50, P < 0.001; model 3: OR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.24–0.68, P < 0.001) (Table 3, Table S4–S5). However, the models showed a non-significant trend towards an increased risk of diabetes-arthritis comorbidity with increasing age, male gender and marital status.

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between unhealthy lifestyle behaviours and the risk of diabetes and arthritis, and their associated comorbidities in middle-aged and older adults using data from the CHARLS. Our results revealed that unhealthy lifestyle behaviours were significantly associated with an increased risk of diabetes and diabetes-arthritis comorbidity. However, no significant association was found for arthritis alone. These results highlight the distinct impact of lifestyle factors on chronic diseases, indicating that diabetes and diabetes-arthritis comorbidity may be strongly influenced by modifiable behaviours, whereas arthritis may be more dependent on non-lifestyle factors such as genetics, ageing, and mechanical stress on the joints [10, 11].

Stratified analyses revealed important demographic differences in the associations between unhealthy lifestyles and diabetes risk. Consistent with previous studies, males were found to be at substantially greater risk than females, suggesting that men may be more susceptible to lifestyle-related metabolic disorders due to differences in fat distribution, hormonal influences, and health behaviours [12, 13]. Additionally, individuals aged 65 years and over were at an increased risk, possibly due to the accumulation of unhealthy behaviours over time, as well as age-related metabolic decline. Notably, the comorbidity of diabetes and arthritis was most prevalent among younger middle-aged adults (45–65 years old) and married individuals. This suggests that lifestyle factors in midlife may play a critical role in the development of multiple chronic conditions later in life [14]. Marriage is often associated with shared dietary and physical activity habits and may amplify the effects of unhealthy behaviours [15]. However, further research is required to investigate the psychosocial and behavioural mechanisms involved.

Our study used ROC analysis to demonstrate the significant impact of unhealthy lifestyles on the risk of both diabetes and diabetes-arthritis comorbidity. This indicates that modifiable lifestyle factors are reliable indicators of disease risk. Our findings imply that lifestyle-based risk assessment tools could facilitate the early identification of individuals at high risk in clinical and public health settings.

This study has several notable strengths. Firstly, the study uses a nationally representative, well-characterised, prospective cohort design, which enables rigorous evaluation of the causal relationship between an unhealthy lifestyle and diabetes-arthritis comorbidity. In addition to these methodological strengths, our results remained consistent when we performed different sensitivity analyses and adjusted for relevant confounders. However, the presence of residual confounding factors cannot be ruled out entirely. Several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to make causal interpretations. Secondly, relying on self-reported data on lifestyles and diseases introduces potential recall bias. Thirdly, the study focused on the Chinese ageing population, so the results cannot be generalised to other ethnic groups. Fourthly, arthritis was self-reported, which is common in large-scale surveys but may lead to misclassification due to discrepancies with clinical or radiographic diagnoses. Fifthly, our healthy lifestyle score did not include dietary patterns, which are a well-established factor in influencing the risk of both T2DM and inflammatory forms of arthritis. The absence of comprehensive dietary data in the dataset limits the comprehensiveness of our lifestyle assessment.

Finally, our study focused on individual-level lifestyle factors. However, future research would benefit from a broader approach, incorporating environmental and social determinants of health such as socioeconomic status, access to healthcare and external stressors like extreme weather events, which can impact food security and overall vulnerability in older populations [16]. A multi-level approach is essential for developing comprehensive public health strategies.

Conclusion

In summary, we found that an unhealthy lifestyle significantly increases the risk of diabetes and arthritis comorbidity. The lifestyle score’s high diagnostic accuracy highlights its potential for risk stratification. These findings emphasise the strong link between an unhealthy lifestyle and chronic diseases in middle-aged populations.
