Personalized survival prediction in elderly dialysis patients: integrating machine learning with traditional survival analysis
Authors: Manuel Huerfano, Elina Huerfano, Natalia Piragauta, Mauricio Sanabria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElderly patients undergoing kidney replacement therapy (KRT) face high mortality rates. Traditional statistical models describe overall survival patterns but may fail to capture non-linear relationships among clinical factors. This study integrates classical survival analysis and machine learning (ML) to enhance individualized survival prediction in elderly patients treated with hemodialysis (HD) or peritoneal dialysis (PD).MethodsWe analyzed a retrospective cohort of 2,501 patients aged ≥ 75 years initiating KRT in Colombia (2009–2013), with follow-up through 2015. Survival was assessed using Kaplan–Meier and Cox proportional hazards (Cox PH) models. Feature importance analysis highlighted vascular access (arteriovenous fistula), albumin, and age as the strongest predictors.ConclusionsHemodialysis is associated with a survival advantage in elderly dialysis patients. Background

The global population is rapidly aging, with the number of elderly individuals projected to exceed two billion by 2050. This demographic transition is accompanied by an increasing prevalence of chronic diseases, including chronic kidney disease (CKD) [1]. Among individuals aged ≥ 75 years, the need for kidney replacement therapy (KRT) has grown substantially due to rising CKD incidence, improved life expectancy, and limited access to kidney transplantation [2]. In Colombia, 57.5% of patients receiving KRT are treated with hemodialysis (HD) and 21.2% with peritoneal dialysis (PD), with a significant proportion being elderly [3].

Despite the widespread use of both modalities, the optimal dialysis strategy for older adults remains controversial. Survival outcomes vary across regions and patient populations, influenced by comorbidities, dialysis modality, and healthcare practices [4]. Previous studies have reported conflicting results, some suggesting a modest survival advantage for HD in elderly patients, while others find no significant difference between modalities [5, 6]. These inconsistencies underscore the need for individualized, evidence-based decision-making in this vulnerable population.

Recent advances in machine learning (ML) have transformed survival prediction by enabling models to capture complex, non-linear interactions among clinical variables [7, 8]. Unlike traditional methods such as the Cox proportional hazards (Cox PH) model, ML-based approaches including Random Survival Forests (RSF), DeepSurv, and DeepHit offer enhanced predictive accuracy and can uncover hidden risk patterns [9–11]. Nevertheless, the application of ML to survival prediction in elderly dialysis patients remains limited.

This study aims to bridge that gap by integrating classical survival analysis with state-of-the-art ML methods to evaluate survival in elderly patients initiating HD or PD in Colombia. Specifically, we sought to: (1) compare survival outcomes between HD and PD using traditional methods; (2) assess the performance of ML models in predicting individual survival probabilities; and (3) identify key prognostic factors to support personalized dialysis care for the aging population.

Methods

Data

This multicenter retrospective cohort study used data from the Renal Care Services database, which includes approximately 10,000 patients treated across 43 nephrology units in Colombia. The registry contains detailed demographic, clinical, and laboratory information for patients undergoing KRT between January 1, 2009, and December 31, 2013, with follow-up until December 31, 2015. Institutional review board approval was obtained prior to data collection.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were aged ≥ 75 years with stage 5 chronic kidney disease (CKD G5, defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2) and initiated chronic kidney replacement therapy (KRT) with either hemodialysis (HD) or peritoneal dialysis (PD). To ensure cohort stability and minimize confounding from acute kidney injury (AKI) or early mortality, a two-month landmark period was established. Only patients who survived and remained on their initial modality for at least 60 days were included in the final analysis. Following this landmark, patients were analyzed based on their initial stable modality, applying an Intention-to-Treat (ITT) principle to account for the real-world outcomes of the first dialysis choice, regardless of subsequent transfers between HD and PD. This approach represents the ‘steady state’ of maintenance therapy and ensures that the assessed vascular or peritoneal access was functional and established for chronic use. Extracted variables included age, sex, dialysis access type (arteriovenous (AV) fistula or vascular catheter for HD; peritoneal catheter for PD), functional status (Karnofsky performance score), comorbidities (diabetes, cardiovascular disease), and baseline laboratory measures (albumin and hemoglobin).

Survival time was defined as the period from the end of the 2-month landmark to death or censoring. Patients who left the dialysis network were administratively censored at their last contact; no patient underwent kidney transplantation during follow-up.

Data preprocessing

Continuous variables were summarized as means ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies (%). Between-group comparisons were performed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and t-tests for continuous ones, with significance set at p < 0.05. To prevent multicollinearity, variables with Pearson correlation coefficients > 0.7 were excluded. Features with > 20% missing data were removed, while remaining missing values were imputed with the mean (for continuous variables) or mode (for categorical ones); this simple imputation method was deemed appropriate as the overall proportion of missing data in the retained features was minimal (< 5%); at this level, the risk of introducing significant bias is considered negligible in large clinical cohorts. Continuous variables were standardized by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation (Z-score normalization) to ensure each feature had a mean of zero and unit variance, and categorical features were encoded as dummy variables. To evaluate the specific prognostic impact of permanent vascular access, patients were categorized based on the presence of a functional AV fistula (coded as 1) or catheter-dependence (coded as 0) at the 2-month landmark. Importantly, as Dialysis Modality (HD vs. PD) was included as an independent covariate in all models, the analysis effectively adjusts for the baseline clinical characteristics of each therapy. This ensures that the ‘AVF’ variable specifically measures the additional survival advantage of achieving a mature fistula compared to the respective standard access baseline of each modality. By doing so, the model avoids the assumption of clinical equivalence between intravenous and peritoneal catheters, as their distinct risks are independently captured by the modality covariate. Outcome-related variables (e.g., cause-of-death categories) were excluded to avoid data leakage in predictive models. For survival modeling, the target variable consisted of time-to-event (days) and an event indicator (death = 1, censored = 0). Neural-network models required this as a two-element array, while Cox-based and tree-based methods used Boolean event indicators.

Classical survival analysis

We first applied Kaplan Meier analysis to estimate survival probabilities and compared groups using the log-rank test. Prior to multivariable modeling, collinearity among covariates was assessed to ensure the statistical validity of the baseline Cox PH [12] model and to maintain the interpretability of feature importance within the Random Survival Forest. Subsequently, the Cox PH model was used to evaluate associations between covariates and mortality risk, assuming proportional hazards over time. Model performance and calibration were evaluated over a pre-specified clinically relevant evaluation window following the 2-month landmark (up to 5 years of follow-up). This methodological approach ensured that any performance gains observed in advanced ML architectures resulted from their ability to capture complex non-linear interactions rather than simple variable redundancy. Risk scores derived from the Cox PH model were used for patient ranking by predicted risk.

Model training

Model selection was performed using the holdout validation method. The dataset was randomly partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets: for Penalized Cox and ensemble models, data were split into training and test sets (80/20 and 70/30, respectively); hyperparameters were selected using internal procedures (including OOB for ensembles). For neural networks, data were split into training/validation/test sets (70/15/15). The training set was used for model fitting, the validation set for hyperparameter optimization, and the test set for final generalization assessment. For ensemble methods, bootstrap resampling was applied, with out-of-bag (OOB) samples used to estimate generalization error. Optimal hyperparameters for each model are detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Model evaluation

Model performance was assessed using two complementary metrics. The Concordance Index (C-index) measures the rank correlation between predicted and observed survival times, ranging from 0.5 (random concordance) to 1.0 (perfect discrimination) [14]. However, to account for potential censoring bias inherent in the C-index, the Integrated Brier Score (IBS) was utilized as the primary metric for comparing model accuracy. The IBS evaluates overall predictive accuracy by integrating calibration and discrimination over time; it represents the mean squared deviation between predicted survival probabilities and actual outcomes, where lower values indicate superior calibration and a closer fit between predicted probabilities and actual survival outcomes [15, 16].

To quantify uncertainty in model performance, we computed 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for both C-index and IBS using a non-parametric bootstrap procedure on the independent test set. Specifically, patients were resampled with replacement B = 1000 times, metrics were recalculated for each resample, and percentile based 95% CIs (2.5th–97.5th percentiles) were reported. For formal comparisons between models, we used a paired bootstrap approach in which the same resampled patient indices were used across models within each bootstrap replicate, enabling estimation of performance differences (ΔC-index and ΔIBS) and their corresponding 95% CIs. When multiple pairwise comparisons were performed, multiplicity was controlled using the Holm procedure. Additionally, we reported the probability of being the best-performing model for each metric, P(best). For C-index, P(best)C was defined as the proportion of bootstrap replicates in which a given model achieved the highest C-index; for IBS, P(best)IBS was defined as the proportion of replicates in which the model achieved the lowest IBS (lower is better). Because the bootstrap was paired across models, P(best) reflects the stability of the model ranking under resampling of the test set.

IBS computation was restricted to a clearly defined evaluation window: the integration was performed from the end of the 2-month landmark up to 5 years of follow-up (1825 days) and restricted to the common time support shared by the observed follow-up times in the test set and the model-specific predicted survival time grid. This ensured numerical stability under censoring and comparability across model.

All analyses were performed in Python 3.11.9, using the following libraries: pandas for data manipulation [17], NumPy for numerical computation [18], scikit-learn for preprocessing and general machine learning utilities [19], lifelines for classical survival analysis [20], scikit-survival for penalized Cox and ensemble survival models [21], and pycox for neural network–based survival modeling [22].

Results

Patient characteristics

The study cohort included 2,501 patients aged ≥ 75 years (median age 80.6 years; interquartile range (IQR): 78–84). Of these, 55.5% (n = 1,387) received HD and 44.5% (n = 1,114) PD. Baseline characteristics were comparable between modalities with respect to functional status (Karnofsky score) and laboratory values (albumin, hemoglobin). However, PD patients had a higher prevalence of diabetes (37.2% vs. 31.4%, p = 0.002), while HD patients were more often male (66.8% vs. 57.0%, p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Traditional survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival curves demonstrated significantly higher survival probabilities for patients treated with HD compared to those receiving PD (log-rank p = 0.006). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis confirmed that PD was independently associated with an increased risk of mortality (Hazard Ratio (HR) = 1.36; 95% CI: 1.20–1.53; p < 0.005). Mortality risk also increased significantly with older age at dialysis initiation (HR per year = 1.05; 95% CI: 1.04–1.07; p < 0.005), female sex (HR = 1.15; 95% CI: 1.01–1.30; p = 0.03), and the presence of diabetes mellitus (HR = 1.24; 95% CI: 1.10–1.41; p < 0.005). In contrast, favorable survival outcomes were linked to baseline laboratory parameters; each 1 g/dL increase in albumin (HR = 0.85; 95% CI: 0.77–0.95; p < 0.005) and hemoglobin (HR = 0.93; 95% CI: 0.91–0.96; p < 0.005) was associated with a significant reduction in the risk of death. These findings, summarized in Table 2 and illustrated in Figs. 1 and 2, underscore that modality, age, sex, comorbid conditions, and nutritional/inflammatory status are the primary determinants of survival in this elderly dialysis population.

Machine learning models

Model performance is summarized in Table 3 using time-dependent C-index (Antolini) and the IBS with 95% confidence intervals obtained via non-parametric bootstrap resampling of the test set. Because the C-index may be influenced by censoring, IBS was considered the primary metric for overall model comparison. DeepSurv achieved the best overall performance, showing the highest discrimination (C-index = 0.692, 95% CI 0.649–0.734) and the lowest prediction error (IBS = 0.145, 95% CI 0.129–0.164). Consistently, DeepSurv had the highest probability of being the best-performing model across bootstrap resamples (P(best) IBS = 0.825; P(best) C-index = 0.781).

Classical CoxPH and CoxPen models performed similarly and close to DeepSurv (CoxPH: C-index = 0.683 [0.638–0.727], IBS = 0.148 [0.132–0.166]; CoxPen: C-index = 0.684 [0.639–0.728], IBS = 0.149 [0.132–0.167]), although their bootstrap probability of outperforming DeepSurv was substantially lower. Among the non-linear ensemble approaches, XGB-AFT showed intermediate performance (C-index = 0.630 [0.582–0.676], IBS = 0.167 [0.148–0.190]), followed by RSF (C-index = 0.619 [0.557–0.678], IBS = 0.179 [0.167–0.192]). DeepHit demonstrated lower overall performance in this cohort (C-index = 0.594 [0.534–0.653], IBS = 0.196 [0.183–0.209]). Overall, DeepSurv provided the most robust balance of discrimination and calibration across the evaluated time horizons (Table 3).

Feature importance analysis

Feature-importance analysis using the Random Survival Forest (permutation importance) identified vascular access type as the strongest predictor of survival, with AV fistula (vs. catheter) showing the largest mean importance (Fig. 3). Other influential variables included hemoglobin level, age at dialysis initiation, functional status (Karnofsky score), diabetes, cardiovascular disease, sex, and albumin. These results highlight clinically actionable domains—vascular access planning, nutritional/inflammatory status, and functional reserve that may be prioritized to improve outcomes in elderly patients initiating kidney replacement therapy (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study integrates classical survival analysis and advanced ML techniques to improve the prediction of survival outcomes in elderly dialysis patients. Our findings confirm a consistent survival advantage for HD over PD, in line with prior evidence showing that dialysis modality is a major determinant of prognosis in this population [5, 6]. Traditional models identified older age, diabetes mellitus, and female sex as predictors of reduced survival, whereas ML-based approaches offered complementary insight by capturing potential non-linear relationships among predictors. In this elderly cohort, albumin should be interpreted as a dual marker reflecting both nutritional reserve and systemic inflammatory status, which may explain its consistent prognostic relevance across models.

These results corroborate previous reports of poorer outcomes with PD among elderly patients, particularly those with diabetes or multiple comorbidities. A recent meta-analysis by Cheng et al. [23] found a significantly higher mortality risk associated with PD compared with HD (HR: 1.17; 95% CI: 1.10–1.25), consistent with our findings. Notably, the Kaplan–Meier curves for HD and PD were largely overlapping during the first approximately 500 days after dialysis initiation, suggesting that the survival difference may not be immediate. This delayed divergence may reflect early outcomes influenced by acute illness and baseline vulnerability at KRT initiation, whereas modality-related factors and cumulative complications may become more apparent over longer follow-up. Similarly, data from the Dialysis Outcomes in Colombia (DOC) Study [5] and the Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study (DOPPS) [1] have shown improved long-term survival in elderly patients receiving HD, possibly due to closer medical supervision and standardized care protocols. In contrast, PD may offer short-term benefits for younger or functionally independent individuals, but these advantages often diminish in frail older adults who rely on caregivers for dialysis administration [24, 25]. Moreover, the structured follow-up and social engagement inherent to in-center HD could contribute indirectly to improved outcomes by facilitating earlier detection of complications and improved adherence to therapy.

From a methodological standpoint, this work underscores the complementary value of ML and classical models in survival prediction. While the Cox proportional hazards model remains a cornerstone of survival analysis [12], it assumes linearity and proportionality of effects that may not fully capture the complexity of patient heterogeneity. ML algorithms such as RSF [9] and DeepSurv [10] can relax these assumptions by modeling non-linear and higher-order relationships. In our evaluation, performance estimates were accompanied by uncertainty quantification, and model comparison prioritized overall prediction error using the IBS while retaining the C-index as a complementary discrimination metric. In this context, C-index values in the ~ 0.6–0.7 range represent fair discrimination in a very elderly population characterized by high comorbidity burden and censoring, where the achievable predictive ceiling is inherently limited.

Across models, vascular access type emerged as a key predictor, with AV fistula being associated with better survival. Importantly, dialysis modality (HD vs. PD) was included as an independent covariate in all predictive models, allowing the analysis to isolate the additional prognostic contribution of AV fistula access beyond baseline modality-associated risk. This finding aligns with prior reviews emphasizing the prognostic value associated with early AV fistula access in elderly patients [26]. Nevertheless, given the observational nature of the data, all associations reported in this study should be interpreted as prognostic rather than causal.

Clinically, integrating ML-driven survival prediction into dialysis care offers the potential to guide individualized treatment decisions. Such models can provide patient-specific survival estimates at clinically meaningful horizons, supporting structured shared decision-making that balances modality selection with factors such as frailty, comorbidity burden, and quality of life. A feasible implementation in routine care could follow a landmark-based workflow, generating individualized survival probabilities after stabilization of treatment, and using risk stratification to guide intensity of follow-up and targeted interventions (e.g., vascular access planning, nutritional optimization, and anemia management). Although HD was associated with superior survival in our cohort, modality choice should remain personalized, considering patient preferences, social support, and functional capacity [24, 25]. In Colombia, where modality selection may also be shaped by access to services and caregiver availability, a parsimonious prediction tool based on routinely collected variables could help standardize prognostic counseling and prioritize modifiable factors within real-world constraints, consistent with the patterns observed in DOC and DOPPS cohorts [1, 5].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, and the analysis was restricted to patients from a single national dialysis network, which may limit generalizability. Furthermore, the binary coding of vascular access (AV fistula vs. others) represents a limitation, as it groups distinct clinical and biological risks, such as the infectious profile of central venous catheters in HD versus the standard intraperitoneal access in PD. This approach was intentionally used to highlight the exceptional role of a functional AV fistula as a primary prognostic factor in elderly dialysis patients. However, because all models were simultaneously adjusted for dialysis modality, the observed prognostic contribution of AV fistula should be interpreted as an additional benefit beyond the baseline risk profile associated with HD or PD. Additionally, assessing vascular access at two months post-initiation introduces potential immortal time bias, as patients must survive this initial period to be classified in the AV fistula group, which may exaggerate its apparent association with improved survival. Missing values in the retained covariates (under 5%) were handled with simple mean/mode imputation rather than multiple imputation; given the low missing fraction this is unlikely to have materially biased our estimates, although multiple imputation would be preferable in datasets with greater missingness. Finally, variables such as residual kidney function, inflammatory biomarkers, and patient-reported quality-of-life measures were not available but could further improve predictive performance. Prospective multicenter validation is warranted before these models can be implemented in routine clinical practice.

In summary, this study highlights the complementary roles of traditional and ML-based survival modeling in characterizing mortality determinants among elderly dialysis patients. Beyond confirming the survival advantage of HD over PD [5, 6], our results emphasize the prognostic impact of vascular access and markers of nutritional/inflammatory status. The incorporation of ML approaches, particularly those that performed robustly under overall prediction error metrics may support more individualized prognostic assessment and inform clinical discussions in nephrology practice.

Conclusions

In this large multicenter cohort of elderly patients initiating kidney replacement therapy, hemodialysis was associated with better survival than peritoneal dialysis. Combining machine learning survival methods with classical approaches enabled individualized survival prediction, with differences in performance across models. In comparative analyses, DeepSurv showed the highest overall discrimination and the lowest integrated Brier score in this cohort. Vascular access and serum albumin were consistently identified as strong predictors of mortality risk, together with age. Overall, these results suggest that integrating classical survival analysis with machine learning can support prognostic assessment and inform clinical decision making in elderly dialysis patients.
