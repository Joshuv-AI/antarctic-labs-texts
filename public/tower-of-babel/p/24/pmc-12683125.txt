Epidemiological investigation and genomic characterization of Gyrovirus galga1 in Guangxi, China
Authors: Dan Yu, Yanfang Zhang, Zhixun Xie, Sisi Luo, Liji Xie, Meng Li, Tingting Zeng, Qing Fan, Minxiu Zhang, Xiaofeng Li, You Wei, Aiqiong Wu, Lijun Wan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGyrovirus galga1 (GyG1), an emerging pathogen within the Anelloviridae family, exhibits expanding host diversity and zoonotic potential, yet its epidemiological patterns and genomic determinants remain poorly characterized. To address this critical knowledge gap, we conducted the first comprehensive molecular epidemiological investigation of GyG1 in Guangxi, China (2020-2025). The result revealed three key advances: (1) Molecular-epidemiological evidence of winter-spring seasonality and age-dependent transmission, with breeder chickens showing biphasic peaks (1–6 and > 21 weeks) contrasting broilers' progressive carriage; (2) Genomic characterization revealing VP1-VP3 as nuclear-localized hydrophilic proteins, identifying VP1 nuclear targeting as a therapeutic vulnerability; (3) Functional annotation of recombination-driven evolution, including a temporal paradox in strain KX708518′s ancestry. This study elucidates GyG1’s ecological dynamics in a high-density poultry region and provides critical data for One Health surveillance and vaccine development, addressing a key gap in gyrovirus countermeasures.Keywords: Gyrovirus galga1, Prevalence, Complete genome, Phylogenetic analysis, Gene function

Introduction

Gyrovirus galga1 (GyG1) is the second gyrovirus identified following Chicken anemia virus (CAV), taxonomically classified within the the family Anelloviridae and genus Gyrovirus (Varsani et al., 2025). Initially reported in Brazil in 2011, it was first detected in chinese poultry populations in 2015 (Rijsewijk et al., 2011; Ye et al., 2015). While chickens are its primary hosts, the virus has also been found in fecal samples from diverse species with diarrheal symptoms, including wild birds, domestic cats or dogs, ferrets, minks and zoo animals as well as humans, highlighting its zoonotic risk(Feher et al., 2014; Ji et al., 2022; Li et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025). Co-infections dominate GyG1 cases in chicken flocks, with clinical signs concentrated in the proventriculus, including hemorrhages, edema, erosion, and visible head swelling and growth retardation (Dos et al., 2012; Von Tonnemann et al., 2025). These pathologies severely impair growth performance, leading to substantial economic losses,and such cross-species transmission risks threaten both poultry industry sustainability and public health security. GyG1 prevalence shows significant regional disparities in chicken flocks: South American poultry flocks exhibit higher rates, with 60.32 % in southern Netherlands and 60.4 %−90.7 % in Brazil (Dos et al., 2012). In contrast, Asian regions demonstrate lower levels, with 1.2 %−32.1 % in China and 20.63 % symptomatic cases in northern Vietnam(Tran et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024b).

GyG1 is a small, non-enveloped virus possessing a single-stranded, circular, covalently closed negative-sense DNA genome(Rijsewijk et al., 2011). Nearly all GyG1 strains feature a conserved genome of 2376 bp, including six nearly identical 22-bp direct repeats(Yao et al., 2017). All sequenced strains contain a 5′-untranslated region (5′-UTR) and three partially overlapping open reading frames (ORFs) encoding VP1 (ORF3), VP2 (ORF1), and VP3 (ORF2), along with a promoter and a polyadenylation signal. VP1 serves as the sole structural protein of GyG1 and constitutes the taxonomic marker for the Gyrovirus genus, spanning nucleotides 953-2335 with 38.8 % homology to CAV. VP2 serves as the regulatory protein, spanning nucleotides 450-1145 with 40.3 % homology to CAV. VP3 serves as an apoptosis-inducing protein, spanning nucleotides 577-951 with 32.2 % homology to CAV, and demonstrates tumor cell-specific pro-apoptotic activity.

Current research on the emerging GyG1 virus remains limited, with incomplete understanding of its etiological roles in specific diseases, epidemiological prevalence, and mechanisms driving host diversity. Yao et al. characterized GyG1-associated viral pathogenesia, emphasizing the necessity for deeper exploration of pathogen epidemiology. To elucidate the infection dynamics and evolutionary characteristics of GyG1, a five-year systematic epidemiological investigation was carried out across 10 cities, 12 live poultry markets, and 52 chicken farms in Guangxi. The study incorporated prevalence analyses stratified by time, age group, economic function of birds, and sampling source, complemented by functional genomic characterization.

Materials and methods

Sampling

From January 2020 to December 2024, an epidemiological survey was conducted using the cluster sampling method in live poultry markets across Nanning City (N1-N6) and Fangcheng City (F1-F6), with a total of 3,456 samples collected (Fig. 1A). Details are provided in Table 1. From August 2023 to February 2025, a comprehensive epidemiological investigation using stratified sampling methods was conducted in 52 poultry farms across 10 cities in Guangxi (Fig. 1B). A total of 3,293 samples were collected and details are provided in Table 2. According to the World Organization for Animal Health (OIE) protocol, the collected samples were stored in 2 mL sterilized centrifuge tubes containing 1 mL PBS with penicillin and streptomycin, and frozen at −80 °C until processing.

DNA Extraction and PCR detection

Viral DNA was extracted from samples using a pre-packaged viral RNA/DNA extraction kit (Biovet Biotechnology, Tianjin, China). For GyG1 detection, the published primers GyG1-F and GyG1-R were used to amplify a 346-bp fragment spanning the overlapping VP2 and VP3 genes from the sample DNA(Ye et al., 2015) (Table 3). The PCR reaction mixture (25 µL total volume) contained 12 µL Taq polymerase (Takara, Dalian, China), 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers, 2 µL DNA template, and 9 µL sterile water. Amplification conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 34 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 58 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. All PCR runs included a positive control (GX1902 strain preserved in our laboratory, Accession No.: MW404234) and a negative control (sterile water). Amplified products were analyzed by 1.2 % (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis.

Complete Genome Sequencing of GyG1 Strains

GyG1-positive samples were initially screened, followed by de-signing six overlapping primers for full-genome amplification of the virus to ensure result integrity (Table 3). Primer pairs F1/R1 amplified the 558–1972 nt fragment, F2/R2 targeted 1796–804 nt, F2/Seq1 covered 1796–209 nt, and Seq2/R2 spanned 2175–804 nt. The PCR reaction mixture contained 1 µL Tks Gflex™ DNA Polymerase, 25 µL 2 × Gflex PCR buffer (Takara, Dalian, China), 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers, 4 µL DNA template, and 18 µL sterile water. Amplification conditions included initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, 34 cycles of 98 °C for 15 s, 56 °C for 15 s, and 68 °C for 45 s, with a final extension at 68 °C for 5 min. Purified PCR amplicons were ligated into the TOPO Vector (Aidlab Biotech, Beijing, China) and sequenced (Sangon Biotechnology, Shanghai, China).

Phylogenetic relationship analysis

The amplified fragments of the GyG1 were assembled using SeqMan software (DNASTAR Lasergene, Madison, WI). Since VP1 is the sole structural protein, and the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) classifies gyrovirus families based on a pairwise identity threshold of 69 % for VP1 nucleotide sequences, we conducted alignments of all GyG1 whole genome sequences and VP1 gene sequences available on NCBI database with that of obtained in this study. The whole-genome and VP1 gene sequences were aligned using ClustalW in MEGA 7, and phylogenetic trees were constructed using the Neighbor-Joining method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates (DNASTAR Lasergene, Madison, WI). The Kimura 2-parameter model was selected for nucleotide sequence analysis to account for transition/transversion rate differences. Resulting trees were visualized and annotated using the iTOL website (https://itol.embl.de/). Additionally, sequence homology was analyzed using MegAlign software (DNASTAR Lasergene, Madison, WI).

Recombination analysis

The RDP v5.5 software was used to predict the possible recombination events between the 14 obtained and 54 reference strains using seven algorithms: RDP, GENECONV, BootScan, MaxChi, Chimaera, SiScan, and 3Seq (Cen and Rasmussen, 2024; Lam et al., 2018). An event was deemed a recombination event if at least five of these seven algorithms identified the gene as undergoing recombination, and predicted recombinants were further verified using SimPlot v3.5.1.

Prediction of protein physicochemical properties

The physicochemical properties of the VP1, VP2, and VP3 proteins encoded by the three ORFs of GyG1 were predicted using the ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) from the Expasy database. Subcellular localization was analyzed via Wolf PSORT (https://www.genscript.com/wolf-psort.html/) on the GenScript platform. For structural characterization: Transmembrane domains were predicted using TMHMM 2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0/); Signal peptides were identified with TargetP 5.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-5.0/); Secondary structure predic-tion was performed using SOPMA (https://npsa.lyon.inserm.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl?page=/NPSA/npsa_sopma.html) and PredictProtein (https://predictprotein.org/); Tertiary structure models were generated through SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/).

Statistical analysis

Prevalence differences were assessed by chi-square tests (two-tailed, α=0.05) with Wilson score 95 % confidence intervals (CI). For age-stratified and multivariable analyses, logistic regression models were used to adjust for potential confounders (e.g., region, sample type). Continuous age effects were evaluated via generalized linear models with binomial distribution and logit link. Pairwise comparisons between age groups were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Statistical significance was determined at p-value < 0.05.

The clinical metadata of 14 GyG1 strains obtained in this study and 54 reference strains are detailed in Table S3. Whole-genome homology analysis and phylogenetic reconstruction of these 68 GyG1 variants revealed that the 14 novel strains exhibited 96.85 %−99.92 % similarity among themselves and 77.3 %−99.92 % with other isolates (Fig. 3A). Phylogenetic clustering delineated the 68 sequences into four major clades (Fig. 3C). Notably, our isolated strains distributed across three distinct clades, with two geographically/temporally separated strains (PV165310 and PV165313) showing identical sequences within a subclade. Protein physicochemical properties prediction

Through analysis by ExPASy software and GenScript, the results are shown in Table 5. It is predicted that VP1, VP2, and VP3 are all unstable proteins, hydrophilic, and primarily localized in the nucleus or nucleus-associated. Analysis of transmembrane domains reveals that VP1, VP2, and VP3 lack transmembrane structures and cannot undergo transmembrane transport (Fig. S1). Signal peptide analysis shows a Sec/SPI value of 0, predicting that VP1, VP2, and VP3 do not contain signal peptides (Figure S2). Additionally, the secondary structures of VP1, VP2, and VP3 proteins all consist of alpha-helices, extended strands, and random coils, but with distinct compositional ratios (Table 6).

Discussion

Since 2011, GyG1 has been identified across multiple species in various global regions, yet the epidemiological characteristics and infection mechanisms of these viruses remain unclear. This study systematically analyzed clinical data-bases constructed across different timepoints (2020-2025), geographic locations (ten prefecture-level cities in Guangxi), sample types (throat/cloacal swabs, visceral tissues, environmental samples), and host parameters (breeder chickens, broilers, layers). The findings demonstrate that multidimensional epidemiological correlation analysis effectively reveals differences in viral transmission dynamics. Experimental evidence confirms the necessity of obtaining comprehensive infectious disease surveillance data.

In this study, the prevalence of GyG1 in Guangxi (9.26–15.97 %) falls within the medium to high end of the range previously reported in China (1.2–32.1 %) yet remains substantially lower than rates in Brazil, with these disparities likely attributable to differences in poultry farming density, biosecurity measures, climate, surveillance strategies, viral pathogenicity, or host susceptibility. Guangxi’s relatively higher detection rate may be influenced by the extended and systematic study design, high poultry density and regional trading activity, sensitive molecular methods, and local management or environmental factors, underscoring the need for further international studies to clarify the underlying drivers of these geographical variations. For breeder chickens, the GyG1 prevalence peaks at 1-6 wks of age, declines after 7 wks, and increased again after 21 wks. In contrast, broilers exhibit an opposing epidemiological pattern: the GyG1 carriage rate approaches zero in newborn chicks (< 1 wk old), increases progressively with age, peaks at 7-20 wks, and drops sharply after 21 wks. The age-related patterns probably reflect both immunological and management influences, the biphasic infection in breeders corresponds with maternal antibody decay (1-6 weeks) and reproductive stress-induced immunosuppression (>21 weeks), while broilers’ progressive infection mirrors their production cycles, with peak exposure during intensive growth periods (7-20 weeks) under high-density rearing conditions. The consistently higher prevalence in breeders versus broilers further suggests an association with poultry usage purposes, supported by Zhang et al. (Zhang et al., 2024a). Moreover, these temporal patterns highlight the complex interplay between viral biology, host immunity, and farming practices in GyG1 epidemiology. Future studies should focus on developing a GyG1-specific ELISA system and implementing longitudinal studies with controlled vaccination regimens‌ to quantitatively assess antibody kinetics.

Notably, flock prevalence was very low in autumn but significantly higher in late winter, contradicting Yao et al.’s conclusion that “autumn constitutes a high-incidence season for GyG1”(Yao et al., 2016). The winter-spring seasonal peak of GyG1 infection emerges from synergistic environmental, epidemiological and biological factors: colder temperatures enhance viral persistence in poultry environments while simultaneously inducing host immunosuppression through cold stress, coinciding with increased poultry density during winter housing and the annual spring trade peak in Guangxi that facilitates viral spread, further compounded by heightened prevalence of immunosuppressive co-infections (avian influenza, Newcastle disease) during winter months that create favorable conditions for GyG1 transmission and pathogenesis. Epidemiological survey data indicate no statistically significant difference in GyG1 prevalence between throat/cloacal swabs (10.0 %) and tissues (11.1 %) (p = 0.319). This finding supports the adoption of non-invasive throat/cloacal swabs as an alternative to traditional necropsy sampling for molecular epidemiological studies of GyG1. Moreover, live poultry markets (15.97 %) consistently exhibited higher GyG1 prevalence than poultry farms (9.26 %). This disparity may stem from environmental and managerial differences. Live poultry markets typically house poultry from multiple batches and sources under mixed feeding conditions, with complex sanitary environments. These conditions substantially elevate viral cross-infection risks. While the spatiotemporal and phylogenetic patterns strongly suggest horizontal transmission through environmental contamination or commercial vaccine, controlled challenge studies are needed to definitively establish transmission routes and efficiency (Varela et al., 2014).

To further analyze the genetic characteristics and evolution of the GyG1 strains prevalent in Guangxi, this study amplified, sequenced, and analyzed complete genomes and VP1 gene sequences from 14 GyG1 strains. The results revealed that these strains exhibited over 93 % nucleotide sequence identity, indicating high conservation of GyG1 during its 14-year evolutionary history. Additionally, the simultaneous circulation of multiple genetic lineages within the same chicken flock suggests continuous diversification through in situ evolution or repeated independent introductions into the host population, reflecting adaptive differentiation of the virus within local hosts. Two spatially and temporally distinct strains shared identical sequences, potentially resulting from interregional transmission via host movement (e.g., breeder chicken translocation) or contaminated fomites preserving ancestral signatures, indicating widespread dispersal of dominant strains through horizontal transmission. Avian species or environmental reservoirs may serve as stable viral reservoirs, explaining sequence conservation across spatiotemporal barriers. Finally, Guangxi isolates formed a tightly clustered, region-specific monophyletic clade in the VP1 phylogeny, suggesting maintenance of a relatively closed transmission chain for this genotype within the region.

Furthermore, the genomic analysis revealed a statistically significant recombination event that profoundly influenced the evolutionary trajectory of strain KX708518. Isolated in Guizhou in 2016, this strain exhibited a typical mosaic genome structure: its VP1 protein region was primarily derived from the 2024 Guangxi isolate PV165313, while the VP2 and VP3 protein regions were secondarily sourced from the 1997 Japanese isolate LC716407. Notably, our phylogenetic analysis revealed a temporal paradox: the major parental strain PV165313 appeared later than the recombinant strain KX708518. This chronological inconsistency likely results from differential evolutionary pressures acting on the parental lineages. The minor parental LC716407 may have undergone antigenic drift through sustained host adaptation, whereas PV165313′s mutations were more frequently purged by immune selection. This asymmetric evolutionary trajectory created an "apparent staircase" effect in the phylogenetic reconstruction, where the recombinant strain appears to predate its major parental component. From an evolutionary dynamic perspective, this recombination event likely occurred in an intermediate host. Such a unique host environment may have accelerated genomic repair and recombination processes of the minor parental strain, ultimately resulting in the temporal misalignment phenomenon. This finding provides new insights into understanding viral evolutionary mechanisms during cross-species transmission.

Bioinformatic analysis revealed GyG1′s VP1-VP3 proteins exhibit structural instability, hydrophilicity, and intrinsic disorder, with VP1 showing strong nuclear localization potential critical for viral replication and genome stability. These findings parallel the protein characteristics observed in CAV, where VP1 interacts with a series of host proteins involved in metabolic pathways, PI3K-AKT signaling, and ribosomal functions, thereby suppressing the host's early immune response (Xu et al., 2025)‌. It is speculated that GyG1 possesses immunosuppressive effects similar to those of CAV‌. Future studies should further validate these predictions through GFP-tagged localization, co-IP assays, and functional characterization of nuclear localization signals.

Overall, this study systematically elucidates the epidemiological patterns and genomic characteristics of GyG1 in Guangxi, China. Future research should focus on three critical priorities: (1) elucidating pathogenicity mechanisms through reverse genetics to functionally validate ORF proteins; (2) implementing One Health surveillance by expanding screening across human populations, wildlife reservoirs, and poultry supply chains to delineate transmission pathways; and (3) advancing vaccine development through epitope targeting of conserved genomic regions identified herein. This work establishes a foundation for understanding GyG1’s ecology and accelerates countermeasure development against this emerging pathogen.
