Matching-Adjusted Indirect Comparison of Tarlatamab for Patients with Platinum-Refractory or Platinum-Resistant Extensive-Stage Small-Cell Lung Cancer
Authors: Franziska Dirnberger, Sachin Patel, Devon J. Boyne, Zhiyi Lan, Jenny Uyei, Jessie Wang, Seoyoon Cho, Lucy DeCosta, Ian M. Bridges, Umit Tapan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionExtensive-stage small cell lung cancer (ES-SCLC) is an aggressive cancer that is traditionally associated with poor survival outcomes, particularly among individuals with platinum-refractory or platinum-resistant disease. In the phase 3 DeLLphi-304 trial (NCT05740566), tarlatamab showed significant clinical benefit compared with single-agent chemotherapies (topotecan, lurbinectedin, or amrubicin) as second-line (2L) therapy for ES-SCLC. Paclitaxel and a combination of cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and vincristine (CAV) are alternative 2L treatments for platinum-refractory or platinum-resistant ES-SCLC that were not evaluated in the DeLLphi-304 trial. To address the lack of head-to-head evidence, unanchored matching-adjusted indirect comparisons (MAICs) were conducted to compare the efficacy of tarlatamab versus paclitaxel and CAV.MethodsThe analyses leveraged individual-level patient data from DeLLphi-304 (tarlatamab) and published aggregate-level data from Owonikoko 2020 (NCT02038647; paclitaxel) and GFPC 0501 (NCT00418743; CAV). Analyses were restricted based on chemotherapy-free interval (CFI) to align with the eligibility criteria of Owonikoko 2020 (CFI < 180 days) and GFPC 0501 (CFI < 90 days). Outcomes included overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), overall response rate (ORR), and disease control rate (DCR). Unanchored MAIC was used to estimate the relative treatment effect after adjusting for baseline imbalances. Measures of effect included hazard ratios (HRs) and odds ratios (ORs).ResultsAfter MAIC reweighting, tarlatamab was associated with longer OS versus paclitaxel [HR (95% CI): 0.35 (0.23, 0.52), p < 0.001] and CAV [HR: 0.48 (0.32, 0.70), p < 0.001]. Tarlatamab was also associated with improved PFS, ORR and DCR relative to paclitaxel (p < 0.05). There were no significant differences between tarlatamab and CAV with respect to PFS, ORR, or DCR (p > 0.05).ConclusionThese results suggest that tarlatamab may increase survival relative to paclitaxel and CAV among individuals with platinum-refractory or platinum-resistant ES-SCLC in the 2L setting. These findings are subject to residual confounding and should be interpreted with caution.Trial RegistrationDeLLphi-304 ClinicalTrials.gov identifier, NCT05740566.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03659-4.

Introduction

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is an aggressive form of lung cancer that accounts for approximately 15% of incident lung cancer cases[1–3]. This disease is often classified as limited-stage (LS) or extensive-stage (ES) based on the Veterans Administration Lung Study Group system, which guides initial treatment strategies [4]. The majority of patients (60–80%) present with ES disease at initial diagnosis, which is characterized by distant metastases or tumors that cannot be treated by a single radiation field [5, 6].

First-line treatment for ES-SCLC typically includes platinum and etoposide, with or without programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors [7, 8]. Although most patients initially respond to first-line therapy, progression inevitably develops within months for the vast majority of patients. Treatment selection for relapsed ES-SCLC in the second-line (2L) setting has traditionally been guided by response to initial platinum therapy. Individuals are classified as having platinum-refractory disease if they experience progression during first-line chemotherapy, platinum-resistant disease if they experience progression shortly after completing first-line chemotherapy, and platinum-sensitive disease if they experience progression long after completing first-line chemotherapy. While clinical practice guidelines are similar with respect to the classification of platinum-refractory disease, they differ in terms of the time window used to define platinum-resistant and platinum-sensitive disease. For example, the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) defines platinum resistance as progression within 90 days of the last dose of first-line treatment whereas some national guidelines like the German S3 guideline defines it as progression within 180 days [8, 9]. Individuals treated for platinum-refractory or platinum-resistant disease have historically had a poor prognosis, with a median overall survival as low as 5–6 months [10–12]. These findings highlight the unmet need for more effective treatment options in this patient population.

Tarlatamab, an extended bispecific T-cell engager (BiTE®) molecule, demonstrated efficacy and a favorable safety profile among individuals with previously treated ES-SCLC in the pivotal phase II DeLLphi-301 study (NCT05060016), leading to its FDA accelerated approval in May 2024 [13]. The comparative efficacy of tarlatamab among individuals with ES-SCLC who received one prior line of platinum-based chemotherapy was evaluated and confirmed in the phase III DeLLphi-304 trial, which compared tarlatamab versus standard of care chemotherapy (topotecan, lurbinectedin, or amrubicin) [14]. Compared to those who were randomly assigned to receive chemotherapy (n = 255), individuals who were assigned to tarlatamab (n = 254) had significantly improved overall survival [OS; hazard ratio (HR); 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.60 (0.47–0.77)]. Tarlatamab also had a significant benefit with respect to progression-free survival (PFS) and objective response rate (ORR), and had an improved safety profile relative to chemotherapy [14]. The survival benefit was also consistently observed across key prespecified and clinically relevant patient subgroups including those with platinum-refractory or platinum-resistant disease.

Although DeLLphi-304 established benefit versus several standard chemotherapy options (i.e., topotecan, lurbinectedin, and amrubicin), there are additional treatments used in global clinical practice that were not captured in the control arm of the head-to-head comparison. By leveraging trials that shared a common comparator arm, a network meta-analysis (NMA) was conducted to indirectly compare efficacy outcomes of tarlatamab with other relevant treatment options, both overall and within subgroups defined by platinum-sensitivity [15]. However, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and vincristine (CAV) and paclitaxel—two alternative treatment options for platinum-refractory or platinum-resistant ES-SCLC used in certain geographies—could not be incorporated into the NMA due to the lack of a common comparator arm [16]. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to conduct an unanchored matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC) to estimate the comparative efficacy of tarlatamab against paclitaxel and CAV among individuals with platinum-refractory or platinum-resistant ES-SCLC who had received one prior line of therapy.

Methods

Data Sources

The analyses leveraged individual-level patient data (IPD) on tarlatamab (DeLLphi-304 trial; NCT05740566) collected from patients who were enrolled between May 2023 and July 2024 and assessed up to the primary analysis data cut-off (January 2025). Published aggregate-level data (AgD) was used for paclitaxel (Owonikoko 2020; NCT02038647) and CAV (GFPC 0501; NCT00418743) [10, 11]. Owonikoko 2020 was a phase II randomized controlled trial that enrolled patients with platinum-refractory ES-SCLC [chemotherapy-free interval (CFI) < 180 days] in North America and Europe between May 2014 and October 2015 to receive either alisertib plus paclitaxel or placebo plus paclitaxel [11]. GFPC 0501 was a phase II randomized controlled trial conducted in France between December 2009 and June 2011, comparing CAV with oral chemotherapy in platinum-refractory ES-SCLC (CFI < 90 days) [10]. Only AgD from the paclitaxel plus placebo (referred to hereafter as paclitaxel) arm in Owonikoko 2020 and the CAV arm in GFPC 0501 were used in the analyses.

All three trials enrolled individuals with platinum-refractory or platinum-resistant ES-SCLC and were restricted to individuals who did not have symptomatic CNS metastases, had adequate laboratory values, and had no major comorbidities. To facilitate cross-trial comparisons and to account for differences in the CFI threshold used to define platinum-resistant disease, the DeLLphi-304 trial IPD was restricted to align with key eligibility criteria of the comparator trials. Specifically, the IPD from the tarlatamab arm of the DeLLphi-304 trial was restricted to individuals with platinum-refractory disease or a CFI < 180 days and who also had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Score (ECOG PS) < 2 for the paclitaxel comparisons, and to individuals with platinum-refractory disease or a CFI < 90 days for the CAV comparisons.

This study involved use of de-identified data from DeLLphi-304 and previously published aggregate-level summary statistics from Owonikoko 2020 and GFPC 0501, compliant with the United States (US) Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. The US Department of Health and Human Services does not consider analysis of de-identified secondary data to meet the definition for human subject research. Therefore, this study was exempt from federal regulations and did not require patient consent or institutional review board approval.

Selection of Baseline Variables for Adjustment

The covariates included in the MAIC were chosen a priori based on a multi-step process which included a review of prior literature, physician interviews, multivariable regression analyses using data from the DeLLphi-301 trial, meta-regression analysis of patient characteristics and outcomes from published clinical studies, and clinical evaluation [17]. The following covariates were identified as relevant prognostic factors or potential effect modifiers for the MAIC analyses and were used in previous studies: age, sex, race, smoking status, ECOG PS, disease stage at initial diagnosis, CFI, brain metastases, liver metastases, and time since initial diagnosis [17–21].

The MAIC analyses adjusted for differences in all available prognostic factors and effect modifiers that were reported in the comparator trials. The comparisons with paclitaxel were adjusted for age, sex, smoking history, time since initial diagnosis, ECOG PS, disease stage at initial diagnosis, brain metastasis, and CFI. The comparisons with CAV were adjusted for age, sex, ECOG PS, and disease stage at initial diagnosis. Due to the lack of reporting in the comparator trials, adjustments could not be made for race or liver metastasis in the paclitaxel analyses, and for race, smoking history, brain metastases, liver metastasis, or time since initial diagnosis in the CAV analyses. Individuals enrolled in the DeLLphi-304 trial who were missing relevant baseline covariate information were excluded from the analyses.

Study Endpoints

Outcomes of interest included OS, PFS, ORR, and disease control rate (DCR). The analyses leveraged data from the intention-to-treat population of each trial (e.g., individuals who did not have an evaluable tumor response were included in the denominator of the ORR and DCR calculations). The definitions of these outcomes were similar between the three trials, with OS defined as the time from randomization until death from any cause, PFS defined as the time from randomization to disease progression or death from any cause, ORR defined as the percentage of patients who achieved a partial response or complete response, and DCR defined as the percentage of patients who achieved a partial response, complete response, or stable disease. All trials assessed tumor response according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST v1.1) criteria. The AgD on outcomes for the comparator trials was extracted from the primary publications, specifically, Fig. 1C (OS), Fig. 1A (PFS), and Table 2 (ORR and DOR) reported in Owonikoko 2020 and Fig. 1 (OS), Fig. 2 (PFS), and Table 5 (ORR and DCR) reported in GFPC-0501 [10, 11].

Statistical Methods

The analyses leveraged IPD from the tarlatamab arm of DeLLphi-304 trial and published AgD for the paclitaxel arm of Owonikoko 2020 and the CAV arm of GFPC 0501 [10, 11, 14]. For OS and PFS, the published Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves from the comparator trials were digitized using WebPlotDigitizer to convert the KM curve figures from the study publications into numeric values for the time points and corresponding survival probabilities [22]. The digitized data were then used to simulate pseudo-IPD for each KM curve using the validated algorithm by Guyot 2012 [23]. The accuracy of the digitization was assessed by comparing the summary statistics from the pseudo-IPD against the published results from the comparator trials.

Due to the lack of a common comparator arm, an unanchored MAIC was conducted to estimate the comparative efficacy of tarlatamab relative to paclitaxel and relative to CAV after adjusting for imbalances in the distribution of baseline effect modifiers and prognostic factors. Within the MAIC analyses, balancing weights were derived such that the average baseline characteristics of the weighted DeLLphi-304 trial matched the published aggregate-level baseline characteristics of the comparator trial. Given the limited sample sizes of the trials, only the mean values of continuous covariates were controlled for in the main analyses as opposed to both means and standard deviations.

For the paclitaxel comparisons, Owonikoko 2020 did not directly report the mean and instead reported the median (range) for age and time since initial diagnosis. Within the paclitaxel analyses, age was modeled as a continuous covariate whereby the reported median value was used as an approximation of the mean under the assumption that this variable was symmetrically distributed. In contrast, the reported median value for time since initial diagnosis could not be used as a reasonable approximation of the mean because this variable was likely to be right skewed. To address this issue, time since initial diagnosis was modeled as a binary covariate whereby the reported median value of the comparator trial was used as a cut-point to create a binary variable in which 50% of the Owonikoko 2020 trial population would be above the median and 50% would be below the median.

To assess the effectiveness of matching in minimizing differences between study populations, standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated before and after weighting [25]. To facilitate the estimation of the SMDs, standard deviations were imputed based on the reported range using the method described in Walter and Yao (2007), where necessary [26]. A weighted Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to estimate HRs for OS and PFS and a weighted logistic regression model was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) for ORR and DCR. In addition, weighted Kaplan–Meier curves and risk tables were presented for the OS and PFS analyses. To account for uncertainty in the derivation of the weights, robust variance estimation was used to estimate the 95% CIs.

[27]. Larger E values (typically greater than 2 or 3) suggest that a substantial degree of unmeasured confounding would be required to fully account for the observed effect estimate.

For the OS and PFS analyses, the proportional hazards assumption was evaluated based on an examination of Schoenfeld residual and log cumulative hazard plots. In instances where there was evidence of a potential violation of this assumption, the difference in the restricted mean survival time (RMST) was assessed [28]. The 95% CI for the RMST in the unweighted (unadjusted) analyses were estimated using the method described in Conner et al. (2019) [29]. To account for uncertainty in the estimation of the weights in the MAIC-weighted (adjusted) analyses, the 95% CIs for the RMST estimates were estimated using the bootstrap percentile method with 10,000 iterations.

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. For both the paclitaxel and CAV analyses, a sensitivity analysis was conducted in which we did not control for covariates that were already balanced between trials (i.e., SMD ≤ 0.1) or for disease stage at initial diagnosis [since information of ES vs. LS was not directly available in the DeLLphi-304 trial and was approximated using the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage]. For the paclitaxel analyses, a second sensitivity analysis was conducted by treating time since initial diagnosis as a continuous variable whereby the reported median value was used as an approximation of the mean. For the CAV analyses, a second sensitivity analysis was conducted in which both the mean and standard deviation of age were controlled for since both values were directly reported in GFPC 0501. Further adjustment for the standard deviations of continuous baseline covariates in the paclitaxel comparisons was not explored because the standard deviations was not reported in Owonikoko 2020. Finally, a third sensitivity analysis was conducted for both the paclitaxel and CAV comparisons using an updated data cutoff of DeLLphi-304 (April 29, 2025).

The MAIC analyses were conducted in accordance with guidance from the EU JCA [30] and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence Data Support Unit Technical Support Document 18 (NICE DSU TSD 18) [24]. The study did not require patient consent or ethics committee review due to the use of de-identified, secondary data. Statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.2) and R codes were based on those published by the NICE DSU TSD 18 [24].

Results

Study Population

A total of 254 patients were assigned to tarlatamab in the DeLLphi-304 trial. After applying key eligibility criteria of the comparator trials and excluding individuals with missing information on relevant baseline covariates, 188 individuals from DeLLphi-304 were included in the comparison with paclitaxel (Owonikoko 2020) and 108 individuals from DeLLphi-304 were included in the comparison with CAV (GFPC 0501) (Fig. 1). MAIC analysis of Tarlatamab vs. Paclitaxel

Prior to weighting, there was a moderate degree of imbalance in the baseline characteristics of DeLLphi-304 and Owonikoko 2020, with SMDs ranging from 0.04 (time since initial diagnosis) to 0.36 (brain metastases; Supplementary Table 1). After reweighting the DeLLphi-304 trial data, the baseline characteristics were well-balanced between trials (Supplementary Table 2). Compared to the original sample size of the restricted tarlatamab arm (n = 188), MAIC reweighting resulted in an ESS of 109.9 (58.4% of the original sample size). There were no extreme weight values (i.e., exceeding 5) based on an examination of the rescaled weights.

A visual examination of Schoenfeld residual and log cumulative hazard plots suggested a potential violation of the proportional hazards assumption. Findings from RMST analyses for OS and PFS were consistent with the primary results, showing benefit of tarlatamab over paclitaxel (Supplementary Table 4). Within a time window of 13.1 months (i.e., the maximum possible follow-up time common to both treatment arms), the OS of individuals assigned to tarlatamab was, on average, 3.3 months (95% CI 2.1–4.6, p < 0.01) longer than those assigned to paclitaxel. Within a time window of 9.3 months, tarlatamab was associated with a 1.2-month (95% CI 0.5–2.0, p < 0.01) longer PFS compared to paclitaxel.

Discussion

Due to the absence of direct head-to-head evidence from a randomized controlled trial and the lack of a common comparator arm needed to facilitate an NMA, an unanchored MAIC was conducted to indirectly compare the efficacy of tarlatamab relative to paclitaxel and CAV among individuals with platinum-refractory or platinum-resistant ES-SCLC. After adjusting for imbalances in clinically relevant baseline covariates that were reported in each trial, tarlatamab was associated with improved OS compared to both paclitaxel and CAV. The magnitude of the relative effect estimates for OS in our unanchored MAIC analyses were consistent with results from the head-to-head comparison of the DeLLphi-304 trial [14] and indirect comparisons of a network meta-analysis [15], which strengthens the conclusions of this investigation and further highlights the uniformly poor efficacy among currently available treatments for 2L ES-SCLC. Collectively, these findings suggest that tarlatamab may improve survival outcomes relative to paclitaxel and CAV among individuals with platinum-refractory or platinum-resistant ES-SCLC.

Study limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, there is a risk of bias due to unmeasured confounding. Unanchored MAICs rely on the strong assumption that all effect modifiers and prognostic factors have been controlled for in the analyses [31]. While the analyses adjusted for several important covariates, certain variables could not be accounted for because they were not reported in the comparator trials. This was particularly evident in the comparison between tarlatamab and CAV, where key factors such as brain metastases, liver metastases, time since initial diagnosis, and smoking history were not reported in GFPC 0501. Although there is a risk of bias, the high E values suggest that an unmeasured confounder would have to be strongly associated with the outcomes, highly imbalanced between treatment groups, and largely independent from the covariates already adjusted for within the analyses to fully account for the findings [27]. However, these E values do not rule out the possibility of residual confounding and should be interpreted with caution because they do not account for the potential impact of multiple unmeasured confounders and do not consider other potential sources of bias highlighted below [32].

Similarly, there is a risk of bias due to differences in the enrollment of individuals with an ECOG PS of 2. Specifically, GFPC 0501 included individuals with an ECOG PS of 0–2 (15.2% of patients had an ECOG PS of 2) whereas DeLLphi-304 was restricted to participants with an ECOG PS of 0 or 1 (despite the eligibility criteria, 2 patients (0.8%) with an ECOG PS of 2 were enrolled in DeLLphi-304). This difference introduces potential bias that is expected to favor tarlatamab and, as such, the results of these comparisons should be interpreted with caution.

There is also a risk of bias due to a lack of between-trial overlap with respect to the receipt of first-line anti-PD-(L)1 inhibitors. Unlike DeLLphi-304, the comparator trials did not likely enroll any individuals who received first-line anti-PD-(L)1 inhibitors because they were conducted in an earlier treatment era that occurred prior to the approval of these therapies. However, prior research suggests that survival from initiation of 2L therapy does not significantly differ based on receipt of first-line anti-PD-(L)1 inhibitors [33, 34]. While prior research suggests that differences in prior receipt of anti-PD-(L)1 inhibitors may not substantially impact our findings, the evidence is limited and bias cannot be ruled out entirely.

There is also a risk of a secular trend bias related to the different time periods during which the trials were conducted. Specifically, there may have been differences between DeLLphi-304 and the historical control arms in terms of disease diagnostics, supportive care, patient monitoring, subsequent lines of therapy, and other aspects of clinical practice. Such differences could not be accounted for in the analyses and there is uncertainty regarding the magnitude and direction of the potential bias. Since their assessment may be more strongly influenced by historical differences in clinical practice, there may be an increased risk of bias in the PFS, ORR, and DCR results relative to the OS findings.

Lastly, in the CAV comparisons for PFS, DCR, and ORR, there is also a risk of bias due to measurement error arising from potential differences in the frequency of tumor response assessment. While DeLLphi-304 and Owonikoko 2020 had a scan interval every treatment cycle, GFPC 0501 reported that the response rates were assessed every 2 months which could have introduced bias in favor of CAV.

Conclusions

An unanchored MAIC was conducted to estimate the comparative efficacy of tarlatamab versus paclitaxel and CAV among individuals with platinum-refractory or platinum-resistant ES-SCLC who had received one prior line of therapy. Results from this investigation suggest that tarlatamab may increase survival relative to paclitaxel and CAV in patients with platinum-refractory or platinum-resistant ES-SCLC in the 2L setting. These findings should be interpreted with caution given the risk of bias, particularly for the CAV comparison where there was increased uncertainty due to residual confounding.
