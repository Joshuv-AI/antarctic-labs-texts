Full-length tau seeded by C-shaped tau fibrils is conformationally variable
Authors: Jia Yi Zhang, Nadia El Mammeri, Mei Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A number of neurodegenerative amyloid proteins have been found to adopt disease-specific structures in the human brain. This prion-like propagation of the amyloid structure requires recruitment of soluble monomers containing various posttranslational modifications to adopt the same structure as the fibril seeds. To understand the structural fidelity of seeding for amyloid proteins that possess large intrinsically disordered regions, here we investigate the seeding of a full-length phospho-mimetic tau protein, 4E tau, using a truncated tau construct that adopts the C-shaped Alzheimer’s disease (AD) fold. Sedimentation gels indicate that four replicas of cross-seeded reactions all showed accelerated fibrillization kinetics. However, solid-state NMR spectra indicate that these cross-seeded samples have structurally variable cores, all of which differ from the AD fold of the seed fibrils, some of which resemble the unseeded triple-stranded structure of the full-length protein, and two of which contain two polymorphs. INEPT NMR spectra indicate that these cross-seeded replicas have similar dynamic disorder for their fuzzy coat, which differs from the dynamics of unseeded full-length 4E tau. These results indicate that accelerated fibrillization kinetics do not necessarily correlate with faithful amplification of the seed structure, and surface-catalyzed nucleation of full-length tau with large intrinsically disordered regions leads to structural polymorphism and structural evolution from the seed fibrils.

Seeded amplification of misfolded β-sheet-rich protein structures underlies many neurodegenerative diseases (1, 2). This structure propagation involves short fibrils (seeds) recruiting soluble monomers to adopt the same pathological structure as the seeds, thus elongating the amyloid fibrils. Accurate propagation of fibril structures by homologous seeding has been demonstrated in vitro for many proteins (3); but their apparent manifestation in neurodegenerative brains (4) remains striking because of the complexity of the chemical environment of the brain and the heterogeneous amino acid sequences of many amyloid proteins in the brain. For tau, high-resolution structures of patient-derived samples show that the protein assembles into a single fibril core structure in each tauopathy (5), even though the adult human brain has six tau isoforms, which are further modified by phosphorylation, acetylation, ubiquitination, truncation and other posttranslational modifications (PTMs) (6, 7). Thus, pathological tau aggregates, even if possessing a unique structure at some time points in a brain, must encounter a large number of chemically different monomers. How the seed fibril structure is amplified, apparently without error, by these chemically dissimilar monomers to attain a single fibril structure is not well understood.

Evidence that amyloid proteins with different amino acid sequences can interact with each other to co-aggregate, that is, cross-seeding, has been reported in vitro and in animal models. In vitro, cross-seeding has been demonstrated for α-synuclein fibrils seeding tau monomers (8), Aβ fibrils seeding islet amyloid polypeptide (IAPP) (9), seeding between wild-type and mutant Aβ (10), and truncated α-synuclein fibrils seeding full-length α-synuclein monomers (11). Measurements of aggregation kinetics and fibril morphologies showed that the ability of amyloid fibrils to template soluble monomers depends on the structure of the seed and the sequence similarity between the seed and monomer. In addition to pairwise seeding between two proteins, cross seeding is the basis of real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC) experiments, where minute quantities of pathological aggregates in the brain are detected by their ability to convert recombinant soluble proteins of different types, lengths and modifications into fibrils (12, 13). In animal models, tau neurofibrillary tangles formed with an accelerated rate after injection of Aβ fibrils (14), and multiple misfolded proteins coexist in various neurodegenerative diseases, implying their cross seeding during disease progression (15, 16).

Compared to the extensive biochemical evidence for amyloid protein cross-seeding, relatively few studies have investigated the molecular conformation of amyloid fibrils induced by a heterologous seed (17, 18, 19). Without direct observation of the protein conformation at the residue-specific level, it is not known whether the substrate adopts the same conformation as the seed or whether they evolve into conformationally distinct aggregates, and whether the outcome of cross-seeding is variable. Here, we investigate the conformation of full-length tau fibrils seeded by a short tau construct that forms the C-shaped Alzheimer’s disease (AD) fold (Fig. 1A). We recently showed that AD-fold tau can be reproducibly assembled in vitro using 4E tau (297–407), which contains four phospho-mimetic glutamate (Glu, E) mutations at the PHF1 epitope: S396E, S400E, T403E, S404E (20) (Fig. 1, B and C). This 4E tau (297–407) construct spans R3, R4, R′, a part of R2 and the C-terminal domain (CT), and is 38-residues longer than the rigid core (residues 306–378) of AD paired helical filament (PHF) tau (21). As the substrate for seeding, we chose full-length 0N4R tau with the same four Glu mutations. In the absence of cofactors or seeds, this full-length 4E tau protein self-assembles into a triple-stranded β-sheet structure (22) that resembles the three-layered structure of tau aggregates in 4R tauopathies such as progressive supranuclear palsy (Fig. 1D) (5). Thus, unseeded full-length 4E tau has the propensity to adopt a distinct structure from the C-shaped AD fold of 4E tau (297–407). Using solid-state NMR spectroscopy, we investigate whether the preformed 4E tau (297–407) seeds (abbreviated as Ctau seeds below) template full-length tau monomers to form the same C-shaped structure and characterize the variability in the rigid-core conformation of the cross-seeded fibrils. We also examine the dynamics of the disordered domains outside the rigid core. Results

4E tau (297–407) seeds accelerate the fibrillization of full-length 4E tau

For the cross-seeding reactions, we combined Ctau seeds and full-length 4E tau monomers at a 3:7 molar ratio. The seed 4E tau (297–407) fibrils were reproducibly assembled into the AD fold using a pH 7.5 HEPES buffer containing 2 mg/ml protein, 60 mM MgCl2 and shaking at 80 rpm (20). After 1 to 2 days, the vast majority of the protein fibrillized, as shown by an intense 15-kDa band in the pellet lane of the sedimentation gel (Fig. S1A). Negative-stain TEM images show twisted filaments with a crossover length of ∼90 nm (Fig. S1C), similar to AD PHF tau. Two-dimensional 15N-13Cα (NCA) correlation spectrum of 13C,15N-labeled 4E tau (297–407) fibrils (Fig. S1B) matched previously reported spectra of samples that were verified by cryoEM to form the C-shaped structure (20), confirming that these fibrils exhibit the AD fold.

The 4E tau (297–407) fibrils are ∼500 nm long and prone to lateral association. Cross-seeding reactions using these unsonicated 4E tau (297–407) fibrils resulted in little fibrillization of the full-length monomers after 4 days (Fig. S1D). To generate a sufficient amount of fibril ends as well as to reduce bundling to facilitate seeding, we bath-sonicated the fibrils (23). Recent studies of α-synuclein fibrils found that bath sonication up to 30 min increased seeding activity while sonication over 60 min disrupted the β-sheet structure (24). We compared the effects of 30- and 60-min bath sonication of 4E tau (297–407) fibrils using TEM. Sonication for 30 min resulted in fibril lengths of ∼100 nm, whereas 60-min sonication left few intact filaments (Fig. S1E). Thus, we used 30 min sonication to generate the seeds for all cross-seeded samples.

To allow comparison of cross-seeded and unseeded full-length 4E tau fibrils, we maintained key conditions of cofactor-free self-assembly of full-length 4E tau. The unseeded fibrillization of full-length monomers used 1.6 mg/ml (40 μM) protein in a pH 6.8 phosphate buffer containing 300 mM NaCl. Therefore, for cross-seeding, we mixed 1 mg of Ctau seeds in 0.5 ml solution with 8 mg of full-length monomers in 4.5 ml of the phosphate/NaCl buffer, giving a final seed/monomer molar ratio of 3:7 and a full-length tau concentration of 1.6 mg/ml. The 5 ml reaction was kept in a 100 ml glass bottle at 37 °C and orbital shaken at 250 rpm with a 25.4 mm diameter. As a control, we prepared an unseeded reaction of full-length tau. We measured sedimentation gels to track the extent of fibril growth over time. In the reaction containing sonicated Ctau seeds, more than half of the monomers fibrillized by day 2 and more than 85% fibrillized by day 4 (Fig. 2A). In comparison, the unseeded reaction showed no fibrils by day 4 and only yielded fibrils after 10 days. To verify that the seeding protocol used here accurately captures the properties of both seeds and monomers, we conducted another control experiment, seeding 13C, 15N-labeled 4E tau (297–407) monomers with unlabeled Ctau seeds. At the same seed: monomer molar ratio of 3:7 as used for the cross-seeding reaction, this self-seeding shortened the lag phase compared to the unseeded reaction, as shown by sedimentation gel (Fig. S1F). More importantly, 2D 15N-13C correlation spectrum of the labeled monomers (Fig. S1G) showed an identical fingerprint as unseeded AD-fold tau, confirming that self-seeding accurately propagated the seed structure to the monomers.

Ctau-seeded full-length 4E tau fibrils have a different rigid-core structure from the seed

To characterize the molecular conformation of the Ctau-seeded full-length 4E tau fibrils, we added 13C, 15N-labeled full-length 4E tau monomers to unlabeled Ctau seeds. Negative stain TEM images of the cross-seeded fibrils harvested after 4 days show 150 to 400 nm long and 10 to 20 nm wide filaments with a mixture of twisted and straight morphologies and with both single and doubly associated filaments (Fig. 2B). Interestingly, the 2D 15N-13C correlation spectrum of this sample 1 differs from the spectra of both the Ctau fibril and the self-assembled full-length 4E tau fibril (Fig. 2C). For example, 5 Gly peaks are observed in the cross-seeded spectrum, while the Ctau fibril exhibits 3 strong Gly peaks and the unseeded full-length 4E tau resolves ∼10 Gly signals. Both the Ctau seeds and the unseeded full-length 4E tau fibrils exhibit an 15N chemical shift of 132 ppm for Y310, whereas the cross-seeded sample shows a larger 15N chemical shift of 135 ppm. Thus, the cross-seeded sample has a distinct conformation from both parent proteins. Sample 1 exhibits narrow 13C linewidths of ∼0.5 ppm, but its 15N linewidths are ∼1.6 ppm, which are larger than those of Ctau fibrils (∼0.6 ppm), indicating that the cross-seeded full-length 4E tau fibril is conformationally more heterogeneous than AD-fold 4E tau (297–407).

We assigned the 13C and 15N chemical shifts of sample 1 using 2D NCACB (Fig. 2D), 2D 13C-13C (CC) (Fig. S2), 2D NCOCX, and 3D CONCA correlation spectra (Fig. S3). No additional 3D 15N-13C correlation spectra were measured due to the limited amount of the 13C, 15N-labeled monomers in the reaction mixture. Given the resonance overlap in the 2D spectra, we made sequence-specific assignment only when a sufficiently long strip of sequential type-assigned residues had no alternative interpretation. We were able to assign 39 residues from the middle of R2 (Q288) to the end of R’ (E391) (Table S1). Most of the assigned residues have β-sheet Cα and Cβ chemical shifts, with the exception of K290, C322, L324, and R349, which interrupt the β-strands (Fig. 2E). Contiguous β-strands are found in R2 and R′, while R3 and R4 contain short β-strands interrupted by many unassigned residues, which are likely due to spectral overlap, structural polymorphism, and low sensitivity. To validate the position of the rigid core in the amino acid sequence, we counted the number of Ala, Ser and Thr peaks in the 2D CC spectrum. We observed 2 Ala, 3 Thr and at least 7 Ser peaks (Fig. S2). There are no Ala residues from R2 to R4 (Fig. 3A), and the closest Ala’s from these repeats are located at the beginning of R1 (A246) and the middle of R′ (A382, A384, and A390). Three Thr residues are present from R2 to the N-terminal half of R′. Thus, the number of Ala and Thr peaks support the interpretation that the fibril core of sample 1 extends from R2 to the middle of R′. Multiple Ctau-seeded full-length 4E tau samples show variable conformations

To investigate the reproducibility of Ctau-seeded fibril structure and generate more fibrils for high-resolution structure determination, we repeated the cross-seeding reaction using identical protein concentrations, reaction volumes, and mass ratio between the seed and monomer. Similar to sample 1, sample 2 showed faster fibrillization compared to unseeded protein, with ∼90% of the monomers fibrillized by day 4 (Fig. 4A). TEM images showed ∼200 nm long fibrils with a mixture of laterally associated non-twisting filaments and twisted filaments with a crossover length of ∼90 nm (Fig. 4B). Despite the similar fibrillization kinetics, sample 2 shows a distinct 2D 15N-13C spectral fingerprint from sample 1 (Fig. 4C). Numerous chemical shift differences are observed for Gly and Cys peaks between the two samples, and the resolved Y310 peak in sample 2 has the same 15N chemical shift (132 ppm) as that of AD-fold tau but 3 ppm smaller than that of sample 1 (Table S1). In comparison, the 2D NCA spectrum of sample 2 has significant similarity with the unseeded full-length 4E tau spectrum. The Ser/Thr region of the 2D CC spectrum also resembles the unseeded full-length tau spectrum, showing 8 Ser and 2 Thr peaks (Fig. 3, C and F). Using 2D NCACB, 2D CC, 2D NCOCX (Fig. 4D, Fig. S5) and 3D CONCA spectra, we assigned the 13C and 15N chemical shifts of 55 residues in sample 2. The immobilized β-strand residues start from Q276 at the beginning of R2 and end at S356 in the middle of R4. Unlike sample 1, we were able to assign long contiguous β-strands in sample 2, for example, at 291CGSKDNIK298, 347KDRVQSKIGS356 and the R3 hexapeptide 306VQIVYK311 (Fig. 4E). The lack of β-strands in R′ is further supported by the fact that the 2D CC spectrum shows only one weak Ala peak, which likely results from residual intensities of random coil Ala residues in the disordered region of the protein. Thus, the rigid core of sample 2 resembles the rigid core of unseeded full-length 4E tau fibrils, both excluding the R’. Interestingly, the N-terminal ten residues (V337-F346) of R4 form a β-strand in unseeded full-length tau but are unassigned in sample 2. This suggests that seeding by Ctau fibrils may have caused structural polymorphism or dynamic disorder to this segment, even though we cannot rule out spectral overlap that precludes assignment.

To investigate if the conformational difference between samples 1 and 2 results from subtle differences in the initial reaction condition or from the intrinsic propensity of the reaction to develop structural polymorphism, we prepared two additional samples starting from a single initial reaction. We mixed 1 ml of sonicated 4E tau (297–407) seeds with 10 ml of 13C, 15N-labeled full-length tau monomers, then split the mixture into two 5.5 ml volumes and placed each in a 100-mL glass bottle for incubation under shaking. Both samples (3a and 3b) reached ∼85% fibrillization by day 4, but sample 3a showed a higher degree of fibrillization (60%) by day 2 compared to sample 3b (15%) (Fig. 5A). Despite identical initial conditions for the fibrillization reaction, samples 3a and 3b show moderately different 2D fingerprints (Figs. 5C, Fig. 3, D and E). In the 2D CC spectra, sample 3a shows no Ala peaks while sample 3b exhibits at least one Ala peak (Fig. 3, D and E). The Ser/Thr chemical shift fingerprints of both samples bear resemblance to that of full-length 4E tau (Fig. 3F), suggesting that the rigid cores of both samples end before R′. The 2D NCA spectra of both samples have varying degrees of similarity to the spectra of sample 2 and unseeded full-length 4E tau. Despite these similarities, resonance assignment yielded only 22 sequentially assignable residues for sample 3a (Fig. 5D, S6) and fewer for sample 3b (Fig. 5F, S7), with most assigned residues found in R2 and R3 (Fig. 5E).

To investigate whether the small number of assignable residues in the two sample 3 replicas reflects a small rigid core or conformational heterogeneity, we calculated the ratio of the integrated intensity of the 2D NCA spectra with the intensity of well-resolved peaks for all four cross-seeded samples. All 2D NCA spectra exhibit strong peaks for one or both Cys residues of the protein (C291 and C322), a resolved Y310 peak, and various resolved Gly peaks (Fig. S8). We thus divided the integrated intensity of each 2D NCA spectrum by the intensity of three reference peaks (one Cys, one Gly, and Y310) and averaged these ratios to obtain an approximate size of the rigid core. This analysis yielded an average size of 91 residues for 4E tau (297–407), in good agreement with the total length of the 73-residue C-shaped AD fold (V306-F378) and an additional immobilized segment C-terminal to F378 (20). For unseeded full-length 4E tau, this intensity analysis yielded an average size of 95 residues, in excellent agreement with the fact that the rigid core spans residues 273 to 362. With these calibrations, we applied this intensity analysis to the four cross-seeded replicas and found average intensity ratios of 213 for sample 3a, 219 for sample 3b, 107 for sample 1, and 136 for sample 2. Therefore, samples 3a and 3b contain twice as many immobilized residues as samples 1 and 2, strongly suggesting that each sample contains two molecular conformations. This conclusion is confirmed by the observation of two Y310 signals in the 2D NCA spectra of both samples (Fig. 5C), indicating two coexisting conformations for the R3 hexapeptide.

To obtain further information about the three-dimensional fold of the two sample 3 replicas, we measured cryoEM data of sample 3b, which exhibits a twisted morphology in the TEM images. Based on 261,237 manually picked particles from 8780 micrographs (Fig. S4C), reference-free 2D classification revealed at least two types of fibrils: 19% of particles show twist and a narrow view that resembles full-length 4E fibrils, whereas 65% of the particles display no twist (Fig. S4D). The remaining 16% of the particles show twist but are inconclusive as to whether they are the same type as full-length 4E tau. Due to the low resolution of the 2D classes, the twisted filaments cannot be aligned to form a complete crossover, precluding helical reconstruction.

Ctau-seeded full-length 4E tau fibrils have the same dynamics among replicas

The above data indicate that the cross-seeding of full-length 4E tau by Ctau seeds led to variable conformations for the rigid cores. Samples 2 and 3 resemble the unseeded full-length tau structure to varying degrees, whereas sample 1 has features of C-shaped tau. Since the rigid core of full-length tau fibrils represents less than ∼25% of the entire protein, whereas the rest is dynamically disordered (25), we next measured the dynamics of the seeded fibrils using 2D scalar-coupled 1H-15N INEPT experiments (Fig. S9), to investigate whether the structurally variable cores have different dynamic disorder (25).

The 2D INEPT spectra of the four cross-seeded replicas show overall similar 1H and 15N chemical shifts as the unseeded 4E tau fibrils (25) (Fig. 6A), allowing us to transfer the assignment from the unseeded protein. However, the intensities of the seeded samples differ from those of the unseeded protein (Fig. S6B). All four replicas show the highest intensities for the C-terminal half of NT1 and N-terminal half of NT2, indicating that these segments undergo the largest-amplitude motion in the seeded fibrils. In contrast, the highest INEPT intensities in de novo formed 4E tau fibrils are shifted to the C-terminal half of NT2 and N-terminal half of P1, whereas NT1 shows low intensities. Discussion

This study investigates the structural outcome of cross-seeding between two tau constructs that differ by the size of their intrinsically disordered domains and their rigid core structures in unseeded reactions. Both proteins contain phospho-mimetic mutations at the PHF1 epitope, but the short 4E tau (297–407) construct adopts the C-shaped AD fold with only a small, disordered region of 38 residues, whereas full-length 4E tau self-assembles into a triple-stranded β-sheet core with a large fuzzy coat of 293 residues. We investigate whether the Ctau seeds can overcome the intrinsic structural propensity of the full-length monomer and convert it to the AD fold. Since intrinsically disordered segments inhibit β-sheet aggregation, we reasoned that seeds with a small, disordered region might be more effective than full-length tau seeds in templating monomers. To further facilitate the amplification of the seed fibril structure, we used a relatively high concentration of seeds (11 wt%) in the reaction mixture. Under these conditions, our data show that full-length tau monomers failed to propagate the seed fibril structure. One of the quadruplicates (sample 1) formed β-strands from R2 to R′. While the R2 β-strands are consistent with the unseeded 4E tau, the presence of β-strands in R′ is a signature of the AD fold, indicating this sample has acquired some aspects of the seed fibril structure. In qualitative difference, a second replica (sample 2) shows considerable chemical shift similarity to unseeded full-length tau fibrils, with β-strands formed in R2, R3 and R4 but no immobilized residues in R' (Fig. 4, E and F). Finally, samples 3a and 3b contain twice as many immobilized residues as samples 1 and 2 and show two Y310 signals in the 2D NCA spectra, indicating that these two samples each contain two molecular conformations. Notably, this structural polymorphism of the R3 hexapeptide has not been observed in unseeded full-length tau fibrils studied to date (22, 26, 27, 28). In contrast to full-length monomers, 4E tau (297–407) monomers successfully propagated the C-shaped structure of the seeds under the same seed : monomer molar ratio (Fig. S1G), indicating that the failure of cross-seeding to amplify the seed structure reflects intrinsic properties of full-length tau monomers interacting with the C-shaped 4E tau (297–407) seeds.

Although full-length 4E tau monomers did not propagate the C-shaped seed structure and showed variable conformations among the replicas, all cross-seeded samples exhibit accelerated fibrillization kinetics, with the lag time shortened from 1 week for unseeded samples to 1 to 2 days (22). Thus, faster fibrillization does not equate faithful propagation of the seed structure. This finding has important implications for interpreting RT-QuIC data (29, 30). Although minute levels of pathological fibrils in brain homogenates can aggregate recombinant monomers in these assays, our data indicate that the substrates can adopt different structures from the seeds. The degree of structural difference between the seeds and the templated fibrils likely depends on the intrinsic conformational propensity of the substrate, the amino acid sequence difference between the seed and monomer, and environmental factors such as pH, salt, and the presence of surfaces such as lipid membranes (31). In the absence of high-resolution structures, it should not be assumed a priori that the templated fibrils adopt the same structure as the seed.

We now consider the possible molecular reasons for the structural evolution of the cross-seeded full-length 4E tau from Ctau seeds. Protein aggregation involves four main mechanisms: primary nucleation of soluble monomers, elongation of existing fibrils by addition of new monomers at fibril ends, surface-catalyzed secondary nucleation, and fragmentation of existing fibrils to create new fibril ends and surfaces (31, 32). The rates of these events have been studied extensively (33). Slow primary nucleation is manifested as a lag phase, whereas secondary nucleation and elongation are manifested as the exponential growth phase. Our observation that the cross-seeded reaction has a shorter lag time than the unseeded reaction (Fig. 2A) indicates that the primary nucleation step is shortened in the seeded reaction, as expected, and is dominated by secondary nucleation and elongation (34). Between these two mechanisms, secondary nucleation is more likely to be responsible for the structural variations among replicas and their structural departure from the Ctau seeds. The Ctau seed surface is uneven: between one end of the Ctau structure that stacks the R3 hexapeptide against the first ten residues of R′ and the other end formed by the triangular R4 β-helix, many hydrophilic residues such as Lys and Glu are interspersed by hydrophobic residues such as Leu and Val. Thus, full-length monomers with their large intrinsically disordered regions can attach to the seed surface with varying orientations and at variable positions to form different conformations (Fig. 7). This heterogeneous association of full-length monomers with the seed surface is consistent with the observed coexistence of two polymorphs within each sample for two of the four replicas (samples 3a and 3b). The observation that full-length tau structure deviated from the seed but fibrillization is still accelerated by the seeds differs from Aβ cross-seeding, where monomers associated with seed fibrils regardless of structural compatibility, but fibrillization was only accelerated when the monomers successfully converted to the seed structure (10). Apart from the structural heterogeneity of surface-catalyzed nucleation of full-length tau, environmental conditions can also influence the outcome of cross-seeding (35, 36). We found that sample 2 adopts a similar conformation as unseeded full-length 4E tau, indicating that pH, buffer and shaking conditions dominated the Ctau surface in this reaction to lead to the unseeded full-length 4E tau structure. Thus, while the seed surface still accelerated the fibrillization of the monomers, the interaction between the monomers and seeds is much weaker than the monomer-monomer interaction in this reaction.

In contrast to the structure evolution in the current cross-seeded tau fibrils, we recently showed that wild-type full-length tau is faithfully templated by AD brain PHF tau in one generation (37). The main difference between these two studies is that AD brain PHF tau seeds contain the full fuzzy coat with a complement of PTMs whereas the current study uses truncated tau with an abridged fuzzy coat as seeds. We suspect that the larger fuzzy coat of AD PHF tau seeds may inhibit secondary nucleation and shift the aggregation mechanism to elongation, which has higher structural fidelity. This interpretation is supported by the fact that the AD PHF tau seeding reactions were slower than the current reactions, requiring 7 days under shaking to reach 50% fibrillization (38). Thus, AD PHF tau templates monomers more slowly, with less secondary nucleation, but propagates the structure more accurately. In addition, the fuzzy coat may need to have appropriate PTMs in order to accurately amplify the seed structure, as dilution of AD PHF tau by unmodified recombinant tau lost the seeding potency for new monomers, even though the first-generation fibrils have the correct rigid-core structure (37). This interpretation is also supported by other emerging experimental data that PTMs and truncations in the fuzzy coat impact the seeding competence of tau (39) and α-synuclein (40). Together, our current and recent data suggest that serial amplification of tau, and possibly other large amyloid proteins, requires a suitably modified fuzzy coat with appropriate dynamic properties (25). Finally, even when the full fuzzy coat with PTMs is present in the seeds to regulate templating, it is possible that only monomers with sufficiently similar amino acid sequences and PTMs as the seeds would be able to amplify the pathological seed structure. Future experiments will be required to further test these hypotheses.

Secondary nucleation has been extensively studied in smaller amyloid proteins such as Aβ (41), IAPP (18), α-synuclein (42), truncated tau constructs (43), and the huntingtin protein (44). By analyzing concentration-dependent aggregation rates and visualizing growing filaments using fluorescence, electron and atomic force microscopies, secondary nucleation was found to be an important mechanism of fibril growth by these proteins. However, most proteins whose secondary nucleation properties have been studied in detail so far have relatively short disordered segments. In comparison, the aggregation mechanisms of proteins containing large intrinsically disordered regions such as tau are much less understood, nor has the structural consequence of cross-seeding between dissimilar seeds and substrates been well characterized. The current data fill these knowledge gaps. Our finding that full-length tau does not propagate the structure of seeds with a small fuzzy coat suggests that in vivo, large amyloid proteins may use ends-on elongation as the main mechanism for structure propagation. Our results thus suggest that the uniqueness of the tau fibril structures within each tauopathy may be partly due to the large size and complex chemical modifications of the intrinsically disordered domains in this protein.

Experimental procedures

Cloning, expression, and purification of tau

Two tau constructs are used in this study: full-length 4E 0N4R tau and 4E tau (297–407), both containing four phospho-mimetic Glu mutations at the PHF1 epitope: S396E, S400E, T403E, S404E. Full-length 4E tau forms a triple-stranded β-sheet core (22) while the 4E tau (297–407) adopts the C-shaped AD fold (20). Both proteins were expressed in E. coli and purified using similar procedures as described before. 13C, 15N-labeled proteins were expressed in M9 media using 3 g/L of 13C-labeled glucose and 1 to 2 g/L of 15N-labeled ammonium chloride as the sole carbon and nitrogen sources, respectively. For seeding reactions, unlabeled 4E tau (297–407) was expressed in M9 media. Full-length 4E tau was purified by heat denaturation, cation exchange chromatography and HPLC, whereas 4E tau (297–407) was purified by urea denaturation, cation exchange chromatography and HPLC. The yield per liter of M9 media was ∼40 mg for full-length 4E tau and ∼160 mg for 4E tau (297–407).

Cofactor-free fibrillization of 4E tau (297–407)

4E tau (297–407) was fibrillized using HEPES buffer as reported recently (20). Briefly, ∼2 mg of dry powder was dissolved in 1 ml of fibrillization buffer, which contains 20 mM HEPES buffer at pH 7.5, 60 mM MgCl2, 10 mM DTT and 1x cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail (1 tablet per 40 ml). The reaction mixture in a 5 ml tube was placed horizontally in a 25.4-mm diameter orbital shaker at 37 °C and shaken at 80 rpm. After 2 days, 5 mM of DTT was added to the reaction mixture. On the third day, fibrillization was complete as determined by TEM and sedimentation gel.

Cross-seeded fibrillization reaction of full-length 4E tau

About 1 ml of freshly-harvested 4E tau (297–407) fibrils was transferred to a 1.5-mL Eppendorf tube and bath sonicated for 30 min. In parallel, lyophilized full-length 4E tau powder was dissolved at 1.6 mg/ml in the fibrillization buffer, which contains 50 mM potassium phosphate at pH 6.8, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM DTT and 1x cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail (1 tablet per 40 ml), and bath sonicated for ∼10 min to ensure complete dissolution. The full-length tau monomer solution was transferred to a 100 ml glass bottle, to which the sonicated 4E tau (297–407) seed fibril solution was slowly added with continuous shaking of the reaction mixture. The final seed: monomer molar ratio was 3:7, which corresponded to a mass ratio of 1:8. A total volume of ∼5 ml and monomer mass of ∼8 mg was maintained for all replicas. When a larger reaction volume of ∼10 ml was tested with the same seed and monomer concentrations, the full-length tau monomers did not fibrillize even after a longer incubation period, suggesting that subtle differences exist in the shaking-induced fragmentation experienced by the larger reaction volume. The glass bottle was flushed with nitrogen gas, then incubated in a 25.4-mm diameter orbital shaker at 37 °C and shaken at 250 rpm. 50 μl aliquots were taken at day 0, day 2 and day 4, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −20 °C for sedimentation assays and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) experiments. To maintain reducing conditions, 5 mM DTT was added and the bottle was flushed with nitrogen gas each time the bottle was opened. After 4 days, 5 x 20 μl final aliquots were taken and flash-frozen for TEM and cryoEM experiments. The remaining reaction was centrifuged at 100,000 g for 2 h at 4 °C in a TLA-55 rotor. The pellet was spun down into a 3.2 mm pencil-style MAS rotor through pipette tips. Residual fibril in the pipette tip was packed into the NMR rotor using a needle, followed by addition of water to ensure adequate hydration of the fibrils.

Self-seeded fibrillization reaction of 4E tau (297–407)

To verify that our seeding protocol accurately captures the properties of the seeds and monomers, we carried out a control experiment of homologous seeding of 4E tau (297–407). A 1 ml seed solution of 4E tau (297–407) fibrils in a 1.5-mL Eppendorf tube was bath sonicated for 30 min. 4.5 mg of lyophilized 4E tau (297–407) powder was dissolved at 2 mg/ml in a 5 ml tube in the fibrillization buffer, which contains 20 mM HEPES at pH 7.5, 60 mM MgCl2, 10 mM DTT and 1x cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail. This monomer solution was bath sonicated for ∼10 min, then 0.96 ml of the seed fibril solution was added to the monomer with continuous shaking. The final seed : monomer molar ratio was 3:7, identical to that of the cross-seeding reaction. The 5 ml tube was placed horizontally in a 25.4-mm diameter orbital shaker and shaken at 80 rpm at 37 °C. 25 μl aliquots were taken at 0, 4, 8, 16 and 24-h time points, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −20 °C for sedimentation assays and TEM analysis. After 24 h, the fibrillization reaction was centrifuged at 100,000 g for 2 h at 4 °C in a TLA-55 rotor, and the pellet was packed into a 3.2 mm MAS rotor.

Sedimentation assay

Each 40 μl aliquot of the fibrillization reaction mixture was centrifuged at 100,000 g for 2 h, the supernatant carefully removed, and the invisible pellet resuspended with 4 μl of 6 M urea and 36 μl of fibrillization buffer. 40 μl of 2 x SDS buffer (4% SDS, 20% glycerol, 200 mM DTT, 5% β-mercaptoethanol, 0.01% bromophenol blue and 0.1 M Tris-HCl, pH 6.8) was added to both the supernatant and pellet, the solutions were boiled at 95 °C for 15 min, and 9 μl of each solution was loaded onto a 12 to 15% polyacrylamide gel and separated by SDS-PAGE. The protein was stained with Coomassie blue, and gel band intensities were obtained by integration in ImageJ.

Transmission electron microscopy

5 μl of fibril solutions were adsorbed onto freshly glow-discharged, 200-mesh Formvar/carbon coated copper grids (Ted Pella) for 60 s, washed three times with 100 mM sodium acetate, washed once with 0.7% w/v uranyl formate, then stained with 0.7% w/v uranyl formate for 12 to 15 s. TEM images were acquired on an FEI Tecnai T12 electron microscope. For 4 E tau (297–407) fibrils, the fibril solution was diluted with an equal volume of acetonitrile and bath-sonicated for 10 s to reduce clumping before TEM.

Cryoelectron microscopy experiments

3 μl of tau fibril solutions were applied to freshly glow-discharged R1.2/1.3 400-mesh carbon gold grids (SPI Supplies). The grids were plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using a 4 °C 95% relative humidity Thermo Fisher Scientific Vitrobot Mark IV system. For sample 1, a 5 s wait time and 5 s blot time were used; for sample 3b, a 7 s wait time and 7 s blot time were used. Cryo-EM data were acquired at the MIT.nano facility. All images were recorded at a dose of ∼32 electrons per Å2 spread over 32 frames using the EPU software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a Krios G3i operating at 300 keV with a Bioquantum K3 camera (Gatan) using an energy slit of 20 eV on a Gatan energy filter.

Helical reconstruction was performed using the RELION-4.0 software (45). The raw EM movies frames were gain-adjusted, aligned, and dose-weighted using RELION’s motion correction program (46). Contrast transfer function parameters were estimated using CTFFIND-4.1 (47). All particles were manually picked, which were first extracted in boxes of 768 pixels, then downscaled to 256 pixels for reference-free 2D classification. Multiple rounds of 2D classification were attempted, discarding only clearly nonfibrillar classes; however, high-resolution 2D classes were not obtained.

Solid-state NMR experiments

Magic-angle-spinning (MAS) solid-state NMR experiments were conducted on a Bruker 800 MHz (18.8 T) NMR spectrometer equipped with a NEO console and a BlackFox 3.2 mm MAS probe. 1H chemical shifts were referenced internally to DSS at 0.0 ppm, while 13C and 15N chemical shifts were referenced indirectly through the ratio of the gyromagnetic ratios to the DSS scale for 13C and the liquid ammonia scale for 15N (48). Typical radiofrequency (rf) field strengths were 50 to 83 kHz for 1H, 50 to 62.5 kHz for 13C, and 25 to 36 kHz for 15N. Reported temperatures are sample temperatures estimated from the water 1H chemical shift using the equation Tsample (K) = 96.9 x (7.83 – δH2O) (49). All spectra of seeded samples were measured under 14 kHz MAS.

Dipolar-coupling-based polarization transfer experiments were used to selectively characterize the immobilized residues, while refocused-INEPT-based experiments were used to detect highly mobile residues. Resonance assignment was conducted using two-dimensional (2D) NCACB, NCOCX and 3D CONCA correlation experiments, in which polarization transfer was conducted using 1H-15N or 1H-13C cross-polarization (CP), 15N-13C SPECIFIC-CP (50), 13C-13C CORD spin diffusion (51) and Cα-Cβ DREAM (52) (Table S3). The DREAM sequence was performed using a 50 to 100% amplitude ramp from 4.5 to 9 kHz on the 13C channel with the 13C carrier frequency at 53.5 ppm. 2D NCOCX spectra were measured using 5 ms 15N-13C SPECIFIC-CP mixing and 82 ms 13C-13C CORD mixing. The 3D CONCA experiment was conducted with two SPECIFIC-CP steps from 13CO to 15N and from 15N to 13CA. For 2D and 3D dipolar correlation experiments, a short 1H-13C CP contact time of 150 μs was used to selectively detect the most rigid β-sheet signals while excluding the mobile residue signals. These multi-dimensional experiments were conducted in blocks of 1 to 3 days each, with adjustments made to the carrier frequencies to correct for field drift between blocks. This allowed multiple blocks of 3D spectra to be added in the time domain before Fourier transformation.

Spectra were processed in Topspin 4.3 and apodized using either the Gaussian window function with LB and GB values of −15 and 0.06, respectively, or using the QSINE window function with an SSB value of 3 to 3.5. Two-fold linear prediction was applied to the indirect dimensions in the 2D and 3D datasets. All spectra are plotted with the lowest contour level chosen to show only a small amount of noise and with a multiplication factor of 1.25 between successive contour levels. Chemical shift assignment was conducted in CCPN (53). First, spin systems were identified and tentatively type-assigned by comparing the 2D NCA, 2D NCACB and 2D CC spectra. Then all possible sequential resonances found in the 2D NCOCX and 3D CONCA spectra were cross-referenced with the 2D CC spectrum to form potential type-assigned neighboring spin systems. Finally, a confident sequence-specific assignment was only made when a sufficiently long strip of sequentially type-assigned residues had no alternative interpretation. Secondary chemical shifts were calculated using the TALOS-N software (54) and reported as ΔδCβ–ΔδCα ≡ (δCβ,expt – δCβ,coil) – (δCα,expt– δCα, coil). Positive ΔδCβ–ΔδCα values indicate a β-sheet, whereas negative values indicate α-helical conformations.
