Relevance of a peripheral site of action outside the brain-blood barrier for the beneficial effects of CB2 receptor activation in experimental ALS in male mice
Authors: Marta Gómez-Almería, Claudia Gonzalo-Consuegra, Carmen Rodríguez-Cueto, María Cabañas-Cotillas, Alberto Jiménez-Amor, Isabel Machín-Díaz, Matthias B. Wittwer, Pawel Dzygiel, Diego Clemente, Uwe Grether, Javier Fernández-Ruiz, Eva de Lago
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND. Cannabinoid type 2 receptor (CB2) activation works against neurotoxic events that kill motor neurons in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Given that amyotrophic lateral sclerosis is a neuromuscular pathology with the skeletal muscle being also affected, we were interested in deciphering whether peripheral CB2 may contribute in these beneficial effects. METHODS. To this end, we used two CB2 agonists: (i) RO-6866945, which crosses the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and acts at both Central Nervous System (CNS) and peripheral compartments; and (ii) RO-6871304, which is a peripherally-restricted ligand that activates CB2 outside the BBB. Both agonists have been evaluated in SOD-1 and TDP-43 transgenic male animals. RESULTS. In both cases, their i.p. administration improved the neurological status of these mice, preserved their spinal motor neurons and attenuated glial reactivity. They also recovered those muscle fibers denervated by the pathology in SOD-1 transgenic mice. To confirm that the efficacy of RO-6871304 is not derived from a possible BBB damage in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis mice that may allow this agonist to reach the CNS, we analyzed their concentrations in neural tissues after acute administration in both experimental models. Our data confirmed that RO-6866945 was detected in the brain, while RO-6871304 was not, whereas both compounds were found in the plasma. CONCLUSIONS. In summary, our data suggest that CB2 located at peripheral sites (skeletal muscle, immune cells, others) may be responsible of therapeutic effects showed by compounds targeting these receptors in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Our study may be of critical importance to move CB2 agonists in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis towards the clinical scenario.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02418-2.

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a chronic, progressive, and highly disabling neurodegenerative disease characterized by the degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons, accompanied by parallel denervation and atrophy of skeletal muscles [36, 40, 75]. This motor neuron loss results from a combination of neurotoxic insults, including protein aggregation, oxidative stress, excitotoxicity, impairments in axonal transport and cytoskeletal dynamics, glial reactivity, chronic inflammation, among others [32, 45, 58, 69]. Approximately 10–15% of ALS cases are familial, associated with mutations in over 40 genes—most notably SOD1 (encoding the antioxidant enzyme superoxide dismutase-1), TARDBP (encoding the RNA-binding protein TDP-43), FUS (encoding another RNA-binding protein, fused in sarcoma), and C9orf72 (encoding a protein involved in endosomal trafficking) [13, 37, 40, 43, 57, 58, 63, 82]. However, the majority of ALS cases (around 85%) are sporadic, potentially triggered by environmental and other unknown factors including specific genetic variants conferring risk. These sporadic forms often also exhibit abnormal protein deposition and aggregation, particularly involving TDP-43 [1, 72].

To date, no treatment has been found to alleviate ALS symptoms or halt disease progression significantly. The current gold standard is riluzole (Rilutek®), an anti-excitotoxic drug approved in 1995 [65]. Two decades later, the antioxidant edaravone (Radicava®), which appears to target mitochondrial oxidative stress, was approved in Japan (2015), United States (2017), and Canada (2018), though it has not yet received approval in Europe [66]. More recently, the FDA approved a combination therapy of sodium phenylbutyrate and taurursodiol (AMX-0035, Relyvrio®) in 2022, which may act on both mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum [3]. The most recent addition is the antisense oligonucleotide Tofersen (Qalsody®), although this treatment benefits only patients with SOD1-related ALS [11]. Numerous additional pharmacological and alternative strategies are under preclinical and clinical investigation [12, 39], including approaches involving medical cannabis or specific cannabinoid compounds [10, 15, 22, 60, 64].

Interest in cannabinoids for ALS treatment began in 2004 with a series of preclinical studies in the classic SOD-1 mutant mouse model [59]. These studies explored the effects of phytocannabinoids such as Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ9-THC) [56] and cannabinol [79], Sativex®-like botanical combinations of Δ9-THC and cannabidiol (CBD) [50], and synthetic cannabinoids like WIN55,212-2 [9] and AM1241 [44, 70]. Other studies focused on inhibiting endocannabinoid-degrading enzymes such as fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) [9] and monoacylglycerol lipase (MAGL) [52]. Since these studies were conducted in SOD-1 models, where oxidative stress predominates, the neuroprotective effects were mostly attributed to the antioxidant properties of cannabinoids [10].

The discovery of additional ALS-related genes since 2005 has enabled the development of new in vitro and in vivo models [75, 78], which have helped to uncover other mechanisms of ALS pathogenesis, including dysregulated RNA metabolism, impaired protein homeostasis, defective intracellular trafficking, and disrupted cytoskeletal and axonal transport systems. Of particular relevance are transgenic models expressing mutant forms of TDP-43 [74, 77], which often show cytosolic TDP-43 aggregates in neurons and glial cells, a hallmark of both familial and sporadic ALS cases (de Boer et al., 2021; [21] Buratti [12],.

Using the TDP-43(A315T) transgenic mouse model [77], our group has carried out several studies to assess the neuroprotective potential of cannabinoids targeting the cannabinoid type-2 (CB2) receptor [26, 28, 61]. Our findings revealed CB2 receptor upregulation in reactive microglia in the spinal ventral horn [26, 28] and in activated astrocytes [28] in TDP-43(A315T) mice—consistent with observations in other neurodegenerative diseases [29, 30]. Notably, increased CB2 receptor expression has also been observed in reactive microglia in post-mortem ALS patient tissues [27, 83] and in astrocytes in canine degenerative myelopathy—a disease linked to SOD1 mutations [31]. Although CB2 receptor upregulation has been reported in SOD-1 mutant mice [70], the specific cell types involved were not identified. This glial CB2 upregulation is thought to represent an endogenous neuroprotective response to glial-mediated toxicity. Building on this hypothesis, we further evaluated the therapeutic potential of pharmacologically targeting the CB2 receptor [28, 61], demonstrating that CB2 activation preserved spinal motor neurons, reduced glial reactivity and toxicity, and improved motor function in TDP-43 mice. In contrast, genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of CB2 accelerated disease progression [61].

Encouraged by these promising findings, we designed a new study to evaluate two novel CB2 receptor agonists developed by Roche Pharma: (i) RO-6866945, which crosses the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and acts on both central and peripheral targets [51], and (ii) RO-6871304, a peripherally restricted ligand that may act on skeletal muscle, immune cells or other peripheral tissues and cell types without central effects [54]. These compounds were tested in two ALS models (SOD-1 and TDP-43 transgenic mice) with the aim of distinguishing central versus peripheral contributions to neuroprotection. This is based on the dual nature of ALS as a neuromuscular disorder, with evidence suggesting: (i) CB2 receptors are also expressed in muscle [16, 19, 85], and (ii) in some cases, ALS may originate in muscle and spread retrogradely to motor neurons [46]. As ALS-related neuropathology is known to compromise the BBB in both patients [35, 76, 80], and SOD-1 mice [33]), potentially allowing RO-6871304 to enter the CNS, we conducted additional experiments to confirm that the action of this compound in our study targeted exclusively peripheral tissues. In addition, to explore the possible contribution of CB2 receptors located in immune cells in our results, we also analyzed the level of infiltration of these cells in the spinal cord, both as a result of the pathology and following the treatment with the peripherally-restricted agonist.

Materials and methods

Animals, experiments and sampling

All experiments were conducted with SOD1(G93A) and TDP-43(A315T) transgenic and non-transgenic littermate sibling male mice bred in our animal facilities from initial breeders (B6SJL-Tg(SOD1*G93A)1Gur/J and B6,Cg-Tg(Prp-TARDBP*A315T)95Balo/J) provided by Dr. Xavier Navarro, Universidad Autónoma de Barcelona, Spain or purchased from Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME, USA), respectively. Given that the progression of the pathological phenotype in these ALS mice was significantly dependent on animal sex, especially in TDP-43 transgenic mice, which showed important sex-dependent differences in the onset of neurological signs and animal survival [77], we decided to conduct separate experiments, in males in this study and in females in follow-up experiments. Mice were subjected to genotyping for identifying the presence or absence of the transgene containing the SOD-1(G93A) or TDP-43(A315T) mutations [59, 77]. All animals were housed in a room with controlled lighting (08:00–20:00 lights on) and temperature (22 ± 1 °C) with free access to standard food and water. All experiments were conducted according to local and European rules (directive 2010/63/EU) and followed the ARRIVE guidelines. All animal experiments were approved by the ethical committees of our university and the regulatory institution (PROEX 201.8/22), which remarked the need to consider the 3R recommendations for the sample sizes in each experimental group. Then, once genotyped, wild-type and transgenic mice were identified by numbered ear marks, prior to the start of the different experiments, they were randomly allocated to the different treatment groups. For data collection, in all behavioural and histological analyses, researchers were blinded to the animal treatment, whereas for the biochemical analyses, due to the form in which the data are collected, blinding was not considered to be necessary.

In a first experiment, we treated non-transgenic and SOD1(G93A) transgenic male mice with the two different CB2 receptor agonists developed by Roche Pharma [51, 54]: (i) RO-6866945, which has been found to cross the BBB, used at the dose of 30 mg/kg/day (according to company data); and (ii) RO-6871304, which is a peripherally-preferred ligand used at the dose of 10 mg/kg/day (also according the dose-dependent experiments carried out by the company). Transgenic and non-transgenic mice were also treated with vehicle (5.3% Cremophor in saline solution) to serve as controls for treatments and induction of the experimental pathology, respectively. In all cases, the compounds and the vehicle were given i.p. (route selected as the most adequate to generate the minimal animal stress with the maximal drug bioavailability) and the treatment was initiated when animals were 60 days old and prolonged daily up to the age of 120 days following the treatment protocol used in our previous studies [50, 62]. During this period, the animal weight gain was recorded (data not shown) together with the animal response in some ALS-related tests (e.g., rotarod, hanging wire, grip strength) and the analysis of the animal neurological score using a previously published standardized scale, which have been found to be powerful tools to detect the muscle weakness in these mice [62].

In a second experiment, we treated non-transgenic and TDP-43(A315T) transgenic male mice with the two RO-6866945 at the dose of 30 mg/kg/day (according to company data) or RO-6871304 at the dose of 10 mg/kg/day (also according the dose-dependent experiments carried out by the company). Again, transgenic and non-transgenic mice were also treated with vehicle (5.3% Cremophor in saline solution) to serve as controls for treatments and induction of the experimental pathology, respectively. In all cases, the compounds and the vehicle were given i.p, and the treatment was initiated when animals were 65 days old and prolonged daily up to the age of 95 days following the treatment protocol used in our previous studies [28, 61]. This also included supplying a special gel diet (DietGel®boost, ClearH20, Portland, ME, USA) during this period (see details in [41]). During this period, the animal weight gain was recorded (data not shown) together with the animal response in the rotarod test, which again has been found to be a powerful tool to detect the muscle weakness in these mice [28, 61].

In the two pharmacological experiments, immediately after the last behavioural recording, animals were humanely killed by rapid decapitation and their spinal cords were dissected and removed. The spinal cord samples (lumbar area) to be used for histology were fixed for 1 day at 4 °C in fresh 4% paraformaldehyde prepared in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), pH 7.4. Samples were then cryoprotected by immersion in a 30% sucrose solution for a further day, and finally stored at − 80 °C prior to be used for Nissl staining and immunohistochemical analysis. In the case of SOD-1 transgenic mice, muscle tissues (tibialis anterior and gluteus medius) were also collected. Gluteus medius samples were rapidly frozen after dissection by immersion in cold 2-methylbutane and stored at −80 °C for analysis of biochemical markers, whereas tibialis anterior samples were fixed for 24 h at 4 °C in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS and subsequently preserved in PBS at 4 °C until used for immunostaining procedures.

In an extension of the first experiment carried out in SOD1(G93A) transgenic male mice, we subjected these animals to a similar chronic treatment with vehicle or RO-6871304 (also including wildtype mice treated with vehicle), and collected their spinal cords for analysis of infiltrated immune cells by using flow cytometry. This additional experiment was carried out only with the peripherally-restricted agonist because the objective in this ocassion was not to differentiate between the central and the peripheral agonists, but to obtain a first evidence that CB2 receptors located in immune cells may contribute in the beneficial effects reported.

Lastly, in an additional experiment aimed at elucidating whether the two agonists retained their properties in relation to their ability or not to cross the BBB in SOD-1 or TDP-43 transgenic mice, and discard that the effects found with the peripherally-restricted agonist were not derived from a deterioration of the BBB by the disease, we acutely treated transgenic mice, in both cases at symptomatic ages (120 days in SOD-1 transgenic mice and 95 days in TDP-43 transgenic animals), with RO-6866945 or RO-6871304, at the same doses used in the previous experiments and collected brain, spinal cord and blood samples at three different times: 30, 180 and 360 min after the treatment. These samples were adequately processed, frozen and stored at −80 °C until being used for the analysis of both agonists using a high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) procedure.

Behavioural recording

Rotarod test. Transgenic and wild-type mice were evaluated for motor weakness using the rotarod test with a LE8200 device (Panlab, Barcelona, Spain). Recordings were initiated just before starting the treatment (at 65 days of age) to have a reference value and repeated weekly during the whole treatment period. In each recording and after a period of acclimation and training (first session: 0 r.p.m. for 30 s; second and third sessions: 4 r.p.m. for 60 s, with resting periods of 10 min between sessions) conducted 30 min before, mice were tested with an acceleration from 4 to 40 r.p.m. over a period of 300 s and the time to fall off was quantified. Mice were tested for three consecutive trials with a rest period of approximately 15 min between trials and the mean of the three trials was calculated.

Hanging wire test. The latency of mice to fall from a wire cage top, which was slowly inverted and suspended at approximately 30 cm to the floor, was used as an index of motor weakness. The test was repeated three times to obtain the mean value of the three trials.

Grip strength test. The muscle strength of all four limbs was analyzed using a specific Grip Strength Meter equipment (Ugo Basile, Comerio, Italy). During the procedure, the animal four paws were positioned on the grid, and its tail was gently pulled backward, applying force at a constant speed and intensity in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions and with previous studies carried out in murine models of ALS [2]. The transducer records the maximum force just before the mouse releases its grip. Three measurements were obtained with 15-minute intervals, and the average was calculated to determine the maximum force with a correction based on the animal weight.

Neurological score. Animals were evaluated for neurological decline using a numerical scale published previously [23] with modifications. The scale ranged from 0 to 15 distributed in three sub-scales (0–5) concentrated on ambulation, strength analysis and hind-foot reflex test. A final score = 0 corresponds to animals which are not symptomatic, whereas a score = 15 reflects a state of total functional loss in hindlimbs and postural control. The assessment of ambulation was carried out placing the animal inside a corridor (10 × 10 × 80 cm) and evaluating postural control and the way in which hindlimbs were leaned during motion. The strength test evaluated the animal ability to drag and offer resistance when the tail was pulled softly to the opposite direction in which the animal moves. Lastly, the hind-foot reflex test evaluated the stiffness of the limbs and their coordination when the mouse was suspended by the tail 10 cm over the surface. The final score is calculated from the sum of values reached in each sub-scale.

Histological procedures

Fixed spinal cords were sliced with a cryostat at the lumbar level (L4-L6) to obtain coronal Sect. (20 μm thick) that were collected on gelatin-coated slides. These sections were used for Nissl-staining and for immunofluorescence procedures. In the first case, slices were stained with cresyl violet following a previously described method [4], which allowed the identification and quantification of cell bodies of spinal motor neurons. A Leica DMRB microscope and a DFC300Fx camera (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) were used to study and photograph the tissue respectively. To count the number of Nissl-stained motor neurons (> 400 µm2) in the ventral horn, high-resolution photomicrographs were taken with a 10× objective under the same conditions of light, brightness and contrast. Counting was carried out with the ImageJ software (U.S. National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA, http://imagej.nih.gov/ij/, 1997–2012). At least six images per animal were analyzed by researchers blinded to the treatments to establish the mean of all mice studied in each group.

For the immunofluorescence analysis in spinal cord samples, slices were used for detection and quantification of the spinal motor neuron marker choline-acetyl transferase (CHAT), the astrocyte marker glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) or the microglial markers ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba-1) or cluster of differentiation 68 (CD68). After preincubation for 1 h with Tris-buffered saline with 1% Triton X-100 (pH 7.5), sections were sequentially incubated overnight at 4 °C with: (i) a polyclonal anti-CHAT (1:100; Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany); (ii) a polyclonal anti-GFAP (1:200; Dako Cytomation, Glostrup, Denmark; (iii) a polyclonal anti-Iba-1 (1:500; Wako Chemicals, Richmond, VI, USA); or (iv) a polyclonal anti-CD68 (1:200; AbD Serotec, Oxford, UK) antibody, followed by washing in Tris-buffered saline and incubation (at 37 °C for 2 h) with an anti-rabbit secondary antibody made in donkey (1:200; Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA), conjugated with Alexa 488 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) rendering green fluorescence for both anti-CHAT, anti-GFAP, anti-Iba-1 or anti-CD68. Imager M2 microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) was used for slide observation and photography. For quantification, high-resolution digital microphotographs were taken with the 10× objective under the same conditions of light and brightness/contrast. They were used to measure the mean density of labelling in a selected area, using again the ImageJ software. All data were expressed in arbitrary units.

For the immunofluorescence analysis in tibialis anterior samples, muscle bundles were used for the detection of neurofilaments (neurofilament 200), synaptic vesicles (synaptophysin), and motor endplates (acetylcholine receptors). Bundles were placed in a 96-well plate and permeabilized with 2% Triton X-100 in PBS. Samples were then blocked for 1 h in PBS containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma-Aldrich, Madrid, Spain), 1% Triton X-100, and 10% normal goat serum (Milford, MA, USA). Following this, samples were incubated for 24 h with MouseOnMouse (Vector, CA, USA) in PBS. After blocking, samples were incubated for 24 h at room temperature with gentle agitation, using the following primary antibodies: (i) anti-neurofilament 200 (1:100, Millipore, MA, USA); and (ii) anti-synaptophysin (1:250, Millipore, MA, USA), both diluted in PBS with 5% BSA and 1% Triton X-100. After washing the primary antibodies with PBS, sections were incubated for 2 h at room temperature with secondary antibodies: (i) an anti-rabbit secondary antibody made in donkey (1:200, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) conjugated with Alexa Fluor 594, which rendered red fluorescence for anti-neurofilament 200; and (ii) an anti-mouse secondary antibody made in donkey (1:200, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) conjugated with Alexa Fluor 594 for anti-synaptophysin. Acetylcholine receptor ligand α-bungarotoxin (BTX) was also added at a concentration of 1.2 µg/ml (BTX-Alexa Fluor™ 488, B13422, ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA). Samples were washed with PBS, rinsed in water, and mounted using an aqueous mounting medium (Dako, CA, USA). They were stored at 4 °C in the dark until microscopic observation. For quantification, muscle bundles were visualized and photographed using an Imager M2 microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), and high-resolution images were captured with an Axioncam 705 mono camera (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The images were visualized using a 60x objective and classified into the following categories: (i) innervated, when the acetylcholine receptor appears intact, and staining of both neurofilament and synaptic vesicles is observed; (ii) partially innervated, when the receptor is fragmented and slightly diffuse, and staining of either neurofilament or synaptic vesicles is present; or (iii) denervated, when the receptor appears highly diffuse and without staining for neurofilament or synaptic vesicles (see details in [47]. At least 50 neuromuscular junctions per animal were analyzed.

Flow cytometry analysis

As indicated before, spinal cords from vehicle-treated wildtype mice, vehicle-treated SOD-1(G93A) transgenic mice and RO-6871304-treated SOD-1(G93A) transgenic mice were harvested for flow cytometry analysis. To this end, animals were transcardially perfused with PBS (pH 7.4) for 5 min to remove circulating erythrocytes. Spinal cords were rapidly dissected, placed in cold Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, with Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺; ThermoFischer, MA, USA), and mechanically minced using scissors. Tissues were enzymatically digested in a solution containing 1 mM CaCl2, DNase I (0.1 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, Madrid, Spain), and collagenase D (0.16 µg/µL; Sigma-Aldrich, Madrid, Spain) for 45 min at 37 °C. The resulting suspension was mechanically dissociated through a 23G needle and filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 440 × g for 5 min at room temperature (RT), and the pellet was resuspended in 30% isotonic Percoll. A density gradient (1 ml 70% Percoll, 1.5 ml 30% Percoll and 0.5 ml sterile 1x PBS) centrifugation was performed at 1200 × g for 30 min at RT (no brake). The myelin layer was carefully aspirated, and mononuclear cells were collected from the 30%/70% interface, filtered through a 70 μm strainer, and washed once in staining buffer [PBS supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 25 mM HEPES, and 2% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S)]. Cells were pelleted again (440 × g, 5 min, RT) and resuspended in 100 µL staining buffer. Fc receptors were blocked using anti-CD16/CD32 antibodies (10 µg/mL; BD Biosciences) for 10 min at 4 °C. Following Fc block, cells were stained for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark with the following fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies diluted in staining buffer: rat anti-CD45-BV510 (clone 30F11, 0.6 µg/ml), and rat anti-CD11b-PerCP-Cy5.5 (clone M1/70, 4 µg/ml). This antibody panel has previously been used by Chiu et al. [17] as a gating strategy addressed to obtain a first experimental evidence that guides the studies that should follow the present one. Cells were washed, centrifuged at 210 × g for 5 min, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed on a CytoFLEX S cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Barcelona, Spain) at the Flow Cytometry Core Facility of Hospital Nacional de Parapléjicos by using FlowJo software v10.9.0 (Tree Star Inc., Ashland, OR, USA). Fluorescence Minus One (FMO) controls were used to set gates and determine positive expression for each marker.

Real time qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from the different muscle samples using Trizol (TRI-Reagent, Sigma-Aldrich, Madrid, Spain) and following the manufacturer’s instructions. The total amount of RNA extracted was quantitated by spectrometry at 260 nm and its purity from the ratio between the absorbance values at 260 and 280 nm. After genomic DNA was removed (to eliminate DNA contamination), single-stranded complementary DNA was synthesized from up to 1 µg of total RNA using the commercial kits Rneasy Mini Quantitect Reverse Transcription (Qiagen, Hilgen, Germany). The reaction mixture was kept frozen at −20 °C until enzymatic amplification. Quantitative RT-PCR assays were performed using TaqMan Gene Expression Assays (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) to quantify mRNA levels for genes encoding glutathione disulfide reductase (Gsr; ref. Mm00833903_m1), collagen α−1(XIX) chain (Col19A1, ref. Mm00483576_m1), sortin nexin 10 (Snx10, ref. Mm00511049_m1), myocyte enhancer factor 2 (Mef2, ref. Mm00600423_m1), calmodulin 1 (Calm1, ref. Mm00486655-m1) and CB2 receptor (Cnr2, ref. Mm00438286_m1) using GAPDH expression (ref. Mm99999915_g1) as an endogenous control gene for normalization. The PCR assay was performed using the 7300 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) and the threshold cycle (Ct) was calculated by the instrument’s software (7300 Fast System, Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

HPLC-MS analysis

To conduct pharmacokinetic studies, a single i.p. dose of the two compounds was administered in SOD1(G93A) and TDP-43(A315T) transgenic mice, following the doses and vehicles previously specified. Animals were sacrificed at intervals of 30, 180, and 360 min after drug administration to obtain samples of brain, spinal cord and plasma. The presence of the compounds in the samples collected was analyzed by using HPLC-MS utilizing an AB Sciex Qtrap 6500 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, MA, USA) along with a Shimadzu liquid chromatography system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The quantification of the compounds in the samples was performed using standard calibration samples. Compound concentrations are expressed in ng/g for brain and spinal cord samples and in ng/ml for plasma samples.

Statistics

GraphPad Prism, version 8.00 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for the statistical assessment and presentation of the data. Data were assessed for the normality of the distribution with the Shapiro-Wilks test. Data that followed a normal distribution were analyzed with the one-way or two-way (for repeated measures) analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Tukey test. Data that were not normally distributed were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s test for multiple comparisons. A p value lower than 0.05 was used as the limit for statistical significance. The sample sizes in the different experimental groups are stated in the figure legends but were always greater than 6 mice per group in most cases.

Treatment with the two agonists in SOD-1 Transgenic mice: neuroprotective effects

The first experiment was aimed at investigating the neuroprotective effects of these two novel CB2 receptor agonists, RO-6866945 and RO-6871304, in SOD-1 transgenic mice. As expected, these mice showed an important loss of weight gain during the progression of the disease compared to wildtype mice, which was not reversed by the treatment with the two different agonists (data not shown). Such weight gain loss was paralleled by the expected deterioration in the animal performance in different behavioral tests such as: (i) rotarod test, in particular at advanced stages of the disease, e.g., 5–8 weeks after the onset of the treatment which correspond to 13–16 weeks of age (see Fig. 1A); (ii) hanging wire test, in particular at 4–8 weeks after the onset of the treatment, which correspond to 12–16 weeks of age (see Fig. 1B); and (iii) grip strength test, in particular at 7–8 weeks after the onset of the treatment which correspond to 15–16 weeks of age (see Fig. 1C). SOD-1 transgenic mice also showed an elevated score in the ALS standardized scale used in this study, again reflecting their neurological deterioration, in particular at 6–8 weeks after the onset of the treatment which correspond to 14–16 weeks of age (see Fig. 1D). Although the treatment with each of these two CB2 receptor agonists did not afford any statistically-significant recovery in this neurological decline, we could appreciate certain trends. For example, the treatment with RO-6866945, which is able to penetrate the CNS through the BBB, showed certain trend towards a recovery in the deteriorated performance of SOD-1 transgenic mice in the rotarod test, in particular at 5–8 weeks after the onset of the treatment (treatment: F(3,200) = 2.0, p = 0.14; treatment x time interaction: F(24,200) = 1.84, p < 0.05; see Fig. 1A). Similar results were also visible in the hanging wire test at 7–8 weeks after the onset of the treatment (K(4) = 7.64, p < 0.05; see Fig. 1B), in the grip strength test at 7–8 weeks after the onset of the treatment (treatment: F(3,64) = 2.44, p = 0.08; see Fig. 1C), and in the neurological scale at 6–8 weeks after the onset of the treatment (treatment: F(3,256) = 21.63, p < 0.0001; treatment x time interaction: F(24,256) = 7.04, p < 0.0001; see Fig. 1D). Interestingly, such trends towards recovery were also evident after the treatment with RO-6871304, which does not cross the BBB at the dose used here (10 mg/kg), although apparently some of these trends appeared to be a little bit lower compared to RO-6866945 (see Fig. 1A-D).

As already described in numerous previous studies (reviewed in [22, 60], the behavioral impairment observed in SOD-1 transgenic mice is directly related to the loss of spinal motor neurons, as measured by Nissl staining (F(3,28) = 24.55, p < 0.0001; see Fig. 2A, B) and immunofluorescence for choline-acetyl transferase enzyme (F(3,25) = 40.77, p < 0.0001; see Fig. 2C, D) in the ventral horn of the lumbar spinal cord sections. The treatment with the BBB-penetrating agonist RO-6866945 partially reversed the loss of spinal motor neurons (see Fig. 2A, C), whereas the peripherally-restricted agonist RO-6871304 showed an unexpected ability to reproduce these recoveries and to a similar extent than RO-6866945 (see Fig. 2A, C). Next, we analyzed immunoreactivity levels for the astrocyte marker GFAP and observed elevated levels of this marker in SOD-1 transgenic mice (F(3,27) = 24.34, p < 0.0001; see Fig. 3A, B), which were scarcely modified by both agonists (see Fig. 3A, B). As regards to the analysis of microglial markers, SOD-1 transgenic mice also presented elevated Iba-1 and CD68 immunoreactivity levels (Iba-1: F(3,26) = 17.18, p < 0.0001; CD68: F(3,23) = 21.14, p < 0.0001; see Fig. 4A-D), as previously reported [62]. The treatment with the peripherally restricted agonist appeared to be more effective in reducing the immunoreactivity for the two markers, showing statistically significant differences (p < 0.05 for Iba-1 and p < 0.01 for CD68) compared to vehicle-treated SOD-1 transgenic mice (Fig. 4A, C). In contrast, the central agonist only showed a trend toward significance for Iba-1 (p = 0.07) (Fig. 4A) and a lower level of significance for CD68 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4C), although, looking at the single values, it was evident that some animals showed a complete reversion in glial activation, but this occurred in groups having a very large variability. It would have been interesting to confirm these changes in glial markers (GFAP, Iba-1 and CD68) using western blot analysis, but all spinal cord tissues collected were prepared for histological procedures and there was no availability for other analyses. Anyway, the immunostaining was always carried out with validated antibodies, appropriate controls, and using standardized quantification protocols.

Treatment with the two agonists in SOD-1 Transgenic mice: effects in skeletal muscle

We also analyzed the expression of some muscle (using gluteus medius) genes that have been found representative of longevity during muscle deterioration in SOD-1 transgenic mice [14]. We evaluated the expression of markers of oxidative stress as Gsr (F(3,25) = 2.76, p = 0.06), markers of muscle differentiation and regeneration as Col19A1 (F(3,25) = 8.97, p < 0.0005) and Mef2 (F(3,26) = 2.97, p < 0.05), markers of endocytosis as Snx10 (F(3,24) = 10.06, p < 0.0005), and markers of calcium signaling as Calm1 (F(3,24) = 4.40, p < 0.05), whose values were always elevated in SOD-1 transgenic mice (Fig. 5A-E). Interestingly, both RO-6866945 and RO-6871304 attenuated the upregulation of Col19, with RO-6871304 appearing to be more effective (Fig. 5B), and Snx10, with RO-6866945 showing greater efficacy (Fig. 5C). By contrast, both agonists were similarly effective, though to a lesser extent, in reducing Gsr (Fig. 5A). However, the peripherally-restricted agonist showed no activity in regulating Calm1 and Mef2 (Fig. 5D, E), where the central agonist was highly effective (Calm1) or showed a trend toward reduction (Mef2) (Fig. 5D, E).

In the same muscle tissues, we also measured the expression of Cnr2 that encodes the CB2 receptor, which showed the expected elevation in SOD-1 transgenic mice (F(3,23) = 4.12, p < 0.05). Treatment with both agonists showed trends towards reduction in Cnr2 expression, with a more pronounced effect observed for RO-6871304 (Fig. 5F). This reduction should be interpreted as originated by the beneficial effects of the two agonists which reverse the elevation experienced by transgenic mice, although a contribution of cellular events related to the regulation of receptor availability in conditions of chronic activation cannot be ruled out. The presence of CB2 receptors (and other elements of the endocannabinoid system) in muscle tissues is something already described in previous studies using normal and/or pathological samples [16, 19, 85]. This had also been observed in our laboratory some time ago using immunostaining for this receptor in wildtype mice as well as in SOD-1 transgenic animals, in which the immunoreactivity levels resulted to be strongly elevated, then confirming the data of mRNA levels obtained in the present study (Fig. 5F). However, it is important to remark that the muscle used in this former analysis was the gastrocnemius and not the gluteus medius used in the qPCR analysis. Given that these former data had not been published yet, we decided to include them here as supplementary material (see Supplementary Fig. 1 A, B).

Next, assuming the potential relevance of muscle CB2 receptors in the effects found here with the two Roche agonists, we wanted to analyze the differences in the degree of innervation of muscle fibers (using tibialis anterior) by motor neurons in wildtype versus SOD-1 transgenic mice, as well as the possible benefits reached with the treatment with the two CB2 receptor agonists. Our data indicated that the percentage of denervated muscle fibers increased in SOD-1 transgenic mice in parallel to a reduction in innervated fibers and no changes in partly innervated fibers, values that were relatively similar to other studies [47], including the percentage detected in wildtype mice for denervated fibers, which should be interpreted as related to the analysis uncertainty. Importantly, the treatment with the agonists re-established the situation seen in wildtype mice (2-way interaction: F(6,69) = 6.81, p < 0.0001; see Fig. 6A, B).

Treatment with RO-6871304 in SOD-1 Transgenic mice: effects in immune cells

To further investigate the role that peripheral CB2 receptors may exert in SOD-1 transgenic mice, we also carried out a preliminary experiment aimed at obtained a first proof-of-concept supporting a potential contribution of immune cells, in which the CB2 receptor is highly expressed (recently reviewed in [71], in the effects found with the peripherally-restricted agonist. To this end, we analyzed by using flow cytometry the levels of infiltrated immune cells in the spinal cord of vehicle- or RO-6871304-treated SOD-1 transgenic mice, as well as vehicle-treated wildtype mice. Our data indicated that, whereas the number of CD45high infiltrated cells tended to be elevated in SOD-1 transgenic mice versus wiltype animals, there was not any statistically significant difference between vehicle- and RO-6871304-treated SOD-1 mice (Supplementary Fig. 2). However, the difference between the transgenic mice treated with RO-6871304 and the wildtype mice reached statistical significance (Supplementary Fig. 2). A preliminary analysis of the cellular composition of these potential infiltrated cells in the spinal cord revealed that this pattern (e.g., a trend towards an increase in vehicle-treated SOD-1 transgenic mice compared to wildtype mice, no statistically-significant differences between vehicle- and RO-6871304-treated SOD-1 mice, a statistically-significant elevation in RO-6871304-treated SOD-1 mice compared to wildtype mice) was observed not only for the total number of infiltrated cells (K(3) = 6.18, p < 0.05), but also for the infiltrated lymphoid (CD45highCD11b−, K(3) = 6.2, p < 0.05) and myeloid (CD45highCD11b+, K(3) = 6.17, p < 0.05) cells (Supplementary Fig. 2). The total number of spinal cord cells followed relatively the same pattern too (K(3) = 6.03, p < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 2). However, it is important to remark in relation with the results obtained in this part of our study its preliminary nature and its limitations. The antibody panel used consisted of CD45 and CD11b which may label both microglial and infiltrated immune (e.g., T lymphocytes) cells, as previously shown by Chiu et al. [17]. However, we assume that, for a stronger differentiation among these two groups of cells, the use of additional antibodies (e.g., CD3, CD19, Ly-6 C, Ly6G, F4/80, CD11c) is mandatory, but this will be addressed in the projected follow-up studies. In absence of these additional analyses, we assume the difficulties for elucidating whether CD45high labelling reflects two distinct cell populations, as we hypothesize, or simply a more activated state of resident microglia. Treatment with the two agonists in TDP-43 Transgenic mice

The second experiment in this study was aimed at elucidating the neuroprotective effects of RO-6866945 and RO-6871304 in TDP-43 transgenic mice. Again as expected, these mice showed an important weight loss across disease progression compared to wildtype mice. This weight loss was not reversed by any of the agonists (data not shown). Again, such weight loss was paralleled by the expected deterioration in the animal performance in the rotarod test (see Fig. 7). The treatment with RO-6866945, which penetrates the BBB, only showed a certain trend towards recovery, but without reaching statistical significance respect vehicle-treated TDP-43 transgenic mice (except at the 2 weeks after the onset of treatment in which the difference was p < 0.05). These results were in line with those observed in SOD-1 transgenic mice, in particular at 1–3 weeks after the treatment initiation (treatment: F(3,96) = 17.01, p < 0.0001; treatment x time interaction: F(12,96) = 2.0, p < 0.05; see Fig. 7). However, such a trend towards recovery disappeared at advanced stages (4 weeks after the onset of treatment; see Fig. 7). By contrast, RO-6871304 showed a different pattern, being poorly active at the beginning of the treatment and showing more pronounced trends towards recovery at 3–4 weeks. Anyway, the effects produced by RO-6871304 did not reach statistical significance at any of the timepoints recorded during the treatment (see Fig. 7).

Analysis of the biodistribution of the two agonists in SOD-1 and TDP-43 Transgenic mice

The results obtained after the treatment with RO-6866945 or RO-6871304 in the two transgenic lines of experimental ALS (e.g., trends towards an improvement in the neurological status of these mice, preservation of spinal motor neurons, attenuation of glial reactivity responses) support the idea that CB2 receptors located outside the BBB, for example, in nerve terminals, Schwann cells, skeletal muscles or immune cells, may be therapeutically important in ALS. To rule out a possible damage in the BBB caused by ALS pathology in the two experimental models, which may have allowed RO-6871304 penetrance in the CNS in a pathological context, we carried out an additional experiment consisting in collecting brain, spinal cord and plasma samples derived from SOD-1 (at 120 days of age) or TDP-43 (at 95 days of age) transgenic mice treated with RO-6866945 or RO-6871304. These samples were used to analyze the concentrations of the two agonists in each compartment using a LC-MS procedure. In SOD-1 transgenic mice, the acute treatment with the central agonist resulted in high levels of this agonist in the brain, spinal cord and plasma at 30 min after the administration, followed by a fast reduction at 180 min resulting in negligible levels at 360 min (Fig. 10A-C). The same pattern was observed in TDP-43 transgenic mice, although the reduction in agonist levels was slower (due presumably to differences in the metabolism rate between both models) with low but still detectable levels at 360 min (Fig. 10D-F). As regards to the levels of the peripherally-restricted agonist, they were undetectable in the brain and the spinal cord of RO-6871304-treated SOD-1 transgenic mice at the three times analyzed (Fig. 10A, B), whereas negligible levels were detected in TDP-43 transgenic mice at 30 min followed by undetectable levels at 180 and 360 min (Fig. 10D, E). By contrast, RO-6871304 was highly detected in plasma samples at values similar to those found for RO-6866945, even despite the differences in the doses used (Fig. 10C, F), something that could be explained by the differences in the ability of the agonists to cross the BBB. In addition, it should be noted that plasma levels of RO-6871304 in SOD-1 transgenic mice were approximately 50% lower than in TDP-43 transgenic animals, which could again be explained by possible differences in pharmacokinetics and metabolism between both models (Fig. 10C, F). Together, these data confirm that RO-6871304 was not biodistributed into the CNS, then indirectly excluding a possible BBB damage in SOD-1 and TDP-43 transgenic mice.

Discussion

As outlined in the introduction, cannabinoids capable of activating the CB2 receptor have shown promising neuroprotective effects in experimental models of ALS [28, 44, 60, 70],b). These effects appear to be primarily mediated through CB2 receptors predominantly located in CNS glial cells, especially upon activation. The activation of glial cells leads to an upregulation of CB2 receptor expression, enhancing their anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective functions [9, 26–28, 31, 83]. In this study, we conducted new experiments to further investigate the CB2 receptor–dependent neuroprotective effects, particularly focusing on non-central locations of these receptors. Considering that ALS is a neuromuscular disease, and that CB2 receptors—along with other endocannabinoid elements—are expressed in muscle tissue [16, 19, 85], and also in other peripheral sites (e.g., immune cells; Porter et al., [54, 55, 71], we hypothesized that peripheral CB2 receptors may also be implicated in ALS and contribute to the beneficial effects observed with CB2 receptor agonists [28, 44, 60, 70],b). Indeed, selective CB2 receptor activation has been shown to protect skeletal muscle from ischemia-reperfusion injury [85]. To explore this, we used two CB2 receptor agonists developed by Roche Pharma: RO-6866945, which crosses the BBB and acts on both central and peripheral targets [51]; and RO-6871304, which, at the dose used, is peripherally restricted—acting primarily on peripheral sites, for example skeletal muscle or immune cells that express CB2 receptors, without central nervous system activity [5, 54].

We tested both compounds in two ALS mouse models: SOD-1 and TDP-43 transgenic mice, in which centrally active CB2 agonists have previously demonstrated efficacy [28, 44, 60, 70],b). These two models differ in their predominant pathogenic mechanisms—oxidative stress and SOD-1 aggregation in the former, and RNA metabolism defects and TDP-43 aggregation in the latter. Our data show that both agonists, regardless of CNS penetration, were scarcely active in improving neurological performance across behavioral tests, with effects that remained as simple trends towards recovery. However, the two treatments were effective in preserving spinal motor neurons and mitigating reactive gliosis. In TDP-43 mice, the central agonist reduced microgliosis, but the peripherally restricted one did not. Therefore, although not all parameters were analyzed equally in both models, the two agonists produced comparable results, particularly in preserving Nissl-stained spinal motor neurons, with RO-6871304 showing greater potency in some outcomes. One option to explain this fact is that CB2 receptors in both CNS and peripheral tissues (likely muscle and immune cells) contribute to the neuroprotection observed. However, our data fit better with the option that only peripheral CB2 receptors mediate these effects. This likely means that the effects of the CNS penetrant agonist would be also due to activity in the systemic compartment instead. This is also supported by the observations made in the analysis of gene expression in muscle tissue, where the brain penetrant agonist was even more effective than the peripherally-restricted one. However, instead of implicating muscle or immune CB2 receptors as the sole contributors, it is also plausible that CB2 receptors located on motor neuron terminals or Schwann cells are key targets—allowing the peripherally restricted agonist to influence neural elements. In a previous study [27], we investigated CB2 receptor expression in motor neurons, but antibody specificity issues limited the validity of those findings. While CB2 receptors have been confirmed in oligodendrocytes [49], their presence in Schwann cells remains unclear. Clarifying whether central or peripheral CB2 activation is primarily responsible will require further research.

Importantly, the possibility that RO-6871304 crosses the BBB due to ALS-associated pathology was addressed. Although BBB disruption has been reported in ALS [35, 76, 80], and partially replicated in SOD-1 mice ([33, 34] our HPLC-MS analysis detected only RO-6866945 in CNS tissue, confirming that RO-6871304 remains peripherally restricted even in disease conditions. This strengthens the conclusion that peripheral CB2 receptors are responsible for the observed effects, although further studies would be required to evaluate downstream CB2-specific signaling events in the spinal cord (as those recently reviewed by Rakotoarivelo et al., [55] to definitively exclude that RO-6871304 is acting in the CNS.

As indicated before, significant improvements in muscle markers in SOD-1 mice suggest a direct action of the agonists on muscle CB2 receptors. Similarly, the restoration of neuromuscular innervation further supports a role for peripheral CB2 activation—possibly at the neuromuscular junction—though the contribution of immune cell CB2 receptors cannot be excluded. If the “dying-back” hypothesis of ALS is valid [24, 25, 46, 53, 81], preserving muscle fibers and neuromuscular junctions through peripheral CB2 activation may indirectly protect motor neurons and reduce glial reactivity. However, this contrasts with studies that dispute a primary role for muscle in ALS [20, 48, 73], highlighting the need for further investigation.

Regarding the potential contribution of peripherally-located CB2 receptor-containing cells, we further investigated immune cells, specifically focusing on their ability to infiltrate the CNS. The number of both myeloid, and particularly, lymphoid infiltrating cells in the spinal cord was moderately elevated in the pathology. The deletereous role of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the physiopathology of the SOD-1 model of ALS has been previously reported [17, 18, 84]. Consequently, we hypothesized that beneficial effects of the peripherally-restricted CB2 receptor agonist were related, at least in part, to the receptors located in immune cells, whose activation might reduce immune cell infiltration [18]. However, our data indicated that the number of infiltrated immune cells after the treatment with RO-6871304 in SOD-1 transgenic mice was largely similar to that in transgenic animals treated with vehicle, with some cases even showing a trend towards further elevation. Nonetheless, this preliminary experiment warrants further investigation particularly regarding immune cell composition, with a focus on T regulatory cells, whose presence has been associated with neuronal preservation in this animal model [68]. Our hypothesis for this further work is that CB2 receptor activation in these immune cells may reduce its toxicity once infiltrated into the CNS instead affecting the extent of cell infiltration, but this will need to be investigated and confirmed. If this is the case, this may also explain that similar levels of infiltrated immune cells in vehicle- and RO-6871304-treated SOD-1 transgenic mice may coexist with reduced levels of glial reactivity measured with GFAP and Iba-1 immunostaining.

From a translational standpoint, the use of a peripherally restricted CB2 agonist like RO-6871304 may offer clinical advantages by reducing the likelihood of CNS-related side effects. Recent evidence suggests CB2 receptors are also present in certain neuronal populations [5, 6, 29, 30], where overstimulation could affect synaptic function and trigger adverse effects [38]. Therefore, the ability of RO-6871304 to replicate the benefits of a centrally active compound without BBB penetration is promising—though peripheral side effects such as immunosuppression remain a concern. In conclusion, both RO-6866945 (centrally active) and RO-6871304 (peripherally restricted) improved neurological outcomes in SOD-1 and TDP-43 mouse models by preserving spinal motor neurons and reducing glial reactivity. In SOD-1 mice, these effects extended to restoration of muscle innervation. The overall efficacy of RO-6871304—despite its peripheral restriction—positions peripheral CB2 receptors, likely in muscle fibers (while not entirely excluding immune cells), as relevant targets for ALS therapy. These findings challenge the traditional CNS-centric view of CB2 receptor–mediated neuroprotection in ALS and provide a rationale for progressing peripheral CB2 agonists into clinical trials. However, further studies are needed—including those involving female subjects, post-symptomatic treatments, and mechanistic evaluations—to support clinical translation.
