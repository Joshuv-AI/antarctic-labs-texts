Rational design of immunological programs for functional regenerative niches
Authors: Zhumei Zhuang, Pengming Wang, Yixiao Liu, Lili Song, Xianzhu Zhang, Hongwei Ouyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe immune system is a master conductor of tissue repair, yet its therapeutic potential remains underexploited. Moving beyond descriptive biology, we propose ‘immunoprogramming’ as an active design paradigm that synergistically integrates stem cells and biomaterials to steer host immunity toward pro-regenerative outcomes. Here, we decode the source-specific immunomodulatory signatures of stem cells—from bone marrow and adipose tissue to dental and iPSC-derived populations—and establish how the biochemical and biophysical language of engineered biomaterials can directly command innate and adaptive immune cell fate. Crucially, we emphasize that within integrated cell-scaffold constructs, a bidirectional crosstalk emerges: the biomaterial primes the stem cell, which in turn execute enhanced context-specific immunomodulation. To translate this synergy into a design discipline, we introduce the Synergistic Immuno-Instructional System (SIIS) framework, which modularizes Cell-Source-Specific Immuno-Modules (CSIMs), Biomaterial-Based Immuno-Code (BIC), and Tissue-Specific Immuno-Niche (TSIN). Finally, we discuss translational challenges and advocate for next generation ‘immuno-informed’ therapeutics that evolve from passive replacement to active immune niche engineering, charting a course for intelligent regenerative medicine.Keywords: Immunoprogramming, Stem cells, Biomaterials, Regenerative immunology, Tissue engineering

Introduction

The field of tissue regeneration is at a pivotal juncture. For decades, the prevailing strategy has been cell-centric, focusing on the delivery of multipotent stem cells, most notably mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), to directly replace lost tissue. While promising, the clinical translation of these therapies has been hindered by inconsistent outcomes, poor cell survival, and a limited understanding of their primary mechanism of action. A critical reassessment has revealed that the therapeutic benefits of MSCs often stem less from their long-term engraftment and differentiation, and more from their potent, paracrine-mediated immunomodulatory capacity [[1], [2], [3]]. This insight catalyzes a profound paradigm shift: the immune system is no longer an inflammatory barrier to be suppressed, but rather a dynamic, programmable ally whose orchestration is central to functional repair. Harnessing this alliance requires shifting from passive cell delivery to the active design of immunoregulatory microenvironments.

To navigate this shift, we introduce the concept of “immunoprogramming.” This review posits that the most potent platform for achieving this lies in the convergence of stem cell biology and biomaterials science. Individually, stem cells provide a sophisticated, responsive biological “software” capable of sensing and modulating inflammation, while biomaterials offer a tunable, engineered “hardware” that can deliver precise physicochemical instructions to immune cells [[4], [5], [6]]. However, their true potential is unlocked in integration. We argue that within a combined construct, a bidirectional crosstalk emerges: the biomaterial scaffold primes and potentiates the stem cells, which in turn interpret these cues to execute enhanced, context-specific immunomodulation. This creates a dynamic positive feedback loop that amplifies repair synergy, demonstrating that the whole is greater than the sum of its parts.

To translate this framework into a design discipline, systematic decoding of the available tools is essential. First, we dissect the source-dependent immunomodulatory signatures of stem cells. While sharing core functions, MSCs derived from bone marrow, adipose, dental, and other tissues possess distinct secretory profiles and potencies shaped by their developmental origin. This heterogeneity is not a drawback but a design feature, allowing for the selection of the most appropriate cellular “repertoire” for a given regenerative challenge. In parallel, we examine biomaterials not as passive scaffolds, but as active immuno-instructive niches. Their biochemical (e.g., chemistry, peptides) and biophysical (e.g., stiffness, viscoelasticity, topography) properties constitute a powerful language that directly commands macrophage polarization, lymphocyte activity and overall inflammatory trajectory [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Understanding this language is the first step toward writing pro-regenerative immune instructions.

The core of this review focuses on the emergent properties of integrated stem cell-biomaterial constructs. We explore how three-dimensional confinement, mechanical cues, and biochemical functionalization of the scaffold fundamentally rewire stem cell phenotype, unlocking a more potent immunoregulatory state. This section moves from descriptive biology to engineering design principles, detailing how to tailor this synergistic dialogue. Finally, we ground this theory in practice, examining how immunoprogramming strategies are being tailored to meet the unique regenerative demands of specific tissues—myocardium, skin, liver, and osteochondral structures—where overriding pathological inflammation and steering immune-driven repair cascades are paramount. We argue that within a combined construct, a bidirectional crosstalk emerges, forming the basis of the “Synergistic Immuno-Instructional System (SIIS)” a theoretical model that integrates cellular, material, and tissue-level instructional cues to achieve predictive and potent immune-mediated repair.

To translate this conceptual framework into a design discipline, this review is structured as follows (Fig. 1): First, we dissect the source-dependent immunomodulatory signatures of stem cells, providing a rational selection guide. Second, we deconstruct the immuno-instructive language of biomaterials, linking physicochemical properties to immune cell programming. Third, we explore the emergent properties of integrated constructs, elucidating how material cues rewire stem cell phenotypes. Fourth, we demonstrate tissue-specific applications of immunoprogramming strategies. Finally, we outline translational challenges and future directions, advocating for a paradigm shift toward intelligent, immune-guided regenerative therapeutics.

The stem cell repertoire: source-specific immunomodulatory signatures for rational selection

The concept of immunoprogramming positions stem cells not merely as therapeutic agents, but as modular biological components within an engineered system. A critical design parameter is the selection of the appropriate cellular module. While mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) from all sources share core immunomodulatory functions—such as dampening T-cell proliferation and polarizing macrophages, their functional identity is profoundly shaped by their tissue of origin. This “developmental imprinting” results in distinct secretory profiles, mechanistic emphases, and regenerative competencies. Therefore, a nuanced understanding of these source-dependent signatures is paramount for the rational design of immunoprogramming constructs, allowing researchers to match a cell's innate “instructional set” with the specific immune and regenerative demands of a target tissue. We introduce the concept of “Cell-Source-Specific Immuno-Modules (CSIMs)” a framework for categorizing stem cells based on their developmental origin and corresponding immunoregulatory profile, enabling rational selection for targeted immunoprogramming applications (Fig. 2).

The stem cell immunomodulation toolkit: source-specific signatures and applications. A comparative schematic summarizing the key immunomodulatory characteristics and therapeutic applications of stem cells derived from different tissue sources. Adipose tissue-derived MSCs (AT-MSCs) offer high accessibility, robust expansion, and a distinct secretome rich in IL-6 and IL-8, positioning them as versatile candidates for soft tissue repair and vascularized regeneration. Dental tissue-derived MSCs (DT-MSCs), including gingival, pulp, and periodontal ligament sources, exhibit high proliferative rates and specialized immunomodulation—such as inhibition of T-cell subsets and induction of M2 macrophage polarization—making them particularly suited for oral-craniofacial and nerve regeneration. Engineered iPSC-derived MSCs provide a scalable, uniform cell source with low immunogenicity and enhanced reparative potential, enabling standardized “off-the-shelf” therapies. Skin-derived MSCs (S-MSCs) demonstrate dynamic, context-dependent immunoregulation and angiogenic capacity, relevant for dermatological and inflammatory conditions. This source-specific functional profiling enables rational selection of MSC modules for targeted immunoprogramming and regenerative applications.

Bone marrow-derived MSCs (BM-MSCs): the benchmark with defined immunoregulatory prowess

As the first-identified and most extensively characterized population, BM-MSCs serve as the historical benchmark [11]. They are defined by a potent, broad-spectrum immunosuppressive capacity, particularly towards adaptive immunity. Their low immunogenicity, due to negligible MHC-II and co-stimulatory molecule expression, underpins their suitability for allogeneic use [12]. Functionally, BM-MSCs robustly suppress T-cell activation, modulate B-cell responses, and are potent inducers of regulatory T cells (Tregs) through the coordinated secretion of key mediators like indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) [13,14]. Comparative studies often rank BM-MSCs highly for T-cell suppression and expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1015. Moreover, BM-MSCs can also effectively enhance the immunomodulatory function of Treg cells through upregulating the expression of IL-10 and factor associated suicide ligand (FasL) [15,16]. However, their translational utility is constrained by invasive harvesting, low yield, and donor-age-dependent functional senescence, limitations that motivate the exploration of alternative sources [17]. Their strong osteogenic bias continues to make them a preferred choice for skeletal immunoprogramming strategies [18,19].

Adipose tissue-derived MSCs (AT-MSCs): the accessible all-rounder with metabolic influence

AT-MSCs have emerged as a highly pragmatic and potent alternative. Their primary advantages are minimally invasive harvest, high yield, and superior in vitro expansibility [20,21]. Immunomodulatorily, AT-MSCs share core functions with BM-MSCs but exhibit a distinct secretory profile often characterized by elevated levels of IL-6 and IL-8 [22,23]. They induce anti-inflammatory IL-10 secretion in monocytes via HGF [24,25], promote regulatory B-cell (Breg) differentiation [26], with some studies suggesting they may surpass BM-MSCs in steering dendritic cells toward a tolerogenic state [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Furthermore, their differentiation potential, particularly towards myogenic and neurogenic lineages, is notable [28,29]. This combination of accessibility, robust growth, and a multifaceted immunometabolic secretome positions AT-MSCs as versatile all-rounders, especially suited for soft tissue repair and vascularized regeneration.

Dental tissue-derived MSCs (DT-MSCs): specialized architects of oral-craniofacial regeneration

DT-MSCs, harvested from gingiva, pulp and periodontal ligament, represent a paradigm of site-specific regenerative specialization [30]. They are characterized by exceptionally high proliferative rates and potent, context-relevant immunomodulation. Gingival MSCs (GMSCs) are assumed to be a suitable cell source in periodontal tissue engineering, which are easy to isolate, uniformly homogenous, and proliferate faster than BM-MSCs without any exogenous growth factor [31]. Their immunomodulatory effects are extensive: they promote M2 macrophage polarization via PGE2, IL-6, and IL-10 secretion, inhibit CD4+ T cell proliferation and Th1/Th2/Th17 differentiation and significantly reduce the activation and maturation of DCs by a PGE2-related mechanism that suppresses the antigen presentation ability of DCs [[32], [33], [34]]. They have been reported to attenuate chemotherapy-induced oral mucositis [35]. Meanwhile, GMSCs also presented promising potential for nerve regeneration and functional recovery [36].

Dental Pulp Stem Cells (DPSCs) also possess strong immunosuppressive capabilities. They can inhibit T and B cell activity [37], and notably, they can convert pro-inflammatory Th17 cells into an immunosuppressive regulatory Th17 phenotype [38]. Like GMSCs, DPSCs can induce macrophage M2 polarization in vivo, which contributes to the suppression of inflammation, as seen in sciatic nerve injury models, highlighting their significant neurogenic potential [39].

Periodontal Ligament Stem Cells (PDLSCs) as a soft connective tissue, while sharing similarities with DPSCs, exhibit distinct functional characteristics [40]. Ding et al. found the PDLSCs failed to express HLA-II DR and costimulatory molecules and possessed marked immunosuppression via PGE2-induced T-cell anergy [41]. Furthermore, PDLSCs can negatively regulate B cell chemotaxis through PD-L1-mediated contact and inhibit B cell apoptosis via IL-6 [42]. A key comparative advantage of PDLSCs over DPSCs is their superior capacity for periodontal regeneration, effectively forming new bone, cementum, and Sharpey's fibers, whereas DPSCs show limited efficacy in this specific context [43]. Their innate predisposition for oral-craniofacial tissue homeostasis and nerve regeneration makes DT-MSCs irreplaceable “specialist tools” for immunoprogramming in this anatomical domain.

Engineered and emerging platforms: iPSC-MSCs and skin-derived MSCs

Induced pluripotent stem cell-derived MSCs (iPSC-MSCs) address the scalability and standardization crisis. Generated from reprogrammed somatic cells [44], they offer a theoretically unlimited, genetically uniform source for “off-the-shelf” therapies with a low risk of immune rejection, as they can be derived from HLA-matched donors or using gene editing to create universal cell lines [45]. iPSC-MSCs not only exhibit stable immune regulatory capabilities—including the suppression of peripheral immune cell activation and the promotion of Treg differentiation—but also demonstrate tissue repair potential beyond that of traditional MSCs [[46], [47], [48]], as evidenced by their dual ability to modulate inflammation and promote bone regeneration in periodontal defect models [49]. Although epigenetic memory during differentiation may affect functional consistency, targeted reprogramming of specific tissue-derived MSCs (e.g., amniotic MSCs) [50,51]enables the construction of engineered cell platforms that combine high conversion efficiency with tissue-specific therapeutic functions. This technological pathway provides a standardized solution to overcome the donor dependence and expansion limitations of primary MSCs, marking a critical turning point in regenerative medicine toward a scalable and controllable era of “cell-based pharmaceuticals."

Skin-derived MSCs (S-MSCs) offer fascinating biological insights. As front-line defenders, they exhibit dynamic immunomodulation [52], suppressing the differentiation of pro-inflammatory Th17 cells and promoting the polarization of macrophages towards the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. This highlights their potential application in stem cell-based therapies for cardiovascular and inflammatory diseases [53]. However, the immune response to S-MSCs can be complex and context-dependent. For instance, Ke et al. reported that intraperitoneal administration of S-MSCs led to an initial increase in the proinflammatory cytokine TNF-α, which itself can promote Th17 differentiation [54]. This paradox underscores that S-MSCs' function is not merely immunosuppressive but involves dynamic immunomodulation, potentially guiding the immune system towards a reparative outcome. Furthermore, their ability to produce angiogenic factors and modulate the oxidative stress environment is crucial for repairing skin injuries and managing dermatological diseases. Their study enriches the toolkit with cells primed by a pro-inflammatory tissue niche.

The immuno-instructive niche: biomaterial cues as directors of immune cell fate

If stem cells provide dynamic biological software for immunomodulation, then biomaterials constitute the engineered hardware capable of writing precise instructional code into the regenerative niche [62,63]. Moving beyond their traditional role as passive scaffolds, biomaterials are now understood as active, immuno-instructive interfaces whose physicochemical properties directly command host immune responses [64]. This section deconstructs the biochemical and biophysical “language” of biomaterials, translating material properties into specific design principles for immune programming. We propose the concept of “Biomaterial-Based Immuno-Code (BIC)” a design lexicon comprising biochemical and biophysical cues that can be systematically programmed to direct immune cell behavior and polarization. We posit that by rationally tuning this code---from nanoscale surface chemistry to macroscale viscoelasticity---we can predictably steer innate and adaptive immune cells toward a pro-regenerative phenotype, thereby setting the stage for synergistic integration with cellular components (Fig. 3).

Immuno-instructive biomaterial niche: biochemical and biophysical cues for immune cell programming. This schematic illustrates the multi-dimensional language of biomaterials used to direct immune cell fate and create a pro-regenerative microenvironment. The design framework is divided into two core domains: biochemical lexicon and biophysical syntax. Biochemical lexicon focuses on molecular-scale cues: surface chemistry (e.g., hydrophilic or zinc-functionalized surfaces) provides the foundation for bio-recognition and inflammation modulation. Peptide and protein functionalization mimics or engineer's native bioactivity to engage specific receptors and program intracellular signaling pathways. Delivery of soluble immunomodulators via controlled-release systems enables sustained, local reprogramming of the immune microenvironment, with advanced systems featuring environmental responsiveness and logical release gating. Biophysical syntax encompasses physical material properties: matrix mechanics, including stiffness and viscoelasticity, acts as a fate-switching signal, where softer or faster stress-relaxing matrices generally favor an anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotype. Topographical and microstructural landscapes, from surface roughness and micro-grooves to 3D pore architecture, guide immune cell morphology, alignment, and function, influencing the polarization state and cytokine secretion. The integration of these biochemical and biophysical cues enables the rational design of biomaterial platforms that can steer immune responses—from pro-inflammatory to anti-inflammatory—and synergize with cellular therapies for advanced immunoprogramming applications.

Biochemical lexicon: molecular recognition and controlled release

The biochemical interface forms the primary vocabulary for cell-material communication. Strategic design at this level allows for the direct engagement of specific immune signaling pathways.

Surface chemistry: the foundation of bio-recognition. The material's surface is the first point of contact, where protein adsorption dictates subsequent immune cell responses [65]. Chemical modifications are not merely coatings but active instructional signals. For instance, hydrophilic and highly sulfated surfaces (e.g., sulfated hyaluronan coatings) —a key scaffold design parameter defined by functional group type (–OSO3H), high negative charge density, and water contact angle θ < 30°—have been shown to downregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α and IL-1β) in macrophages while upregulating anti-inflammatory IL-10, effectively skewing polarization toward an M2-like state [66,67]. Mechanistically, sulfated hyaluronic acid coatings have been shown to attenuate inflammatory signaling pathways in macrophages by downregulating the activity of transcription factors involved in inflammatory gene expression, including NF-κB, while simultaneously inducing anti-inflammatory proteins [68]. Conversely, specific functional groups or ions (e.g., zinc on nanotubular titanium) —a key scaffold design parameter typically achieved by anodization or hydrothermal treatment, yielding nanotube diameters of 30–80 nm and sustained Zn2+ release rates in the range of ∼1–10 μg/mL over 7–14 days— can be incorporated to actively engage anti-inflammatory pathways: zinc ion-modified surfaces induce GSK-3β phosphorylation and inactivation through sustained activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, reducing downstream inflammatory cytokine transcription [69], demonstrating that surface chemistry is a programmable variable for immune instruction [[70], [71], [72]].

Recent advancements have transformed surface chemistry from a static, one-time instruction into a platform for dynamic and adaptive immune dialogue. A seminal study engineered a titanium implant surface grafted with a thermo-responsive polymer brush, poly (di (ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate). This design leverages the body's inherent thermal energy to trigger a hydrophilic-to-hydrophobic transition at physiological temperatures, enabling on-demand, stimulus-responsive release of the cytokine. This resulted in the intelligent, in-situ switching from pro-inflammatory to anti-inflammatory, showcasing a move from passive instruction to active, temporally controlled immunomodulation [73,74]. Even more sophisticated are bio-orthogonal, click chemistry-based surfaces. Another pioneering approach involved functionalizing a material surface with azide groups. Upon implantation and the onset of inflammation, the elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) at the site trigger a covalent, in-situ “click” conjugation with DBCO-capped anti-inflammatory peptides. This ROS-responsive surface represents a dynamic scaffold design parameter where the key variables are the density of azide groups (typically 102–104 groups/μm2) and the reactivity threshold (responsive to H2O2 concentrations >50 μM). This strategy exemplifies in-situ sensing and adaptive functionalization, representing a prototype for closed-loop immunoprogramming where the material actively responds to and modulates its local microenvironment [75]. Recent studies have confirmed that ROS-responsive hydrogels promote M2 polarization by regulating the Nrf2/NF-κB pathway, while also enhancing osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs and accelerating infected diabetic ulcer healing through activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 axis and suppression of NF-κB signaling [76,77]. Overall, the most advanced surface chemistry strategies now adhere to the “right time, right place, right dose” paradigm. By exploiting intrinsic material properties (e.g., thermoresponse) or micro-environmental triggers (e.g., ROS and pH), these systems deliver precise immunomodulatory cues spatiotemporally, moving beyond blanket immunosuppression towards context-aware regulation [63].

Peptide and protein functionalization: mimicking native bioactivity. Incorporating bioactive sequences from the native extracellular matrix (ECM) allows materials to “speak” language cells inherently understand. The presentation of adhesive motifs like RGD within hydrogels not only supports cell attachment but also suppresses pro-inflammatory macrophage activation (reducing iNOS, TNF-α) and promotes reparative markers (increasing IL-10) [78]. RGD-functionalized surfaces represent a key scaffold design parameter defined by ligand density—typically ranging from 1 to 1000 μM, with densities above ∼200 μM required to effectively support integrin-mediated adhesion and downstream signaling—and spatial presentation mode (homogeneously distributed versus clustered presentation). Traditional RGD peptides activate integrins αvβ3/α5β1, triggering FAK activation and downstream PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK pathways, which collectively generate a baseline anti-inflammatory tendency [79]. Notably, RGD ligand density critically influences YAP nuclear localization: higher density enhances FAK/ROCK activation and YAP nuclear translocation, while ROCK/FAK inhibition reduces cell spreading and YAP nuclear localization [80]. This demonstrates that precise ligand density regulation enables quantitative control over mechanotransduction, linking biochemical presentation to nuclear transcriptional programs. More sophisticated strategies involve presenting endogenous immunomodulatory peptides (e.g., α-MSH), which directly bind to cognate receptors on immune cells like microglia and macrophages to suppress inflammatory cascades, leveraging nature's own regulatory machinery [81].

The frontier has now shifted from mimicking natural sequences to de novo engineering of protein components with enhanced or novel functionalities. Through computational design and directed evolution, researchers have engineered fibronectin type III domain variants obtained through directed evolution selectively activate the integrin β1-associated ILK-PINCH-Parvin complex, specifically enhancing AKT phosphorylation without overactivating ERK. This selective activation drives macrophages toward a pro-regenerative phenotype characterized by high expression of Arginase-1 and VEGF, while avoiding the fibrotic side effects associated with broad ERK activation [82]. Multifunctional chimeric peptides represent another advance: by fusing a macrophage scavenger receptor A-targeting sequence with a phosphatidylserine-mimicking peptide, these constructs activate Rac1-and CDC42-mediated efferocytosis pathways while simultaneously inhibiting TLR4/MyD88 signaling, synergistically inducing STAT6 phosphorylation and NF-κB inactivation [83,84]. These examples demonstrate that the design of peptide-functionalized surfaces can achieve pathway-specific immune programming, moving beyond general adhesion promotion toward targeted molecular instruction.

Delivery of soluble immunomodulators: sustained microenvironment reprogramming. Biomaterials serve as sophisticated reservoirs for the localized and controlled release of soluble immunomodulatory agents, creating a sustained therapeutic microenvironment. The controlled release of anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-4 from scaffolds activates the JAK1/STAT6 pathway [85], while sustained release of IL-10 activates the JAK1/STAT3 pathway, directly driving macrophages toward M2a or M2c phenotypes, respectively [86]. Controlled release systems represent a scaffold design parameter defined by key variables including release duration (days to months), release kinetics (zero-order, first-order, or pulsatile), and stimulus responsiveness (e.g., ROS-, pH-, or enzyme-triggered release). For example, IL-4 release from scaffolds can be sustained for up to 73 days, maintaining therapeutic concentrations in the ng/mL range, with day 53 supernatant still promoting M2 polarization. These JAK-STAT pathways are master regulators of macrophage polarization, and their sustained engagement by scaffold-based delivery produces more durable phenotypic changes than soluble bolus administration. Similarly, the release of small molecule drugs (e.g., dexamethasone) or cytokine antagonists (e.g., IL-1Ra) from macroporous or nanoparticle-based systems allows for the precise dampening of pathological inflammation [[87], [88], [89]]. For instance, sustained release of dexamethasone from scaffolds binds to the glucocorticoid receptor, promoting MKP-1 expression, which dephosphorylates and inactivates p38 MAPK and JNK, potently suppressing inflammatory responses [90,91]. The release kinetics of such small-molecule drugs—typically following first-order or zero-order profiles with release durations from days to weeks—directly influence the duration and intensity of the anti-inflammatory signal. This mechanism is fundamentally different from cytokine-based JAK-STAT activation, yet both can achieve M2-biased outcomes, suggesting that diverse upstream signals can converge on common downstream transcriptional programs.

The next generation of delivery systems aims to become “immune microenvironment-sensing feedback processors [92]." a therapeutic system designed for infected wounds and rheumatoid arthritis utilizes a triple-network hydrogel responsive to both reactive oxygen species (ROS) and pH. This hydrogel can scavenge ROS and regulate the local pH, thereby protecting cells from oxidative stress. Additionally, it promotes macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype via the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways. This leads to a reduction in inflammatory cytokines, exerts anti-inflammatory effects, and contributes to remodeling the inflammatory microenvironment [93]. An even more sophisticated concept is the cytokine “logic-gate” release system. One team developed microparticles co-loaded with growth factors (BMP2, VEGF and TGF-β), whose release is governed by matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) activity. The synergistic release of both cytokines occurs only when high levels of MMP-2 (indicative of active inflammation and tissue remodeling) are present in the lesion. This “AND-gate” logic ensures potent immunomodulation is activated precisely when and where needed, significantly enhancing therapeutic safety and efficacy [94,95]. We believe that the defining features of future delivery systems are “environmental responsiveness” and “logical programmability.” These systems evolve from simple depots into smart constructs that can decode complex biosignals in the local immune milieu (e.g., pH, enzymes, ROS) and execute pre-programmed drug release logic in response, thereby dynamically guiding the immune response through its necessary stages.

Biophysical syntax: mechanics and topography as instructive forces

Physical forces and structural features are potent, yet often underestimated, regulators of immune cell behavior. Emerging evidence demonstrates that immune cells—particularly macrophages—are exquisitely sensitive to the mechanical and topographical language of their extracellular environment. This section examines how mechanical signals (stiffness and viscoelasticity) and structural cues (surface topography and 3D microarchitecture) are sensed and transduced into profound changes in immune cell phenotype, polarization, and function, ultimately shaping the regenerative or inflammatory trajectory of the host response.

Matrix mechanics: stiffness and viscoelasticity as fate-switching signals. Recent studies have provided a detailed account of how tissue-level mechanical signals—particularly stiffness, shear stress, and cyclic stretch—affect monocyte and macrophage activation and their underlying mechanisms [96]. Here, we focus on describing how the mechanical properties of elastic and viscoelastic materials govern macrophage polarization, a pivotal determinant of implant integration and tissue repair outcomes [64,[97], [98], [99]]. A well-established design principle indicates that increasing matrix stiffness (e.g., from ∼2 kPa to >30 kPa in synthetic hydrogels) promotes a pro-inflammatory (M1) macrophage phenotype, characterized by enhanced cell spreading, actin stress fiber formation, and elevated secretion of cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6. In contrast, softer, more compliant matrices tend to favor an anti-inflammatory or regulatory (M2-like) state, facilitating a pro-regenerative microenvironment [[100], [101], [102], [103], [104]]. Matrix stiffness is a key scaffold design parameter—typically expressed as elastic modulus or storage modulus G′, ranging from very soft (∼0.1 kPa, mimicking brain tissue) to very stiff (>100 kPa, mimicking bone), with the immunomodulatory transition zone falling between 1 and 30 kPa.

The mechanistic basis for stiffness sensing involves two major signaling axes operating in parallel. On stiff matrices (>30 kPa), integrin clustering activates FAK, triggering downstream MEK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways, which upregulate MLCK expression and enhance cell contractility [105]. The RhoA/ROCK pathway synergistically stabilizes the actin cytoskeleton through LIMK-mediated inhibition of cofilin. The resulting increase in cytoskeletal tension drives YAP/TAZ nuclear translocation, where they bind TEAD transcription factors to upregulate pro-inflammatory genes (e.g., CTGF, CYR61, TNF-α), promoting M1 polarization [106]. On soft matrices (∼1 kPa), a distinct mechanosensing pathway predominates. Macrophage exhibit sustained, low-frequency Ca2+ oscillations through the Piezo1 channel; these signals activate CaMKII, which phosphorylates CREB to drive M2 marker gene transcription (Arg1, Retnla) [107]. Notably, matrix stiffening increases Piezo1 expression, and Piezo1 activation further promotes YAP activation through a Rac1-dependent mechanism, forming a positive feedback loop that reinforces the pro-inflammatory phenotype on stiff substrates [106]. Thus, the balance between the integrin-FAK-YAP/TAZ axis and the Piezo1-CaMKII-CREB axis determines macrophage fate in response to matrix stiffness.

However, native tissues are predominantly viscoelastic rather than purely elastic, exhibiting time-dependent stress relaxation under deformation [108]. Recent studies have shifted focus toward understanding how viscoelasticity—a property describing a material's ability to dissipate energy over time—modulates cellular behavior. Pioneering work by Mooney and colleagues revealed that matrices with faster stress relaxation rates, even at equivalent stiffness values, significantly enhance stem cell expansion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation [109]. Nevertheless, the impact of viscoelasticity on immune cells remains underexplored. The field is now moving beyond characterizing viscoelasticity's effects to engineering it predictably for immune modulation. A landmark study designed a family of hyaluronic acid hydrogels with independently tunable stiffness and stress relaxation times. They discovered that fast-relaxing viscoelastic gels, regardless of being soft or stiff, consistently shifts macrophage metabolism from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) via VASP/HIF1α signaling, facilitating a reparative phenotype [110]. Matrix viscoelasticity is a scaffold design parameter characterized by stress relaxation half-time (t1/2), ranging from ∼170 s (fast-relaxing, low-molecular-weight alginate) to ∼3300 s (slow-relaxing, high-molecular-weight alginate), reflecting the material's ability to dissipate energy over time through weak bond dissociation and polymer sliding. Additionally, fast relaxation facilitated optimal integrin clustering and YAP/TAZ nuclear translocation—a pathway associated with pro-regenerative signaling—suggesting viscoelasticity can override stiffness as a dominant cue under certain conditions [111].

Our recent investigation compared gelatin-based hydrogels in two distinct structural forms: colloidal (particulate) gels versus monolithic (bulk) gels. These systems exhibited markedly different viscoelastic profiles—colloidal gels demonstrated rapid stress relaxation, whereas bulk gels behaved in a predominantly elastic manner. Importantly, macrophages cultured on viscoelastic colloidal matrices displayed a significantly attenuated inflammatory response, with reduced expression of M1 markers in vitro and diminished fibrotic encapsulation upon subcutaneous implantation in vivo [101]. Although the precise mechanotransduction pathways remain to be fully elucidated, these findings underscore matrix viscoelasticity as a critical, yet often overlooked, design parameter for programming immune-compatible biomaterials and implantable devices.

Topographical and microstructural landscapes: shape-guided immune response. Beyond global matrix mechanics, the local topographical and microstructural features of a biomaterial—including surface roughness, patterned grooves, fiber diameter and orientation, and pore size—provide another layer of biophysical instruction that macrophages can sense and respond to.

Surface topography: Micro- and nano-scale surface features have been shown to direct macrophage polarization independently of substrate stiffness. In general, nano-scale features (e.g., nanotubes, nanopits, or nanoflake arrays with feature sizes <100 nm) promote an M2-like, anti-inflammatory phenotype, while micro-scale features (e.g., micron-sized pillars, grooves, or roughness in the micron range) tend to induce a more pro-inflammatory or pro-fibrotic response [[112], [113], [114], [115]]. For instance, titania nanotube arrays of specific diameters (30-50 nm) significantly reduce TNF-α and IL-1β secretion while increasing IL-10 and TGF-β1 from macrophages, compared to flat or micron-scale topographies [113]. Surface topography is a scaffold design parameter defined by roughness (Ra 0.1–10 μm), groove/ridge arrays (width 0.5–500 μm), nanopillars/nanopores (diameter 20–200 nm), and isotropic versus anisotropic alignment.

The mechanistic basis for topography-driven polarization involves distinct signaling pathways activated by feature size. Nano-scale structures (e.g., nanoflake arrays) promote high expression of integrin β2 and FAK, activating the PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling pathway. This pathway drives macrophages toward an M2 phenotype characterized by high Arginase-1 and VEGF expression, while simultaneously suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine production [116]. In contrast, micro-scale structures activate the ROCK-YAP/TAZ mechanotransduction pathway, which enhances osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells but is associated with a more pro-inflammatory macrophage response. When nano- and micro-structures are present in a shared culture environment, they produce synergistic effects: nano-structures polarize macrophages toward M2 via PI3K-AKT-mTOR, and these M2 macrophages in turn promote osteogenesis of co-cultured MSCs through paracrine signaling.

Fiber diameter in electrospun scaffolds also plays a decisive role in pathway selection. Microfibers (1–50 μm) activate the NF-κB pathway through enhanced integrin clustering and FAK phosphorylation, inducing M1 (pro-inflammatory) macrophage polarization, with cells forming large lamellipodia and exhibiting reduced migration speed. Nanofibers (200–600 nm) activate STAT3/STAT6 pathways by limiting integrin cluster size and FAK activation, inducing M2 (anti-inflammatory) macrophage polarization, with cells elongating and exhibiting increased mesenchymal migration characteristics [117]. Fiber orientation further modulates signaling: aligned fibers upregulate SOX5/9 and RUNX2 expression by guiding the directional alignment of the cytoskeleton, while randomly oriented fibers tend to promote a more astrocytic or fibroblastic phenotype [118].

3D microstructure (porosity & architecture): The three-dimensional pore network of a scaffold dictates cell infiltration, spatial organization, and paracrine signaling gradients [119,120]. While non-porous implants often trigger fibrous encapsulation, interconnected porous architectures facilitate host integration [121]. However, the design is non-linear, for the simultaneous study of multiple parameters, for example, Jiang et al. found that macrophages within smaller and softer pores (diameter of 30 μm) exhibit proinflammatory phenotype, whereas larger and stiffer pores (80 μm) induce an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. Pore architecture is a scaffold design parameter defined by pore size (5–500 μm), porosity (50–95%), and pore interconnectivity, with the optimal immunomodulatory pore size range falling between 40 and 80 μm. Mechanistically, small pores promote M1 polarization via YAP/TAZ cytoplasmic retention and RhoA/Rac1 imbalance-driven NF-κB activation, whereas large pores induce M2 polarization and osteogenesis through FAK/PI3K/AKT/STAT6-mediated YAP nuclear translocation [122]. Here, we noticed that the microstructure of scaffolds can influence the immune response to induce and control tissue regeneration, it can decrease the mechanical stability of the implant. Our research investigated the macrophage polarization and inflammatory response induced by colloidal gels composed of gelatin or GelMA nanoparticles versus bulk GelMA hydrogels with different viscoelastic properties. We demonstrated that hydrogel elasticity plays a more dominant role rather than the structural feature in determining in vitro macrophage-mediated inflammation [101]. Thus, for in situ regeneration of tissue with structural functions, the microstructure should be optimized in addition to mechanical strength and morphology. This interplay underscores that microstructural design cannot be isolated from mechanical context.

The frontier of microstructural design lies in creating dynamic or spatially graded architectures that guide immune responses in time and space. An innovative study used 4D bioprinting system has excellent shape-morphing controllability in mild conditions, reliability and biocompatibility, which could be utilized for the purposes of implantation [123,124]. This demonstrates that a single, evolving microstructure can actively orchestrate the temporal sequence of the immune response. Topography and microstructure function as spatial constraints that direct cellular shape and congregation, which in turn govern fate decisions through mechano-metabolic coupling. The future of physical immuno-programming lies in moving from static to temporally evolving (4D) architectures that can match and guide the dynamic phases of the immune-healing cascade.

Towards rational design: integrating biochemical and biophysical cues

The preceding subsections delineate discrete vocabulary elements. However, the true power of the biomaterial code lies in their integrated and hierarchical application. Biochemical and biophysical cues do not act in isolation; they engage in crosstalk. For example, the presence of an RGD peptide can alter how a cell perceives substrate stiffness via integrin-mediated adhesions. Similarly, a sustained release of IL-4 (biochemical) from a viscoelastic hydrogel (biophysical) may produce a synergistic, more durable M2 polarization than either cue alone. This crosstalk can exhibit complex, non-linear logic. A seminal study systematically decoupled these variables using a library of hydrogels with orthogonal control over stiffness, immobilized RGD density, and soluble TGF-β1 presentation. They discovered a hierarchical gating effect: the pro-regenerative effect of soluble TGF-β1 was entirely contingent on the presence of a minimal RGD density for integrin engagement. Furthermore, this biochemical signal pair (RGD + TGF-β1) could only reprogram macrophages on soft substrates, failing on stiff ones [125]. This establishes a clear design rule: biochemical signals are gated by adhesive ligand presentation, which in turn is modulated by the underlying mechanics.

The mechanosensing pathways described above—Piezo1-Ca2+-CREB (soft matrix), VASP/HIF1α-OXPHOS (fast relaxation), and RhoA/ROCK-YAP/TAZ (stiff matrix)—have been well characterized in macrophage or MSC monocultures. However, in integrated constructs where both cell types co-exist, three interaction modes emerge. First, bidirectional paracrine crosstalk: soft matrices simultaneously promote MSC secretion of IDO1/PGE2 via YAP/TAZ [126] and macrophage M2 polarization via Piezo1, creating synergistic anti-inflammatory amplification. Second, metabolic coupling: fast-relaxing matrices shift macrophage metabolism to OXPHOS [110], while α-KG co-delivery preserves MSC OXPHOS, suggesting shared metabolic dialogue. Third, temporal asymmetry: macrophages respond to stiffness within hours, while MSCs exhibit slower but persistent epigenetic remodeling (“mechanical memory”) [127]. Fourth, pathway hierarchy: fast stress relaxation can override stiffness as the dominant cue, inducing M2 polarization regardless of matrix stiffness [110]. These emergent interactions underscore that co-culture responses cannot be predicted from monoculture studies alone, highlighting the need for integrated models in immunoprogramming design.

Here, we also define BIC by three operational components: inputs (designable material properties), mechano-biochemical transduction pathways, and measurable outputs. Inputs include: surface chemistry; matrix stiffness (0.1–100 kPa) [100,101]; viscoelasticity (stress relaxation t1/2 170–3300 s)109; topography (fiber diameter, pore size) [116]; ligand density [80]; and degradability. Mechano-biochemical pathways include: integrin-FAK-YAP/TAZ [106]; Piezo1-Ca2+-NF-κB; RhoA/ROCK; and stiffness-dependent chromatin remodeling [127]. Measurable outputs include: M1/M2 polarization ratio (iNOS/Arg-1, CD86/CD206) [101]; YAP/TAZ nuclear localization; NF-κB activation; and stress fiber formation.

Programming principles of integrated construction: from signal integration to emergent immunoregulation

The convergence of stem cells and biomaterials transcends simple co-delivery; it establishes a synergistic interface where material cues instruct cellular behavior, and primed cells in turn orchestrate a refined immune response (Fig. 4). This section shifts from cataloguing individual components to articulating the core programming principles that govern this integration. We propose that the emergent therapeutic potency of integrated constructs arises from the orchestrated activation of four cardinal regulatory axes: epigenetic reprogramming, metabolic coupling, biochemical co-signaling. Together, these principles convert passive constructs into active immunoregulatory devices capable of navigating the dynamic landscape of tissue repair.

Epigenetic priming: physical signal-mediated persistent cellular reprogramming

Biomaterial scaffolds serve not only as structural support but as epigenetic editors that durably alter stem cell transcriptional programs. The physical properties of biomaterials, including three-dimensional architecture, mechanical stiffness, and surface topography—can serve as potent epigenetic editing signals and are transduced into chromatin remodeling events, effectively “hard-coding” a pro-regenerative phenotype.

Epigenetic effects of 3D structure and mechanical stiffness: Substrate stiffness operates as a tunable dial for immunomodulatory potency. Encapsulation of MSCs within soft (∼2–5 kPa), compliant hydrogels upregulate immunosuppressive mediators such as IDO1 and PGE2 via transcriptional co-activators like YAP/TAZ, regulating the chromatin accessibility of genes associated with immunomodulation (e.g., PTGS2, TNFAIP6) [126,[128], [129], [130], [131]]. Recent evidence reveals that these mechanical signals induce lasting epigenetic imprints: culturing MSCs on soft, nano-grooved substrates within confined microwells (mimicking a regenerative niche) specifically opens chromatin regions near pro-immunomodulatory genes while closing pro-fibrotic gene loci through histone modifications (e.g., accumulation of H3K27me3) [127,[132], [133], [134]].

The persistence of stiffness-induced epigenetic memory is finely modulated by stimulation intensity and duration. MSCs briefly cultured on soft matrices (∼1 kPa) for 3 days retain multilineage differentiation potential and delay replicative senescence, whereas prolonged passaging on stiff matrices leads to irreversible epigenetic drift and irreversible loss of therapeutic potency. A key finding concerns the kinetic asymmetry of memory writing and erasure: memory encoding typically requires several days of sustained stimulation, while memory erasure may require substantially longer recovery periods and, under certain conditions of excessive or prolonged stimulation, may become irreversible [135]. This kinetic asymmetry has important clinical implications: scaffold-mediated priming conditions must be precisely optimized to ensure that MSCs acquire sufficiently robust and durable immunomodulatory functions in vitro, while avoiding over-programming that could lead to irreversible phenotypic stiffening or functional drift. Furthermore, dynamic material systems—such as photoresponsive hydrogels with reversible stiffness switchin—enable active modulation of the mechanical microenvironment, allowing precise control over the formation and erasure of stiffness-induced epigenetic memory in vitro. When exposed to fluctuating substrate stiffness, early passage MSCs demonstrate rapid adaptive responses, while late passage MSCs exhibit impaired mechanosensitivity, underscoring the importance of timing in clinical manufacturing protocols [8,136].

Topography and 3D microarchitecture as epigenetic cues: Beyond stiffness, the nano- and micro-scale architecture of a scaffold surface provides critical epigenetic structural instructions. The epigenetic imprint induced by topographical features demonstrates remarkable long-term stability. MSCs cultured on nanostructured surfaces (e.g., titania nanotubes, hybrid micro-whiskers), aligned fibrous networks, or roughened topographies consistently exhibit enhanced immunomodulatory function compared to those on flat or smooth controls. These features enhance integrin clustering, cytoskeletal organization, and activation of Hippo pathways, leading to increased secretion of PGE2, VEGF, and other trophic factors for at least 7-14 days after removal from the topographical cue, indicating that physical topography can establish durable functional programming [59,[137], [138], [139], [140], [141], [142]]. However, this memory can be actively modulated by the host microenvironment upon transplantation. In fibrotic diseases characterized by stiff, type I collagen-rich scar tissue, implanted MSCs may be reprogrammed toward a myofibroblast phenotype—an irreversible fate transition that severely compromises MSC therapeutic efficacy [143]. These findings highlight that while topographical priming confers durable benefits, the post-transplantation microenvironment can either preserve, enhance, or override the primed state. Therefore, understanding how the host pathological microenvironment interacts with MSC epigenetic memory is a key prerequisite for optimizing scaffold design and transplantation strategies. The emerging use of dynamic or 4D bioprinted architectures that evolve over time offers a promising strategy to actively guide MSCs through sequential therapeutic phases, potentially preventing undesirable reprogramming by the diseased host niche.

The microscale porosity of a 3D scaffold dictates spatial confinement and mass transport, which directly modulates MSC-immune interactions. MSCs in a nanoscale porous hydrogel/scaffold, in contrast to a microscale porous scaffold, significantly suppressed the diffusion of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-17 and TNF-a into the biomaterial in vitro, and reduced the infiltration of CD3+, CD4+, and CD4+CD17+ T lymphocytes in immunocompetent mice [144]. Similarly, Ansari et al. demonstrated that MSCs laden alginate hydrogels with smaller pores and lower porosity, but higher elasticity, significantly suppressed the infiltration of proinflammatory cytokines and the activation of NF-kB signaling [129]. Yuan et al. explored the influence of the structures of collagen scaffolds (hydrogels, sponge, and membrane) on immunological properties of the seeded MSCs. The results showed that MSCs encapsulated in hydrogels with smaller pore sizes and lower porosity can more effectively sequester their paracrine signals, creating a concentrated anti-inflammatory niche that suppresses the infiltration and activation of pro-inflammatory T lymphocytes. In fact, micro-structure, stiffness, and surface chemistry may have a synergistic effect on MSCs. scaffolds with different biophysical and biochemical properties had different effects on the immunomodulatory function of MSCs [145]. This phenomenon suggests that biomaterial-imposed physical cues induce stable epigenetic remodeling in stem cells, converting them into sustained producers of immunoregulatory factors.

Epigenetic regulation and DNA repair in MSC programming. The functional programming of MSCs is also governed by a broader epigenetic network integrating DNA methylation, RNA modifications, and DNA repair mechanisms DNA methylation dynamics, catalyzed by DNMTs and reversed by TET enzymes, regulate MSC differentiation and immunomodulatory function—MSCs induce regulatory T cells through epigenetic conversion, with the DNA methylation profile of MSC-induced Treg-like cells closely resembling that of natural Tregs [61]. N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification further modulates MSC immunomodulation by regulating RNA stability through YTHDF2 [146]. Critically, the maintenance of genomic integrity during MSC expansion depends on PARP2-mediated DNA repair, which catalyzes PARylation to recruit repair factors to DNA damage sites. PARP2 function is regulated by helical domain conformational dynamics and post-translational phosphorylation modifications [147], and its expression is closely linked to MSC senescence [148,149]. Notably, epigenetic and DNA repair systems intersect with mechanotransduction: treatment with the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor 5-azacytidine resets YAP localization, partially alleviating mechanical memory in MSCs expanded on stiff substrates [150]. Together, these mechanisms ensure that MSCs acquire stable functional programming while maintaining genomic integrity during scaffold-mediated priming and large-scale manufacturing.

Metabolic coupling: energetic synchrony between scaffold and cell

The inflammatory microenvironment is metabolically hostile characterized by hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and elevated oxidative stress, which collectively drive stem cell exhaustion and loss of function. This bioenergetic crisis manifests as a shift from efficient oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to compensatory glycolysis, impairing the cells' ability to execute immunomodulatory and reparative functions. Integrated stem cell–biomaterial constructs offer a sophisticated strategy to counteract this decline through metabolic coupling—a bidirectional synergy in which the scaffold serves not only as a structural support but also as a dynamic metabolic interface that tunes cellular energetics toward a sustained, pro-regenerative state.

Scaffold as a metabolic reservoir: Beyond transient metabolic support, scaffold-mediated metabolic coupling can establish a durable “metabolic memory” in MSCs. Pioneering work demonstrates that co-delivery of MSCs with α-ketoglutarate (α-KG)—a key metabolite in the TCA cycle—within a hydrogel scaffold preserves mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) under inflammatory conditions. In contrast, MSCs alone shift toward glycolysis and lose immunomodulatory capacity. This metabolic memory operates in synergy with epigenetic remodeling—YAP/TAZ, the central integrators of mechanotransduction, directly influence mitochondrial biogenesis and functional status, creating a self-reinforcing loop that ensures long-term stability of the primed state [151]. However, this metabolically primed state is not irreversible. It can be actively reset through targeted interventions. This metabolic bolstering not only enhances cell survival but also amplifies paracrine secretion, resulting in superior bone regeneration outcomes [152]. Beyond simple metabolite delivery, advanced material systems can sequentially or selectively release metabolic modulators in response to microenvironmental cues. For instance, ROS-scavenging hydrogels, such as the supramolecular polyvinyl alcohol-gallic acid (PVA-GA) system, restore mitochondrial function through the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, reducing intracellular ROS levels, diminishing mitochondrial damage, and resetting MSCs from a state of metabolic exhaustion to a healthier functional status [153,154]. This demonstrates that metabolic priming is both durable and tunable, offering a strategic dimension for modulating MSC programming stability. From a manufacturing perspective, the durability of metabolic memory (typically 7-14 days after priming) provides a sufficient window for scale-up production and quality control testing, while its reversibility ensures that any unintended off-target effects could potentially be corrected through metabolic resetting strategies.

Biochemical co-signaling: ligand presentation and controlled release logic

Biochemical functionalization is the most direct means of conferring active signaling capabilities to biomaterials. By integrating specific biomolecules (e.g., proteins, peptides, cytokines) into the material, precise and cooperative regulation of stem cells and host immune cells can be achieved. This process involves not only simple molecular delivery but also systematic design across two levels: ligand engineering and soluble factor integration.

Immobilized ligand engineering. Immobilized bioactive molecules do not merely provide cell adhesion sites; they represent a crucial means of achieving structural signal programming through sustained, localized signal stimulation. For instance, the incorporation of high-molecular-weight hyaluronic acid (HA) into a matrix alongside MSCs can synergistically modulate lymphocyte phenotypes, significantly increasing the ratio of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 to the pro-inflammatory cytokine IFN-γ, thereby enhancing the local immune-tolerant microenvironment [155]. Similarly, compared to short peptides, immobilizing full-length N-cadherin domains can more effectively activate endogenous signaling pathways within MSCs, significantly upregulating the secretion of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), promoting angiogenesis and tissue repair [156,157]. These examples demonstrate that the material can act as a “co-therapist,” engaging complementary receptor pathways to achieve a superior immunoregulatory outcome.

Soluble factor integration: creating a pro-regenerative feedback loop. The most dynamic level of synergy involves the scaffold's-controlled release of bioactive molecules that directly prime the MSCs. As previously discussed, scaffolds releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as: IL-1, IL-6, IL-11, IL-18 and TNF-α) have been shown to be intricately linked with the pathophysiology of several disease states such as osteoarthritis [158]. However, prolonged exposure to an environment rich in pro-inflammatory mediators leads to the formation of fibrotic scars, granulation tissue and increased formation of type I and III collagen [64]. Therefore, reasonable modulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines offers a new strategy that can further enhance tissue repair. Scaffolds releasing anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-4, IL-10) or growth factors (e.g., IGF-1) create a localized microenvironment that pre-conditions MSCs, enhancing their survival, homing potential, and immunomodulatory efficacy upon implantation [159,160]. This establishes a powerful feed-forward loop: the scaffold primes the MSCs, and these enhanced MSCs then more effectively modulate the local immune landscape, which is itself being shaped by the scaffold's sustained release profile. This recursive interplay is the hallmark of a truly integrated and self-reinforcing immunoprogramming system.

The frontier now involves designing scaffolds that deliver metabolic primers to fundamentally re-energize MSCs. A seminal study engineered a scaffold that co-delivered MSCs with sustained release of alpha-ketoglutarate (α-KG), a key metabolite in the mitochondrial TCA cycle. In the harsh, metabolically demanding inflammatory microenvironment, control MSCs underwent metabolic exhaustion, shifting to glycolysis and losing immunomodulatory function. In contrast, α-KG-primed MSCs maintained oxidative phosphorylation, increasing their mitochondrial membrane potential. This metabolic bolstering translated into dramatically improved outcomes in bone regeneration, highlighting that supporting MSC metabolism is a potent strategy to enhance their resilience and potency [152]. Here, we can know that beyond receptor-level signaling, the most profound biochemical partnership involves metabolic co-regulation. Scaffolds that provide metabolic substrates or modulate nutrient sensing pathways can prevent MSC functional exhaustion in hostile disease microenvironments, ensuring they retain their programmed immunoregulatory capacity.

Synthesis: principles for designing the synergistic interface

The evidence consolidates into key principles for engineering synergistic immunomodulation: 1) Dimensionality is activating: 3D culture is a non-negotiable prerequisite for priming therapeutic MSC phenotypes. 2) Mechanics are instructive: stiffness, topography, and microstructure must be co-designed as a unified mechanical instruction set to tune MSC immunopotency. 3) Biochemistry is collaborative: the scaffold should be functionalized not merely for cell adhesion, but for cooperative signaling and sustained molecular crosstalk. 4) Integration is emergent: The therapeutic outcome of the integrated construct is greater than the sum of its parts, governed by bidirectional feedback between cell and material. Building on the latest research, we must now add two higher-order principles: 5) Temporality is instructional: Dynamic materials that evolve their properties can guide MSCs through sequential therapeutic phases. 6) Memory is programmable: The combination of physical and biochemical cues can be designed to impose a stable epigenetic and metabolic imprint on MSCs, creating long-lasting “educated” cell phenotypes that function as pre-programmed living medicines. This expanded framework moves beyond viewing scaffolds as mere carriers to recognizing them as programmable cellular manufacturing platforms that can reliably produce therapeutic cell states with defined immune functions.

By adhering to these principles, the field can move beyond combinatorial trial-and-error toward the rational design of intelligent, immuno-instructive niches capable of navigating the complex pathophysiology of tissue repair, as explored in the following tissue-specific applications.

Myocardial repair: resolving chronic inflammation

The limited regenerative capacity of the adult mammalian heart renders susceptible to adverse remodeling following injury. In recent years, significant progress has been made in medical and surgical treatments for myocardial repair. Implantation of MSCs-based therapeutics into diseased hearts has been shown to improve cardiac function and reduce scar size. Furthermore, the development of advanced hydrogel delivery systems has enhanced stem cell retention and targeted delivery. For example, Rufaihah et al. designed a vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-mimetic peptide-functionalized alginate hydrogel to encapsulate MSCs, which significantly increased vascular density in the infarct zone and improved cardiac function in a murine model [161]. These therapeutic effects are primarily mediated through the modulation of endogenous immune repair mechanisms. Compared to MSCs cultured in two-dimensional systems, which often exhibit limited repair potential and develop fibrogenic characteristics over time, three-dimensional collagen scaffolds can enhance the cardioprotective effects of MSCs by modifying their immunomodulatory properties [162,163]. Recent studies further indicate that shifting macrophage phenotypes from pro-inflammatory M1 to reparative M2 is a key immunoregulatory strategy for promoting myocardial repair. For instance, human cardiac adipose tissue-derived MSCs can polarize human macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory, reparative phenotype, offering new insights into the pathogenesis and treatment of various myocardial injuries [[164], [165], [166]]. The central role of macrophages in this process has been validated: depletion of macrophages nearly abolishes the therapeutic benefits of MSCs.

To enhance this macrophage-driven repair process, current research focuses on optimizing the secretory function of MSCs. For example, modulating the expression of immunoregulatory genes such as monocyte chemotactic protein-induced protein (MCPIP) can further amplify their ability to promote angiogenesis and suppress maladaptive inflammation, thereby achieving the dual goals of reducing scar size and improving cardiac function [167]. However, the most cutting-edge strategies are shifting from “replacement” to “in situ programming.” Instead of delivering MSCs directly, researchers designed an intelligent, injectable reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive hydrogel encapsulating mannose-functionalized, danshensu sodium capable of reprogramming macrophage metabolism. In the myocardial infarction zone, high levels of ROS triggered hydrogel degradation and drug release, selectively targeting macrophages infiltrating the infarct area to a reparative phenotype with potent angiogenic and anti-inflammatory capabilities, significantly restoring cardiac function, outperforming direct MSC delivery. This study demonstrates that in situ reprogramming of endogenous immune cells by delivering programming instructions via materials can circumvent many limitations of stem cell transplantation, enabling more precise therapy [168].

However, the translation of MSC-based therapies for myocardial repair faces several hurdles. First, the inflammatory microenvironment in the infarcted heart—characterized by high levels of IFN-γ and ROS—can significantly increase MSC immunogenicity by upregulating MHC class I/II expression, potentially triggering immune rejection [169]. Second, the scalability of producing sufficient clinical-grade MSCs under cGMP conditions remains challenging, with current automated platforms (e.g., Quantum®, CliniMACS Prodigy®) still costly and requiring specialized expertise [170]. Third, regulatory approval for cardiac MSC products has proven difficult, as illustrated by the three submission cycles required for FDA approval of Ryoncil, primarily due to concerns over batch-to-batch consistency and potency assay validity. Addressing these challenges will require integrated strategies combining immune protection (e.g., alginate encapsulation), scalable manufacturing, and rigorous quality control.

Skin wound healing: resolving inflammation in the chronic wound niche

Chronic wounds, such as diabetic ulcers, are often trapped in a state of persistent inflammation that hinders progression through normal healing stages. particularly when delivered via engineered biomaterial matrices, can break this cycle by suppressing the pro-inflammatory microenvironment and guiding an orderly transition to the proliferative phase [[171], [172], [173]]. Studies have shown that secretory factors from adipose-derived MSCs cultured on dopamine-modified bioceramic scaffolds can accelerate wound closure, enhance angiogenesis, and promote macrophage switching toward a reparative phenotype in diabetic animal models [59,174]. Similarly, systemically administered human umbilical cord MSCs can home to wound sites and improve healing through immunomodulatory mechanisms [137]. Combinatorial strategies, such as co-delivering MSCs with antioxidant agents like epigallocatechin gallate, further highlight the therapeutic potential of precisely regulating the wound immune microenvironment to fully harness the regenerative capacity of stem cells.

In recent years, immunomodulatory wound healing strategies have continued to evolve. MSC-derived exosomes have gained widespread attention as an efficient cell-free therapeutic approach. These nanovesicles, carrying miRNAs and proteins, clikevely induce M2 macrophage polarization, promote angiogenesis, and accelerate re-epithelialization, demonstrating repair effects similar to those of their parent cells in diabetic wound models [175]. Beyond bioactive components, the design of biomaterial scaffolds themselves increasingly emphasizes active immune regulation. For example, engineered hydrogels capable of specifically scavenging reactive oxygen species in chronic wounds can significantly reduce local inflammation and drive the wound into the proliferative phase, thereby providing a favorable microenvironment for stem cell function and tissue repair [176]. Additionally, the current frontier lies in creating smart material systems that dynamically respond to changes in the wound microenvironment. A hydrogel integrates a piezoelectric nanogenerator that converts mechanical stress at the wound site into microcurrent, providing local piezoelectric stimulation to the wound bed evenly and symmetrically in real time. Such an effect significantly promotes re-epithelialization and collagen deposition and increases angiogenesis and secretion of growth factors in wound tissue [177,178]. This system, integrating “active recruitment” and “in situ empowerment,” achieved nearly twice the healing speed of static hydrogels or single-factor release in a diabetic mouse model, representing the direction of next-generation active smart wound dressings.

Despite these advances, clinical translation of MSC-based wound healing therapies confronts distinct challenges. The chronic wound microenvironment—with persistent inflammation, hypoxia, and protease activity—not only impairs endogenous healing but also accelerates the clearance of transplanted MSCs, limiting their therapeutic window [179,180]. Furthermore, regulatory barriers for combination products (MSCs + scaffold) are particularly stringent for wound care applications, as the scaffold material itself must demonstrate biocompatibility and controlled degradation profiles while the cellular component requires independent potency validation [181]. Scalability is also a concern: the fabrication of electrospun scaffolds with precisely controlled fiber diameters (200-600 nm for M2 polarization) must be integrated with cell expansion workflows in a cGMP-compliant manner, a process that current manufacturing platforms are not yet optimized to handle [182]. Future solutions may involve off-the-shelf, cryopreserved MSC-scaffold products, but the impact of freeze-thaw cycles on scaffold structural integrity and cell viability requires thorough validation [183].

Liver regeneration: rebalancing immunity in a metabolic organ

While the liver possesses a remarkable regenerative capacity, this ability can become overwhelmed under chronic injury, leading to fibrosis and functional failure. MSC-based therapies aim to reconstitute the dysregulated hepatic immune microenvironment, suppress the inflammatory cascade that drives hepatocyte apoptosis and collagen deposition [184,185], and thereby restore hepatic homeostasis. In models of chemically-induced and immune-mediated liver injury, MSCs exert protective effects through multiple pathways: downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway, and upregulating protective mediators including heme oxygenase-1 [186], thereby mitigating liver damage and promoting repair. Recent studies have further revealed that MSCs can achieve deep immunomodulation by reshaping the hepatic immune landscape, particularly through regulating macrophage phenotypic switching. RNA-seq studies demonstrate that MSC infusion significantly enriches the subset of reparative TREM2+ macrophages in the liver while reducing inflammatory CD44+ monocyte-derived cells [187]. This shift is closely associated with the upregulation of mitophagy-related genes and the suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome activity. Such “reeducation” of macrophages, along with a tendency toward M2 polarization and elevated serum IL-10 levels, effectively inhibits apoptosis and promotes the transition of the injured liver toward a regenerative microenvironment [188].

Moreover, MSCs can systemically modulate adaptive immune responses and correct imbalances in T-cell subsets. Preclinical and clinical studies have shown that in patients with hepatitis B-associated cirrhosis, MSC treatment upregulates regulatory T cells and restores the Treg/Th17 ratio, further stabilizing immune homeostasis [189]. Notably, strategies such as chemical preconditioning, hypoxia, or inflammatory cytokine priming can functionally enhance MSCs, significantly improving their in vivo homing capacity, survival, and immunomodulatory efficacy, offering optimized approaches for cell-based therapy in end-stage liver disease [187,190].

The liver is a highly immunotolerant organ, yet repeated administration of allogeneic MSCs can induce donor-specific antibody formation, potentially compromising long-term efficacy and safety. Bezstarosti et al. reported that although the incidence of DSA formation was limited (3 out of 20 patients), the strategy of avoiding HLA mismatches at the split antigen level is insufficient to prevent mismatches at the amino acid level [191]. This finding has direct implications for MSC therapy in liver diseases, where repeated dosing may be required due to the rapid clearance of MSCs by the hepatic reticuloendothelial system. From a regulatory perspective, the lack of a clear consensus definition of MSCs and the inherent heterogeneity of adherent MSC populations (comprising subclones with varying potencies) make batch-to-batch consistency a persistent challenge [192]. Scalability is further complicated by the need for large-scale hypoxic culture systems that maintain MSC functionality while achieving clinically relevant cell numbers. Addressing these challenges will require collaborative efforts to establish standardized potency assays and to develop immune-evasive MSC lines through gene editing or surface engineering.

Osteochondral regeneration: fusing immunity with skeletal repair

Osteochondral regeneration represents a profound integration of the immune system and skeletal repair mechanisms. Articular cartilage possesses limited intrinsic regenerative capacity due to the low proliferative potential of resident chondrocytes, making its functional restoration a significant challenge in orthopedics. Successful regeneration requires not only a deep understanding of the underlying biology but also synergistic strategies from materials science and immuno-engineering to precisely modulate the local immune microenvironment and guide the formation of functional tissue. During the initial phases of the bone repair cascade, the immune response plays a pivotal regulatory role. The inflammation triggered by material implantation or tissue injury directly influences the recruitment and fate determination of mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs). For instance, Ma et al. demonstrated that implanting hydrophilic, nanostructured titanium dioxide (TiO2) surfaces significantly enhanced peri-implant bone formation by mitigating excessive inflammatory cell responses [193]. This finding suggests that proactively steering the early immune reaction through material surface engineering is an effective strategy to direct subsequent osteogenic differentiation.

MSCs serve not only as a primary cellular source for regeneration but also as crucial modulators of the immune microenvironment. They exhibit homing potential to injury sites and, through the secretion of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins and chemokines, recruit and regulate immune cells such as macrophages, thereby facilitating the mineralization process [194]. Notably, the immunomodulatory function of MSCs is characterized by a “license-by-inflammation” principle: their immunosuppressive capacity is potently activated within a microenvironment rich in pro-inflammatory cytokines like IFN-γ, TNF-α, or IL-1α/β. Research by Ren et al. elucidated that such cytokines induce the expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) in MSCs, leading to the production of nitric oxide (NO), which suppresses local T-cell responses [195]. This reveals a dynamic crosstalk between inflammatory signals and stem cell function, forming the theoretical basis for immunomodulatory tissue regeneration. Building on this principle, guiding immune cells toward a pro-repair phenotype through tissue engineering strategies has become key to enhancing regenerative outcomes. For example, MSC-biomaterial constructs can upregulate the expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in macrophages via iNOS- and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2)-dependent pathways, exerting positive immunomodulatory effects during fracture healing [196]. Furthermore, studies indicate that MSC-based constructs can promote macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, attenuating scaffold-induced inflammation and enhancing cartilage tissue regeneration [197].

The physical properties of biomaterials, particularly their mechanical characteristics, are central to designing immunomodulatory scaffolds. Recent research emphasizes that the mechanical stiffness and extracellular matrix viscoelasticity of scaffolds, such as hydrogels, independently and significantly influence immune cell behavior and stem cell differentiation. For instance, hydrogels with stiffness mimicking native cartilage can promote an M2-like macrophage phenotype through mechanotransduction pathways, thereby creating a favorable microenvironment for robust chondrogenic differentiation and matrix deposition by MSCs. More profound insights show that tuning only the local mechanical compliance of hydrogel networks—without altering the bulk stiffness—is sufficient to significantly accelerate MSC chondrogenesis by enhancing cytoskeletal organization and the expression of the mechanosensitive channel TRPV4 [198,199]. This offers a novel perspective for achieving precise immuno-skeletal synergistic regeneration through rational material mechanics’ design. Such immunomodulatory strategies have demonstrated translational potential in various bone defect models. Both bone marrow-derived and adipose tissue-derived MSCs, when loaded onto appropriate scaffolds, have been successfully applied in models of craniomaxillofacial defects, critical-sized defects, and hard palate reconstruction [[200], [201], [202]]. Furthermore, in periodontal regeneration, Duan et al. showed that combining induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) with a scaffold containing enamel matrix derivatives significantly promoted the regeneration of periodontal ligament, alveolar bone, and cementum [143], further validating the efficacy of the tripartite synergistic strategy involving stem cells, biomaterials, and the immune microenvironment [203].

Osteochondral regeneration presents unique translational challenges due to the weight-bearing nature of the target tissue and the stringent requirements for scaffold mechanical properties. Immune rejection risks are compounded by the fact that many osteochondral defect patients are elderly or have comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis) that alter both MSC functionality and immune status. Donor age negatively affects the immunoregulatory properties of MSCs, with cells from older donors exhibiting reduced immunosuppressive capacity and increased senescence [58]. Scalability for osteochondral applications is particularly demanding, as the required cell numbers for large joint defects (e.g., knee) are substantially higher than for other tissues, and the scaffold fabrication processes—such as 3D printing or freeze-drying—must achieve precise pore architectures while maintaining mechanical integrity under load. Regulatory approval for osteochondral products is further complicated by the dual classification of scaffolds (as medical devices) and MSCs (as cell therapies), requiring joint review by multiple regulatory centers. Emerging solutions include the development of standardized 3P quality control protocols (covering Process, Product, and Patient dimensions) and the use of bioreactor-based manufacturing systems that integrate scaffold seeding and dynamic culture in a closed, automated platform [204].

Conclusions and outlook: engineering the next generation of immuno-instructive therapeutics

This review has charted the transition of regenerative medicine from a cell-centric to an immune-centric paradigm, where the deliberate orchestration of the host immune response emerges as the cornerstone of therapeutic success. We have dissected how the source-specific immunomodulatory signatures of stem cells and the instructive physicochemical language of biomaterials can be harnessed individually and, more powerfully, in synergistic combination. Biomaterial concept of the stem cell–biomaterial construct as a dynamic ‘immuno-instructive niche’ that actively programs the wound microenvironment toward repair represents a fundamental shift in our approach to tissue engineering.

Synthesizing principles and confronting complexity

The evidence consolidated herein establishes several foundational principles. First, the immunomodulatory function of MSCs is not a fixed attribute but a plastic property exquisitely sensitive to microenvironmental cues, necessitating careful source selection and in vitro priming. Second, biomaterials are potent immunomodulators in their own right; their physical and chemical properties can be designed to evoke predictable immune responses, moving from being biologically inert to immunologically active. Third, and most critically, the combination of stem cells and biomaterials creates a therapeutic synergy where the whole is greater than the sum of its parts. This synergy is rooted in bidirectional crosstalk: the material educates the cell, and the educated cell, in turn, directs a more robust and precise immune-mediated repair. However, this complexity introduces challenges, such as donor variability and the dynamic, non-linear nature of in vivo immune responses, which demand a more sophisticated, systems-level understanding.

The core design philosophy of SIIS—actively programming the local immune microenvironment through the integration of biomaterial physicochemical signals and MSC immunomodulatory capacity—exhibits distinctive features compared to conventional approaches. Conventional methods focus on direct structural filling or cell replacement, often suffering from passive immunocompatibility and limited tissue integration. In contrast, SIIS employs active immunomodulation to establish a pro-regenerative microenvironment before guiding endogenous repair, thereby promoting biological integration and shortening the inflammatory phase. As clinical evidence demonstrates, the therapeutic advantage of MSC-based therapies in myocardial infarction and diabetic ulcers derives from immune modulation rather than direct differentiation, confirming the SIIS “immune–regeneration coupling” mechanism. Notably, the comparable efficacy of autologous and allogeneic MSCs in heart failure therapy supports the feasibility of allogeneic “off-the-shelf” SIIS products, which promise to overcome the scalability and consistency bottlenecks that plague autologous cell therapies. Navigating the translational pathway: safety and scalability

The translational journey of these advanced therapies is fraught with specific hurdles. While allogeneic MSC therapies offer an “off-the-shelf” advantage, concerns regarding donor-associated heterogeneity, accelerated infectious risk, and the potential for supporting aberrant tissue growth (e.g., in pre-malignant niches) must be rigorously addressed through improved screening and manufacturing controls [206,207]. The inverse correlation between donor age and MSC immunopotency further complicates clinical translation, highlighting the need for strategies to rejuvenate or enhance the function of cells from older donors. These challenges underscore that the therapeutic window of MSC-based therapies must be precisely defined, balancing potent immunomodulation with long-term safety [58]. Beyond safety, scalability demands innovation in manufacturing. Microfluidic-based bioreactors that simulate the mechanical and biochemical niche may enable mass production of functional MSCs with consistent potency. Additionally, 3D bioprinting allows spatial patterning of different CSIMs within a single construct, potentially enabling sequential immunomodulation—first suppressing acute inflammation (via high-IDO MSCs), then promoting regeneration (via high-VEGF MSCs).

Recent clinical trials have provided direct evidence for the therapeutic potential of MSC-based immunomodulation across multiple indications. In myocardial repair, the PREVENT-TAHA8 phase III trial (420 patients) demonstrated that allogeneic Wharton's jelly-derived MSC infusion significantly reduced heart failure incidence (HR 0.43, P = 0.024) and hospital readmission (HR 0.22, P = 0.015), with a meta-analysis of 13 RCTs (1184 patients) confirming comparable efficacy of autologous and allogeneic MSCs in improving LVEF [208,209]. In chronic wound healing, a phase I/II trial showed complete closure of refractory diabetic foot ulcers within 4.2 weeks using umbilical cord MSC derivatives demonstrated that MSC-derived exosomes achieved complete healing in 62% of patients with an average healing time of 6 weeks versus 20 weeks in controls [210,211]. In end-stage liver disease, a phase II trial (72 patients) showed a 37% reduction in collagen proportion (P < 0.001) in alcoholic cirrhosis patients receiving autologous BM-MSCs [212], and a multicenter phase I/II trial confirmed the safety of an “off-the-shelf” allogeneic umbilical cord MSC product in acute-on-chronic liver failure [213]. In osteochondral and cartilage repair, accumulating clinical evidence supports the efficacy of MSC-based therapies. A randomized controlled trial (48 patients with early knee OA) demonstrated that intra-articular autologous adipose-derived MSC injections achieved significant cartilage regeneration on MRI (medial femoral lesion volume decreased by 50.06 mm3 in the ADSC group versus an increase of 36.44 mm3 in the hyaluronic acid control group, P < 0.05), with superior and sustained improvements in WOMAC pain, stiffness, and function scores throughout 6 months of follow-up [214]. Collectively, these clinical findings validate the core premise of the SIIS framework—that immunomodulation, rather than direct cell replacement, drives therapeutic benefit—and support the feasibility of allogeneic “off-the-shelf” strategies for scalable manufacturing.

Future horizons and unanswered questions

Beyond the cell to precision engineering: Looking forward, the field is poised to move beyond whole-cell therapies toward more controlled and scalable paradigms. some scientists are beginning to shift their interest to MSC–derived EV, which encapsulate a critical fraction of the MSC's immunomodulatory cargo—including proteins, lipids, and regulatory RNAs—while potentially offering a superior safety profile by eliminating risks associated with cell replication and permanent engraftment [[215], [216], [217]]. Recent advances in extracellular vesicle engineering and synthetic biology offer promising pathways. For instance, MSC-derived exosomes can be bioengineered to carry specific immunomodulatory miRNAs (e.g., miR-146a for NF-κB suppression) and targeted to specific immune cells via surface modification with macrophage-specific peptides [[218], [219], [220]]. The future lies in engineering these EVs and designing smarter biomaterials to create highly specific, personalized, and combination-based regenerative products. This will involve: (1) Decoding the specifics: Precisely identifying which EV-borne factors (e.g., specific miRNAs) are responsible for therapeutic effects to ensure consistency and potency. (2) Developing predictive models: Creating advanced in vitro and silico models that better recapitulate the dynamic human immune response to implants. (3) Implementing quality-by-design: Establishing rigorous, mechanistically informed potency assays to replace current, often inadequate, cell-based metrics.

The AI-decoded immune-material interface: The complexity of immunoprogramming—with its multidimensional parameter space (cell type × material property × tissue context × temporal dynamics)—demands computational approaches. To concretely operationalize AI within the SIIS framework, we define three core components: First, input datasets. Each BIC module is encoded as a ∼10-dimensional feature vector covering surface chemistry, ligand type/density, stiffness, viscoelasticity, topography, and pore size [221]. CSIM inputs include cellular parameters such as source tissue, passage number, and donor age, while TSIN inputs include tissue-specific immune microenvironment features including lesion stiffness range, oxygenation status, and cytokine background. Second, predicted outputs. These are multi-layered—ranging from proximal endpoints (macrophage M1/M2 marker ratio, cytokine secretion profile) to distal tissue repair functional endpoints such as left ventricular ejection fraction for myocardial repair, wound closure rate for skin healing, and fibrosis score for liver regeneration [222]. Third, AI-guided CSIM–BIC integration pathways. Machine learning algorithms trained on high-throughput screening data are beginning to predict how specific material features influence macrophage polarization, with random forest models achieving 92.8% accuracy in predicting macrophage inflammatory responses to nanofiber scaffolds [222], and interpretable machine learning identifying matrix stiffness as the dominant environmental cue regulating MSC immunomodulatory potency. Multi-scale computational frameworks integrating molecular dynamics simulations, agent-based models, finite element analysis, and graph neural networks have achieved 92.3% accuracy in predicting scaffold immunocompatibility, identifying surface topography and degradation kinetics as more critical predictors than chemical composition. Furthermore, machine learning models applied to single-cell transcriptomic data can predict MSC donor potency and generate integrated potency scores to match patients with MSC products possessing the most suitable immunomodulatory profile for personalized therapies [223]. Together, these approaches position AI as the “intelligent scheduling engine” of SIIS, systematically learning the mapping among CSIM, BIC, and TSIN modules and iteratively optimizing design parameters to bridge the gap between computational prediction and in vivo therapeutic outcomes.

However, critical gaps remain: What are the fundamental “grammar rules” governing immune cell–material communication, and how can we encode these into next-generation biomaterials? The integration of single-cell multi-omics with advanced material characterization techniques (e.g., spatial transcriptomics on implant surfaces) will be essential to decode this “dark matter” of immuno-material interactions. We acknowledge that the translation from computational prediction to in vivo validation remains a significant bottleneck, and future work will focus on integrating single-cell multi-omics with advanced materials characterization, employing transfer learning and active learning strategies, and establishing standardized platforms for cross-laboratory data sharing to accumulate high-quality training data at scale.
