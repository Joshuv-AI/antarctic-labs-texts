Persistent postnatal IGF2 expression alters adult skeletal architecture in Igf2G/A mice
Authors: Shady Younis, Anika Shimonty, Samantha Perez-Menendez, Xiaomeng You, Sruthi M Sridevan, Soha Ben-Tahar, Harper Andrews, Sandra Shefelbine, Leif Andersson, Julia F Charles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIGF2 is an imprinted growth factor essential for fetal development. A single nucleotide variation at a conserved ZBED6 binding site within Igf2 intron 3 induces post-natal IGF2 expression and resulted in increased lean mass in multiple species. While the role of IGF2 in muscle growth is established, its impact on the adult skeleton remains incompletely defined.MethodsWe studied 13-week-old male and female Igf2G/A knock-in mice carrying the pig-derived G ➔ A substitution that prevents ZBED6 binding. We quantified Igf2 expression in bone and visceral tissues, measured body and organ size, assessed femoral geometry and microarchitecture by micro-CT, examined growth plate morphology, evaluated bone turnover markers (P1NP, CTX1), and tested whole-bone mechanical properties.ResultsIgf2G/A mice exhibited increased size, body weight, length, and kidney mass, while liver mass trended higher. Igf2 mRNA levels were elevated in kidney, liver, and bone tissues. Femurs demonstrated greater length and larger periosteal perimeter, with increased cortical area in both sexes but no changes in cortical thickness or bone mineral density. Trabecular parameters remained unchanged in males but improved in females, characterized by higher BV/TV, increased trabecular thickness and number, and reduced spacing. Growth plate metrics were predominantly unaffected, except for a modest increase in mean thickness observed in Igf2G/A females. Serum P1NP and CTX1 levels showed no genotype-dependent differences. Mechanical testing revealed reduced elastic modulus in both sexes of the Igf2G/A compared to wildtype and lower ultimate stress in females, while other mechanical properties remained unchanged. Circulating IGF1 and IGFBP3 levels as well as bone expression of Igf1/Igf1r/Igfbp3 were unchanged, suggesting small if any impact of the greater GH-IGF axis on the phenotype.ConclusionsPost-natal IGF2 expression alters the adult murine skeleton by augmenting longitudinal growth and cortical accrual and inducing female-specific trabecular gains, with selective decrements in material properties. These data establish IGF2 as a regulator of postnatal bone architecture and mechanics and extend the functional scope of the conserved ZBED6–IGF2 regulatory axis to the skeleton.Keywords: Insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF2), Bone microarchitecture and biomechanics, ZBED6–IGF2 axis, Sex differences in bone, Bone growth

Introduction

Following the discovery of growth hormone (GH) more than half a century ago, scientists discovered two proteins mediating the activities of growth hormones, later named insulin-like growth factor 1 and 2 (Yakar et al., 2018). Insulin like growth factor 2 (IGF2) is one of the major proteins regulating fetal growth and development in both rodents and humans (Constância et al., 2002; Giabicani et al., 2019; Sélénou et al., 2022). In humans, the gene is located at chromosome 11p15.5 and regulated by 4 promoters whereas in mouse it is located on chromosome 7 and regulated by 3 promoters (Younis et al., 2018; Chang and Bartolomei, 2020). Igf2 is a maternally imprinted gene in both humans and mice, leading to the expression of only the paternally inherited allele (Ekström, 1994). Mice born with a disrupted paternal Igf2 are small, whereas mice born with a disrupted maternal Igf2 have normal growth (Constância et al., 2002; Kaneda, 2011).

IGF2 is highly expressed during development in both humans and mice. However, in mice, expression decreases postnatally, whereas in humans, the level remains high (Bondy et al., 1990; Yakar et al., 2018; Holly et al., 2019). Pro-IGF2 is post-translationally modified to form the mature form of IGF2 protein containing 67 amino acids (Duguay et al., 1998). IGF2 binds to the tyrosine kinase receptor type 1 IGF receptor (IGF-1R). It can also bind to the insulin receptor (IR) (Sélénou et al., 2022).

A previous study using quantitative trait locus mapping found a mutation in domestic pig Igf2 gene which results in larger muscle size in these pigs. The mutation was a single nucleotide substitution from G to A in pig Igf2 intron 3 (Van Laere et al., 2003). A follow up study found that this nucleotide substitution disrupts the interaction between IGF2 and a transcriptional repressor ZBED6 (Markljung et al., 2009). This results in a three-fold increase in Igf2 expression in skeletal muscle. ZBED6 and its binding site within Igf2 are highly conserved among placental mammals. Disruption of this regulatory interaction by either silencing ZBED6 or by CRISPR-mediated knockout in mice-derived C2C12 myoblasts induced Igf2 expression and myoblast growth, suggesting that IGF2-ZBED6 interaction is conserved in pigs and mice (Markljung et al., 2009; Younis et al., 2020).

In our previous study, we used a ZBED6-deletion model and an IGF2-overexpression model carrying the pig mutation to determine the effect of increased IGF2 expression in mice. The ZBED6-null mice had the gene deleted globally, whereas the IGF2-overexpression mice had the single nucleotide substitution (G ➔ A) in the binding site of ZBED6, located in first intron of Igf2. Both of the transgenic models had increased IGF2 expression in serum. They exhibited increased growth rate, larger size, higher lean mass, and larger organs. However, there was no change in white adipose tissue. This study showed a crucial role of IGF2 in growth and development, specifically in skeletal muscle, as well as an important role of ZBED6 in regulating IGF2 (Younis et al., 2018). Building on this, we next asked whether increased IGF2 similarly influences the adult skeleton. We hypothesized that elevating IGF2 would recapitulate aspects of the muscle phenotype in bone, increasing skeletal size and altering bone architecture and mechanics in adult mice.

To test this hypothesis, we used the IGF2 overexpression mice (referred to as Igf2G/A mice in this paper) in which the ZBED6 repressor cannot bind its conserved intronic site in Igf2, leading to sustained IGF2 upregulation in adult mice. Consistent with our hypothesis, elevated IGF2 modulated bone parameters in 13-week-old Igf2G/A mice. Both male and female Igf2G/A mice had increased Igf2 mRNA in bones, and their bones were longer. Both male and female Igf2G/A mice had increased cortical area, whereas trabecular parameters were different only in female Igf2G/A mice. Their mechanical properties were also altered. Overall, our study suggests an effect of IGF2 on mouse skeletal architecture.

Results

Igf2 G➔ A substitution results in larger mice with increased IGF2 levels

The mutant mouse was generated by a single-nucleotide substitution of G to A at the ZBED6 binding site GGCTCG in the first intron of Igf2 located at chromosome 7 (Younis et al., 2018). These mice were previously examined for range of physiological properties, including growth parameters and metabolic features (Younis et al., 2018). Consistent with the previous study, both male and female Igf2G/A mice had significantly higher total body weight (Fig. 1A) as well as body length (Fig. 1B) at 13 weeks of age. Male Igf2G/A mice also had higher kidney weights normalized to body weights, whereas female Igf2G/A mice had a trend of higher kidney weights which did not reach significance (p = 0.052) (Fig. 1C) but no significant difference in normalized liver weights (Fig. 1D). We measured kidney and liver Igf2 mRNA levels in male mice and confirmed the expected significant increase expressed in mutant mice (Fig. 1E-F). These data are consistent with the previous study using these transgenic mice (Younis et al., 2018). Because excess IGF2 has been reported to reduce levels of the key skeletal regulator IGF1 (Wilson et al., 1989), we next measured serum IGF1 and IGFBP3 levels (Fig. 1G-H) in these mice. Male Igf2G/A mice had slightly lower serum IGFBP3 and a trend towards lower IGF1 levels, with no significant change observed in female mice.

Igf2G/A mice had higher bone Igf2 expression and larger bones

Male and female WT and Igf2G/A mice femur lengths were measured (Fig. 2A). Both male and female Igf2G/A mice had significantly longer femurs compared to their WT controls. Igf2 mRNA expression in bone was significantly increased in the Igf2G/A male and female mice compared to their WT counterparts (Fig. 2B), whereas bone Igf1, Igf1r, and Igfbp3 expression levels remained unchanged (Fig. 2C-E). There was no significant difference in Igf2 expression levels between male and female mice. Taken together, this suggests that the increased Igf2 expression may modulate bone growth in mice.

P1NP and CTX1 levels were measured in serum as markers of bone formation and resorption, respectively. No significant difference was found between WT and Igf2G/A mice irrespective of sex (Fig. 2F-G).

Igf2G/A mice have wider bones but few differences in other bone parameters

Micro-CT analysis was done on both WT and Igf2G/A mice femurs. Cortical area and periosteal perimeter were significantly increased in both male and female Igf2G/A mice compared to WT (Fig. 3A-B). However, no significant difference was found in the cortical thickness or cortical bone mineral density (Fig. 3C-D), regardless of sex. 3D rendering of the cortical bone shows no visual differences in these bones (Fig. 3E).

The male Igf2G/A femoral trabecular bone volume fraction was not significantly different compared to WT (Fig. 3F). Additionally, no significant difference could be found in trabecular thickness or trabecular spacing (Fig. 3G-H) between WT and Igf2G/A mice. In contrast, Igf2G/A female mice had higher trabecular bone volume fraction compared to WT females (Fig. 3F). Consistent with this, mutant female mice had increased trabecular thickness and lower trabecular spacing (Fig. 3G-H). However, there was no significant difference in the trabecular tissue mineral density in either sex (Fig. 3I). 3D rendering of the trabecular bone shows difference between genotypes in females but not in males (Fig. 3J). This suggests that trabecular bone size and parameters are more impacted by the increased Igf2 expression in females than males.

Femoral growth plate thickness and volume was measured in both males and female mice. Mean growth plate thickness was higher in female Igf2G/A mice compared to WT (Fig. 3K), though no significant difference was seen in males. No significant differences could be found in growth plate volume or maximum growth plate thickness (Fig. 3L-M) between male WT and Igf2G/A mice of either sex. However, measuring the growth plate in adult mice, where longitudinal growth is minimal, may miss early growth phenotypes.

Bone mechanical properties are modestly affected by increased Igf2 expression

Femoral mechanical properties were measured in WT and Igf2G/A mice of both sexes (Fig. 4). Female Igf2G/A mice had lower ultimate stress (Fig. 4A) and lower elastic modulus (Fig. 4B) compared to WT females. Male Igf2G/A mice also had lower elastic modules compared to WT males. No other mechanical property was significantly different in either males or females, including ultimate load, work to fracture (total area under the curve), ductility, calculated as elastic work/total work, and yield stress (Fig. 4 C—F).

Discussion

While the effects of IGF2 on development are well studied, its potential impact on the adult mouse skeleton remain poorly understood. One of the most important roles of IGF2 is myogenesis, where IGF2 regulates mesoderm differentiation into myoblasts (Sélénou et al., 2022). As bone and muscle both originate from the paraxial mesoderm (Pourquié, 2001; Buckingham et al., 2003; Tani et al., 2020), their development may be influenced by the same or similar factors, including IGF2. This raises the question of whether post-natal IGF2 impacts bone structural and mechanical parameters.

IGF2 regulates growth during fetal or prenatal development (Constância et al., 2002; Randhawa and Cohen, 2005), including by increasing endochondral ossification in the growth plate by modulating BMP9 (Chen et al., 2010). The importance of IGF2 specifically for prenatal growth is demonstrated by IGF2-null mice, which are smaller when born but catch up to their wildtype counterparts by 3 months of age (DeChiara et al., 1991). Loss-of-function in IGF2 promoter P2 results in dwarf mice with impaired mesenchymal stem cell differentiation and osteoclastogenesis (Hardouin et al., 2011). This suggests a role of IGF2 primarily during development.

In the postnatal period, the level of IGF2 decreases (Bondy et al., 1990). As IGF2 is crucial during fetal development (Constância et al., 2002), post-natal expression of IGF2 might be expected to impact bone growth after birth. We sought to determine the effects of post-natal IGF2 on 13-week-old male and female mice. We chose this age as although sexual maturity occurs at 6–8 weeks of age, longitudinal bone growth continues while peak bone mass occurs around 16–20 weeks of age (Jilka, 2013). To investigate the impact of post-natal IGF2 expression on the skeleton we utilized an IGF2 knock in model, characterized by a substitution of a G to an A in the first intron of Igf2 gene (Igf2G/A mice). Previous studies have shown that a G to A substitution in the third intron of Igf2 gene in domestic pigs, corresponding to intron 1 in mice, disrupts the binding of a transcriptional repressor, ZBED6 to Igf2. This results in an increase in IGF2 in skeletal muscle and is correlated to both increased muscle mass and decreased fat (Van Laere et al., 2003; Markljung et al., 2009). Our previous study using transgenic mice with the same nucleotide substitution exhibited elevated Igf2 mRNA expression accompanied by upregulation of the intronic microRNA miR-483 (Naboulsi et al., 2021). These molecular changes were associated with increased serum IGF2 levels and higher growth rate and lean mass (Younis et al., 2018). These transgenic mice also had enlargement of multiple organs including kidney, liver, and heart and additional metabolic alterations (Younis et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2021).

Igf2G/A mice at 13 weeks of age have increased femur length and periosteal perimeter compared to WT in both males and females, with significantly increased Igf2 expression in bone.

The lack of significant difference in cortical or trabecular bone mineral density, as well as similar levels of bone turnover markers serum P1NP and CTX1 suggest that, at least in adult mice, IGF2 does not modulate bone mineralization. One limitation of our analysis is that since we only examined mice at skeletal maturity, while IGF2 is persistently expressed postnatally, we cannot distinguish the effect of persistent IGF2 expression on juvenile skeletal development versus on the adult skeleton.

Additionally, we observed both sex and site-specific impacts of post-natal IGF2 on long bone architecture. While cortical area was increased in both male and female Igf2G/A mice due to increased periosteal perimeter, trabecular bone volume/tissue volume and trabecular parameters were increased only in female Igf2G/A mice. This suggests that IGF2 has a more consistent impact on cortical bone growth compared to its effect on trabecular bone, which is sex specific.

Bone is a sexually dimorphic organ in both humans and mice. Usually, females have a lower bone mineral density compared to males. Females also suffer from osteoporosis at both a higher rate and at an earlier age compared to males. Female and male bones respond to stimuli differently, have different fracture healing properties, and different gene expression profiles. These changes are more evident with aging (Glatt et al., 2007; Gabet and Bab, 2011; Alswat, 2017; Mumtaz et al., 2020; Kurapaty and Hsu, 2022; Lu et al., 2022). Growth hormones and sex steroids are interconnected (Yakar et al., 2018). The interaction between IGF1 and sex differences have been extensively studied. IGF1-deficient mice do not display the typical skeletal sex differences (Bikle et al., 2001). IGF1 also interacts with E2 to modulate bone growth (Venken et al., 2005; Yakar et al., 2018). However, the interaction between IGF2 and sex steroids is not well studied. We have observed marked differences between female and male Igf2G/A mice in our study. Female Igf2G/A mice displayed more pronounced differences in bone parameters compared to WT than their male counterparts, aligning with our previous study where the female transgenic mice had a higher increase in muscle mass (Younis et al., 2018). Altered expression of IGF2 has a higher effect on female musculoskeletal system compared to males, indicating a sex-specific effect. Additional studies to investigate the contribution of sex hormones to the observed sex differences in bone phenotype are needed.

As IGF2 has been shown to impact the broader growth hormone-IGF axis, an important modulator of bone growth, we assessed circulating levels of IGF1 and IGFBP3 as well as Igf1, Igf1r, and Igfbp3 gene expression levels in bone. We found no significant differences between WT and Igf2G/A mice in these measures other than a slightly lower circulating IGFBP3 in male Igf2G/A mice. While we did not directly measure circulating GH levels, the absence of major changes in circulating IGF-1 and IGFBP-3, as well as in the expression levels of Igf1, Igf1r, and Igfbp3 in bone suggests that the observed bone phenotype is unlikely to be driven by major alterations in GH–IGF1 signaling. Rather, these findings suggest a model in which persistent postnatal IGF2 expression contributes to the observed bone phenotype. However, endocrine adaptations or local interactions within the growth plate and bone microenvironment cannot be completely ruled out. Future studies are needed to investigate this more closely.

Adult female Igf2G/A mice had lower elastic modulus and lower ultimate stress, suggesting altered bone material properties. Elastic modulus, the measurement of resistance against deformation elastically that the bone exerts in response to stress, is often used to refer to the intrinsic stiffness of bone. A lower elastic modulus suggests a less stiff bone (Turner and Burr, 1993; Chattah et al., 2009). On the other hand, ultimate stress is the maximum stress a material can sustain before failure, which can be an indicator of bone strength. Similar to the elastic module, the lower ultimate stress of the female Igf2G/A mice suggests less strong bone in these mice. The mechanism by which IGF2 modulates bone material properties will require further study.

An important consideration is whether the skeletal phenotype arises from local IGF2 expression within bone or from systemic increases in circulating IGF2. One previous study has shown that modest increase in circulating IGF2 levels did not highly affect bone growth or turnover (Wolf et al., 1995). In our Igf2G/A mice model, Igf2 expression is increased across multiple tissues, including bone, precluding clear separation of local versus systemic effects. Previous studies have shown that IGF2 derived from stem cells, and not osteoblasts, modulates skeletal homeostasis and development (Wang et al., 2025). Additionally, IGF2 has been shown to increase osteoclastogenesis under hypoxic conditions (Kondo et al., 2022). Thus, the observed phenotype likely reflects a combination of both mechanisms. Future studies employing tissue-specific or inducible approaches will be necessary to delineate the relative contributions of local and endocrine IGF2 signaling as well as the cellular target within bone mediating the bone phenotype.

Bone and muscle interact with each other both mechanically and biochemically (Brotto and Bonewald, 2015; Bonewald, 2019). Bone cell-derived osteokines and muscle-derived myokines can modulate tissue functions in both physiological and pathological conditions (Pin et al., 2021; Shimonty et al., 2023). A recent study utilizing both global (KO) and muscle-specific (mKO) folliculin-interacting protein 1 (FNIP1) knock-out mouse model has shown an increase in IGF2 in these mice muscles. At 8–12 weeks, both KO and mKO mice had lower trabecular bone volume and decreased mechanical functions. FNIP1 significantly increased nuclear TFEB levels, which in turn increased IGF2 levels. In patients suffering from limb-girdle muscular dystrophy, Igf2 mRNA level and systemic bone loss were higher than control population. Systemic AAV9-Igf2 delivery in wildtype neonate mice resulted in high IGF2 levels in the skeletal muscle and significantly reduced bone mass, elastic modulus, maximum force, and compressive ultimate stress. There was a marked increase in osteoclast numbers, with no change in osteoblasts. Taken together, this suggests that the muscle FNIP1-TFEB-IGF2 axis is necessary for regulating bone metabolism (Mao et al., 2024). In our previous study, we found that the Igf2G/A mice had higher skeletal muscle mass, specifically in females. Our current study shows a similar effect on bones. The higher muscle mass may regulate the bone size in these mice. On the other hand, the longer bone may also modulate the muscle mass. Since our model is a whole-tissue genetic model, we cannot determine if the effects of persistent IGF2 expression on bones are a local endocrine effect, or a systemic effect being modulated by the higher muscle mass. We plan to further investigate bone-muscle interactions in these mice to determine if the increase in muscle modulates the higher bone growth or vice-versa.

Changes in IGF2 have been correlated with different disease conditions (Boonen et al., 1999; Minuto et al., 2005; Jeyaratnaganthan et al., 2010; Dynkevich et al., 2013; Livingstone, 2013; Livingstone and Borai, 2014; Sélénou et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2022; Mao et al., 2024). In a recent population study, urinary-derived IGF2 was positively associated with chronological age, with females having an upregulation compared to males (Yu et al., 2024). Therefore, there is a need to understand how IGF2 affects adult bone. Our study demonstrates the effects of IGF2 on adult male and female mice bones and suggests a sex-specific effect of IGF2 on bones. To our knowledge, this is one of the first works to examine the effects of post-natal IGF2 expression in adult bone. Our findings highlight IGF2's sex-specific impact on adult bone, offering insights into its potential role in sex-dependent bone metabolic disorders such as osteoporosis.

Methods & materials

Animal experiments

20 Igf2G/A mice (10 male, 10 female) and 18 wild type (11 male, 7 female) littermates aged 12–15 weeks were utilized in this study. Mice were group-housed with free access to food and water in the pathogen-free facilities of Uppsala University and Karolinska Institute. All animal procedures were approved by the Uppsala Ethical Committee on Animal Research and the Stockholm Ethical Committee. Mice body weight and body length were measured at the time of sacrifice. Body length was measured from the tip of the nose to the base of the tail.

Micro-computed tomography

Femurs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and then transferred to 70% ethanol. Femur μCT scanning was performed using a Scanco Medical μCT 35 system (Scanco Medical AG) with an isotropic voxel size of 7 μm, X-ray tube potential of 55 kVp, X-ray intensity of 145 mA, and an integration time of 400 ms. Three-dimensional microstructural properties of bone were calculated using software supplied by the manufacturer. For trabecular bone analysis, a region beginning with 0.28 mm proximal to the growth plate and extending 1.4 mm proximally was selected. For cortical bone analysis, a region of 0.6 mm in length at the femoral midshaft was used. A semi-automated contouring approach was used to distinguish cortical and trabecular bones. Global threshold values with bone/marrow cut-off at 645 mg HA/cm3 for trabecular bone and 793 mg HA/cm3 for cortical bone were selected. Results were reported according to guidelines provided by ASBMR (Bouxsein et al., 2010).

Growth plate

Growth plate volume, mean thickness, and maximum thickness were calculated using a semi-automated macro in ImageJ (Fiji, National Institutes of Health and Laboratory for Optical and Computational Instrumentation) using microCT images of the growth plate. A range of interest was selected as the 60 slices surrounding the midpoint slice between the first and last appearance of a complete epiphyseal plate. The generated subsections were contoured and interpolated to create ROIs on each slide. These ROIS were utilized to create .tif files of the growth plates, which were analyzed with the BoneJ thickness plug-in and the histogram function.

Mechanical properties

Bone biomechanical properties were assessed using the three-point bending test performed on an Instron 5966 and computed for a post-processing analysis. 27 mice femurs were utilized for the test in a displacement control mode with a load rate of .5 mm/min. Load was monitored by a 40 N load cell, and the femurs were loaded in the anterior-posterior orientation using the three-point bending set up. This set-up included a plunger with a radius of .25 mm and span length of 8 mm. The conclusion of the test was marked with a 40% load drop in each case. The moment of inertia and the maximum distance around the medio-lateral axis used for stress–strain calculations were determined from a 100 μm region at the center of the bone where loading occurred. The ultimate load was identified as the peak of the force–displacement curve. From the stress–strain curves, yield stress (0.2% offset method), ultimate stress (peak stress), elastic modulus (slope of the linear region), work to fracture (area under the stress–strain curve), and ductility (area under the plastic region of the stress–strain curve) were calculated.

Serum biomarker analysis

Blood was collected at the time of sacrifice via cardiac puncture. Serum was separated from blood using serum separation tubes (BD Medical, NJ, US). Serum PINP was measured using Rat/Mouse PINP EIA kit (IDS, UK), serum CTX-1 was measured using RatLaps CTX-1 EIA kit (IDS, UK), serum IGF1 was measured using mouse/rat IGF-1 quantikine ELISA kit (R & D systems, USA), and serum IGFBP3 was measured using mouse IGFBP-3 quantikine ELISA kit (R & D systems, USA). Manufacturer's protocols were used in all cases. A Synergy H Hybrid Reader (BioTek) was used to read EIA plate absorbance.

Quantitative real-time-polymerase chain reaction

Liver, kidney, and bones were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and transferred to −80 °C until use. For bones, 2 tibia and 1 femur from each mouse were used. Epiphyses were cut and marrow flushed before snap freezing them. Total RNA was extracted by homogenization with a bullet blender bead beater (Navy Rino RNA lysis kit; Next Advance) followed by extraction using RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. For liver and kidney, the tissues were cut into small pieces prior to homogenization. RNA was then reverse transcribed to cDNA using a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems). Transcript levels of Igf2 were measured by real-time PCR using TaqMan gene expression assay (TaqMan IGF2 primer: Mm00439564_m1) performed on a StepOne Plus real-time PCR machine (Applied Biosystems, USA). Transcript levels of bone Igf1 (forward: GTCGTCTTCACACCTCTTCTACCT, reverse: GCACAGTACATCTCCAGTCTCCT), Igf1r (forward: CTCAGGCTTCATCCGCAACAG, reverse: GTTCTCCAACTCCGAGGCAATG), and Igfb3 (forward: CGATTCCAAGTTCCATCCACTCCAT, reverse: ACCGTATTCTGTCTCCCGCTTAGA) were measured using SYBR gene expression assay. Gene expression was normalized to housekeeping gene TBP (tata-binding protein) levels (TaqMan primer, Hs00427620_m1, VIC Primer Limited, SYBR primer forward: CTACCGTGAATCTTGGCTGTAAAC, reverse: AATCAACGCAGTTGTCCGTGGC) using the standard 2-ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

Data was presented in graphs as individual values with standard deviation (SD) bars. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 7 (Graphpad Software). Data met the assumptions of two-way ANOVA. Experimental groups were compared using two-way ANOVA (genotype * sex) for parametric data with Tukey post-hoc test. p values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. * = p < 0.05, ** = p < 0.01, *** = p < 0.001, and **** = p < 0.0001. ns indicates not statistically significant.
