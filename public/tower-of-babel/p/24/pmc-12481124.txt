Introduction

Hamate fractures are very rare and only encompass 2 % of carpal bone fractures [1]. Hamate fractures occur in conjunction with 4th or 5th metacarpal fracture dislocations, accounting for less than 1 % of all hand trauma [2]. The main mechanism of injury in a hamate body fracture is a direct blow over the hypothenar eminence or forceful dorsopalmar compression. The position of the wrist at the time of the injury determines the morphology of the fracture. When the wrist is radially deviated the ulnar aspect of the hamate is stabilized and an axial force can lead to sagittal plane fractures in the radial aspect of the hamate. However, when the wrist is held in ulnar deviation, the hamate firmly abuts the triquetrum and an axial force can result in coronal plane fractures. There are two types of hamate fractures which include fractures of the hook of the hamate (type I) and fractures of the hamate body (type II) [3]. Type I fractures can be further subdivided into three types: (type I—I) fracture of the tip of the hook of hamate, (type I-II) fractures of the middle part of the hook of hamate, (type I-III) fractures at the base of the hook of hamate. Hamate body fractures are further subdivided into fracture line orientation, type IIA for coronal and type IIB for transverse. Fractures of the hamate that are nondisplaced can be treated with short arm immobilization for 4–6 weeks. However, displaced fractures of the hamate body or nondisplaced hook fractures can be treated with open reduction internal fixation, percutaneous wire fixation, or excision depending on the size of the fracture fragment. Complications of untreated hamate fractures include posttraumatic arthritis, chronic 4th and 5th carpometacarpal (CMC) dislocation, and avascular necrosis [1,4]. The following is a case report of a patient with a displaced coronal hamate body fracture with subluxation of the 5th CMC joint managed at on academic setting. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [5].

Case report

The patient is a 35-year-old male construction worker who sustained a right wrist injury after punching a piece of wood. He presented to urgent care two days after the injury and x-rays at that time were read as negative for fracture (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). However, on Figure 1there is notable overlap between the 5th metacarpal and hamate body indicating the joint is likely not reduced. Additionally, on Fig. 2 there is a subtle lucency though the hamate that is noticeable. 16 days after the injury he presented to the emergency department due to increased ulnar sided wrist pain located over the hamate and again had x-rays that were read as negative for fracture. Then 2 days thereafter he met with an orthopaedic surgeon outside of our practice who placed him in a volar splint and ordered a CT scan. CT scan was completed the same day which showed a coronal hamate body fracture with 4 mm of diastases with subluxation of the 5th CMC articulation (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). He finally presented to our office 3.5 weeks after his injury, with a plan for right hamate body open reduction internal fixation utilizing headless compression screws. His examination demonstrated no ulnar nerve or FDP ring/small finger dysfunction. He underwent the procedure 39 days after the initial injury. During surgery, a single incision was made over the dorsal aspect of the wrist, over the 4th and 5th CMC joints. Dissection was carried down to the articulations and the capsule was elevated off of the hamate as well as the base of the fourth and fifth metacarpals. A combination of axial traction as well as a “joystick” K-wire in the base of the fourth and fifth metacarpals allowed for debridement of the fracture site with a small angled curette. The fracture was then reduced and an oblique K-wire was placed through the hamate into the base of the fifth metacarpal. Following this, two K-wires were placed from the dorsal aspect of the hamate into the hook of the hamate. Radiographs were taken to make sure not to violate the palmar cortex to protect the ulnar neurovascular structures. Appropriately sized headless compression screws were placed (2.3 mm Trimed screws, Santa Clarita, CA). Intraoperative radiographs were taken to confirm position of the headless compression screws (Fig. 5). The patient was placed into an ulnar gutter splint for immobilization. He presented back to the office for postoperative visits at 2 weeks and 6 weeks after surgery and has done very well. He was placed into an ulnar gutter splint until his 6 week post-surgery visit. Radiographs demonstrate interval healing with a congruent 5th CMC joint (Fig. 6, Fig. 7), and his hand motion was full. Discussion

We present a case of a Milch type IIA hamate body fracture with 4 mm of displacement, diagnosed in a delayed fashion and treated with two headless compression screws. To our knowledge, this is the only case report that discusses using two headless compression screws into the hook of the hamate to stabilize a Milch type IIA hamate body fracture. Previous literature has evaluated using a two-incision approach for open reduction and internal fixation of intra-articular hamate body fractures using headless compression screws which demonstrated excellent stability post operatively [6]. While the two-incision approach is an effective technique for open reduction, in our case, we were fortunate to achieve reduction of the hamate body fracture without the need for open reduction.

Acute fractures that are non-displaced including either the hook or the body, and can be treated with six weeks of immobilization [7]. Acute and displaced hamate body fractures can be treated with percutaneous wires, plates, or headless compression screws [8]. In 2017, Quadlbauer found that for unstable scaphoid fractures, two headless compression screws showed higher union rates and equal clinical outcomes compared with one headless compression screw [9]. However, we are unsure if this remains true for hamate fractures. In theory, two screws should prevent rotation and provide a more stiff construct. Additionally, two screws increases the distance between rotational pivot points, which is expected to improve rotational stability. In a 2019 study, Rijckevorsel et al. had nine patients with hamate fractures treated with headless compression screws and found that headless compression screws are a safe alternative with good functional outcomes [10]. Rijckevorsel also stated that only one screw can be positioned in the hook of hamate due to its confined space and they place an additional compression and antirotation screw that was much shorter for the corpus of the hamate. However, we believe in certain patient populations with enough area within the hook of the hamate that will accommodate two headless compression screws will prevent rotation and allow for compression that is superior to one headless compression screw. In addition to favorable patient anatomy that can accommodate two headless compression screws, the availability of smaller-diameter screws further facilitates their placement in the hook of the hamate. As this is a case report we understand the limitations interims of generalizability of this technique. However, two headless compression screws could be suggested for patients with significant rotation instability of fracture fragments intraoperatively.

Conclusion

There are several key learning points from this case report. If your clinical suspicion is high for a hamate fracture, a CT should be obtained. These fractures are likely missed due to how rare they are and lack of appropriate imaging. Two headless compression screws through a single dorsal incision are an effective treatment option for rotationally unstable coronal hamate body fractures.
