Expanded voluntary counselling and testing services in Türkiye: insights from an HIV model
Authors: Zikriye Melisa Erdogan, Emine Yaylali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVoluntary Counselling and Testing (VCT) centres provide free and anonymous Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) testing along with counselling services. They remove barriers to testing and lead to higher HIV awareness. The current healthcare system in Türkiye achieves high treatment rates in the continuum of care. However, the estimated diagnosis rates are far below the global targets, posing a significant challenge. This also creates an opportunity to intensify prevention efforts against the burden of the disease. Expanded VCT services is a suggested strategy for Türkiye, but research evaluating its effectiveness is lacking.MethodsWe developed a dynamic compartmental model with different testing sites as separate compartments, distinguishing VCT centres from others. Using the data from each VCT centre, we quantified the impact of this strategy through various scenarios.ResultsWe found that under the assumption that the VCT strategy primarily serves high-risk populations, it could yield 50 to 74-fold more pay-off in terms of the number of cases prevented per additional test than other facilities. A two- and four-fold increase in both types of testing facilities (VCT and other) would reduce annual incidence in 2033 by 71% and 91%, respectively, while prevalence was estimated to be 34% and 51% lower compared to no action.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that prioritizing VCT expansion is highly efficient strategy compared to other testing sites. Future research could focus on resource allocation, costs and benefits of the programs to ensure the optimal allocation of the resources to the most cost-effective public health strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24350-3.

Introduction

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) diagnosis acts as an entry point into HIV treatment and care continuum [1] and plays a key role for people living with HIV (PLHIV) to know their status. Awareness of status is critical since late diagnosis affects linkage to care, quality of life and life expectancy of patients. Furthermore, the lack of knowledge of one’s HIV status contributes to the spread of the disease in the community as diagnosed patients are less likely to transmit [2]. In [3] around 45% of the new transmissions in the US were attributed to patients who unaware of their status, which highlights the importance of testing and diagnosis in HIV prevention efforts.

The Joint United Nations Program on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) aim to reach that by 2030, 95% of PLHIV diagnosed, 95% of those diagnosed with HIV receive antiretroviral therapy (ART), and 95% of all people on ART achieve viral suppression to reduce HIV disease burden in the world [4]. Global estimates show that there are 39.9 million PLHIV at the end of 2023, of which 86% knew their status, while 89% were receiving ART and 93% achieved VLS [5]. As for Türkiye, 48,273 total number of cases are reported since the first case in 1985 until November 7, 2024. The annual number of diagnosis has reached its peak value in 2023 with 6,329 new cases and increased by approximately 4.5-fold in the last decade [6, 7]. Overall, current data shows an increasing HIV trend except 2020 and 2021 due to COVID-19 disruption. Based on the previous estimates on continuum of care, 48–50% for the first 95, 86–88% for the second 95 and 85–87% for the third 95 is achieved in Türkiye [8]. Although estimates suggest high treatment and VLS rates for patients, the first step in the continuum of care about individuals’ awareness of HIV status is considerably behind the targets presenting the largest gap in reaching HIV targets [8, 9]. Early diagnosis of HIV is also a challenge considering around 48-52.4% of cases are diagnosed late (CD4 level < 350 cell/mm3) and 24-30.6% with advanced disease (CD4 level < 200 cell/mm3) in the country, which might be due to the lack of testing strategies aimed at key populations [10].

A study involving 100 PLHIV from 11 cities in Türkiye highlights the critical importance of anticipated stigma in delayed HIV testing despite the small sample size [11]. Late diagnosis and delayed treatment initiation could be attributed to key barriers such as stigma and discrimination, difficulty in access to testing centres, lack of confidentiality in testing based on a systematic review in the Caribbean [11, 12] also revealed that 77% of respondents from Türkiye reported not receiving pre- or post-test counselling, which might show providing insufficient counselling.

VCT centres differs from other test sites in terms of sustainable, safe, free and anonymous way of testing and providing pre- and post-test counselling services. These centres not only help increase individuals’ awareness of HIV after the counselling sessions, but also reduce the potential concerns people may associate with testing [13]. VCT model is a promising tool and an effective solution to overcome the barriers of stigma and prejudice which leads hesitancy of seeking help from healthcare institutions [14]. National HIV Control Program, published by the Turkish Ministry of Health (MoH) in 20191, established three primary goals including strengthening the service capacity to advance HIV response. Although expanding VCT centres, both in quality and quantity, was defined as one of the key activities to achieve this goal [13], no study has been conducted to evaluate the outcomes of VCT initiatives for HIV in Türkiye. Thus, our study is the first to quantify the outcomes of expansion of VCT centres in the country. Unlike conventional approaches where compartmental models evaluate the effects of promoted VCT centres by changing HIV testing rates, our study introduces two distinct testing sites- VCT centres and other testing sites such as hospitals or clinics - as separate compartments in the model.

This study is focused on testing and diagnosis stage of the continuum of care and the goal is to make a model-based analysis to estimate the effects of expanded VCT strategy and scrutinize the most successful public health policy. In this study, we address the following questions: (i) What are the effects of increasing VCT centres in Türkiye? (ii) Which testing strategy should be prioritized in policy making, VCT centres or other testing sites? (iii) What is the value of combined efforts in eliminating the disease in Türkiye?

This paper is organized as follows: we describe the details of the method in the second section and present the results in the third section. Forth section discusses the findings and concluding remarks.

Materials and methods

Model overview

We developed an HIV model using a deterministic dynamic compartmental model. One of the most basic compartmental models is known as the SIR model, where individuals in the population belong to one of the compartments categorized as susceptible (S), infected (I) or recovered (R). The progression from the S compartment to the I compartment is possible when disease transmission occurs. Based on the rates between the compartments, those infected can move to the R compartment when they regain their health [15].

The structure of our model was focused on the HIV awareness of individuals who tested at different testing sites. Thus, the population was divided by HIV status (HIV positive and HIV negative), awareness of HIV status (aware and unaware) and type of testing site (VCT and others). “VCT” refers to centres where individuals get free and anonymous testing along with counselling while “other” corresponds to hospitals or clinics other than VCT centres.

The model diagram is shown in Fig. 1. First, the susceptible population was stratified into high-risk (SH) and low-risk (SL) HIV negative (HIV –) individuals unaware of their status. After HIV – individuals unaware of their status test negative, they become aware and transition to either the HIV – aware tested by VCT (SVCT) or the HIV – aware tested by other testing sites (SO). Infected individuals enter the HIV + unaware (U) compartment and, upon diagnosis, they move to the HIV + aware individuals tested by VCT (IVCT), or HIV + aware individuals tested by others (IO). Patients who receive treatment are classified into the ART compartment. The mathematical representation of the model is presented as a system of differential equations in the supplementary and solved in R software using deSolve package [16, 17].

Model assumptions

We assumed same treatment rates for both test sites due to the lack of data and different transmission rates for low- and high-risk populations. HIV + aware individuals and patients on ART have less infectivity. Furthermore, HIV – aware individuals become eligible for retesting after 2 years, moving back to the HIV – unaware compartment.

Engaging in a risk behaviour and perceived risk of individuals effect testing site decision. Having a history of risky sexual behaviour was associated with using VCT [18] and was the most common reason for seeking VCT services [19]. As for perceived risk, when people don’t feel at risk of getting HIV or low risk of infection, they refuse to use VCT [20–22]. Thus, we assumed high-risk individuals apply for testing at VCT centres, while low-risk individuals utilize other facilities.

The impact of testing at VCTs on sexual risk behaviour is studied in terms of unprotected sex and sexual partners. Evidence from meta-analyses suggests VCT recipients were significantly less likely to engage in unprotected sex with no significant effect on the number of sexual partners [23] and VCT is indeed effective in reducing sexual risk behaviours only for key groups [24]. Thus, people who tested by VCT centres were assumed to spread the disease with a lower transmission rate. We used country-specific data and incorporated behavioural change in unprotected sexual acts. We calculated cumulative probability of HIV infection (p) by a Bernoulli model [25], and quantified the overall change in transmission rate after comparing probabilities with and without VCT. The details are given in Figure S1 and Table S2 in the supplementary.

Model parameters

Parameters were estimated based on several sources such as the medical literature, the Turkish Statistical Institute and Turkish Ministry of Health (MoH). Inputs of the model that could not be estimated from data and literature have been calibrated. To distinguish different testing sites, we utilized data from MoH showing the number of tests sent to each VCT centre, the number of applied tests and the results of the tests (HIV + or HIV -) between 2016 and 2020 for each VCT centre [7].

The high-risk individuals included the following groups: Sex workers (SW), Men Who Have Sex with Men (MSM), People Who Inject Drugs (PWID), partners of People Living with HIV (PLHIV), people who have multiple sexual partners and mobile workers. The details are shown in Table S1 in the supplementary. We also considered that 44% of sailors, 42% of truck drivers and 30% of tourism workers in Türkiye engage in risky sexual intercourse based on [26].

Model calibration

We estimated the force of infection parameters using the number of diagnosed cases between 2018 and 2022. We used Approximate Bayesian Computation Sequential Monte-Carlo (ABC-SMC) with an updated kernel. The approach starts with sampling the parameters from their prior distributions, simulating the model using these samples and creating intermediate distributions with the parameters whose distance to the observed data is less than the threshold. Through these intermediate distributions, the results converge towards an approximated posterior distribution(s) for the calibrated parameter(s) [27]. The details of the algorithm can be found in [27].

One of the components of ABC-SMC is kernel function to perturb the sampled parameters. We have adopted the kernel function of the algorithm using Covariance Intersection, a well-known method for combining two different estimates when the correlations between them are unknown [28]. Details of calibration methods can be found in the supplementary.

Model scenarios

We designed four fundamental scenarios including the base case to address our research questions (Table 1). In scenario 1, current testing sites in the model are maintained. In scenario 2, we only increase the number of VCT centres. In [29], the relationship between the number of VCT sites and the number of visitors were not linear. We found capacity utilizations between 65% and 95% by dividing the number of tests performed to the number of tests sent to each VCT centre in Türkiye. Thus, we applied 2.6-fold and 3.8-fold increase for minimum and maximum values in scenario 2.3 and scenario 2.4, respectively. In scenario 3, we aim to compare the effects of VCT centres and others. In scenario 4, we evaluated their combined effects on the results.

Results

Calibration results

We obtained values for the calibrated parameters using ABC-SMC method. First, number of particles in the algorithm was assigned 500 and 10 simulation runs and then with 5000 particles for 1 simulation run. The results were compared in terms of Mean absolute error (MAE) and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE). Although they were close to each other, the values with highest density of posterior distributions with 500 particles and 10 runs produced less error. Thus, we selected the peak values for the calibrated parameters (Table S3 and Figure S2 in the supplementary).

Model results

Estimations for incidence, prevalence, number of diagnosed cases between 2018 and 2033 are shown in Figs. 2, 3 and 4. The results were compared with the base case. In Scenario 2, there was a slight change in incidence, with the largest effect observed in scenario 2.2, which is expected as the rate of increase in testing site is the highest. “VCT strategy” alone caused 3% and 10% decrease in incidence rates for scenarios 3.1 and 3.2 in 2033, respectively. As for “others strategy”, 10% and 25% decrease in incidence were found in 2023, while the incidence decreased by 70% and 90% in 2033 for Scenarios 3.3 and 3.4, respectively.

In terms of prevalence in 2033, there were 1% and 4% decrease for scenario 3.1 and 3.2 whereas 33% and 51% decrease for scenario 3.3 and 3.4, respectively. The results for scenarios 3.3 and 3.4 were highly similar to those in scenarios 4.1 and 4.2. For scenarios 4.1 and 4.2, the reduction in diagnosed cases in 2033 were 56% and 86%, and the annual incidence would be reduced by 71% and 91% while the prevalence for the same year is estimated to be 34% and 51% less compared to the base case.

The trajectories for each compartment are given in Figure S3, S4 and S5 in the supplementary. The number of individuals in the HIV – unaware (SH and/or SL) compartments were decreasing while the ones in the HIV – aware (SVCT and/or SO) compartments were increasing based on the related strategy for all scenarios. HIV + unaware population declined with the strategies as more people get tested. Moreover, HIV + aware (IVCT and IO) tended to increase at the beginning, then the number of individuals in these compartments decreased after one or two years for scenario 4.

The detailed results for sub-strategies of Scenario 3 between 2023 and 2033 were presented in Table 2. Total tests were calculated by summing annual values and shown for “VCT centres” and “other test sites” in the second and fourth columns, respectively. Increased tests in the following columns reflects the difference in total tests compared to the base scenario (Scenario 1). Prevented incidence was obtained by comparing incidence with the base scenario and dividing this value by the increased tests yielded the metric titled “Prevented incidence per additional test”. Comparing this metric across strategies allowed us to calculate the effectiveness ratio. As shown in the last column, a 2-fold increase in VCT centres results in a 50-fold greater effectiveness (prevented incidence per additional test), while a 4-fold increase leads to a 74-fold higher impact compared to other facilities.

Discussion

Türkiye has low HIV prevalence [10], however, there is a growing trend in HIV diagnoses. Lack of incentives for testing and limited number of VCT centres are one of the factors contributing to this trend [31]. According to a report including over 21,000 participants, around 77% of Turkish population do not have accurate knowledge about HIV [32]. This indicates the need for interventions such as VCT, which was shown to significantly improve participants’ HIV knowledge and their risk behaviours [33]. In Türkiye, seven VCT centres operate in five different cities and more anonymous test centres would help controlling the rising trend in the number of cases [10, 32]. Since, there is no in-depth analysis of this strategy in Türkiye, we assessed its impact with an HIV model comparing different public health strategies.

The changes in each compartment were compatible with expectations: as the number of test sites increased, the corresponding compartments for that test site also increased. Following the peak, the estimated number of diagnoses showed a downward trend in subsequent years for other testing sites. This result aligns with the principle typically seen in classical SIR models, where epidemic pattern demonstrates a decline after the model reaches its peak.

The base case scenario led to an increase in the number of patients on treatment. In scenarios 3 and 4, the number of patients on treatment was higher than those in the base case at the beginning, however it fell below the base case after 2030 and 2032, respectively. This result suggests economic burden of treatment would be reduced in the long-term. The strategies defined in scenarios 3 and 4 resulted in fewer diagnoses than those in the base case shortly after 2025 and 2026, rapidly showing their impact. In scenario 4, preventing up to 91% of the annual incidence and 51% of PLHIV seems possible with the combined efforts compared to taking no action in 10 years, which could substantially reduce HIV incidence in the country.

Both risk groups are included in the “HIV + Unaware” compartment. In scenarios 2 and 3, an increase in a specific test site affects both “HIV + and aware” compartments. This is driven by the model’s dynamics, where the more diagnoses occur, the more individuals modify their risky behaviour, leading to fewer interactions and less transmission between low- and high-risk individuals.

Compared to tests conducted by the Ministry of Health (MoH), VCT centres have approximately 100 times greater HIV positivity rates because individuals who perceive themselves to be at higher risk are tested primarily at VCT centres [34], while tests conducted by the MoH were typically mandatory before blood transfusions, organ donations and for registered sex workers. HIV tests are also applied before surgical operations, job application, pregnancy situations [7] or prior to registration at certain recreational facilities, such as swimming pools [35]. Our model shows that when the number of “other facilities” are improved, there will be 46 to 105 million more tests in 10 years than the current implementation. Applying this strategy might not be cost-effective due to its low positivity rates. Our model, which assumes VCTs primarily serve high-risk populations, suggests this targeted testing strategy could be 50- to 74-fold more efficient in terms of prevented HIV incidence per additional test compared to other facilities.

MSM group prefer VCT option for its anonymous testing procedure and specialized services [36], therefore, VCT strategy supports targeted testing for high-risk populations. HIV profile in Türkiye has been changing over the last decade, with a growing number of newly diagnosed cases identifying themselves as MSM [37]. Thus, initiating campaigns targeting these groups to raise awareness about free and anonymous HIV testing services would leverage the efforts of expanding VCT centres. Also, it would be combined with education as it is the most cost-effective way to prevent the disease spread [32].

This study has some limitations. Since there are none to moderate effect as well as supporting evidence [23, 24, 38], the impact of VCT on sexual risk behaviour is complicated [39]. Thus, studies are needed to provide the impact of VCT centres on risky behaviours within the Turkish population. Furthermore, increasing the number of “VCT centres” alone had a smaller effect on each compartment than “other testing sites” in the model. This result is attributed to the 37-fold difference in the size of low- and high-risk population which determines the number of eligible people for the corresponding tests. In this study, high-risk population was underestimated since the lack of national-level data. The risk level of individuals would be assessed at diagnosis by adapting a risk estimator tool. It would reveal the pattern of low- and high-risk patients at each test site and pave the way for more comprehensive analysis.

Another limitation of this study is the reliance on grey literature, including reports and non-peer-reviewed sources. However, in the absence of peer-reviewed data, grey literature enabled the analysis to progress. As more comprehensive data become available in the future, they should be prioritized.

We used calibration method to estimate the force of infection parameters in the model. The estimated values for high-risk individuals were 2.5 times higher than the low-risk individuals. The model results heavily rely on the force of infection for low-risk individuals based on the sensitivity analysis (Figure S6 in the supplementary). It is possible to undervalue the role of VCT centres due to underestimated difference in transmission between low- and high-risk populations. A larger risk disparity in infection between high-risk and low-risk populations would likely increase the relative advantage of targeting high-risk individuals through VCT, thereby leading to greater overall efficiency of this strategy. Consequently, our findings may represent a conservative estimate of the true impact and potential benefits of VCT in real-world settings, supporting its prioritization in public health planning.

The current healthcare system in Türkiye is good at providing treatment to HIV patients and linking them. However, the estimated diagnosis rates are far below the global targets, posing a significant challenge. On the other hand, it creates an opportunity to intensify prevention efforts against the burden of the disease. Given the critical value of enhanced VCT centres, our findings suggest that prioritizing VCT expansion represents a highly efficient strategy, though a formal cost-effectiveness analysis is needed to guide policy. However, this result does not imply ignoring testing efforts in other facilities. Future research could focus on resource allocation, costs and benefits of the programs to ensure the optimal allocation of the resources to the most cost-effective public health strategies.
