Fluid removal intensity in intermittent hemodialysis for acute kidney injury: net ultrafiltration rate characterization and outcomes
Authors: Lisa K Paxton, Tom Larsen, Timothy Fazio, Nigel D Toussaint, Rinaldo Bellomo, Emily J See
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn patients receiving continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) for acute kidney injury (AKI), excessive net ultrafiltration (NUF) rates >1.75 mL/kg/h have been associated with increased mortality, delayed AKI recovery and complications of hemodynamic instability. There is limited information on fluid removal practices and NUF rates in AKI patients treated with intermittent hemodialysis (IHD).We conducted a retrospective study of AKI patients who underwent IHD at our center between 2020 and 2023. The primary outcome was NUF rate, assessed as body weight-scaled, body surfaces area-scaled and unadjusted values. Secondary outcomes included mortality and measures of renal recovery at 90 days (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR], major adverse kidney events [MAKE], IHD dependence and days to liberation from IHD).We studied 74 patients (median age 62 years [IQR 46–71], median baseline eGFR 81 mL/min/1.73 m2 [IQR 71–96]). The median NUF rate was 5.61 mL/kg/h (weight scaled), 228 mL/h/m2 (BSA-scaled), and 462 mL/h (unadjusted). Higher fluid removal rates were observed in patients with better baseline kidney function, while older age, diabetes and hypertension were associated with lower rates. No significant differences in AKI recovery, dialysis dependence, or change in renal function at 90 days were observed between high and low NUF groups. By 90 days, 45 patients (60.8%) had developed a MAKE.Among AKI patients, median NUF rates during IHD was higher than reported for CRRT and influenced by comorbidity and pre-morbid eGFR. At 90 days, MAKE was common, two thirds of patients had a > 25% eGFR reduction, and one in 12 had died.Keywords: Acute kidney injury, renal replacement therapy, intermittent hemodialysis, net ultrafiltration, renal recovery, major adverse kidney events

Introduction

There is global recognition of the increasing incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI) and its association with short- and long-term morbidity and mortality [1,2]. In the absence of targeted therapies, renal replacement therapy (RRT) remains the mainstay of supportive management for severe AKI. It is plausible that elements of the RRT prescription and its delivery could impact AKI patient outcomes, including the likelihood of survival and renal recovery. In this regard, secondary analyses of randomized trials have reported associations between RRT modality and timing of RRT initiation and patient-centered outcomes [3,4]. It is unclear whether other key elements of RRT prescription and delivery, including intensity of machine fluid removal, are associated with similar risk.

In the treatment of AKI, two RRT modalities are commonly utilized. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) provides slow and gentle removal of fluid and solutes, making it better tolerated by critically ill patients with hemodynamic instability. Intermittent hemodialysis (IHD) is delivered over 3- to 4-h, achieves more rapid and intensive clearance, and is typically reserved for patients with greater hemodynamic stability. Current evidence does not demonstrate superior patient outcomes with CRRT compared to IHD [5].

Intensity of machine fluid removal by RRT is operationally quantified by the net ultrafiltration (NUF) rate. The NUF rate is calculated as the total volume removed by ultrafiltration per hour divided by patient weight [6]. The optimal rate of fluid removal for patients receiving RRT for AKI is unknown. In patients receiving CRRT, NUF rates >1.75 mL/kg/h have been associated with delayed liberation from RRT7 and increased mortality [8–10]. Moreover, among patients receiving chronic IHD, high NUF rates (>13 mL/kg/hour) have been associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [11].

In current practice, there exists a large evidence gap pertaining to fluid removal for patients receiving IHD for AKI. Many AKI studies neglect IHD exposure and guidelines are not prescriptive regarding its delivery [5,12]. The distribution, effect of scaling, and predictors of NUF rate have not been systematically investigated, nor has the relationship between NUF rate during IHD and outcome despite its wide use in the management of AKI. Accordingly, we performed a retrospective observational study of AKI patients who received IHD to describe current NUF rate practice, identify predictors of high NUF rate, and assess whether differences in NUF rate were associated with patient and renal outcomes. We hypothesized that NUF rate with IHD would be higher than reported for CRRT; that demographics, comorbidities, and illness severity factors would be associated with NUF rate; and that higher NUF rate would be associated with adverse outcomes.

Methods

Study design

We performed a single-center retrospective observational study of patients admitted to a large quaternary center in Melbourne, Australia, between 1st August 2020 and 31st July 2023. Ethics approval was obtained prior to commencement (Melbourne Health HREC QA202265) and the need for informed consent was waived.

Study population

All hospitalized adults (aged ≥ 18 years) who received IHD for AKI during the study period were eligible for inclusion. AKI was diagnosed and staged using serum creatinine measurements according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines [11]. Patients were excluded if they were on chronic RRT prior to the index hospitalization, had preexisting stage 5 chronic kidney disease (CKD), or were a kidney transplant recipient. Additionally, patients were excluded if their final RRT modality was CRRT, since the association between fluid removal intensity during IHD and renal recovery could not be assessed in this group.

Data collection

Data pertaining to baseline characteristics and outcomes were obtained from the hospital-wide electronic medical record (EMR). Comorbidities were identified using ICD-10 coding algorithms and included cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, diabetes, hypertension, malignancy, chronic liver disease, lung disease and autoimmune disease. Admission characteristics of interest included unit (surgical vs non-surgical), type (elective vs emergency), intensive care unit (ICU) status, and use of CRRT and vasopressors. Etiology of AKI was determined from renal biopsy reports (where available) or inpatient notes.

Baseline kidney function was defined as the lowest creatinine value 7 to 365 days before AKI admission, as collected from the hospital pathology database as well as external pathology databases. Data pertaining to IHD prescription were extracted from the hospital EMR and included treatment time, total ultrafiltration, number of IHD sessions and patient weight. Intradialytic hypotension was defined as a systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg or a decline in systolic blood pressure of >20 mmHg during a dialysis session. For patients admitted to the ICU, degree of fluid overload was estimated as cumulative fluid balance divided by admission weight. For those who received prior CRRT, the median NUF rate was determined per patient. The key elements of MAKE (death, kidney function, and dialysis status) were extracted from hospital pathology and EMR data sources. Patients were followed for 90 days after commencement of IHD or until death, transfer to another dialysis facility, or loss to follow up.

Clinical outcomes

The primary outcome was NUF rate, which was assessed three ways: (1) scaled to body weight as the net volume of fluid removed in mL divided by the treatment time in hours and pre-dialysis weight in kilograms (mL/kg/h); (2) scaled to body surface area as the net volume of fluid removed in mL divided by the treatment time in hours and body surface area in m2; and (3) unadjusted, calculated as the net volume of fluid removed in mL divided by the treatment time in hours (mL/h). Where pre-dialysis weight was unavailable, the admission weight was used.

Secondary outcomes included mortality (30- and 90-day) and measures of renal recovery at 90 days (eGFR, major adverse kidney events [MAKE], RRT dependence and days to liberation from IHD). MAKE was defined as the composite of a 25% reduction in eGFR, RRT dependence, and death at 90 days.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were expressed as frequencies (n, %), means (standard deviation, SD), or medians (interquartile range [IQR]), as dictated by data type. Between-group comparisons were performed using the chi-squared test, unpaired t-test, or Wilcoxon rank sum test, as appropriate. Predictors of NUF rate were analyzed using univariable and multivariable linear regression models. Candidate predictor variables for potential model inclusion were identified from a review of the literature. The final multivariable model included all variables associated with the outcome of interest either on univariable analyses (p value <0.2) or based on biological plausibility. The association between NUF rate and outcome was explored by dichotomizing patients into “high” and “low” NUF rate groups according to the median value of the cohort. In sensitivity analyses, NUF rate was divided into quartiles. Multiple imputation by chained equations was performed to account for missing data. Data were analyzed using R Studio (R studio, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Between 1st August 2020 and 31st of July 2023, 74 hospitalized adults underwent IHD for AKI and were eligible for inclusion. As shown in Table 1, the median age was 61.5 years, 36.5% of patients were female, and the median baseline eGFR was 81 mL/min/1.73 m2. Baseline kidney function was not available for five patients. The most common comorbidities were hypertension, diabetes, and malignancy. Three-quarters of patients had been admitted to the ICU, two-thirds had received vasopressors, and more than half were mechanically ventilated. CRRT was delivered prior to IHD in all patients admitted to the ICU. The median NUF rate on CRRT was 1.12 mL/kg/h and did not differ according to whether patients subsequently had a high or low NUF rate on IHD. Patients with a higher IHD NUF rate tended to have more fluid accumulation at the time of IHD initiation.

Net ultrafiltration

The median NUF rate was 5.61 mL/kg/h scaled to body weight [IQR 4.34 to 7.55], 228 mL/h/m2 scaled to body surface area [IQR 161 to 323] and 462 mL/h unadjusted [IQR 400 to 636] (Figure 2, Supplementary Figure S1). There were no significant differences in the distribution of NUF rate by session or patient regardless of whether it was scaled to body weight, body surface area, or unadjusted. NUF rate distribution across strata such as age, baseline eGFR, hypertension and diabetes are shown in Supplementary Figures S2-S5 Factors associated with NUF rate (explored as a continuous variable) on univariable and multivariable linear regression analyses are presented in Supplementary Figures S6-S11. A higher baseline eGFR was associated with higher NUF rate across all definitions, though this did not remain significant on multivariable analysis.

Renal recovery

The median time from IHD initiation to liberation was 6 days and did not differ significantly between high and low NUF rate groups, regardless of when NUF rate was scaled or unadjusted (Table 3, Supplementary Figures S12-S14). Overall, 9 patients (12.2%) remained dialysis-dependent at 90 days. Of these, 8 patients had a biopsy-proven reason for slow recovery: 5 patients had glomerulonephritis and received concurrent immunotherapy, 2 patients had cortical necrosis, and 1 patient had paraprotein-related AKI. The remaining patient was diagnosed clinically with hepatorenal syndrome. In patients who were successfully liberated from RRT, there was no significant difference in eGFR change at 90 days between high (−29%) and low (−25%) NUF rate groups (p = 0.55).

Major adverse kidney events

By 90 days, 45 patients (60.8%) had developed a MAKE, including 24 patients (64.8%) in the low NUF rate group and 21 patients (56.7%) in the high NUF rate group. The most common type of MAKE was a 25% reduction in eGFR (n = 40, 66%). A total of 6 patients (8.1%) died within 90 days of commencing RRT, including five who had a NUF rate of less than 5.61 mL/kg/h and were dialysis-dependent at the time of death.

Relationship between intradialytic hypotension and outcomes

We assessed outcomes stratified by intradialytic hypotension (Supplementary Table S6) and found that any episode of intradialytic hypotension was associated with an increase in the number of dialysis sessions, days to liberation from dialysis, and dialysis dependence at 90 days. However, we did not show any differences in MAKE and mortality according to intradialytic hypotension status.

Relationship between ICU admission and outcomes

We performed a sensitivity analysis comparing patients admitted to ICU versus those managed solely on the general medical ward (Supplementary Tables S7 and S8). Of the 74 patients included in this study, 57 (77%) patients were admitted to ICU. Those admitted to ICU were younger and had higher baseline eGFR. We did not see any significant difference in outcomes between ICU and non-ICU patients, although those admitted to ICU had a better median eGFR at 90 days.

Discussion

Key findings

This study provides important preliminary insights into NUF rates and outcomes in patients receiving IHD for AKI. The median NUF rate was 5.61 mL/kg/h scaled to body weight, 228 mL/h/m2 scaled to body surface area, and 462 mL/h unadjusted. NUF rate on IHD was considerably higher than on CRRT in our cohort. Older age, hypertension, and a lower eGFR at baseline were associated with a lower NUF rate. Duration of IHD as well as the number of IHD sessions did not affect NUF rate. IHD was most often preceded by a course of CRRT treatment. Time to liberation from RRT did not differ according to NUF rate; however, many patients had intrinsic forms of AKI, which affect renal recovery independent of fluid removal intensity. Two in three patients had a MAKE when assessed at day 90, with a marked reduction in eGFR being especially common. One in 12 patients died by day 90. In this cohort, we were unable to demonstrate that higher NUF rates were associated with adverse outcomes, likely due to low patient numbers and residual confounding.

Relationship to previous studies

There are no data on NUF rates during IHD in patients with AKI. Thus, existing literature on fluid removal intensity in AKI only pertains to critically ill patients receiving CRRT. In this patient population, NUF rates are much lower than in IHD (median NUF rate ∼1.38 mL/kg/h) [8]. Factors associated with a higher NUF rate (>1.75 mL/kg/h) during CRRT in prior studies have included younger age and higher baseline eGFR, which is in keeping with our findings [7,9]. Other factors reported to be associated with higher NUF rate during CRRT were female sex, lower body weight and use of mechanical ventilation [7,9]. In contrast to AKI patients, much higher median NUF rates (between 8.9 and 12.1 mL/kg/h) have been reported in the chronic dialysis population [11,13].

Regarding renal recovery, the CRRT literature has identified a consistent association between higher NUF rates and decreased liberation from RRT, even after accounting for the competing risk of death and patient fluid balance [7]. Although this finding was not replicated in our study, our cohort had a greater proportion of patients with intrinsic kidney disease and was underpowered to detect such a difference. Previous studies have reported an association between high NUF rate and mortality. A secondary analysis of the multicenter Randomized Evaluation of Normal vs Augmented Level (RENAL) of RRT trial of critically ill patients requiring CRRT found that NUF rates ≥ 1.75 mL/kg/h were independently associated with decreased adjusted 90-day survival, even after controlling for fluid balance [9]. Murugan and colleagues also found that a moderate NUF rate (1.04 mL/kg/h) was associated with lowest 1-year adjusted mortality [8]. Further analyses to assess for heterogeneity of effect found that both lower (<1.01 mL/kg/h) and higher (>1.75 mL/kg/h) NUF rates were associated with harm, suggesting a ‘U-shaped’ association [10]. However, these studies involved hundreds of patients providing a statistical power not available in our investigation.

Clinical implications

Intensity of fluid removal is a modifiable component of the RRT prescription. Therefore, understanding whether fluid removal practices influence patient-centered outcomes including renal recovery and survival is of clinical value. Our findings imply that NUF rates during IHD for AKI are lower than typical NUF rates for chronic IHD. Moreover, they also imply that NUF rates are modulated downward according to age, hypertension, and baseline eGFR. This suggests that nephrologists are cognizant about high NUF rates in some patient groups and adjust the prescription accordingly. Ultimately, the intensity of fluid removal should be guided by individual patient factors such as fluid responsiveness, hemodynamic reserve, degree of fluid overload, and ongoing fluid balance requirements. The likely association between fluid removal intensity, hemodynamic instability, and renal recovery warrants specific consideration. Although our observations imply that most patients recover early, such recovery is often incomplete by day 90 and that MAKE is high in this population.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides important preliminary insights into fluid removal practices for patients receiving IHD as their primary or secondary RRT modality for the treatment of AKI. The data were directly extracted from the EMR of a large academic center and contain granular detail on the prescribed NUF rate each session. They demonstrate that, in this setting, modulation of NUF is according to patient features and that, despite such adjustments, most patients either have a significant decrease in eGFR, fail to become dialysis independent, or die by day 90. Finally, by reporting NUF using three different scaling approaches, this study sets a methodological benchmark that future studies may adopt or critique.

These strengths must be balanced against several limitations. As, a single-center study with a relatively small cohort, it was not possible to make strong inferences about the association between fluid removal intensity and patient outcomes since we could not adjust for the heterogeneous nature of AKI etiology, physiological derangements, and patient characteristics. In particular, exploration of NUF rate in unique AKI phenotypes including sepsis-associated AKI warrants particular consideration in future work. Tolerance to ultrafiltration can vary markedly in those with isolated kidney failure and those admitted to ICU with multi-organ failure, this heterogeneity may have limited our ability to detect meaningful differences in the cohort. Next, given the observational nature of this study, residual confounding is likely, we were unable to collect complete data relating to patient fluid balance, urine output and the use of diuretics which would influence NUF rate. Our assessment of NUF rate across quartiles was likely limited by low patient numbers which may have affected our ability to detect a consistent pattern. Larger studies are required to further define the relationship between NUF rate and outcomes of relevance as it is unlikely to be solely linear. Additionally, NUF rates across CRRT and IHD modalities are not directly comparable, since one treatment is administered continuously, and the other over a 3- to 4-h session.

There is no single accepted definition of intradialytic hypotension, especially in the context of IHD for AKI, and our dataset did not include information on other markers of circulatory stress, such as mean arterial pressure, vasopressor drug infusion rates, or the onset of arrhythmias, which may have provided a more comprehensive impression of hemodynamic instability. Additionally, we did not have reliable information on whether episodes of intradialytic hypotension involved intervention, and if so, what the intervention was, which may have influenced our findings. Whilst we found that intradialytic hypotension was associated with a delayed time to liberation from IHD, we interpret these associations with caution, as patients who remained on IHD for longer accrued more sessions of dialysis increasing the opportunity to develop intradialytic hypotension, hence introducing a time-dependent confounding. Despite these limitations, our findings are hypothesis-generating and would be of particular interest to explore in a larger, prospective cohort.

Conclusions

In summary, fluid removal practices in IHD for the management of AKI indicate a median rate of NUF of 5.61 mL/kg/h, 462 mL/h and 228 mL/h/m2, which is further modulated according to age, history of hypertension and baseline eGFR. The duration and number of IHD sessions do not appear to impact upon the NUF rate in this cohort. In this unique population and with such an approach, the majority of patients had either died, failed to recover to dialysis independence, or had persistent loss of eGFR >25% at 90 days. Further investigations into how these outcomes can be improved by optimizing modifiable IHD related factors such as NUF requires larger cohorts. However, our findings provide the initial epidemiologic background for future studies.
