Prefixoids ≠ ≠ prefixes: not all inseparable complex verbs are (underlyingly) the same
Authors: Cora Cavirani-Pots, Michael Travis Putnam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The goal of this paper is to investigate the structural distinctions that exist between prefixoids and prefixes in modern Dutch, with an empirical focus on inseparable complex verbs (ICVs). Adopting a derivational, separationist approach to the syntax-morphology interface (Distributed Morphology), we maintain that the fundamental difference between prefixoids and prefixes is that the former are clitics while the latter are initially merged with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sqrt{\text {roots}}$$\end{document}roots lower in the first phase (vP) prior to when these complex predicates are labeled (by v). As clitics, prefixoids are adjoined to the left-edge of the first phase (with the phase head v). Our theoretical claims are further substantiated by data extracted from the Dutch Celex Lexicon, providing a detailed assessment of the categorical status of the stem that prefixoids and prefixes attach to, their ability to contribute a resultative interpretation, and their potential effects on the argument structure of the ICV.

Introduction

The aim of this paper is to investigate the main structural differences between prefixes and prefixoids in Dutch. To achieve this goal we take a closer look at the inventory of pre-verbal elements in Dutch, and advance the claim that beyond the standard division between separable complex verbs (SCVs) and inseparable complex verbs (ICVs) (see, e.g., Booij 1990; Roberts 1991; Neeleman 1994; Koopman 1995; Wurmbrand 1998, 2000; Zeller 2001; McIntyre 2002; Ramchand and Svenonius 2002; Biskup et al. 2011), the latter category consists of at least two distinct sub-classes, namely, (i) prefix-headed-ICVs (prefix-ICVs for short) and (ii) prefix oid-headed-ICVs (prefixoid-ICVs for short). A canonical example of a SCV is given in (1), of a prefix-ICV in (2), and of a prefixoid-ICV in (3). For the remainder of this paper, we focus on the distinction between prefix-ICVs (2) and prefixoid-IVCs (3).

The key morphosyntactic difference between SCVs on the one hand and both classes of ICVs on the other, is that in the former, the particle is separated (hence seperable complex verb) from the verbal part of the complex verb in V2-constructions (1),1 whereas prefixes and prefixoids appear together, i.e., are bound, with the verbal part in V2-position (2)–(3). The fundamental morphosyntactic distinction between prefixes and prefixoids in these ICVs is the fact that prefixes cannot be used as free morphemes, whereas prefixoids can (e.g., achter ‘behind’ can also be used as a standalone preposition). Given that they can appear as free morphemes, but seem to be used as a sort of prefix in ICVs, these elements have occasionally been labeled as prefixoids in the literature on Dutch morphology (Smessaert et al. 2019). The combination of a prefixoid and verb has also been called a pseudocompound by the same authors, due to the fact that these prefixoid-ICVs comprise two free morphemes and may thus be considered compounds, but at the same time they do not behave as canonical compounds. For example, in Dutch verbal compounds—a rather unproductive class of compounds, see, e.g., de Haas and Trommelen (1993), Ackema (1999), Smessaert et al. (2019)—the main stress always falls on the left-hand member of the compound, like canonical compounds in the language. In contrast, the main stress in prefixoid- and prefix-ICVs falls on the head of the complex verb.

The identical stress pattern, nonseparability, and other shared features between prefix-ICVs and prefixoid-ICVs have led authors of earlier studies (e.g., Zwanenburg 1997 and Blom 2005) to argue for a unified analysis of these ICVs as complex verbs with a prefixed element. However, these previous studies did not carry out a detailed investigation into the morphosyntactic and semantic properties of prefix-ICVs and prefixoid-ICVs, which we will do in this paper.

Two important empirical observations we make straight away are related to the prefixoid/prefix category and the (im)possibility of stacking these two pre-elements in a sequence at the left-edge of the complex predicate. With respect to the category of the prefixoid/prefix, it is noteworthy that diachronically all Dutch verbal prefixes stem from prepositions (de Haas and Trommelen 1993), whereas the prefixoids are derived from three categories, namely (i) prepositions, (ii) adverbs, and (iii) adjectives. An example of the first category was already presented in (3) above; examples of the second and third categories are given in (4) and (5), respectively.

An empirical observation that has, to the best of our knowledge, never played a significant role in the discussion of prefix(oids) in Dutch and related languages concerns the (in)ability to stack prefix- and prefixoid-elements in complex verbs. While the stacking of two prefixes (6) and two prefixoids (7) results in ungrammatical structures, prefixoid-prefix-‘stacked’ complex structures are well-formed (8-a). Interestingly, the opposite stacked order, i.e. prefix-prefixoid, is impossible (8-b).

These stacking data are important for two reasons: First, they show that prefixoids and prefixes cannot be assumed to occupy exactly the same position in the (functional sequence of the) v/VP, otherwise all stacking data would be ungrammatical. Second, the grammaticality of prefixoid-prefix stacking in (8-a) indicates that prefixoids attach to the verbal stem after genuine prefixes have been added/derived.2 We will elaborate on these data and how they can be accounted for by our analysis of the prefix-prefixoid distinction throughout the remainder of the paper.

We build our analysis of the structural distinction that exists between prefixes and prefixoids in ICVs on three theory-independent criteria: namely, whether they can (i) ‘verbalize’ (e.g., combine with) a nonverbal stem to form an ICV, (ii) add resultativity, and (iii) have an effect on the argument structure of the stem they attach to. In what follows, we demonstrate that prefix-ICVs and prefixoid-ICVs behave differently when it comes to these three criteria and should therefore not receive the same analysis (see Sect. 3.2). We classify prefixoids as clitics, which adjoin to the v phase head, whereas prefixes lexicalize a Res-head, to which the root of the ICV adjoins. As such, prefixoids and prefixes differ fundamentally from one another in terms of (i) how they enter the narrow syntax and (ii) their position in the first phase (i.e., the vP, the phase in which the event is built (Ramchand 2008). It is on the basis of these distinct structures that we build our analysis of prefixoid-ICVs and prefix-ICVs (see (9) and (10), respectively).

This paper is structured as follows: In Sect. 2, we provide a detailed empirical overview of Dutch ICVs. We annotated data extracted from the Dutch Celex Lexicon based on the three aforementioned criteria for prefixoid-ICVs (n = 195) and prefix-ICVs (n = 400). In general, the results of the annotation study show that prefixes have more impact on the syntax and semantics of the first phase compared to prefixoids; i.e., they show higher frequencies for all three properties. Furthermore, the results show a difference between P-based prefixoids and A(dv)-based prefixoids, in that the former still seem to impact the first phase (though to a lesser degree than prefixes), whereas the latter have virtually no impact on the syntax and semantics of the first phase. We integrate these empirical findings into a theoretical analysis in Sect. 3. Adopting the primary tenets of Distributed Morphology (Halle and Marantz 1993; Embick and Noyer 2007), we propose that the fundamental difference between prefixoids and prefixes can be reduced to the clitic status of the former (relying on Pair Merge to the phase head v), while prefixes are merged in narrow syntax prior to the completion of the first phase. Building on Folli and Harley ’s (2020) analysis of bimorphemic English verbs, we also argue that prefixes lexicalize Res, to which the root of the ICV is then adjoined, i.e., prior to verbalization. Section 4 concludes the paper.

Methodology

In this section, we describe how we generated the samples of prefixoid-ICVs and the prefix-ICVs. We furthermore make explicit for which syntactic and semantic properties these samples were annotated and how the annotation was executed.

Creation of samples

Based on a full list of prefixoids which occur in ICVs as found in the Algemene Nederlandse Spraakkunst ‘General Dutch Language’ (Haeseryn et al. 1997), we searched through the Dutch Celex Lexicon (Baayen et al. 1995) for all instances of Dutch prefixoid-ICVs. The total number of prefixoid-ICVs (unique word forms) was 195. Annotating these forms manually was a feasible task, and all prefixoid-ICVs were thus included for annotation. A random sample of prefix-ICVs was selected from the same corpus focusing on the three verbalizing prefixes in Dutch, namely ver-, ont-, and be-. For ver- we made a random selection of 150 verbs out of a total of 618 unique word forms, for ver- 150 verbs out of a total of 358 unique word forms, and for ont- 100 verbs out of a total of 192 unique word forms. The combined sample of the three types of prefix-ICVs is a total of 400 prefix-ICVs.

Annotation of properties

Before starting the actual annotation, we first annotated the category of the prefixoid in all the prefixoid-ICV datapoints. As already mentioned in the introduction, prefixoids in Dutch prefixoid-ICVs can be prepositions, adverbs or adjectives (Haeseryn et al. 1997). To date, however, the exact frequencies of these categories in prefixoid-ICVs has never been investigated. All annotations were done by the first author, who is a native speaker of Dutch.

Both the prefixoid-ICV and the prefix-ICV data set was further annotated for the three properties presented at the beginning of this section. Regarding Property I (whether the prefixoid/prefix can attach to a nonverbal stem), it is known that the Dutch prefixes under consideration are able to combine with a nonverbal stem (de Haas and Trommelen 1993). As such, these three prefixes can be said to have ‘verbalizing capacity’, i.e., are able to take a nonverbal stem and create a verbal output (see, e.g., Vanden Wyngaerd et al. (2022) for a Nanosyntactic account of Dutch deadjectival ver-ICVs, but see Neeleman and Schipper (1993) for a different analysis of such verbs, in which an empty verbalizing head intervenes between the nonverbal stem and the prefix). In (11), ver- combines with a nominal stem, in (12) with an adjectival stem, and in (13) with a non-lexical stem (henceforth NLS), i.e., with a stem that cannot appear without a categorizing affix. Apart from these three types of stems, the three prefixes under consideration can also combine with a verbal stem, an example of which is given in (14).

Until now, no investigation has been carried out addressing whether Dutch prefixoids in prefixoid-ICVs are also capable of attaching to non-verbal stems, like the examples of ver- in (11)–(13). Properties II and III were chosen for the same reason; i.e., both a resultative effect and an effect on the argument structure of the stem have been reported as core properties of Dutch verbalizing prefixes (Hoekstra et al. 1987), but these properties have not yet been systematically investigated for prefixoid-ICVs. All three properties additionally relate to the syntactic structure and semantics of the first phase and will thus be very informative for the theoretical analysis of Dutch prefixoid-ICVs and prefix-ICVs. For each annotation, the author consulted the Van Dale Dutch dictionary online (professional version), as well as other native speakers and the etymological dictionary when necessary. The data set has been made Open Access via the Open Science Framework, and can be found here.

We now turn to presenting how we annotated the data for the three properties under consideration. We first discuss the annotation of Property I, i.e., the category of the stem to which the prefixoids/prefixes attach. For each ICV, the stem was identified and its category was labeled as either V (verbal), N (nominal), A (adjectival), or NLS (non-lexical stem), with each categorization checked against the Van Dale Dutch dictionary. Stems were categorized as N or A only when they were unambiguously nominal or adjectival. In cases of verbal vs. nominal/adjectival ambiguity, we conservatively assigned these stems to the verbal category. For example, in the prefix-ICV ver-rook-en (lit.pref-smoke-inf), the stem may be analysed as either of category V or N; in such cases we label it as V.

A final note with respect to Property I concerns stems which contain other derivational morphology. For example, the prefix-ICV ver-fromm-el-en lit. pref-nls-suff-en contains the verbalizing suffix-el. Even though this suffix attaches to a non-lexical stem, this stem combined with verbalizing suffix-el forms the stem to which the prefix attaches, and hence that stem was labeled as V.

Second, we discuss the annotation of Property II, i.e., the resultative effect of the prefixoid/prefix on the compositional semantics of the ICV. In order to test this, we applied Wechsler’s test (2005) for resultativity both on the stem of the ICV in cases of a verbal stem, and on the ICV itself. We did this to find out whether the attachment of the prefixoid/prefix has a resultative effect. Let us first illustrate Wechsler’s test for resultativity, which is shown in (15) (Wechsler 2005, 256).

The sentence in (15) and its entailment show that we are dealing with a resultative predicate (‘flat’), expressing the final (result) state of the sentential object referent, namely ‘the metal’. As previously mentioned, it has been claimed that the Dutch prefixes be-, ver-, and ont- have a resultative effect (Hoekstra et al. 1987). To test this systematically and to extend the test to the potential resultative effect of prefixoids on the compositional semantics of the ICV, we applied Wechsler’s test to both the verbal stem and the ICV. As an example, to find out whether the prefix be- contributes resultative meaning to the prefix-ICV belopen ‘to tread’, we first applied Wechsler’s test on the verbal stem lopen ‘to walk’ (16) and then to the entire ICV belopen (17).

Given that the verbal stem is not resultative, but the prefix-ICV is, this prefix was annotated as adding resultativity. Note that the non-resultativity of lopen in (16) cannot solely be due to it not selecting an object DP; resultativity can also apply to the subject. An example of a resultative meaning targeting the subject is given in (18). We therefore also annotated for subject resultativity in the dataset.

Whenever a verbal stem was determined to license a resultative interpretation based on Wechsler’s test, the effect of the prefix/prefixoid on the ICV was annotated as not resultative.3 In the case of a non-verbal base, only the resultativity of the ICV could be tested. In those cases, we relied directly on the outcome of Wechsler’s test on the ICV. In sum, for ICVs with a verbal base, we applied Wechsler’s test both on the stem and on the ICV itself, and only in cases in which the stem was not resultative but the ICV was, we annotated a resultative effect of the prefix/prefixoid. For ICVs with a nonverbal base, we could only apply Wechsler’s test to the ICV itself, and annotated a resultative effect of the prefix/prefixoid if the ICV was resultative.

Last, we discuss the annotation of Property III, i.e., whether the prefix/prefixoid can alter the argument structure of the stem it attaches to. Similar to our analysis of Property II, we could only determine the argument structure of the stem when this stem was verbal. In all other cases (i.e., nominal stems, adjectival stems, and NLSs), we treated the prefix/prefixoid as altering argument structure, given that its attachment yields a verbal predicate. For ICVs with verbal cases, we annotated for the argument structure of the stem and that of the ICV, and furthermore the auxiliary selected by the stem and the ICV. If one (or both) of these factors showed a change after the attachement of the prefix/prefixoid, we annotated for that particular ICV that the prefix/prefixoid has an effect on the argument structure. For example, the verbal stem kletsen ‘to chat’ selects a subject but no object DP, as illustrated in (19). The prefix-ICV be-kletsen ‘to chat about something’ does select an object DP (20). This means that for the prefix-ICV be-kletsen we annotated that the prefix has an effect on argument structure.

When the dictionary provided multiple entries for a verbal stem/ICV, the first entry was used, or, in the case of the verbal stem, the entry whose semantics is closest to the semantics of the ICV. With the annotation techniques in place, we now move on to the results, which we discuss below.

Argument structure

Data summary

The results of the annotation of data from the Celex Lexicon for Properties I–III revealed the following patterns: First, with respect to Property I (the category of the stem to which prefixoids/prefixes attach), it was shown that prefixoids never attach to adjectival stems, very rarely to NLSs, infrequently to nominal stems, and most frequently to verbal stems. Furthermore, A-based and Adv-based prefixoids only attach to verbal stems. Prefixes can attach to all four types of stems.

Second, with respect to Property II (whether or not prefixoids/prefixes have a resultative effect on the compositional semantics of the ICV they are part of), it was shown that prefixoids add resultativity less frequently than prefixes. A-based and Adv-based prefixoids never add resultativity. The (absence of a) resultative effect of the prefixoid/prefix notwithstanding, the majority of ICVs in both datasets are resultative. This means that in those cases in which a prefixoid/prefix does not add or contribute to the resultativity of the event, it still strongly prefers to attach to stems with inherent resultative semantics.

Third, concerning Property III (whether or not prefixoids/prefixes have an effect on the argument structure of the stem they attach to), it was shown that overall, prefixoids have an effect on the argument structure of the base less often than prefixes do.

In summary, we have seen that with regards to the three investigated properties, prefixes outperform prefixoids in all three respects. Based on these observations, it seems to be the case that prefixes have a strong syntactic and semantic effect within the first phase, whereas most prefixoids do not. Within the group of prefixoids, A-based and Adv-based prefixoids have virtually no effect on the syntax and semantics of the first phase. They never attach to nonverbal stems, which indicates that they cannot verbalize, nor are they the cause of the resultative semantics of the ICV that they are part of. Although they do sometimes alter the argument structure of the stem they attach to, this mostly involves an object DP being selected as an obligatory reflexive (object) DP.

Theoretical analysis

In this section, we present our theoretical analysis of prefixoid- and prefix-ICVs in Dutch. In Sect. 3.1, we lay out our theoretical prerequisites, namely (i) the event decomposition as proposed by Ramchand (2018) and (ii) some key components of the analysis of Folli and Harley ’s 2020 account of verb framed and satellite framed languages, on which we will build our analysis. In Sect. 3.2, we propose that the crucial distinction between Dutch prefixoids and prefixes in ICVs can be accounted for based on three structural differences in their internal and external syntax. We argue therefore that (i) prefixoids are clitics, whereas prefixes are not, (ii) clitics are adjoined via Pair Merge, whereas prefixes enter the structure via Set Merge, and (iii) prefixoids are attached high (at the edge of vP), whereas prefixes are attached low (lexicalizing Res, to which a root is adjoined prior to verbalization).

Equipped with the key parts of Folli and Harley (2020) and the structural distinction between prefixoids and prefixes, we discuss our analysis of prefixoid- and prefix-ICVs in Dutch in Sect. 3.3.

Theoretical prerequisites

Lexicalizing resultativity

In our analysis, we adopt a fine-grained decomposed event structure of the first phase (vP) as developed and advocated by Ramchand (2008, 2019) among others. According to the tree structure in (51), the event semantics associated with the first phase consist of functional heads that establish (i) the initiator of an event (initP), (ii) the process (or dynamic nature) of an event (procP), and (iii) the result state of an event (resP). We adopt the functional projection ResP in our analysis of Dutch prefix-ICVs.

Folli and Harley (2020)

Folli and Harley (2020) present a head movement approach to Talmy’s typology of verb-framed (VF)/satellite-framed (SF) languages (Talmy 1975, 1985).4 Dutch, like English, is a SF language (Hoekstra et al. 1987), in which Result can be expressed both by the verb and by a satellite, unlike VF languages (e.g., Italian) in which Result can only be lexicalized by the verb. To illustrate that Dutch is a SF language, consider the examples below: in (52) the result state is lexicalized by the adjective, and in (53) by the particle of a particle verb. In VF languages, such structures are impossible.5

Folli and Harley (2020, 444) propose that the fact that VF languages are not able to lexicalize Result via elements other than the verb is due to a specific type of head movement parameter, namely a ‘Result-to-v’ parameter. More specifically, they argue that in VF languages, certain flavors of v (along with change-of-state v that select for a Result) come with an uninterpretable Res feature. This feature is specified with a movement-triggering EPP feature that forces Res to move to v. This means that Res cannot be lexicalized by an adjective or particle, for example. In other words, in a language that has this parameter switched on, Result can only be expressed by the verb. For SF languages, Folli and Harley (2020) argue that the lexicon contains both a change-of-state v with an EPP-bearing [uRes] as well as one without the EPP-feature. Put differently, Res can be moved to v in some cases (e.g., Result being expressed by the verb), but in other cases Res will be stranded and lexicalized independently from the verb.

With the key components of Folli and Harley (2020) in place, we can now move on to our proposal regarding the internal and external syntax of Dutch prefxoids and prefixes.

Structural differences between Dutch prefixoids and prefixes

In our account, prefixoids and prefixes essentially differ from each other with respect to (i) their internal syntax (i.e., whether they are X0 or XP-elements), (ii) their point of initial merger (i.e., vP-internal or at the edge of the ‘first phase’ (v/Voice), and (iii) what sort of Merge (i.e., Set vs. Pair Merge) is involved in introducing them into narrow syntax. We claim here that prefixoids are clitics, adjoined via Pair Merge at the edge of the first phase (Svenonius 2004; Epstein et al. 2016; Šereikaitė 2017; Manzini and Pescarini 2022), whereas prefixes are not clitics, and are Merged vP-internally, lexicalizing Res.7

Let us elaborate on the specifics of these distinctions. Turning first to our treatment of prefixoids as clitics, we propose that these elements are min-max categories, since, in principle, they are particles and there is nothing that would ban their existence according to Bare Phrase Structure principles (Chomsky 1995; Lohndal 2014).8 We illustrate this process of cliticization by adjunction of the prefixoid with the tree structure in (58):

In (58) we adopt the label of prefixoids as Appl(icative)P. Admittedly, this label is mostly chosen to reflect the modifier/adjunct status of the prefixoid in prefixoid-ICVs. The key distinction that we want to make here is that P- and/or A/Adv-based elements do not label the vP.9

An important debate in the recent literature on clitics concerns the exact nature of their derivation, i.e., whether clitics are derived via Set Merge (which treats the ‘head-like’ status of clitics as fundamentally derived by operations in the phrasal syntax) or whether they are derived via Pair Merge (as items merged directly with phase heads) (Epstein et al. 2016; Manzini and Pescarini 2022). Utilizing Pair Merge to derive clitics, i.e., prefixoids, accounts for their adjunct-like behavior and their label-less status (Wood and Marantz 2017). We therefore propose that Dutch prefixoids have clitic-status and are derived via Pair Merge.10

In contrast, for Dutch prefixes we assume that they are derived via Set Merge. We furthermore propose that they lexicalize the functional head Res, to which the root of the ICV is adjoined. This means that we propose that the prefix categorizes the root as Res at first. Only later will this Res-root complex be recategorized by v, following Folli and Harley (2020) (see Sect. 3.1.2).11 The merge position for prefixes is illustrated in (59), with the root head-adjoined to it.

To recap, although both prefixoids and prefixes are bound morphemes in ICVs, the former are clitics, and have a much looser connection with the predicates they modify than prefixes, to which the root is directly adjoined. This structural distinction contributes to our explanation of the stacking data presented in the introduction of this paper in examples (6)–(8), which will be discussed in more detail in the next section, together with the discussion of the derivations of Dutch prefixoid- and prefix-ICVs.

Deriving Dutch prefixoid- and prefix-ICVs

In this section we apply the components of our analysis as laid out in the previous two sections to our Dutch data provided in Sect. 2. First, we discuss the analysis of prefix-ICVs and how the empirical generalizations from Sect. 2 follow from the proposed structure in Sect. 3.3.1. Second, we do the same for prefixoid-ICVs in Sect. 3.3.2, followed by our analysis of the stacking patterns—as discussed at the beginning of this article—in Sect. 3.3.3.

Prefix-ICVs

We claim that all three Dutch prefixes, be-, ver-, and ont-, can function as Res-heads. All prefix-ICVs therefore have the structure given in (60), in which the root has adjoined to Res, and this complex has been head-moved and adjoined to v.

The fact that the three prefixes lexicalize Res explains that they contribute a resultative component to the compositional semantics of the ICV they are part of. Recall, however, that the prefixes do not always do so; they also combine with roots whose lexical semantics is resultative, which means that the prefixes are in principle not needed to render the ICV resultative. We believe this is not a problem for our analysis, though, because the resultative semantics of roots is not syntactic resultativity, which means that in narrow syntax there would not be a double instance of Res.12 An overlap between lexical features and syntactic features will not cause a crash of the derivation, and will be canceled out at LF.

Let us now discuss the other two properties which we investigated in Sect. 2. Recall that the three prefixes can combine with any type of stem, namely verbal, nominal, adjectival, and NLS. Our analysis can account for these data because in our structure, any root can merge with Res. After this, the Res-complex head-moves and is verbalized by v through incorporation. Recall furthermore our empirical finding that the prefixes often have an effect on the argument structure of the stem they attach to. What we mean with ‘the argument structure of the stem’ here is the idea that the conceptual content of roots are compatible/incompatible with certain structured meanings. These empirical findings are also in line with our analysis. That is, the prefixes lexicalize Res, which always select a DP argument. This means that in cases in which the root’s conceptual content is incompatible with the presence of certain argument, the use of the prefix will make it possible for the ICV to have this additional argument. With this in place, we now move on to the discussion of prefixoid-ICVs and how our analysis fares in explaining the empirical findings presented in Sect. 2.

Prefixoid-ICVs

Recall from Sect. 3.2 that we propose prefixoids in prefixoid-ICVs to be clitics, which lexicalize an Appl-head. The structure we propose for prefixoid-ICVs is repeated here in (61).

Due to their status as clitics, they cannot extend beyond ApplP. Let us start with the discussion of the A(dv)-based prefixoids. Recall from Sect. 2 that A(dv)-based prefixoids never add resultativity, never combine with a non-verbal stem, and almost never have an effect on the argument structure. We suggest that these are lexical elements that (i) contain roots and (ii) are categorized as ApplPs.13 These roots incorporate into Appl-heads, but adjoin with v (i.e., a phase head), which blocks them from starting their own internal functional sequence. Due to their status as a clitic version of these lexical items, these elements are more reduced—or ‘more minimal’—than their more elaborated adjectival or adverbial counterparts that appear in other syntactic contexts. This is, for example, clear from the stress pattern in ICVs; the prefixoid never bears stress, which often results in vowel reduction, compared to the full vowels in their lexical uses. Given that they are adjoined as clitics to v, it is expected that they only combine with roots that have already been verbalized, and that they do not alter the argument structure of that verbalized structure.14 Given that they do not lexicalize a Res head, it follows that they do not add resultative meaning to the compositional semantics of the ICV.

Turning now to the P-based prefixoids, we suggest that those that can have a resultative effect, namely aan ‘at,’ door ‘through,’ om ‘around,’ over ‘above’ and onder ‘under’ are memorized as indivisible roots, i.e., that univerbation between the root and prefixoid has taken place. We think this is a plausible analysis, given the low number of P-based prefixoids that add a resultative interpretation. We propose the same solution for the low number of cases in which a P-based prefixoid combines with a nonverbal stem or alters the argument structure; these numbers are fairly low and are thus very likely to be stored as indivisible units rather than as productive derivational patterns.

Prefixoid-prefix stacking

Finally, let us return to the stacking data that were presented at the beginning of the paper, which showed that only prefixoid-prefixing is allowed in Dutch ICVs. In our analysis, we have proposed that Dutch prefixes lexicalize a Res head. We adopted a structural analysis proposed by Folli and Harley (2020) for bimorphemic verbs in English (like destroy) which we extended to Dutch prefix-ICVs. Recall that in the structure for prefix-ICVs, the prefix is the Res head, to which the root adjoins. This complex then head-moves to v, which verbalizes the complex. Given our proposed analysis of prefixoids as clitics which adjoin to v, it follows that prefixoid-prefix-stacking is possible. For example, for the ICV with stacking, overbelichten ‘overexpose’, the structure would be the following (62).

In this structure, the prefix be- lexicalizes Res, to which the root of the ICV adjoins. The complex then moves to v, which then labels the entire complex as verbal. This structure is spelled-out, since the first phase is now finished. Building on Uriagareka ’s (1999) cyclic spell-out domains, we claim that the prefixoid in this structure is only adjoined (via Pair Merge) after the first cycle of spell-out. This is the only configuration in which two pre-elements can co-exist within the first phase. Res can only be lexicalized once; hence, prefix-prefix stacking is ruled out. The only aspect that the current analysis does not directly account for is the ban on prefixoid-prefixoid stacking, given that the type of adjunction we propose for prefixoids could be applied recursively. We leave this puzzle for future research; however, we want to tentatively suggest that in Dutch, v has only one dedicated edge position for modifier-like elements which are not XP-elements. Additional evidence in support of this claim can be found in recursive modification in the nominal domain in Dutch, which is quite common (de Haas and Trommelen 1993; Smessaert et al. 2019), but not so in the verbal domain (Ackema 1999). For example, verbal compounds can only consist of one modifying element (e.g., mast-klimm-en lit. mast climb inf) ‘to climb masts’, but not snel-mast-klimm-en lit. quickly mast climb inf). We leave a full analysis of the ban on prefixoid-prefixoid stacking in Dutch ICVs for future work.

Conclusion

The goal of this paper was to advance our understanding of the structural differences that exist between prefixoids and prefixes in Dutch ICVs. In the introduction, we discussed prefixoid-prefix-stacking patterns, an observation which seemed to suggest that prefixoids and prefixes occupy different structural positions. We conducted an in-depth annotation study of 195 prefixoid-ICVs and 400 prefix-ICVs, focusing on three properties which relate to the syntax and semantics of the first phase. The results of our annotation showed that, generally, prefixes have a strong effect on the syntax and semantics of the first phase compared to prefixoids, with respect to attaching to nonverbal stems, adding resultativity and adding an argument. Furthermore, within the set of prefixoids, we found a difference in behavior between A(dv)-based prefixoids and P-based prefixoids. The former never have an effect on the first phase, whereas some of the P-based prefixoids seem to have an effect, e.g., sometimes combining with a nonverbal stem, adding resultativity, or affecting the argument structure of the stem.

Approaching these general differences between prefixes and prefixoids in Dutch ICVs from a decompositional perspective to the syntax-morphology interface, we claimed that the key difference between prefixoids and prefixes can primarily be reduced to the clitic-nature of the former. Prefixes in ICV structures are not clitics, and lexicalize a Res head, to which the root adjoins. This takes place lower in the first phase, i.e., prior to the categorization of the Res-root complex by v. With this analysis we built upon the analysis of bimorphemic English verbs as suggested by Folli and Harley (2020) in their account of VF/SF variation. For the cases in which a P-based prefixoid does affect the first phase, we claimed that this is only apparent and not syntactic; we argued that in these (limited) cases, the root and prefixoid are memorized as one unit, and hence the resultative semantics/conceptual content compatible with certain argument structure comes from the root. Given the low occurrences of prefixoid-ICVs with resultative meaning, argument structure change or a nonverbal stem, it is not hard to conceive that their specific meaning components can be stored in an idiomatic way. Our analysis provides a straightforward account of the stacking behavior of these elements as well, even though future work is still needed to explain the ban on prefixoid-prefixoid stacking.

Another avenue for future research would be to expand our investigation to include other West Germanic languages which have ICVs, and in particular German and Afrikaans. Both languages have ICVs with prefixoids and prefixes (Van Huyssteen 2020; Wurmbrand 2000), but have never been investigated with respect to the core properties for which we annotated in our annotation study. Furthermore, particularly with respect to Afrikaans, recent work on comparing derivational morphology in Dutch and Afrikaans has shown that patterns that look similar on the surface, on closer scrutiny reveal crucial distinctions in underlying structure (see, e.g., Cavirani-Pots and De Villiers 2023). P-based prefixoids in ICVs in German and Afrikaans are discussed in the literature cited above, but whether A(dv)-based prefixoids exist and whether they originate from the same pool of A(dv)-elements in these languages requires investigation. A cross-linguistic West Germanic comparison investigating the possible effect of prefixoids and prefixes on the syntax and semantics of the first phase would therefore be a useful extension of the current study.
