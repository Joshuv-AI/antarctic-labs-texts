Synthetic streamflow generation for the ungauged Omo Kuraz watershed using MIKE 11 NAM parameter regionalization
Authors: Kassa Tesfaye Erenso, Abdella Kemal Mohammed, Tarun Kumar Lohani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study generated synthetic streamflow for the ungauged Omo-Kuraz Dam watershed in the Omo-Gibe River Basin, southern Ethiopia, using the MIKE 11 NAM conceptual rainfall–runoff model. Due to the absence of direct gauged data at the dam site, the downstream Omorate gauging station was utilized for model calibration (2010–2016) and validation (2017–2020), after which the optimized parameter sets were transferred to the ungauged watershed. Sensitivity analysis evaluated nine core hydrological parameters. The model achieved strong predictive performance, yielding coefficient of determination R2 values of 0.84 and 0.80 and Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE) values of 0.67 and 0.59 during the calibration and validation phases, respectively. The resulting synthetic streamflow indicated a mean annual discharge of 324.16 m3/s for the Omo-Kuraz watershed, compared with 510.53 m3/s observed at the Omorate station. Comparative analysis revealed that the observed streamflow exhibits a highly regulated baseline ranging from 260 to 1436 m3/s due to upstream river management, whereas the simulated design runoff captures a wider natural hydrographic variability between 83 and 2224 m3/s. To enhance spatial precipitation representation across complex terrain, the high-resolution (0.05°) CHIRPS satellite dataset was integrated to verify gauge-based inputs and map orographic patterns originating in the northern highlands. While the synthetic records are subject to inherent uncertainties from unmodeled reservoir operations and rainfall interpolation, the outcomes demonstrate that the MIKE 11 NAM framework provides a highly practical tool for streamflow generation in data-scarce catchments, offering key baseline data for water resources planning in the Omo-Gibe Basin.

Introduction

Hydrological models simulate the hydrological processes within catchments that can be used to evaluate the influence of livelihood on watershed hydrology. Simulation allows to address the impacts on water resources, management and ease of use of sustainable water resources. Rainfall-runoff modelling plays a significant role in water resources development, inter-basin water transfer, control and management of water resources, thus helping in decision-making, policy formulations and other benefits1.

Models, which include the Soil and Water Assessment Tool (SWAT), Hydrologiska Byråns Vattenbalansavdelning (HBV), and the MIKE 11 NAM have been extensively used in different parts of the world to predict streamflow and assess water balance components2–4. MIKE 11 NAM is a popular and commercial hydrological model that has been used to simulate rainfall-runoff processes and water balance5. Aherwar and Aherwar6 have compared two models such as the SCS-CN and MIKE 11 NAM for rainfall-runoff simulation in Shipra River basin of Madhya Pradesh, India and observed that MIKE 11 NAM model has a better performance than SCS-CN with four performance evaluation techniques. The NAM model used in MIKE 11 is a lumped conceptual rainfall runoff model that uses reduced storage components to simulate catchment hydrological processes.

Hydrological model comparison was conducted in rainfall and runoff process estimation using three models including the MIKE 11 NAM model, which resulted to promising model performances7. Across the globe, MIKE 11 NAM model has been often used for rainfall-runoff modelling and most of the results show that the model performed excellently for rainfall-runoff, especially in data-scarce areas8,9,Belachew, 201810–12,. Even though the model is commercially accessible and not as widely used worldwide, studies carried out in Ethiopia have shown that it works efficiently, particularly in gauged areas like the Upper Guder, the Shaya, and the Bilate River13–15. In the context of ungauged basins, synthetic streamflow generation has become a crucial area of research. Data from adjacent gauged basins may be used to calibrate and validate MIKE 11 NAM and the optimized parameters can subsequently be applied to ungauged areas to generate streamflow2. Numerous studies have successfully used this method at Nzhelele River and the Maybar experimental watershed, where parameters were effectively transferred from gauged to ungauged basins16,17.

In southern Ethiopia, the Omo-Gibe River Basin is a vital region for the development of hydropower and agriculture. However, the absence of gauging stations has made it difficult to effectively monitor and regulate streamflow in the downstream basin. The Omo Kuraz Dam is a diversion dam located in the southern portion of the Omo-Gibe basin, has significant effects on how water supplies are planned for the Omo Kuraz sugar development and other large-scale agricultural projects in the region12. To mitigate this challenge, this study uses a well-calibrated and validated MIKE 11 NAM model to generate streamflow for the Omo-Kuraz Dam by importing model parameters from the neighbouring Omorate Gauging station16,17. Omorate gauging station is a prime candidate for parameter transfer, this is due to its being located downstream of the Omo-Kuraz Dam and has many of the same hydrological characteristics. After calibrating and validating the MIKE 11 NAM model for the Omorate River gauging station, the optimized parameters were transferred to the Omo Kuraz Dam to generate a streamflow. The study assessed the water balance component of the gauged and ungauged rivers and confirmed the artificial streamflow produced for the Omo Kuraz Dam.

Furthermore, the Omo Kuraz Dam watershed generated streamflow will be instrumental datasets for efficient water resource management in an ungauged area. Using the calibrated and validated MIKE 11 NAM model coupled with the application parameter transfer technique, gives a viable foundation for simulating streamflow in data-scarce study areas. In Ethiopia, where many watersheds are still ungauged, this study will add to the expanding body of research on hydrological modelling in ungauged basins and has important ramifications for water resource management. The objective of this work is to synthetically generate streamflow for an ungauged Omo-Kuraz Dam via transferring the MIKE 11 NAM model’s parameters from a calibrated and validated Omorate hydrological gauging station. The novelty of the study is using an integrated methodological framework to reconstruct streamflow in river basins with limited data and the incorporation of high-resolution (0.05°) gridded CHIRPS data into the MIKE 11 NAM framework to systematically remove the lowland dry-bias of sparse ground networks around the ungauged site. The method creates artificial upstream flow series by combining downstream flow transfer methods with rainfall runoff simulation using the NAM model built into MIKE 11. This framework enables the estimation of hydrological conditions in catchments that are either poorly or not at all gauged by utilizing hydrological modeling and available downstream data. The methodology offers a workable option for hydrological analysis and water resource assessment in settings with limited data.

Materials and methods

Study area description

The Omo-Gibe River Basin, a vital water resource in southwestern Ethiopia, originates in the Shewa Highlands and flows southward as a principal stream within an endorheic drainage basin. Often viewed as a single entity, the system is formed by the Gibe River with its largest upper reach and major tributaries like the Gojeb River, which flows from the western forested shrublands of less-arable uplands to join the main channel. As the river traverses the tectonically active Great Rift Valley, it carves through complex lacustrine and alluvial sediments, descending from the fertile highlands near Jimma into increasingly arid, desert-like environments. The entire course remains within Ethiopian territory until it reaches its final terminus in the delta of Lake Turkana on the Kenyan border. Downstream of the Denchya River confluence, the Omo River undergoes a marked transformation, becoming a moderately flowing, meandering river traversing the floodplain towards Lake Turkana18. The Omo Kuraz Dam, a key water management infrastructure project is situated within a defined watershed of 49,000 km2 (19,000 sq mi). Climate data such as rainfall, temperature (maximum and minimum temperature), relative humidity, sunshine hour, solar radiation, and wind speed for each station were collected from the Ethiopian Meteorology Institute and the Ministry of Water & Energy are the sources of streamflow records, covering a 35-year span from 1986 to 2020. In accordance with the model’s specifications, all hydro-meteorological input data was processed and applied at a daily time step. Soil data and a Digital Elevation Model (DEM), both with a spatial resolution of 30 × 30 m, are used to depict physical land properties. The Ministry of Agriculture in Ethiopia provided the soil data, and the USGS Earth Explorer platform provided the topographic elevation information.

Methodology

The inverse distance weighting (IDW) interpolation technique was primarily used to fill in the missing climatic data. The IDW approach uses records from neighboring weighted stations to calculate the average value of the missing data points in a particular station20,21. The streamflow data at the Omorate gauging station was then filled in using a linear regression equation. The IDW approach was also used to interpolate the areal climate data based on each meteorological station’s data. Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW), which was chosen for its resilience, minimal data requirements, and capacity to maintain spatial variability and inter-station temporal consistency in data-scarce contexts, was used to estimate missing rainfall values. MIKE 11 NAM model description

The hydrological model MIKE 11 NAM was developed by the Danish Hydraulic Institute22 and has been adopted throughout the world as the most handy and user-friendly. An automated calibration strategy for the model was formulated by employing the model performance evaluation, water balance of the catchment and shuffled complex evolution algorithm23. This model was used to evaluate the rainfall-runoff process, synthetic streamflow generation, to understand water use effects, and estimated water balance components3,5,12–15,24. Owing to commercial availability the MIKE 11 NAM model is rarely used across the globe13,25. Input data for the MIKE 11 NAM model includes streamflow, rainfall, and PET time series datasets. The water balance components such as stream flow, AET, recharge, overland flow, interflow, and base flow were estimated using the MIKE 11 NAM model4,24. The catchment is depicted in the MIKE 11 NAM model as a lumped system made up of surface, root zone, and groundwater storage. Overland flow, interflow, and baseflow are the divisions of rainfall, and surface and soil moisture storage are used to model evapotranspiration. The watershed’s rapid and delayed hydrological responses may be simulated thanks to the model’s structure.

Model sensitivity analysis and performance evaluation

The MIKE 11 NAM model is embedded with nine parameters such as Umax (surface storage capacity), Lmax (root zone storage), CQOF (overland flow coefficient), CKIF (interflow routing constant), CK1,2 (overland flow routing constants), TOF (overland flow threshold), TIF (interflow threshold), TG (groundwater recharge threshold), and CKBF (baseflow recession constant) are some of the calibrated parameters14. The sensitive parameters were identified by changing one parameter at a time while keeping the rest eight parameters constant15. Calibration of the MIKE 11 NAM model was carried out using identified sensitive parameters tuned till optimal values were attained and performances evaluated by statistical indices in comparisons of observed and simulated streamflow. Model validation was put in place using an independent dataset, and without changing optimized parameters attained during the calibration period. The model performance was examined via statistical evaluation techniques. Besides the error statistics, the model performance will be also carried out with visual observations of the match between the observed and computed streamflow plots. The statistical indices were evaluated for testing the model performance26. Three statistical indices, including the Nash and Sutcliff Efficiency (NSE), Coefficient of determination (R2), and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) were used. The NSE27 offers a better understanding of the prediction efficiency of a given model. The value of NSE ranges from -∞ to 1.0, with a higher value showing good agreement between model simulations and observations (Kahsay et al., 2017). The ‘R2’ measures the ‘goodness of fit’ between the observed and computed data. It ranges between 0 and 1 with the higher values showing better agreement28,29 and the lower value shows disagreement between the observed and model-simulated data. Although R2 is frequently used to assess the goodness of fit, it is insensitive to the additive and proportional differences between model simulations and observations29. To overcome this discrepancy, NSE is often employed together with R2. Further, the RMSE of the data points was used which characterizes the variance of the estimation errors. As errors are squared before they are averaged, the RMSE gives relatively high weight to significant errors. The RMSE value ranges from 0 to ∞, and the value with lower value shows better performance29,30. Synthetic streamflow generation of the Omo-Kuraz River Sub-basin

Numerous studies followed hydrological model parameters transferring to simulate ungauged area’s streamflow and inflow to dams16,17,31. Using the MIKE 11 NAM model, parameters were transferred to an ungauged area from a nearby calibrated and verified gauged watershed in the Nzhelele River2. Also, MIKE 11 NAM model performance was observed efficaciously during simulating streamflow for ungauged areas16. Similarly, synthetic streamflow was generated for the ungauged Omo-Kuraz River gauging station using data from the nearby Omorate River gauging station. To do this primarily, the MIKE 11 NAM model was sensitive parameters identified, calibrated (1/1/2010 to 31/12/2016) and validated (1/1/2017 to 31/12/2020) for the nearby Omorate River gauging station. Secondly, the fitted and verified nine MIKE 11 NAM model parameters were transferred to the Omo-Kuraz Dam station. Thirdly, streamflow was simulated for the Omo-Kuraz Dam using MIKE 11 NAM and cross-verified by water balance components comparison, regression equation and correlation matrix at 95% confidence limits.

Validation procedure

Human activities distract the components of the water balance, including evapotranspiration (ET), runoff, interception, and infiltration32–34. The proportional contributions of the various water balance components demonstrate regional and temporal heterogeneity across the country35–39. The water balance analysis’s findings were cross-verified by regression equation and correlation matrix at a 95% confidence level5,24. This multi-phase validation procedure guaranteed the Omo-Kuraz Dam synthetic streamflow’s reliability.

Model scope and hydrological assumptions

The MIKE 11 NAM model is a deterministic, lumped, conceptual rainfall–runoff model that simulates catchment-scale discharge by routeing precipitation and potential evapotranspiration through interconnected surface, root-zone, and groundwater storage reservoirs23,40,41. Because the NAM mathematical structure is fundamentally formulated to represent terrestrial land-surface hydrological processes, it omits explicit parameters or nodes for reservoir volumetric storage, human-managed operational release rules, or backwater hydraulic routing associated with dam regulation. The generated streamflow hydrographs thus isolate the catchment’s natural, climate-driven rainfall–runoff response under the available hydro-meteorological boundary conditions. Accordingly, simulated outputs at the Omorate gauging station must be evaluated with the understanding that observed streamflow profiles particularly during the post-Gibe III operational phase reflect highly regulated flow regimes driven by upstream reservoir management that remain outside the structural configuration of this modeling framework.

Precipitation forcing and spatial processing via remote sensing (CHIRPS)

The Climate Hazards Group InfraRed Precipitation with Station data (CHIRPS) dataset was used as an independent precipitation source to evaluate the reliability of the gauge-based rainfall data under data-scarce conditions42. Daily CHIRPS precipitation data with a spatial resolution of 0.05° (approximately 5.5 km) were acquired due to their proven performance in representing the spatial distribution of precipitation over Ethiopia’s complex terrain43.

The Omo-Kuraz Dam and Omorate catchments were delineated from a Digital Elevation Model (DEM) using QGIS. Catchment-average precipitation was extracted from the CHIRPS grids using the zonal statistics tool to generate continuous daily rainfall time series for each watershed44,45. The extracted datasets were subsequently subjected to quality control, and temporal consistency checks before further analysis.

The primary rainfall input to the MIKE 11 NAM model was derived from the available ground meteorological stations. Missing rainfall records were completed using linear regression and inverse distance weighting (IDW) interpolation before the station data were spatially interpolated to produce watershed-average precipitation. The gauge-derived watershed-average precipitation was then independently evaluated using the CHIRPS dataset over the same study period (2010–2020). This comparison was undertaken to assess the consistency of the interpolated rainfall inputs and to verify their spatial representativeness over the delineated catchments. It should be noted that the rainfall inputs used in this study represent spatially averaged precipitation over the entire delineated catchments, rather than point measurements from individual meteorological stations or the average precipitation of the entire Omo-Gibe River Basin. Following this independent verification, the gauge-derived watershed-average precipitation was adopted as the hydro-meteorological forcing input for the MIKE 11 NAM rainfall–runoff simulations.

Umax denotes the maximum storage capacities of the upper soil moisture zones, and Lmax denotes the maximum storage capacities of the lower soil moisture zones, both measured in millimeters (mm). The runoff characteristics are governed by several coefficients: CQOF is the runoff coefficient for quick flow, while TOF, TIF, and TG are dimensionless parameters representing the time constants or drainage factors for overland flow, interflow, and groundwater recharge. Time-related movement is captured by CKIF, which measures the time constant for interflow in hours, and CK1,2, which represents the storage time constants for the routing of surface runoff. Finally, CKBF indicates the time constant for baseflow (hr), defining the rate at which groundwater contributes to the streamflow over time. The most sensitive parameters in the NAM model, according to sensitivity analysis, are Umax, Lmax, CQOF, and CKBF. These parameters regulate baseflow, soil moisture dynamics, and runoff generation, which has a significant impact on simulated streamflow and the overall Water Balance46.

Although the model achieved acceptable statistical performance during both the calibration and validation periods, the MIKE 11 NAM framework is a conceptual rainfall–runoff model that does not explicitly represent reservoir storage or operational release rules. Consequently, the differences between the simulated and observed streamflow during the validation period are expected and primarily reflect the influence of upstream reservoir regulation, particularly from Gibe III, rather than deficiencies in the calibrated parameter set or the conceptual structure of the model.

Parameter calibration and validation

The model was calibrated till the simulated streamflow showed good agreement with the observed values by altering parameters and followed by validation for further application of the MIKE 11 NAM model. The performance of the MIKE 11 NAM model was depicted as 0.84 and 0.80 for R2, and 0.67 and 0.59 for NSE during the calibration and verification periods, respectively. Daniel N. However, other metrics are recognized and suggested for further research, such as Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Percent Bias (P_bias). Sensitivity analysis and model performance evaluation

The sensitive parameters identified in this research were contrasted with those found in earlier research using the MIKE 11 NAM model5. The Lmax and CQOF were highly sensitive, the Umax was moderate, the CK1,2 and TOF were less sensitive, and the remaining four parameters were insensitive47. The identified sensitive parameters in the upper Guder catchment were Umax, Lmax, CQOF and CK1,215. The majority of the sensitive parameters that were found were similar to earlier research conducted in several global study areas12,48. During the calibration and verification phases, the MIKE 11 NAM model performance results in Ethiopia’s Bilate River basin were 0.83 and 0.76 for R2, 0.79 and 0.73 for NSE, and 4.0 and 5.2 for RMSE, respectively14. In the automated optimization, Umax (10.01 mm), Lmax (299.98 mm), and CK1,2 (10.00 h) all ended up near their operational limits. This is typical of parameter equifinality in data-scarce East African watersheds41. The model’s structural mechanism tries to maximize catchment retention against strong regional evapotranspiration. It aligns with performance benchmarks seen in scarce, heterogeneous Ethiopian networks49,50. Accordingly, the structural validity of this setup is confirmed by the highly stable baseflow recession constant (CKBF = 2416.2 h). That constant accurately captures the slow groundwater release found in the lower Omo Gibe rift hydrology.

During the calibration and validation periods, the performance assessment results were 4.21 and 5.24 for RMSE, 0.93 and 0.95 for R2, and 0.87 and 0.84 for NSE5. The simulated mean annual streamflow increased from 324.16 m3/s at the Omo-Kuraz Dam to 510.53 m3/s at the downstream Omorate gauging station, reflecting the progressive accumulation of runoff from the intervening drainage area. This downstream increase is consistent with the enlargement of the contributing catchment and the inflow from tributary streams.

The relatively high watershed-average precipitation estimated for the Omo-Kuraz catchment should be interpreted in relation to the delineated watershed rather than the downstream lowland environment. Unlike point rainfall observations or generalized basin-scale averages, the rainfall inputs used in this study represent spatially averaged precipitation over the entire catchment. The delineated watershed extends from the semi-arid lower Omo Valley into the humid Ethiopian highlands, where orographic uplift generates considerably higher rainfall than that recorded at low-elevation stations. Accordingly, the computed catchment-average precipitation is expected to exceed rainfall measured at individual stations and may also be higher than generalized annual rainfall values reported for the broader Omo-Gibe River Basin57,58.

The close agreement between the independently derived CHIRPS dataset and the interpolated gauge observations indicates that both datasets consistently represent the spatial distribution of rainfall across the study catchment. This independent comparison supports the suitability of the precipitation inputs adopted for the MIKE 11 NAM simulations. Nevertheless, uncertainties associated with rain-gauge density, spatial interpolation, satellite retrieval algorithms, and the complex topography of the basin remain unavoidable sources of uncertainty in watershed-average precipitation estimation. These factors should therefore be considered when interpreting the simulated streamflow and water-balance components for the ungauged Omo-Kuraz watershed.

The MIKE 11 NAM model was calibrated by adjusting the governing parameters within physically realistic limits to improve the agreement between simulated and observed streamflow. Model performance decreased slightly from the calibration period (NSE = 0.67) to the validation period (NSE = 0.59), which is expected for large catchments characterized by heterogeneous rainfall, limited hydrometric observations, and simplified conceptual model structures. It should also be noted that the observed streamflow used for validation was recorded after the operation of Gibe III, and therefore already reflects regulated flow conditions. Because reservoir operation is not explicitly represented in the MIKE 11 NAM model, part of the difference between simulated and observed discharge during validation is attributable to upstream flow regulation rather than deficiencies in the rainfall–runoff model itself. Overall, the model performance remains within the range generally considered satisfactory for conceptual hydrological modelling in data-scarce basins49,50. The relatively stable baseflow recession constant (CKBF = 2416.2 h) further indicates that the model adequately represents the slow groundwater drainage processes that contribute to streamflow recession in the lower Omo-Gibe Basin.

The monthly Design Synthetic Runoff (DSR) and Analysed Synthetic Runoff (ASR) series were compared to evaluate seasonal flow characteristics and their implications for water resources planning at the Omo-Kuraz Dam. The DSR series represents the historical runoff conditions developed during the original design studies for the upstream Omo River18, whereas the ASR series was generated in this study by transferring the calibrated MIKE 11 NAM model parameters from the Omorate gauging station to the ungauged Omo-Kuraz catchment.

The differences between the two-runoff series mainly reflect the different datasets and modelling approaches used in their development. The DSR was derived from historical hydrological analyses prepared for engineering design, whereas the ASR was developed using rainfall and streamflow observations collected during 2010–2020 through the calibrated MIKE 11 NAM rainfall–runoff model. The observed streamflow data used for calibration and validation spans the post-commissioning phase of the Gibe III reservoir, meaning the discharge records at the Omorate station inherently capture the structural impacts of upstream flow regulation. Since the MIKE 11 NAM model is a conceptual rainfall–runoff model and does not explicitly simulate reservoir storage or operational release rules, part of the differences between the historical design runoff and the analyzed synthetic runoff may reflect the influence of regulated downstream flow conditions rather than deficiencies in the model itself.

The analyzed synthetic runoff reproduces the seasonal pattern of streamflow while providing an updated representation of runoff conditions based on recent hydro-meteorological observations. The generated streamflow should therefore be interpreted within the conceptual assumptions of the MIKE 11 NAM model and the influence of upstream reservoir regulation on the observed calibration data. Future studies integrating reservoir operation modules or naturalized streamflow records could further improve the representation of regulated flows within the lower Omo-Gibe River Basin.

Study limitations

Although this research presents a useful framework for producing synthetic streamflow time series data in the ungauged Omo-Kuraz basin, some caution should be exercised when interpreting the results. Rainfall data inputs were obtained from available meteorological stations and interpolated spatially to provide watershed-average precipitation. While the interpolated rainfall was independently verified using CHIRPS satellite precipitation product data during a similar climatic period (2010–2020), uncertainties due to rain-gauge density, spatial interpolation, algorithms determining satellite retrievals and complex terrain remain.

MIKE 11 NAM is a conceptual rainfall–runoff model that handles the catchment component hydrological process simulations; it does not have an explicit reservoir operation representation. Thus, observed streamflow is an aggregate response of the catchment to precipitation rather than a representation of detailed reservoir storage and release dynamics. Since the validation was performed using streamflow observed after Gibe III commissioning, results from this analysis are therefore inevitably characterized by regulated flow conditions and some of the differences between simulated and observed discharge are likely due to reservoir regulation.

In addition, synthetic streamflow for the ungauged Omo-Kuraz watershed was generated through parameter regionalization from the Omorate catchment. While this approach is widely applied in data-scarce regions, it assumes hydrological similarity between the two catchments and therefore introduces some uncertainty into the transferred parameters. Future studies could further improve streamflow simulation by incorporating reservoir operation modules, additional hydro-meteorological observations, and distributed hydrological modelling.

Conclusions

Commercially available MIKE 11 NAM hydrological model was used to estimate water balance components to evaluate water use effects and streamflow synthetic generation for ungauged study areas. This study focuses on synthetic streamflow generation for the ungauged Omo-Kuraz Dam by transferring calibrated and verified MIKE 11 NAM model parameters from the closest downstream Omorate hydrological gauging station. After preparing input datasets, the MIKE 11 NAM model’s sensitivity analysis identified parameters such as Umax, Lmax, CQOF, CK1,2 and TOF, from nine parameters. The model performance evaluation statistical indices have resulted as 0.84 and 0.80 for R2, and 0.67 and 0.59 for NSE during the calibration and verification respectively. Spatial variability of rainfall across the catchment was mapped using the high-resolution CHIRPS satellite dataset which allows the validation of sparse ground-gauge observations by comparing satellite-derived rainfall against the long-term catchment mean. In contrast to previous studies embarrassed by the lowland location of gauges, this watershed-scale approach captured rainfall contributions from humid northern highlands and upstream flow networks. CHIRPS successfully captured ungauged orographic and convective precipitation processes that are often missed by conventional monitoring systems. Therefore, satellite-based verification reduced input uncertainty and provided a scientifically reliable basis for synthetic streamflow generation in this data-scarce basin.

The study also found a good correlation between simulated and observed flow, further demonstrating the model’s effectiveness in ungauged areas. Both the Omorate and Omo-Kuraz water balance analyses demonstrated significantly dominated by actual evapotranspiration loss across the watershed. Subsurface routes have a major role in controlling lateral water routing across downstream gradients, where interflow significantly surpasses direct overland surface runoff. This demonstrates that the basin is a highly sensitive hydrological system dominated by infiltration, where streamflow generation is nearly exclusively dependent on slow subsurface transmission as opposed to quick surface drainage.

This study demonstrates that the MIKE 11 NAM conceptual rainfall–runoff model can provide a practical approach for generating synthetic streamflow in data-scarce ungauged catchments through parameter regionalization from a nearby gauged watershed. The generated streamflow series offers a useful hydrological baseline for water resources assessment and preliminary planning in the Omo-Kuraz watershed, where observed discharge records are unavailable. The modelling results should, however, be interpreted within the assumptions of the conceptual model, particularly the use of spatially interpolated rainfall inputs and the absence of explicit simulation of reservoir operations. Future studies could improve streamflow estimation by incorporating reservoir operation rules, naturalized streamflow records, additional rainfall observations, and semi-distributed or fully distributed hydrological models to better represent spatial variability and regulated flow conditions. Such improvements would further enhance the reliability of hydrological assessments for the lower Omo-Gibe River Basin and other ungauged catchments with limited hydro-meteorological observations.
