Behavior of day-old turkey poults following routine treatments, with and without meloxicam administration, changes in a phased manner
Authors: Sheridan Alfirevich, Hamideh Keshavarzi, Victoria Sandilands, Alison Small
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPainful husbandry treatments, e.g. infrared beak treatment (IRBT), de-snooding (DS), and hot blade toenail trimming (TT), are routinely performed on day-old commercial turkey poults without analgesia. This study assessed the behavioral response following treatments with or without meloxicam. 150 day-old male Nicholas Select turkey poults were randomly assigned to five treatment groups: a low-pain control (IRBT, diluent); a high-pain control (IRBT, TT, DS, diluent); 2.5 (IRBT, TT, DS, 2.5 mg/kg meloxicam); 5 (IRBT, TT, DS, 5 mg/kg meloxicam); and 10 (IRBT, TT, DS, 10 mg/kg meloxicam) mg/kg meloxicam. As the study was designed to mimic a commercial setting, in which poults receive vaccinations via the Nova-Tech IRBT machine, there was no non-IRBT control. Poults were monitored by video for 24 hours post treatment, and behavior annotation carried out offline at half-hourly intervals. There were three distinct phases to the poults’ behavior. The period of highest activity and most variation between treatment groups occurred during the initial 6-hour observation period, after which activity reduced across all treatment groups and was at a low level from hour 13 onwards. Post-hoc analysis was carried out within Phase 1 (0-5 hours) and Phase 2 (8-11 hours). As there was no non-IRTB-treated control, it is unclear if the phased change in behavior was related to the treatments or reflects normal poult behavior post-hatching. There was not a clear meloxicam dose-response when all treatments were included in the analysis. However, within-phase analysis for Phase 1 shows poults in the high-pain control group were less active than some meloxicam-treated groups at certain time blocks Whilst the findings indicate a likely analgesic effect of meloxicam, pharmacokinetic data and more frequent behavioral observations, particularly in the first 6 hours, are required to further evaluate the impact on acute pain response.Keywords: Analgesia, de-snooding, pain, toe-clipping, Husbandry procedures, beak-trimming

Introduction

Day-old commercial turkey poults may undergo routine non-curative husbandry procedures at hatcheries, including infrared beak treatment (IRBT), de-snooding (DS), and toenail trimming (TT). There is ample evidence to suggest that these procedures are painful (Cheng, 2006; Gentle et al., 1991; Marchant-Forde, et al., 2008; Schwean-Lardner and Mench, 2018), however, they continue to be legally permitted in most countries, other than in some European countries (e.g. beak trimming has been banned in European countries such as Austria (Austrian Federal Government, 2004), The Netherlands (Government of The Netherlands, 2013), Sweden (Swedish Government, 2018) and Norway (Norwegian Government, 2009)), largely based on animal welfare justification (Riber and Hinrichsen, 2017). Commercial turkeys may be particularly prone to damage from injurious pecking, cannibalism and skin scratching with welfare outcomes reportedly worsened by having intact beaks and toenails (Fournier, et al., 2012; Struthers, et al., 2022).

In Australia, there are no analgesics registered for use in poultry and they are not routinely administered when painful husbandry procedures are performed. This is despite poultry being likely to perceive and respond to pain in a similar way to mammals (Douglas, et al., 2018; Gentle, 2011). Marchant-Forde et al. (2008) studied the impact of different beak alteration methods on day-old chick behavior. Regardless of whether hot-blade or infrared treatment was utilized, chicks with their beaks altered spent less time eating and drinking with fewer eating and drinking bouts, were less active and preened more, likely indicative of acute pain response (Marchant-Forde, Fahey and Cheng, 2008). Other studies assessing IRBT in day-old poults and layer chicks have found minimal difference in behavioral response between treated birds and controls (Gentle and McKeegan, 2007). Studies in other poultry species have shown that birds can respond positively to analgesics when experiencing likely painful conditions such as lameness or keel bone fractures (Caplen, et al., 2013; Mc Geown, et al., 1999; Nasr, et al., 2012).

The broader context of this research is to investigate the behavioral effects of non-steroidal analgesic therapy (Meloxicam) on male day-old turkey poults following routine hatchery treatments. For the purposes of this study, multiple painful husbandry procedures are performed in close succession on male poults to elicit maximum pain response, and this is likely indicative of commercial hatchery practice in countries where these procedures continue to be legally permitted. This preliminary study focuses on evaluating the behavioral profile of poults during the first 24 hours following routine treatments, with and without meloxicam administration. Due to resourcing constraints, a first -pass evaluation of turkey poult behavior in the first 24 hours post-treatment was conducted to inform planning of the next stages of the research. This paper reports on the phased changes observed in poult behavior as an outcome of that first-pass evaluation.

Materials and methods

Ethics Statement

The research was undertaken at a commercial turkey hatchery in Australia. The standards set out in the Australian Code for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purposes were followed and research approval was granted by CSIRO FD McMaster Laboratory Chiswick Animal Ethics Committee (ARA 22/09). The application was also reviewed and approved by the University of Edinburgh’s Animal Welfare and Ethical Review Board (OS08-22).

Experimental Design

The aim was to study the behavior of day-old poults following routine painful husbandry treatments with or without meloxicam administration. For this, 150, day-old male Nicholas Select turkey poults from the same prime aged breeder flock were randomly assigned to 5 treatment groups (n = 30 per group) as outlined in Table 1. The low-pain control group was infrared beak treated (IRBT) and diluent (i.e. sterile water) injected only. Poults in the high-pain control group were subject to all painful procedures (IRBT, de-snooded, DS and hot blade toenail trimmed, TT) and diluent injected. Poults in the meloxicam treatment groups underwent all painful procedures (IRBT, DS and TT) and were administered varying doses of meloxicam (2.5 mg/kg, 5 mg/kg or 10 mg/kg). As this study was designed to mimic a commercial setting, in which poults receive vaccinations via the Nova-Tech IRBT machine, there was no non-IRBT control. Dose rates were based on published studies in poultry. For example, meloxicam has been administered to broilers subcutaneously at a dose rate of 5mg/kg to determine its analgesic effect on lameness with positive results (Hothersall et al., 2016). As the faster metabolism of younger animals can hasten drug metabolism and elimination (Pugh, et al., 2017), it was considered important in this study to evaluate higher than published dose rates used in older poultry.

All poults were removed from hatchers approximately 12-24 hours post-hatching, manually vent-sexed and visually graded for navel quality according to standard hatchery procedures. Male poults with healthy navels were housed in groups of 10 in cardboard standard 70 × 50 cm hatchery boxes designed to transport 100, day-old poults per box (Figure 1). Thus, space allowance was 350 cm2 per poult. Each box contained two poults from each treatment group to minimize variation due to box position or conspecific influence. Once assigned to treatment groups and boxes, the poults were color marked on their heads using different colored water-based paint markers (POSCA Water-Based Paint Markers, Tokyo, Japan), using one or two separate dots for individual identification to facilitate behavioral monitoring and recording. Colors were randomized so that they did not correspond to treatment groups for blinding purposes. The sides of the boxes were raised and reinforced with cardboard to prevent the poults from escaping. Box flooring consisted of a thin foam mat to aid poults’ mobility and to absorb any waste and water spillage from drinkers.

Photo depicting set-up of boxes in the setter room for video camera monitoring.

Poults were individually weighed to calculate the meloxicam dosage based on their bodyweight, and their weights were recorded. Weighing poults individually was considered necessary due to likely weight variation between poults and to ensure an appropriate dose rate of meloxicam was administered. To minimize variability, the injection volume was kept consistent between poults and treatment groups at 0.15 mL per poult, by using sterile water for injection (B.Braun, Melsungen, Germany), either as the placebo for the control group, or as the diluent for all other groups where meloxicam was given (Ilium Meloxicam 5 Injection for Dogs and Cats; Troy Animal Healthcare, Glendenning, Australia). Sterile water was also thought to reduce the risk of the diluent altering the stability or composition of the meloxicam. Syringes of diluent and meloxicam were drawn up with a 25-gauge needle, and the subcutaneous injection was given at approximately the same time that IRBT was performed. This injection timing was designed to replicate conditions in commercial hatcheries, where meloxicam administration would be simultaneous with IRBT, if carried out using the Nova-Tech PSP machine.

IRBT was the first treatment performed, using the Nova-Tech commercial IRBT machine and standard settings. Following IRBT, snoods were then removed manually according to standard procedures. Next, TT of a single medial toenail on each foot was performed using hot blade and cautery, which resulted in the tip of the toe being amputated. Given the study was performed at a small commercial turkey hatchery, Microwave Claw Processor (MCP) technology was not available for this procedure. All procedures were performed by trained and experienced hatchery operators as per standard hatchery operating procedures. To minimize variation in treatment outcomes, the same person performed the same procedure on each poult. Immediately after completing all treatments for a box of poults, the box was moved to the climate-controlled, hatchery setter room, at 26-28°C, for 24 hours and positioned on the floor for monitoring (Figure 1). The treatment process for each poult in the hatchery's treatment room took approximately 30 minutes. The 24-hour monitoring period commenced between 11.30am-12.00pm. The monitoring period was limited to 24 hours to reflect the likely duration of effect for meloxicam in poultry based on published pharmacokinetic data. For example, Shao et al. (2022) administered meloxicam to layer hens via intravenous, oral and intramuscular routes and determined that there was a short onset to peak concentrations (0.8 h to 3.08 h) and a likely analgesic threshold of between 14.9 h and 19.2 h depending on the route of administration.

Continuous video camera monitoring (EXIR Fixed Turret Network Camera, Model DS=2CD2385G1-I, recorded to a Network Video Recorder, Model DS-7608NI-I2/8P, HiKvision, Hangzhou, China) was performed with one camera positioned over groups of 3-4 boxes.

Ad libitum water access was provided via a small manually filled bell-drinker, and ad libitum feed access was provided as a commercially-formulated turkey pre-starter crumble. The temperature and humidity of the room were monitored and recorded automatically throughout the trial using data loggers (Tinytag Ultra 2, Gemini Data Loggers, Chichester, UK) and temperature/humidity probes after which data was downloaded using the logger software. The room maintained a consistent temperature of 26-28 degrees and 62-65 % relative humidity. Although not standard or recommended practice, the lights in the room remained on for 24 hours, at a level of 20-50 Lux at bird level, to facilitate behavioral observations (use of night-vision cameras would have prevented differentiation of the color markings on each individual poult) and to ensure poults remained active.

At the end of the observation period, poults were humanely killed using carbon dioxide gas in accordance with routine commercial hatchery procedures. This was considered necessary due to the use of an unregistered product, under the constraints of the Australian Pesticides and Veterinary Medicines Authority (APVMA) Small Trials Permit 7250, at higher than published dose rates, with no data to determine an appropriate meat withholding period prior to slaughter.

Data collection

Video footage was reviewed offline using SmartPlayer software (Hanwha Techwin America, Teaneck, NJ, USA). An ethogram was constructed to monitor and record the poults’ behavior over a single 24-hour observation period, which was adapted from the ethogram used in Marchant-Forde et al. (2008) to assess behavior of day-old layer chickens following IRBT and hot blade beak trimming. The ethogram was informed by an initial pilot study using day-old turkey poults (data not shown) in which additional categories ‘pushing’, ‘jumping’ and ‘nuzzling’ were performed. ‘Beak wiping’ did not appear in the pilot study observations, so was not included as a category, and ‘Peck floor’ and ‘Peck feeder’ from the Marchant-Forde ethogram were combined into a single category ‘peck object’. Behaviors were recorded using MS Excel (Microsoft Inc., Redmond, WA, USA). The ethogram included active behaviors such as walking, jumping, pushing other birds, eating and drinking, as well as sedentary behaviors such as sitting/resting, sleeping, and preening (Table 2). Behaviors were also summarized into ‘total active’ and ‘total sedentary’. Each poult was monitored for 1 minute at 30-minute intervals for the first 12 hours, to reflect the period where peak meloxicam concentration was likely to be reached and then reduced to 1 minute at hourly intervals for the next 12 hours. The behaviors displayed during the 1-minute observation period were recorded if they were performed at any stage. There was no duplicate recording if the same behavior was performed multiple times. A single observer, a poultry veterinarian with experience assessing poult behavior, completed all behavioral observations to minimize observer variability.

Statistical analysis

Behavioral observation data were analyzed using R statistical analysis software package (R Core Team, 2021). The behavioral observation data were summated into hourly time blocks for the first 12 hours to provide a metric that could be aligned with the data from the 12-24 hour interval (observations were already performed at hourly intervals during this period). Data were analyzed as summated ‘total active’ and ‘total sedentary’ groupings due to infrequent observations of each individual behavior.

For statistical analysis of behavioral observation data (total active, and total sedentary), given the nature of the outcome variable—count data—and its distribution, Poisson regression was initially identified as an appropriate modelling approach. However, two common issues associated with such models are overdispersion (when the variance exceeds the mean) and zero inflation (a high proportion of zero values) (Elhai, et al., 2008). In this study, the proportion of zeros was less than 10 %, indicating that zero inflation was unlikely to be a concern or to meaningfully affect the results (Lee, et al., 2023). Therefore, each model was assessed for overdispersion. When overdispersion was not present, a Poisson regression model (using “glm” function in R) was used; otherwise, a negative binomial regression model was applied to analyse the outcome variable. The fixed effects in the model were treatment (levels 1–5, corresponding to different treatment groups as described in Table 1), time (4-time blocks; 1 = 0-6 h; 2 = 7-12 h; 3 = 13-18 h; 4 = 19-24 h), box (1, ..,15), and the interaction of treatment and time. For all analysis, 95 % confidence intervals were calculated with significance determined at P ≤ 0.05. Tendencies were noted where P < 0.10. An ANOVA test was employed to assess the significance of each parameter. To make comparisons between treatments, a post-hoc Tukey’s test was performed on the estimated marginal mean (MM) counts predicted from the statistical model.

The activity plot shows a phasic pattern in individual activity, with increased activity during the first 12 hours (see Figure 2). Therefore, to further understand behavioral differences and the impact of time during the first 12 hours where activity was at the highest level, a further analysis was conducted for each phase of total active: Phase 1 (0-5 hours) and Phase 2 (8-11 hours), with each hour defined as 0-1 h, 1-2 h, etc. Hours 6, 7 and 12 were excluded as those data were transitional between each phase. During this period of peak activity, treatment had no significant effect on sedentary behaviors and no further analysis was performed using this data. To make comparisons between treatments during each hourly observation interval in Phase 1 and Phase 2, a post-hoc Tukey’s test was performed on the estimated marginal mean (MM) counts predicted from the relevant statistical model. Hourly differences in behavior during the remaining 12 hours was not statistically analyzed post-hoc, as activity levels were extremely low.

Results

Overall 24 hours

Descriptive findings: The summated total number of active behaviors performed by individual poults at each time point was collapsed into 1-hour blocks for the first 12 hours and plotted (Figure 2). The activity of poults appeared to follow distinct phases over time. The initial 6-hour observation period showed the highest level of activity and variability between treatment groups. This was followed by a sharp decline in activity between the observed 6-8 hour period, which persisted until the 11th hour, whereby activity dropped again, with some variation between treatment groups maintained. For the remaining 12 hours, the behavior patterns between treatment groups appeared to converge and exhibit similar trends over time. The average of activity was 19.94 during the first 6 hours, 8.22 during the second 6 hours, and 5.86 during the final 12 hours.

Results of statistical analysis: Over the full 24-hour observation period (summarized as 4-time blocks), time and box significantly affected total active behaviors performed (P ≤ 0.01, Table 3A). However, treatment groups and interactions between treatment and time was not significant (P > 0.05, Table 3A). Therefore, the interaction was removed from the model and a post-hoc comparison was conducted among the treatment groups (Table 4).

Phase 1 (0-5 hours)

Based on the results of Poisson regression for Phase 1, treatment had no effect on total active behaviors although time and box were significant (P ≤ 0.05; Table 3B). The post-hoc comparison of treatments groups did not indicate any difference between treatment groups (Table 5). However, the interaction of treatment and time was close to significant (P = 0.05) over the full 5-hours period (Table 3B). Therefore, to further explore potential treatment differences between groups at each hourly time point during this phase, a statistical analysis following with post-hoc compression using Tukey’s test was performed. In Phase 1, statistically significant differences between treatment groups at each hourly time point were observed only during hours 2 and 4 (Table 6). During hour 2, poults in the high-pain control group were significantly (P = 0.04) less active compared to those in the 10 mg/kg meloxicam group. During hour 4, poults in the high-pain control were significantly (P = 0.02) less active than birds in the 2.5 mg/kg meloxicam group (Table 6). At hour 2, and hour 4, all other treatments were similar.

Discussion

Poults displayed three distinct phases of behavioral adaptation over the 24-hour observation period following treatments, which aligns somewhat with the findings of Roehrig and Torrey (2019), who observed shifts in behavioral profile after the first 6 hours post IRBT. In the current study, the higher activity level during the first 6 hours of observation could reflect increased noise and disturbance of poults during normal hatchery operational hours (Jones and Harvey, 1987; Zhao, et al., 2023), as observations commenced at approximately 11:30-12:00 and all staff left the hatchery approximately 6 hours later at 17:30-18:00. However, following the initial 6 hours, activity reduced with no apparent correlation with hatchery operating times. Staff would normally return the following morning at 5:00, approximately 17 hours after observations commenced. Activity levels appeared to increase slightly, but not statistically significantly, around this time but not to levels observed during the initial 12 hours. This suggests that the observed higher levels of overall activity in the first 12 hours post treatment are related to physiological processes, or any acute effect of the treatments having subsided, rather than to external stimulation. Alternatively, it is plausible that poults may have gradually acclimatized to their surroundings after an initial period of higher activity.

The study by Roehrig and Torrey (2019) investigated female poult behavior during the initial 24-hour period following placement on-farm to understand the relationship between feeding behavior and early mortality. Prior to placement, those poults were similarly infrared beak treated, and toe processed as per standard hatchery procedures. During the first 6 hours after placement, poults spent 26.21 ± 0.73 % of their time budget performing active behaviors (Roehrig and Torrey, 2019). After this period, their behavior shifted from active to resting with time spent active reducing to 7.71 ± 0.29 % for the remainder of the observation period (Roehrig and Torrey, 2019). A similar shift in activity at the 6-hour time point occurred despite poults on-farm likely being exposed to increased environmental complexity compared to the current study. As was the case in the current study, lights were kept on for the full 24-hour period and this could also be a potential cause of delayed lethargy or reduced activity (Roehrig and Torrey, 2019).

There was no non-IRBT control group in the current study that had not undergone any painful husbandry treatments at the hatchery. All poults in the Roerhig and Torrey study (2019) had also been subject to treatments. Therefore, it is difficult to conclude that the phased changes in behavioral pattern is related to either pain levels or treatment effects. Rather, the pattern observed may be indicative of normal poult behavior in the period post-hatching. Previous studies evaluating the effect of IRBT alone on behavioral response in both chicks and poults report conflicting results on activity following IRBTalthough observations during the initial 24 hours post-treatment are limited in these studies (Fournier, et al., 2015; Gentle and McKeegan, 2007; Marchant-Forde, Fahey and Cheng, 2008; Struthers, Fiss, Classen, Gomis, Dickinson, Crowe, Herwig and Schwean-Lardner, 2022). Gentle and McKeegan observed behaviors only in the first hour, and then on a weekly basis, while Marchant-Forde et al. observed behaviors for only 10 minutes on each of days 0-4 then weekly and Fournier et al. observed behaviors for only 10 minutes on each of days 1, 3 and 5. Struthers et al. did observe behavior by scan sampling every 15 minutes for the initial 24 h after IRBT in turkey poults, finding that IRBT poults spent more time resting compared to control turkeys (62.43 % of observations versus 54.29 % of observations). In the current study, we found no significant effect of treatment, although in hours 2 and 4 post-hoc analysis of marginal means indicated that the high-pain group may have been less active than the other treatments, which aligns with the findings of Struthers et al. (2022). Further, more detailed, annotation of behaviors within each phase of the current study may provide greater insight into treatment differences.

Despite statistically significant differences in activity between control groups and meloxicam treated groups at some hourly time points throughout the 24-hour observation period, there was no clear dose-response effect of meloxicam on total active behaviors that was consistent over time. It may be that turkey poults show individual behavioral differences or responses to meloxicam that mask its effects, but this is unlikely: poults in the initial period after hatching appear to spend considerable time sleeping and displaying sedentary behaviors which is potentially why it was difficult to establish differences between treatment groups (Roehrig and Torrey, 2019). Based on the initial findings, performing continuous or more frequent observations, particularly in the first 6 hours, is warranted to understand the treatment group differences. More information on behaviors performed could also have been recorded, including time spent performing each behavior, the number of bouts and the mean length of those bouts similar to the behavioral observation data collected by Marchant-Forde et al. (2008).

It was apparent in this study that the behavior of individual poults is influenced by the behavior of other individuals and the wider group in the box. The phased changes in behavioral pattern identified may be associated with this effect. Social learning and social transmission of behavior are well recognized in young chicks and this is a possible explanation for the box effect observed (Nicol, 2004). For example, one study found that chicks drank more in the presence of conspecifics with their intake the highest when conspecifics were also drinking (Franchina, et al., 1986). To further explore the cause of the phased change in behavioral pattern and potential social influence, poults could have been housed individually. However, individual housing may have reduced their social comfort, altering their behavior and compromising their welfare (National Research Council (US) Committee for the Update of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, 2011; Panksepp, et al., 1980). Alternatively, poults from the same treatment group could be housed in the same boxes and boxes treated as experimental units. This approach would significantly increase the number of poults required for the study to overcome the potential box effect and have implications with regard to animal ethics approval.

The method of applying meloxicam (i.e. by injection) was considered the most accurate dosing method available. While other studies have demonstrated that young birds can associate location with morphine or amphetamine-related drugs (e.g. Bronson et al., 1996) or have enabled birds to self-dose with analgesics (e.g. Nasr et al., 2013), these may rely on significant food or water intake (which is low in young birds), a period of learning that the ingestion of dosed feed/water has a positive effect, and also results in different dose intakes between birds (Bronson, et al., 1996; Nasr, et al., 2013). The time to peak blood concentration of meloxicam and duration of activity in day-old poults has not been elucidated and is unknown for each of the dose rates used in this study. There are likely to be significant interactions over time between peak pain levels and peak meloxicam blood and tissue concentrations as well as interactions between these and other factors, including influence of environment and social behavior. Pharmacokinetic data is required to understand the relationship between expected pain levels and behavior for each treatment group and refine focus periods for continuous or increased frequency observations.

Conclusion

Providing effective analgesia to commercial day-old turkey poults undergoing routine painful husbandry procedures at the hatchery is crucial for optimizing animal welfare outcomes whilst these procedures continue to be legally permitted and justified on animal welfare grounds. This research is novel and provides insight into poult behavior in the initial period following painful treatments being performed. The study suggests a phased change in the pattern of poults' behavior within the first 24 hours post-hatching and post-treatment, indicating potential changes in response to meloxicam administration at various dose rates. However, further investigation to fully understand this effect and provide recommendations on the use of meloxicam for analgesia in day-old poults. This is the subject of further study and will be reported in due course.

Limitations of the study

A resourcing decision had to be made at the start of the study - to sample observations over a 24-hour period or to use more intense or continuous observations over a much shorter time-frame. As the pharmacokinetics of meloxicam is unknown, we decided that the first evaluation of the footage would be the longer time-frame presented here, and based on these data to make decisions regarding the next stages of the research. As there was no control group that had not received any painful treatments, it is difficult to conclude that the phased changes were due to treatments alone and not reflective of normal poult behavior.
