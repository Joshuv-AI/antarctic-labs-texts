Mitral Annular Subvalvular Aneurysm With Leaflet Perforation and Left Atrium–to–Left Ventricle Fistulous Communication
Authors: Daniela Harsanyi, Ana Carolina Loayza Benchimol Barbosa, Jessica Silva Nicolau, José Roberto de Oliveira Silva Filho, Léa Maria Macruz Ferreira Demarchi, Antonio de Santis, Elinthon Tavares Veronese, Carlos Manuel de Almeida Brandão, Flávio Tarasoutchi, Paulo Rogério Soares, Thiago Luis Scudeler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMitral annular subvalvular aneurysm (MASA) is a rare cardiac condition with variable etiology and potentially life-threatening complications.Case SummaryA 35-year-old previously healthy man of African descent presented with progressive dyspnea and weight loss. Multimodality imaging revealed a posterior MASA complicated by leaflet perforation, left atrium–to–left ventricle fistula, and severe mitral regurgitation. Despite negative microbiological and histological results, morphological and intraoperative findings strongly suggested healed infective endocarditis rather than a congenital origin. Urgent mitral valve replacement with aneurysm exclusion was successfully performed.DiscussionThis case highlights the diagnostic and surgical value of multimodality imaging—including echocardiography, cardiac computed tomography, and magnetic resonance—in confirming MASA and supporting an acquired inflammatory etiology when infectious work-up is inconclusive.Take-Home MessagesMASA secondary to healed endocarditis should be suspected in the presence of leaflet perforation, atrioventricular fistula, and severe mitral regurgitation. Comprehensive multimodality imaging is essential for diagnosis and surgical planning.Key words: cardiac computed tomography, echocardiography, endocarditis, magnetic resonance, mitral regurgitation, mitral subvalvular aneurysm

History of Presentation

A 35-year-old man of African descent, having resided in Brazil for 1 year, was employed at a manufacturing facility under conditions that predisposed him to infectious diseases, including overcrowding and poor sanitation. He reported an 8-kg weight loss over the previous 6 months, along with progressively worsening dyspnea, prompting his evaluation in our emergency department.

On physical examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable, with normal vital signs. Cardiac auscultation revealed a high-pitched, blowing, grade 5/6 holosystolic murmur at the apex radiating to the left axilla, consistent with severe mitral regurgitation. Pulmonary examination identified bibasilar crackles. Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm and signs of left atrial enlargement. Laboratory tests revealed normal hemoglobin, preserved renal function, and normal protein C levels.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated severe mitral regurgitation and a cystic structure arising from the posterior mitral leaflet (Figures 1 and 2, Video 1, Video 2, Video 3, Video 4). Transesophageal echocardiography (Figure 3, Video 5, Video 6, Video 7, Video 8), cardiac computed tomography (Figure 4), and cardiac magnetic resonance (Videos 9 and 10) confirmed the presence of an aneurysm of the posterior mitral leaflet, complicated by perforation with left atrium (LA)–to–left ventricle (LV) flow and marked regurgitation. Blood cultures collected on the second day of hospitalization were negative. Serologic tests for HIV, syphilis, and hepatitis B and C were also negative.

Past Medical History

The patient had no known medical conditions, no family history of cardiovascular or infectious diseases, and was not taking any regular medications. He denied any prior illnesses before the onset of his current symptoms.

Differential Diagnosis

Mitral annular subvalvular aneurysm (MASA) is a rare but potentially life-threatening condition, more commonly reported in individuals of African descent. Its etiology remains unclear, but it may be congenital, traumatic, or inflammatory in origin. The differential diagnosis included mitral valve abscess, flail leaflet due to chordal rupture, congenital mitral diverticulum, and mitral valve pseudoaneurysm. However, the presence of a saccular structure demonstrating systolic expansion, diastolic collapse, and color Doppler evidence of direct LA-to-LV flow supported the diagnosis of MASA. The absence of previous cardiac surgery or congenital structural anomalies further reinforced the suspicion of MASA as a sequela of healed infective endocarditis.

Investigations

Chest computed tomography was unremarkable, showing no evidence of pulmonary infection, granulomatous disease, or neoplastic lesions. The patient exhibited no phenotypic features suggestive of connective tissue disorders. Laboratory tests were negative for the diagnosis of collagenosis. Blood cultures remained negative, and serologic testing for Coxiella burnetii and Bartonella species also yielded negative results.

Management

Given the high risk of hemodynamic deterioration despite the absence of active infection signs, the patient underwent urgent surgical mitral valve replacement. The procedure involved closure of the aneurysmal cavity at the posterior mitral annulus and implantation of a 31-mm mechanical mitral valve prosthesis, with preservation of the posterior leaflet. The surgery proceeded without complications, requiring 109 minutes of cardiopulmonary bypass and 92 minutes of aortic cross-clamping. Outcome and Follow-Up

After cardiac surgery, the patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery in the intensive care unit. Histopathological examination of the excised mitral valve did not confirm a definitive diagnosis (Figure 7). Histochemical stains for fungi, bacteria, and acid-fast bacilli were negative.

Twelve days postoperatively, after the initiation of anticoagulation with unfractionated heparin, the patient developed a pericardial effusion without clinical signs of cardiac tamponade. Pericardiocentesis was performed, yielding 150 mL of hematic, loculated fluid. Cultures from both the pericardial fluid and peripheral blood grew Klebsiella pneumoniae and Staphylococcus epidermidis, consistent with a nosocomial infection. Targeted intravenous antibiotic therapy with meropenem and vancomycin was initiated. Follow-up transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography showed no evidence of vegetations or intracardiac masses. The patient was transferred to the medical ward on postoperative day 27 to complete the course of antibiotic therapy.

Discussion

MASA is an uncommon structural abnormality of the mitral valve apparatus, traditionally associated with congenital weakness of the posterior fibrous mitral annulus, particularly in individuals of African descent.1,2 Although congenital attenuation of the annular fibrous tissue remains the leading hypothesis,2,3 alternative etiologies include trauma, rheumatic heart disease, tuberculosis, and infective endocarditis—particularly culture-negative infective endocarditis.4,5

In the current case, a young man of African descent presented with heart failure symptoms and was found to have a posterior mitral leaflet aneurysm complicated by leaflet perforation, severe mitral regurgitation, and an LA-to-LV fistula. Despite absence of fever, persistently negative blood cultures, and inconclusive histopathology, several features strongly support an acquired postinflammatory etiology—most likely healed infective endocarditis—rather than a congenital origin. The aneurysm's location adjacent to the posterior annulus, its fenestrated morphology, the fistulous tract, and intraoperative evidence of cystic degeneration and annular destruction are atypical for isolated congenital MASA and align more closely with the sequelae of an indolent, unrecognized endocarditic process.

From a pathophysiological perspective, inflammatory destruction of annular and leaflet tissue during infective endocarditis may lead to localized weakening, aneurysmal dilation, and eventual perforation, with fistulous communication forming through progressive tissue breakdown. This mechanism is consistent with the operative and imaging findings in our patient.

Culture-negative infective endocarditis accounts for up to 31% of infective endocarditis cases and is often associated with prior antibiotic exposure or infection by fastidious organisms such as Bartonella spp, C. burnetii, and Tropheryma whipplei.6 In our patient, extensive microbiological work-up—including serologies for HIV, syphilis, hepatitis B and C, Coxiella, and Bartonella—was negative. Although the patient denied prior antibiotic use, subclinical treatment before presentation cannot be entirely excluded. Intraoperative findings of cystic degeneration and friable tissue reinforce the hypothesis of a healed infectious process.4,7

Multimodality imaging was pivotal for diagnosis and surgical planning.8 The integration of transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography, cardiac computed tomography, and cardiac magnetic resonance provided comprehensive anatomical characterization. The identification of a saccular structure with systolic expansion, diastolic collapse, and direct LA-to-LV flow is pathognomonic for MASA, and these findings are consistent with previous case series in which submitral aneurysms have mimicked mitral valve prolapse, flail leaflet, or abscess.1,5,9

Surgical intervention remains the definitive treatment for MASA, particularly in the presence of structural valve damage, hemodynamic compromise, or risk of rupture. Repair can be technically challenging given the proximity of the aneurysm to the posterior mitral annulus and subvalvular apparatus, especially when complicated by perforation. In our case, successful management was achieved with closure of the aneurysmal cavity and mitral valve replacement with a mechanical prosthesis, ensuring complete aneurysm exclusion while preserving posterior leaflet continuity. Notably, the intraoperative findings of a fenestrated aneurysm and fistulous tract corroborated the preoperative imaging.

This case contributes to the limited literature describing the coexistence of MASA, leaflet perforation, and LA-to-LV fistula—a triad that is exceptionally rare. It underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach, early recognition through advanced imaging, and timely surgical intervention. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for MASA secondary to healed infective endocarditis when confronted with severe mitral regurgitation and complex subvalvular pathology, even in the absence of active infection.

Conclusions

This case illustrates a rare presentation of mitral annular subvalvular aneurysm complicated by leaflet perforation and LA-to-LV fistula in a previously healthy young man. Despite negative microbiological and serological findings, the lesion's anatomical complexity and intraoperative features strongly supported a postinflammatory etiology, most consistent with healed infective endocarditis. It emphasizes the pivotal role of multimodality imaging—including echocardiography, cardiac computed tomography, and cardiac magnetic resonance—in accurately characterizing complex subvalvular pathology, guiding surgical planning, and enabling timely intervention to prevent potentially fatal complications.
