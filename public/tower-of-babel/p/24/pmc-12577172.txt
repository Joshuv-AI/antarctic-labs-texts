Right ventricular vulnerability: a case study on anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity post-pulmonary embolism recovery
Authors: Cheavar A Blair, Bryana M Levitan, Tori Brown, Nakeya Dewaswala, Amit Arbune
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRight ventricular (RV) dysfunction may arise from several reasons, including pulmonary embolism (PE) or cancer therapy-related cardiotoxicity. PE elevates pulmonary pressures, straining the RV, which can cause dysfunction, while anthracyclines, though primarily recognized for impacting the left ventricle, can also impair the RV. The interplay between these conditions remains poorly understood, particularly the RV’s susceptibility to anthracycline cardiotoxicity after recovering from PE-induced strain. This case study highlights a patient who initially had PE-related RV dysfunction; however, data from three medical centers revealed RV recovery after PE, followed by RV failure, with subsequent left ventricular (LV) failure after chemotherapy.Case presentationA 41-year-old woman with a history of recurrent sub-massive pulmonary embolism initially treated with embolectomy and tissue plasminogen activator (tPA), was ultimately diagnosed with right pulmonary artery sarcoma. She underwent a pneumonectomy and pulmonary artery reconstruction with plans for chemotherapy and radiation therapy. The patient had RV dilation followed by dysfunction, which resolved on follow-up study after surgery. Subsequently, she received radiation therapy, along with paclitaxel, followed by doxorubicin. During chemotherapy, she developed progressive dyspnea and lower extremity edema. Follow-up transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated preserved LV size and function but revealed RV dilation and reduced systolic function. As her LV ejection fraction (LVEF) did not meet the criteria for cardiotoxicity, doxorubicin therapy was continued without modification. Over the next several months, her clinical status worsened, with severe RV dilation, continued lower extremity swelling, and functional decline. She underwent right heart catheterization, which revealed RF volume overload and preserved cardiac output. Subsequent cardiovascular MRI (CMR) revealed biventricular dysfunction. Within eight months of initial RV recovery post-pneumonectomy, she was diagnosed with biventricular failure due to anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity.ConclusionsThis case highlights the need to expand cardiotoxicity monitoring to include a thorough evaluation of RV function in patients undergoing anthracycline-based therapy. While our patients’ RV function recovered after pneumonectomy, the significant decline in RV function within eight months after anthracycline treatment indicates its vulnerability to cardiotoxic stress. Incorporating RV assessments into cardiotoxicity evaluations could allow for earlier detection and intervention, reducing long-term cardiac risks. Current guidelines focus mainly on left ventricular metrics; with our case, we emphasize the need for a more comprehensive approach to cardiac monitoring in these patients.Keywords: Anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity, RV dysfunction, Case report, Echo, Pulmonary embolism

Introduction

The susceptibility of the right ventricle (RV) to anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity, with and without pre-existing RV injury, remains poorly understood. Anthracyclines are cornerstone chemotherapeutic agents for treating a wide range of malignancies, including breast cancer, lymphoma, leukemia, sarcomas, and metastatic cancers. However, their use is frequently constrained by dose-dependent cardiotoxicity, which can ultimately lead to heart failure [1]. While previously considered irreversible, emerging evidence suggests that early detection of anthracycline-induced myocardial injury may enable recovery [2]. Current guidelines from the American Society of Echocardiography and the European Society of Cardiology define cancer therapy-related cardiotoxicity (CTRCD) based on left ventricular (LV) function, specifically as a reduction in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) exceeding 10–15% and an LVEF below 50% [3, 4] These guidelines prioritize LV dysfunction, often overlooking RV structural and functional changes that may be clinically significant yet remain undetected.

We report the case of a patient with a history of sub-massive pulmonary embolism (PE) and obesity, later diagnosed with right pulmonary artery sarcoma, who developed RV dysfunction likely secondary to elevated pulmonary artery pressures and RV strain (Fig. 1). After receiving multiple cycles of doxorubicin, cardiovascular MRI (CMR) revealed anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity. Despite pre-existing RV dysfunction, clinical assessments focused on a normal LVEF, which guided the ongoing administration of chemotherapy. This approach neglected RV-specific changes, which could have warranted treatment modification to mitigate further RV injury. This case underscores the critical need for comprehensive echocardiographic protocols that include routine evaluation of RV function during chemotherapy, particularly in patients with prior RV impairment. Furthermore, it highlights the necessity of research into RV dysfunction as an early and sensitive marker of cardiotoxicity, potentially preceding detectable declines in LVEF.

Case report

Patient 1

A 41-year-old woman with a history of sub-massive pulmonary embolism (PE) and obesity was diagnosed with right pulmonary artery sarcoma, which was treated with surgical resection and reconstruction. During her initial presentation with the sub-massive PE, given the complete occlusion of the right pulmonary artery, the patient was treated with catheter-directed embolectomy, thrombolysis with tPA, and sent home on direct oral anticoagulation. Six months from the index presentation, the patient presented to a local ER with similar symptoms. CT demonstrated similar sub-massive PE on the right pulmonary artery, extending into the left pulmonary artery. With the recurrent PE and further extension while being on anticoagulation, there was a suspicion of underlying malignancy, and she was referred to another hospital where she was eventually diagnosed with pulmonary artery sarcoma. The patient underwent surgical resection of her sarcoma and pulmonary artery reconstruction. The patient had a transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) before surgery, which showed RV dilation and dysfunction, which subsequently resolved on follow-up TTE before initiation of chemotherapy. She subsequently received radiation therapy along with paclitaxel, followed by doxorubicin. A few months after starting chemotherapy, she reported progressive dyspnea on exertion and lower extremity swelling. A pre-chemotherapy TTE was reported as normal in LV size and function, LVEF 56%, and qualitatively normal in RV size and systolic function. However, a follow-up TTE, 3 months later, indicated signs of RV pressure and volume overload. Despite the progression of her dyspnea, she continued to receive doxorubicin, with dexrazoxane eventually added to her regimen for the last 2 cycles. The RV changes on TTE were initially attributed to her earlier PE and sarcoma. However, it was important to note that the initial TTE after PE showed no RV function or pressure changes. Later, a repeat TTE revealed severe RV dilation, reduced RV systolic function, and normal LV size and function (Fig. 2). Repeat ECGs demonstrated a new first-degree AV block, incomplete right bundle branch block, and right axis deviation.

Echocardiogram during Chemo treatment (A-D), normal LV size and function, dilated RV. (E-F) Septal flattening in systole is indicative of RV pressure overload. G) Mild tricuspid regurgitation. H) Flow reversal in the hepatic vein consistent with elevated right-sided pressures. F)

An initial right heart catheterization (RHC) showed elevated right-sided filling pressures, normal left-sided filling pressures, mildly elevated mean pulmonary artery (PA) pressure, mildly elevated pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), and mildly reduced cardiac output (CO) and cardiac index (CI). This led to a working diagnosis of RV failure, likely due to the treatment and surgery for her pulmonary sarcoma. Her dyspnea and lower extremity swelling continued to worsen, prompting a repeat RHC three months later. The repeat RHC showed severely elevated right-sided filling pressures, normal PA pressure, low CO, and normal left-sided filling pressures. She was started on sildenafil for the suspected pulmonary hypertension and was then referred to the cardio-oncology clinic for cardiotoxicity evaluation.

Given her significant anthracycline dose, a CMR was performed with suspicion of anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity, which revealed reduced biventricular function (LVEF 18% and RVEF 27%), diffuse global hypokinesis, and end-diastolic flattening of the interventricular septum to the left, suggestive of RV volume overload and no late gadolinium enhancement. With a cumulative anthracycline dose of 495 mg/m², she was diagnosed with anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity and started on guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT), leading to partial recovery of left ventricular (LV) function, LVEF 47%. Had the earlier changes to her RV been attributed to chemotherapy, it might have been possible to have prevented both LV and RV failure.

Discussion

The RV has often been overlooked in evaluating CTRCD, with current guidelines predominantly directing clinicians to assess LV systolic function using GLS and/or LVEF. In this report, we present a case of anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity that initially manifested as RV dysfunction. This case involved a clinical dilemma for the diagnosis of RV dysfunction, which was initially attributed to a prior sub-massive pulmonary embolism and pulmonary artery sarcoma resection, followed by reconstruction. The situation is complicated since the patient received care at three medical institutions across two states. Upon detailed review of the records, it was concluded that the patient had initial RV dilation followed by dysfunction after the massive PE. However, the patient’s RV had recovered in size and function. Furthermore, with high doses of cardiotoxic chemotherapy in the form of doxorubicin, the patient had RV failure and subsequent biventricular failure. This case underscores the critical need for a comprehensive and quantitative assessment of the RV in the context of CTRCD. The RV has been a predictor of prognosis in heart failure patients. Data suggests that early RV dysfunction parallels LV dysfunction in breast cancer patients [2]. Additionally, a prospective study using 2D speckle-tracking echocardiography demonstrated that anthracyclines could lead to RV dysfunction in adult patients with lymphoma [3]. In the case we presented, RV dilation and RV dysfunction with subsequent LV dysfunction. We recommend careful attention to the RV and LV for early detection of CTRCD, especially in patients with prior RV insult with pressure overload, like PE, which is common in oncology patients. Furthermore, standardizing RV structural and functional evaluation, such as RV size, RV volume with 3D, RV GLS, and RV systolic function using fractional area change (FAC) or 3D RVEF, will allow for longitudinal follow-up and comparison.

Clinicians should be aware that anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity can impair RV structure, function, and mechanics [5]. Since anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity can cause changes at the cellular level, the RV is bound to be affected similarly to the LV and potentially even sooner, given the less muscle mass [2]. Recognizing RV structure, function, and IVC compressibility index in the cardiac assessment of patients undergoing chemotherapy, especially those receiving anthracyclines, is crucial. This case demonstrates the importance of early recognition of anthracycline cardiotoxicity based on RV structure, function, and IVC compressibility before the LV systolic function declines. Further research and more extensive studies are needed to evaluate the use of RV structural and functional changes for the initiation of treatment for chemotherapy-induced cardiac dysfunction before LV function declines.
