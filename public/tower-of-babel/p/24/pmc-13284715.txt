Structure basis for single-strand nucleic acid targeting by IscB and variants
Authors: Chengtao Xu, Qi Yang, Xiaolin Niu, Ailong Ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransposon-encoded IscB was defined as the evolutionary ancestor of CRISPR-Cas9. This compact RNA-guided endonuclease has since been engineered for genome-editing applications. We previously repurposed IscB and related Cas9s as efficient RNA editors by removing their double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) recognition module, the target-adjacent motif (TAM)/protospacer adjacent motif-interacting domain. Here, we report four cryo-electron microscopy structures of IscB, with or without TAM-interaction domain (TID), in complex with single-stranded nucleic acid (ssNA) targets. Structures reveal that, regardless of TID presence, IscB engages ssNA using the same mechanism. IscB initially facilitates formation of a 10-nt seed duplex with ssNA; further base-pairing is blocked by an alternatively positioned HNH nuclease that acts as a roadblock. In this intermediate state, neither HNH nor RuvC is competent for target cleavage. Only upon full duplex formation is the HNH roadblock dislodged by the duplex extension between guide RNA and ssNA. HNH and RuvC nuclease active sites become exposed as the result. A similar set of conformational rearrangements likely governs IscB activity during dsDNA target interrogation. Guided by the structural and mechanistic insights, we introduced mutations to either improve ssNA binding or ease HNH dislodging. Both approaches improved the RNA-targeting efficiency of IscB in vitro and in human cells.

Introduction

IscB is a compact RNA-guided endonuclease encoded by IS200/IS605 transposable elements. It is considered the evolutionary ancestor of the CRISPR-associated effector Cas9 [1–3]. While only two-fifths the size of Cas9, IscB shares a similar domain architecture, featuring an arginine-rich bridge helix and an HNH endonuclease domain inserted into different regions of the RuvC nuclease domain [4, 5]. In Cas9, the bridge helix nucleates the assembly of a ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex with two noncoding RNAs, the CRISPR RNA (crRNA) and trans-activating CRISPR RNA (tracrRNA) [6–11]. Double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) is accommodated into Cas9 through protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) interaction and unwound to form the so-called R-loop structure, where the target-strand (TS) DNA forms heteroduplex with the guide portion of crRNA and the non-target-strand (NTS) DNA loops out. TS and NTS are subsequently cleaved by HNH and RuvC nucleases of Cas9, respectively [12]. A large recognition (REC) lobe composed of multiple α-helical domains facilitates DNA unwinding and R-loop formation [9, 12–18]. In IscB, this REC lobe is replaced by the scaffold portion of ωRNA (obligate mobile element-guided activity RNA) [3], which is the functional equivalent of crRNA and tracrRNA. Target DNA recognition in IscB is initiated through TAM (target-adjacent motif) recognition, a mechanism that mirrors PAM recognition in Cas9 [19, 20]. R-loop formation and the DNA cleavage mechanism in IscB are mechanistically conserved as in Cas9 [4, 5, 9, 12–17, 21, 22]. IscB additionally encodes an N-terminal Pro-Leu-Met-Pro-motif-containing domain, which is important for RNP assembly [3].

Following its discovery and structure determination [4, 5, 23, 24], IscB has been extensively engineered for better genome-editing performance in mammalian cells [23–32]. For example, we improved the editing efficiency of an IscB protein from the human gut metagenome (O. geu IscB) by 30-folds in human cells by introducing a combination of eight-amino-acid (8-aa) substitution [29]. Recently, we further converted IscB into single-stranded nucleic acid (ssNA)-targeting enzymes by removing its TAM-interaction domain (TID). The resulting R-IscB variant is quite efficient in mediating messenger RNA (mRNA) knockdown, splicing perturbation, A-to-I editing, and trans-splicing [32]. Here, we aimed to gain deeper mechanistic understanding of its ssNA-targeting mechanism by determining high-resolution structures. We report the cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of the full-length IscB and the TID-less R-IscB, bearing their corresponding beneficial mutations and bound to single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) or single-stranded RNA (ssRNA). We confirm that the beneficial mutations for dsDNA targeting do not alter IscB structure appreciably; therefore, they likely function through improved nucleic acid contacts. Importantly, we found that RNA-guided ssNA recognition in both full-length and TID-less IscB proceed through a two-step process. IscB first promotes the formation of a 10-nt seed duplex between guide RNA (gRNA) and target ssNA; additional base-pairing was blocked by the alternatively docked HNH at this functional state. Neither HNH nor RuvC is capable of cleaving targets in the seed-duplex state. The HNH active site is oriented to the backside of IscB, and RuvC active site is blocked by the side-tracked gRNA. In the second snapshot, gRNA is fully base-paired with the DNA target, the HNH roadblock is dislodged to a non-blocking conformation, and the RuvC active site becomes exposed. Inspired by the structural observations, we introduced mutations into R-IscB to either favor ssNA binding or facilitate the seed-to-full duplex transition. A good percentage of variants showed improvement in target binding. Overall, this is a productive effort to improve R-IscB into a robust single-stranded genome editor.

Materials and methods

Plasmid construction

Engineered OgeuIscB and R-IscB-VR4 plasmids for RNP purification have been described previously [5, 32]. Detailed sequences are summarized in Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Table 1. ωRNA-V2 uses the optimized scaffold sequence and has a 16-nt guide at the 5´ side (5´-AAAAGAGTGAACGAGA) [29]. ωRNA was cloned into pUC57 between HindIII and EcoRI, and under the control of an lpp promoter and a T7 terminator. Mutations in R-IscB were introduced into the R-IscB-VR4 expression vector by polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based site-directed mutagenesis. For in vivo editing experiments, codon-optimized R-IscB-VR4 plasmid has been described previously [32]. Mutations and guide sequences were introduced via PCR mutagenesis. See Supplementary Tables for key sequence information.

Protein expression and purification

Plasmids encoding IscB (enOgeuIscB, R-IscB-VR4 and its variants) and ωRNA were co-transformed into Escherichia coli T7 Express plysY cells. The cell culture was grown in an LB medium supplemented with 0.75 g L-cysteine per liter culture at 37°C until the optical density at 600 nm reached 0.8. Expression was induced by adding isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside to a final concentration of 0.5 mM at 16°C overnight. Cells were collected by centrifugation and lysed by French press in buffer A (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM TCEP, 5% glycerol, 2.5 mM MgCl2) with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride. The supernatant after centrifugation was applied onto Strep-Tactin XT resin (IBA Lifesciences) preequilibrated in buffer A. Resin was then washed with 15 ml of buffer A plus 0.1 mM CaCl2 and 10U DNase I (Thermo Scientific), 20 ml of buffer B (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 1 M NaCl, 0.5 mM TCEP, 2.5 mM MgCl2), and 40 ml of buffer A. Proteins were eluted from the resin using 10 ml of buffer A plus 50 mM Biotin. The IscB-containing fractions were pooled, desalted, and further purified on MonoQ 5/50GL (Cytiva) with a NaCl gradient from 100 mM to 1M. The desired IscB fractions were pooled, concentrated, and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL; Cytiva) equilibrated with buffer C (50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM TCEP, 2.5 mM MgCl2). The first peak was collected, concentrated, and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C.

Biolayer interferometry

Biolayer interferometry (BLI) tests were conducted on Octet R8 (Satorius) in a BLI buffer of 50mM HEPES pH 7.5, 40mM NaCl, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 0.05% Tween-20, and 0.1% BSA at 37°C under a shaking speed of 1,000 rpm. Briefly, streptavidin biosensors (Satorius) were first loaded with ssRNA (500 nM biotinylated ssRNA in BLI buffer), baselined, associated in defined concentrations of catalytically dead R-IscB variants (30, 100, 300 nM in BLI buffer), and dissociated in the buffer with no proteins. Baseline correction was conducted in Octet Analysis Studio v13.0.3.52 software, and the corrected traces were plotted and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 11.

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Corning, 10-013-CV) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (Corning, 35-010-CV) and penicillin/streptomycin (MP Biomedicals). Cells were maintained in 5% CO2 at 37°C and passaged regularly upon reaching 80% confluency. Cells were seeded in antibiotic-free media 24 h before transfection to aim for ∼60% confluency at the time of transfection. Transfection was performed with jetOPTIMUS (Polyplus, 101000006) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell confluency measurement

Cell confluency measurements in HEK293T cells were carried out in 24-well format. Each well contained ∼1.3 × 105 cells. Specifically, 500 ng of R-IscB plasmids or control plasmids was diluted into 50 μl of jetOPTIMUS buffer plus 0.5 μl of jetOPTIMUS, then introduced into each well. After transfection, cell confluency and fluorescence signals were monitored for 72 h using the Incucyte Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius) and quantified using the built-in software. Default parameters were used for image processing.

Differential scanning fluorimetry

Two microliters of purified protein samples (WT-IscB or R-IscB, 1 mg/mL) and 2 μl of 50× SYPRO orange staining dye (Invitrogen) were mixed in a differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) buffer of 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, and 5 mM MgCl2 to a final volume of 20 μl. The melting curve measurements were performed using a CFX96 real-time system (Bio-Rad) with the following temperature protocol: hold at 25°C for 5 min, ramp from 25°C to 95°C at 0.5°C per 10 s, then hold at 25°C for 5 min. The melting temperatures (Tm) were determined from the negative first derivative of the relative fluorescence units with respect to temperature (dRFU/dT) plotted as a function of temperature.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 11 by Student’s t-test. P-values are indicated in the figure legends.

Cryo-EM sample preparation, data acquisition, and processing

DNA oligonucleotides for cryo-EM were synthesized (Integrated DNA Technologies). IscB was incubated for 20 min at 37°C with the target ssDNA/RNA substrates in binding buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, 2.5 mM MgCl2). Substrates were supplied at a two-fold molar excess to IscB (0.5 mg/ml final concentration). 3.5 μl of the mixture were applied to a Quantifoil holey carbon grid (1.2/1.3, Cu 200 mesh), which had been glow-discharged with 25 mA at 0.26 mBar for 30 s (PELCO easiGlow). Grids were blotted with Vitrobot blotting paper (Electron Microscopy Sciences) for 5.5 s at 4°C, 100% humidity, and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using a Mark IV Vitrobot (Thermo Fisher). Data were collected on a Krios G3i Cryo Transmission Electron Microscope (Thermo Scientific) with a Ceta 16M CMOS camera 300 kV, and a Gatan K3 direct electron detector. The total exposure time of each movie stack led to a total accumulated dose of 50 electrons per Å2 which fractionated into 40 frames. Dose-fractionated super-resolution movie stacks collected from the Gatan K3 direct electron detector were collected at a pixel size of 0.4125 Å. The defocus value was set between −1.0 and −2.5 μm.

Motion correction, CTF-estimation, blob particle picking, 2D classification, 3D classification, and non-uniform 3D refinement were performed in cryoSPARC v.4.7.1 [33]. Refinements followed the standard procedure, a series of 2D and 3D classifications with C1 symmetry were performed, as shown in Supplementary Figs S2 and S6, to generate the final maps. A solvent mask was generated and was used for all subsequent local refinement steps. CTF post-refinement was conducted to refine the beam-induced motion of the particle set, resulting in the final maps. The detailed data processing and refinement statistics for cryo-EM structures are summarized in Supplementary Figs S2 and S6 and Table 1.

Results

Cryo-EM reconstruction of ssDNA-bound IscB reveals two conformational states

We previously showed that the full-length OgeuIscB was also capable of high-affinity ssNA binding, albeit it strongly preferred dsDNA targets. The dsDNA preference was traced to its TID domain, deletion of which converted IscB into an exclusive ssNA binder [32]. To first understand how ssNA is accommodated in the context of full-length OgeuIscB, we incubated engineered IscB (enIscB) with a guide-complementary ssDNA at 37°C for 20 min before snap-freezing the sample for cryo-EM single-particle reconstruction (Fig. 1A and B; Supplementary Fig. S1) [29]. Two conformational states were resolved from this dataset (Fig. 1C–E and Supplementary Fig. S2). They differ significantly in the extent of the duplex between gRNA and target ssDNA (tDNA), the HNH nuclease location, and the RuvC status. As explained below, these structural differences are consistent with two sequential events in ssDNA binding, a seed-duplexed state (2.86 Å in resolution, Fig. 1D and Supplementary Figs S2 and S3) depicting a partially bound ssDNA target, and a fully-duplexed state (2.84 Å; Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. S2) depicting a fully recognized ssDNA target. The former state is reconstructed from ∼30% of the total single particles, whereas the latter from ∼35%; no other distinct conformations can be reconstructed to high resolution from the remaining ∼35% of the particles (Supplementary Fig. S2). These observations support our previous conclusion that IscB recognizes ssNA through seed-sequence recognition [32], and further suggest that a kinetic bottleneck in the form of a conformational checkpoint governs the transition from the seed-duplexed to the fully duplexed state.

The previously determined dsDNA-bound IscB structure (PDB: 7UTN) can be docked seamlessly into our newly obtained cryo-EM map (Fig. 2A). The TID domain of IscB adopts the same overall orientation even when TAM-proximal dsDNA is absent (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S4). While the overall conformation of the gRNA–tDNA heteroduplex is largely similar, the density corresponding to the first and last nucleotide of the 10-bp heteroduplex is significantly weaker in the ssDNA-bound structures (Fig. 2C). The first base pair appears to be disfavored by the nearby lip-like loop structure in the P1D domain of IscB (K400–T406), whose bulky residues impose geometric distortion on the local base pairs. This distortion is avoided when IscB binds dsDNA, as the TAM-proximal dsDNA pulls the downstream TS DNA further away from the P1D lip. We speculate that the P1D lip may bias IscB toward dsDNA binding over ssNA to some extent.

HNH roadblock and nuclease sequestration in the seed-duplexed state

The most distinctive structural feature of the seed-duplexed state is the alternatively positioned HNH nuclease. Previous structures captured IscB only in the fully R-looped state, in which HNH is either invisible due to high mobility (PDB: 8CTL) or bound to the gRNA–tDNA heteroduplex in a pre-catalysis state (PDB: 8CSZ) [5]. Here, we captured an intermediate state in which HNH serves as a roadblock, preventing the propagation of the gRNA–tDNA heteroduplex (Fig. 3A). HNH is stabilized to this conformation through contacts mediated by a linker connecting RuvC to HNH (R195–S221, named L1, Fig. 3B). The function of this linker was poorly understood, and its conformation poorly defined in previous structures. Here, we show that residues R195–M199 fold into a short helix that bridges an extensive network of hydrophobic contacts between HNH and RuvC (Fig. 3C). In particular, F196 at the tip of the helix extends into a hydrophobic pocket in HNH, formed by Y293, M228, N229, and L258. The rest region of L1 folds into an extended loop to mediate two layers of contacts against RuvC (Fig. 3D). The interactions include hydrophobic contacts from K205, V206, W209, and Y211 to the surface of RuvC.

Trapping HNH in the path of the gRNA–tDNA heteroduplex not only restricts the ssDNA recognition to the first 10-nt but also leads to three significant functional consequences. First, it orients the HNH active site toward the backside of IscB, preventing it from cleaving the partially base-paired DNA and RNA targets (Fig. 3E). In this new location, the HNH active site is flanked by the P2 stem of ωRNA, which may restrict access of non-target nucleic acids to HNH for cleavage. Second, the first 10 nt of the gRNA follow the line of positive charges along the bridge helix until encountering the HNH roadblock (G7 to A16). This stretch of gRNA base-pairs with the complementary nucleotides in ssDNA to form the seed duplex. The first and last ssDNA residues are poorly resolved in the duplex, suggesting that they are in an equilibrium of base pairing and unpairing (Figs 2C and 3F). Lastly, steric clash with the HNH roadblock redirects the gRNA toward the RuvC active site. A stretch of 3 nt (A4–A6) can be traced, and the density suggests that the gRNA travels across the RuvC active site uncleaved, possibly because of suboptimal orientation or nonoptimal coordination geometry (Fig. 3G). Although not cleaved, the gRNA physically occludes the RuvC active site from accepting ssDNA substrates for cleavage. Thus, neither HNH nor RuvC is capable of cleaving the RNA-guided target in the seed-duplexed state.

Conformational rearrangement and nuclease activation in seed-to-full duplex transition

The fully duplexed state depicts the aftermath of seed-to-full-duplex transition. The gRNA–tDNA heteroduplex extended to its full extent (15 bp modeled), and the overall conformation is similar to that depicted in the dsDNA-bound IscB structure (PDB: 7UTN; Cα r.m.s.d = 0.667Å; Fig. 3H and Supplementary Fig. S5). This is coupled with the disappearance of gRNA density from the RuvC active site and the dislodging of the HNH roadblock. In the seed state, the last α-helix of HNH and the following α-helix in RuvC are separated by a 90° elbow, in the form of a 4-aa linker (H294-A295-L296-S297, named L2). In the fully duplexed state, this elbow morphs into an imperfect α-helix, converting two halves of α-helices into a long, slightly curved α-helix, from HNH straight into RuvC (Fig. 3I). Concurrently, the contacts between L1 and RuvC that were present in the seed-duplexed state are lost, and this linker becomes disordered and disappears from the cryo-EM density map (Fig. 3J).

Notably, in the fully-duplexed state, the HNH densities are only visible at low contour level, and the local resolution only allowed rigid-body docking of the HNH core structure (V222-Y293). This suggests HNH is either highly mobile or only occupies this conformation transiently. These two possibilities are not mutually exclusive.

ssRNA-bound R-IscB structures defines the impact of TID deletion

TID domain deletion was the critical step in converting dsDNA-targeting IscB to ssNA-targeting R-IscB. To explore ways to further improving RNA-targeting activity, it is important to first characterize the structural impact of TID deletion. We therefore programmed R-IscB with an ssRNA target and determined its cryo-EM structure (Fig. 4A and B; Supplementary Figs S1 and S6). All structures contain uncleaved ssRNA target because R-IscB has negligible RNA cleavage activity and the incubation time did not allow cleavage reaction to reach equilibrium. As with full-length IscB, R-IscB adopted two conformational states, the seed-duplexed state and the fully duplexed state, each resolved from approximately equal populations of single particles (Fig. 4C–E and Supplementary Fig. S6). This suggests that neither TID deletion nor substrate identity (ssRNA versus ssDNA) significantly alters the conformational switching-based target recognition mechanism. The two conformational states superimpose extremely well with their counterparts in full-length IscB (r.m.s.d. in the 0.5–0.6 Å range; Supplementary Fig. S5), further indicating that TID deletion does not significantly alter the overall IscB structure. DSF results further revealed that removal of TID not only did not reduce the thermal stability of IscB RNP but appeared to improve its stability at high temperature (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Interestingly, the geometry of the gRNA–target RNA duplex is superimposable with the gRNA–tDNA heteroduplex observed in previously solved structures despite the additional 2′-hydroxyl in each nucleotide, and the duplex geometry deviates from the standard A-form with significantly widened major grooves. This observation suggests that the duplex geometry is dictated by contacts from IscB, including the line of positive charges along the bridge helix. Some local structural changes surrounding the deleted TID are noted. In an effort to ensure correct folding of the R-IscB RNP, we previously preserved the flexible linker from P1D to TID and fused it directly to the β-strand following TID, which zips to the edge of the β-sheet in RuvC. The R-IscB structure reveals, however, that neither the linker nor the β-strand matters for the structural integrity of R-IscB: while the entire P1D domain is well defined in the cryo-EM density, the linker beyond P1D (after Y433) and the fused β-strand are absent from the map (Fig. 4F). Avoidance of mismatch-containing off-targets in Cas9 and related systems is achieved through conformational checkpoint and physical contacts to the guide-target duplex. Since both properties are largely unchanged in the R-IscB structures, we expect that TID removal will not increase the off-targeting propensity in the resulting R-IscB construct.

Structure-inspired residue substitutions further improve ssRNA binding in R-IscB

Next, we sought to further improve the ssNA-binding activity of R-IscB based on the new mechanistic insights. The structures reveal that a P1D loop extends outward like a lip, straining the base-pairing geometry at the beginning of the seed duplex. We therefore introduced three clusters of mutations to either reduce the bulkiness of side chains bulk in this loop or shorten its size by one amino acid. Among them, a M402A/D403A double substitution was found to bind ssRNA target 40-fold faster in kon and 22-fold tighter in Kd than the parental version (Fig. 4G), as measured by BLI. We consider the association rate constant a more reliable indicator of R-IscB performance, as koff is too slow to be quantified with confidence.

Our structural analysis further revealed that even after 20 min of incubation at 37°C, ~50% of IscB molecules remained trapped in the seed-duplexed state, suggesting that eviction of the HNH roadblock is a rate-limiting step in target recognition. Lowering the energetic barrier of HNH roadblock eviction would, in theory, accelerate target recognition and improve RNA-targeting performance. Our structures reveal that F196 stabilizes HNH in the roadblocked conformation by inserting into a hydrophobic pocket at the HNH–RuvC interface. We therefore designed two substitutions, F196H and F196W, to weaken this ball-and-socket interaction. F196H was found to bind ssRNA target two-fold faster in kon and three-fold tighter in Kd than the parental version, which supports our mechanistic hypothesis.

As a consequence of TID removal, the linker from P1D to TID is absent from the EM density, and the linker connecting RuvC to P1D shows signs of elevated flexibility. To explore whether this elevated conformational flexibility might weaken the first base pair in the gRNA–target RNA duplex, as observed in the cryo-EM map, we attempted to reduce the footprint of the RuvC–P1D linker by removing one amino acid from it. The resulting variant (C378HKAN382 to AKIN) showed slightly improved binding behavior relative to the parental version (Fig. 4H). Additional tested designs that were either neutral or detrimental to ssRNA binding are documented in Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. S8.

We put forward a few R-IscB variants for further in vivo efficacy testing using two previously described assays in HEK293 cells [32]. In the RNA-guide base-editing assay, successful adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) editing in mRNA by R-IscB fused ADAR2dd would correct a premature stop codon to a read-through tryptophan, leading to restoration of mCherry fluorescence (Fig. 5A and B). After 48-h transfection, the variant with the removal of linker beyond Y433 (named rmNGG) showed statistically significant improvement in average fluorescence intensity (from 1960 ± 70.1 to 2138 ± 122 a.u.; Fig. 5C and D) and in the percentage of mCherry-positive cells (from 42.65 ± 1.50% to 48.92 ± 0.55%). Discussion

In this study, we attempted to provide the structural basis for ssNA recognition in the context of full-length IscB and its truncated ssNA-targeting variant, R-IscB. The structures assured that removal of the TAM-interaction domain does not alter the overall structure, nor the RNA-guided target-recognition mechanism. Based on these structures, we conclude that ssNA binding is a native function of wild-type IscB, assisted by an elaborate conformational switching mechanism to ensure stringent target validation. While this manuscript was under review, a parallel study confirmed the presence of this conformational switch during DNA interrogation and defined the sequential order of the seed-to-full duplex transitions by capturing an additional resting-state structure [34]. This new study confirms that the seed-to-full duplex transition mechanism is conserved across both dsDNA and ssNA substrates and thus represents an intrinsic property of IscB.

We consider the TID a gain-of-function domain in IscB that channels the attention of IscB toward dsDNA targets, as perpetual TAM-searching would prevent IscB from engaging in ssNA binding. Could there be naturally occurring RNA-targeting IscB systems? Such systems could have regressed from a DNA-targeting ancestor through the acquisition of TID inactivation or deletion mutations, and likely a RuvC inactivation mutation as well. We found no informatic evidence supporting this possibility (E. Koonin and K. Makarova, personal communications). However, the converse scenario is also conceivable, that the RNA-targeting systems may have preceded DNA-targeting IscB. This possibility is supported by the discovery of a lineage called IshB, which lacks both RuvC and TID domains but retains HNH. Under this model, IshB-like ancestors may not have acquired RuvC and TID domains, representing an earlier, RNA-compatible state from which the DNA-targeting activity was later acquired [3].

We reveal the presence of an elaborate conformational switching mechanism that serves two lines of important functions. It functions as a safety mechanism to autoinhibit the nuclease activities at the resting state, preventing IscB from cleaving random nucleic acids. Such activities would be quite toxic to the host cell. The blockage of RuvC active site by the gRNA is particularly noteworthy, as it is simple yet efficient. The conformational switch also serves as a quality control mechanism to prevent off-targeting on a partial-matching target. Base-pairing beyond the seed region is required to pay the energetic cost of opening the HNH gate. We note that these two mechanisms are conserved in principle in larger Cas9 RNPs, even though the specific molecular contacts are not. Early R-loop states were captured by cryo-EM for SpCas9 [21, 22], in which the authors show alternatively positioned HNH and occluded HNH active site. The RuvC active site has not been observed to be blocked by the gRNA; however, multiple domain insertions trap the RuvC active site so deep inside that it may be sufficiently sequestered that way. Overall, our work reinforces the idea that Cas enzymes are conditional nucleases activated not only by RNA-guided substrate recruitment, but also by autoinhibition in the resting state and conditional activation in the target-bound state. We have observed at least two types of conditional activation mechanisms, either an allosteric activation that controls the accessibility or geometry of the nuclease active site [35] or a conditional recruitment mechanism of the nuclease in trans [36].

Focusing on the effort to better use the utility of R-IscB, we have gained mechanistic insights from the comparison of full-length and R-IscB structures. Structural features straining the binding of ssNA have been identified. The realization of a steep kinetic and energetic barrier present in the target recognition process is instrumental. Initial efforts to incorporate these insights into further engineering have produced positive leads, which have been demonstrated to be effective in in vivo RNA editing experiments. Even though we focused this study on ssNA binding, we anticipate the mechanistic insights will be applicable to a large extent to the dsDNA recognition process by IscB.
