Impact of integrating body and organ donation education into anatomy teaching on medical students’ concepts of respect for life: a mixed-methods cluster randomized trial
Authors: Songyuan Yao, Juhua Ma, Xiaojun Guo, Hongping Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRespect for life is a core component of medical humanities and professional identity. This study evaluated the effects of systematically integrating body/organ donation education into an anatomy course on medical students’ concepts of respect for life, explored underlying mechanisms, and examined whether the intervention affected academic performance.MethodsAn explanatory sequential mixed-methods design was used, combining a cluster randomized controlled trial (RCT) with qualitative inquiry. From February to July 2025, eight Systemic Anatomy classes (N = 336) at Hubei University of Medicine were randomized by class (1:1) to an intervention group (n = 169) or a control group (n = 167). The intervention group received an 18-week integrated teaching module on body/organ donation and respect for life; the control group received routine teaching only. Background

Respect for life refers to an integrated set of cognition, emotional identification, and behavioral intention regarding the dignity, value, and rights of life [1]. It constitutes a foundational element of medical humanities and professionalism and profoundly influences medical students’ development of professional identity, adaptation to learning, cultivation of clinical empathy, and ethical decision-making capacity. Body and organ donation are cornerstones of modern medical education and healthcare systems. Body donation provides irreplaceable resources for learning real human anatomy in an authentic, complex, and tangible context, helping students develop spatial anatomical cognition and early clinical thinking [2, 3]. Organ donation, by contrast, concerns the continuation of life and public ethics and reflects social civilization and the evolution of ethical norms through its legislation and practice [4–6]. Against the backdrop of the Healthy China initiative and high-quality medical education development, a key reform agenda is how to embed the value of ‘revering life, respecting life, and protecting life’ throughout medical training while maintaining the quality of professional skills training [7, 8].

The anatomy course is a natural juncture where students first confront death and the boundaries of life. For many, the first entry into the dissection laboratory and the first encounter with a cadaver (‘silent mentor’) evokes complex emotions such as shock, tension, and avoidance [2, 9], and may trigger deep reflection on the meaning of death, dignity [10], and medical responsibility. If such experiences remain confined to technical training, students may adopt ‘de-emotionalized’ coping strategies and even develop value desensitization. Conversely, within a well-regulated ethical framework, commemorative rituals, donor narratives, family communication, and public advocacy can be used to link professional learning with life education, humanistic care, and professional identity construction. This can transform the ‘first confrontation with death’ into a ‘first understanding of physicians’ responsibilities,’ allowing respect-for-life concepts to be awakened, clarified, and progressively internalized through contextual experience [11–13].

In recent years, medical education worldwide has emphasized the medical humanities, empathy development, and professionalism, advocating experiential, contextualized, and service-learning approaches to facilitate value formation [7, 8, 14]. Education related to body/organ donation, with its high contextuality and strong ethical attributes, has been recognized as a high-quality vehicle for life education and professional spirit cultivation [8, 13, 15]. Many institutions have incorporated commemorations, volunteer outreach, legal/ethical instruction, and reflective writing into anatomy teaching or social practice courses to foster gratitude and respect for donors, rational understanding of death, empathic concern for patients, and a sense of civic responsibility [13, 16]. However, two gaps remain. First, implementation often relies on experiential summaries without replicable modules or validated evaluation tools—‘much is done, but effects are hard to demonstrate.’ Second, research frequently focuses on satisfaction or one-time surveys [15, 17, 18], lacking evidence from pre–post change, controlled comparisons, and mechanistic explanation. Thus, it remains unclear in what dimensions, to what extent, and through which pathways integrated teaching influences students’ respect-for-life concepts [19].

Accordingly, this study developed a systematic integration of body/organ donation education into anatomy teaching, including value clarification, contextual experience, and reflective internalization. Using a mixed-methods design, we tested changes in death cognition, gratitude and respect, transfer of humanistic care, ethical/social value identification, and professional responsibility/social commitment, and used qualitative narratives to explain students’ experiences and the roles of key teaching components. The aim was to provide evaluable and scalable evidence for life education centered on the ‘silent mentor’ and to support high-quality implementation of humanistic education and ideological-political elements within anatomy and other foundational medical courses [7, 8, 19].

Methods

Study design

An explanatory sequential mixed-methods design was adopted, integrating a cluster randomized controlled trial (RCT) with qualitative research. First, a two-arm parallel cluster RCT with pretest and posttest measurements (T1/T2) quantified the effect of the integrated module on respect-for-life concepts. Second, based on quantitative findings, purposive stratified sampling was used to conduct semi-structured interviews and collect open-ended responses among intervention students to explain potential mechanisms and the roles of key teaching components. Quantitative and qualitative data were triangulated for integration.

No important changes were made to the trial protocol after commencement. No interim analyses were planned or conducted, and no stopping guidelines were applied. The trial was completed as planned, with no early termination. No patients or members of the public were involved in the design, conduct, or reporting of this study, as the intervention was delivered to medical students in an educational setting. Both groups continued their regular medical curriculum throughout the trial; no additional concomitant medical interventions were provided as part of the study.

Setting and participants

The study was implemented within the 18-week Systemic Anatomy course at Hubei University of Medicine, which includes lectures and dissection laboratories. The intervention group received the integrated module in addition to routine teaching, whereas the control group received routine teaching only.

Participants were undergraduate medical students enrolled in the course. Written informed consent was obtained and anonymous codes were assigned before the study. Inclusion criteria were: (1) first-time enrollment in Systemic Anatomy; (2) voluntary participation with signed consent; and (3) completion of both T1 and T2 assessments within the required timeframe. Exclusion criteria were: (1) prior systematic training on body/organ donation or long-term participation in related volunteer services (recorded and excluded or treated as covariates as appropriate); (2) severe missingness in key questionnaire items at T1 or T2 that could not be corrected; and (3) inability to complete the intervention or follow-up due to leave of absence, major change, etc.

Randomization, blinding, and contamination control

Cluster randomization was performed at the class level. Eight intact classes were included and randomly assigned (1:1) to the intervention or control group (4 classes each) using a computer-generated random sequence. Allocation concealment was ensured by sealing the randomization list with a researcher not involved in teaching implementation.

Because of the educational nature of the intervention, blinding of teachers and students was not feasible. To reduce measurement and analytic bias, questionnaires were collected using anonymous codes; after data entry, group information was masked as ‘A/B’ so that statisticians remained blinded during primary analyses. To reduce social desirability bias, all surveys were anonymous, students were informed that no answers were “correct,” and responses were not linked to academic performance.

To minimize information spillover, the control group was not given access to the key components of the intervention (e.g., commemorative activities, policy workshops, communication simulations, public surveys, and community advocacy). Activities requiring centralized participation were scheduled at different times or held in different locations. Any school-wide publicity events that might have exposed both groups were documented and described in the discussion section. No formal contamination assessment was performed, which is acknowledged as a limitation (see Discussion).

Sample size

Quantitative sample: Each class had approximately 35–45 students, with a planned recruitment of about 336 students. Assuming ~ 10% attrition or invalid responses, we anticipated ~ 302 valid matched cases (about 151 per group), sufficient for testing group × time interaction effects on the primary outcome.

Qualitative sample: Sixteen intervention students were selected using purposive stratified sampling. Based on posttest results, eight students with the largest improvements in total respect-for-life scores and eight with minimal/no improvement or slight decline were recruited, while ensuring diversity in gender and background. Interviews continued until thematic saturation was reached. Potential participants were contacted by email or in person. No invited students refused to participate or dropped out.

Intervention

Intervention group: On top of routine Systemic Anatomy teaching, an 18-week integrated module on body/organ donation and respect for life was embedded throughout the course. Control group: Routine teaching strictly followed the existing Systemic Anatomy syllabus, including complete lecture and laboratory teaching, without participation in any intervention activities or the solemn-moment ritual.

The integrated module was delivered by the regular anatomy teaching staff, who were experienced in medical education and received a brief orientation on the intervention components.

Outcomes and measures

Assessment timepoints were T1 (baseline, before the module) and T2 (posttest, after all course activities).

The primary outcome was the total score of the Multidimensional Respect-for-Life Scale, reflecting overall respect-for-life concepts. Secondary outcomes included: the Body Donation Attitude and Behavioral Intention Scale; the Incremental Social Responsibility and Behavioral Intention Scale; the Subjective Experience and Overall Evaluation Questionnaire for the integrated module; and the final course grade for Systemic Anatomy.

Multidimensional Respect-for-Life Scale: developed based on a cognition–emotion–behavioral intention model; 15 items across five dimensions; 5-point Likert scoring; total score 15–75, with higher scores indicating stronger concepts. Cronbach’s α = 0.892. No formal content or construct validity testing was conducted (see Discussion).

Body Donation Attitude and Behavioral Intention Scale: developed based on the Theory of Planned Behavior; 7 items (including one reverse-scored item); 5-point Likert scoring; total score 7–35, with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes and intentions. Cronbach’s α = 0.835. No formal validity testing was conducted (see Discussion).

Incremental Social Responsibility and Behavioral Intention Scale: developed to measure incremental changes triggered by the course; 6 items; 5-point Likert scoring; total score 6–30, with higher scores indicating stronger internalization and translation intention. Cronbach’s α = 0.894. No formal validity testing was conducted (see Discussion).

Subjective Experience and Overall Evaluation Questionnaire: self-developed with two parts — (i) effectiveness ratings (5-point Likert) for 10 core components, and (ii) perceived gains via a multiple-choice checklist plus one open-ended question. Internal consistency for the rating part was good (Cronbach’s α = 0.856). No formal validity testing was conducted for this exploratory questionnaire.

Anatomy course grade: The official final grade from the academic system was used to assess potential effects on professional learning. The grade was on a 100-point scale and comprised the theory exam (40%), laboratory assessment (40%), and process evaluation (20%), per the syllabus.

All scales described above were developed by the research team for this study. The English versions of these instruments are provided in Additional file 1.

No harms or adverse events were anticipated or systematically assessed, as the intervention consisted of educational activities. No participants reported any unintended effects.

Data collection and quality control

All quantitative questionnaires were administered in class via the Wenjuanxing platform at T1 and T2. Students completed the surveys anonymously within a specified timeframe using personal mobile devices. The platform set a minimum response time per item and recorded timestamps. Data were de-identified immediately after export.

Qualitative data were collected after T2 through semi-structured interviews and open-ended responses in the intervention group. Interviews covered: initial feelings when first encountering cadavers; impacts of the solemn moment and Qingming commemoration; changes in understanding of death and donation; willingness and perceived capability to explain donation to family/public; and changes in understanding of future medical responsibility.

Interviews were conducted by the first author, who had no prior relationship with participants. Each interview was conducted in a separate room, audio-recorded, lasted approximately 40 min on average, and was transcribed verbatim. Thematic analysis followed Braun & Clarke’s six-phase framework. Two researchers (the first author and a trained research assistant) independently coded the first five transcripts, compared codes, and resolved disagreements by consensus; no formal inter-rater reliability was calculated. An initial codebook was developed inductively from the first three transcripts and refined iteratively as subsequent transcripts were coded. When coding disagreements arose, they were first discussed between the two coders; if consensus could not be reached, a third researcher (co-author Juhua Ma) with expertise in medical ethics was consulted to facilitate resolution. After the coding framework was finalized through consensus, the remaining transcripts were coded by the first author, with regular audit checks by the research assistant to ensure ongoing consistency with the agreed codebook. The final themes were reviewed and approved by all members of the research team. Data saturation was reached after 14 interviews. NVivo 14 was used for code management. A reflexivity journal was maintained. Reporting follows Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research(COREQ)guidelines (see Additional file 2).

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0 was used. For the primary outcome, repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) tested the group × time interaction. Secondary scale outcomes were analyzed similarly (repeated-measures ANOVA/ANCOVA as appropriate). Final anatomy grades were analyzed using linear mixed models to account for class-level clustering. Two-sided α = 0.05 was used. Missing questionnaire data were handled with multiple imputation.

Reporting guideline

This cluster randomized trial is reported in accordance with the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines. The trial protocol and statistical analysis plan are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Results

Participant flow and follow-up

Using a cluster randomized design, 336 students from eight classes were randomized in February 2025 into the intervention group (4 classes, n = 169) and the control group (4 classes, n = 167). All students completed baseline assessment (T1). After the 18-week intervention, posttest assessment (T2) was conducted in July 2025. A total of 308 matched datasets were obtained (intervention n = 155; control n = 153), yielding an overall valid follow-up rate of 91.67%. Twenty-eight students were excluded due to absence from T2 (intervention 7; control 8), unmatched pre–post IDs (intervention 4; control 4), or > 20% missingness in key posttest items (intervention 2; control 3).

Primary outcome: change in respect-for-life concepts

Repeated-measures ANOVA (2 groups × 2 timepoints) on the total score of the Multidimensional Respect-for-Life Scale showed a significant group × time interaction (F(1,306) = 45.321, P < 0.001, partial η²=0.136). Simple-effects analyses indicated a marked increase in the intervention group (T1: 51.21 ± 6.55; T2: 61.84 ± 5.82; Δ = 10.63, P < 0.001), whereas the control group showed no significant change (T1: 50.85 ± 6.71; T2: 52.32 ± 6.44; Δ = 1.47, P = 0.123) (Table 2).

Secondary outcomes

Item-level analyses indicated higher scores for all items in the intervention group (all P < 0.001), with particularly high means for intention to register as a volunteer (B4), to communicate with family (B5), and to participate in public advocacy (B7) (Table 3).

Subjective experience with the module

Intervention students rated the effectiveness of module components highly (Table 5). Among the 10 components, the Qingming Festival commemoration received the highest rating (4.71 ± 0.55), followed by the 5-minute solemn moment (4.54 ± 0.63) and the life story forum (4.42 ± 0.73). Adherence and process evaluation

All nine planned core activities were completed (completion rate 100%). The recurring 5-minute solemn moment was implemented in all 18 dissection classes. Recorded formats were: silent gratitude (50.00%), clinical case guidance (30.00%), and a combination of both (20.00%).

Anatomy course performance

Using linear mixed models to account for class-level clustering, no statistically significant between-group difference was found in final Systemic Anatomy grades (estimated mean difference β = 1.24, 95% CI − 1.86 to 4.34; P = 0.432). Mean grades were 82.7 ± 7.5 for the intervention group and 81.9 ± 8.1 for the control group. Comparisons of theory exam and laboratory assessment subscores also showed no significant differences (all P > 0.05).

Discussion

This study used a cluster RCT combined with mixed-methods integration to systematically examine the impact of deeply integrating body/organ donation education into an anatomy course on medical students’ respect-for-life concepts. The results demonstrate that an integrated module designed around a ‘knowledge–emotion–behavior’ framework can effectively improve key dimensions—death cognition, gratitude and respect toward donors, and professional responsibility—without negatively affecting academic achievement in anatomy. Below we discuss the meaning, mechanisms, practical implications, and limitations in light of existing literature.

Key findings and significance: promoting internalization without compromising professional learning

Quantitatively, the intervention group showed significant and moderate-to-large improvements in the total respect-for-life score and in three key dimensions (A1, A2, A5), with partial η² ranging from 0.080 to 0.136. This provides strong evidence that an integrative intervention—using the ‘silent mentor’ as an emotional vehicle and combining structured ethics education, high-frequency ritual experiences, and authentic social practice—can go beyond purely technical training and elicit positive changes in cognitive reframing, emotional activation, and professional identity [11–13]. This directly addresses a long-standing challenge in medical education: how to operationalize humanistic value education within foundational biomedical courses and achieve measurable impacts [7, 8].

Two complementary findings are particularly important. First, changes extended beyond concepts to intentions: the intervention group showed markedly higher donation attitudes/intentions (Cohen’s d = 1.746) and incremental social responsibility/behavioral intention (Cohen‘s d = 1.686), indicating a shift from ’knowing‘ to ’believing‘ and toward ’intending to act‘ [15, 17, 20]. These large effect sizes, while substantial, are plausible given the intensive 18-week intervention, which combined repeated emotional rituals (e.g., solemn moments, Qingming commemoration) with authentic social practice activities (e.g., community advocacy, public surveys) that directly targeted attitudes and behavioral intentions. Comparable effect sizes have been reported in similar medical education interventions focusing on value-based outcomes [8, 13]. However, these effect sizes should be interpreted with caution. The primary and secondary outcome measures were self-developed instruments with acceptable internal consistency but without formal content or construct validation. Consequently, the magnitude of change may not be accurately estimated, and the findings are best viewed as preliminary evidence supporting the promise of the integrated module rather than definitive proof of its efficacy. In addition, the lack of blinding and the exclusive reliance on self-reported data introduce potential performance and detection biases that may have inflated the observed effects to some degree, reinforcing the need for a cautious interpretation. Furthermore, in this single-institution cluster randomized trial, complete prevention of information spillover between groups could not be guaranteed. Should any contamination have occurred, it would most likely have biased the results toward the null (i.e., reducing between-group differences), because control group participants might have been incidentally exposed to elements of the intervention. Therefore, the statistically significant between-group differences observed here may represent a conservative estimate of the true intervention effect, which further strengthens confidence in the efficacy of the integrated module rather than undermining it. Second, the intervention did not compromise professional learning outcomes: final anatomy grades did not differ significantly between groups (P = 0.432). This addresses a common concern that adding humanistic content might ‘crowd out’ technical learning. Instead, well-designed integration may add value by enhancing meaning-making and motivation while maintaining mastery of core knowledge and skills [8, 13, 16].

Mechanisms: an experiential–reflective pathway of value internalization

Qualitative themes clarified the micro-level dynamics of internalization. Ritualized experiences such as the recurring solemn moment served as emotional anchors, binding abstract respect to a concrete ‘silent mentor’ figure and initiating gratitude and reverence (consistent with A2). Concurrently, reflection on death ethics helped students reframe initial anxiety into rational respect, supporting gains in death cognition (A1). Social practice provided real contexts for externalizing knowledge and influencing others, enabling students to move from passive learners to active advocates and strengthening confidence and civic responsibility. Ultimately, students actively transferred humanistic care into imagined future clinical roles, suggesting early professional identity formation consistent with A5 [21]. Together, these findings delineate a coherent pathway of “emotional engagement → cognitive reframing → behavioral empowerment → identity construction,” highlighting the central role of experiential and reflective learning in shaping values [8, 19].

Practical implications and theoretical contributions

The module was structured, modular, and operational. Students rated the emotionally engaging and contextualized components highest—especially the Qingming commemoration and the 5-minute solemn moment—suggesting that ritualized, high-emotion elements are effective catalysts for deep learning [11–13]. Diverse social practice activities (policy interpretation, community education) were essential for translating knowledge into action confidence [22–25].

Methodologically, this study combined rigorous causal evidence from a cluster RCT with qualitative mechanism explanations, addressing limitations of prior research that relied mainly on cross-sectional surveys or descriptive experience summaries [15, 17, 18]. Notably, interaction effects were not significant for A3 (transfer of care) and A4 (value identification), although the intervention group showed numerically higher scores at T2. Several factors may explain this. First, statistical power may have been insufficient for dimension-level analyses. Second, ceiling effects at baseline cannot be ruled out (control group means were 10.04 and 11.32 on a 3–15 scale). Third, these broader constructs may require longer-term, multi-course reinforcement rather than a single module [7, 8]. Fourth, measurement sensitivity for these dimensions may be limited due to lack of construct validation. Future studies should address these possibilities. This provides empirical cues for designing longitudinal, programmatic medical humanities curricula.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center study with a relatively homogeneous sample, generalizability across regions and cultures warrants further validation [26–29]. Second, outcomes were assessed immediately after the course, without long-term follow-up to determine the durability of conceptual changes or whether intentions translate into real behaviors [19, 22]. Third, the trial registration was retrospective. Although the application was submitted on 15 August 2025 (before data collection was completed), formal approval was granted on 19 March 2026 due to administrative processing delays. This does not conform to prospective registration best practices and may affect the perceived level of evidence. Future studies should complete registration and obtain a publicly accessible registration number before study initiation. Fourth, several methodological constraints should be noted: (a) the primary and secondary outcomes relied on self-developed scales with acceptable internal consistency but without established content or construct validity, which may introduce measurement error and limit the precision of the estimated effects; (b) blinding of instructors and students was not feasible, which may have introduced performance and detection bias. All outcomes were self-reported, raising risks of recall inaccuracy and social desirability bias, especially in the intervention group. Although we used anonymous surveys and decoupled responses from grades, these biases cannot be fully excluded; (c) despite efforts to separate activities, contamination between groups in a single-institution setting cannot be fully ruled out. No formal contamination assessment was conducted. If contamination occurred, it would likely bias the results toward the null (i.e., underestimate the true intervention effect), meaning that the significant effects detected in this trial are likely conservative and, rather than weakening the evidence, reinforce confidence in the intervention’s effectiveness. (d) the non-significant interaction effects for A3 (transfer of care) and A4 (value identification) may reflect insufficient statistical power, ceiling effects at baseline, or the need for longer-term reinforcement. Future research should conduct multi-center longitudinal studies, develop rigorously validated assessment tools, extend the integration model to broader medical curricula [7, 8], and incorporate objective behavioral measures and contamination assessments where feasible.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that systematically integrating body/organ donation education into anatomy teaching is an effective and feasible strategy for medical humanities innovation. Although the results are encouraging, it should be noted that the outcome measures were self-developed and have not undergone full psychometric validation; therefore, the precise magnitude of the observed improvements remains uncertain and requires confirmation in future studies with validated instruments. The approach significantly promotes multi-dimensional internalization of respect-for-life concepts and strengthens intentions for social advocacy and public service, while preserving professional learning outcomes. The mechanism follows a clear experiential–reflective pathway. By highlighting the non-inferiority in anatomy grades, the model underscores its practical advantage and scalability. These findings provide an evidence-based teaching paradigm to deepen ideological-political education and cultivate physicians with both technical excellence and humanistic compassion [7, 8, 13, 19]. It should be noted that this trial was retrospectively registered; therefore, the results should be interpreted with appropriate caution, and future studies with prospective registration are warranted.
