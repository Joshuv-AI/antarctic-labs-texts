Short-Term Outcomes and Immunologic Mechanisms of Adrenocorticotropic Hormone in Infantile Epileptic Spasm Syndrome: A Multicenter Prospective Trial in Southern China
Authors: Yanping Ran, Bingwei Peng, Jianwei Li, Yanqun Chang, Xiuying Wang, Cuiling Zhuo, Kelu Zheng, Yani Zhang, Jie Yu, Haixia Zhu, Yang Tian, Chi Hou, Wenxiao Wu, Wenlin Wu, Xiaojing Li, Wen-xiong Chen

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAdrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) is a first-line drug and standard therapy in infantile epileptic spasm syndrome (IESS) with different response rates. The mechanism of ACTH for treatment of IESS remains incompletely understood. This study aimed to evaluate short-term outcomes and immunologic mechanisms of ACTH in treating pediatric patients with IESS.MethodsA multicenter prospective trial of pediatric patients with IESS treated with ACTH over 2 weeks was performed. Short-term responders were defined as those with resolution of epileptic spasms and hypsarrhythmia (if present) at day 14 without epileptic spasm recurrence over 30 days after ACTH therapy, while non-responders included those with persistent spasms or hypsarrhythmia after treatment, or recurrence within 30 days. The presence of hypsarrhythmia on pre-therapy electroencephalogram (EEG) was a risk factor for the short-term efficacy (p = 0.029). The presence of hypsarrhythmia on the pre-therapy EEG was a risk factor for short-term efficacy of ACTH administration. Introduction

Infantile epileptic spasm syndrome (IESS) is a common developmental and epilepsy encephalopathy usually occurring within the first 2 years after birth, with a peak onset at 3–12 months [1]. IESS is characterized by epileptic spasms, mostly with hypsarrhythmia on the electroencephalogram (EEG) and developmental retardation [1, 2].

Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), a 39-amino acid peptide secreted by the pituitary gland, was first reported for the treatment of infantile spasms (IS) in 1958. ACTH is a first-line drug and standard therapy in IS [3, 4]. However, its mechanism of action remains incompletely understood. ACTH is known to modulate the immune response. Potentially ACTH effects the function of the hypothalamus–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis [5], acts on melanocortin receptors, regulates the lymphocyte subpopulations and cytokines, and inhibits the inflammatory response of the nervous system [6–8]. Previous studies have found that patients with IESS have significantly reduced T cell subsets at the onset of the disease compared to control groups [8, 9]. Following ACTH or hormone therapy, levels of some types of lymphocytes decrease compared to pre-treatment levels [8, 9]. But studies assessing the prognostic value of lymphocyte subsets for ACTH therapy in children with IESS have not been previously reported.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the short-term outcomes after ACTH therapy and explore the relationship between lymphocyte subpopulations and the short-term efficacy of ACTH treatment by measuring lymphocyte subpopulations in children with IESS before ACTH treatment, to provide potential guidance for clinical practice.

Materials and Methods

This multicenter prospective study was performed at three hospitals (Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center, Guangdong Maternal and Child Health Hospital, Dongguan Children’s Hospital) in Southern China. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center (approval number 2021211A01) and was conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients’ parents or legal guardians for the publication of any potentially identifiable images or data included in this article.

Patients

Patients with IESS were recruited between January 2018 and December 2024. Inclusion criteria included (1) meeting the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) diagnostic criteria for IESS [1], (2) epileptic spasms confirmed by EEG, (3) no corticosteroid used in the 2 months prior to enrollment, (4) first-time ACTH treatment and completion of the treatment, and (5) follow-up time ≥ 30 days.

Patients were excluded if their data were missing or incongruent, or the ACTH treatment was not completed, or children with preexisting infections and abnormal liver function, or children with immune system disorders, such as immunodeficiency diseases, with hematologic disorders, or with connective tissue disorders.

Data Collection

Clinical data collected included perinatal history, maternal history, family history, sex, age of onset, other seizure types, and neurodevelopmental status before IESS diagnosis.

ACTH Treatment

All patients received natural ACTH (produced by Shanghai Shangyao Biochemical First Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) via one of two regimens based on the drug’s instructions for use: (1) The ACTH dosage was set at 2 units (U)/kg. ACTH was added to 100 ml of 5% dextrose solution and administered intravenously once a day for at least 6 h continuously for 2 weeks. (2) The ACTH dosage was set at 1 U/kg in the first week and 2 U/kg in the second week, intramuscular injection twice daily. Prednisone acetate tablets were given at 2 mg/kg orally for 2 weeks after ACTH therapy. Hormone therapy regimen after 28 days was adjusted according to individual suitability.

Blood routine, urine routine, stool routine, liver and kidney function, cardiac enzymes, electrocardiogram, chest X-ray, EEG, cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), epilepsy gene or whole-exome sequencing, metabolic examination (urinary organic acid analysis, blood acylcarnitine and amino acid analysis), lymphocyte subpopulation (including the counts and percentage of CD3+ T cells, CD3+CD4+ T cells, CD3+CD8+ T cells, CD3−CD19+ B cells, CD3−CD16+CD56+ natural killer (NK) cells, and CD3+CD4+/CD3+CD8+) were checked before ACTH treatment. Patients with contraindications for ACTH therapy such as with serious infections, tuberculosis, or heart failure were excluded. Regular monitoring of blood pressure, blood sugar, blood routine, liver and kidney function, cardiac enzymes, and electrolytes was performed in children who started ACTH treatment.

Classification of Seizures and Short-Term Efficacy Outcome Measures

Classification of seizure types was based on the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) 2017 guidelines [10]. Short-term responders were defined as those with the following criteria: (1) freedom from both epileptic spasms and hypsarrhythmia (if present) at day 14 based on video-EEG, (2) no recurrence of epileptic spasms over 30 days [11, 12]. Non-responders included those with persistent spasms or hypsarrhythmia after treatment, or recurrence within 30 days.

Etiology of IESS

Data collected for each patient included birth history, maternal history, perinatal history, family history, cranial MRI, epilepsy gene or whole-exome sequencing, and metabolic tests (urinary organic acid analysis, blood acylcarnitine and amino acid analysis). The etiology was classified into three categories as per previous reports [13–15]: (1) genetic etiology or possible genetic etiology (the latter was defined as unknown etiology with severe developmental delay), (2) structural etiology, (3) unknown etiology, including the following two situations: (a) subject without cause was identified despite comprehensive etiological investigations; (b) subject who underwent complete MRI and metabolic screening but declined genetic testing, resulting in an undetermined etiology, yet who exhibited normal or mildly delayed neurodevelopment.

Neurodevelopmental Assessment

The neurodevelopmental history after birth of included subjects was obtained. As a result of the early onset of IESS, most affected children exhibit developmental stagnation or regression after disease presentation. This often makes it challenging for parents or guardians to provide accurate developmental histories or pre-onset Gesell Developmental Diagnosis Scale (GDDS). Consequently, only pretreatment GDDS are available for recruitment. Pretreatment neurodevelopmental status was classified into the following two categories: (1) severe developmental delay if development quotient (DQ) less than 70 via GDDS. (2) mild developmental delay or normal neurodevelopment of DQ more than 70 via GDDS.

Follow-up

The children were followed up for more than 1 month by outpatient visits or telephone. The frequency of seizures and the adjustment of the treatment were recorded during the follow-up process. EEG was repeated 2–4 weeks after treatment. The adjustment of medication during the follow-up was based on the reports from the parents and guardians, who would record clinical manifestations, frequency and duration of convulsive seizures and other types of seizures of subjects.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS 22.0 software was used for statistical processing. Measurement data with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and comparisons between the two groups were performed using the t test. Measurement data with non-normal distribution were expressed as median (range), and comparisons between the two groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Count data were expressed as frequency [n (%)], and comparisons between groups were performed using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test. Short-term efficacy-related influences were explored using multivariate logistic regression. ROC curve analysis was adopted to predict the value of lymphocyte subpopulations for predicting short-term outcome after treatment with ACTH in children with IESS. p < 0.05 indicates statistical significance.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

Between January 2018 and December 2024, 181 infants were diagnosed with IESS, of whom 151 met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled (Fig. 1). Of the 151 patients, 89 (58.9%) were boys and 62 (41.1%) were girls. Neurodevelopment before diagnosis was normal or mild developmental delay in 21 cases (13.9%) and severely delayed in 130 cases (86.1%). Isolated epileptic spasms were present in 115 cases (76.2%), while 36 cases (23.8%) exhibited additional seizure types. There were 117 cases (77.5%) with hypsarrhythmia and 34 cases (22.5%) without hypsarrhythmia on the pre-therapy EEG. Among the 151 cases, 72 (47.7%) had a genetic etiology or possible genetic etiology, and the remaining subjects included 32 children with structural etiologies and 47 with unknown causes. The median age of onset was 4.7 months (0.03–22.5 months). The median duration of IESS from onset to ACTH treatment was 2 months (0.3–59 months), 99 patients (65.6%) had a disease course of 3 months or less, and 52 patients (34.4%) exceeded 3 months.

ACTH was administered intravenously in 38 cases (25.2%) and intramuscularly in 113 cases (74.8%). Only one patient received ACTH monotherapy, while the remaining 150 (99.3%) were treated with a combination of ACTH and antiseizure medications (ASMs). Of the 150 children with ASMs, 55 (36.7%) children took one type of ASM, while 75 (50.0%) children were on a combination of two types of ASMs, 15 (10.0%) children with three types of ASMs, and 5 (3.3%) children with four types of ASMs. Among these 150 children, vigabatrin was used in only 10 patients (6.6%). A summary of data from the 150 patients with IESS using ASMs is shown in the Supplementary Material.

Short-Term Outcomes of ACTH Therapy

Following 30 days of ACTH treatment, epileptic spasms were absent in 54 patients (35.8%) but persisted in 97 (64.2%). Post-treatment EEG was performed on 149 patients, revealing hypsarrhythmia in 72 (48.3%) and its absence in the remaining 77 (51.7%). Forty-four patients (29.1%) responded to ACTH treatment.

Hypsarrhythmia on pre-therapy EEG was significantly correlated with the short-term efficacy (p = 0.029) (Table 1): 65.9% of patients in the short-term efficacy group showed hypsarrhythmia on pre-therapy EEG, although 82.2% of patients in the no short-term efficacy group demonstrated hypsarrhythmia on pre-therapy EEG. The proportion of patients with a disease course of less than 3 months and the proportion of patients who used 0–1 type of ASMs in the short-term efficacy group were all higher than those in the no short-term efficacy group, but the differences were not statistically significant (Table 1).

Predictive Value of Lymphocyte Subpopulations for Short-Term Efficacy of ACTH in Treatment of IESS

Peripheral blood lymphocyte subpopulations were measured before ACTH treatment in 81 children. The short-term responder group showed a lower percentage of CD3+CD8+ T cells (p = 0.080) and a higher CD3+CD4+/CD3+CD8+ ratio (p = 0.077) compared to the non-responder group, though these differences were not statistically significant (Table 3). No significant differences were observed between groups in the counts or percentages of CD3+ T cells, CD3+CD4+ T cells, CD3−CD19+ B cells, CD3−CD16+CD56+ NK cells, or in the count of CD3+CD8+ T cells (Table 3).

Discussion

IESS is a severe form of an early infantile epileptic encephalopathy with a generally poor prognosis. Relief of epileptic spasms as soon as possible and improvement hypsarrhythmia on EEG are critical for the treatment of subjects with IESS and the improvement of their prognosis [16]. ACTH has been recognized as the first-line treatment for IESS in guidelines [17]. As ACTH becomes more accessible, more and more physicians are using ACTH as the treatment of first choice for IESS [18]. ACTH exerts anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects in patients with IESS by modulating B cell activity to reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines and by regulating the conversion of T lymphocyte subsets to reduce lymphocytic infiltration [8]. However, ACTH treatment is not indicated for all patients with IESS; furthermore, ACTH is expensive and has adverse effects [14, 19]. Therefore, it is necessary to explore the factors influencing the efficacy and the immune mechanism of ACTH in the treatment of IESS, in order to provide the basis for precise treatment of patients with IESS. Currently there are few clinical studies on immunologic indicators and specific mechanisms for assessing the short-term efficacy of ACTH in the treatment of IESS. Peripheral blood lymphocyte subpopulation is an important indicator to study the immune mechanism in IESS. In this study, we analyzed the lymphocyte subpopulation of children with IESS to investigate the immune mechanism of ACTH treatment for IESS and to find an immune indicator for efficacy assessment.

Our study showed that children with IESS had a higher short-term responder rate to ACTH therapy when the percentage of CD3+CD8+ T cells was less than 19.495% and the ratio of CD3+CD4+/CD3+CD8+ was higher than 2.585. Previous studies have shown that the counts of CD3+CD8+ T cells and CD3+CD4+ T cells decreased in patients with IESS after ACTH or hormone therapy [8, 9]. There are immunological alterations in patients with IESS. ROC curve was used to explore the predictive value of lymphocyte subsets for the efficacy of ACTH in subjects with IESS. We found that the AUC values for predicting the short-term efficacy of ACTH therapy using the CD3+CD8+ T cell percentage and CD3+CD4+/CD3+CD8+ ratio were about 0.6. The sensitivity and specificity were 0.607 and 0.64 for CD3+CD8+ T cell percentage, and 0.821 and 0.44 for the CD3+CD4+/CD3+CD8+ ratio. Their predictive value is limited. The main reason was that the etiopathogenesis and mechanism are complex, involving not only neuroimmune inflammation but also gene and epigenetic regulation altering expression of “vulnerability” genes, stress and HPA axis activation, altered neuronal transmission and pathways, and dysfunction of metabolic pathways [8]. As we know, CD3+CD8+ T cells plays a negative regulatory role in the immune response by inhibiting the function of CD3+CD4+ T cells [20]. Previous studies have found that patients with IS have decreased CD3+CD4+/CD3+CD8+ ratios and CD3+CD4+ T cells counts, and increased CD3+CD8+ T cells counts and CD3−CD19+ B cells counts [21, 22]. Wang et al. also found a significantly lower ratio of CD3+CD4+/CD3+CD8+ and a higher CD3−CD19+ B cells counts in patients with IS compared to normal children before ACTH treatment [20]. After ACTH treatment, there were significantly higher percentages of CD3+CD4+ T cells and CD3−CD19+ B cells in patients with IS than before ACTH treatment [20]. The results of the aforementioned studies and ours suggest that patients of IESS have abnormal transformations in lymphocytes. CD3+CD4+ T cells are important in immune responses through the release of cytokines [23]. CD3+CD8+ T cells act indirectly by regulating CD3+CD4+ T cells [24]. Immunological alterations in patients with IESS could be modified by ACTH therapy.

However, in the analysis of the difference in lymphocyte subpopulations between the different efficacy groups of ACTH treatment for the patients with IESS, no statistical difference in the counts and proportions of CD3+CD8+ T cells between them was seen, which may be related to the small sample size in this study. In humoral immunity, CD3−CD19+ B cells are stimulated by antigens and produce antibodies that bind to the corresponding antigens, thereby triggering an immune response [25]. The functional upregulation and activation of CD3−CD19+ B cells lead to the secretion of regulatory cytokines that alter neural excitability and neurotransmitters in children with IESS to promote epilepsy.

According to the 2022 ILAE classification of epileptic syndromes, IESS includes West syndrome with hypsarrhythmia on EEG and infants with epileptic spasms who do not meet the full criteria for West syndrome [1]. An updated definition of IESS no longer requires hypsarrhythmia on EEG. This change emphasizes the importance of early diagnosis and treatment. One criterion for treatment remission in IESS was the absence of hypsarrhythmia on EEG post treatment [11, 12]. Early treatment with ACTH in IESS improves developmental outcomes and prognosis [16, 19]. In our study, hypsarrhythmia on the pre-therapy EEG was the only risk factor for short-term efficacy of ACTH treatment (p < 0.05). Previous multivariate analyses [26] also suggest that the absence of hypsarrhythmia on EEG means epilepsy is more likely to be controlled.

The short-term responder rate to ACTH therapy in our IESS cohort was 29.1%, which is lower than the previously reported range of 40–75% [27–29]. The main reason was there were more patients with refractory disease and severe developmental delay in our study. We found 62.9% of children had received 2–4 types of ASMs in the duration of ACTH therapy. A higher proportion of children in the non-responder group had received 2–4 types of ASMs compared to those in the short-term responder group. On the other hand, the median age of onset in this study was 4.7 months, earlier than the previously reported range of 5.2–7 months [30, 31]. Previous studies have shown that a 1-month increase in age of onset of disease is associated with a 1.09-fold increase in the odds of treatment response (95% CI 1.03–1.15) [31]. The onset age of IESS was higher in the short-term responder group than in the non-responder group in our cohort. Consequently, earlier age at onset and the use of a greater number of ASM types were associated with more difficult-to-control epilepsy in the current study.

Previous studies show that short-term outcomes of ACTH treatment in children with IESS are related to etiology [15, 31–34]. ACTH treatment demonstrated lower efficacy in patients with hereditary etiology and higher efficacy in those with unknown etiology [29, 31]. A 2021 Japanese study retrospectively analyzed 107 patients with IS and reported a response rate of 64% in cryptogenic, 65% in acquired structural, and 30.6% in congenital etiological groups [31]. Similarly, a Chinese study of 155 cases found ACTH effective in 27.8% of hereditary and 72.2% of cryptogenic etiologies [15]. As a result of the limited sample size, incomplete genetic testing in some patients, and the lower response rate to ACTH in this cohort, no significant differences in ACTH therapy efficacy were observed across etiological groups in our study. Furthermore, it was not possible to further assess whether lymphocyte subpopulations varied across etiological groups in the current study.

ACTH used in the current study can be administered either intravenously or intramuscularly according to the official instructions for drug use. Both routes of administration effectively control patients’ spasms, but intramuscular injection is associated with fewer adverse reactions and greater safety [35]. The majority of patients in the current study received intramuscular injection. Furthermore, the current study revealed no statistically significant difference in the administration regimens between groups with different treatment responses.

Limitations of this study are follows: (1) The short-term response rate to ACTH therapy in our IESS cohort was lower than previously reported. (2) Lymphocyte subpopulations were not examined in 46.4% (70/151) of patients with IESS because of economic issues, resulting in an insufficient sample for assessment of the short-term outcomes of ACTH therapy and the correlation with lymphocyte subsets. (3) Some children did not complete the genetic testing. Some children with genetic etiology were neglected and this led to bias in the results. (4) The study only examined lymphocyte subpopulations before treatment; further study is warranted to determine lymphocyte subset differences among etiological groups. Dynamic changes of lymphocyte subpopulations during and after treatment should also be examined in future studies.

Conclusion

The percentage of CD3+CD8+ T cell and the CD3+CD4+/CD3+CD8+ ratio demonstrate predictive value for the short-term efficacy of ACTH therapy in children with IESS. However, the predictive utility of those parameters is limited and should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical information. This study concludes that children with IESS exhibit immunoinflammatory disorders, and ACTH treatment may control spasms by modulating lymphocyte subpopulations and suppressing the immune-inflammatory response. Further studies are necessary to explore the immunopathogenic mechanism of IESS and the treatment mechanism of ACTH, in order to provide an appropriate and accurate treatment for IESS.
