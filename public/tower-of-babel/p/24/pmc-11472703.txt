Prostatic leiomyoma: A case report
Authors: Manoj Kumar Deepak, Muthu Veeramani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A pure leiomyoma of the prostate, a rare tumor with fewer than 30 documented cases, typically initiates as focal points within the gland, causing prostatomegaly. Pathological examination is crucial for diagnosis, distinguishing it from leiomyosarcoma. Complete tumor resection is preferred, with methods like transurethral resection, open adenomectomy, or prostatectomy. Here, we detail a 72-year-old male's case of prostatic leiomyoma, treated via Robotic prostatectomy, highlighting the importance of accurate diagnosis and tailored therapy for this rare condition.

Introduction

Prostatic leiomyoma, a rare and benign smooth muscle tumor of the prostate, presents a unique clinical challenge due to its rarity and the potential for misdiagnosis. With fewer than 30 cases documented in medical literature,1 leiomyoma of the prostate is often overlooked during routine urological evaluations and is often labelled as nodular hyperplasia of the prostate. This tumor often begins as focal nodules within the prostate, leading to prostatomegaly and associated symptoms. Accurate pathological examination is imperative to distinguish leiomyoma from the more aggressive leiomyosarcoma, as the treatment approaches and prognoses for these conditions diverge significantly.

The preferred treatment for prostatic leiomyoma is complete surgical resection,2 which can be achieved through various methods including transurethral resection, open adenomectomy, or prostatectomy. Among these options, robotic prostatectomy has emerged as a particularly advantageous approach, especially for large prostatic tumors like leiomyoma. The precision offered by robotic surgery facilitate the complete resection of the tumor, thereby minimizing the chances of recurrence. Additionally, robotic surgery offers benefits such as reduced blood loss, shorter hospital stays, and quicker recovery times compared to traditional open surgery.

Microscopic examination revealed prostatic tissue with a tumour (focally) within it. Sections show a fairly circumscribed tumour composed of wavy spindle shaped cells arranged in short and long intersecting fascicles and traversed by thin walled vascular channels. The tumour cells showed vesicular nuclei, moderate amounts of eosinophilic cytoplasm and indistinct cell borders. Scattered lymphocytes are seen within the tumour. There is no evidence of significant nuclear pleomorphism, brisk mitotic activity, infiltrative growth pattern or necrosis.

On Immunohistochemistry- The tumour cells were positive for SMA, desmin, PR and CD34 (patchy), while negative for STAT-6 and myogenin. Ki-67 : 1 %.

Based on the immunohistochemical findings, a definitive diagnosis of pure leiomyoma of the prostate was established. The patient's postoperative course proceeded without complications. Removal of the transurethral catheter occurred after 8 days, and the patient achieved complete continence on follow up.

Discussion

A pure leiomyoma of the prostate represents a rare occurrence, first documented by Kaufman and Berneike in 1951. Since then, fewer than 30 cases of prostatic leiomyoma have been reported. Given its rarity, leiomyoma of the prostate is often overlooked during routine urological evaluations. It is commonly misdiagnosed as nodular hyperplasia of the prostate until the final biopsy report, which frequently reveals the true diagnosis, surprising clinicians. Leiomyomas are benign tumors originating from smooth muscle fibers and can occasionally manifest in the genitourinary system,3,4 including the kidney, ureter, bladder, urachus, prostate, urethra, and seminal vesicles. Although more commonly found in the gastrointestinal and female genital tract, leiomyomas in the genitourinary system remain relatively rare.

Potential pathogenic mechanisms proposed for the development of leiomyomas include chronic inflammatory and infectious processes.4 Additionally, embryonic remnants of the Müllerian tube have been suggested as a possible origin for true leiomyomas of the prostate. Periglandular prostate smooth muscle and the prostate capsule are other possible locations of origin. The exact mechanism is however still unknown.5

Symptoms associated with a pure leiomyoma of the prostate often resemble those of benign prostatic hyperplasia, including lower urinary tract symptoms and acute urinary retention. In rare instances, macroscopic hematuria has been reported, and compression from the tumor may occasionally lead to rectal symptoms.6

Imaging techniques play a crucial role in diagnosing prostate tumors. On transrectal ultrasound, leiomyomas typically present as well-defined masses with either hyperechogenic or hypoechogenic characteristics. CT images may vary, depicting heterogeneous or homogeneous masses with or without contrast enhancement, depending on the extent of necrotic tissue. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) aids in distinguishing glandular from leiomyomatous tissue, with leiomyomas typically displaying homogeneous signals following gadolinium administration. Image variability is often attributed to histological degeneration within the lesion.

Definitive diagnosis relies on pathological examination to exclude malignancies such as leiomyosarcoma. Immunohistochemical analysis of a leiomyoma typically reveals strong positivity for myogenic markers such as desmin and actin. While cells may exhibit immunoreactivity against androgen receptors and show variable progesterone receptor reactivity, they generally lack estrogen receptor and Ki-67 reactivity. Presence of atypia may indicate potential risk for malignant transformation, characterized by multinucleate giant cells with vacuoles.7,8

It's crucial to differentiate between a true leiomyoma of the prostate, a rare entity, and a more common form where nodules of leiomyomatosis coexist with benign hyperplasia.9 A genuine leiomyoma lacks a glandular component, capsule, or pseudocapsule exceeding 1 g. In pure leiomyomas, smooth muscle cells entirely replace prostatic tissue. Identifying leiomyomas is vital, particularly considering the potential for malignancy in cases with nuclear atypia.

Given the risk of malignant transformation, especially with cellular atypia, existing literature uniformly advocates for complete tumor resection as the preferred treatment approach. Various methods, including transurethral resection, open adenomectomy, and prostatectomy, have been employed for leiomyoma treatment. Access to the prostate and the leiomyoma, which are usually large is best achieved via a robotic approach. Robotic prostatectomy offers complete removal of the prostatic tissue along with the leiomyoma. However, due to its rarity, no comparative studies regarding treatment options for prostatic leiomyoma are available.

Leiomyoma of the prostate boasts an excellent prognosis owing to its benign nature and the minimal recurrence rate following complete removal.10

Conclusion

Leiomyoma of the prostate represents a rare tumor and should be considered in the list of potential diagnoses when an atypical large mass with normal serum PSA levels within the prostate is encountered. Definitive diagnosis relies on histopathological examination. A genuine leiomyoma of the prostate is devoid of glandular prostatic tissue. However, the presence of cellular atypia suggests a potential for malignant transformation.

Optimal management involves complete resection of the tumor. When complete resection is achieved and no cellular atypia is evident, the prognosis for leiomyoma of the prostate is excellent.
