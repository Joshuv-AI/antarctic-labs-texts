Ultrasound-guided lung recruitment maneuver in pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome
Authors: Zi-Jiang Yang, Yong Wu, Xiao-Yun Zhao, Xu-Sheng Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

To compare the efficacy of bedside ultrasound-guided lung RM (US) with that of maximal oxygen-guided lung RM (OXY) in the management of PARDS. Introduction

Pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome (PARDS) is a complex clinical condition characterized by extensive alveolar collapse, resulting in varying degrees of lung volume reduction, most notably a significant decrease in functional residual capacity1–3. This extensive alveolar collapse markedly elevates pulmonary shunting, leading to refractory hypoxemia, which poses substantial treatment challenges and is associated with a high mortality rate4,5.

In the management of the primary disease, lung protective ventilation is administered6,7, Nonetheless, certain patients with hypoxemia may not exhibit improvement. In such cases, lung recruitment therapy may be considered. The objective of this therapy is to reopen collapsed alveoli and maintain their patency using appropriate positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) to prevent further collapse, reduce extravascular lung water, enhance oxygenation, and avert the progression of lung injury. While the techniques and experience related to lung recruitment in adult acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) are well-established, there remains limited experience in applying these methods to pediatric ARDS (PARDS)8,9. The 2023 Pediatric Acute Lung Injury Consensus Conference (PALICC) has updated the diagnostic and management guidelines for PARDS. However, due to insufficient and ineffective clinical research data, the implementation of lung recruitment in PARDS patients is neither recommended nor opposed at this time10.

The objective of this study is to investigate a safe and effective approach for the management of Pediatric Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (PARDS) lung recruitment in children. Lichtenstein D11 pioneered the use of ultrasound for diagnosing interstitial lung syndrome in 1994. Bouhemad B12 has reported favorable clinical outcomes in the lung recruitment of adult ARDS lungs utilizing scoring method for evaluating improvements in ultrasound indicators. Pulmonary ultrasound can be conducted at the bedside, offers high reproducibility, and poses no risk of radiation exposure, rendering it particularly suitable for pediatric patients13,14.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Taihe Hospital in Shiyan, and written informed consent was obtained from the parents or guardians of the participants. The study included 24 pediatric patients, aged between 4 weeks and 14 years, who underwent tracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation. The mean age of the participants was 36.79 ± 38.72 months, comprising 14 males and 10 females, all of whom met the Pediatric Acute Lung Injury Consensus Conference (PALICC) definition of Pediatric Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (PARDS)15. The duration of mechanical ventilation was restricted to less than 72 h16, with a Positive End-Expiratory Pressure (PEEP) level maintained between 5 and 15 cmH2O. The Servo-i ventilator (Maquet, Getinge Group, Sweden), suitable for both pediatric and adult patients, was employed.

Exclusion criteria included: meeting the PARDS diagnostic criteria but requiring mechanical ventilation for more than 72 h; hemodynamic instability; adjustment of vasoactive drug dosage by more than 50% within the preceding 6 h; blood lactate levels exceeding 2 mmol/L; conditions such as bronchiolitis, congenital diaphragmatic hernia, restrictive lung disease, or pulmonary fibrosis; continuous inhalation of nitric oxide or oral administration of sildenafil; severe traumatic brain injury; and the use of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) for lung support.

Methods

Preparation prior to lung recruitment

Prior to each lung recruitment procedure, adjust the FiO2 concentration to 100%. Commence lung recruitment after a 5-minute interval. Increase the dosage of sedatives from the period preceding lung recruitment to two hours post-procedure to ensure a state of deep sedation. Utilize midazolam at a dosage of 2–8 µg/kg/min to achieve a Ramsay sedation score of 4–517 and administer rocuronium bromide at 1.0 mg/kg/h for muscle relaxation. If the patient exhibits restlessness, experiences a heart rate (HR) fluctuation exceeding 20 beats per minute, develops new arrhythmias, or shows a decrease in systolic blood pressure exceeding 20 mmHg, or a reduction in pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2) greater than 5% during lung recruitment, the procedure should be immediately discontinued.

Lung recruitment method

In accordance with the protocol established by Wolf GK at Boston Children’s Hospital18, 24 pediatric patients underwent lung recruitment therapy (refer to Fig. 1).Utilize a linear array probe to assess the diaphragm’s thickness at the mid-axillary line, capturing measurements at both end-inspiration and end-expiration. Subsequently, calculate the diaphragm thickening fraction. A diaphragm thickening rate exceeding 20–30% indicates that lung recruitmen maneuver may be appropriate. Conversely, if the diaphragm thickening rate is below 18%, lung recruitmen maneuver is not advised19.

The initial phase of mechanical ventilation employs a volume control ventilation mode to deliver air at a low flow rate, with a tidal volume set at 6 ml/kg of ideal body weight. The pressure-volume (P-V) curve should be examined to identify the lower inflection point.

The pressure-controlled ventilation mode is adopted in the lung recruitment section. PEEP=15cmH2O and PPlat= 30cmH2O are used as the starting point of lung recruitment treatment. At this time, the respiratory rate is reduced to 20 ~ 25 times/min under the condition of deep sedation and muscle relaxation, the inspiratory time is increased to 1.2 s, FiO2 is maintained at 100%, and the parameters are adjusted after 2 min. PEEP and PPlat are both up-regulated at 5cmH2O and then maintained for 3 min. During this period, blood gas analysis and lung ultrasound are performed to determine the degree and sufficiency of lung recruitment. If PPlat reaches 50cmH2O but still cannot be reopened, the lung reopening operation needs to be stopped.

Determination of the endpoint of lung recruitment

To ascertain the endpoint of lung recruitment, pulmonary ultrasound signs under varying Positive End-Expiratory Pressure (PEEP) conditions are compared with those observed under Zero End-Expiratory Pressure (ZEEP) conditions. The ultrasound re-aeration score is calculated12, and when this score ceases to increase, anatomical lung recruitment is considered achieved, indicating that ultrasound-guided lung recruitment is adequate. Furthermore, if arterial blood gas analysis reveals that PaO2 + PaCO2 exceeds 400 mmHg, this suggests that maximal oxygenation and sufficient lung recruitment have been attained20, At this juncture, the combined PEEP and pressure control (PC) is identified as the lung opening pressure. Pulmonary opening pressure refers to the minimum airway pressure necessary to reopen collapsed alveoli during mechanical ventilation. This pressure must counteract factors such as alveolar surface tension, the elastic recoil of lung tissue, chest wall resistance, and gravitational compression. If the plateau pressure reaches 50 cmH2O without successful reopening6, the procedure should be halted to prevent barotrauma. Following successful lung recruitment, PEEP is gradually reduced. Continuous monitoring of oxygenation status and dynamic lung compliance is conducted to ensure hemodynamic and respiratory stability. Peripheral oxygen saturation is maintained between 97% and 100%, and the optimal PEEP value is determined through titration.

Pulmonary ultrasound examination methodology

In this study, a GE ultrasound machine equipped with a linear array probe (L4-12t,4.2–13.0 MHz) was utilized for dynamic ultrasound assessments conducted before, during, and after lung recruitment. The pulmonary regions were systematically divided into six specific probing points: the upper left BLUE point, lower left BLUE point, left PLAPS point, upper right BLUE point, lower right BLUE point, and right PLAPS point. Ultrasound examinations were conducted at the end of each respiratory cycle. The procedure was divided into two distinct phases: initially, the patient was positioned supine, allowing for the examination of bilateral upper and lower BLUE points; subsequently, the patient’s ipsilateral body was elevated to facilitate scanning of the PLAPS points on both sides of the posterior thorax. Each exploration point was meticulously assessed for fluid accumulation, consolidation, and interstitial changes, with particular attention given to any abnormalities at the diaphragm points. Emphasis was placed on real-time ultrasound monitoring of regions exhibiting the poorest lung ventilation to maintain hemodynamic stability throughout the entire lung recruitment process.

In 21 out of 24 cases, a low inflection point was identified at 13.5 ± 3.4 cmH2O using pressure-volume (P-V) curves. The remaining patients did not exhibit a discernible inflection point, potentially due to pulmonary lesions. Specifically, when the number of collapsed alveoli is minimal, the P-V curve lacks distinct features, and when alveolar collapse is extensive, the current ventilator settings may be inadequate to reopen them, resulting in the absence of a low inflection point. For the three children without identifiable low inflection points, the Positive End-Expiratory Pressure (PEEP) was set at baseline segment + 2 cmH2O.

Lung recruitment endpoints were achieved in 23 of the 24 patients, with ultrasound-induced lung recruitment endpoints frequently occurring after or concurrently with maximal oxygenation endpoints. Thus, both maximal oxygenation and ultrasound serve as effective guides for lung recruitment therapy. One child, diagnosed with leukemia and complicated by fungal pneumonia and secondary Pediatric Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (PARDS), experienced intracranial hemorrhage due to thrombocytopenia, necessitating the premature cessation of treatment.

In comparison to the maximum oxygenation technique, the ultrasound method demonstrated a significantly higher reaeration score (t = 3.62, P = 0.002) as illustrated in Fig. 2, and a greater lung opening pressure (t = 3.33, P = 0.003) as shown in Fig. 3. These findings suggest that the ultrasound method facilitates more comprehensive lung recruitment. The 95% confidence intervals for the ultrasound reaeration score (URS) and the differential in opening pressure between the oxygenation (OXY) and ultrasound (US) methods are [– 3.281, -0.893] and [– 3.882, -0.900], respectively.

The comparison between the maximal oxygenation endpoint and the ultrasound endpoint revealed that the ultrasound method resulted in higher PaO2 levels, lower PaCO2 levels, and a more pronounced improvement in dynamic lung compliance (Cdyn), with statistically significant differences. These findings suggest that ultrasound-guided lung recruitment may enhance lung ventilation, gas exchange, and dynamic lung compliance more effectively.

The historical control group included 25 children with ARDS who had previously received standard mechanical ventilation treatment, selected based on specific inclusion criteria. There were no statistically significant differences in gender, age, or etiology between these children and the 24 children in the experimental group. The duration of hospitalization in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) for the experimental group (11.31 ± 2.43 days) was significantly shorter than that of the historical control group who had not undergone lung recruitment (12.50 ± 2.73 days) (t = -2.11, p = 0.038). Importantly, no serious complications, such as pneumothorax, atelectasis, hypotension, or arrhythmia, were observed in either the historical control or experimental groups.High-pressure ventilation has the potential to exacerbate alveolar epithelial damage, trigger the release of inflammatory mediators, and induce or aggravate ventilator-associated lung injury (VALI)21,22. However, upon observation and analysis of the experimental group, no significant increase in the incidence of VALI was detected.

Discussion

During lung recruitment, ventilator settings are elevated, necessitating close monitoring of lung recruitment and alveolar overexpansion. Initially, the oxygenation index (P/F ratio) was used to assess lung recruitment23. Later, Borges JB et al. identified a 1:1 relationship between PCO2 and PO2 in alveoli at 100% oxygen concentration, suggesting lung recruitment is adequate when PaO2+PaCO2 ≥ 400mmHg, and alveolar collapse occurs at ≤ 380mmHg20. Other assessment methods include the P-V curve, pulmonary CT, and ultrasound. However, the P-V curve requires reduced respiratory rates and deep sedation, which can cause CO2 retention in children and fails to differentiate between alveolar recruitment and hyperinflation.While lung CT is considered the gold standard for assessing lung recruitment24, its application in critically ill pediatric patients necessitates their transport out of the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU), often requiring multiple repetitions. This process incurs cumulative transportation risks, exposes patients to potentially hazardous high-dose radiation, and involves significant costs. Consequently, there is a clinical demand for diagnostic methods that are bedside-accessible, reproducible, safe, and possess high sensitivity and specificity. In this regard, pulmonary ultrasound has emerged as a significant alternative. Pulmonary ultrasound primarily evaluates the B-ultrasound characteristics of two types of Pediatric Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (PARDS) lesions: interstitial lung syndrome and alveolar syndrome (pulmonary consolidation).

This study necessitates that the same pediatric subject sequentially achieve the endpoints of maximal oxygenation and ultrasound-induced lung recruitment. If the plateau pressure (PPlat) reaches 50 cmH2O without successful recruitment, the lung recruitment process should be terminated. The findings from the lung recruitment procedures indicated that in 20 out of 24 pediatric cases, the endpoint determined by maximal oxygenation did not correspond to complete lung recruitment as assessed by ultrasound, with a certain proportion of B2 signs still observable. Further increases in positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) and PPlat can facilitate the recruitment of partially collapsed alveoli, particularly at the PLAPS point, located in the lower lobe region of the lung. This approach can lead to the amelioration of some B2 signs and even C signs, thereby significantly enhancing lung ventilation and gas exchange function, and reducing anatomical shunting within the lungs.In three out of twenty-four pediatric cases, the endpoint of lung recruitment, as determined by maximal oxygenation, was achieved. Despite continued increases in PEEP and PPlat, no further improvements in ultrasound indicators were observed, suggesting that the endpoint of lung recruitment was concurrently reached according to both criteria. Statistical analyses indicate that the ventilation status at the lung recruitment endpoint was enhanced under both standards. Compared to maximal oxygenation, ultrasound-guided lung recruitment results in more comprehensive alveolar recruitment, effectively addressing pulmonary heterogeneity in pediatric patients. This approach significantly enhances dynamic lung compliance and arterial oxygenation levels.

It is important to acknowledge the potential risk of alveolar overdistension during ultrasound-guided lung recruitment. The fundamental characteristic of Pediatric Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (PARDS) is the heterogeneous alteration of lung tissue, where variations in compliance and resistance influence the rate of gas exchange within lung units, each possessing distinct time constants. To optimize lung recruitment, strategies such as reducing respiratory rate, extending inspiratory time, and increasing inspiratory pressure are employed. However, these approaches may inadvertently lead to overdistension of alveoli with shorter time constants. Consequently, protective measures are implemented: if alveoli remain collapsed despite the plateau pressure (PPlat) reaching 50 cmH2O, the lung recruitment procedure must be discontinued.

Positive pressure ventilation has the potential to decrease right ventricular preload while increasing afterload, which can exacerbate right ventricular dysfunction. Consequently, lung protective ventilation strategies should prioritize not only the optimization of blood gases but also the preservation of right ventricular function25,26. In this study, no significant alterations were observed in heart rate, mean arterial pressure, or central venous pressure among pediatric patients before and after lung recruitment, suggesting that the hemodynamic impact of maximal oxygenation and ultrasound-guided lung recruitment is minimal. Conversely, Das A et al. reported that the elevated intrathoracic pressure associated with lung recruitment may adversely affect cardiac function, necessitating an increased cardiac output. Mercado P conducted lung recruitment procedures on 20 patients with moderate to severe ARDS. Several patients experienced transient mild hypotension, left ventricular reduction with decreased ejection fraction, right ventricular enlargement, and reduced cardiac output. Upon completion of lung recruitment, all hemodynamic parameters returned to baseline levels27.

Bhattacharjee S et al. performed a comprehensive meta-analysis and systematic review involving a large sample of 2,480 patients from seven randomized controlled trials, focusing on lung recruitment in adult ARDS patients. The findings indicated that combined interventions, such as lung recruitment strategies and PEEP titration, did not confer any benefit regarding mortality, ICU length of stay, or overall hospital stay in patients with ARDS28.

Conclusion

Pulmonary ultrasound facilitates real-time assessment of subpleural alveolar aeration levels, aligning with the observational criteria necessary for guiding lung recruitment. The findings of this study substantiate the following: (1) At the conclusion of ultrasound-guided lung recruitment, pediatric patients with Pediatric Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (PARDS) exhibit more complete lung recruitment, alongside marked improvements in dynamic lung compliance and arterial oxygen partial pressure. (2) Pulmonary ultrasound has emerged as a prominent area of research within critical care, attributed to its operability, reproducibility, non-invasive nature, and absence of radiation exposure at the bedside. It demonstrates a sensitivity and specificity exceeding 95% for detecting ultrasound indicators of alveolar/interstitial syndrome in PARDS, thereby effectively guiding lung recruitment to the desired endpoint29. (3) A potential limitation is the risk of alveolar overdistension, which can be effectively mitigated by maintaining plateau pressure (PPlat) below 50 cmH2O.

Owing to constraints in research duration and conditions, the sample size in this study is relatively limited, necessitating further validation of the conclusions through an expanded sample. Additionally, a comprehensive analysis of the hemodynamic impact has not yet been undertaken; further elucidation will be addressed in future research endeavors.
