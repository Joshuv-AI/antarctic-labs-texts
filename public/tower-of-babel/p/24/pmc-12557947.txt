Sophora alopecuroides biflavones glycoside, isolated from Sophora alopecuroides, inhibits the secretion of hepatitis B virus surface antigen through direct interaction with its antigenic loop domain
Authors: Ya Wang, Linlin Wang, Ge Yang, Lijun Qiao, Rongmei Gao, Huiqiang Wang, Haiyan Yan, Kun Wang, Shuo Wu, Hua Huang, Yuhuan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh levels of circulating hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) contribute to the dysfunction of the host immune response against HBV and the persistence of viral infection. This study aimed to investigate the effects and mechanisms of Sophora alopecuroides biflavones glycoside (SABG), isolated from the traditional herbal medicine Sophora alopecuroides, on HBV replication and HBsAg secretion.MethodsTo systematically evaluate the antiviral efficacy, a comprehensive set of experimental analyses was employed, including qPCR, RT-qPCR, ELISA, Western blot and particle gel assay. The DIA-based proteomic analysis, CETSA, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulation analyses were combined to explore the potential mechanisms.ResultsWe discovered that SABG could not only remarkably inhibit intracellular level of HBV core DNA but also significantly reduce the extracellular levels of HBV DNA, pgRNA, HBsAg and e antigen (HBeAg). Intriguingly, SABG had no impact on the intracellular levels of HBV pgRNA and core protein; instead, it caused abnormal accumulation of S protein. Further mechanistic studies revealed that SABG could bind directly to the antigenic loop (AGL) domain of HBs, thereby obstructing its post-translational cellular vesicle-based transport process. Finally, we observed a synergistic effect of SABG and lamivudine in the inhibition of intracellular level of HBV core DNA and the extracellular level of HBsAg.ConclusionOur studies reveal that SABG exhibits an antiviral activity against HBV by directly binding to the antigenic loop of surface protein, thereby impeding its secretion, which provides pharmacological evidence for the potential use of SABG as a candidate for the alternative treatment of HBV infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-025-02970-w.

Background

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a serious global public health issue, with 296 million HBV carriers worldwide, who are at risk of progressing to cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and liver failure, leading to approximately 820,000 deaths annually [1, 2]. The current approved therapeutic treatments for chronic hepatitis B (CHB) include pegylated-interferon α (PEG-IFNα) and nucleot(s)ide analogues (NAs). However, it is difficult to be feasible for the eradication of HBV and virological cure, due to the persistence of integrated HBV DNA and covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) and impaired HBV-specific immune responses [3]. A functional HBV cure, which is defined as a finite treatment followed by sustained loss of HBV surface antigen (HBsAg), with or without anti-HBs seroconversion, and undetectable HBV DNA in serum, is currently considered as the ideal goal for CHB therapy [4]. Unfortunately, few patients achieve a functional cure with current therapies, and most CHB patients require lifelong treatment.

HBV belongs to the hepatotropic DNA virus family and contains a ~ 3.2 kb partial double-stranded relaxed circular DNA (rcDNA) [5]. A key aspect of HBV biology involves the substantial release of both complete and incomplete virus particles [6, 7]. Mature nucleocapsids harboring rcDNA acquire HBsAg-mediated envelopment, assembling into infectious HBV virions destined for extracellular export. Unlike infectious virions, HBV produces three forms of incomplete virus particle, including RNA-encapsidating particles, genome-deficient empty virions, and subviral particles (SVPs) which lack both the genome and the capsid. These SVPs are spherical or filamentous lipoprotein structures, solely composed of small (S), middle (M), or large (L) S proteins, and are roughly 22 nm in diameter. As the most abundant viral particles, their blood concentration in infected individuals can reach up to 100,000 times higher than those complete virions. The elevated circulating HBsAg levels, driven predominantly by SVPs, are hypothesized to induce functional impairment of HBV-specific cytotoxic T cells and promote immune tolerance. This process may facilitate the progression to chronic hepatitis B infection [8, 9]. In CHB patients, sustained HBsAg presence coincides with pronounced upregulation of T cell inhibitory receptors (including PD-1, CTLA-4, and TIM-3), coupled with compromised T cell proliferative capacity and diminished cytokine secretion [10–12]. These patients also display an increase in unconventional memory B cells and an insufficiency in serum anti-HBsAg antibody production [13, 14]. Moreover, CHB patients with lower baseline HBsAg levels (< 1500 IU/ml) have a significantly higher rate of achieving functional cure when receiving PEG-IFNα therapy [15]. Therefore, diminishing or eradicating HBsAg levels could aid in reinvigorating host immune function. This restoration represents a critical prerequisite for attaining a functional cure of chronic hepatitis B.

Traditional Chinese medicines (TCMs) have a long-standing history in China of providing effective strategies for chronic liver diseases and virus infections. For instance, several TCMs, including Salvia miltiorrhiza, Artemisia capillaris and Sophora flavescens have been proven to exhibit anti-HBV efficacy [16–18]. Sophora alopecuroides (Sophora alopecuroides L.) is a Fabaceae perennial herb, and mainly distributed in the desert and semi-desert areas of northern China [19]. In China, the initial documentation of Sophora alopecuroides appears in Shennong’s Classic of Materia Medica, a foundational text in TCM pharmacology. As an important ethnic medicine, Sophora alopecuroides has long been traditionally used for multiple therapeutic purposes, including clearing heat and detoxifying, promoting diuresis, exerting antiseptic effects, treating gastrointestinal disorders and hyperlipidemia, and managing heart diseases as well as rheumatism [20–22].

Sophora alopecuroides contains various chemical components including alkaloids, flavonoids, volatile oil. Accumulating evidence indicates that it plays an important role in multiple applications, such as anti-tumor [23, 24], anti-bacterial [25], anti-inflammation activities [26, 27], as well as the protection of central nervous system [28, 29] and liver [30]. Among these components, researchers have conducted extensive investigations on the alkaloids, particularly matrine and oxymatrine. These two alkaloids have been demonstrated to exhibit significant anti-HBV effects and registered as anti-HBV agents, which are commonly used as adjuvant medications in combination therapies [31–33]. In contrast, the research on the potential therapeutic effects of other chemical components in Sophora alopecuroides L. is relatively limited. In our previous research, we discovered that the total flavonoids extracts from Sophora alopecuroides L. could not only effectively inhibit the HBV DNA replication in HepG2.2.15 cells, but also reduce the serum viral load in the duck hepatitis B model [34]. To further confirm the antiviral effect and mechanism of the flavonoids, we have successively separated four flavonoids components: Sophora alopecuroides biflavones glycoside, (3,7,3′,4′)-tetramethoxy flavone, butein and 3′-methoxyluteolin from Sophora alopecuroides [35]. Herein, we report the research progress on Sophora alopecuroides biflavones glycoside, covering its anti-HBV effect and related molecular mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human hepatoblastoma cells HepG2.2.15, which expresses steady HBV genome, were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 400 µg/mL G418 (Gibco) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Compounds

Isolation and identification of the flavonoid ingredients in Sophora alopecuroides. For the isolation process, take the seeds of Sophora alopecuroides and add 10-fold the volume of water. Then, perform reflux extraction three times, with each extraction lasting for 1 hour. Collect the extracts and concentrate them under reduced - pressure conditions. Next, add moderate amount of water to dilute the concentrated solution. After that, add 3-fold the volume of ethanol, mix well, and let it stand for 12 h. Filter the mixture, concentrate the filtrate under the condition of reduced pressure to obtain the extracts. Mix the extracts with a moderate amount of polyamide, and then pass the polyamide chromatography column. Subsequently, elute the chromatography column successively with water, 20% ethanol, 40% ethanol, 60% ethanol, and 95% ethanol. Handle the fraction eluted with 20% ethanol repeatedly using Sephadex LH − 20 (in methanol) to obtain the compound. The compound is a light-yellow powder (methanol), with a melting point of 263–264 °C. Its 1H-NMR(400 MHz, DMSO-d6) δ values and 13C-NMR (100 MHz, DMSO-d6) δ values are consistent with those reported in the literature [35]. Thus, it is identified as Sophora alopecuroides biflavones glycoside (5”-methoxy-6-C-β-D-glucopyranosyl- 6”-C-β-D- xylopyranosyl cupressuflavone, SABG).

Lamivudine (3TC, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), Bay41-4109 (MedChemExpress), and NVR3-778 (synthesized in-house) were dissolved in DMSO and stored at −20℃.

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity of SABG in HepG2.2.15 cells was determined using the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) as previously described [36].

HBV DNA extraction and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assay

Intracellular HBV core DNA was extracted from HepG2.2.15 cells as previously described [36]. Briefly, cells were lysed with lysis buffer containing 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, and 50 mM NaCl. After centrifugation, cell debris and nuclei were removed and the supernatant was digested at 37 °C for 1 h with protease K (Sigma). The nucleic acids were extracted with phenol and precipitated with 2 volumes of ethanol. The pellet was washed with 75% ethanol and dissolved in nuclease-free water. And then HBV core DNA was quantified by qPCR using TransStart Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech) with primers 5’ -GGCTTTCGGAAAATTCCTATG-3’ (sense) and 5’ -AGCCCTACGAACCACTGAAC-3’ (antisense). Quantification of supernatant HBV DNA was performed using the HBV-specific nucleic acid quantitative detection kit (Sansure Biotech Inc, Changsha, Hunan, China). All qPCR runs were carried out using an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA).

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) assay

Total cellular RNA extraction from HepG2.2.15 cells was performed using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Encapsidated HBV RNA was extracted as previously described [37]. HBV RNA from the culture medium’s supernatant was isolated using a FastPure Viral DNA/RNA Mini Kit (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). The isolated HBV RNA was then digested with DNase I (Invitrogen) to remove contaminating DNA. The level of HBV pgRNA was determined using the One-Step HiScript II RT-qPCR SYBR Green Kit (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) as described previously [36]. The level of HBV pgRNA was detected with the sense primer 5′- GAGTGTGGATTCGCACTCC − 3′and the antisense primer 5′- GAGGCGAGGGAGTTCTTCT − 3. The level of GAPDH mRNA was detected using the sense primer 5′-CTCTGGAAAGCTGTGGCGTGATG-3′ and the antisense primer 5′-ATGCCAGTGAGCTTCCCGTTCAG-3. Relative quantification was carried out using the comparative threshold cycle (∆∆CT) method.

Protein extraction and Western blot assay

Cellular protein extraction was performed by lysing cells in M-PER reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in the presence of a halt protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo). The primary antibodies included antibodies against GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology), HBc (synthesized by GenScript, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China), and HBs (Novus Biologicals).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Levels of HBsAg and HBeAg and in cell culture supernatants were measured by commercial ELISA kits (Chemclin, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Particle gel assay

Intracellular HBV capsids were analyzed through a native agarose gel electrophoresis-based assay [38] by probing the membrane with an anti-HBc antibody (synthesized by GenScript, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China).

Cellular thermal shift assay

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) was carried out as previously described [39]. Briefly, HepG2.2.15 cells were cultured in complete medium for 96 h. Following digestion with trypsin, the cell pellet was resuspended in PBS supplemented with protease inhibitors (Thermo). Cellular proteins were released via three consecutive freeze-thaw cycles. After centrifugation for 20 min (12, 000 rpm, 4 °C), cell lysate was incubated with DMSO or SABG for 30 min at room temperature and heated at the indicated temperatures (37–72 °C) for 3 min, The supernatant was collected and subject to Western blotting to detect the levels of HBs or HBc.

Transient-transfection assay

HepG2 cells were transfected with indicated plasmid (0.5 µg) using TransIntro EL transfection reagent (TransGen Biotech). In total, 6 h after transfection, the culture media was substituted with fresh media containing various concentrations of the compounds and cultured for extra 72 h. The intracellular HBV core protein and S protein was tested via the Western blot assay.

Proteomics analysis

HepG2.2.15 cells cultured in completed medium were treated with DMSO or 50 µg/mL SABG for 72 h. Cells were harvested and then the data-independent acquisition (DIA)-based proteomic analysis was performed as described previously [39]. The Majorbio Cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com) was utilized to conduct bioinformatic analysis of proteomic data. Proteins exhibiting a fold change >1.2 or < 0.83 and a statistically significant P-value < 0.05 were classified as differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). Subsequently, all identified proteins underwent functional annotation via GO (http://geneontology.org/) and KEGG pathway (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) enrichment analyses.

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation

The interaction between SABG and HBs and their binding mode was computationally deciphered using Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) software. The detailed structure of HBs protein was sourced from the study by Wang et al. [40]. The optimized conformation underwent analysis and visualization through PyMOL software.

Molecular dynamics simulations were executed in Gromacs 2022, employing the Amber99sb-ildn force field for protein systems and GAFF2 parameters for small molecule energetics. The TIP3P model was applied to incorporate water into the system, construct a 10 × 10 × 10 nm3 water box, and establish a self-equilibrating ion system. Electrostatic interactions were calculated using the Particle-Mesh Ewald (PME) approach. The Gromacs embedded program was then used to analyze and visualize the radius of gyration, root-mean-square deviation, and root-mean-square fluctuation.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean and were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with significance thresholds set as * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, and *** P < 0.001.

Results

SABG inhibits HBV replication without affecting the transcription of pgRNA in HepG2.2.15 cells

A CCK assay was first employed to evaluate the cytotoxicity of SABG (the chemical structure is shown in Fig. 1A) extracted from Sophora alopecuroides in HepG2.2.15 cells. The results showed that cell viability remained unaffected when the concentration of SABG was 100 µg/mL or lower (Fig. 1B). Hence, 100 µg/mL of SABG was chosen as the maximum concentration for subsequent experiments.

To determine the anti-HBV activity of SABG, the intracellular HBV core DNA levels in HepG2.2.15 cells treated with different concentrations of SABG for 6 days were detected by a qPCR assay. We observed that 25, 50, and 100 µg/mL of SABG substantially suppressed the levels of intracellular HBV core DNA achieving 40.8%, 51.3% and 72.4%, respectively. The nucleos(t)ide analog 3TC was included as a reference drug and led to a reduction in the intracellular HBV core DNA level by more than 85% (Fig. 1C).

To evaluate the impact of SABG on HBV pgRNA, total cellular and encapsidated pgRNA were extracted and quantified by a qRT-PCR assay. SABG had no effect on the intracellular HBV pgRNA level (Fig. 1D). However, SABG significantly reduced the level of encapsidated pgRNA. In contrast, 3TC (used as a negative control) did not decrease but instead increased the encapsidated pgRNA level. Meanwhile, NVR 3–778, which served as a positive control, also led to a significant reduction in encapsidated pgRNA (Fig. 1E). Taken together, these data indicate that SABG exhibits potent antiviral activity against HBV, as it interferes with HBV replication and pgRNA packaging without impacting pgRNA transcription.

SABG treatment effectively inhibits the extracellular levels of HBV-related products in HepG2.2.15 cells

To further evaluate the anti-HBV activity of SABG, the culture supernatants were harvested six days post-treatment and the virological indicators including HBV DNA, pgRNA, HBsAg, and HBeAg were measured. As presented in Fig. 2A-B, SABG treatment could lead to significant reduction in HBV DNA and a modest one in HBV pgRNA. Moreover, ELISA assay results revealed that the levels of extracellular HBsAg and HBeAg were significantly suppressed following SABG treatment, with the suppression of HBsAg levels being more pronounced (Fig. 2C-D). Consistent with its antiviral mechanism and previous reports, HBV DNA polymerase inhibitor 3TC treatment did not lower the levels of extracellular HBV pgRNA, HBsAg and HBeAg, but entirely reduced HBV DNA level. Collectively, the results demonstrated that SABG effectively inhibited the extracellular levels of HBV-related products, including HBV DNA, pgRNA, HBsAg and HBeAg.

SABG may obstruct the secretion of HBsAg by interfering with its post-translational cellular vesicle-based transport process

The secretion of HBV virions and incomplete viral particles is associated with viral protein translation and transport, particle assembly, and other biosynthetic pathways. To determine the biosynthesis pathways susceptible to SABG, we then utilized the particle gel assay and Western blot assay to measure the intracellular capsid levels and HBV surface (HBs) and core (HBc) proteins levels, respectively, in cells treated with SABG. Meanwhile, 3TC, along with capsid assembly modulators (CpAMs) Bay 41–4109, or NVR 3–778 was used as control compounds. As shown in Fig. 3A and D, 3TC failed to alter the levels of capsid and HBV core protein. In contrast, class I CpAM Bay 41–4109 could completely abolish the formation of HBV capsid and class II CpAM NVR 3–778 could induce a capsid electrophoresis mobility shift and had no effect on the levels of HBc and HBs proteins. These results are consistent with their proposed mechanism and previous findings [41–43]. Interestingly, SABG treatment did not alter the content of capsid and HBc protein, but it could significantly promote the accumulation of HBs protein in cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3A and D). The above results were further validated in HepG2 cells transfected with plasmid pHBV1.3, pCMV-HBc or pCMV-HBs (Fig. 3E and H, Fig. S2). Given the substantial reduction in the extracellular HBsAg level following SABG administration, we tentatively inferred that SABG effectively impeded the secretion of HBsAg.

To gain a deeper understanding of the potential function of SABG in relation to HBs, we conducted a data-independent acquisition-based proteomic analysis to investigate differentially expressed proteins in HepG2.2.15 cells treated with SABG. As shown in Figure 3I, we did observe a significant modulation of cellular vesicle trafficking proteins, including PDCD6IP, STX17, SEC31A, SNX24, SEC22A. These proteins are tightly involved in regulating intracellular membrane transport and vesicle fusion processes, thereby influencing the post-translational trafficking and secretion of HBsAg [44, 45]. Moreover, the KEGG pathways analysis revealed that there are 16 proteins related to transport, catabolism, fording, sorting and degradation underwent significant systemic changes in the presence of SABG (Fig. 3J). Taken together, these results imply that SABG may block the extracellular secretion of HBs by interfering with its post-translational cellular vesicle-based transport process.

To clarify whether the observed effect of SABG is specific to HBV or represents a broader influence on ER-Golgi trafficking, we further conducted a Gaussia luciferase secretion assay. As shown in Fig. S1, the levels of Gaussia luciferase in both cells and culture supernatants remained unchanged after SABG treatment. This finding demonstrates that SABG had no effect on the intracellular transport or secretion of Gaussia luciferase. Therefore, it can be concluded that SABG specifically targets HBV secretion without affecting general ER-Golgi-mediated trafficking pathways.

SABG directly bind to the antigen loop of HBV surface antigen

To further explore whether the compound SABG directly interacts with HBs, we sequentially conducted cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA), molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation analysis. Firstly, CETSA experiment was conducted using HepG2.2.15 cell lysate incubated with either SABG or DMSO to confirm the interaction of compound SABG and HBs. As expected, incubation with SABG had a remarkable impact on the thermal stability of HBs, as evidenced by a leftward shift of CETSA melting curve (Fig. 4A, B). In contrast, it had no impact on the thermal stability of HBc (Fig. 4A, C).

Subsequently, we carried out molecular docking simulations using Molecular Operating Environment software (MOE) to comprehend the interaction between the target compound SABG and HBs, and the resulting binding mode was visualized by PyMOL. As illustrated in Fig. 4D and E, the optimized conformation of the interaction compound between SABG and HBs was displayed in an intuitive manner and SABG binds to the antigenic loop (AGL) of HBs protein [46]. Hydrogen bonds were observed between hydroxyl oxygen of SABG and the side chains both Ile110, Ser114, Thr116 and Ile150. Additionally, the phenyl group of SABG established hydrophobic interaction with Cys121, Cys124, Cys149, which are located within the conformational epitopes of the “a” determinant and are crucial for HBV infectivity [47].

To comprehensively understand the binding mode of SABG to the antigen loop of HBs, we next conducted a 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation using Gromacs software [48, 49]. The volume and structural state of protein-SABG complex was monitored by radius of gyration (Rg). The larger the Rg value indicated a looser the structure of the complex. After SABG binding, the Rg value decreased, suggesting that the complex structure tightened (Fig. 4F). The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) was used to reflect the overall changes in atomic coordinates. The protein exhibited significant fluctuations within the first 20 ns, followed by a decrease in amplitude, and finally converged at the end of the simulation (Fig. 4G). Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) was used to analyze the fluctuations of protein amino acid residues, and we noted that the two monomers constituting the HBs dimer exhibited larger fluctuations in the head and tail residues than in the middle residue (Fig. 4H). Notably, HBs protein and compound SABG can interact with each other as shown in Fig. 4I (residue decomposition diagram), and the contribution of Pro111 residue to binding was remarkably higher than that of other residues. By examining different frames in Fig. 4J-M during 100 ns MD process, we found that SABG stably binds to the AGL domain of HBs, causing a structural contraction of HBs (Fig. 4J-M). These results elucidate the underlying structural factors that disturb HBs secretion induced by SABG.

Based on above molecular dynamics simulation analysis, we further performed individual single mutations on each of the nine amino acid residues in Fig. 4I. After treating HepG2 cells transfected with pHBV1.3 or its derived mutant plasmids with SABG, we measured the intracellular levels of HBs. As shown in Fig. 5, the P108A and C124A mutants no longer elicited an intracellular accumulation in HBsAg upon SABG treatment. The results indicate that the two residues P108 and C124 are critical for the binding interaction between SABG and HBs.

Effect of SABG and 3TC alone and in combination on HBV DNA, HBsAg and hbeag in HepG2.2.15 cells

Currently, nucleos(t)ide analogues are the most commonly used standard - of - care medications for chronic hepatitis B. Long-term treatment with nucleos(t)ide analogues inhibits viral replication remarkably. However, HBsAg reduction is rarely achieved. In contemporary antiviral therapies against HBV, the use of drug cocktails or combinations has become more prevalent [3]. The combined effects of SABG with 3TC on intracellular HBV DNA, as well as extracellular HBsAg and HBeAg levels, were then examined in HepG2.2.15 cells. After a 6-day treatment, when compared to the mock-treated control, both SABG and 3TC monotherapy showed similar alternation in intracellular HBV core DNA level or HBsAg/HBeAg secretion as previous results (Fig. 5A-B). SABG monotherapy was less effective than 3TC monotherapy in reducing HBV core DNA. A significantly enhanced synergistic efficacy of SABG and 3TC combination in inhibiting intracellular HBV core DNA was observed. The synergistic effect of SABG and 3TC was also evident in reducing the extracellular HBsAg level, regardless of whether the treatment lasted for 3 or 6 days (Fig. 5C and D). These results indicate the potential of SABG to be used as adjuvant drug in combination therapy.

Discussion

In our previous research, we first reported that the total flavonoids from Sophora alopecuroides exhibited anti-HBV activity both in vitro and in vivo [34]. However, the roles and mechanisms of each flavonoid component were still illusive. We have successfully isolated and identified four flavonoid components from Sophora alopecuroides. In this article, we primarily focus on the anti-HBV activity and mechanism of the main flavonoid component SABG. Our data demonstrated that SABG can reduce the intracellular HBV DNA and encapsidated pgRNA levels, and decrease the levels of HBV DNA, pgRNA, HBsAg and HBeAg in extracellular supernatant. Further mechanistic studies confirmed that SABG can specifically target HBs by binding to its AGL domain, inducing conformational changes, that may impede HBs secretion. Combination administration experiments showed that the SABG and 3TC have a synergistic effect at reducing both intracellular HBV core DNA and extracellular HBsAg levels.

High levels of circulating HBsAg in chronic HBV carriers not only suppresses the immune activity of monocytes, dendritic cells and natural killer cells, but also causes the exhaustion of cytotoxic T lymphocytes and helper T (Th) cells, and thus finally contributes to HBV persistent infection [50]. In addition, HBsAg also promotes the disease progression from CHB to liver cirrhosis and HCC.

HBsAg serves as the primary structural component of both mature HBV particles and subviral envelope particles. Beyond its scaffolding role in virion assembly, evidence suggests that HBsAg plays critical regulatory roles in viral entry and egress processes. Based on their mechanisms of action, current therapeutic strategies targeting HBsAg can be classified into two distinct categories: one is entry inhibitors, such as bulevirtide, which mimic the HBV preS1 domain to block the HBsAg binding to entry receptor sodium taurocholate co-transporting polypeptide (NTCP) [51]. And the other is secretion inhibitors, such as nucleic acid polymers (NAPs) and HBsAg traffic-inhibiting oligonucleotide polymers (STOPs). NAPs have demonstrated rapid clearance of circulating HBsAg in clinical studies without significantly affecting other viral markers [2]. Furthermore, a phase II trial involving 40 patients revealed that NAPs combined with tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) were associated with HBsAg seroclearance and functional cure achievement [52]. Despite these advances, natural compounds directly targeting HBsAg for antiviral activity remain largely unexplored. Our findings established that SABG exerts its antiviral effects through a novel secretion inhibition mechanism, thereby providing a molecular rationale for developing SABG-based anti-HBV therapeutics.

The S protein leaves the ER through the COP II vesicle transport machinery. After budding from the ER or the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC), the SVPs are transported through the Golgi apparatus and ultimately secreted from hepatocytes through exocytosis. A DIA-based proteomic analysis indicated SABG treatment triggered a significant alteration in the expression of cellular vesicle trafficking proteins (Fig. 3I-J), which imply that SABG could potentially modulate post-translational trafficking mechanisms and suppress HBsAg secretion.

HBsAg is a 226-residue integral membrane glycoprotein embedded within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). Its topology is characterized by four transmembrane domains (TM1- TM4), two cytosolic loops (CYL-1, and CYL-2) and an extracellular antigenic loop (AGL) (Fig. 6) [53]. Within the HBs, the AGL domain (residues 99–169) traverses the extracellular region between transmembrane domains TM2 and TM3. This surface-accessible region displays identical structural topology on both infectious virions and subviral particles, harboring the immunodominant “a” determinant. This region is abundant in cysteine residues that form disulfide bonds to stabilize its structural integrity. Furthermore, the AGL domain interacts with heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) for subsequent non-reversible, high-affinity interaction between PreS1 domain of L protein and NTCP [46]. Therefore, AGL domain is a pivotal viral factor of HBV infectivity. In addition, AGL domain can regulate the secretion of virions through an essential N-linked glycosylation site at N146, as evidenced by glycosylation-deficient mutants exhibiting impaired secretion [54]. Notably, our data reveal that SABG specifically targets the AGL domain through structural interference, resulting in dose-dependent suppression of virion secretion (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). While these findings establish AGL as a pharmacologically tractable target, the impact of SABG on viral infectivity remains to be evaluated.

Conclusions

In summary, our study has successfully provided evidence that SABG can target HBsAg by binding its AGL domain, thereafter inhibiting HBV replication and secretion (Fig. 7). Moreover, SABG and 3TC exhibit a synergistic effect in anti-HBV activity. Our findings expand the antiviral spectrum of Sophora alopecuroides and present a parent compound that can be followed by structural optimization to serve as a novel anti-HBV agent with a unique mechanism.
