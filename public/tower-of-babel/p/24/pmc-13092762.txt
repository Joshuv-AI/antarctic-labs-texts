Evaluation of Imaging Modalities for Superior Mesenteric Artery Stenosis: A Single Centre Study
Authors: Alexandra A. Brandtzäg, Jonas P. Eiberg, Mikkel Taudorf, Lars B. Lönn, Timothy A. Resch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTo evaluate intra-arterial pressure measurement (IAPM) in relation to pre- and intra-operative diagnostic methods for assessing superior mesenteric artery (SMA) stenosis in patients with a diagnosis of chronic mesenteric ischaemia.MethodThis secondary analysis compares computed tomography angiography (CTA), duplex ultrasound (DUS), digital subtraction angiography (DSA), and IAPM, using IAPM as the reference standard. Thirty patients were enrolled in the Copenhagen Mesenteric Stent Study (COMESS), a randomised clinical trial evaluating covered vs. uncovered stents for treating SMA stenosis (COMESS trial, ClinicalTrials.org identifier NCT05244629). All included patients were thoroughly examined upon referral to exclude other relevant causes of chronic abdominal pain. All patients had a clinical diagnosis of chronic mesenteric ischaemia and >50% SMA stenosis on CTA. All underwent DUS, DSA, and IAPM. DUS assessed the peak systolic velocity (centimetres per second) across the SMA. During endovascular intervention, DSA was performed in the anteroposterior and lateral projections. A dedicated pressure wire measured the trans-stenotic mean arterial pressure gradient between the SMA and aorta.ResultsThere was a statistically significant positive correlation between DUS and IAPM (r = 0.698, p = 0.003, 95% confidence interval 253–373), indicating a moderately strong association, with a peak systolic velocity of 372 cm/sec on DUS corresponding to a mean arterial pressure between 15 and 20 mmHg. DSA showed a non-significant correlation with IAPM (r = 0.376, p = 0.11, standard deviation 0.128), with 59% stenosis corresponding to a 10 mmHg gradient. There was no significant correlation between CTA and IAPM (r = 0.143, p = 0.50, 95% confidence interval −43 – 60).ConclusionDUS shows the best association with IAPM when differentiating a significant SMA stenosis. In contrast, CTA demonstrated no significant correlation with IAPM; however, CTA is of importance for differential diagnosis. Therefore, when SMA stenosis is identified on CTA, further confirmation with DUS is recommended, for moderate to less than pre-occlusive or occlusive SMA stenosis, to determine the need for DSA and potential endovascular treatment.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic mesenteric ischaemia (CMI) is most frequently caused by atherosclerosis in the mesenteric arteries, primarily the superior mesenteric artery (SMA).1 Diagnosing CMI is challenging, and guidelines are inconsistent regarding diagnostic methods.2, 3, 4 No reporting standards exist and there is great variability in the literature. The definition of significant stenosis in most arteries is a 60–70% narrowing of the diameter on digital subtraction angiography (DSA) or computed tomography angiography (CTA), or a trans-lesional pressure gradient of 10–20 mmHg.5 In the SMA, >50% stenosis (corresponding to a peak systolic velocity [PSV] of >295 cm/sec) can be considered significant.3,6

Although DSA has historically been the gold standard for diagnosing a significant SMA stenosis, it remains an invasive procedure and is seldom used as the first modality when evaluating CMI.3 During angiography, intra-arterial pressure measurement (IAPM) can assess the haemodynamic significance of a stenosis. This is a well established technique in the renal and iliac arteries, where a mean arterial pressure (MAP) gradient >10 mmHg or a systolic pressure gradient >20 mmHg is commonly used to define a significant stenosis.7, 8, 9 Lundin et al.10 established the utility of IAPM in SMA stenosis and demonstrated reasonable performance of grading SMA re-stenosis with CTA when using IAPM as reference.5

CTA is the preferred method for diagnosis of SMA stenosis in clinical practice due to its non-invasiveness, widespread availability, and high resolution imaging.11 CTA also allows for assessing other comorbidities and differential diagnoses. Although CTA is excellent for diagnosing arterial occlusions, stenoses are often overestimated, particularly due to ‘blooming’ artefacts in patients with calcified lesions.12, 13, 14

Duplex ultrasound (DUS) is an excellent, non-invasive diagnostic modality to determine a significant SMA stenosis. A normal DUS examination can effectively exclude a significant SMA stenosis.3,6,15,16 However, accurately visualising severely diseased mesenteric arteries remains challenging, even for experienced practitioners. In addition to common limitations such as bowel gas and respiratory motion, patients with CMI may present additional difficulties due to arterial calcification and inconsistent collaterals.

Studies comparing the diagnostic performance of different imaging modalities in the pre-operative assessment of CMI are few. Assessing the performance of diagnostic methods is of importance to prevent unwarranted interventions in patients who may not have typical clinical signs of CMI. Although IAPM is used to establish the haemodynamic significance of the stenosis, it is an invasive procedure. Therefore, there is a need to compare non-invasive imaging modalities with IAPM, to establish appropriate diagnostic criteria in the pre-operative setting.

This study aimed to compare CTA, DUS, DSA, and IAPM in patients with symptomatic atherosclerotic disease of the SMA, using IAPM as the reference standard.

Study design

The Copenhagen Mesenteric Stent Study (COMESS) trial (ClinicalTrials.org identifier NCT05244629)17 is a prospective, randomised controlled trial of patients with a diagnosis of CMI undergoing elective stenting of SMA stenosis with covered vs. bare metal stents. This study reports a secondary analysis of COMESS comparing CTA, DUS, DSA, and IAPM of the SMA in patients enrolled in the COMESS trial, using IAPM as reference standard. Data collection was carried out prospectively throughout 2023–2024.

METHOD

Patients

The study included 30 patients with >50% SMA stenosis on CTA. Patients were referred to a tertiary centre for evaluation of SMA revascularisation with no history of previous surgical or endovascular CMI treatment. Patients were referred for treatment if they fulfilled the diagnostic criteria (relevant clinical symptoms, CTA verified SMA stenosis >50%, DUS with PSV of >295 cm/sec)6 and had clinically relevant symptoms consistent with CMI.

Treatment of CMI is centralised to a tertiary centre. Patients are referred to a multidisciplinary conference where the patient history and previous diagnostic workup are available and evaluated. When necessary, further diagnostic workup or alternative diagnoses are recommended. If no other cause is found, patients undergo DUS in the outpatient clinic. Endovascular treatment (EVT) of the SMA is scheduled when relevant.

Elective CMI patients were included if they had a SMA stenosis >50% and <4 cm length on CTA with atherosclerotic or atherothrombotic aetiology. Asymptomatic patients or patients with signs of acute mesenteric ischaemia, previous laparotomy due to acute mesenteric ischaemia, lesion length >4 cm, or significant stenosis on CTA were excluded.17

Ethics

The trial was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Regional Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients participating in this study (ClinicalTrials.org identifier NCT05244629) (Regional Ethics Committee in the Capital Region of Denmark, H-22004003).

Imaging methods

CTA. CTA was used to determine the degree of SMA stenosis. CTA was performed with a slice thickness from 0.625 – 2.00 mm. Intra-arterial contrast volume ranged from 50 – 100 mL per examination (Omnipaque 350 mg/mL, GE Healthcare AS, Chicago, USA; Iomeron 350 mg/mL, Bracco Imaging S.p.A., Milan, Italy). The SMA was assessed in sagittal, axial, and coronal projections, with the degree of stenosis calculated using the North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial (NASCET) method ([B −A]/B) × 100, where A is the minimum luminal diameter and B the largest diameter distal to the stenosis or occlusion.10,18 Lesion length was measured. Stenosis morphology and its location relative to take off from the aorta as well as distal branches were noted.19 Patency of the coeliac (CA) and the inferior mesenteric arteries (IMA) were registered. CTA was reconstructed at a 3D workstation (TeraRecon Aquarius software; TeraRecon Inc, Durham, NC, USA), where the degree of stenosis was estimated on centreline reconstructions, also according to the NASCET method.18

DUS. All patients had non-fasting, pre-operative DUS of the SMA, CA, and IMA using a standard 2D curved array abdominal ultrasound transducer (EPIQ Elite US system, C5-1 curved array transducer; Philips Ultrasound, Bothell, WA, USA). PSV and end diastolic velocity were recorded in the long axis of the artery while keeping the Doppler angle <60°.15 All examinations were performed by two experienced ultrasonographers. A PSV of >295 cm/sec and >240 cm/sec in SMA and CA, respectively, was used as thresholds for >50% stenosis as suggested by AbuRahma et al.,6

DSA. DSA (Azurion, Release 2.2 L7; Koninklijke Philips N.V., Amsterdam, The Netherlands) was performed via femoral approach. The CA and SMA were visualised with aortic angiography using 15–20 mL of iodinated contrast medium (Visipaque, 270 mg iodine/mL, GE Healthcare AS, Chicago, USA) at a rate of 20 mL/sec. The mesenteric ostia were assessed in anteroposterior and lateral projections.14 The degree of SMA stenosis was estimated in the lateral projection using the NASCET method.18 Patients with a stenosis of >50% on angiography were included.17

IAPM. IAPM was performed using the Omniwire (Koninklijke Philips N.V.) across the SMA stenosis. The trans-stenotic pressure gradient was only performed in the target vessel, the SMA. MAP was recorded simultaneously in the aorta and SMA, establishing the gradient. IAPM was measured 1–2 cm distal to the stenosis.20 The pressure wire was calibrated in the aorta before crossing the lesion, after which a 15 second simultaneous measurement was taken to determine the trans-stenotic pressure gradient. IAPM was performed in all patients without SMA occlusion, with a threshold of >15 mmHg deemed to indicate a significant stenosis.17,21,22 The threshold was based on the previous literature as no consensus or European Society for Vascular Surgery recommendation was available during COMESS protocol development.

Statistical analysis

Spearman's rank correlation coefficient assessed the inter- and intra-rater variability for CTA, DSA, and DUS measurements. All CTA and DSA measurements were made by the primary author (A.A.B.). CTA measurement variability was established by performing the same measurements twice in a blinded fashion by the same (intra-) or two different (A.A.B. and T.A.R.) (inter-) observers. Interobserver variability of DUS measurements was established by performing the same measurement twice by two different vascular ultrasonographers.

Linear regression was used to evaluate the association between IAPM and CTA, DUS, and DSA and to examine the potential impact of CTA slice thickness on CTA measured stenosis. The effect of any degree of CA and IMA stenosis on SMA PSV was assessed using descriptive statistics and linear regression. Diagnostic thresholds between CTA and IAPM, DUS and IAPM, as well as DSA and IAPM were assessed with receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, with area under the curve (AUC) as a result. The analysis compared the different modalities with IAPMs of 10, 15, and 20 mmHg, based on the literature.1,5,23,24

AUC values are interpreted as follows: 0.9–1.0 excellent, 0.8–0.9 good, 0.7–0.8 fair, 0.6–0.7 poor, 0.5–0.6 failure. The DeLong method was used to compare the data obtained from the ROC curve. All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (v.4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A p value of <0.050 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Thirty consecutive patients with a clinical diagnosis of CMI and >50% SMA stenosis by CTA were included; among them, 60% were ”women”. The mean degree of stenosis was 74.3% on CTA and 73.6% on DSA (Table 1). DUS demonstrated a mean PSV of 344 cm/sec, and the mean trans-stenotic pressure gradient was 23.3 mmHg. Inter- and intra-rater variability of the diagnostic modalities

There was no significant interobserver variability in stenosis estimation on CTA between rater 1 and rater 2, with mean values of 64.9% and 68.2% (p = 0.73). Intra-observer analysis showed strong correlation for CTA (p = 0.058, ρ = 0.71), although it did not reach statistical significance. For DUS, the interobserver variability between two experienced ultrasonographers demonstrated a strong correlation, with no significant difference between the measurements (p < 0.001, ρ = 0.97). Lastly, a high level of correlation was found in the intra-observer analysis (p = 0.046, ρ = 0.74) for DSA.

A stenosis detected on CTA (r = 0.143, p = 0.55, standard deviation [SD] 0.1097) was not statistically significantly associated with IAPM (Fig. 1). Effect of mesenteric artery stenosis on SMA DUS velocity

At baseline, 50% of patients had CA stenosis, whereas IMA stenosis was present in 9.4% (Table 1). The mean SMA PSV was not significantly affected by CA stenosis (p = 0.32). Furthermore, no significant correlation was found between SMA PSV and either CA stenosis (r = 0.04, 95% confidence interval [CI] −103.36 – 133.71, p = 0.79) or IMA stenosis (r = −0.05, 95% CI −248.89 – 187.69, p = 0.77). Interpretation of the IMA comparison is limited, by the small number of patients with IMA stenosis.

DISCUSSION

The present study aimed to compare CTA, DUS, DSA, and IAPM in the evaluation of SMA stenosis in patients with CMI. Despite the small sample, the findings suggest DUS appeared most reliable for detecting a significant SMA stenosis, even in a non-fasting state. CTA and DSA may be less reliable but still provide relevant clinical information. The low prevalence of CA and IMA stenosis probably reflects selection of patients with clinically significant SMA stenosis and symptoms.

The suggested diagnostic criteria for SMA stenosis vary due to heterogeneous and inconsistent literature findings.1,3,24 DSA and trans-stenotic pressure determined by IAPM may be considered the most accurate.11,25 However, the IAPM threshold for significant stenosis remains poorly defined, and values between 10 and 20 mmHg are often quoted.7, 8, 9 Establishing a more precise IAPM cutoff is relevant in order to avoid unwarranted procedures. Furthermore, given that DSA and IAPM are invasive procedures, it is relevant to better define the relationship between these and other non-invasive pre-operative methods.

Previous DUS research has shown variation as to which SMA PSV threshold best determines significant stenosis. Moneta et al.15 demonstrated that a PSV of 275 cm/sec was a reliable indicator of SMA stenosis >70% in a non-stented vessel. Mitchel et al.16 concluded that a PSV of 275 cm/sec is not relevant in the stented SMA, and a PSV of >400 cm/sec should be considered significant. In contrast, AbuRahma et al.6 found that a PSV of 400 cm/sec corresponded to 70% stenosis. The current findings support higher PSVs as suggested by AbuRahma et al.6 Although patients are referred with CTA, DUS findings can alter EVT referral, ultimately resulting in fewer patients undergoing angiography. The findings of this study demonstrate that a trans-stenotic MAP gradient of 15–20 mmHg corresponds to a PSV of 372 cm/sec on DUS. However, DUS was less accurate in detecting IAPM gradients <10 mmHg, making it clinically unreliable for low grade stenosis.

Current guidelines recommend fasting patients for mesenteric artery DUS. Moneta et al.25 demonstrated a postprandial increase in SMA PSV in healthy individuals without SMA stenosis, whereas another study found that in patients with significant SMA stenosis on fasting DUS, the postprandial SMA PSV increase is blunted, suggesting that very limited additional diagnostic information is gained from a postprandial study, thereby inferring that there is little difference in the ability to identify high grade SMA stenosis between a fasting and a postprandial study; although perhaps more studies are noted with false positive results for high grade stenosis using postprandial DUS studies in patients with moderate SMA stenosis.26 As all patients in the present study had >50% SMA stenosis, using non-fasting DUS studies probably had little effect on the measured SMA PSV.26 At the study centre, patients with suspected SMA stenosis are examined in non-fasting state for practical and clinical considerations, differing from guidelines.3 Symptomatic CMI patients rarely eat large meals, limiting postprandial hyperaemia, and studies show both fasting and non-fasting SMA velocities reliably detect high grade stenosis.26 DUS results are interpreted alongside the clinical picture and other diagnostic methods when deciding on EVT, in accordance with current guidelines.

Previous studies indicate fair performance in grading stenosis on CTA with 10 mmHg MAP gradient.10 This study showed poor performance evaluating the diagnostic utility of CTA, particularly at higher trans-stenotic gradients. Although not directly assessed in this study, previous literature suggests that variability in CTA quality and arterial calcifications may contribute to blooming artefacts, potentially overestimating stenosis severity.14

Additionally, CTA remains an important screening tool for CMI diagnosis, as it is regularly performed in patients with abdominal pain. Many CTAs lack a dedicated SMA protocol, which might impact diagnostic accuracy. Thus, CTA should generally be combined with DUS to establish the significance of SMA stenosis. Clinical symptoms and findings should then guide the best clinical pathway. A poor quality CTA and DUS indicating a velocity of <370 cm/sec, combined with non-specific symptoms, may justify repeating the CTA or exploring alternative diagnoses. However, if the clinical presentation is more suggestive of CMI, direct invasive diagnostics such as DSA and IAPM could be considered to confirm the diagnosis and, if appropriate, initiate immediate intervention.

An IAPM of 10 mmHg corresponded to 79% stenosis on CTA and 59% stenosis on DSA, highlighting variability between the modalities, with CTA indicating a more significant stenosis. Interestingly, DSA was better than DUS at predicting a stenosis at a pressure gradient of 10 mmHg. However, DSA demonstrated greater uncertainty at higher pressure gradients, probably due to the inability to measure IAPM in SMA occlusion, potentially introducing bias into the findings. Clinically, this suggests that cases with borderline DUS probably benefit from conventional DSA, especially when clinical findings are consistent with CMI.

A combined imaging approach is preferable to determine the indication for treatment of CMI in the context of SMA stenosis. Low grade stenosis on DSA should not preclude stenting if pre-operative CTA and DUS indicate a significant lesion and IAPM is unavailable. If pre-operative DUS is not possible, IAPM should be performed for verification of the stenosis when clinical symptoms are typical.

Limitations

The sample size was small, which may explain the wide CIs observed. Some patients were referred with suboptimal CTA scans, probably affecting diagnostic accuracy; further analysis showed no significant differences in stenosis measurements across different CTA slice thicknesses. DUS and IAPM were not possible in 30% of patients due to near or total SMA occlusion, which represents a statistical limitation and may affect the generalisability of the findings. Nonetheless, this reflects routine clinical practice with a heterogeneous patient population. In this study, only the presence of CA and IMA stenoses were recorded, and stenosis severity was not assessed. Given the small sample size, and low prevalence of IMA stenosis, IMA analysis should be interpreted with caution.

DUS was performed on non-fasting patients in this study, which might impact and overestimate the measured duplex velocities compared with those measured fasting. Angiography and IAPM were also performed in a non-fasting state.

Conclusion

In this study, DUS showed the best association with IAPM when differentiating a significant SMA stenosis. In contrast, CTA shows limited diagnostic accuracy when assessing IAPM; however, CTA is of importance for differential diagnoses. Therefore, when an SMA stenosis is identified on CTA, further confirmation with DUS should be considered to determine the need for DSA and potential EVT. Larger prospective trials should be performed to validate these observations.
