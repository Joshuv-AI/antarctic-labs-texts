Measurement invariance and validation of the LPFS-BF 2.0 in clinical and community samples of Turkish adolescents
Authors: Fatma Coşkun, Ömer Faruk Akça, Ayhan Bilgiç, Carla Sharp, Joost Hutsebaut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven the limited number of validation studies on the LPFS-BF 2.0 in adolescents and scarce test–retest data, the current study aimed to examine the psychometric properties of the Turkish version of the LPFS-BF 2.0 in adolescents.MethodsThe scale was translated in accordance with International Test Commission guidelines and administered to 170 clinical and 184 community adolescents aged 12–18 years (N = 354). Exploratory factor analyses (EFA) were conducted separately in the two groups using Principal Axis Factoring (PAF). Factor retention was evaluated using eigenvalues greater than 1, scree plot inspection, and Horn’s parallel analysis, followed by confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to test the theoretically expected two-factor structure. Multi-group confirmatory factor analysis was used to examine measurement invariance across clinical and community samples. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, convergent validity was evaluated through correlations with the Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI), Borderline Personality Features Scale for Children–Short Form (BPFSC-SF), Personality Belief Questionnaire–Short Form (PBQ-SF), and number of psychiatric diagnoses, and test–retest reliability was examined in a clinical subsample.ResultsEFA revealed a two-factor structure in the clinical sample and a three-factor structure in the community sample. Horn’s parallel analysis supported a two-factor structure in both samples, and CFA confirmed the two-factor model reflecting self- and interpersonal functioning, with acceptable fit indices in both groups. Measurement invariance analyses indicated configural, metric, and partial scalar invariance across the two samples, supporting the comparability of the scale across diagnostically distinct adolescent groups. Internal consistency was acceptable to good in the clinical group (α = 0.72–0.83) and somewhat lower in the community group (α = 0.61–0.79). LPFS-BF 2.0 scores showed significant positive associations with theoretically related constructs, including borderline personality features, maladaptive personality beliefs, and psychological distress, providing support for convergent validity. Test–retest reliability in a subsample of clinical participants yielded moderate correlations over a six-month interval (r = 0.46–0.51).ConclusionsOverall, the findings support the Turkish LPFS-BF 2.0 as a brief, reliable, and valid measure of self- and interpersonal functioning in adolescents, consistent with the DSM-5 Section III conceptualization of personality pathology. The results further suggest that the scale functions similarly across clinical and community samples and may be useful in both research and clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-05188-9.Keywords: The Level of Personality Functioning Scale- Brief Form 2.0, Validity, Adolescent, Personality functioning, Alternative model for personality disorders

Background

In recent years, conceptualizations of personality pathology have shifted toward dimensional models that emphasize impairments in core personality functioning rather than categorical symptom clusters [1–4]. Both the DSM-5-TR Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD) and the ICD-11 classification conceptualize personality disorders primarily in terms of impairments in self- and interpersonal functioning [5–7]. Within the AMPD, these impairments are operationalized as Criterion A and constitute the central indicator of personality pathology severity [5, 6]. This transition reflects a broader shift in contemporary psychiatric nosology. Deficits in identity, self-direction, empathy, and intimacy are now viewed as core indicators of personality dysfunction [6, 8, 9]. Within the DSM-5-TR AMPD, these impairments are distinguished from, but complementary to, the pathological personality trait domains described under Criterion B [6, 8, 9]. This conceptualization is consistent with contemporary theoretical formulations summarized by Zimmermann, Hopwood, and Krueger, who highlight personality functioning as the central organizing construct of DSM-5 Criterion A [10].

Personality pathology in childhood and adolescence has long been debated due to concerns about stigmatization and the developmental fluidity of identity formation [11]. However, empirical evidence increasingly indicates that personality dysfunction emerges early, demonstrates moderate stability, and is clinically meaningful when assessed dimensionally [8, 12, 13]. Dimensional approaches, as adopted in DSM-5-TR and ICD-11, allow clinicians to differentiate between transient developmental difficulties and more pervasive impairments in personality functioning, making them particularly suitable for adolescent assessment [6–8, 14].

To operationalize Criterion A, several clinician-rated and self-report instruments have been developed to assess impairments in self- and interpersonal functioning [15–20]. Among these tools, the DSM-5 Level of Personality Functioning Scale (LPFS) [17] provides a structured evaluation of disturbances in identity, self-direction, empathy, and intimacy—domains central to the AMPD model. Although the LPFS offers a comprehensive clinician-rated assessment, its length and administration demands have highlighted the need for briefer self-report alternatives. In response, Hutsebaut et al. developed the Level of Personality Functioning Scale–Brief Form (LPFS-BF), a 12-item measure designed to efficiently capture core impairments in personality functioning [21]. A revised version, the LPFS-BF 2.0, was later introduced to address limitations of the original yes/no format and to incorporate a Likert response scale, improving psychometric sensitivity and yielding a consistent two-factor structure reflecting self- and interpersonal functioning [22]. Accumulating research indicates that the LPFS-BF 2.0 demonstrates adequate validity and reliability in adult populations [23–25]. However, research in adolescents remains limited.

To date, only two studies have examined the LPFS-BF 2.0 in adolescents, including community samples and adolescents exposed to parental abuse and neglect [26, 27]. However, to our knowledge, no previous study has evaluated the LPFS-BF 2.0 in a psychiatric outpatient sample of clinically diagnosed adolescents or examined measurement invariance across clinical and community groups. Furthermore, the psychometric properties of the Turkish version of the LPFS-BF 2.0 have not previously been investigated in an adolescent sample. Moreover, no culturally adapted, brief self-report measure of personality functioning is available for Turkish adolescents. Although the Turkish version of the LoPF-Q 12–18, a comprehensive self-report measure of personality functioning, has previously been validated in Turkish adolescents [28], its relatively large number of items may limit its feasibility in some clinical and research settings. Therefore, a brief measure such as the LPFS-BF 2.0 may provide a practical alternative for the assessment of personality functioning. Despite evidence that cultural norms shape the development and expression of identity, autonomy, and interpersonal relatedness during adolescence, further research on brief personality functioning measures remains needed.

The present study aimed to evaluate the factor structure, reliability, convergent validity, and measurement invariance of the Turkish version of the LPFS-BF 2.0 in clinical and community adolescent samples. By examining its structural consistency across diagnostically distinct groups and situating the scale within a culturally specific developmental context, this study contributes to the limited literature on Criterion A assessment in adolescence and offers a brief, developmentally appropriate tool for research and clinical use in Turkey. Based on previous findings regarding the LPFS-BF 2.0, we hypothesized that: (1) the Turkish version of the LPFS-BF 2.0 would demonstrate a two-factor structure reflecting self- and interpersonal functioning; (2) the scale would show acceptable internal consistency and temporal stability; (3) LPFS-BF 2.0 scores would be positively associated with related constructs, including borderline personality features, maladaptive personality beliefs, general psychopathological distress, and number of psychiatric diagnoses, thereby supporting convergent validity; and (4) the proposed two-factor model would demonstrate measurement invariance across clinical and community adolescent samples.

Methods

Participants

Two adolescent samples were included in the study. The clinical sample consisted of 170 adolescents aged 12–18 years who were admitted to the Child and Adolescent Psychiatry Outpatient Clinic of Necmettin Erbakan University Faculty of Medicine, Konya, Türkiye for various psychiatric complaints that did not meet the exclusion criteria. Exclusion criteria for the clinical group were intellectual disability, schizophrenia spectrum disorders, bipolar disorder, autism spectrum disorder, and severe neurological or physical illnesses. The community sample comprised 184 adolescents aged 12–18 years recruited from one public middle school and one public high school in the same district of Konya, Türkiye. Schools were selected based on institutional approval and logistical accessibility rather than probabilistic sampling. Information about the study was provided in group sessions conducted by school counsellors in collaboration with the research team. Participation was voluntary, and no randomization or stratified sampling procedures were applied. Adolescents were excluded from the community group if they had any current mental health problem, a significant neurological or physical condition, or a history of psychopharmacological treatment in the previous six months. For the community sample, information regarding current mental health problems was obtained through school counseling services and adolescents’ self-reports during the recruitment process. Written informed consent was obtained directly from participants aged 18 years. For participants under 18 years of age, written informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians. In addition, written assent was obtained from the adolescents themselves prior to participation. Participant recruitment and baseline data collection were conducted between July and November 2024.

Measures

Sociodemographic data form

A researcher-developed form was used to collect information on demographic and background characteristics.

Level of Personality Functioning Scale–Brief Form 2.0 (LPFS-BF 2.0)

The LPFS-BF 2.0 is a 12-item self-report measure designed to assess impairments in self- and interpersonal functioning [21]. It is a revised version of the original LPFS-BF and incorporates a four-point Likert response format (0 = “very wrong or mostly wrong” to 3 = “very right or mostly right”). Items 1–6 assess self-functioning, and items 7–12 assess interpersonal functioning [22].

Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia–Present And Lifetime Version, Turkish Version (K-SADS-PL-TR)

The K-SADS-PL is a semi-structured diagnostic interview used to assess current and lifetime psychopathology in children and adolescents [29]. Turkish validity and reliability studies were conducted by Gökler et al. [30], and the DSM-5–adapted version was validated by Ünal et al. (2019) [31].

Borderline Personality Features Scale for Children–Short Form (BPFSC-SF)

The original 24-item BPFSC assesses borderline personality features in youth across four domains: affective instability, self-harm, interpersonal problems, and identity disturbance [32]. Sharp et al. developed an 11-item short form with a single-factor structure and a 5-point Likert scale [33]. The Turkish adaptation demonstrated good psychometric properties [34].

Personality Belief Questionnaire–Short Form (PBQ-SF)

The PBQ-SF is a 65-item measure assessing dysfunctional beliefs associated with nine DSM-IV/DSM-5 personality disorder patterns [35]. Each item reflects core beliefs related to avoidant, dependent, passive-aggressive, obsessive-compulsive, antisocial, narcissistic, histrionic, schizoid, and paranoid personality traits. The Turkish version has established reliability and validity [36].

Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI)

The BSI is a 53-item, five-point Likert-type instrument assessing general psychopathology across five subscales: hostility, anxiety, depression, negative self-concept, and somatization [37]. The Turkish adaptation demonstrated good psychometric properties [38].

Procedure

The study was approved by the local university ethics committee (Decision No: 2024/5047; Date: 28/06/2024). All procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional and national ethical guidelines.

Translation process

The translation of the LPFS-BF 2.0 followed International Test Commission (ITC) guidelines. Two bilingual researchers independently translated the scale into Turkish, and a synthesized version was produced after consensus discussions. This version was then back-translated into English by a native English-speaking psychologist who was blinded to the original instrument. A committee of three researchers reviewed and reconciled discrepancies to ensure semantic, conceptual, and functional equivalence.

Assessment procedure

Written informed consent was obtained from parents and assent from adolescents. All participants completed the sociodemographic data form, LPFS-BF 2.0, BPFSC-SF, PBQ-SF, and BSI. A trained child and adolescent psychiatrist administered the K-SADS-PL-TR to determine DSM-5 psychiatric diagnoses in the clinical sample. Data collection occurred between July and November 2024. For the test–retest reliability analysis of the LPFS-BF 2.0, all adolescents from the original clinical sample were eligible for follow-up contact approximately six months after the initial assessment. 45 participants who could be reached and agreed to participate completed the follow-up evaluation. Follow-up was conducted only in the clinical group due to limited access to the school-based community sample during the academic year. Although this restriction may introduce potential bias (e.g., regression to the mean), the retest nonetheless provides meaningful information regarding the short-term temporal stability of the instrument in a treatment-seeking population.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 20.0 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) and AMOS (Analysis of Moment Structures) were used for the statistical analyses. Parallel analysis was conducted in R using the psych package. Reliability was evaluated using internal consistency and test–retest methods. A subsample of 45 clinical participants completed the LPFS-BF 2.0 again after six months, and test–retest reliability was assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients and item–total correlations. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ≥ 0.70 were considered indicative of acceptable internal consistency [39, 40]. Test–retest reliability was assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients. Following Evans [41], correlation coefficients between 0.40 and 0.59 were interpreted as moderate and coefficients ≥ 0.60 as strong. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted using principal axis factoring (PAF) as the extraction method, followed by an orthogonal Equamax rotation with Kaiser normalization, separately in the clinical and community samples. Sampling adequacy was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) statistic and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Because the eigenvalue > 1 criterion is prone to over-extraction, factor retention was primarily determined using Horn’s parallel analysis based on common-factor eigenvalues (1,000 random datasets, 95th percentile criterion) [42], supplemented by the Kaiser criterion and scree plot inspection. Although a two-factor structure was anticipated based on prior research, EFA was used to explore potential cultural or sample-specific variations in the Turkish adolescent population. Subsequently, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to evaluate the fit of the hypothesized two-factor structure in both the clinical and community samples. Model fit was evaluated using multiple fit indices, including the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Following recommendations by Hu and Bentler [43] and Kline [44], CFI and TLI values ≥ 0.90 were considered indicative of acceptable fit and values ≥ 0.95 indicative of good fit. RMSEA values ≤ 0.08 and ≤ 0.06 were considered indicative of acceptable and good fit, respectively, whereas SRMR values ≤ 0.08 were considered indicative of acceptable fit. Measurement invariance across groups was examined using multigroup CFA, testing configural, metric, full scalar, and partial scalar models sequentially. Invariance was evaluated using changes in approximate fit indices as well as the chi-square difference test. Measurement invariance was considered supported when changes between nested models were ΔCFI ≤ 0.010 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015. In addition, ΔSRMR values ≤ 0.030 for metric invariance and ≤ 0.010 for scalar invariance were considered indicative of invariance [45, 46]. Where full scalar invariance was not supported, partial scalar invariance was examined by releasing non-invariant intercepts [47, 48]. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Convergent validity was evaluated by examining associations between LPFS-BF 2.0 scores and the BSI, BPFSC-SF, and PBQ-SF. These measures were selected because they assess constructs theoretically related to impairments in personality functioning, including general psychopathological distress, borderline personality features, and maladaptive personality-related beliefs. Although they do not directly assess personality functioning, significant associations with these constructs were expected based on previous research linking personality dysfunction to broader manifestations of psychopathology and personality pathology. The PBQ-SF subscales were examined separately because they represent theoretically distinct maladaptive belief domains associated with personality pathology. Evaluating these domains individually allowed for a more nuanced examination of the associations between personality functioning and specific maladaptive cognitive patterns. To control for inflated Type I error associated with multiple correlations, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure was applied [49]. A post hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.7 [50]. Based on the total sample size of 354 adolescents (170 clinical and 184 community participants), an alpha level of 0.05, and a medium effect size assumption (d = 0.50) for between-group comparisons, the achieved statistical power exceeded 0.99. This analysis provides additional support for the adequacy of the sample size to detect medium-sized group differences.

Results

354 adolescents participated in the study, of whom 170 were clinical and 184 were community members. The distributions of age (t = 1.75, p = 0.08) and sex (χ² = 2.66; p = 0.26) were similar in the groups. In the clinical group, the mean LPFS-BF 2.0-self score was 15.17 ± 4.59, the LPFS-BF 2.0-interpersonal score was 13.18 ± 4.10 and the LPFS-BF 2.0-total score was 28.35 ± 7.78. In the community group, the mean LPFS-BF 2.0-self score was 12.84 ± 4.30, the LPFS-BF 2.0-interpersonal score was 11.38 ± 3.25, and the LPFS-BF 2.0-total score was 24.23 ± 6.50. In the clinical group, the mean scores of LPFS-BF 2.0-self (t = 4.91, p < 0.001), LPFS-BF 2.0 -interpersonal (t = 4.55, p < 0.001) and LPFS-BF 2.0-total (t = 5.40, p < 0.001) were significantly higher compared to the community group. The magnitude of these differences was in the medium range (Cohen’s d = 0.49–0.58), indicating meaningful differences between clinical and community adolescents. Factor analytic results

Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses were used to establish the construct validity of the LPFS-BF 2.0. Initially, the data for the community group (KMO = 0.80, χ²= 485.218, p < 0.001) and the clinical group (KMO = 0.85, χ²= 522.345, p < 0.001) were found to be suitable for exploratory factor analysis. Subsequent data analysis on both groups of participants indicated that the clinical group had a two-factor structure, whereas the community group had a three-factor structure. Horn’s parallel analysis based on common-factor eigenvalues was conducted separately in both samples [42]. In both groups, only the first two empirical eigenvalues exceeded the corresponding eigenvalues generated from 1,000 random datasets. In the clinical sample, the first two empirical eigenvalues (3.69 and 0.53) exceeded the 95th percentile random eigenvalues, whereas the third eigenvalue (0.28) did not. Similarly, in the community sample, the first two empirical eigenvalues (3.12 and 0.69) exceeded the corresponding random eigenvalues, whereas the third eigenvalue (0.28) did not. The parallel analysis results are presented in Fig. 1. Although the Kaiser criterion suggested a three-factor solution in the community sample, the parallel analysis supported retention of only two factors. Therefore, the theoretically expected two-factor structure was retained for subsequent confirmatory factor analyses in both groups. Tables 2 and 3 present the factor loadings of the LPFS-BF 2.0 items obtained from exploratory factor analyses conducted using Principal Axis Factoring (PAF) with orthogonal Equamax rotation in the clinical and community samples, respectively. Multi-group confirmatory factor analysis was used to assess the factor loadings and whether there were any differences between the loadings in the clinical and community groups. In the clinical group, a two-factor structure was found with acceptable fit values (TLI = 0.920, CFI = 0.940, RMSEA = 0.060). Also, in the community group, a two-factor structure was found with overall acceptable fit, although the TLI value (0.870) was slightly below the recommended cutoff (CFI = 0.900, RMSEA = 0.070). Measurement invariance results

To assess whether the factor structure of the scale was equivalent across the clinical and community samples, a series of multigroup confirmatory factor analyses were conducted. First, a configural model (with no equality constraints) was tested and demonstrated acceptable model fit (CFI = 0.918, RMSEA = 0.045, SRMR = 0.051), indicating that the same factor structure was applicable across both groups. Subsequently, metric invariance was tested by constraining the factor loadings to be equal across groups. Changes in model fit relative to the configural model remained within recommended thresholds (ΔCFI = 0.006, ΔRMSEA = − 0.004, ΔSRMR = 0.005), supporting metric invariance [45, 46]. Next, scalar invariance was tested by additionally constraining the item intercepts to be equal across groups. The full scalar model resulted in a deterioration in model fit (ΔCFI = − 0.028), indicating that full scalar invariance was not supported. Therefore, a partial scalar invariance model was tested by freely estimating the intercepts of Items 1 and 2 across groups. The partial scalar model demonstrated acceptable fit (CFI = 0.918, RMSEA = 0.041, SRMR = 0.056), and changes in fit indices relative to the metric model remained within recommended criteria (ΔCFI = − 0.006, ΔRMSEA = 0.000, ΔSRMR = 0.000). The chi-square difference test was also non-significant (Δχ²(8) = 13.11, p = 0.108), supporting partial scalar invariance [47, 48]. Overall, these findings indicate that the LPFS-BF 2.0 demonstrates configural, metric, and partial scalar invariance across the clinical and community samples. Reliability analyses

Cronbach’s α coefficient and inter-item and item-total correlations were used to assess the internal consistency of the LPFS-BF 2.0, and the test-retest method was used to assess the consistency of the scale over time. Table 5 shows the mean inter-item, mean item-total, test-retest correlations and Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of the LPFS-BF 2.0 subscales. In the clinical group, the Cronbach’s alpha values were 0.78 for LPFS-BF 2.0-self, 0.72 for LPFS-BF 2.0-interpersonal and 0.83 for LPFS-BF 2.0-total. In the community group, Cronbach’s alpha values were found to be 0.79 for LPFS-BF 2.0-self, 0.61 for LPFS-BF 2.0-interpersonal and 0.79 for LPFS-BF 2.0-total. All items in both groups had mean item scale correlations greater than 0.30. Test–retest correlations were r = 0.46 (95% CI = 0.17–0.67) for LPFS-BF 2.0- self, r = 0 0.48 (95% CI = 0.19–0.69) for LPFS-BF 2.0- interpersonal, and r = 0.51 (95% CI = 0.24–0.71) for LPFS-BF 2.0- total. Convergent validity analyses

The relationships between the results of the LPFS-BF 2.0 and the BPFSC-SF, BSI, PBQ-SF were assessed by criterion-based validity analysis of the respective scores of the clinical and control groups. In addition, the K-SADS-PL-TR was used to determine the correlation between the LPFS-BF 2.0 and the total number of diagnoses in the clinical group. The LPFS-BF 2.0-self, LPFS-BF 2.0-interpersonal and LPFS-BF 2.0-total scores were found to be positively correlated with the total number of diagnoses in the clinical group. In both the clinical and control groups, the LPFS-BF 2.0-self, LPFS-BF 2.0-interpersonal and LPFS-BF 2.0-total scores were found to be positively correlated with the BSI global severity index score. In both groups, it was found that the LPFS-BF 2.0-self, LPFS-BF 2.0-interpersonal and LPFS-BF 2.0-total scores were positively correlated with the BPFSC-SF total score. Also, it was found that the LPFS-BF 2.0-self, LPFS-BF 2.0-interpersonal and LPFS-BF 2.0-total scores were positively correlated with the PBQ-SF subscales in both the clinical and control groups. (Except for no correlation between the LPFS-BF 2.0-self score and the obsessive-compulsive subscale of the PBQ-SF, in the control group). Following Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction, all statistically significant correlations remained significant, and the overall pattern of results was unchanged. The observed correlations ranged from small to strong in magnitude. Several associations were moderate to strong, whereas others were in the small-to-moderate range. Given that the validation measures assessed related but distinct constructs, the findings suggest that the LPFS-BF 2.0 is associated with relevant indicators of psychopathology and personality pathology. Discussion

This study aimed to examine the psychometric properties of the LPFS-BF 2.0, a brief questionnaire assessing Criterion A as defined in the AMPD, in a Turkish adolescent population. To our knowledge, only two studies have previously examined the validity and reliability of the LPFS-BF 2.0 scale in an adolescent sample [26, 27], and no studies have included clinical samples. Our study included participants from both a clinical and a community sample. In this context, the inclusion of both clinical and community adolescents strengthens the contribution of the present study. In addition, by including clinically diagnosed adolescents—who were not represented in prior studies—this research provides important data regarding Criterion A functioning in treatment-seeking adolescents. The results confirmed the two-factor structure of the scale after both exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis in the clinical group, in line with the interpretation of self and interpersonal functioning originally proposed by the authors and described in the DSM-5 Part III Alternative Model of Personality Disorders. In the community group, although the EFA identified a three-factor structure, parallel analysis indicated that the third eigenvalue did not exceed the random-data threshold [42], and the theoretically grounded two-factor model was tested and confirmed by confirmatory factor analysis. This approach — retaining a theoretically motivated CFA model despite a divergent EFA solution — is consistent with established practice when the additional factor lacks conceptual coherence and statistical support from parallel analysis. The LPFS-BF 2.0 showed strong reliability estimates and showed significant correlations with the BPFSC-SF, BSI and PBQ-SF in both groups and with the number of current diagnoses in the clinical group. The results support the reliability and validity of the Turkish adaptation of the LPFS-BF 2.0 as an operationalization of the personality pathology construct outlined in AMPD Criterion A. Taken together, these findings also suggest that the scale operates similarly across diagnostically distinct samples, which is further supported by the configural, metric, and partial scalar invariance results.

The clinical group demonstrated significantly higher LPFS-BF 2.0 scores than the community group, consistent with theoretical expectations that impairments in personality functioning are more pronounced among adolescents experiencing psychiatric difficulties. Although the absolute score differences were modest, the observed effect sizes were consistently in the medium range (Cohen’s d = 0.49–0.58), suggesting meaningful differences between clinically referred and community adolescents. These findings are broadly consistent with previous research on adolescent personality functioning. For example, Cosgun et al. reported substantially higher levels of personality functioning impairment among adolescents with personality disorders compared with a school-based sample, with a large effect size (d = 1.2) [28]. The somewhat smaller effect sizes observed in the present study may reflect differences in sample composition, as our clinical group consisted of a heterogeneous psychiatric outpatient sample rather than adolescents diagnosed specifically with personality disorders. In contrast, Balzen et al. found no significant differences in LPFS-BF 2.0 scores between adolescents with a history of parental abuse and neglect and a community sample [27]. Taken together, these findings suggest that personality functioning impairment may be more strongly associated with clinically diagnosed psychopathology than with psychosocial risk exposure alone.

Factor structure

In their study of 240 adults from a clinical sample group, in which Hutsebaut and Feenstra developed the LPFS-BF scale, a two-factor structure was identified [21]. Although the DSM-5 characterizes the LPFS as a unidimensional construct, previous research has yielded inconsistent factor topologies. However, many studies have reported that the two factors—self and interpersonal functioning—are strongly correlated, suggesting the existence of a higher-order general factor of personality functioning. Morey identified a singular factor solution, thus claiming that Criterion A is a unidimensional construct [19]. Although Morey reported that a single component explained the majority of variance in the LPFS-SR, it is important to note that this was based on a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) conducted on the four domain-level scores rather than an item-level factor analytic approach. Thus, Morey’s findings do not directly inform the latent structure of the LPFS at the item level [19]. Zimmermann et al. determined that the LPFS is most accurately understood as a two-dimensional construct [51]. Two distinguishable but highly correlated dimensions of personality functioning were identified: self-functioning and interpersonal functioning. In the LPFS-BF 2.0 update by Weekers et al., a two-factor structure was again identified, in line with earlier studies reporting distinct but correlated dimensions [22]. In our research, the two-factor structure of the scale was confirmed by both exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses in the clinical group. In the community group, the EFA suggested a three-factor structure; however, parallel analysis indicated that the third factor did not meet the retention criterion, and the theoretically motivated two-factor CFA model showed acceptable fit.This study revealed a two-factor structure for the self- functioning and interpersonal functioning dimensions of the LPFS-BF 2.0 in a clinical population—two strongly correlated dimensions that may reflect a higher-order general factor of personality functioning. The two-factor structure was supported in research with adults, consistent with the theoretical framework of the DSM-5 AMPD [23, 24, 52]. This scenario suggests that dimensions of personality functioning can be more clearly delineated, particularly in clinical populations. In the community sample, the EFA yielded a three-factor structure based on the Kaiser criterion; however, the items loading onto the third factor did not form a conceptually coherent dimension, and parallel analysis confirmed that its eigenvalue did not exceed the values expected from random data [42]. Accordingly, the theoretically grounded two-factor model was carried forward to CFA. This finding contrasts with the single-factor structure identified by Wu and colleagues in the adolescent community sample [26]. Differences in results may be due to the type of sample (clinical versus community), age range, cultural setting, or the form of the scale used (translated versions, linguistic adaptations). Nonetheless, the interpretability of the third factor extracted in the community sample is limited. The items loading onto this factor (see Table 3) did not form a conceptually coherent dimension, and no clear thematic structure could be identified. This ambiguity may reflect heterogeneity within the community sample, developmental variability, or lower symptom salience, which can lead to less stable factor structures in non-clinical populations. Similar concerns about the inconsistency of factor solutions across Criterion A measures have been raised in prior research [53, 54], further highlighting the importance of examining structural replicability across diverse samples. In addition, items 7, 8, and 11 showed notable cross-loadings across factors in the community sample. This pattern may indicate that certain interpersonal functioning items also capture aspects of self-functioning during adolescence, a developmental period in which identity formation and interpersonal maturation occur concurrently. Accordingly, the observed cross-loadings may reflect developmental overlap between self- and interpersonal functioning rather than clearly separable latent dimensions. Cultural influences on interpersonal relationships and item interpretation may also have contributed to this loading pattern. Despite the structural differences observed in the exploratory analyses, the demonstration of configural, metric, and partial scalar invariance suggests that the LPFS-BF 2.0 functions similarly across clinical and community adolescents. Although full scalar invariance was not achieved, partial scalar invariance was established after freeing the intercepts of Items 1 and 2, indicating that most model parameters were comparable across groups [47, 48]. These findings provide additional support for the cross-group applicability of the LPFS-BF 2.0 in Turkish adolescents. This finding supports the robustness of the scale and enhances the generalizability of the results. Rather than serving solely as a linguistic adaptation, the present study contributes to the broader psychometric evaluation of the LPFS-BF 2.0 by demonstrating its structural stability across distinct adolescent populations.

Adolescence is a developmental period in which identity, autonomy, and interpersonal relatedness are still consolidating, which may reduce the stability of Criterion A indicators [11, 13]. This developmental variability, combined with lower symptom salience in non-clinical adolescents, may help explain why the community sample showed a less stable factor solution. In addition, culturally reinforced norms in Turkish adolescents—such as relational interdependence, strong family involvement, and expectations around emotional moderation—may influence the interpretation of interpersonal items, contributing to reduced homogeneity and the emergence of an additional, non-coherent factor [7, 14].

Reliability

The LPFS-BF 2.0 scores from both the clinical and community groups showed strong internal consistency as evidenced by Cronbach alphas, mean inter-item correlations and item-total correlations. A satisfactory index with high reliability is a Cronbach’s alpha value above 0.70 [55]. In the clinical group, Cronbach’s alpha values were found to be 0.78 for LPFS-BF 2.0-self, 0.72 for LPFS-BF 2.0-interpersonal and 0.83 for LPFS-BF 2.0-total. In the community group, Cronbach’s alpha values were found to be 0.79 for LPFS-BF 2.0-self, 0.61 for LPFS-BF 2.0-interpersonal and 0.79 for LPFS-BF 2.0-total. The Cronbach’s alpha values obtained in our study generally indicate that the LPFS-BF 2.0 has an acceptable internal consistency. The consistency values for the self-functioning, interpersonal functioning and total scales in the clinical sample are largely consistent with the study by Weekers et al. (self: α = 0.79, interpersonal: α = 0.71, total: α = 0.82) [22]. On the other hand, the low Cronbach’s α value obtained for the interpersonal functioning subscale in the community sample was relatively low (Cronbach’s α = 0.61), and this finding should be interpreted with caution. This difference may be explained by the non-clinical nature of the sample, the more heterogeneous responses to interpersonal items in the community sample, or the influence of cultural and developmental factors. In particular, the fact that the interpersonal functioning dimension may be less consolidated in community adolescents, or that some items may be more developmentally abstract, could contribute to the lower level of consistency. In addition, the three-factor structure obtained in the community sample suggests that the structural integrity of the scale may be more fragmented in this group, potentially reducing subscale homogeneity. Future studies should further examine the reliability and dimensionality of the interpersonal functioning subscale in larger and more diverse adolescent samples.

In their study with an adult clinical sample, Weekers and colleagues examined changes in LPFS-BF 2.0 scores before and after treatment among 39 participants, reporting moderate correlations between pre- and post-treatment scores (r = 0.49 for total, 0.47 for self-functioning, and 0.51 for interpersonal functioning) [22]. While these findings reflect sensitivity to change, they do not directly assess test–retest reliability. To date, only one study has formally examined the test–retest reliability of the LPFS-BF 2.0, in which Gritti et al. evaluated a community-based adult sample with an average retest interval of 11.5 weeks and reported high temporal stability (r = 0.83 for total score; r = 0.79 for self-functioning; r = 0.73 for interpersonal functioning) [25]. In the current study, six-month item-level test–retest correlations ranged from r = 0.30 (item 10) to r = 0.52 (item 1), suggesting moderate temporal stability at the item level in a clinical adolescent sample. It is also possible that ongoing treatment processes during the six-month interval reduced stability estimates, a factor commonly observed in adolescent clinical samples. These findings contribute to the growing literature on the LPFS-BF 2.0 and highlight potential age- and sample-related variability in its temporal stability.

Convergent validity

The study assessed the criterion convergent validity of the LPFS-BF 2.0 by examining its correlations with the BPFSC-SF, BSI and PBQ-SF in both groups. The K-SADS-PL-TR scores were used to determine the association between the LPFS-BF 2.0 and total diagnoses in the clinical group. Weekers et al. evaluated the correlation of the LPFS-BF 2.0 with the BSI and Severity Indices of Personality Problems Short Form (SIPP-SF) domains and found moderate correlation coefficients [22]. Wu et al. assessed the validity of the LPFS-BF 2.0 with the Personality Inventory for DSM-5 - Brief Form (PID-5-BF), BPFSC, Youth Self-Report (YSR) and Levels of Personality Functioning Questionnaire - Ages 12–18 (LoPF-Q 12–18) in an adolescent sample. They found that the LPFS-BF 2.0 showed significant correlations with each scale [26]. In our study, the LPFS-BF 2.0-self, LPFS-BF 2.0- interpersonal and LPFS-BF 2.0- total scores were found to be positively correlated with the total number of diagnoses in the clinical group. In both the clinical and control groups, the LPFS-BF 2.0-self, LPFS-BF 2.0-interpersonal and LPFS-BF 2.0-total scores were found to be positively correlated with the BSI global severity index score, the BPFSC-SF and almost all subscales of PBQ-SF (Except for no correlation between the LPFS-BF 2.0-self score and the obsessive-compulsive subscale in the control group). These convergent patterns are theoretically expected, as all comparison measures reflect affective instability, interpersonal difficulties, or maladaptive beliefs that are conceptually linked to Criterion A functioning. Overall, the present findings provide support for the reliability, validity, and measurement invariance of the LPFS-BF 2.0 in Turkish adolescents. However, these findings should be interpreted in light of several considerations, including the relatively lower internal consistency observed for the interpersonal functioning subscale in the community sample, the moderate test–retest coefficients, and the absence of a direct comparison measure of personality functioning.

Limitations

This study has limitations, particularly the inclusion of both clinical and community groups, which was limited by small sample sizes in each category. The psychometric instruments used in this study, specifically the BSI and PBQ-SF, are not validated for adolescents in our population. As a result, we have had to rely on psychometric instruments that have only recently been assessed in adults. A notable limitation of the current study is the extremely small sample size of the retest group. In addition, the six-month retest interval should be considered when interpreting the stability coefficients. While longer intervals may provide valuable information regarding the temporal stability of personality functioning, they also increase the likelihood that developmental changes, symptom fluctuations, and treatment-related factors may influence observed scores. This consideration may be particularly relevant for adolescents receiving psychiatric care. Therefore, the test–retest findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. This study used only self-report data, which may have led to exaggerated correlations between measures due to shared method variance. Another limitation is the lack of an interview-based measure of personality functioning, which limits the use of a multi-method approach and may restrict the depth of convergent validity assessment. Furthermore, convergent validity was examined using theoretically related constructs, including psychopathological distress, borderline personality features, and maladaptive personality-related beliefs, rather than a direct measure of personality functioning. Additionally, although this study followed ITC guidelines during translation, the absence of detailed reporting on the translation/back-translation steps should be addressed in future research. Furthermore, cultural or cross-national measurement invariance could not be tested; therefore, the generalizability of the findings beyond Turkish adolescents remains uncertain.

Conclusıons

While the present findings should be interpreted in light of the study limitations, they support the use of the LPFS-BF 2.0 as a brief measure of personality functioning in Turkish adolescents. Notwithstanding these limitations, our findings suggest that the present research demonstrates that the LPFS-BF 2.0, as developed by Hutsebaut et al. [21] and updated by Weekers et al. [22], may be useful for assessing impairments in self and interpersonal functioning in Turkish adolescents as outlined in DSM-5 Section III. The feasibility of obtaining a reliable and accurate assessment of DSM-5 Section III impairments in self and interpersonal functioning with only 12 items suggests that these dimensions can be assessed despite resource constraints such as time and money. This study is robust due to the inclusion of participants from both clinical and community settings. This study is notable as one of the few investigations of the reliability and validity of the LPFS-BF 2.0 in adolescents. To our knowledge, the LoPF-Q 12–18 is currently the only validated self-report measure of personality functioning available for Turkish adolescents. The validity and reliability of the culturally adapted Turkish version of the LoPF-Q 12–18 was tested in Turkish adolescents by Cosgun et al. [28]. Although the LoPF-Q 12–18 provides a comprehensive assessment of personality functioning, its 97-item format may limit its feasibility in some clinical and research settings. In contrast, the LPFS-BF 2.0 provides a brief and accessible alternative that may increase feasibility in busy clinical environments and large-scale research contexts.

In conclusion, the Turkish adaptation of the LPFS-BF 2.0 serves as a concise and accessible instrument that provides a rapid, reliable and valid assessment of the severity of personality pathology, specifically aligned with the principles of the AMPD model. In addition, the demonstration of configural, metric, and partial scalar invariance supports the use of the LPFS-BF 2.0 across both clinical and community adolescent populations. This study demonstrates the potential of the LPFS-BF 2.0 as a concise instrument that may assist researchers and clinicians in assessing impairments in self and interpersonal functioning as described in DSM-5 Section III.
