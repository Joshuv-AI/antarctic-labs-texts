Study on the Mechanism of the Wet Carbonation Reaction and Performance Characterization of Ammonium Chloride-Modified Steel Slag
Authors: Guilin Li, Shaoqi Kong, Jiadong Guo, Haojie Xia, Kunjie Li, Gan Feng, Xudong Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Base-activated waste carbonation (BAWC) is a highly promising carbon sequestration strategy that utilizes alkaline solid waste to react with CO2, converting it into stable mineral carbonates. This approach achieves dual objectives of carbon storage and resource utilization. This study proposes a wet carbonation process for steel slag modified with ammonium chloride, aiming to achieve effective CO2 sequestration while promoting the resource recovery of steel slag, thereby mitigating greenhouse gas emissions. The study first investigates the modification effect of ammonium chloride solution on steel slag, demonstrating significant improvements in its physicochemical properties, particularly in terms of reactivity and surface structure. Subsequently, a wet carbonation process is employed, and the effects of temperature, stirring speed, carbonation time, and liquid-to-solid ratio on the carbonation reaction are systematically examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), and other characterization techniques. The carbonation products and CO2 sequestration efficiency of the modified steel slag under these four factors are analyzed. Experimental results indicate that the primary mineral components of the modified steel slag are Ca2SiO4, MgO, and CaO, confirming its potential as an industrial waste material with excellent CO2 storage capacity. After carbonation, the main products are calcite-type calcium carbonate crystals and silica gel. Microstructural analysis reveals that the carbonated slag exhibits a denser and more compact structure, with reduced porosity and disordered aggregates of polyhedral crystals. Under optimized reaction conditions (60 °C, 30 min of CO2 bubbling, liquid-to-solid ratio of 200 g/L, and stirring speed of 600 rpm), the modified steel slag achieves a maximum CO2 sequestration efficiency of 32.2%, meaning that each kilogram of modified slag can mineralize and absorb 0.322 kg of CO2. These findings demonstrate the significant potential of the wet carbonation process for both steel slag recycling and CO2 capture.

Introduction

With the rapid development of the global economy, extensive human activities coupled with large-scale exploitation and combustion of coal have resulted in massive CO2 emissions, significantly increasing atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations and driving continuous global temperature rise. According to the latest report from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), the global average surface temperature has already increased by approximately 1 °C between 1850 and 1900 and is projected to rise by up to 2 °C by 2100. These findings underscore the immense challenges ahead in mitigating global warming. In response, researchers and engineers have explored multiple approaches to reduce CO2 emissions, proposing Carbon Capture, Utilization, and Storage (CCUS) technologies. , As a key component of CCUS, CO2 mineralization converts Ca2 + and Mg2 + from natural minerals or alkaline solid wastes into stable carbonates (e.g., CaCO3, MgCO3) and silica gel. This process not only facilitates CO2 sequestration but also enhances the consolidation and mechanical strength of waste-based cementitious materials. − The International Energy Agency (IEA) predicts that by 2050, global CCS/CCUS could mitigate over 7.6 billion tons of CO2 annually. , As of March 2024, more than 50 operational CCUS projects worldwide have a reported cumulative capacity of 42.16 million tons/year, while IEA estimates suggest this figure may reach 69.65 million tons/year. In China alone, over 120 CCUS demonstration projects (operational or planned) boast a CO2 capture capacity exceeding 6 million tons/year. Notably, the National Energy Group has developed an integrated CO2 mineralization process for fly ash bricks, achieving a carbonation rate exceeding 5% and sequestering over 10,000 tons of CO2 annually at full production capacity. Alkaline industrial wastes, such as carbide slag, steel slag, , and waste concrete, have emerged as promising feedstocks for CCUS. Key findings include the following: Wang et al. observed a 5–15% strength improvement in slag/fly ash-stabilized soil after 3 h of CO2 carbonation. Ding et al. developed a slag-based carbonation filler (SCFM) with a maximum CO2 storage capacity of 1.83 mg per gram. Yang et al. demonstrated that carbonation curing significantly enhances the strength of steel slag-based cementitious systems. These studies confirm that metallurgical wastes (e.g., blast furnace slag, steel slag) not only effectively sequester CO2 but also improve the mechanical performance of cementitious materials.

As a byproduct of steel production, steel slag possesses significant resource potential. The steel industry accounts for approximately 14% of China’s total carbon emissions, with about 0.8 tons of CO2 emitted per ton of crude steel produced, presenting a formidable challenge for emission reduction in this sector. The treatment and utilization of steel slag has long been a major challenge for the steel industry. Traditional disposal methods such as stockpiling, landfilling, and direct burial not only consume substantial land resources but also create negative environmental impacts. With growing public awareness of environmental issues, the dual problems of high CO2 emissions and difficult steel slag utilization in steel enterprises demand urgent solutions. Utilizing steel slag for CO2 mineral sequestration offers a promising approach. Based on a 40% CaO content in steel slag, this method could potentially absorb 92.4–154 million tons of CO2 annually while producing 201–350 million tons of precipitated calcium carbonate. This waste-to-resource strategy enables high-value utilization of both CO2 and steel slag, demonstrating considerable application prospects.

Steel slag, a byproduct of steelmaking, is primarily composed of CaO, SiO2, Fe2O3, MgO, and other components, with CaO accounting for approximately 44.9% of its composition. During utilization or storage, steel slag undergoes hydration reactions with ambient moisture, producing alkaline hydration products such as Ca­(OH)2 and Mg­(OH)2. These alkaline compounds exhibit chemical instability and progressively react with atmospheric CO2 through carbonation processes, forming stable substances including calcium carbonate (CaCO3) and magnesium carbonate (MgCO3). This inherent carbonation capability enables steel slag to effectively sequester CO2. −

In recent years, research on the application of steel slag in CO2 sequestration has become a focal point in academic circles. , CO2 sequestration technologies can be primarily categorized into two types: direct and indirect sequestration. , Direct sequestration methods are further subdivided into hot and cold approaches. , The hot carbonation process utilizes waste gas CO2 to treat hot steel slag, achieving dual functions of modification and carbon sequestration. Tian et al. demonstrated that temperature is a critical factor influencing carbonation efficiency, with higher temperatures enhancing reaction effectiveness while the presence of silicon dioxide accelerates sequestration rates. However, hot carbonation faces technical challenges: excessive temperatures may cause CaCO3 decomposition, while insufficient temperatures reduce reaction rates. Therefore, optimizing the utilization of waste heat during steel slag discharge remains an unresolved technical challenge. Cold direct carbonation mainly includes dry and wet methods. Dry carbonation involves reacting crushed steel slag directly with CO2 gas to form CaCO3 but suffers from low reaction efficiency and incomplete conversion of CaO and MgO. Wet carbonation addresses the hydration expansion issues of CaO and MgO by reacting steel slag slurry with CO2 under controlled temperature and pressure conditions. Rushendra Revathy et al. found that the slurry method shows advantages in CO2 capture capacity, though reaction efficiency of CaO and MgO still requires improvement. Chang et al. achieved approximately 127 kg of CO2 fixed per ton of steel slag by processing slag to below 44 μm under conditions of 700 psi and 160 °C for 60 min. While high-pressure treatment significantly enhances reaction outcomes, the demanding equipment and safety requirements hinder large-scale industrial application. Furthermore, the carbonate products face limitations in separation and utilization, typically being used as low-value industrial materials. , Residual-free CaO and MgO may pose potential risks to system stability and safety. In summary, direct steel slag carbonation remains in the research phase, constrained by complex reaction kinetics, operational difficulties, product separation challenges, and purity limitations, and has yet to meet the requirements for large-scale, high-value applications. The technology currently faces multiple technical bottlenecks that must be overcome before achieving industrial viability.

In the realm of indirect sequestration, hydrothermal carbonation (HTC) has emerged as a novel approach for steel slag valorization, attracting significant attention due to its dual benefits in resource recovery and carbon sequestration by effectively converting calcium, magnesium, and other alkaline metal elements in steel slag into stable carbonate minerals. This process not only reduces the leaching risk of harmful elements (e.g., heavy metals) from steel slag, mitigating potential environmental threats to soil and water bodies, but also offers a promising solution to address global climate change through carbon sequestration. Among various calcium extraction methods, indirect hydrothermal carbonation is particularly valued for its strong controllability and excellent calcium selectivity. − This approach typically employs chemical reagents (e.g., inorganic acids, organic acids, or ammonium salts) to leach soluble Ca2 + and Mg2 + from steel slag followed by CO2 introduction to precipitate high-purity carbonates. Common leaching agents include strong acids, weak acids, and salts. Strong acids (e.g., HCl, H2SO4, or HNO3) exhibit poor Ca and Mg capture efficiency due to high acid consumption, strong corrosiveness, and complex side reactions. − Weak acids (e.g., acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, or valeric acid) also demonstrate limited extraction efficiency for Ca and Mg owing to their variable acid strengths. − The limitations of weak acids in Ca and Mg extraction are 3-fold: (1) their slow reaction rates prolong processing time; (2) high-concentration weak acid solutions increase overall acid consumption by enhancing the solubility of CaO and Ca2SiO4; and (3) the reaction rate significantly decreases during the initial stage of Ca2SiO4 treatment, leading to calcium loss. Furthermore, during acid leaching experiments, the gradual decrease in solution pH inhibits CaCO3 precipitation under acidic conditions. The innovative pH-swing indirect mineralization method, which involves steel slag leaching with acidic reagents followed by mineralization in alkaline conditions to obtain high-purity CaCO3 and MgCO3 products, represents a complex experimental process that promotes effective industrial solid waste mineralization. Compared to acid leaching, ammonium salt solvents are considered more operationally feasible and economically viable for steel slag treatment due to their moderate acidity, excellent calcium dissolution capacity, and low corrosiveness. Initially proposed by Yogo et al. as self-regenerating solvents to improve process economics after carbonation, ammonium salts (NH4Cl or NH4NO3) have become the most common reagents for dissolving alkaline components in steel slag. Their dissolution selectivity follows the order (CaO and Ca2SiO4) > (Ca3MgSi2O8, Ca2MgSi2O7, and MgO) > Ca2Al2SiO7 > MgCr2O4, making ammonium salt leaching more competitive than acid leaching from a reagent recovery perspective, particularly for alkaline steel slag. Eloneva et al. investigated the selectivity and leaching efficiency of calcium ions from steel slag using ammonium salt solutions (NH4Cl, NH4NO3, and (NH4)2SO4) via indirect hydrothermal methods. Besides effectively dissolving calcium from steel slag, these solvents demonstrated calcium selectivity, yielding pure CaCO3 precipitates from the resulting solutions. Xie et al. compared four ammonium salts (NH4NO3, NH4Cl, CH3COONH4, and (NH4)2SO4) as solvents, finding that NH4Cl exhibited comparable calcium extraction efficiency to NH4NO3 and CH3COONH4, with superior extraction capability. As a solvent, NH4Cl offers excellent solubility and stability while avoiding precipitation with sulfate ions (SO4 2–), thereby ensuring efficient calcium extraction. Moreover, NH4Cl’s advantages as a common chemical reagent include low economic cost (enhancing experimental feasibility and economic viability), simplified experimental procedures, and minimized side reactions (improving result accuracy). Consequently, NH4Cl was selected as the ideal solvent for this study due to its high calcium extraction efficiency, stability, and cost-effectiveness.

To further enhance the efficiency of steel slag wet carbonation processes, researchers have explored various modification methods to improve the slag’s reactivity. Ammonium chloride (NH4Cl) has emerged as a prominent modifying agent due to its ability to facilitate reactions between slag minerals and CO2, thereby improving both reaction rates and carbonate yields during wet carbonation. As one of the key research focuses in slag modification, NH4Cl introduction significantly enhances slag solubility, promoting more efficient reactions between Ca2 +/Mg2 + ions in the slag and CO2. This mechanism contributes to overall performance improvement of the wet carbonation process.

In terms of the Life Cycle Assessment (LCA), the wet carbonation technology of ammonium chloride-modified steel slag demonstrates significant carbon reduction potential. Through this technology, carbon dioxide can be effectively converted into stable carbonates, thereby reducing greenhouse gas emissions. LCA can comprehensively identify and evaluate the environmental burdens of a product or process throughout its entire life cycle, including resource consumption, impacts on the natural environment, and potential risks to human health. According to ISO 14040 and ISO 14044 standards, LCA evaluates the environmental factors and potential impacts throughout a product’s life cycle, covering all stages from raw material acquisition, production, and use to disposal and recycling.

From the perspective of industrial recycling economics, the wet carbonation technology using ammonium chloride-modified steel slag offers certain economic advantages. As an industrial waste byproduct, steel slag has a relatively low acquisition cost, while byproducts such as calcium carbonate and magnesium carbonate have considerable market value, particularly in construction materials, wastewater treatment, and other fields. By optimizing process parameters such as temperature, pressure, and liquid-to-solid ratio, carbonation efficiency can be improved, energy consumption reduced, and operational costs further lowered. Ma et al. conducted a systematic cost analysis of four wet CO2 mineralization schemes for blast furnace slag using the LCA method, revealing that material costs and energy costs accounted for approximately 59%–70% and 10%–20% of the total cost, respectively. However, the scalability of this technology still faces challenges, primarily in process stability, equipment investment, and the regeneration and recycling of ammonium chloride. In particular, further research is needed on ammonium chloride recovery and regeneration technologies to reduce operational costs and enhance overall economic efficiency.

Overall, the wet carbonation technology of ammonium chloride-modified steel slag holds significant application potential in carbon dioxide capture and storage. With continued process optimization, improved ammonium chloride recovery efficiency, and the expansion of application fields for carbonate products, this technology is expected to not only contribute to carbon emission reduction but also promote the resource utilization of industrial waste, thereby further advancing the goal of carbon neutrality. Future research can focus on enhancing the reaction rate, developing more efficient ammonium chloride recovery technologies, and exploring diversified applications of carbonate products, driving this technology toward broader practical implementation.

This study aims to investigate the modification effects of ammonium chloride (NH4Cl) on the wet carbonation process of steel slag, with particular focus on analyzing its enhancement of the slag’s reactivity. Through comprehensive examination of multiphase reaction mechanisms, we will systematically explore NH4Cl’s influence on carbonate precipitation and CO2 sequestration under various conditions. Furthermore, by conducting detailed characterization of the modified slag’s properties, this work will evaluate its potential for CO2 sequestration and resource utilization, thereby providing both theoretical foundations and technical support for sustainable steel slag management.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Materials

The modified steel slag was obtained from solid waste generated during steel production at a magnesium smelting plant in the Henan Province. The raw material appeared as irregular chunks or granules with particle lengths ranging between 2 and 3 cm (Figure a). For preparation of modified slag powder, the raw material was first crushed using a jaw crusher and then ground to 200–300 mesh (corresponding to 50–74 μm particle size) using a planetary ball mill. The resulting powder morphology is shown in Figure (b), with its microstructure observable in Figure (c). After NH4Cl solution modification and subsequent drying at 105 °C, the treated slag powder exhibited modified surface characteristics (Figure c). SEM imaging (Figure d) and XRD analysis (Figure e) revealed Ca2SiO4 as the dominant crystalline phase, along with minor components including MgO and CaO as evidenced by weaker diffraction peaks. The hydration of Ca2SiO4 and CaO produces alkaline compounds that readily react with CO2 in moist environments to form stable calcium carbonate crystals and other hydration products. This carbonation mechanism provides excellent CO2 sequestration capacity, making it suitable for industrial waste utilization. The experimental procedure is as follows: first, we added an appropriate amount of deionized water into the flask and heat it up to the preset temperature of the water, then weighed modified steel slag according to the experimental requirements, and added it into the flask. Analytical Methods for Carbonation Product Characterization

At the end of the experiment, the solid-phase product from the carbonation reaction was filtered, collected, and treated with anhydrous ethanol to stop the hydration reaction. It was then dried at 105 °C for 12 h, milled to a particle size below 75 μm, and mixed well for subsequent testing on carbonation products and carbon sequestration efficiency. Each experiment was repeated three times to minimize errors.

Particle Size Distribution (PSD)

A small amount of the sample was placed in a beaker, mixed with tap water until turbid, and analyzed using a Malvern Mastersizer 2000 laser particle sizer. The shading was maintained between 5% and 20% during the measurement.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD)

The sample was ground to <75 μm and analyzed with a D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer. Using X-rays from a Cu target, the device provided qualitative and quantitative analysis of the carbonation product’s chemical composition, crystallinity, and phases, with results analyzed using Jade software.

Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

The sample was mixed with potassium bromide (1:200), pressed into tablets, and tested with a Nicolet iN10 and iZ10 infrared spectrometer, equipped with an MCT detector and a KBr beam splitter.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM-EDS)

A small sample was mounted on conductive adhesive, gold-coated, and analyzed using a ZEISS GeminiSEM 300. Morphological and elemental details were observed with an accelerating voltage range of 0.02–30 kV and a probe current range of 3 pA-20 nA, using an SE2 secondary electron detector.

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TG-DTG)

Approximately 20 mg of the sample was heated from 30 to 1000 °C in a nitrogen atmosphere at a rate of 20 °C/min using a TGA 5500 thermogravimetric analyzer. Mass changes during heating were recorded to analyze carbonation product behavior.

Results and Discussion

Changes in Particle Size of Modified Steel Slag before and after Carbonation

Particle size distribution reflects the distribution of particles of different sizes in the powder and can reveal the uniformity of the particles. The smaller the uniformity coefficient of particle size distribution, the more uniform the distribution of particles, and vice versa. Specific surface area indicates the total surface area possessed by solid-phase particles per unit mass. The smaller the particle size, the greater the number of particles per unit volume, and therefore the greater the specific surface area. D10 indicates that particles with a size less than 10 μm accounted for 10% of the total sample, while the physical meanings of D50 and D90 are similar to that of D10 and will not be repeated here. Usually, D10 represents the fine particle size of solid-phase powders, D50 represents the average particle size, and D90 represents the coarser particle size. According to the data in Table and Figure , it can be seen that after the carbonation reaction, the particles of modified steel slag were reduced to fine-end particle size, average particle size, and coarse-end particle size. Meanwhile, the specific surface area of the modified steel slag particles increased significantly after the carbonation reaction, and the uniformity coefficient of particle distribution also increased. This indicates that the carbonation reaction resulted in a smaller particle size, a more uniform particle size distribution, and a narrower range of particle size distribution for the modified steel slag particles. The NH4Cl solution extracted Ca2+ with a very high selectivity due to the fact that the pH value of the NH4Cl solution during the modification process (9.41) was in the range in which only Ca2+ could be extracted (8.0–10.5), thus extracting the steel slag particles. 10.5), thereby extracting calcium from steel slag to form Ca2+, f-CaO, etc. Kodama et al. demonstrated that NH4Cl solution extracted Ca2+ with very high selectivity by the composition of the reaction, pH, and conversion results in calcium extraction experiments using HCl, CH3COOH, and NH4Cl solutions. The formation of soluble compounds led to the dissolution or decomposition of some of the large particles in the steel slag, thus reducing the coarse particles in the slag. It can also be visualized from the SEM images in Figure that the calcium carbonate crystals of carbonation products are mainly uniformly dispersed in rhombic and cubic shapes, and most of the particle sizes are less than 1 μm. Similarly, Yi et al. obtained similar conclusions in carbonation experiments of refining slag at different temperatures, and the particle sizes of the carbonation product calcium carbonate crystals increased with the increase of carbonation temperature.

pH Change before and after the Slurry Carbonation Reaction

Wet carbonation of alkali solid waste refers to the process of using carbonic acid gas (CO2) to react with alkali solid waste (usually alkali-metal or alkaline-earth metal-rich wastes, such as slag, coal ash, industrial wastes, etc.) to generate carbonate or other carbonation products, which is essentially a process of capturing and immobilizing carbon dioxide in solid waste through a wet reaction, thus realizing the resourceful use of the wastes and carbon dioxide emission reduction. Therefore, the decrease rate of pH of the slurry supernatant can reflect the carbonation rate of modified steel slag. Figure shows the pH change of the carbonation reaction of modified steel slag slurry under different conditions. As can be seen from Figure , before the carbonation reaction, the pH of the slurry is maintained at 7.8–9.2. This is mainly because the alkaline reaction between the steel slag and the ammonium chloride solution mainly involves two chemical processes.

pH change of carbonated slurry of modified steel slag slurry under different conditions: (a) different speeds,(b) different temperatures, and (c) different solid–liquid ratios.

First, the calcium oxide (CaO) in the steel slag reacts with water to form calcium hydroxide (Ca­(OH)2), and this reaction releases hydroxide ions (OH–), which makes the solution alkaline. The specific reaction formula is CaO+H2O→Ca(OH)2 1

Calcium hydroxide (Ca­(OH)2) further releases hydroxide ions (OH–) when dissolved in water: Ca(OH)2(s)→Ca2+(aq)+2OH−(aq) 2

Second, ammonium chloride (NH4Cl) undergoes partial hydrolysis in water to produce ammonia (NH3) and amine ion (NH4+): NH4Cl(aq)⇋NH4+(aq)+Cl‐(aq) 3

Ammonia (NH3) is a strong base that absorbs hydrogen ions from water (H+) to produce ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH): NH3(aq)+H2O(l)⇋NH4+(aq)+OH‐(aq) 4

Finally, the CaO in the steel slag reacts with the ammonia in the ammonium chloride solution, possibly producing calcium chloride (CaCl2) and ammonia (NH3). In this reaction, the hydroxide ions (OH–) provided by the CaO combine with the NH4+ in the ammonia to release more OH–, further increasing the alkalinity of the solution. The reaction equation is CaO(s)+2NH4+(aq)+2OH‐(aq)→CaCl2(aq)+2NH3(aq)+H2O(l) 5

Therefore, the reaction between steel slag and ammonium chloride solution is alkaline, mainly due to the reaction of calcium oxide (CaO) with water to form calcium hydroxide (Ca­(OH)2), releasing hydroxide ions (OH–), and the reaction of ammonia in the ammonium chloride solution to form more OH–, which improves the alkalinity of the solution.

When CO2 gas enters the slurry, the pH of the slurry decreases rapidly as the hydroxide ions in the reaction system are consumed. This suggests that the rate at which hydroxide ions are produced by hydrolysis of modified steel slag is much lower than the rate at which the slurry absorbs CO2 gas and produces hydrogen ions, leading to a sustained decrease in slurry pH. For example, the pH of the slurry decreased from 8.6 to about 6.8 when the aeration time was 5 min. As the carbonation reaction proceeded, the pH of the slurry stabilized after 30 min and remained between 6.1 and 6.9, indicating that the rate of hydroxide ion production from the hydrolysis of modified steel slag was basically balanced by the rate of hydrogen ion production from the absorption of CO2 gas at this time. Overall, the longer the time of CO2 gas, the lower the pH value of the slurry, indicating that the carbonation degree of modified steel slag is higher.

From Figure (a), it can be seen that when the rotor speed is 600 r/min, the pH of the slurry basically tends to be stabilized after 15 min, while the pH of the slurry tends to be stabilized for about 25 min when the rotor speeds are 300 and 1800 r/min. The main reason is that stirring at a proper speed has the effect of stirring the solid-phase components in the liquid uniformly, accelerating the dissolution, and effectively enhancing the carbonation reaction rate. With the increase of stirring speed, the change of pH value is first due to the hydrogen ions released from the dissolution of carbon dioxide to make the solution pH drop, and then under the effect of accelerated stirring, hydroxide ions react with carbon dioxide to form carbonate precipitation, which leads to a gradual recovery of the pH value, and this change of pH value shows a trend of decreasing and then increasing. Huijgen et al. studied the influence of stirring rate on the conversion rate through the stirring rate of steel slag carbonation rate of about 25 min; the rate has little effect on the carbonation rate of steel slag, which suggests that the mass transfer of Ca2+ from the particle surface to the solution is relatively rapid, while diffusion from the interior of the particles to the surface of the particles is the decisive step in the whole carbonation reaction. From Figure (b), it can be seen that the pH of the slurry increases with the increase of the carbonation reaction temperature; especially at 90 °C, the pH of the slurry carbonation reaction for 15 min is 6.7, and the pH of the slurry carbonation reaction for 30 min reaches 6.1, indicating that the higher temperature of the slurry is more conducive to the carbonation reaction of modified steel slag. This is because the high temperature in the carbonation environment promotes the molecular thermal movement. On the one hand, the high temperature favors the Ca2+ transport rate, and on the other hand, it enhances the diffusion of CO2 in the aqueous solution, thus accelerating the precipitation reaction of CaCO3. This explains why the rate of pH decrease accelerated with increasing suspension temperature after CO2 injection, while the rate of pH decrease was slowest at room temperature. However, the increase in temperature leads to a decrease in the solubility of carbon oxides in solution, which is manifested as a decrease in the amount of carbon dioxide in aqueous solution. However, while the increase in temperature promotes the diffusion rate of Ca2+ and CO2, it also reduces the solubility of Ca2+ and CO2 in aqueous solution to some extent, with a decrease in the amount of Ca2+ and CO3 2–. This phenomenon can also be verified in the diffraction peak intensities of calcium carbonate crystals in Figure (b), i.e., the intensity of the main diffraction peak of calcium carbonate crystals increases significantly with increasing pulp temperature. The reaction temperature has a complex effect on the carbonation rate. Huijgen et al. found that the carbonation rate increased with increasing temperature in the range of 25 to 175 °C, mainly due to the accelerated leaching of Ca2+at high temperatures, and the leaching of Ca2+ showed Arrhenius-type temperature dependence in the range of 50 to 150 °C, which suggests that the temperature is a rate-controlling factor for the leaching of Ca2+ in this range. Leaching is a rate-controlling step, and above the optimal reaction temperature (>200 °C), CO2 dissolution determines the rate of steel slag carbonation. Therefore, within a certain temperature range, the slurry temperature variation plays an important role in the carbonation reaction of modified steel slag. From Figure (c), it can be seen that when the solid–liquid ratio is 100 g/L, the pH of the slurry is 6.6, and when the solid–liquid ratio is 300 g/L, the pH of the slurry is 6.9. The pH of the slurry increases significantly with the increase of the solid–liquid ratio, which indicates that with the increase of the ionic strength as well as the increase of the solubility of calcium, the higher the degree of carbonation reaction of the modified steel slag.

Contour plot and response surface plot of the interaction effect of three factors on carbonization efficiency.

The experiment systematically investigated the effects of four factors (stirring speed, temperature, air supply time, and solid-to-liquid ratio) on the carbonation behavior of ammonium chloride-modified steel slag using a single-factor design. A total of 13 experimental runs were conducted, with each run repeated three times to improve the reliability and accuracy of the data, eliminate random errors, ensure the stability of the results, and enhance the repeatability and adaptability of the experiment. In each experimental group, only one factor was varied while keeping the others constant when the air supply time was fixed, to clearly identify the effect of each variable on the final reaction. The experiment utilized repeated measures ANOVA and one-way ANOVA tests from SPSS software. The repeated measures ANOVA in SPSS was used to examine situations where multiple measurements were taken under different conditions for the same group of subjects, to determine whether there was an inherent correlation between the data. The one-way ANOVA tests in SPSS were used to check whether there were significant differences between the three experimental groups, followed by posthoc tests such as LSD and Duncan’s (D) method in the one-way ANOVA to further identify which groups exhibited significant differences. Analysis of the Mineral Composition of Carbonation Products

The mineral phases of the modified steel slag stock sample and its carbonation products were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD). According to the results of the graphical analysis [Figures (e) and ], the main mineral phase of the modified steel slag sample is Ca2SiO4. After the hydration reaction, the mineral phases in the slag are mainly transformed into Ca2SiO4 and Ca­(OH)2. With the carbonation reaction, the mineral phase of the modified steel slag changed significantly, mainly to CaCO3. However, no diffraction peaks of Ca­(OH)2 were observed in the plots, which indicated that the Ca­(OH)2 generated by the hydration reaction was completely reacted with CO2. In addition, the diffraction peak intensities of minerals such as Ca2SiO4 and CaO significantly weaken or disappear after carbonation, indicating that these calcium-rich minerals and different forms of calcium silicate minerals can react with CO2 to form CaCO3. In particular, in Figure (a), the intensity of the main diffraction peak of CaCO3 (2θ = 29.4°) increases significantly with the increase of stirring speed, which is closely related to the phenomenon of cosolubilization of solid-phase constituents and CO2 gas in the liquid phase. Further analysis revealed that CaCO3 mainly existed in the form of calcite (2θ=29.4°, 47.5°) and aragonite (2θ=23.1°), and the ratio of different cementitious materials affected the crystalline morphology of the mineralized products.

Diffraction patterns of carbonation products of modified steel slag under different conditions. (a) Different speeds, (b) different temperatures, (c) different carbonation times, and (d) different solid–liquid ratios.

In Figure (b), the intensity of the main diffraction peak (2θ = 29.4°) of CaCO3 crystals gradually decreases with increasing temperature. The main reason for this phenomenon is that although increasing the temperature can increase the solubility of calcium-rich components in water, the solubility of CO2 in the liquid phase decreases at high temperatures, and therefore, simply raising the temperature did not significantly improve the carbonation efficiency of modified steel slag. ,

In Figure (c), the intensity of the main diffraction peak (2θ = 29.4°) of CaCO3 crystals was significantly enhanced with the increase of carbonation time, which is consistent with the results of Wang et al., indicating that the increase of carbonation time contributes to the enhancement of calcium carbonate crystal content. The main diffraction peak intensity of calcium carbonate crystals (2θ = 29.4°) significantly increases. The primary reason for this is that Ca2SiO4 in the modified steel slag particles, under the influence of water, can rapidly release calcium ions and other components, thereby forming a silica-rich, low-calcium gel layer. At the same time, the calcium and magnesium ions released from the modified steel slag particles react with the carbonate ions in the pore solution by crystallization to form precipitates, and these precipitates form a network-like structure on the surface of the modified steel slag particles and in the pore structure. These calcium carbonate precipitates and gel-like products are tightly wrapped around the particle surface to form dense layered carbonation products. Therefore, the carbonation reaction of modified steel slag can generate a large number of calcium carbonate crystals as well as highly polymerized silica gels in a short time, which explains the variation of the diffraction intensity of calcium carbonate crystals with the extension of carbonation time in Figure (c).

In Figure (d), the intensity of the main diffraction peak (2θ = 29.4°) of CaCO3 crystals shows a tendency to increase and then decrease with the increase of the liquid–solid ratio. The reason for this is that when the liquid–solid ratio is low, the solid-phase components cannot be mixed sufficiently in the liquid, which leads to the difficulty of calcium-rich components to be completely dissolved into the liquid-phase system, thus reducing the number of calcium carbonate crystals produced.

In X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns, the height and peak area of diffraction peaks are usually positively correlated with the content of crystals in the sample and their crystallinity. Based on this principle, the total diffraction peak area (A) and peak height (H) of CaCO3 crystals in Figure were quantitatively calculated using Jade analysis software, and the results are shown in Table . From the data in Table , it can be seen that there is a significant effect of different process parameters on the crystallization behavior of calcium carbonate crystals. In terms of stirring speed, with the increase of stirring speed, the total peak area of calcium carbonate crystals showed A1800r/min > A600r/min > A300r/min in order, indicating that the high speed stirring helps the crystal generation whereas the peak height showed H600r/min > H1800r/min > H300r/min, suggesting that too high stirring rate may lead to the decrease of crystal size or crystallinity. In terms of slurry temperature, with the increase of temperature, the order of peak area changes was A60 °C > A30 °C > A90 °C, indicating that the medium temperature was favorable for the generation of crystal number, while the order of peak height was H30 °C > H60 °C > H90 °C, suggesting that the high temperature might inhibit the complete growth of crystals. As for the carbonation reaction time, prolonging the reaction time significantly increased the degree of crystallization of CaCO3, and the peak areas and peak heights showed the trends of A60 min > A30 min > A5 min> A1 min and H60 min > H30 min > H5 min > H1 min, which indicated that the crystals were continuously generated and tended to mature. As for the solid–liquid ratio, with the change of the solid phase content in the slurry, the peak areas and peak heights of calcium carbonate were in the order of A200g/L > A300g/L > A100g/L and H200g/L > H300g/L > H100g/L, which indicated that an appropriate increase in the solid phase content could help the formation and growth of the crystals, but too high a ratio of the liquid phase could dilute the reactants and inhibit the formation of crystals. In conclusion, the stirring speed, slurry temperature, carbonation time, and solid–liquid ratio have significant effects on the crystal structure formation of calcium carbonate, and the regulation of these parameters can effectively optimize the crystallization behavior and material properties of calcium carbonate.

Chemical Structure Analysis of Carbonation Products

Figure shows the infrared spectra of carbonation products and hydration products of modified steel slag. From Figure (a), it can be seen that the hydration products of modified steel slag are located at the peaks at 713, 874, 1047, 1426, and 1794 cm–1, which may be attributed to the characteristic infrared vibrations of the silicate products in modified steel slag. The XRD patterns, on the other hand, exhibit different diffraction peaks for each polycrystalline species, thus allowing a clear distinction. After the carbonation reaction [Figure (b)–(e)], new characteristic absorption peaks appeared near 711, 874, and 1405∼1455 cm–1 after carbonation of modified steel slag. These absorption peaks mainly originated from the characteristic vibrations of C–O bonds in the calcium carbonate crystals generated during the carbonation process: among them, the peak at 711 cm–1 was related to the out-of-molecular-plane vibrations of C–O bonds in calcite-type calcium carbonate, resulting in a specific infrared absorption peak, and the peak near 874 cm–1 was attributed to the C–O bonds in aragonite-type calcium carbonate. C–O bonds in calcium carbonate vibrate in the molecular plane, leading to a specific IR absorption peak region, while the absorption peaks in the range of 1405 to 1455 cm–1 reflect the antisymmetric telescoping vibrations of the C–O bonds in the molecule of calcium carbonate crystals in the direction of their bonding axes, leading to pronounced absorption peaks. − Compared with Figure (a), the carbonated samples showed significant C–O bond absorption bands at 711 and 874 cm–1 with anomalous peak intensities. Second, asymmetric broad absorption peaks appeared at 1405–1455 cm–1 for the carbonated sample, corresponding to the stretching vibration of the carbonate group, and then it indicates that the modified steel slag formed calcium carbonate crystals with low crystallinity during the carbonation process. The peaks at 1794 and 3695 cm–1 correspond to −OH, which is associated with incomplete carbonation of Ca­(OH)2. Wei et al. found that the presence of Si–O–Si bonding during the carbonation process may have a certain effect on the identification of silica gel. However, the significant increase in the area of the absorption peak at 1120 cm–1 still provides sufficient evidence that modified steel slag does form silica structures during the carbonation reaction. Pan et al. found that absorption peaks between 1010 cm–1 and ∼1050 cm–1 corresponded to silica gels with a high degree of polymerization. Therefore, the broad peak near 1047 cm–1 corresponds to the asymmetric stretching vibration of Si–O–Si in highly polymerized calcium-modified silica gel, which gradually dominates and enhances during the carbonation curing process, which confirms that the highly polymerized calcium-modified silica gel is one of the carbonation products. Therefore, the absorption peak at 1047 cm–1 is the Si–O stretching vibration peak, which corresponds to the highly polymerized silica gel. It can be seen that the modified steel slag produced a large number of calcium carbonate crystals and silica gel during the carbonation reaction, and this analytical result was also confirmed in the XRD pattern.

Calcite exhibits a well-defined cubic morphology, reflecting its highly stable and symmetric crystal structure, which is consistent with its thermodynamic stability under ambient conditions. Aragonite has an acicular morphology, a typical rhombohedral crystal structure, and a uniform distribution of elongated rod-like grains, consistent with its substable nature under synthetic conditions. Calcium carbonate minerals have been observed to have a spherical or globular morphology, which is indicative of their hexagonal crystal structure, and usually occur as aggregates due to their high surface energy and tendency to minimize surface area.

It can be seen that the temperature change affects the nucleation rate, growth rate, and crystal transformation of calcium carbonate crystals. Despite the similar composition of the samples, the morphology of the crystals changed with increasing temperature. This is in agreement with the findings in the literature that increasing the temperature will induce the production of more needle and rod aragonite type calcium carbonate crystals. SEM images from experimental studies by Bao et al. show that spherical aragonite is easily formed at low temperatures, whereas aragonite is more stable at high temperatures. Typically, larger calcite and aragonite crystals are formed at high temperatures compared to low temperatures, and it is clear that due to the high thermodynamic stability of calcite crystals with respect to temperature, they may precipitate at different temperatures, and thus the substable phases formed at different temperatures are calcite and aragonite, respectively. This result is in good agreement with deBoer’s experimental observation that aragonite is stable at low temperatures whereas the formation of aragonite is mainly determined by the increase in temperature because of the higher growth rate at high temperatures compared to calcite and aragonite. On the other hand, the change of temperature directly affects the formation conditions of crystals and the growth mechanism of crystals; therefore, the polycrystalline forms of calcium carbonate crystals under different temperature conditions show different molecular structures, which further determines their physical properties, stability, and chemical activity, and the different temperature conditions not only affect the appearance and morphology of the crystals but also change the internal structure of the crystals, which leads to different crystalline forms. This indicates that the carbonation products of modified steel slag are mainly calcium carbonate crystals. This is because CO3 2 – reacts rapidly with high-concentration CO2 gas in a humid environment to form calcium carbonate crystals and silica gel. Therefore, during the carbonation reaction, CO2 gas is mineralized and absorbed by the calcium-rich mineral phases in the modified steel slag, leading to an increase in the C content in the carbonation products, while Si is converted into silica gel.

Reaction mechanism of the Ca+ leaching process.

Carbonation reaction occurs heterogeneously between Ca2+ extracted from an aqueous solution of ammonium salt and carbon dioxide gas to produce precipitated calcium carbonate (CaCO3). The initial pH of the NH4Cl solution used in the experiment was 9.41, which falls within the pH window (8.0–10.5) where Ca2 + has the maximum selective extraction ability. This greatly minimizes the competition from ions such as Fe3 +, Al3 +, and Mg2 +. This is because ammonium chloride is a highly selective additive, used to dissolve calcium in steelmaking slag, with a selectivity range between 0.95 and 0.97. Therefore, NH4Cl has good selectivity for the leaching of Ca2 +, effectively avoiding interference from other metal ions in the slag. Furthermore, after the carbonation reaction, the D50 of the particles decreased from 55.3 to 21.0 μm, and the specific surface area increased from 460 to 870.2 m2/kg (experiment number 1). This significant change can be attributed to the dissolution and disintegration of large particle structures caused by the leaching of Ca2 +, indicating that NH4Cl has strong permeability and reactivity toward the internal structure of the steel slag. Subsequently, in the modified steel slag, the peaks of the original Ca2SiO4, CaO, etc. weakened or even disappeared, while distinct CaCO3 crystal diffraction peaks appeared, indicating that the Ca2 + leached by NH4Cl participated in the subsequent carbonation reaction. Additionally, the typical CO absorption peaks at 711 and 874 cm– 1 in the FTIR spectrum were significantly enhanced, further verifying that the formation of calcium carbonate is closely related to the effective release of Ca2 +. The wet carbonation process of modified steel slag involves the reaction between gas, liquid, and solid phases, which can be mainly divided into three stages: first, after the modified steel slag comes into contact with water, the dissolution reaction occurs, releasing a large amount of OH– and Ca2+, which makes the pH of the slurry rise rapidly to above 8.5. The chemical reaction at this stage is mainly the hydrolysis process of modified steel slag. Second, CO2 gas dissolves in the alkaline slurry and ionizes to form CO3 2–, HCO3 –, and H+, which react with Ca2+ in the slurry to form calcium carbonate crystals and preferentially adhere to the surface of modified steel slag particles. In this process, the pH of the slurry decreases rapidly, the amorphous calcium carbonate in the liquid phase is gradually transformed into crystalline calcium carbonate, and the main process of the reaction is the carbonation reaction of modified steel slag. Finally, as the carbonation reaction continues, the pH of the slurry gradually tends to be neutral or acidic. At this stage, the rate of OH– generated by hydrolysis of modified steel slag is close to the rate of H+ generated by dissolution and ionization of CO2 gas, indicating that the carbonation reaction is basically complete. Large-Scale Application Analysis of Ammonium Chloride Modified Steel Slag Carbonation

Comparison of Ammonium Chloride Modified Steel Slag Carbonation Methods

Common leaching agents for steel slag carbonation include strong acids, weak acids, and salts. During the acid leaching process, as the reaction progresses, the pH of the solution gradually decreases, which is not conducive to the precipitation of calcium carbonate. For the process of promoting mineralization in an alkaline environment after steel slag acid leaching, an innovative indirect mineralization method based on pH swing technology is required, which is relatively complex. In contrast, ammonium salt solvents, due to their weaker acidity, better calcium dissolution ability, and lower corrosiveness, have become a more operable and cost-effective choice for steel slag treatment. Ammonium salts (such as NH4Cl or NH4NO3) were first proposed by Yogo et al. and can spontaneously regenerate after carbonation, thus improving the economic efficiency of the process. Ammonium salts (NH4Cl or NH4NO3), initially proposed by Yogo et al. as a solvent, regenerate spontaneously after carbonation to improve the economic efficiency of the process. Ammonium salts are the most commonly used agents for dissolving alkaline components in steel slag, with the sequence being (CaO and Ca2SiO4) > (Ca3MgSi2O8, Ca2MgSi2O7, and MgO) > Ca2Al2SiO7 > MgCr2O4. Therefore, for alkaline steel slag, ammonium salt leaching is more competitive than acid leaching from the perspective of reagent recovery. Xie et al. used four ammonium salts as solvents in experiments, including NH4NO3, NH4Cl, CH3COONH4, and (NH4)2SO4. When NH4Cl was used as the solvent, the calcium extraction efficiency was comparable to that of NH4NO3 and CH3COONH4, showing a high calcium extraction ability.

Zhang et al. selected ammonium salts as leaching agents and added microwave heating technology during the leaching process to accelerate the leaching reaction and improve the leaching rate. The experimental results showed that the heating rate of NH4Cl, NH4Ac, and (NH4)2SO4 in the microwave field decreased in the order of (NH4)2SO4 > NH4Ac > NH4Cl. However, the pH value of NH4Cl solution decreased the fastest, so compared to the other solutions, NH4Cl was better used as a leaching agent for steel slag. Similarly, Li et al. explored the microwave-enhanced ammonium salt leaching of steel slag and reached the same conclusion. However, this method, utilizing microwave heating technology, increases the difficulty of experimental operation.

Large-Scale Application and Impact of Ammonium Chloride

In the indirect carbonation process, the reuse and recycling of chemical reagents can significantly reduce operational costs. However, this method still faces some critical challenges in practical application. Although studies, such as that by Chu et al., suggest that recycling ammonia gas and ammonium sulfate from the leachate can lower costs, the feasibility and actual benefits of this approach still require further verification. In practice, the recovery and reuse of chemical reagents are often limited by various factors, such as the complexity of the equipment, recovery efficiency, and potential environmental and safety impacts. For example, the recovery of ammonium sulfate requires highly efficient separation technologies and may encounter contamination from impurities in the solvent, making the recovery process more difficult and expensive.

Additionally, the solvent recyclability issue raised by Said et al. cannot be ignored. While NH4Cl has been shown to enhance calcium leaching efficiency after multiple cycles, the gradual loss and degradation of the solvent may still affect the long-term stability of the process. Factors such as solvent quality control, corrosiveness, and the environmental impact of the solvent itself may become significant bottlenecks to its recycling. Particularly in steel slag indirect carbonation experiments, the nonrecyclability of the solvent may lead to high additional costs, and the benefits of the recovery process may not immediately offset these extra expenses.

Therefore, although the recycling of chemical reagents theoretically reduces costs, the complexity of practical operations, environmental impacts, and equipment investment must be fully considered. More targeted and sustainable research is needed to address efficiency issues in the recycling process, particularly how to improve recovery rates and avoid secondary pollution. This also means that in practical applications, a cost–benefit analysis of chemical reagent recycling is required, rather than solely relying on theoretical models.

Applications and Economic Viability of Carbonated Steel Slag

Although carbonated steel slag has significant advantages over ordinary steel slag in terms of performance, and its application range is wide, including in construction materials, paper and paint fillers, refractory materials, agriculture, and pharmaceuticals, there are still many critical issues when applying it to the recovery process of high-value-added products.

First, although recovering high-value-added products (such as high-purity CaCO3, SiO2, molecular sieves, etc.) during the CO2 mineralization process seems economically competitive, the technical complexity and cost issues of this process cannot be ignored. The production of high-value-added products often requires more refined separation and purification processes, which not only increases energy consumption but may also lead to higher equipment investment and operational costs. Therefore, while, theoretically, recovering these high-value-added products can improve economic benefits, their actual feasibility and long-term economic viability still need to be deeply evaluated.

Moreover, the economic analysis by Chu et al. suggests that the net benefit of sequestering 1 ton of CO2 through an indirect carbonation process is 1371 dollars. However, the calculation of this benefit may have certain limitations. Specifically, the study’s calculation does not fully consider the long-term costs of equipment and technological investments at an industrial scale, including reactor maintenance, chemical reagent consumption, and waste treatment expenditures. Even if the process theoretically achieves both CO2 sequestration and high-value-added product recovery, as the scale expands, the actual economic benefits may be affected by technical bottlenecks, market demand fluctuations, and rising raw material costs, which means the expected net benefits may not be realized.

Furthermore, although carbonated steel slag can help the steel industry achieve emission reduction goals to some extent, the environmental issues involved in its large-scale application should also be considered. The recovery of high-value-added products is often accompanied by the use of more chemical reagents and complex processes, which may lead to secondary pollution or waste accumulation. Therefore, how to ensure minimal environmental impact while maintaining economic benefits is still a key issue that this technology needs to address.

In conclusion, although the recovery of high-value-added products from carbonated steel slag holds certain economic potential, challenges in technical feasibility, cost control, and environmental impact still exist. Future research needs more field validation and technological optimization to ensure the economic and sustainable viability of this method in industrialization.

Carbonated Steel Slag as a Cementing Material

Due to the similarity in composition between raw steel slag and cement, both containing a large amount of silicate minerals, untreated steel slag can be used as a raw material for cement clinker. However, due to the decomposition of calcium carbonate during calcination, the application of carbonated steel slag as a cement clinker material is relatively limited. Additionally, the hydration activity of steel slag as a clinker is low, but carbonation can enhance its activity by reducing the content of the RO phase.

Numerous studies have shown that carbonated steel slag can be used as a cement substitute material. For instance, cement prepared with 10 wt % carbonated BOFS achieved 98% of the compressive strength of ordinary Portland cement (OPC) after 28 days. Mo et al. investigated the effects of steel slag carbonation on the performance of cementing materials, including pore size, compressive strength, and CO2 absorption rate, and found that carbonation treatment reduced pore size (from 0.3 to 0.1 μm) and increased compressive strength to 44.1 MPa. Therefore, carbonation is an effective technique for improving the performance of steel slag as a cementing material. However, the practical application of carbonated steel slag as a cementing material is limited because of its low decomposition performance, making it difficult to form granular materials.

Carbonated Steel Slag as Aggregates

The application prospects of carbonated steel slag as aggregates are promising because the carbonated steel slag particles tend to agglomerate into larger particles. Carbonated steel slag can be used as a raw material for hydraulic materials to produce high-strength concrete. Mo et al. studied the effects of combining steel slag with aggregates on the performance of aggregate concrete, including bulk density, crush value, compressive strength, and CO2 absorption rate. They found that the bulk density of the aggregates could reach 1230–1300 kg/m3, the crush value could range from 15.68% to 22.48%, the compressive strength could reach 45.5 MPa, and the CO2 absorption rate could reach 10.5%. Nielsen et al. examined the changes in the microstructure and properties of the combination of steel slag and aggregates, including pore area, compressive strength, and CO2 absorption rate. They found that the combined microstructure was more compact with an increased total pore area, significantly improving the material’s frost resistance and durability. The compressive strength reached 47 MPa, and the CO2 absorption rate reached 10.6%. Therefore, the combination of carbonation reaction and aggregates is an effective method to enhance the performance of aggregate concrete.

Carbonated Steel Slag Concrete

Using carbonated steel slag to manufacture blocks in different ways is also a feasible application. Blocks, being common construction materials, are typically used in walls, foundations, and other structures. Since carbonated steel slag enhances its mechanical properties, it improves the compressive strength and stability of concrete blocks. Therefore, using carbonated steel slag in block production can make these blocks more durable and structurally secure. Portland cement and natural aggregates are replaced by steel slag (a mixture of EAFS and BOFS) and expanded lightweight blast furnace slag, and carbonated steel slag blocks are developed through accelerated carbonation. The results show that carbonated steel slag blocks possess good mechanical properties and durability. More importantly, the developed blocks are priced at only 85% of the cost of traditional concrete blocks. Additionally, aerated steel slag blocks (ASSB) were prepared by using aluminum powder as a foaming agent combined with accelerated carbonation. Due to the high-temperature stability of CaCO3, the prepared ASSB exhibits higher fire resistance.

Carbonated Steel Slag Self-Healing Concrete

Carbonated steel slag self-healing concrete is a new material with excellent performance and the ability to self-repair. The superior performance of carbonated steel slag self-healing concrete is mainly due to the synergistic effect of carbonation and hydration reactions. Its self-healing ability is particularly beneficial for buildings in coastal areas. Carbonated steel slag self-healing concrete exhibits excellent crack resistance, frost resistance, durability, and corrosion resistance. The self-healing is achieved by adding chemical or biological agents with healing effects to the cementitious materials, while the self-healing of concrete occurs through internal chemical reactions in the cement material that generate healing substances. Pang et al. studied the self-healing performance of concrete using carbonated basic oxygen furnace slag (BOFS) as aggregates and the unpeeled self-healing mechanism. Due to the high reactivity of steel slag and its ability to form carbonates, it provides an ideal material for self-healing concrete. Accelerated carbonated steel slag can provide sustainable repair capability in concrete, allowing structures to better resist crack propagation and water ingress during long-term use, thus improving the durability and structural integrity of the concrete.

Carbonated Steel Slag in Other Environmental Protection Materials

In addition to the common applications mentioned above, carbonated steel slag plays an important role in the preparation of other environmental protection materials. For example, carbonated steel slag can be used for soil improvement. In agriculture, the use of carbonated steel slag can improve the pH of the soil, enhance soil fertility, and promote plant growth. Due to the slow-release characteristics of the carbonates generated during the carbonation process, it provides long-lasting soil improvement effects in agricultural applications. For instance, Qi et al. found that carbonated BOFS could effectively promote the formation of humic acid (such as amino acids), sugars, and phenolic compounds under environmental temperatures, thereby accelerating the transformation of organic waste during composting.

Interestingly, carbonated steel slag can also be used as a wastewater treatment material. The calcium carbonate (CaCO3) generated during carbonation has strong adsorption properties, allowing it to effectively adsorb heavy metal ions, pollutants, and harmful substances in water. This gives it great potential for water treatment and wastewater management. Said et al. used an extraction reactor, aided by ammonium salt (NH4NO3, CH3COONH4, and NH4Cl) aqueous solutions, to selectively extract calcium from BOFS. After removing or recovering the remaining residues, the calcium-rich solution was reacted with CO2 in a carbonation reactor to form precipitated calcium carbonate (PCC). In addition, Lee et al. used a similar carbonation process to produce nano CaCO3, which was then commercialized as a high-value-added product. By modifying and optimizing the treatment process of carbonated steel slag, its performance in water treatment can be improved, further promoting its application in environmental remediation.

Therefore, when developing new methods for treating steel slag, the comprehensive impact of various influencing factors on the carbonation reaction should be considered. The enhanced performance of carbonated steel slag expands its applications in many fields. It can be used as a supplementary cementitious material (SCM) to produce various building products. More broadly, it can serve as aggregate for producing various construction materials, such as roadbeds, concrete slabs, concrete blocks, and self-healing concrete. Moreover, it can be used to stabilize/solidify contaminated soil or sediments. Another interesting application is that the precipitated calcium carbonate separated from carbonated steel slag can be commercialized as a value-added product to yield high economic returns.

Prospects of Life Cycle Environmental Benefit Assessment

The Life Cycle Environmental Benefit Assessment (LCEBA) of alkaline industrial solid waste (such as steel slag) is a comprehensive environmental evaluation method primarily used to analyze the positive and negative environmental impacts throughout its processes of generation, treatment, resource utilization, and final disposal. This method supports policy-making, the design of resource utilization schemes, and environmental optimization. However, the process may result in net CO2 emissions; therefore, assessing the environmental impact of the preparation process and its relationship with CO2 mineralization is of critical importance. To ensure industrial feasibility, environmental impacts must be quantified through a full Life Cycle Assessment (LCA). LCA can comprehensively identify and evaluate the environmental burdens associated with a product or process across its entire life cycle, including resource consumption, impacts on the natural environment, and potential risks to human health. According to ISO 14040 and ISO 14044 standards, LCA evaluates the environmental factors and potential impacts throughout a product’s life cycle, covering all stages from raw material acquisition, production, and use, to disposal and recycling.

From a research and development perspective, LCA is an important tool for product design improvement and innovation decision-making. By conducting an LCA evaluation of the CO2 sequestration potential of alkaline process solid waste, it has been found that global alkaline industrial solid waste mineralization can sequester 4.02 Gt of CO2 annually. Ma et al. conducted a systematic analysis of the costs associated with four wet CO2 mineralization schemes for blast furnace slag using the LCA method. The results showed that material costs and energy costs accounted for 59% to 70% and 10% to 20% of the total costs, respectively. Huang used LCA to comprehensively analyze 11 evaluation indicators, including Global Warming Potential (GWP), Acidification Potential (AP), and Eutrophication Potential (EP) for seven types of CO2 mineralized concrete blocks. The results indicated that the equivalent carbon emission for 1 m3 of wollastonite-based concrete block was 292 kg. By replacing steam curing with mineralization curing, more than 30% CO2 reduction could be achieved. Based on the environmental benefit analysis, optimization directions for mineralization curing technology in terms of formulation and process were proposed.

Global Warming Potential (GWP) is a dimensionless coefficient used to represent the contribution of a unit mass of greenhouse gas to the atmospheric greenhouse effect over a specific period, equivalent to the contribution of carbon dioxide under the same conditions. GWP is a key indicator for evaluating the environmental impact of producing carbonated materials. In the process of wet mineralization of fly ash (FA) to sequester 1.0 t of CO2, the electricity consumption is approximately 1,055 MJ. Assuming an average GWP of electricity of 0.5 and a CO2 mineralization capacity of FA of about 0.027 (CO2 equivalent), the GWP of the mineralization process is approximately 0.004. Since the GWP of the mineralization process is lower than the CO2 absorption, the process is considered carbon-negative (i.e., the CO2 sequestered is greater than the CO2 emitted). Ghasemi and others have reached similar conclusions. It is important to note that current GWP data are mainly based on laboratory results, lacking industrial-scale mineralization test data, and do not consider energy consumption during the transportation of coal-based solid waste. Therefore, the actual greenhouse gas emissions may be underestimated, and future research should consider these factors.

The analysis of environmental impact categories and indicators mainly covers several dimensions, including ecological environment, resource consumption, air quality, water quality, and waste management. In terms of the ecological environment, the impact of the steel slag carbonation process on soil, air, and water bodies is assessed, with a focus on CO2 emission reduction, potential heavy metal release, and waste disposal. Resource consumption includes energy use and raw material consumption, which directly affect production costs and resource sustainability. Air quality indicators primarily focus on carbon emissions, particulate matter emissions, and the control of gas pollutants. Regarding water quality, attention must be given to harmful substances in wastewater, especially ammonia nitrogen and heavy metals, which could cause pollution to water bodies. The feasibility of waste treatment and disposal must also be thoroughly analyzed to ensure that byproducts from carbonation can be effectively recycled or safely disposed of.

In the process stage analysis, the environmental impact of steel slag carbonation is generally divided into three stages: raw material preparation, the reaction process, and post-treatment of waste. In the raw material preparation stage, the focus is on the source of steel slag, its pretreatment, and its impact on resource consumption. The reaction process stage is the core of carbonation, involving CO2 capture, the efficiency of the mineralization reaction, and any pollutants that may be generated during the reaction. In the post-treatment stage, the disposal or recycling of byproducts is evaluated to minimize the environmental negative impact of waste. At the same time, the energy efficiency, carbon emission levels, and potential secondary pollution risks of the entire process should be thoroughly analyzed to optimize the process and reduce adverse effects.

Modified steel slag carbonation technology has significant environmental advantages. First, it effectively improves CO2 capture and mineralization efficiency, making it particularly suitable for utilizing industrial exhaust gases, thus helping to achieve carbon reduction goals. Additionally, through the resource utilization of steel slag, landfill use is reduced, heavy metal release is lowered, and the ecological burden is minimized. Moreover, the byproduct CaCO3 is stable and recyclable, further optimizing the overall environmental benefits and can substitute traditional building materials, reducing environmental damage caused by mineral extraction. However, the production and use of ammonium chloride come with higher costs and may cause secondary pollution issues, such as the potential hazard of NH4 + residues to water bodies. Therefore, waste liquid treatment and pollution control should be strictly monitored in the process design. Although the process is complex and initial investment is high, its potential environmental benefits and economic returns still make it highly promising for application.

By conducting a life cycle assessment (LCA) of the wet carbonation process and resource utilization of ammonium chloride-modified steel slag, the carbon emissions and energy consumption of the process can be analyzed. This not only helps identify the cost contributions of each process step to evaluate its economic feasibility but also provides a comprehensive understanding of each stage involved in the wet carbonation process (such as raw material acquisition, carbonation reaction, product generation, and sequestration). This clarifies the actual effect of the technology on greenhouse gas reduction. This process aids in optimizing each step of the carbonation process, ensuring that the technology delivers genuine positive environmental benefits.
