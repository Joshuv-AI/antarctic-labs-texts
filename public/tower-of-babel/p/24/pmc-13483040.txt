Mathematical modeling of the population-level impact of hypothetical herpes simplex virus type 2 curative therapy
Authors: Houssein H Ayoub, Keith R Jerome, Hiam Chemaitelly, Anna Wald, Laith J Abu-Raddad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHerpes simplex virus type 2 (HSV-2) infection is a prevalent lifelong infection and the leading cause of genital ulcer disease (GUD), yet no vaccines or curative interventions are currently available. Emerging curative approaches have demonstrated proof-of-concept efficacy in preclinical studies, but their potential population-level impact remains unknown. This study assessed the potential impact of curative therapy on HSV-2 disease burden and transmission.MethodsA dynamic HSV-2 transmission model was developed for the United States and calibrated to prevalence estimates from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys. Curative therapy was assumed to be introduced in 2030 and scaled to 20%, 50%, or 80% coverage by 2040 under two scenarios: (i) treatment of symptomatic individuals only and (ii) treatment of all individuals with HSV-2 infection.ResultsHere we show that, by 2050, at 80% coverage, curative therapy for symptomatic individuals reduces the number of 15–49-year-olds with GUD and GUD days by 86.7% and 86.2%, respectively, and decreases the incidence rate and annual new infections by 54.0% and 51.7%, averting 5.4 million cumulative infections. The numbers needed to treat to avert one prevalent or incident symptomatic HSV-2 infection, one incident HSV-2 infection, and one year of GUD days are 0.90, 3.4, and 0.61, respectively.ConclusionsCurative therapy could substantially reduce HSV-2 incidence and recurrent GUD, yielding population-level benefits beyond individual cure.Subject terms: Viral infection, Drug development, Epidemiology

Introduction

Herpes simplex virus type 2 (HSV-2) infection is a lifelong sexually transmitted infection (STI) that affects over half a billion people globally and imposes a substantial burden of morbidity and economic costs1–3. Most infections are asymptomatic or unrecognized, yet the infection is characterized by frequent subclinical viral shedding and, in some individuals, recurrent symptoms4,5. HSV-2 is the leading cause of genital herpes and the main driver of genital ulcer disease (GUD) worldwide6–11. Perinatal transmission, though rare, can result in neonatal herpes with high mortality and long-term morbidity12–14. The infection is strongly linked to HIV infection and is thought to have amplified its spread15–17. Beyond direct clinical outcomes, HSV-2 infection imposes social and psychological burdens, adversely affecting relationships, quality of life, and mental health18–21.

Despite decades of research, no preventive or therapeutic HSV vaccine exists22,23. The virus’s ability to establish latency and evade immune responses has undermined past vaccine candidates22,23. Current management relies on antiviral therapy, which suppresses viral replication, alleviates symptoms, and reduces transmission but does not eradicate latent infection4,24,25. The World Health Organization (WHO) has emphasized the urgent need for new tools, including vaccines and therapies, to reduce HSV-related morbidity and transmission26–28.

HSV (both HSV-1 and HSV-2) establishes lifelong infection by retreating into sensory neurons, where it persists in a latent state shielded from immune responses5,29. This biology has fueled efforts to develop curative strategies that go beyond suppression to achieve permanent viral clearance or functional silencing30–35. Current approaches focus either on eradicating latent virus from neurons or enforcing deep latency to prevent reactivation and transmission30–35.

Gene-editing platforms such as CRISPR/Cas9 and HSV-specific meganucleases, often delivered via adeno-associated virus vectors, have demonstrated proof-of-concept efficacy in animal models, eliminating up to 90–97% of latent virus and markedly reducing recurrences31,32. Parallel research on epigenetic modulators aims to “block and lock” viral genomes, rendering infection clinically irrelevant even if persistence remains33–35. Together, these advances represent a paradigm shift, with the potential to transform HSV-2 infection from a lifelong burden into a condition that can be cured outright or functionally neutralized.

Against this background, this study evaluated the potential population-level impact of curative HSV-2 therapy on infection and disease outcomes by modeling HSV-2 transmission dynamics under a range of curative therapy scenarios. The analysis was designed to generate an evidence base to inform strategic investment in curative approaches, guiding prioritization based on predicted population-level impact and supporting efforts to accelerate, de-risk, and advance these interventions toward clinical translation and large-scale implementation. The analysis focused on the population of the United States (U.S.), where population-based, sex- and age-stratified HSV-2 prevalence data have been collected for decades through the National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys (NHANES)36–38, providing a robust empirical foundation for model development and evaluation of intervention impact.

A key concept underlying this study is that treating individuals with the infection generates population-level benefits beyond direct clinical gains. For symptomatic individuals, cure eliminates both disease and the ability to transmit HSV-2, thereby indirectly preventing additional infections and removing the need for preventive measures in HSV-2–discordant partnerships, such as condom use or antiviral suppressive therapy. For asymptomatic or unrecognized infections, cure also halts infectiousness, reducing onward transmission. Moreover, curing HSV-2 may reduce the risk of acquiring or transmitting HIV. Thus, the availability of curative therapy represents not only a medical intervention for those affected by the disease but also a population-level strategy that could bring HSV-2 closer to elimination under the principle of treatment as prevention, as has been established for HIV39,40 and hepatitis C virus41,42.

The model-based analysis demonstrates that curative therapy substantially reduces HSV-2 transmission, incidence, and GUD burden at the population level, with effects emerging rapidly after introduction and increasing over time. At high coverage, curative therapy markedly lowers both new infections and recurrent disease, averting millions of infections and most GUD episodes. Programmatic effectiveness is high, with only a few treatments required to avert a single symptomatic or any infection. These findings indicate that curative therapy provides both direct clinical benefits and strong indirect effects through reduced onward transmission.

Methods

Mathematical model

A population-based deterministic compartmental model was developed to characterize HSV-2 transmission dynamics under scenarios with curative therapy. The model builds on earlier HSV-2 modeling frameworks43–47 and is formulated as a system of coupled nonlinear differential equations. The population is stratified by HSV-2 infection status, symptom presentation, stage of infection, sex, age group, and sexual risk group. A schematic of the model structure is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1 of the Supplementary Material, with full model equations and description provided in Supplementary Section S1 and Supplementary Table S1.

The natural history of HSV-2 infection was represented by three stages: primary infection, latent infection, and reactivation. Each stage was stratified by the presence or absence of symptoms (Supplementary Fig. S1). Symptomatic disease was defined as HSV-2–attributable GUD, characterized by vesicles, ulcers, or other genital lesions1,48.

The period after primary infection was divided into three intervals—<1 year, 1–9 years, and ≥10 years—to capture temporal variation in HSV-2 shedding and symptomatic recurrence49 (Supplementary Fig. S1). Individuals in latency underwent episodic reactivations, either symptomatic or asymptomatic, during which they shed virus and were infectious43,50.

The population was stratified into 20 five-year age groups, each further divided into five sexual risk groups ranging from low to high. Variation in sexual risk across groups was modeled using a power-law function informed by partnership data51 and network modeling studies52–54, with risk behavior varying also by sex, age, and calendar time.

Sexual partner mixing across age and risk groups was represented through matrices that incorporated both assortative mixing (partners preferentially selecting those within their own age or risk group) and proportionate mixing (no preferential bias in partner selection)55. Description of partnership change rates by age and risk, as well as the method used to balance partnerships between women and men55, are provided in Supplementary Section S1.

Curative therapy was assumed not to induce behavioral disinhibition. Although behavioral disinhibition has been hypothesized in the context of STI treatment or prevention, empirical evidence at the wider population level generally does not support the occurrence of appreciable or sustained risk compensation effects56,57.

The force of infection was determined by sexual partner change rates, HSV-2 transmission probability per sexual act and per partnership, and mixing patterns across age and risk groups. The model was implemented, calibrated, and analyzed using MATLAB R2019a (The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA).

Data sources and model parameters

Model parameters were informed by current knowledge of HSV-2 natural history, epidemiology, and sexual behavior (Supplementary Sections S1 and S2). Parameters related to HSV-2 natural history were updated through a targeted literature review to incorporate new evidence that has emerged since the development of earlier modeling studies1,43–46,48. Supplementary Table S2 summarizes parameter values, sources, and justifications.

Demographic inputs, including age- and sex-specific population sizes, historical trends, and future projections, were obtained from the United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs Population Division database58.

Age- and sex-stratified HSV-2 prevalence estimates were derived from ten biennial rounds of the nationally representative NHANES surveys conducted between 1988 and 201636–38. The 1976–1980 survey was excluded due to procedural differences and previously reported limitations in measurement45.

Each survey round used standardized methodologies for analytical procedures and laboratory testing36–38. Data on demographics, number of sexual partnerships in the past 12 months, age-specific partner distributions, and HSV-2 serostatus were extracted, merged across rounds, and analyzed following NHANES guidelines38. All estimates were weighted using NHANES sampling weights to ensure representativeness of the U.S. population.

Model calibration

The demographic component of the model was first calibrated to reproduce the sex-specific and age-specific demographic projections for the U.S., as provided by the Population Division of the United Nations58. Accordingly, the model simulations capture dynamic changes in population age structure, including the size and composition of sexually active and reproductive-age groups, as well as broader demographic processes such as cohort replacement, population aging, and long-term demographic shifts.

The epidemiological component was then fitted to NHANES time-series, sex-specific, and age-specific HSV-2 prevalence data from 1988 to 201638. This calibration enables inference of transmission dynamics over a longer time horizon because prevalence reflects the cumulative history of past incidence, while current incidence shapes future prevalence. By reproducing observed prevalence patterns across age, sex, and calendar time, the model’s bio-behavioral parameters are constrained to capture the integrated effects of HSV-2 transmission both before and beyond the NHANES observation period38. When combined with demographic reconstruction and forward population projections, this approach yields a coherent representation of HSV-2 transmission dynamics spanning several decades, from the pre-NHANES era into the future38.

The model therefore captures the post–World War II rise in HSV-2 prevalence, with accelerated growth after 1960 associated with the “sexual revolution” and the introduction of oral contraception45,59–61, followed by a marked decline beginning in the mid-1980s after the emergence of HIV/AIDS45,61,62. This decline, reflecting changes in population-level sexual behavior61,63, has since stabilized into a slow, gradual downward trajectory that is projected to persist through 2050 (Supplementary Fig. S2)45.

Model fitting was performed using a nonlinear least-squares approach, employing the Nelder-Mead simplex algorithm64, in accordance with established methodology45,65.

Epidemiologic measures of curative therapy impact

The impact of HSV-2 curative therapy was evaluated using several epidemiologic measures. For GUD, two outcomes were assessed: (1) the number of individuals experiencing at least one HSV-2–attributable episode within a year, representing those with recurrent GUD in that year1, and (2) the total annual number of person-days with HSV-2–attributable GUD—hereafter referred to as GUD days—calculated as the cumulative number of days lived with GUD across all individuals in the population. Further methodological details are provided in Supplementary Section S3.

The model also estimated HSV-2 incidence rate (new infections per 1,000 susceptible individuals per year) and incidence (annual number of new infections). The number of infections averted was defined as the difference in cumulative new infections between scenarios with and without curative therapy.

Programmatic effectiveness measures of curative therapy

Several effectiveness measures were calculated, defined as the ratio of the total number of treatments to the number of specific epidemiologic outcomes averted over the study horizon. These measures serve as proxies for cost-effectiveness in the absence of cost data66,67, as the per-treatment cost of curative therapy has not yet been established. From a programmatic perspective, they represent the number of treatments required to prevent a given outcome from program initiation through a specified year.

Effectiveness measures included: (1) the number needed to treat (NNT) to avert one prevalent or incident symptomatic infection, (2) the NNT to avert one new incident infection regardless of symptoms, and (3) the NNT to avert one GUD person-year, defined as 365.25 GUD days in the population.

The number of symptomatic infections averted was calculated as the sum of treated symptomatic prevalent cases and incident symptomatic infections averted. The number of GUD person-years averted was defined as the difference in cumulative GUD person-years between scenarios with and without curative therapy.

It is important to note that interpretation of NNT estimates requires nuance, as their definition depends on the type of infection treated (symptomatic or any) and the type of infection averted (incident, prevalent, symptomatic, or any). Treating a symptomatic infection automatically averts a prevalent symptomatic case and, by also preventing transmission, results in an NNT below one. Because roughly one in three HSV-2 infections is symptomatic, treating all infections would avert one symptomatic case for every three treated. Furthermore, each symptomatic infection involves an average of approximately 70 symptomatic days per year, or about 6 GUD person-years over a 30-year infection duration. Treatment at the onset of infection would avert all 6 years, but in the modeled scenarios, treatment typically occurs later—once available—thereby averting fewer GUD person-years.

Treatment program scenarios

Curative therapy was assumed to be introduced in the U.S. population in 2030 and scaled up at a constant rate to reach coverage levels of 20%, 50%, and 80% among eligible individuals aged ≥15 years by 2040, with the treatment rate sustained thereafter. Scale-up was modeled as a uniform probability, such that each eligible individual had an equal fixed chance of receiving treatment at any time. Treatment was assumed to be successful in all individuals; however, treated individuals were not assumed to have protective immunity and therefore remained susceptible to reinfection upon subsequent exposure.

At any time t, treatment coverage was defined as the proportion of eligible individuals already treated, calculated as the number of treated individuals alive at t divided by the sum of treated individuals alive at t and untreated but eligible individuals at t. Individuals treated more than once—for the first infection and for reinfections—were counted by the number of treatments received.

Two program scenarios were assessed. In the first, treatment was restricted to individuals with symptomatic HSV-2 infection; in the second, it was offered to all individuals with HSV-2 infection regardless of symptoms. Outcomes were projected under each of the three coverage targets (20%, 50%, 80%), yielding results for six program–coverage combinations. Although treating individuals with the infection regardless of symptoms is ambitious and likely impractical, this scenario was modeled to reflect the possibility that curative therapy could incentivize individuals to ascertain their infection status, thereby enabling treatment and cure of many asymptomatic infections.

Although curative therapy programs were modeled for the population aged ≥15 years, results are presented primarily for individuals aged 15–49 years, the conventional group for assessing epidemiologic measures and intervention impact for STIs1,66,68. The relevant age group is specified with each result to ensure clarity.

Uncertainty analysis

A multivariable uncertainty analysis was conducted using Monte Carlo simulations to quantify variability around key estimates, following established methods45,65. A total of 1,000 model runs were performed, each sampling HSV-2 prevalence from the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of NHANES data and model parameters from predefined uncertainty ranges using triangular distributions with ±30% variation around point estimates (Supplementary Table S2). This range was not intended as a formal probabilistic characterization of parameter uncertainty, but rather as a structured uncertainty envelope in settings where empirical confidence intervals are not consistently available across parameters69. The selected range reflects moderate uncertainty while avoiding excessive dispersion that could compromise model identifiability or calibration stability.

Parameter sampling was constrained within biologically plausible bounds (e.g., proportions restricted to the [0,1] interval). Although this approach to uncertainty analysis does not capture all sources of structural and parametric uncertainty and is not a substitute for fully data-driven distributions69, it represents a pragmatic and commonly adopted strategy in transmission dynamic modeling when parameter-specific uncertainty bounds are unavailable45,65,69–71. Its primary purpose is to evaluate the robustness of model conclusions under plausible parameter variation, rather than to generate formal statistical confidence measures.

For each run, the model was recalibrated to NHANES prevalence data to ensure consistency with observed epidemiological patterns45. The impact of curative therapy was assessed using relative measures comparing intervention scenarios with a counterfactual no-intervention scenario, such that the estimates reflect differences between internally consistent, data-informed model realizations. They are therefore less sensitive to uncertainty in individual parameter values, as the same parameter sets are applied across both scenarios, and shared sources of uncertainty are largely neutralized when contrasts are evaluated. Outcomes were aggregated across simulations to generate 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs).

Sensitivity analysis

The model assumed that sexual behavior was unaffected by the presence or absence of symptoms. To assess the impact of varying this assumption, a sensitivity analysis was conducted in which symptomatic individuals were assumed to reduce their frequency of coital acts by 25% and 50% during symptomatic periods, relative to asymptomatic individuals. The model was refitted to the data under these assumptions, and intervention outcomes were re-estimated.

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with established ethical guidelines and principles. Since all data were obtained from publicly available sources, no data collection involving human participants was undertaken. As a result, formal ethics approval was not required for this research.

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the article. HHA and LJA had full access to all the data in the study and had the final responsibility for the decision to submit for publication.

Results

Model calibration

Model calibration is presented in Supplementary Figs S2–S5 and includes fits to the age-specific distribution of HSV-2 prevalence among women (Supplementary Fig. S3) and men (Supplementary Fig. S4), the temporal evolution of HSV-2 prevalence by sex (Supplementary Fig. S2A) and for both sexes combined (Supplementary Fig. S2B), and United States demographic trends (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Impact of curative therapy in symptomatically infected individuals

Fig. 1 shows the projected impact of curative therapy on GUD outcomes when treatment was introduced in 2030 and restricted to symptomatic HSV-2 infections among individuals aged ≥15 years, with coverage scaled up to 20%, 50%, and 80% by 2040. By 2050, the cumulative number of treatments ranged from 6.5 million at 20% coverage to 18.3 million at 80% coverage (Supplementary Fig. S6A), reflecting both initial treatments and retreatments following reinfection after cure.

Curative therapy is assumed to be introduced in 2030 and scaled up at a constant rate to reach 20%, 50%, and 80% coverage by 2040, with the treatment rate sustained thereafter.

Introduction of curative therapy led to substantial reductions in GUD outcomes compared with the no-therapy scenario. By 2050, the 20% coverage scenario reduced the number of individuals aged 15–49 years experiencing at least one GUD episode within a year (Fig. 1A) and the total annual number of GUD days (Fig. 1B) by 28.9% and 28.6%, respectively. At 80% coverage, reductions increased to 86.7% and 86.2%.

Fig. 2 shows the corresponding impact on HSV-2 infection outcomes. By 2050, incidence rate (Fig. 2A) and annual new infections (Fig. 2B) among individuals aged 15–49 years declined by 15.6% and 14.3% under 20% coverage, and by 54.0% and 51.7% under 80% coverage. The cumulative number of infections averted among those aged ≥15 years ranged from 1.2 million at 20% coverage to 5.4 million at 80% coverage (Fig. 2C).

By 2050, the cumulative number of treatments ranged from 19.2 million at 20% coverage to 51.2 million at 80% coverage (Supplementary Fig. S6B), reflecting both initial treatments and retreatments following reinfection after cure.

Programmatic effectiveness measures of curative therapy

Fig. 5 presents the estimated programmatic effectiveness of curative therapy. When treatment was restricted to symptomatically infected individuals, the NNT to avert one symptomatic infection (prevalent or incident) was 0.93, 0.92, and 0.90 at 20%, 50%, and 80% coverage, respectively (Fig. 5A). In contrast, when individuals with HSV-2 infection were treated regardless of symptoms, the corresponding NNTs were 2.7, 2.6, and 2.5.

Programmatic effectiveness measures of curative therapy under two program scenarios.

(i) treatment restricted to symptomatic individuals and (ii) treatment of all infected individuals. Estimated outcomes include (A) the number needed to treat (NNT) to avert one symptomatic infection (prevalent or incident), (B) the NNT to avert one new infection regardless of symptoms, and (C) the NNT to avert one GUD person-year, defined as 365.25 days of GUD in the population. Curative therapy is assumed to be introduced in 2030 and scaled up at a constant rate to reach 20%, 50%, and 80% coverage by 2040, with the treatment rate sustained thereafter.

For averting one new (incident) infection regardless of symptoms, the NNT was 5.3, 4.3, and 3.4 at 20%, 50%, and 80% coverage, respectively, when treatment was restricted to symptomatic individuals (Fig. 5B). In contrast, when individuals with HSV-2 infection were treated regardless of symptoms, the corresponding NNTs were 8.3, 6.4, and 4.9.

For averting one GUD person-year, the NNT was 0.79, 0.70, and 0.61 at 20%, 50%, and 80% coverage, respectively, when treatment was restricted to symptomatic individuals (Fig. 5C). In contrast, when individuals with HSV-2 infection were treated regardless of symptoms, the corresponding NNTs were 2.3, 2.0, and 1.7.

Uncertainty and sensitivity analyses

The uncertainty analysis, incorporating variability from the 95% CIs in NHANES prevalence estimates and uncertainty in model parameters, produced relatively narrow 95% UIs (Supplementary Fig. S7), affirming the robustness of the study findings. The UIs were particularly narrow for outcomes related to symptomatic infections, which are directly affected by curative therapy, but wider for outcomes reflecting overall infection, as these are influenced by indirect effects through transmission dynamics.

Similarly, a sensitivity analysis assuming symptomatic individuals reduced their frequency of coital acts by 25% or 50% during symptomatic periods yielded results consistent with the main analysis (Supplementary Fig. S8), further supporting the validity of the conclusions.

Discussion

The modeling results indicate that curative therapy can markedly reduce HSV-2 transmission, incidence, and prevalence, offering a population-level strategy to move closer to elimination under the principle of treatment as prevention. Benefits would materialize within a few years of implementation and expand over time, with reductions in epidemiologic outcomes exceeding treatment coverage levels due to indirect effects on transmission. With few treatments required to avert a symptomatic or any infection, curative therapy may have the potential to be cost-effective or even cost-saving. These findings emphasize the urgency of investing in the development of curative therapies and integrating them into public health strategies to reduce the global burden of HSV-2 infection.

A key finding is that intervention coverage strongly influences programmatic effectiveness, with greater effectiveness achieved at higher coverage across all measures (Fig. 5). At 20% coverage, 5.3 and 8.3 treatments were needed to avert one new infection in the symptomatic-only and all-infected scenarios, respectively, compared with only 3.4 and 4.9 treatments at 80% coverage. Notably, the 80% coverage scenarios required less than threefold more treatments than the 20% coverage scenarios, despite coverage being fourfold higher (Supplementary Fig. S6). These results highlight the importance of rapid scale-up of curative therapy, once available, to maximize population-level benefits and cost-effectiveness.

This pattern differs from prophylactic vaccination in the general population, where effectiveness is less dependent on coverage—that is, the number of vaccinations needed to avert one infection does not typically change substantially with increasing coverage46,66–68,72. This is because vaccination is administered to individuals with varying levels of exposure risk, including some who may never be exposed, whereas treatment targets those capable of transmitting the infection and therefore more directly interrupts onward transmission. Furthermore, since cured individuals remain susceptible to reinfection, reinfections are relatively common at low coverage but decline markedly at high coverage, as fewer high-risk individuals with HSV-2 infection remain to sustain transmission.

The study further indicates that, across all coverage levels, curative therapy targeting symptomatic infections requires fewer treatments to avert each HSV-2 outcome than strategies treating all infected individuals (Fig. 5). This greater effectiveness reflects, in part, the higher infectiousness of symptomatic cases, who shed virus at approximately twice the rate of asymptomatic individuals (Supplementary Table S2). By contrast, treating all infections, although less effective per treatment, yields a substantially greater population-level impact by addressing the full reservoir of infection, including asymptomatic individuals who sustain transmission.

These findings highlight a trade-off between effectiveness and overall impact. Targeting symptomatic individuals is more cost-effective, particularly in resource-constrained settings or where treatment costs are high. In contrast, broader treatment strategies are preferable when the objective is to prevent transmission to sexual partners, reduce population-level transmission, and ultimately achieve infection elimination, especially if treatment costs decline over time.

The results demonstrate a substantial impact of curative therapy, because it targets individuals with HSV-2 infection who can transmit the virus and who, on average, engage in higher-risk sexual behavior than uninfected individuals, thereby contributing disproportionately to HSV-2 transmission dynamics45,73. The estimated impact is likely conservative, as the analyses assumed that cured individuals are as susceptible to reinfection as infection-naïve individuals. This assumption may not hold, since immunity may reduce susceptibility74, and approaches such as gene editing may confer long-lasting effects in ganglionic neurons, further lowering reinfection risk. Consequently, the true population-level impact of curative therapy could be greater than that estimated under the conservative assumption of equal susceptibility to reinfection.

Moreover, the analyses quantified only infection and GUD outcomes, whereas curative therapy would also be expected to prevent other clinically important sequelae, including neurological complications, adverse reproductive and perinatal outcomes, and, downstream, HIV infection. The availability of a curative therapy may also help alleviate the stigma surrounding HSV-2 infection and mitigate associated psychosocial harms.

The study did not account for potential behavioral responses to the availability of curative therapy. Although behavioral disinhibition has been hypothesized in the context of STI treatment or prevention, empirical evidence at the population level generally does not support substantial or sustained risk compensation effects56,57. Nonetheless, the perception of reduced risk could influence sexual behavior among some individuals, which, if present, could attenuate the projected population-level impact of curative therapy.

This study has limitations. Suppressive antiviral therapy was not explicitly modeled as a separate intervention. However, its effects are largely incorporated through calibration to population-based HSV-2 prevalence data, which capture the cumulative influence of factors shaping transmission dynamics over several decades, including the real-world use of suppressive therapy alongside behavioral changes. Accordingly, the counterfactual scenario without curative therapy reflects an epidemiological context in which suppressive therapy is already in use.

Importantly, over the past three decades, suppressive therapy has had a limited impact at the population level, owing to its relatively low uptake in routine practice and its only partial ( ~ 50%) effectiveness in reducing transmission4,21,75. Consequently, its contribution to overall transmission dynamics remains constrained21. As such, the absence of an explicit suppressive therapy compartment is not likely to materially bias the projected trends. In contrast, curative therapy eliminates infection and infectiousness, thereby obviating the need for ongoing suppressive therapy and generating additional downstream benefits through the avoidance of long-term treatment and associated costs.

The model assumed no change in sexual behavior by infection status, though individuals may reduce activity during symptomatic periods. However, sensitivity analysis reducing coital frequency among symptomatic individuals by up to 50% showed limited effect on estimated effectiveness (Supplementary Fig. S8), supporting the robustness of the results.

In the all-infected treatment scenario, primary asymptomatic or unrecognized infections were assumed eligible for treatment for consistency with other scenarios, but such cases are rarely identified before seroconversion. Nonetheless, given the short duration of primary infection, treatment during this phase is unlikely, even if included in the model, making this assumption inconsequential for the study results.

Future projections were calibrated to historical and contemporary data, but underlying trends may shift with changes in sexual behavior, healthcare access, or STI prevention strategies. Parameterization relied on robust cohort data on HSV-2 natural history4,49,76–81, but follow-up durations were relatively short.

As clinical efficacy data for curative therapy are not yet available, but proof-of-concept studies in animal models suggest cure rates exceeding 90%31,32, the analysis assumed that all treated persons achieved successful cure. If real-world efficacy were ultimately lower, the projected population-level impact would remain quantitatively and qualitatively similar, but with interpretation shifting from treatments administered to successful cures achieved.

The model did not consider potential interactions between HSV-2 and HSV-1 epidemiology. Although some evidence suggests partial cross-protection between the two infections82–84, the extent and population-level relevance of this effect remain uncertain. Available evidence is more consistent with a stronger population-level influence of HSV-1 on HSV-2 than the reverse, in part because HSV-1 is typically acquired earlier in life, often during childhood, before exposure to HSV-211,45,65. In principle, large-scale reduction or elimination of HSV-2 may alter susceptibility to HSV-1 acquisition, including an increase in genital HSV-1 infection, and thereby indirectly influence HSV-1 epidemiology.

Finally, this analysis focused on epidemiological outcomes and did not account for implementation dimensions of curative therapy, including the public health infrastructure required for delivery and the screening challenges associated with identifying and treating individuals, particularly those with asymptomatic infection. Future studies should assess the costs, feasibility, and operational requirements of screening, treatment delivery, and clinical care, and incorporate these elements into comprehensive public health and economic evaluations once reliable clinical, programmatic, and cost data become available.

This study has strengths. It evaluated different program scenarios and coverage levels, providing robust and policy-relevant insights to inform curative therapy development, program design, and implementation. A dynamic mathematical model was employed, capturing non-linear transmission dynamics, sexual contact structures, and the biological features of HSV-2 infection, while generating population-wide estimates. The analysis drew on standardized, population-based NHANES data, offering reliable, nationally representative estimates over several decades36–38. The model was rigorously parameterized with high-quality data on HSV-2 natural history and population demographics, stratified by sex, age, and sexual risk behavior, ensuring a realistic representation of HSV-2 epidemiology. Together, these strengths enabled the first, to our knowledge, comprehensive assessment of how curative therapy could reshape both HSV-2 transmission and the burden of recurrent GUD in the U.S. population.

Conclusion

Curative therapy could substantially reduce HSV-2 transmission, incidence, and recurrent GUD within two decades of introduction, even at moderate coverage. By directly targeting individuals with HSV-2 infection and capable of transmitting the virus, such therapy offers benefits that extend beyond individual cure to population-level control. These findings underscore the urgency of advancing curative therapies through development, licensure, and scale-up, and highlight their potential role as a cornerstone of future strategies to reduce the health, psychosocial, and economic burden of HSV-2 infection.
