Postoperative Ketorolac Use and the Odds of a Nonunion in Operatively Treated Scaphoid Fractures
Authors: Alexander D. Jeffs, Océane Mauffrey, Nicholas C. Bank, Stephen J. Perle, Sonja A. Samant, Stephen M. Himmelberg, Gregory M. Knoll, J. Megan M. Patterson, Reid W. Draeger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeKetorolac is a potent nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug that is beneficial in the ambulatory hand surgery setting. Reluctance exists in the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, such as ketorolac, after operative treatment of fractures at high risk for nonunion, such as scaphoid fractures. We hypothesized that postoperative ketorolac administration within 5 days of operative treatment for a scaphoid fracture would coincide with a higher rate of nonunion than no postoperative ketorolac administration.MethodsA retrospective cohort was obtained from the TriNetX database. Patients ≥18 years old who underwent operative treatment of a scaphoid fracture from 2013 to 2023 were included. Patients with open fractures, prior nonunion, and infection were excluded. Cohorts were defined based on postoperative ketorolac use within 5 days of surgery. After propensity score matching, nonunion and nonunion revision surgery rates were determined and compared with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. Statistical significance was reported as an alpha error of < .05.ResultsA total of 2,063 matched patients (1,044 ketorolac; 1,019 no ketorolac) were compared. No considerable differences in nonunion rate and nonunion revision surgery rate were observed between the ketorolac and no ketorolac groups (4.3% vs 3.9%; OR, 1.102 [95% CI, 0.714–1.703] and 1.0% vs 1.5%; OR, 0.655 [95% CI, 0.302–1.417]).ConclusionsPostoperative ketorolac use within 5 days of operative treatment does not considerably increase the rate of nonunion or nonunion revision surgery in operatively treated scaphoid fractures.Type of study/level of evidenceTherapeutic III

Opioid medications are a common pain management modality in hand surgery. In recent years, the United States opioid epidemic has shed light on the potentially devastating consequences of opioid use following even short-term prescriptions.1,2 In this context, multimodal pain control with modalities such as gabapentin, acetaminophen, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and regional nerve blocks has gained prominence to control postoperative pain, while decreasing the use of opioids.3,4 NSAIDs have been found to be an effective analgesic for pain.5,6 Specifically, the use of NSAIDs alone has been found to reduce the amount of opioid medication needed for postoperative breakthrough pain.3,5,7 The NSAID ketorolac is a potent short-term antipyretic and analgesic that nonselectively inhibits prostaglandin synthesis by blocking cyclooxygenase-1 and -2 activity. Unlike other NSAIDs, ketorolac has the benefit of intravenous (IV) administration and can be given in the immediate postoperative period when oral administration is not possible because of nausea, vomiting, unconsciousness, or dysphagia. This is particularly beneficial in the ambulatory surgery setting prior to discharge.

Despite these benefits, some reluctance exists in the prescription of NSAIDs, such as IV ketorolac, for immediate postoperative pain management because of their potential impairment of bone healing.8, 9, 10 The data on the impact of NSAIDs on bone healing following surgical fixation of orthopedic injuries are mixed. In particular, although high-level data support oral or IV NSAID use after soft-tissue-only hand surgery, there is a paucity of evidence regarding their effects in the setting of upper-extremity fractures—and less in operatively treated hand or carpal fractures.6,8,11, 12, 13 Understanding the potential impact of immediate NSAID administration on upper-extremity fractures at high risk for nonunion following fixation is therefore important. Scaphoid fractures represent the most common site of nonunion in the hand and upper extremity.14, 15, 16 Specifically, in operatively treated fractures, the nonunion rate ranges from 4% to 25%.17,18 However, the impact of early NSAID administration on nonunion rate following scaphoid fracture fixation remains largely unknown.

The present study aimed to determine the rate of scaphoid fracture nonunion following operative treatment with and without postoperative ketorolac administration in the TriNetX database. We hypothesized that postoperative ketorolac administration within 5 days of operative treatment for a scaphoid fracture in adult patients would coincide with a higher rate of nonunion than no postoperative ketorolac administration.

Materials and Methods

Study population

The TriNetX Research Network database was queried on April 7, 2025. TriNetX is a global federated health research network containing deidentified electronic medical record data of approximately 140 million patients from 105 health care organizations. For this study, patient cohorts and outcome measures were defined using the International Classification of Disease, 10th Edition (ICD-10) diagnosis codes and Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes. The database was retrospectively queried for patients who underwent primary scaphoid fracture repair between 2013 and 2023 (CPT 25628). Patients were included in the study if they were at least 18 years old. Patients with open fractures, postoperative infections, prior scaphoid nonunion, and follow-up less than 2 years were excluded. Patients that met inclusion criteria were then divided into two cohorts based on usage of ketorolac (RXNORM:35827) within 5 days after surgery. The index event was defined as the date of operative treatment and marked the beginning of the follow-up period. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines to ensure methodological rigor and transparency.19 All patient data were deidentified in compliance with Section 164.5154(a) of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act Privacy Rule, and the study was therefore exempt from institutional review board approval.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of interest were diagnosis of scaphoid nonunion as defined by ICD-10 diagnosis codes (ICD-10: S62.001-002, S62.009, S62.011-016, S62.021-026, S62.031-036) and scaphoid nonunion revision surgery defined by CPT code: 25440. They were assessed beginning 90 days after operative treatment.

Statistical analysis

The postoperative ketorolac group and no postoperative ketorolac group were propensity score matched 1:1 to account for demographics, including age, biologic sex at birth, ethnicity, and race, as well as comorbidities, such as nicotine dependence (ICD-10: F17), tobacco use (ICD-10: Z72.0), cannabis related disorders (ICD-10: F12), chronic kidney disease (ICD-10: N18), osteoporosis (ICD-10: M81.0), vitamin D deficiency (ICD-10: E55), body mass index, and hemoglobin A1c levels. A power analysis was completed and assumed two independent study groups with an expected difference in the nonunion rate between the groups of 5%.18 We estimated a sample size of at least 686 patients per group to achieve 80% power. Comparisons of continuous and categorical variables between the postoperative ketorolac group and the no postoperative ketorolac group were performed using two-tailed independent t tests and χ2 tests, respectively. Outcome frequencies between cohorts were compared using odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. Statistical significance was reported as an alpha error of < .05.

Results

Demographics

From 2013 to 2023 there were 4,979 patients in the TriNetX Research Network database who underwent operative treatment of a scaphoid fracture. Of this group, 1,219 (24.5%) of them received ketorolac within 5 days after surgery. Before propensity matching, there were notable differences in sex, nicotine dependence, body mass index, bone mineral density and vitamin D deficiency between patients who did and did not receive postoperative ketorolac. After matching, both cohorts had 1,201 patients, and there were no considerable differences in demographic or comorbidities (Table 1). Nonunion rate

In the postoperative ketorolac group, the nonunion rate was 4.3% (45 of 1,044) compared with 3.9% (40 of 1,019) in the no postoperative ketorolac group. Nonunion revision surgery rate

In the postoperative ketorolac group, the nonunion revision surgery rate was 1.0% (11 of 1,044) compared with 1.5% (16 of 1,019) in the no postoperative ketorolac group. There was no considerable difference in the odds of nonunion revision surgery between the postoperative ketorolac and no postoperative ketorolac group (OR, 0.655; 95% CI, 0.302–1.417) (Table 2).

Discussion

In recent years, multimodal pain control has become an increasingly important aspect of orthopedic practice, as opioid-sparing pain regimens gain prominence amid the national opioid epidemic. Though NSAIDs, such as ketorolac, have become a critical component of postoperative pain management, concerns remain regarding their effects on bone healing. This study used a multicenter database to assess the potential impact of ketorolac use in the 5-day postoperative period on rates of nonunion diagnosis and nonunion revision surgery in operatively treated scaphoid fractures. In this study, patients with operatively treated scaphoid fractures had overall low rates of nonunion and nonunion revision surgery, regardless of ketorolac exposure during the 5-day postoperative window.

The use of NSAIDs after surgery in hand surgery patients undergoing bony procedures remains controversial. Several preclinical studies have suggested cyclooxygenase inhibition, the mechanism of action of NSAIDs, contributes to delayed or even halted fracture healing with subsequent nonunion.7,20,21 Studies also indicate that the impairment is proportional to the duration of treatment and is most notable in the early stages of fracture healing.10,20,22 This timeline is of particular interest in the use of postoperative ketorolac administration, which is typically administered intravenously immediately following surgery or in the postacute care unit.23, 24, 25 Both IV and oral (PO) courses are sometimes prescribed for 3–5 days after surgery. The present study looked specifically at patients given IV or PO ketorolac within 5 days of surgery and found no evidence of such impairment in fracture healing, suggesting short-term administration may not carry the same risks observed in animal models.

Several human studies have associated NSAID use with delayed union and nonunion in long bone fractures. A single center retrospective review of 1,901 surgically repaired upper and lower-extremity long bone fractures assessed the impact of NSAID use on rates of nonunion/malunion and infection. They found that patients who received NSAIDs in the postoperative period were twice as likely to experience complications such as nonunion, malunion and infection, than those who did not.26 A similar retrospective study isolated to the upper extremity, found that in 659 patients with operatively treated humeral shaft fractures, there was a notable increased rate of nonunion in patients with NSAID use.25 Aside from long bone fractures, assessment of the impacts of NSAID use on hand and upper-extremity fractures is limited. Chang et al9 investigated the effect of NSAID use on union rates following operative treatment of distal radius fractures. They found that NSAID use had no notable impact on nonunion rate when given for a short duration (<2 weeks).9 However, they included multiple types of NSAIDs, predominately PO naproxen, and acknowledged that their findings in metaphyseal bone might not be transferable to fractures of bones with a known lower healing potential.

Scaphoid fractures are at an increased risk of nonunion, both with or without operative treatment, making it important to understand modifiable factors that may influence healing outcomes. The established nonunion rate following operative treatment of a scaphoid fracture is likely slightly higher than what we observed in this study, regardless of ketorolac use. This is potentially explained by our exclusion of established risk factors for nonunion, such as open fractures, infections, and prior nonunion. Given the multifactorial nature of nonunion, excluding these high risk cases helped isolate postoperative ketorolac as a potential contributor to nonunion. Overall, the impact of NSAID use on scaphoid fracture nonunion rate is not well established. Similar to this study, Kim et al22 performed a large propensity score-matched retrospective cohort study of 8,693 patients with upper and lower-extremity fractures, comparing NSAID users to nonusers. Their analysis included operatively treated humerus, radius and ulna fractures but excluded scaphoid fractures. Kaplan–Meir survival analyses showed that NSAID use ≤3 weeks after operative treatment did not increase the rate of nonunion.22 These findings are consistent with our results, which showed no statistically significant increase in nonunion or nonunion revision surgery rates following ketorolac administration within 5 days of scaphoid fracture fixation.

In the context of a low observed risk of nonunion, the potential benefits of immediate NSAID use are promising in the ambulatory surgery setting. Harrison et al3 investigated multimodal pain management in ambulatory hand surgery. They assigned patients who underwent carpometacarpal arthroplasty or open reduction internal fixation of distal radius fractures to four different postoperative pain regimens. The patients that were randomly assigned to receive postoperative ketorolac with acetaminophen and oxycodone reported superior pain control than those managed with opioids alone. Moreover, patients in the ketorolac group used fewer oxycodone tablets than patients in the other groups. Better immediate postoperative pain control is vital in the ambulatory surgery center, where patients are discharged the same day. Additionally, complication rates such as emergency department visits and sedation were also higher among patients who did not receive ketorolac. Although our analysis did not investigate pain scores or opioid use in ketorolac patients, these remain important topics to consider in future investigations.

There are, however, several limitations to this study. Although propensity score matching was used to control for key demographic and comorbidity variables, unmeasured confounders may still exist such as fracture displacement, surgical technique and approach, and surgeon experience and training. Moreover, the use of ketorolac was identified through prescription data which does not guarantee patient compliance or account for variations in dosage and duration beyond the assessed five-day postoperative period. Type of ketorolac administration, IV or PO, was not able to be determined from the database either. Further, radiographic confirmation of union status was not available so diagnostic codes were used to determine nonunion and revision which may be subject to misclassification. The database also lacks clinical outcome variables such as pain scores, opioid usage, and functional recovery which are important elements of postoperative care. The generalizability of our findings may be limited to health care systems represented within the TriNetX database and may not reflect practice patterns or outcomes in other settings.

Despite these limitations our study provides evidence that postoperative ketorolac use, within 5 days of operative treatment, does not considerably increase the rate of nonunion or nonunion revision surgery in operatively treated scaphoid fractures. These findings support the implementation of ketorolac into immediate multimodal pain management protocols for scaphoid fractures following operative treatment. Further prospective randomized studies are warranted to confirm these results and explore the impact of ketorolac on pain control, functional outcomes, and opioid usage in this patient population.
