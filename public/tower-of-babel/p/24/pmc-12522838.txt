Safety and efficacy analysis of different embolization methods of Glubran-2 glue in the treatment of arterial gastrointestinal bleeding
Authors: Guijun Huo, Yuqi Zeng, Xiao Wang, Dayong Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study summarized our experience in treating arterial gastrointestinal bleeding with Glubran-2 glue embolization, and analyzed efficacy and safety of Punctate embolism and Flake Embolism.MethodClinical data collected between January 2009 and January 2021 from patients with arterial gastrointestinal bleeding treated with Glubran-2 glue were analyzed retrospectively. Subsequently, univariate analysis and binary logistic regression analysis were performed to examine the risk factors associated with clinical outcomes and 30-day postoperative mortality. Finally, according to the embolization methods, it is divided into Punctate embolism(n=37) and Flake embolism(n༝44), and the curative effects of the two groups of embolization methods are analyzed.ResultFor a total of 81 patients, the technical success rate was 100%, the clinical success rate was 71.6% (n = 58), and the 30-day mortality rate post-endovascular interventions was 13.6% (n = 11). In addition, there was a significant negative correlation between the volume of blood transfusion and clinical outcome (P < 0.05), Furthermore, clinical outcome, coagulopathy, and heart rate were identified as independent risk factors for the 30-day mortality rate post-endovascular interventions. Finally, the clinical success rate (86.5%VS59.1%) of Punctate embolism is much higher than that of Flake embolism, and the mortality rate (5.4%VS20.5%) is obviously lower (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe findings of the study confirm that the use of Glubran-2 glue embolization offers a secure and effective treatment strategy for treating arterial gastrointestinal bleeding. The results suggest that punctate embolism may be associated with improved outcomes compared to flake embolism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03236-6.Keywords: Glubran-2 glue, Iodine oil, Punctate embolism, Flake embolism, Arterial, Gastrointestinal bleeding

Introduction

Acute gastrointestinal bleeding is a common clinical emergency typically categorized into upper gastrointestinal bleeding or lower gastrointestinal bleeding based on the ligament of Treitz [1]. In the absence of an effective and prompt treatment strategy, arterial gastrointestinal bleeding often poses a life-threatening risk due to the severity of the condition and the difficulties in identifying the bleeding site [2, 3]. Although endoscopic treatment is the first-line treatment for arterial gastrointestinal bleeding, there are still many patients whose lives are in danger because of poor endoscopic hemostasis effect [4, 5].In this context, the use of transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE) has been crucial in diagnosing and treating arterial gastrointestinal bleeding since the introduction of interventional radiology (IR). In addition to being less invasive, more effective, and safer than surgery, TAE also contributes to quicker recovery [6].

Typical embolic agents used for gastrointestinal arterial bleeding include coils, PVA particles, and gelatin sponges [7]. Among many available embolic agents, which one is the best to use alone or in combination is a heated debate, as a permanent liquid embolic agent, Glubran-2 glue offers distinct advantages over solid embolic agents such as coils [8]. First, Glubran-2 is highly effective in reaching challenging vessels and achieving quick embolization and hemostasis in unstable patients [9, 10]. Secondly, Glubran-2 glue is highly effective in patients with coagulation disorders, wherein solid embolic agents such as coils fail to work effectively [11]. Furthermore, the expense of utilizing Glubran-2 adhesive is considerably lower than that of using coils, and the duration of the procedure is considerably shorter [12, 13]. However, the disadvantage is that of a much slower learning curve compared to other embolic agents.

This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of Glubran-2 glue in the treatment of gastrointestinal arterial bleeding and the risk factors associated with clinical success and mortality. We analyzed the difference and curative effect of punctate embolization and flake embolization, and summarized the application experience of Glubran-2glue in our center.

Materials and methods

Clinical data

A retrospective analysis was conducted on patients with arterial gastrointestinal bleeding who received Glubran-2 glue treatment at our institution from January 2009 to January 2021. Because this was a retrospective study of all eligible patients treated at our institution over a 12-year period, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. The participant screening process for this study is presented in Fig. 1(Flow chart of the study population). A total of 173 patients with acute gastrointestinal bleeding treated between 2009 and 2021 were screened. Patients with venous bleeding or those not treated with Glubran-2 were excluded, leaving 86 patients with arterial bleeding treated with Glubran-2. Among them, 5 patients with negative angiography were excluded, resulting in 81 patients included in the final analysis. Human participants’ names have been removed from all sections of the manuscript. The study was approved by the ethics committee of The Affiliated Suzhou Hospital of Nanjing Medical University and strictly complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. The ethical approval number is KL901463, and ‘Clinical trial number: not applicable’. Subsequently, the fundamental patient information was gathered, comprising pathogenesis, gender, age, coagulopathy, preoperative blood pressure, heart rate (HR), hemoglobin content (HB), red blood cell count (RBC), blood transfusion volume, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels, concentration of urea and creatinine (CRE) in the blood, procedural time duration, angiographic findings, as well as the use of microcatheters and other embolic materials. Following this, the risk factors associated with clinical success rate and postoperative mortality at 30 days were analyzed. The diagnostic criteria for coagulopathy included the following: (1) an international normalized ratio (INR) >1.5; (2) an activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) >45 s; and (3) a platelet count < 80,000/µL [14]. The clinical data of this study were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB). We confirm that all research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines/regulations. The measurements of angiographic images were independently performed by two separate physicians. In case of any discrepancies between their measurements, a third physician was consulted to resolve the differences and ensure accuracy.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Clinical manifestation of gastrointestinal bleeding, including hematemesis and black stool; (2) arterial gastrointestinal bleeding; (3) failure of conservative or endoscopic treatment; and (4) embolization using Glubran-2 glue alone or in combination with other embolic agents.

Analysis of the bleeding site

The angiographic results were categorized as either positive or negative. The positive results were further categorized into direct and indirect indications. The direct indications comprised the leakage of the contrast medium from the blood vessels, exhibiting patchy, cloud-like, linear changes. The indirect indications observed through angiography included the following—pseudoaneurysm, arteriovenous malformation, beaded changes in diseased vessels, spasm, stenosis, and uneven thickness. The negative results comprised the ruling out of venous bleeding upon identification of the bleeding site, based on clinical symptoms, imaging, and gastroscopy. However, no direct or indirect indications of bleeding were detected during the operation.

Treatment methods

Arterial access was obtained using the Seldinger technique via the common femoral artery. Subsequently, a sheath was inserted, and DSA (ArtisZeego, SIEMENS, Germany) was utilized for selective contrast. The rates, doses, and pressures varied based on the target vessel. Furthermore, a microcatheter was utilized for superselective angiography in cases where the target vessel was challenging to access (We have now specified that commonly used microcatheters included 2.0–2.7 F (such as Progreat, Terumo; Renegade, Boston Scientific), selected based on target vessel diameter and tortuosity. Superselective catheterization was achieved with the assistance of 0.014–0.018-inch hydrophilic guidewires.). This was achieved by combining a highly lubricated guide wire with an alternative catheter selection. Angiography was first performed to assess direct signs of bleeding (contrast extravasation) and indirect signs (pseudoaneurysm, arteriovenous malformation, spasm). Punctate embolization was selected when a discrete bleeding point was visualized and catheter advancement within 1 cm of the site was feasible. Flake embolization was selected when bleeding points were unclear, multiple, or anatomically inaccessible. Following angiography to assess the blood flow velocity and flow rate of the bleeding artery, Glubran-2 glue and super-liquefied lipiodol were mixed in a 1:3 ratio (the mixing ratio was adjusted according to the blood flow velocity and flow rate of the target vessels. For high-flow vessels or large-caliber arteries, a higher concentration of Glubran-2 (1:2) was used to ensure rapid polymerization and avoid reflux. For low-flow or distal small branches, a more diluted mixture (1:4) was preferred to achieve better distal penetration.). The microcatheter was flushed with a 5% glucose solution before and after injection to prevent adhesion. The mixture was then injected into the bleeding artery through the microcatheter (under DSA) until the bleeding artery was completely filled. Then, the microcatheter was promptly withdrawn. For rapid blood flow and thicker-walled blood vessels, coils were used for protective embolization or to slow blood flow. Following embolization, the angiography revealed that the development of the target vessel was completely arrested. The method of embolization of target vessels is divided into Punctate embolism and Flake embolism. Figure 2 (Punctate embolism) and Fig. 3 (Flake embolism) show the specific operation methods and surgical procedures for Punctate embolism and Flake embolism. In our practice, efforts were made to standardize key aspects of the procedures, including indications, embolic ratios, microcatheter use, and definitions of outcomes, which were guided by departmental protocols. New staff were trained under the supervision of senior surgeons before independent practice, and outcome assessments were independently reviewed by two physicians, with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

Provocative testing

After selectively inserting the catheter into the suspected blood supply artery, nitroglycerin (dissolved in 100 mL of physiological saline, slowly injected about 4 ml) is slowly injected through a microcatheter to increase local blood flow through vasodilation, thereby revealing occult bleeding. After drug injection, immediate follow-up angiography should be performed. If new bleeding signs are found (such as contrast agent extravasation, pseudoaneurysm, or vascular irregularity), immediate ultra selective embolization treatment should be performed. If no bleeding is found, the stimulation test will not be repeated to avoid excessive risk. This technique is limited to patients with hemodynamic instability, strong clinical suspicion of active bleeding, and clear CT results, and is only performed by senior interventional physicians with > 10 years of experience. All patients completed the procedure under close monitoring, with sufficient resuscitation equipment on site to address the risk of worsening bleeding or inducing hemodynamic deterioration.

Definition

Technical success

The absence of pseudoaneurysms or arteriovenous malformations during the complete occlusion of the target vessels and arteriography was defined as technical success.

Clinical success

Following technical success, the absence of ongoing or recurrent bleeding within 30 days post-embolization was defined as clinical success. Conversely, a decrease in prognostic hemoglobin levels exceeding 20 g/L or the need for re-operative intervention was defined as a clinical failure.

Punctate embolism

The distance between the microcatheter and the bleeding site was less than 1 cm, and the embolization range was less than 1 cm.

Flake embolism

The distance between the microcatheter and the bleeding site was more than 1 cm, and the embolization range was more than 1 cm.

Complications

In accordance with the criteria defined by the Society of Interventional Radiology, complications were considered major if they necessitated surgery or prolonged hospitalization. In contrast, the complications were considered minor if they did not necessitate surgical intervention or prolonged hospitalization [15].

Statistical methods

The patients were categorized into two groups—the clinical success group and the clinical failure group—according to their clinical outcomes. Furthermore, they were categorized into another two groups—the survival group and the mortality group. All findings were subjected to statistical analysis using SPSS 26.0, and the measurement data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Additionally, the Kruskal-Wallis test was utilized to compare the two groups. The enumeration data were presented as the number of cases or percentage, and Fisher’s exact test was utilized to compare patient groups. P-value lower than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Moreover, multivariate binary logistic regression was utilized to analyze factors that exhibited statistically significant differences.

Results

Basic results

Technical success was achieved in all 81 patients., achieving a 100% technical success rate. Furthermore, 58 patients (71.6%) achieved clinical success, whereas 23 patients (28.4%) experienced clinical failure, with 4 cases necessitating a secondary surgery, no major complications were recorded. In our study, a total of 13 patients experienced minor complications, including post-embolization syndrome in 9 cases, transient ischemic events in 3 cases, and catheter-related complication in 1 case. Additionally, there were 11 fatalities (13.6%) in the study, the specific causes of death of the deceased patients are shown in Fig. 4 (Causes of death). 6 patients died from bleeding, 2 from anastomotic fistula due to surgery, 1 from multiple organ failure, 1 from severe pulmonary infection, and 1 from an unknown cause (Fig. 4). The causes of bleeding were divided into 10 categories, and the specific cause of bleeding for the patient is shown in Fig. 5 (Causes of bleeding), which included iatrogenic (29 patients), gastric ulcer (17 patients), duodenal ulcer (9 patients), gastroduodenal ulcer (4 patients), gastric cancer (9 patients), liver cancer (2 patients), arteriovenous malformation (3 patients), esophageal and cardiac mucosa laceration (1 patient), superior mesenteric aneurysm (1 patient), and uncertain (6 patients) (Fig. 5). The specific blood vessels causing bleeding in the patient are shown in Fig. 6 (Target vessel), and the target vessels included the left gastric artery (LGA) (16 patients), right gastric artery (RGA) (7 patients), short gastric artery (SGA) (1 patient), gastroduodenal artery (GDA) (24 patients), pancreaticoduodenal artery (PDA) (2 patients), proper hepatic artery (PHA) (2 patients), common hepatic artery (CHA) (1 patient), superior mesenteric artery (SMA) (10 patients), inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) (8 patients), LGA and RGA (4 patients), LGA and GDA (1 patient), LGA and CTA (1 patient), LGA and EA (1 patient), GDA and PDA (2 patients), GDA and SMA (1 patients), with a total of 91 target vessels (Fig. 6). Furthermore, a total of 50 patients were exclusively embolized with Glubran-2 glue, whereas 31 patients underwent additional embolization with various materials such as coils (n = 20), gelatin sponges (n = 8), microspheres (n = 1), coils and stents (n = 1), as well as coils and gelfoam (n = 1) (Fig. 7). Although limited by the sample size, it is difficult to draw a clear conclusion, the results suggest that punctate embolization is particularly effective in different etiologies and target vessels.

Discussion

In the present study, a total of 81 patients were successfully treated using Glubran-2 glue, achieving a technical success rate of 100%. The clinical success rate of this study was 71.6% and the mortality rate was 13.6%, both of which were much lower than those reported in the literature [16], Of the patients who died, only 54.5% died of bleeding, and the rest died of other uncontrollable causes. The mortality rate was significantly correlated with clinical outcome, blood coagulation and heart rate. The clinical success rate of punctate embolization group was significantly higher than that of flaky embolization group, and the mortality rate was significantly lower than that of flaky embolization group.

Several embolic agents are currently available in the market, each with its unique advantages and disadvantages. However, it is very challenging to accurately reach the target vessel using embolizers such as coils for twisted and elongated blood vessels, which result in a higher rate of proximal embolization [17].Considering the large number of anastomotic networks in the gastrointestinal tract, which is likely to lead to re-bleeding, the clinical success rate is low, Furthermore, if the blood coagulation function is compromised, the prompt treatment of hemorrhages can be highly challenging. Moreover, although gelatin sponge and microspheres can be mixed with contrast and administered under fluoroscopic control, cause mechanical vascular occlusion by depositing in vessels, bBut solid particles have a hard time adapting to the shape of blood vessels, so it is difficult to embolize completely [18]. Particles and liquid embolics guarantee rapid and effective mechanical embolization, not requiring the activation of coagulation. PVA particles and microspheres cause mechanical vascular occlusion by depositing in vessels and they are available in different size ranges, which determine their ability to penetrate even very small vessels and even aberrant small vessels in bleeding cancers [19]. Non-adhesive liquid embolic agents (NALEA) represent the other class of liquid embolic agents besides NBCA and are very effective even in patients with coagulopathy and in high-risk pseudoaneurysms by not exerting mechanical pressure on the vessel wall unlike coils with interesting implications in gastrointestinal pseudoaneurysms [20, 21]. However, NALEA has to be used with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), which can cause vasospasm, endothelial wall damage, and pain [20]. Previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of various embolic agents, including coils, particles, and gelfoam, in the management of gastrointestinal bleeding. Glubran-2 glue is a liquid embolic agent mixed with lipoalcohol that can navigate narrow and curved blood vessels and is unaffected by clotting problems. These properties make Glubran-2 particularly advantageous in cases of diffuse bleeding, multiple bleeding sites, or when distal superselective embolization is feasible. However, liquid embolics also carry unique risks, including non-target embolization, reflux, and catheter adhesion. Compared to the above embolizers, glubran2 has many advantages and provides more precise embolization, but it is generally accepted that using glubran2 glue safely requires a steep learning curve [22, 23].

This study innovatively proposed two different embolization methods: punctate embolism and flake embolism, and punctate embolism has great advantages over flake embolism. Below we will discuss the indications of two different embolization methods from the following three aspects. Firstly, if we want to perform Punctate embolism, the angiography result must be positive and the bleeding point must be clearly seen. If punctate embolization is performed without seeing the bleeding point, it may lead to poor embolization effect and failure to stop bleeding, and may also lead to ectopic embolization and ineffective embolization. In the event that bleeding can be staunched with punctate embolization, broadening the scope of embolization through the execution of flake embolization was deemed nonessential. Secondly, the distance between the microcatheter and the bleeding site should be less than 1 cm to perform punctate embolism. For some twisted vessels, this may require a significant amount of time trying to reach the target vessel. Thirdly, flake embolism was deemed essential if the bleeding point was unclear or if multiple bleeding sites in the area were present, thus necessitating the expansion of the embolization range or the simultaneous embolization of the collateral branches. This methodology entails only a minimal amount of intubation and does not demand exact superselection to the bleeding site. If the patient needs surgery next, we can also try a sheet embolization. However, for flake embolization, which is more comprehensive and reliable due to the fluidity of Glubran-2 glue, ectopic or excessive embolization may occur. However, in some patients who experience acute gastrointestinal bleeding, vasospasm can occur leading to negative angiographic results. Correspondingly, our center utilized preoperative auxiliary examinations, specifically contrast-enhanced CT results, to perform provocative tests at suspected bleeding sites, followed by precise embolization, In some cases it is even possible to use urokinase for provocation testing [24]. Five patients in the study underwent provocation tests, and the precise embolization of target vessels resulted in favorable outcomes. However, caution should be exercised when using the provocation test due to its controversial nature and the potential risk of uncontrollable bleeding, as observed by many experts [25]. In addition, in this study, due to spanning 12 years and involving 3 surgeons, the surgical outcome may be related to the surgeon’s accumulated experience or influenced by advances in instruments and materials.

Our findings are broadly consistent with published data on NBCA embolization for non-variceal gastrointestinal bleeding. In the meta-analysis by Chevallier et al., technical success rates were reported at 98.8% and clinical success rates at 78–88%, which align with the 100% technical success observed in our series, although our clinical success rate (71.6%) was somewhat lower [26]. This discrepancy may be related to patient selection, heterogeneity of bleeding etiologies, or the learning curve associated with adopting the punctate/flake classification system. When compared with the single-center experience reported by Abdulmalak et al., our outcomes are similar in terms of clinical success but showed lower mortality, possibly reflecting our focused expertise in gastrointestinal bleeding accumulated over a longer timeframe [8]. In comparison with earlier landmark series, such as those by Jae et al., and Morishita et al., our results fall within the expected outcome ranges and are consistent with the increasing emphasis on superselective approaches [17, 22]. Importantly, unlike previous studies, our work introduces a novel technical classification—punctate versus flake embolization—providing a systematic framework for technique selection within a single embolic agent category. This fills an important gap in the literature, where most prior studies focused on embolic agent comparisons rather than procedural technique classification. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that temporal trends, learning curve effects, and economic considerations were not fully analyzed, representing areas for future research.

This section describes the specific experience in using Glubran-2. Firstly, the combined use of Glubran-2 and lipiodol not only enhanced X-ray visibility but also altered the polymerization rate [23]. As the mixing concentration was inversely proportional to the polymerization time, the polymerization time could be effectively controlled by adjusting the mixing concentration in accordance with the shape and length of the target vessels. Nevertheless, there is a possibility of incomplete or excessive distal vascular embolism. Thus, the surgeon must precisely regulate the mixed concentration of Glubran-2 and lipiodol. Secondly, the adjunctive use of microcatheters and coaxial catheters contributed to a high embolization efficiency. Herein, out of 81 patients, microcatheters were used in a total of 75 patients, resulting in a high microcatheter utilization rate (92.6%), the operation of some target diseased vessels is relatively simple, and microcatheters were not used in consideration of patients’ economic levels, but it is relatively dangerous not to use microcatheters. Notably, the adhesion between the microcatheter and Glubran-2 glue was reduced due to the coating on the hydrophilic surface of the microcatheter [23, 27]. This reduced the risk of catheter adhesion and blockage and enhanced the embolization efficacy. Moreover, the microcatheter is thinner and more selective for narrow or twisted blood vessels. This allowed for precise embolization, reducing the occurrence of ectopic embolism when guided by the path map. Rinsing the microcatheter with a glucose solution was deemed highly essential before injecting a mixture of Glubran-2 and lipiodol using a 1 ml syringe at a suitable rate under continuous fluoroscopy. This is attributed to the fact that injecting too quickly can result in reflux and ectopic embolism. Conversely, an injection administered too slowly is likely to result in premature polymerization, thereby failing to arrest the bleeding. Furthermore, various types of embolic materials were combined to enhance the efficacy of embolization in various target vessels. In this study, 22 patients were assisted with coil embolization to prevent ectopic embolism caused by high blood flow velocity and potential backflow of embolic agent in large diameter blood vessels. Some patients in the study underwent additional embolization via gelfoam and microspheres, all with good results, which is consistent with results reported in the literature [18]. In terms of complications, no catheter entrapment, large area ectopic embolism from embolic agents, distal non-target vascular embolism, ischemic necrosis of vital organs such as the gastrointestinal tract or mesentery, or any serious clinical consequences were observed in this study, which aligned well with the findings of Yamakado et al. [28].

The safe and effective use of Glubran-2 requires not only appropriate operator expertise but also adequate institutional infrastructure. Essential prerequisites include dedicated microcatheter systems, high-quality angiographic imaging facilities, and readily available resuscitation support. Previous studies have consistently emphasized the steep learning curve associated with cyanoacrylate embolization, underscoring the risk of non-target embolization and other complications when used without sufficient experience. Therefore, in centers with limited prior exposure, a structured training program and stepwise adoption strategy are strongly recommended to optimize patient safety and outcomes.

Nevertheless, there are still limitations to our research. Firstly, this is a retrospective, single-center study rather than a prospective study. Secondly, our research was carried out by three skilled surgeons. Thus, minor variations, which are attributable to the expertise and experience of the chief surgeon, could not be ruled out. Thirdly, several patients in the study were treated with a combination of embolization techniques, including coils or gelatin sponges, rather than using only Glubran-2 glue. This is highly likely to contribute to the positive outcomes observed from the use of Glubran-2 glue in the occlusion of blood vessels in the current study. Fourthly, this study is an exploratory research based on clinical experience using a 1 cm incision. In the future, factors such as vascular caliber, blood flow velocity, and collateral circulation need to be considered, and large-scale prospective studies are also needed for validation. Fifthly, the selection of punctate or flake embolization methods is often influenced by anatomical factors, the severity of bleeding, and the surgeon’s judgment, which is difficult to avoid in retrospective studies and may lead to selection bias. Sixthly, as a single institutional study conducted by three skilled surgeons, the results may not represent what other centers with different levels of expertise, equipment, or patient populations can achieve. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating the results of this study. Seventhly, during the 12 year study period, there were significant developments in imaging, catheterization, embolization materials, and surgeon experience. Due to the limited number of cases each year, it was difficult to conduct statistically effective trend analysis, which is also one of the limitations of this study. Eighthly, the definition of clinical success was restricted to the absence of rebleeding within 30 days, which mainly reflects short-term hemostatic efficacy. Longer follow-up data were not consistently available, and therefore the durability of embolization and the risk of delayed complications could not be fully evaluated. Ninthly, although all-cause mortality and bleeding-related mortality were analyzed, only 54.5% of deaths were directly related to bleeding. Thus, the observed mortality likely reflects not only embolization outcomes but also underlying comorbidities and patient condition. Future prospective studies with extended follow-up, more comprehensive complication reporting, and detailed classification of bleeding-related versus non-bleeding mortality are needed to provide a more complete picture of the effectiveness and safety of embolization. Finally, to facilitate the adoption of this classification system, future research should focus on developing standardized protocols, conducting multicenter prospective validation, assessing inter-observer reliability, and establishing structured training programs.

Conclusion

Our findings confirm that Glubran-2 embolization is a safe and effective treatment for arterial gastrointestinal bleeding, particularly in patients with hemodynamic instability, vascular anomalies, or coagulopathy. The results suggest that punctate embolism may be associated with improved outcomes compared to flake embolism.
