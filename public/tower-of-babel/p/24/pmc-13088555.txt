Engineering MSC-exosomes for diabetic bone regeneration: from mechanism to delivery
Authors: Guangmei Ran, Hongrui Jin, Qian Yang, Wentao Zhai, Jun Lu, Wenjie Jiang, Jingjing Luo, Shichang Fang, Yinchang Zhang, Huan Liu, Jian Zuo, Jiating Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapidly growing diabetic population is at high risk of dental implant failure due to a disrupted peri-implant immune microenvironment. Mesenchymal stem cells-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos) have emerged as a potent nanotherapeutic platform to remodel this hostile niche. Their mechanisms involve reprogramming macrophage polarization to alleviate inflammation, delivering pro-angiogenic miRNAs to restore vascular-osteogenic coupling, and modulating neuro-immune crosstalk to reestablish homeostasis. Collectively, these actions break the vicious cycle of impaired healing. Furthermore, engineering strategies such as membrane modification, integration with biomaterials, and preconditioning of parent cells can enhance the targeting, stability, and controlled release of MSC-Exos, thereby improving osseointegration outcomes in diabetic models. These engineering innovations, which focus on precise delivery and controlled release, are as critical to therapeutic development as elucidating the underlying biological mechanisms. This review systematically delineates the mechanisms by which MSC-Exos recalibrate the diabetic bone immune niche to foster osseointegration and critically discusses the clinical translation prospects of engineered exosome-based therapies.Keywords: Bone immune microenvironment, Dental implant osseointegration, Diabetes, Exosome, Mesenchymal stem cell

Introduction

According to the latest statistics from the International Diabetes Mellitus (DM) Consortium, there were 589 million adults aged 20–79 years with DM worldwide in 2024, and it is expected to increase to 853 million in 2050 [1]. Beyond its metabolic consequences, DM imposes a significant burden on the musculoskeletal system, leading to impaired fracture healing and compromised bone regeneration around orthopedic and dental implants [2]. This significantly elevates the risk of implant failure in diabetic patients. Experimental studies in rat models have corroborated these clinical observations, demonstrating that DM negatively affects osseointegration, as evidenced by disorganized skeletal tissue, inferior trabecular parameters, and decreased levels of key growth factors [3].

At the core of these pathological changes lies an imbalance in the bone immune microenvironment induced by DM. Under diabetic conditions, the balance between classically activated (M1, pro-inflammatory) and alternatively activated (M2, anti-inflammatory) macrophages is skewed [4]. The sustained polarization towards the M1 phenotype creates a chronic inflammatory state, characterized by the release of factors like tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), which directly inhibit osteoblast differentiation and bone formation [5].

Crucially, immune dysregulation does not act alone. The persistent inflammatory milieu, dominated by abnormally polarized macrophages, directly contributes to vascular dysfunction. Prolonged DM and its associated cytokine dysregulation can cause bone marrow hypoperfusion, resulting in systemic microcirculatory disorders [6]. This vascular compromise hinders the delivery of nutrients and oxygen, leading to an insufficient local blood supply. Consequently, a decrease in peri-implant microvessel density is observed, endothelial cell migration is inhibited, angiogenesis is impaired, and the bone healing process is ultimately compromised [7]. Concurrently, hyperglycemia damages Schwann cells (SCs), disrupting neurotrophic factor secretion and nerve-bone signaling [8]. Furthermore, metabolic clearance mechanisms such as autophagy are impaired. These immune, vascular, neural, and metabolic elements are interconnected and collectively drive the failure of osseointegration in DM.

Given this multifaceted pathology, an ideal therapeutic strategy must be capable of simultaneously targeting multiple pathways. Mesenchymal stem cells-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos) have emerged as a promising nanotherapeutic platform. They exhibit low immunogenicity and innate targeting capabilities [9], which are crucial for safe and efficient delivery. Moreover, their multifaceted regulatory functions enable them to simultaneously intervene in multiple pathological processes of diabetic bone regeneration [10]. Importantly, their therapeutic potential aligns perfectly with the challenges of the DM microenvironment: they can reprogram macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype [11], promote angiogenesis via pro-vasculogenic miRNAs [12], repair SCs function and neuro-immune crosstalk [13], and restore metabolic homeostasis. Moreover, their engineering potential, such as loading onto implant coatings for localized release, enhances their clinical applicability [14].

In view of the specificity of the multidimensional disorder of the DM bone immune microenvironment and the multifaceted therapeutic potential of MSC-Exos, this review aims to systematically delineate the mechanisms by which MSC-Exos recalibrate the diabetic peri-implant bone immune niche. We will focus on their roles in reprogramming macrophage polarization, promoting vascular-osteogenic coupling, repairing neuro-immune interactions, and re-establishing metabolic clearance homeostasis, which collectively depict the multi-target therapeutic framework of MSC-Exos against the diabetic bone immune environment (Fig. 1). Furthermore, we place equal emphasis on evaluating advanced engineering and delivery strategies to maximize the therapeutic efficacy of MSC-Exos, and critically discuss the associated barriers and challenges hindering their clinical translation.

MSC-Exos overview

Exosomes (Exos) are extracellular vesicles with diameters between 30 and 150 nm that are secreted by almost all cell types [15, 16]. Their formation and secretion follow a specific endosomal-multivesicular body (MVB) pathway: early endosomal membranes invaginate to form intraluminal vesicles (ILVs), which aggregate to become MVBs; ultimately, the MVBs fuse with the cytoplasmic membrane, releasing their ILVs (i.e., exosomes) into the extracellular space. This process involves four key stages: budding, invagination, MVB formation and secretion [17]. Exos carry a wide range of functional biomolecules, including nucleic acids (e.g., miR-181c), long non-coding RNA, proteins (e.g., CD73, the molecular chaperone heat-shock proteins), tetraspanins(CD9, CD63, CD81), and lipids (e.g., cholesterol, sphingomyelins) [11, 15, 18]. These active components are transported to receptors via Exos and mediate intercellular communication, which in turn regulates tissue homeostasis and physiological processes [19]. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) have a high self-renewal capacity, and their secreted exosomes (MSC-Exos) have a stable membrane structure with low immunogenicity, and have a variety of therapeutic potentials. MSC-Exos from different tissue sources (e.g., bone marrow, adipose, umbilical cord) differ in miRNA and protein profiles, which may affect their specific therapeutic efficacy. Current studies have shown that MSC-Exos can alleviate type 1 DM(T1DM) by upregulating β-cell autophagy or can modulate macrophage polarization to reduce atherosclerosis progression through the miRNAs it carries, BMSC-Exos overexpressing miR-210 attenuates chondrocyte inflammatory injury by inhibiting the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) pathway, and adipocyte-derived MSC-Exos overexpressing miR-210 attenuates cartilage cell inflammatory injury, adipocytes or MSC-Exos are to some extent resistant to the damage of the high-glucose microenvironment and protect the content activity, and they carry miR-181c which still shows pro-bone regeneration potential under high-glucose conditions [9, 10, 18, 20]. MSC-Exos naturally have certain enriched properties and can be engineered to enhance targeting. On the other hand, the DM microenvironment negatively affects MSC-Exos function. For example, the bone regeneration capacity of DM-derived BMSC-Exos is significantly weaker than that of normal-derived BMSC-Exos, resulting in impaired MSC-Exos-mediated osteogenic differentiation and angiogenic activity [21]. This suggests that transplantation therapy using autologous-derived MSC-Exos from DM patients may have some limitations or potential risk of diminished effect. In addition, the high glucose environment can induce dysregulation of miRNA expression in Exos (e.g., increased expression of miR-129-5p), which further hinders osteogenic differentiation of the jawbone by targeting the frizzled class of receptor 4 to inhibit the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [22]. Different pretreatment modalities can be targeted to modulate miRNA loading of MSC-Exos. For example, inflammatory factor stimulation increased the expression of anti-inflammatory miR-146b [23], whereas danshensu extract pretreatment targeted miR-27a-5p enrichment [24], and this customization feature provides the possibility to precisely regulate the bone immune microenvironment.

Characterization of bone regeneration site immunity and impaired osseointegration in the DM microenvironment

Among diabetic patients, patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), accounting for approximately 90–95% of all diabetic cases, are characterized primarily by insulin resistance, a diminished cellular response to insulin, and are frequently associated with broader metabolic abnormalities [25, 26]. Studies have shown that dental implants in patients with T2DM exhibit lower and slower osseointegration rates compared to non-T2DM patients, resulting in a higher rate of dental implant treatment failure in patients [27]. Studies have demonstrated that reduced bone remodeling (low bone conversion rate) is associated with an increased risk of developing DM [28]. Mechanistic studies further suggest that DM leads to impaired bone and bone marrow microcirculation [29]. Under hyperglycemic conditions, the core feature of the bone immune microenvironment may be the remodeling of the peri-implant microenvironment through the imbalance of immune-bone metabolism-neurovascular, i.e., the multiple cascade effects of inflammation-osteoconjugation, broken vascular-neural coupling, and autophagy-metabolic homeostasis hindering osseointegration. Sustained hyperglycemia not only leads to the accumulation of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and advanced glycation end products (AGEs), causing cellular damage and affecting macrophage function [30], but also mediates persistent inflammatory responses in the bone microenvironment: Dysregulated macrophages secrete factors such as TNF-α and IL-1β, where TNF-α blocks the Wnt/β-catenin pathway to inhibit osteocalcin (OCN) expression, and IL-1β activates the NF-κB pathway to promote osteoclastogenesis, which collectively lead to impaired bone formation [31]. The above pathological changes form a positive feedback loop, further amplifying the imbalance in the bone immune microenvironment. The DM ischemic microenvironment decreases the local oxygen partial pressure, diminishes hypoxia inducible factor-1 (HIF-1α) stability, and Exos promotes DM wound healing through the HIF-1α axis [32]. At the same time, this link is accompanied by decreased secretion of vascular endothelial growth (VEGF) and reduced collagen deposition. In contrast, MSC-Exos accelerates DM foot ulcer healing by up-regulating VEGF expression and promoting collagen synthesis [33]. In the DM state, a combination of hyperglycemia, chronic inflammation, and oxidative stress leads to tissue damage, which manifests itself in the nervous system as neuropathy (e.g., pain, regenerative deficits), and the DM microenvironment significantly disrupts the immune homeostasis and inhibits macrophage polarization toward the reparative M2 phenotype polarization, and this polarization imbalance is key to exacerbating nerve damage and impeding repair [13, 34]. The high glucose environment significantly impairs cellular metabolic clearance, with reduced levels of autophagy and impaired osteogenic differentiation in DM-derived stem cells [35, 36]; activation of autophagy (e.g., metformin, empagliflozin) reverses this effect [37, 38]. High glucose impairs mitophagy: impaired mitophagy mediated by the mitochondrial receptor NIP3-like protein X accelerates cellular senescence in periodontal membranes [39]; in vascular endothelial cells it leads to accumulation of functionally aberrant mitochondria and damage through inhibition of the PINK1/Parkin pathway [40]. Irisin, on the other hand, regulates oxidative stress and mitophagy through activation of SIRT3 signaling, thereby promoting osteogenesis [41]. Lipotoxicity-polarized macrophage Exos inhibits Miro1 expression by carrying miRNAs that block mitophagy and exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction, suggesting that targeting Miro1 may be a novel strategy to improve bone metabolism in DM [42].

The complexity of the diabetic bone immune microenvironment extends far beyond macrophages, it also involves a broader spectrum of dysregulated immune cells, including neutrophils, T cells (TCs), and dendritic cells, which collectively exacerbate inflammation and impede bone healing. Neutrophils undergo abnormal activation under high glucose stimulation, leading to excessive release of neutrophil extracellular traps. These reticular structures, rich in cytotoxic substances, have been demonstrated to directly inhibit osteoblast function, representing a key mechanism impeding bone healing [43]. Adaptive immune cells, particularly CD4 + TCs, are also deeply involved. Systemic chronic inflammation induced by diabetes leads to an imbalance in Th17/Treg cell subsets (increased pro-inflammatory Th17 cells and decreased anti-inflammatory Treg cells), which is directly associated with mandibular dysplasia [44]. Activated TCs migrating to the bone marrow continuously secrete factors such as TNF-α and RANKL, which not only exacerbate local inflammation but also directly stimulate osteoclast differentiation, leading to increased bone resorption and enhanced bone fragility [45]. In addition, the number of dendritic cells, immune sentinels, is significantly reduced in the bone marrow of diabetic patients [46], with their antigen presentation and immunoregulatory functions disrupted, potentially further undermining local immune homeostasis. These multiple, synergistic dysregulations of both innate and adaptive immune cells collectively weave a self-perpetuating chronic inflammatory network, severely impairing bone regenerative capacity under diabetic conditions.

Core mechanisms of MSC-Exos regulation of the immune microenvironment

Reprogramming of macrophage polarization

The imbalance in the polarization ratio of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages to anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages is a key factor in DM bone integration disorder. MSC-Exos may remodel macrophage phenotype mainly through pathways such as CD73/adenosine(ADO) signaling axis:

CD73/ADO signaling axis dominates polarization transition

Large amounts of inflammatory mediators in DM patients reduce M2-type macrophage polarization [47], which leads to a chronic inflammatory state that ultimately prevents bone regeneration (e.g., alveolar bone healing) [48]. The ADO A2b receptor (A2bR) on the surface of target cells (e.g., macrophages) is a key target downstream of the CD73-ADO signaling pathway. Two ectonucleotidases, CD39 and CD73 [11, 49], are present on the cell surface and BMSC-Exos membranes, which synergistically catalyze the hydrolysis of ADO triphosphate (ATP) to ADO monophosphate (AMP), which is then converted into ADO [50]. Structural biology studies have shown that the physical proximity of CD39 and CD73 significantly enhances the catalytic efficiency of the ATP-ADO pathway through substrate channeling effects: the catalytic efficiency of the engineered CD39-CD73 fusion protein is higher than that of the monomeric CD39-CD73 enzyme mixture [51], which provides a molecular basis for synergistic action of the two enzymes in the natural state. Generated ADO activates macrophage A2bR, which inhibits the phosphorylation of IκB kinase β through the cAMP/PKA pathway, preventing the degradation of IκBα, thereby inhibiting NF-κB nuclear translocation, and ultimately inducing macrophage polarization toward the M2 anti-inflammatory type [11, 52]. Notably, A2bR exerts its anti-inflammatory effects through the cAMP/PKA pathway in macrophages under high glucose environments, whereas it induces oxidative stress via overactivation in endothelial cells [53]. High glucose-induced oxidative stress is a key pathological feature of the widespread DM microenvironment with complex regulatory mechanisms [54]. In macrophages, however, this receptor-mediated ADO signaling exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting NF-κB and induces M2 polarization to promote repair. CD73, a unique biomarker for MSCs, is also present in MSC-Exos and has been identified as a key bioactive component of BMSC-Exos in the promotion of wound repair [55], and CD73 expression levels were positively correlated with the promotion of M2 polarization and inhibition of bone resorption [11, 56]. Activated M2 macrophages activate the Smad3 signaling pathway in BMSCs to up-regulate key osteogenic genes, such as Runx2 and OCN, by secreting TGF-β, which ultimately promotes the deposition and mineralization of bone matrix and effectively promotes the process of osteogenic differentiation [11, 14]. Thus, CD73/ADO may be a key regulatory node in the imbalance of DM bone immune microenvironment by remodeling macrophage homeostasis and promoting osteogenic differentiation. Engineering strategies targeting this node, such as local delivery of MSC-Exos or A2bR agonists overexpressing CD73, have been shown to significantly elevate bone-implant contact (BIC) in DM animal models [11, 56]. This exemplifies a direct mechanism-to-engineering translation, where understanding a key molecular axis (CD73/ADO) directly informs the design of targeted delivery systems (e.g., implant coatings). In this pathway, MSC-Exos surface CD73 works in concert with CD39 to catalyze the generation of ADO, which then activates the macrophage A2bR/cAMP/PKA axis to inhibit NF-κB signaling and drive M2 polarization, thereby creating a pro-healing microenvironment(Fig. 2).

Specific miRNA-mediated inflammation suppression

As a class of highly conserved non-coding RNAs, miRNAs play a crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis by regulating gene expression [57]. Exos are important carriers for intercellular communication, delivering biomolecules such as miRNAs, which are involved in the regulation of a variety of physiopathological processes [58]. In the DM bone microenvironment, Exos of different cellular origins play critical roles by delivering specific miRNAs, which significantly affect the improvement of the bone immune microenvironment. Among these, certain exos and their miRNA cargo exert bone-protective functions. For instance, MSC-Exos can suppress the IRF1/NF-κB pathway in macrophages by delivering miR-23a-3p, thereby driving their polarization toward the M2 phenotype, which alleviates inflammation and promotes osteogenesis [59]. Additionally, under high-glucose conditions, MSC-Exos engineered to overexpress miR-181c can directly target osteoblasts/macrophages, synergistically upregulating osteogenic markers such as OCN and OPG while inhibiting bone resorption, thereby demonstrating clear osteogenic potential [60]. Conversely, other Exos exacerbate bone destruction. Exos derived from bone marrow macrophages under diabetic conditions act as destructive factors; the miR-144-5p they carry significantly impedes osteogenic differentiation by targeting and suppressing Smad1 expression in MSCs [61]. Meanwhile, miR-381-3p derived from diabetic macrophages disrupts the autophagic flux in gingival epithelial cells by inhibiting ATG7 through targeting NR5A2, thereby exacerbating local inflammatory responses [62]. Moreover, studies on Exos-miRNA in other tissues have provided insights for diabetic bone repair. For example, in a diabetic corneal disease model, MSC-Exos-delivered miR-125a-5p reduced hyperglycemia-induced apoptosis by suppressing endoplasmic reticulum stress [12], suggesting its potential for similar protective effects in the bone microenvironment. On the contrary, studies confirm that the miR-17-5p/Smad7 axis is a key mechanism underlying impaired MSC-Exos function in diabetic bone marrow, while silencing miR-17-5p restores their bone regenerative capacity [63]. These findings further confirm that the functional properties of Exos are highly dependent on the state of their parental cells and specific miRNA cargo. The specific miRNAs carried by exosomes serve as core messengers, playing a central role in cross-cell regulation within the diabetic bone microenvironment. They achieve this by inhibiting inflammasome activation, regulating cell fate (such as apoptosis and autophagy), and enhancing osteogenic gene expression. The central role of specific miRNAs underscores the rationale for engineering strategies aimed at either enriching therapeutic miRNAs or silencing pathogenic ones within Exos cargo.

Vascular-osteogenic coupling

In the DM bone microenvironment, impaired angiogenesis is one of the core mechanisms of failed osseointegration. The high-glycemic environment disrupts vascular homeostasis through a dual pathway: on the one hand, it inhibits the secretion of pro-vascular factors (e.g., VEGF, platelet-derived growth factor), which impairs endothelial cell migration and tube-formation; on the other hand, it significantly induces the expression of the anti-vascular factor thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1), which creates a localized anti-angiogenic microenvironment [7, 64]. Metformin pretreatment enhances exosomal miR-139-3p, which targets FOXC2 to promote angiogenesis [7]. Earlier studies have confirmed that up-regulation of TSP-1 expression, a key anti-vascular factor induced by high glucose, is closely associated with DM vasculopathy: plasma TSP-1 concentration is significantly elevated in patients with T1DM [65], and high glucose activates vascular smooth muscle cells’ TSP-1 transcripts via the hexosamine pathway [66], and TSP-1 overexpression is accompanied by decreased vessel density and neointimal thickening in DM animal models reduction and neointimal thickening [67]. These pathological changes disrupt vascular-osteogenic coupling by inhibiting microvascular neogenesis, ultimately leading to impaired bone regeneration. MSC-Exos can have vascular repairing effects. MSC-Exos enhances proliferation and migration of fibroblasts from chronic wounds in a dose-dependent manner [68]; BMSC-Exos promotes wound healing through upregulation of miR-21-5p, which leads to improved angiogenesis and fibroblast function [69], and the role of this miRNA in promoting fibroblast viability and migration has been independently validated [70]; compared to MSCs, MSC-Exos exhibits better stability, lower immunogenicity, and stronger receptor cell internalization [71], and MSC-Exos accelerates DM wound healing by promoting blood perfusion [72, 73]. Multicellular sources of Exos can exert synergistic effects. In addition to MSC-Exos, osteoblast-derived Exos is also involved in vascular-osteogenic coupling. Recently, it was found that Exos secreted by 3D cultured osteoblast cell line MC3T3-E1 cells stimulated by LIPUS could improve the vascularization of the bone regeneration microenvironment by activating the PI3K/Akt pathway in endothelial cells to enhance their migration, tube formation and VEGF expression [74]. Notably, angiogenesis regulation has a cross-organ common mechanism. In DM retinopathy, the Müllerian glial cell-derived Exos miR-9-3p significantly promotes retinal endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tube-forming ability by targeting and inhibiting the sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor S1P1 to rescind its inhibitory effect on VEGF receptor 2 activation [75]. While this pro-angiogenic mechanism of exosomal miR-9-3p has been elucidated in diabetic retinopathy, its direct role in the bone microenvironment remains to be experimentally validated. Nonetheless, it presents a compelling hypothesis and a potential engineering strategy for enhancing peri-implant vascularization. Meanwhile, adipose MSC-Exos enhance BMSCs osteogenic potential by activating the Wnt3a/β-catenin pathway [76], providing a new strategy for DM fracture repair. The above mechanisms suggest that MSC-Exos can ameliorate vascular dysfunction, primarily by counteracting high glucose-induced angiogenic inhibition and delivering pro-vasculogenic factors to enhance endothelial cell activity, thereby reinstating the critical vascular-osteogenic coupling necessary for bone repair. (Fig. 3). Targeted delivery of these pro-angiogenic Exos or their miRNA mimics via engineered scaffolds represents a key strategy to spatiotemporally control this critical healing phase.

MSC-Exos restore neuro-immune crosstalk to enhance osseointegration

Diabetic peripheral neuropathies (DPN) are one of the most common chronic complications of DM [77]. Neurotrophic regulation of bone metabolism can play an important role in bone homeostasis. However, hyperglycemia can affect peripheral nerve function, which may disrupt this regulation. Notably, nerve fibers are present in normal tissues in metabolically active areas such as the periosteum, cortical bone, and bone marrow cavities, where they are often accompanied by blood vessels involved in the regulation of bone metabolism and regeneration after bone tissue injury [78]. It was found that chemical severance of sympathetic nerves resulted in a significant decrease in osteoblast activity and a decrease in bone matrix deposition in the corresponding regions of bone tissue, suggesting that the sympathetic nervous system can regulate osteoblast activity and plays a crucial role in bone formation [8, 79]. SCs has a key role in peripheral nerve regeneration. High glucose levels adversely affect SCs apoptosis, metabolism, proliferation, and migration. The hyperglycemic microenvironment leads to the formation of AGEs, the end products of glycosylation, in the mid to late-stage of SCs, which impairs protein function and triggers a cascade of inflammatory responses, which can lead to additional oxidative stress and activation of NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammatory pathway induces cellular pyroptosis, leading to abnormal SCs function [80]. Notably, SCs pyroptosis plays a key role in hyperglycemic injury, and targeted inhibition of the gasdermin D pore-forming activity (e.g., via pharmacological blockade) has been shown to protect neuronal cell function [81], which provides a new strategy for intervening in the treatment of DPN. Studies have shown that MSC-Exos can inhibit the TLR4/NF-κB pathway by delivering specific miRNAs (e.g., miR-146a), which significantly reduces the rate of NLRP3 inflammatory vesicle activation and pyroptosis in SCs and restores its neurotrophic factor secretion function, and in the model of DPN, MSC-Exos engineered and modified with miR-146a accelerates nerve conduction velocity recovery and myelin regeneration [82]. Notably, the efficacy of MSC-Exos (e.g., via miR-146a delivery) in inhibiting SCs pyroptosis and promoting nerve repair has been demonstrated in models of DPN. Given the shared pathological basis of hyperglycemia-induced nerve damage, these mechanisms are strongly implicated in the impaired neuro-osteogenic crosstalk observed in diabetic bone, though direct evidence in bone models is an area for future investigation. In addition, MSC-Exos can scavenge damaged mitochondria and resist high glucose-induced oxidative stress by upregulating SCs autophagy activity [83]. In addition, bone marrow SIRT1-enriched MSC-Exos could significantly alleviate DM peripheral neuropathy and promote neural axon regeneration by protecting mitochondrial functions, such as maintaining membrane potential and reducing ROS generation [84]. The study reported that the Exos of SCs was combined with porous Ti6Al4V scaffolds and then implanted into femoral defects of New Zealand white rabbits, confirming that SCs-derived Exos effectively enhanced the bioactivity of titanium scaffolds, which in turn demonstrated its ability to promote the migration and osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs [85]. High glucose in the DM environment directly impaired the function of SCs and reduced the secretion of neurotrophic factor secretion, brain-derived neurotrophic factors secretion, and inhibits peri-implant osteogenesis via the miR-15b-5p/thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) signaling axis [8]. In contrast, MSC-Exos provides a new pathway for re-establishing nerve-bone signaling by repairing SCs function (inhibiting pyroptosis, enhancing autophagy) and delivering neuroprosthetic factors (e.g., miR-146a) [82, 83]. So the common basis of DM neuropathy and impaired bone healing is high glucose damage to peripheral nerves. In addition, an imbalance of the neuropeptides Calcitonin gene related peptide (CGRP) and human serum substance P (SP) released from sensory nerve endings impairs their ability to regulate macrophage polarization and angiogenesis [13, 86]. MSC-Exos have a significant effect on the release of the neuropeptides from sensory nerve endings. Exos has a regulatory effect on neuropeptides (e.g., CGRP, SP) released from sensory nerve endings. In a DPN model, MSC-Exos increased CGRP expression in dorsal root ganglia and promoted M2 polarization by inhibiting macrophage TNF-α secretion, while enhancing endothelial cell VEGF expression to improve neurovascular coupling [87]. Achieving such coordinated “neuro-immune-vascular” repair in diabetic bone may require sophisticated engineering to ensure Exos are delivered to and retained within this multifaceted niche. This “neural-immune-vascular” regulatory mechanism may synergistically promote bone healing microenvironmental homeostasis.

Metabolic-clearance reorganization

Mitochondrial dysfunction [88] and impaired autophagic flow [89] are not only core pathologic features of diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM), but also key components of metabolic clearance imbalance in the DM bone microenvironment. In neuronal cells, MSC-Exos improves mitochondrial function by delivering factors such as miR-21, and engineered preconditioning (e.g., hypoxia, drug stimulation) significantly enhances its metabolic regulation [83, 90]. It was shown that after DCM rats were treated with human umbilical cord MSC-Exos, the Exos significantly up-regulated the levels of phosphorylated p-AMPK and phosphorylated p-ULK1 activating the AMPK-ULK1 pathway, which inhibited excessive autophagy and attenuated autophagy imbalance in cardiomyocytes [91]. In the DM bone microenvironment, autophagic activity is generally inhibited and restoration of autophagic homeostasis promotes osteogenesis [35–38]. In DM complications, autophagy exhibits tissue-specific regulation: myocardial tissue often exhibits excessive autophagy, while bone tissue tends to exhibit insufficient autophagy. The inhibition of excessive autophagy in myocardial tissue by human umbilical cord MSC-Exos provides a mechanism reference for restoring autophagy homeostasis in the bone microenvironment. Hypoxia-pretreated BMSC-Exos can restore keratinocyte autophagic flow by delivering miR-4645-5p targeting inhibition of the HIF-1α/BNIP3 pathway, which provides a new idea for the intervention of autophagy disorder in DM bone microenvironment [92]. Animal studies have shown that peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR)β/δ can produce mTOR inhibition by up-regulating AMPK phosphorylation, leading to enhanced autophagic activity, which improves osteogenic differentiation of murine BMSCs and promotes bone regeneration in cranial defects under high glucose conditions in DM rats, whereas inhibition of PPARβ/δ attenuates murine bone marrow MSCs’ osteogenic differentiation with the addition of another AMPK inhibitor Compound C or autophagy inhibitor 3-MA inhibited the osteogenesis of murine BMSCs under high glucose conditions, suggesting that PPARβ/δ agonists promote the osteogenic differentiation of murine bone marrow MSCs through AMPK/mTOR-regulated autophagy [93]. The above mechanism suggests that human umbilical cord MSC-Exos may similarly regulate osteoblastic autophagy thereby alleviating oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage caused by high glucose environment, providing a precise therapeutic strategy for DCM targeting autophagy regulation. In addition, AGEs-pretreated MSC-Exos was enriched with miR-146a to inhibit vascular calcification in a TXNIP-dependent manner. TXNIP expression as an oxidative stress hub is elevated in high glucose environments, which then hinders osteogenic differentiation through inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway; therefore, AGEs-pretreated Exos carries miR-146a, which inhibits both vascular calcification and autophagy in a TXNIP-dependent manner. TXNIP-dependent manner simultaneously inhibits vasculopathic calcification and deregulates the inhibition of bone formation [94], while enhancing pro-repair capacity in a hypoxic environment, which provides a new strategy for synergistic intervention in DM vascular-bone coupling disorders. Engineered modified MSC-Exos delivering let-7f miRNA inhibited SDF-1α-mediated cell migration [95], and adipose stem cell Exos reduced podocyte apoptosis by promoting autophagic flow, which provides a reference for osteoblastic metabolic clearance [96]. These mechanisms collectively contribute to the reorganization of metabolic clearance in the diabetic bone microenvironment, wherein MSC-Exos restore autophagic flux and mitochondrial fitness in osteoblastic cells, thereby alleviating oxidative stress and supporting osteogenesis under diabetic conditions (Fig. 4). Reorganizing metabolic clearance through Exos likely demands engineering for sustained local release, ensuring prolonged modulation of autophagy and mitochondrial function in the hostile DM.

Potentiation methods for engineered Exos

In recent years, diverse engineering strategies have been developed to enhance the therapeutic efficacy and applicability of Exos for diabetic bone regeneration. These strategies can be systematically categorized as follows.

Parental cell pretreatment

Serving as the foundational approach, pretreatment of MSCs can pre-program the content and function of their secreted Exos. For instance, the overexpression of functional proteins (e.g., CD73, GDNF) or specific miRNAs (e.g., miR-21-5p) significantly enhances the regulatory activity of derived Exos and improves vascularization in diabetic wounds [7, 11]. Similarly, pretreatment of MSCs with metformin or under hypoxic conditions can enhance the pro-angiogenic and anti-inflammatory capacity of the resultant Exos, while also improving their performance in high-glucose environments by modulating cellular clearance pathways [7, 32].

Exosome-directed membrane modification

This strategy focuses on conferring novel targeting or delivery properties directly to the Exos membrane. A prominent example is the co-modification with the cell-penetrating peptide trans-activator of transcription (TAT) and the rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG), which targets the blood-brain barrier. This modification significantly enhances the ability of Exos to cross the blood-brain barrier, showing promise for treating cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury and offering new ideas for CNS-associated bone immunomodulation [97]. The use of targeted aptamers, such as those against Bone Sialoprotein, enhances the site-specific enrichment of Exos at bone tissue [98]. Furthermore, aptamer-mediated strategies can be designed to silence pathogenic exosomal miRNAs (e.g., miR-9-5p in osteosarcoma), demonstrating potential for reversing pathological microenvironments [99].

Optimization of cargo loading

Efficiently loading therapeutic molecules into Exos is crucial. Novel loading techniques have been developed that increase loading efficiency while better preserving exosomal membrane integrity compared to traditional methods like electroporation [100]. An alternative strategy involves using cholesterol-modified miRNAs, which can bind directly to the exosomal lipid bilayer, effectively avoiding membrane damage associated with physical loading methods [101].

Exos-material composite systems

The integration of Exos with biomaterial carriers has greatly expanded their application by enabling controlled and stimuli-responsive release. For example, phenylboronic acid-polyvinyl alcohol hydrogels can intelligently release Exos specifically in high-glucose environments, allowing precise regulation of the bone immune microenvironment in diabetes [102]. Other smart systems include matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9)-responsive hydrogels that dynamically release Exos in response to elevated protease levels in the diabetic wound microenvironment [103], and glucose fluctuation-responsive systems that sense changes in glucose concentration to optimize spatiotemporal release for bone defect repair [104]. Sericin/ε-polylysine hydrogels promote healing of diabetic-infected bone defects by slowing hexokinase 2 (HK2)-mediated glycolysis via a sustained Exos release, which in turn reprograms macrophages [105]. Surface modifications of implants also fall into this category; for instance, polydopamine-modified titanium implants can efficiently load M2 macrophage-derived Exos, enabling local delivery of miR-23a-3p to inhibit the NLRP3 inflammasome and enhance bone-implant contact under diabetic conditions [14]. Nanocarriers like zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) protect Exos from enzymatic degradation and enable pH-responsive release [106], while polyethylene glycol (PEG)/DNA hybrid hydrogels responsive to MMP-9 release stem cell-derived Exos carrying synergistic miRNAs (e.g., miR-126-5p and miR-150-5p) to promote vascularized bone regeneration [107].

Exos mimicry and membrane engineering

To overcome the yield limitations of natural Exos, biomimetic vesicles are engineered. One approach involves embedding liposomes with key exosomal membrane proteins (e.g., CD63, LAMP2B) and functional miRNAs (e.g., miR-21-5p), aiming to retain targeting capabilities while alleviating production bottlenecks [104]. Membrane stability and functionality can be further enhanced through chimeric copolymer modifications, which also enable stimulus-responsive activation [108]. Fusion of Exos with macrophage membranes enhances their chemotaxis toward inflammatory sites [109]. Engineering techniques derived from bacterial membrane vesicles (e.g., combining hypervesiculation with Sec secretion pathway targeting) provide valuable references for designing efficient mimetic delivery vectors [110]. Additionally, engineering Exos to overexpress specific miRNAs (e.g., miR-181b) can enhance their therapeutic function, such as promoting M2 macrophage polarization via the PRKCD/AKT pathway to significantly improve osseointegration [111].

Production process optimization and standardization

Ensuring the quality, reproducibility, and scalability of engineered Exos is paramount for clinical translation. The application of bioreactors for 3D dynamic culture of MSCs not only increases Exos yield but also enhances their purity [112]. Strict adherence to international standards, such as the Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles 2018 (MISEV2018) guidelines, during production is essential, combining these standardized protocols with single-particle analysis techniques effectively ensures batch-to-batch consistency in Exos composition, guaranteeing quality control [15].

Emerging frontiers in smart delivery

Looking forward, engineering strategies are evolving towards even greater sophistication and biomimicry. Emerging frontiers include the development of biomimetic delivery systems (e.g., cell membrane-camouflaged Exos for enhanced homing) and stimuli-responsive “smart” materials that release Exos on-demand in response to specific pathological cues of the diabetic bone microenvironment, such as elevated glucose, reactive oxygen species, or enzymatic activity [113, 114]. These next-generation approaches aim to move beyond passive delivery to create dynamically interactive, feedback-controlled therapeutic systems.

Towards clinical translation in oral and maxillofacial regeneration

The engineering strategies outlined above are not merely proof-of-concept but are paving the way for tangible clinical applications. The translational potential of MSC-Exos is being actively explored in oral and craniofacial contexts. For instance, preclinical studies have demonstrated that ADSC-derived Exos in hydrogel can prevent medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw by modulating local immunity [115]. More sophisticated designs, like MSC-Exos/ZIF-8 composite hydrogels, have shown exceptional efficacy in cranial defect models [116]. These collective efforts signal that the transition from sophisticated engineering strategies to targeted clinical applications for oral and craniofacial regeneration is actively underway, though challenges in standardized manufacturing and definitive efficacy trials remain to be fully addressed. The trajectory from foundational evidence to sophisticated application is clear. While a 2020 systematic review first quantified the significant pro-regenerative and pro-angiogenic effects of MSC-Exos in bone and periodontal defects [117], recent breakthroughs have focused on overcoming the diabetic microenvironment. For instance, a 2025 study employed an injectable thermosensitive hydrogel to deliver MSC-Exos, successfully promoting diabetic periodontal bone regeneration by regulating endothelial mitochondrial function [118]. This evolution from proof-of-concept to mechanism-driven, disease-targeted engineering exemplifies the field’s maturation towards clinical readiness.

Engineering strategies for enhanced dental implant osseointegration

The diabetic microenvironment poses distinct challenges to dental implant osseointegration, including chronic inflammation, impaired angiogenesis, and dysregulated bone metabolism. General Exos delivery approaches often prove insufficient in this context, prompting the development of engineered strategies specifically designed to localize and sustain Exos activity at the implant–bone interface. Recent studies illustrate several targeted engineering solutions. For example, Exos derived from normal SCs were shown to rescue peri-implant osteogenesis in type 2 diabetic rats by delivering miR-15b-5p, which downregulates TXNIP to alleviate oxidative stress and inflammatory suppression of bone formation [8]. In another approach, macrophage-derived Exos were hybridized with calcium phosphate nanoparticles [119], this composite not only stabilizes the vesicles but also enables pH-responsive release under acidic inflammatory conditions, significantly enhancing bone regeneration in diabetic models. Hypoxia-preconditioned BMSC-Exos have also been reported to improve diabetic osseointegration by promoting angiogenesis and osteogenesis through the miR-106b-5p/HIF-1α axis [120]. Furthermore, smart delivery systems that respond to pathological cues in the DM have been engineered: a blood-glucose-fluctuation-responsive hydrogel system was developed to controllably release exosomes from Smpd3-overexpressing MSCs, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation and suppressing inflammation under glycemic variability [121]. These examples demonstrate how engineering, through parental cell preconditioning, vesicle surface modification, and stimulus-responsive biomaterial design, can tailor Exos delivery to overcome the specific barriers of the diabetic implant site. Collectively, such strategies advance Exos-based therapies from general regenerative concepts toward precision nanomedicines capable of enhancing dental implant success in diabetic patients.

Challenges, future perspectives and clinical translation

Summary and core thesis

The failure of osseointegration in diabetes stems from a self-perpetuating vicious cycle, wherein immune imbalance (chronic inflammation) triggers and amplifies downstream pathologies in the vascular, neural, and metabolic compartments. This review has synthesized evidence that MSC-Exos represent a uniquely suited therapeutic platform to disrupt this cycle. Their inherent biocompatibility and multi-target cargo enable them to concurrently recalibrate macrophage polarization, restore vascular-osteogenic coupling, repair neuro-immune crosstalk, and reorganize metabolic clearance. Importantly, this native therapeutic potential is substantially amplified by smart engineering, including targeted modifications, stimulus-responsive delivery systems and cargo optimization, which transforms MSC-Exos from biological agents into precision nanomedicines.

Critical challenges and current limitation

Despite the remarkable preclinical promise of engineered MSC-Exos, their translation into clinical practice is hindered by several formidable hurdles that merit critical acknowledgment. First, manufacturing and standardization represent a primary bottleneck: scaling up the production of pure, potent, and consistent batches of engineered Exos in compliance with Good Manufacturing Practice standards remains a major challenge, and the inherent heterogeneity of Exos even from a single source further complicates quality control and regulatory approval processes. Second, pharmacokinetic and delivery barriers persist: ensuring sufficient Exos accumulation at the diabetic bone defect site via systemic or local administration is difficult, as the complex biological microenvironment may cause Exos degradation, while off-target distribution could introduce unforeseen safety risks. Third, critical gaps exist in understanding safety and immunogenicity: the long-term safety profiles of engineered Exos, particularly those with modified membranes or loaded with synthetic molecules, are largely uncharacterized, and the potential for immune reactions following repeated administration demands thorough investigation. Finally, the complexity of the diabetic disease microenvironment adds another layer of difficulty: the diabetic milieu may impair the functional efficacy of autologous MSC-Exos, and the optimal Exos source (allogeneic versus autologous), pretreatment strategies, and dosing regimens for diabetic patients remain to be clearly defined. Critically, these challenges are not merely biological but also biophysical and pharmacological. They underscore the imperative that advanced delivery and engineering are not ancillary but central to translating the potent biological mechanisms of MSC-Exos into effective, reliable, and scalable clinical therapies for diabetic bone regeneration.

Future research directions: from bench to smart therapeutics

To bridge the gap between proof-of-concept studies and clinical application, future research must shift its focus from merely describing therapeutic effects to rationally designing targeted solutions. Therefore, we propose convergent research pathways to address current limitations. For precise cargo control, moving beyond bulk Exos analysis, integrating single-Exos omics with high-throughput functional screening will be crucial for identifying precise functional miRNA biomarkers determining therapeutic efficacy, such as miR-125a-5p-rich Exos subpopulations. Concurrently, aptamer-mediated silencing technologies can be developed to actively neutralize pathogenic exosomal miRNAs within host cells in the diabetic microenvironment, including the miR-17-5p/Smad7 axis regulating disease progression. For intelligent delivery systems, next-generation biomaterials can evolve from passive carriers into microenvironment-responsive platforms. Dynamic hydrogels responsive to local glucose levels, pH shifts, or matrix metalloproteinase activity could enable on-demand exosome release, adapting to the evolving microenvironment conditions of diabetic wounds or implant sites while optimizing spatiotemporal drug dosing. For next-generation engineering strategies, biomimetic approaches can effectively overcome current technical limitations in Exos modification. Bacterial membrane vesicle engineering techniques, such as combining supervesicularization with Sec pathway targeting, offer valuable insights for achieving high-yield Exos production and efficient cargo loading. Furthermore, integrating CRISPR-Cas systems with Exos engineering may enable direct genomic or transcriptomic editing of recipient cells at bone defect sites, opening new frontiers in regenerative medicine for diabetic bone repair. Additionally, insights from the pathological microenvironments of other bone disorders can inform novel therapeutic strategies for diabetic bone defects. For instance, the discovery that exosomal miR-9-5p drives osteosarcoma progression via the MAPK signaling pathway demonstrates the potent disease-modulating role of specific exosomal miRNA cargo in bone disorders [99]. This finding suggests a reverse-engineering therapeutic strategy: employing silencing technologies such as aptamers to neutralize unidentified, harmful exosomal signals that disrupt bone regeneration within the diabetic bone microenvironment.

Roadmap for clinical translation

The final step toward clinical translation requires a structured, collaborative research and development approach built upon three core pillars. First is standardization, which mandates the widespread adoption of MISEV guidelines across all preclinical research stages and rigorous characterization of Exos. Second is advanced modeling, incorporating patient-derived organoids and humanized animal models that more accurately replicate the pathological features of diabetic bone complications, enabling more predictive efficacy and safety assessments. Third is modular clinical trial design, initial studies should prioritize localized, controlled applications, like Exos-loaded dental implants or injectable hydrogels for fracture repair, to establish robust safety profiles and proof-of-concept before advancing to systemic delivery strategies. The ultimate goal of this multi-pronged approach is to develop a universal platform technology that can be customized not only to improve osseointegration of dental implants in diabetic patients but also to treat non-union fractures, enhance prosthetic joint fixation, and optimize spinal fusion outcomes in this high-risk population.
