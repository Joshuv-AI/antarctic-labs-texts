Comments on “heavy metals and their relationships with lung function and airway inflammation: insights from a population-based study”
Authors: Tao Luo, Tao Chen
Published: 2025

To editor

We read with great interest the article titled “Heavy metals and their relationships with lung function and airway inflammation: insights from a population-based study” by Wang et al. [1]. This study systematically evaluates the relationship between multiple heavy metal exposures and both lung function and airway inflammatory biomarkers for the first time. Using advanced statistical modeling, the authors assessed the independent and combined effects of various heavy metals while conducting detailed subgroup analyses across demographic populations. These findings contribute important epidemiological evidence linking heavy metal pollution to respiratory health risks, with implications for developing respiratory disease prevention strategies.

The analysis accounted for demographic and health-related covariates such as age, sex, race, education, marital status, PIR, BMI, smoking status, and serum nicotine levels. Occupational variables—including job type and exposure history—were omitted from the model. Workers in high-risk occupations like mining, smelting, manufacturing, and welding often carry higher heavy metal body burdens [2]. These exposures may independently affect lung function and airway inflammation [3]. Unadjusted occupational factors could bias the observed association between heavy metal exposure and lung function outcomes. Future models should integrate NHANES occupational categories or industry codes alongside exposure intensity data to classify and adjust for these variables. For complex exposure patterns, subgroup analyses of high-risk occupations may elucidate how occupational factors mediate heavy metal effects on lung function. Including occupational variables would further disentangle the combined influence of environmental factors on respiratory health, enhancing both model validity and result accuracy.

The covariates adjusted for smoking-related factors included smoking status (current/former/never-smoker) and serum nicotine levels, yet these measures fail to capture the full complexity of smoking exposure. Classifying individuals solely by smoking status overlooks critical variations in intensity and frequency. Research shows tobacco contains elevated heavy metals—cadmium, lead, arsenic, chromium, and mercury—which accumulate at higher concentrations in smokers’ biological matrices than in non-smokers [4]. Smoking directly impairs lung function and triggers airway inflammation while simultaneously delivering substantial heavy metal doses [5]. This dual role complicates epidemiological analyses by making smoking both a potential confounder and mediator. Conventional covariate adjustment may be insufficient to distinguish smoking-related metal exposure from environmental sources, risking biased estimates. Refined smoking metrics—incorporating intensity, secondhand exposure, and validated biomarkers—should augment traditional pack-year and status variables to enhance exposure assessment. Advanced analytical methods like structural equation modeling (SEM), directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), or mediation analysis could better disentangle smoking’s confounding and mediating effects through heavy metals. Investigating smoking-status effect modification may further reveal whether heavy metal associations with pulmonary and inflammatory outcomes differ across population strata.

Multivariate linear regression assessed associations between five heavy metals and FeNO alongside pulmonary function parameters. Heavy metal concentrations were analyzed by quartile, with the first quartile (Q1) serving as the reference group for trend tests. The manuscript omits details regarding logarithmic or alternative normalization of the heavy metal concentration distributions. Since environmental heavy metal concentrations often display pronounced skewness, applying natural logarithmic or Box-Cox transformations before analysis would strengthen the statistical approach. The analysis should further report distribution characteristics pre- and post-transformation, including any enhancements to model fit.

The study fails to perform standard diagnostic tests for linear regression models, such as residual diagnostics, heteroscedasticity checks, or multicollinearity assessment via variance inflation factors (VIFs). These omissions undermine the validity of the regression outcomes and their clinical interpretation. Repeated testing of multiple exposure indicators against lung function and inflammatory markers increases the risk of false-positive associations. Correcting for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method would mitigate type I error inflation and strengthen result credibility. While the BKMR analysis modeled nonlinear effects, it lacked critical methodological transparency regarding kernel function choices, parameter settings, and sensitivity testing for nonlinear components. Proper BKMR implementation necessitates clear reporting of kernel specifications, hyperparameter values, and MCMC convergence metrics. Sensitivity analyses examining nonlinear term stability and model fit diagnostics would further substantiate these findings.

In summary, the recommendations presented in this letter aim to assist researchers in more comprehensively and accurately evaluating the relationship between heavy metal exposure and lung function, as well as airway inflammation, in future studies. Particular emphasis should be placed on methodological optimization regarding occupational exposure assessment, smoking behavior analysis, and statistical modeling approaches. Through more rigorous covariate control and model diagnostics, these measures will not only enhance the scientific validity and interpretability of research findings but also provide robust evidence for developing targeted public health interventions and respiratory disease prevention strategies. Consequently, this will contribute to improved population respiratory health and better prevention and management of related diseases.
