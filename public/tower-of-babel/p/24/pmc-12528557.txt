Comparison of different silane primer systems on composite resin bond strength to lithium disilicate ceramics
Authors: Necla Demir, Aybuke Orhan Uner, Sema Nur Ozturk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aims to evaluate the effect of different primers applied to HF acid-etched lithium disilicate ceramics on the shear bond strength between ceramic specimens and composite resin after thermal aging. 72 disc specimens with a thickness of 1.5 mm were prepared from three different types of lithium disilicate ceramics: IPS e.max CAD, CEREC Tessera, and GC Initial LiSi Block. The specimens were divided into six groups, with 12 samples in each group. The surfaces were treated with 9.5% HF acid. In Gluma group, Gluma Ceramic Primer and Gluma Bond Universal were applied in sequence. For Compo-B group, a mixture of Ceramica Silane Activator and Nova Compo-B Plus universal adhesive was applied, followed by Nova Compo-B Plus. After composite application, all specimens underwent 5000 thermal cycles. A universal testing machine was used to perform the shear bond strength test. Two-way ANOVA and Tukey HSD tests were conducted to compare MPa values according to the surface treatments. Statistically significant differences were found in shear bond strength values among the different repair systems applied to lithium disilicate ceramics (p < 0.001). The GC Initial ceramic group showed significantly higher bond strength compared to the other groups. The use of different primers significantly affected the shear bond strength of lithium disilicate glass ceramics. The immediate application of silane resulted in higher bond strength values in lithium disilicate ceramics.

Introduction

In recent years, aesthetics has become a crucial concept in dentistry. With advancements in technology, the development of new materials and techniques has made it easier and more feasible to enhance dental aesthetics and overall facial appearance. Aesthetic dentistry aims not only to improve the visual appeal of teeth but also their function. The use of indirect all-ceramic restorations has become increasingly popular among dentists, particularly as a means of compensating for the relatively poor aesthetic outcomes of metal-ceramic restorations (Gungor et al. 2016; Niaz et al. 2015). Today, due to their superior ability to mimic the appearance of natural teeth, dental ceramics have become the most preferred materials for fixed prosthetic treatments (Erturk and Dalkiz 2016).

The preference for dental ceramics is not solely based on aesthetics. Other important properties include color stability, high translucency, biocompatibility, impermeability to water, low thermal conductivity, and their ability to resist discoloration and foreign body reactions (Gehrt et al. 2013; Shillingburg et al. 1997). As a result, dentists increasingly prefer lithium disilicate glass ceramics, which contain 70% lithium disilicate crystals (Li2Si2O5), due to their high aesthetic qualities and resemblance to natural teeth (Duymus 2016; Bessa et al. 2025). However, issues with the physical and adhesive properties of ceramics still persist. One of their major disadvantages is their low fracture resistance. Parafunctional habits and trauma often lead to complications such as fractures and chipping in ceramic restorations, which shortens their lifespan (Meirelles et al. 2020; Gunduz et al. 2016; Jager et al. 2006). Studies have shown that damage in reinforced ceramics typically occurs as either chipping in the overlying porcelain or fractures that expose the underlying structure (Meirelles et al. 2020; Raigrodski et al. 2006).

Repairing damaged restorations instead of replacing them offers the benefit of less invasive treatment and creates a more economically sustainable treatment plan, which is crucial for patient satisfaction (Kaya and Sarac 2022). The effective use of repair systems in dentistry has thus become an important area of research. Repairs of fixed prostheses can be carried out intraorally or extraorally (Galal et al. 2024). Extraoral repairs involve the removal of the restoration, which is then repaired by a technician and recemented. However, this process risks causing damage to the tooth and surrounding tissues during restoration removal, and repeated firings can lead to further damage to the porcelain (Kaya and Sarac 2022; Kurt et al. 2013). Intraoral repairs, on the other hand, involve the direct bonding of the fractured piece or, if the fractured piece is not available, repairing the damaged area using composite resin after surface treatments (Pameijer et al. 1996). The bond strength between lithium disilicate glass ceramic and composite resin is influenced by the properties of the retentive area. Establishing a durable and reliable bond between dental ceramics and composite resins is critical. This bond is typically achieved through the following mechanisms: chemical bonding with a silane coupling agent and micromechanical bonding through techniques such as hydrofluoric acid (HF) etching, sandblasting, tribochemical silica coating, or roughening with diamond burs (Kaya and Sarac 2022; Abd Wahab et al. 2011; Hickel et al. 2013). Studies have shown that the combination of HF etching and silane coupling agents can achieve adequate bond strength (Ozcan 2002). The silane coupling agent improves the wettability of the porcelain surface, facilitating the resin's penetration throughout the entire surface (Blum et al. 2012).

The aim of this research was to determine the effects of two different silane application methods — single-bottle pre-hydrolysed silane and two-bottle silane hydrolysed during application — on the composite resin’s shear bond strength to lithium disilicate glass ceramic blocks after hydrofluoric acid (HF) etching and thermal ageing. The null hypothesis of this research posits that the type of silane application system used on lithium disilicate glass ceramic will not significantly influence the bond strength of composite resin to the ceramic blocks.

Materials and methods

In this study, 72 specimens were prepared from three different types of lithium disilicate ceramic blocs (IPS e.max CAD, Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein; CEREC Tessera, Dentsply Sirona, York, PA, USA; GC Initial LiSi Block, GC, Tokyo, Japan). Our in vitro study was carried out at Selcuk University Faculty of Dentistry Research Center. The blocks were cut with a thickness of 1.5 ± 0.05 mm using a diamond cutting disc (Buehler Diamond Wafering Blade, Series 15 LC, Buehler, USA) with a low speed sectioning device (Isomet 1000, Buehler, Lake Bluff, IL, USA) and samples were prepared in accordance with the block dimensions (1.5 mm × 12 mm × 14 mm). Gluma group

In accordance with the manufacturer's instructions, one drop of GLUMA Ceramic Primer (Heraeus Kulzer GmbH, Hanau, Germany) was applied to the specimen surfaces using a bonding brush (Flexible Bond Brush RLN37, Imicryl, Konya, Türkiye) and allowed to dry for 20 s. The surface was then dried with air–water spray. Subsequently, GLUMA Bond Universal (Heraeus Kulzer GmbH, Hanau, Germany) was applied to the entire surface for 20 s, dried with an air spray for 5 s, and polymerized for 10 s with a LED light curing unit (Valo LED, Ultradent Products Inc, USA).

Compo-B group

Following the manufacturer's instructions, one drop each of Ceramica Silane Activator (Imicryl, Konya, Türkiye) and Nova Compo-B Plus universal adhesive (Imicryl, Konya, Türkiye) were mixed in a mixing dish. This mixture was applied to the surface using a bonding brush (Flexible Bond Brush RLN37, Imicryl, Konya, Türkiye) and left for 30 s according to the manufacturer’s instructions, then dried with air–water spray. Afterward, Nova Compo-B Plus (Imicryl, Konya, Türkiye) was applied to the entire surface using the bonding brush and left for 20 s. The surface was dried for 5 s and polymerized for 10 s with a LED light curing unit.

After the surface treatments, the repair composite material (Nova Compo C, Imicryl, Konya, Türkiye) was applied to the specimens using a two-part mold with a diameter of 4 mm and a height of 2 mm. The bonding surface area of ​​the composite resin applied with the two part mold on the ceramics was found to be 12.56 mm2. The prepared specimens were subjected to thermal cycling (Nova, Konya, Türkiye) between water baths at 5 ± 2 °C and 55 ± 2 °C, with a dwell time of 30 s in each bath and a transfer time of 5 s, for a total of 5000 cycles. Before the shear bond strength tests, the samples were stored in an incubator at 37 °C (Nuve incubator EN 120) for 24 h. All specimens were then subjected to shear bond strength testing using a universal testing machine (Instron Corp., Norwood, MA, USA) at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with Minitab V14. The Shapiro–Wilk Test was used to examine the conformity of the data to normal distribution. Two-Way Analysis of Variance Test was used to examine the shear bond strength results according to group and subgroup. Multiple comparisons were made with Tukey Test. Results were presented as mean ± standard deviation. A significance level of p < 0.050 was considered to be statistically significant.

Discussion

This study evaluated the effect of different primers applied to lithium disilicate glass–ceramic blocks on the shear bond strength of composite resin. The results showed that bonding was significantly influenced by different primer applications, with the Compo B primer yielding better results in terms of average bond strength. Since different primer applications altered the bond strength of the composite to lithium disilicate glass ceramic surfaces, the null hypothesis was rejected.

Surface roughening techniques are effective methods for modifying the surface of ceramics (Pisani-Proenca et al. 2006). The success of surface treatments varies depending on the type of ceramic used (Neis et al. 2015; Kukiattrakoon and Thammasitboon 2007). After mechanical or chemical roughening, the application of silane has been shown to be highly effective in increasing the bond strength between ceramic surfaces and composite resin (Phoenix and Shen 1995). In order to standardize the surface treatment before etching, silicon carbide papers of different grits were used on the lithium disilicate specimens, which is one of the mechanical conditioning methods that create surface roughness in ceramics (Neis et al. 2015).

Given that the focus was on lithium disilicate ceramics and intraoral repair, HF acid etching was selected as the surface treatment (Capa et al. 2006). Colares et al. noted that the pretreatment of lithium disilicate ceramics with HF acid and silane is an alternative method for repairing ceramic restorations with composite resin; however, they found that sandblasting should be avoided as a surface treatment (Colares et al. 2013). Nagai et al. evaluated the effect of etching with orthophosphoric and hydrofluoric acid on the bond strength between lithium disilicate glass ceramics and composite resin, achieving the best results with HF acid followed by silane application (Nagai et al. 2005). HF acid etching is considered to be the most effective method for achieving high bond strength in lithium disilicate glass ceramics (Neis et al. 2015; Colares et al. 2013). For these reasons, HF acid was selected as the surface treatment in this investigation.

Lithium disilicate glass ceramics are the type of glass ceramics with the lowest amount of glassy phase and the highest silica content (Ucar and Kavrama 2021). Through the application of HF acid and silane, the glassy phase is removed, exposing the silica structure (Willers et al. 2022). Silane reacts with the exposed inorganic silica particles, increasing the wettability of the surface, and is subsequently applied as a chemical conditioning agent (Borges et al. 2003). Silane hydrolyzes the porcelain surface and is effective in penetrating and removing the glassy phase, making the silanization process a critical step in activating the substrate surface and establishing a durable bond with composite resins (Blum et al. 2012; Matinlinna et al. 2018; Kimmich and Stappert 2013). Hydrophilic monomers such as 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA) in bonding agents are crucial for the water in the repair area. HEMA absorbs and retains water, and the stoichiometric arrangement of silanes may interfere with surrounding water flow and absorption, affecting long-term stability (Lung and Matinlinna 2012; Staxrud and Dahl 2015). The presence of HEMA in the composition of Compo B could explain why the highest bond strength values were obtained with this primer.

Yoshihara et al. applied adhesive systems with and without silane to resin nanoceramic blocks and found that the group treated with silane had significantly higher bond strength values (Yoshihara et al. 2016). In this investigation, Gluma was applied as a pre-hydrolyzed one bottle silane solution, while Compo-B was a two-bottle non-hydrolyzed silane system. Pre-hydrolyzed silane solutions become cloudy and unusable over time after being opened, giving them a relatively short shelf life. This is because the most effective time for silane bonding is immediately after hydrolysis, as free silanol diminishes over time, reducing bonding efficacy (Lung and Matinlinna 2012; Hooshmand et al. 2004). The more recent two-bottle silane systems have a longer shelf life because the two separate solutions are mixed in the required amount just before use, allowing the silane to hydrolyze at a low pH (Lung and Matinlinna 2012). Lung et al. conducted a study similar to this one, comparing pre-hydrolyzed and non-hydrolyzed silane applications. Their results were consistent with the findings of this investigation (Lung and Matinlinna 2012).

The composition, microstructure, and mechanical properties of lithium disilicate ceramics can change with aging (Al-Thobity and Alsalman 2021). Alsulimani et al. reported that thermal aging has a significant impact on the bond strength of lithium disilicate ceramics (Alsulimani et al. 2024; Garoushi et al. 2023).

The amount of lithium disilicate crystals in the ceramic composition also affects bond strength. The proportion of lithium disilicate crystals is 90% in CEREC Tessera, 81% in GC Initial, and 80% in IPS e.max CAD (Alhomuod et al. 2023). The crystal sizes in the compositions are 1–1.5 μm in GC Initial, 3–6 μm in IPS e.max CAD, and 0.5 μm in CEREC Tessera (Alsulimani et al. 2024; Alhomuod et al. 2023).

The low bond strength values observed in CEREC Tessera ceramic specimens treated with Gluma could be attributed to the smaller size of the lithium disilicate crystals compared to other groups, making them more susceptible to the effects of thermal aging (Alsulimani et al. 2024). Ceramics with smaller crystals are more exposed to their surroundings and are more affected by temperature differences, making them more prone to degradation (Alsulimani et al. 2024; Yao et al. 2017). This finding is supported by Peumans et al. (Peumans et al. 2000). Changes in crystal content and crystallization parameters, as well as manufacturing techniques, lead to different microstructures in lithium disilicate ceramics, which affect their mechanical and clinical properties (Aboushelib and Sleem 2014).

In studies evaluating bond strength in intraoral repair of glass ceramics or porcelain, shear bond strength tests are commonly used (Matinlinna et al. 2018; Lundvall et al. 2009; Sadeghi et al. 2015). Similar to the findings of Angin and Tekce, the IPS e.max CAD material demonstrated the lowest bond strength value among the glass ceramic blocks used in this investigation (Angin and Tekçc 2024). The highest bond strength was observed in the GC Initial ceramic group. These results are due to the chemical compositions of the materials. Researchers have stated that the bond strength between the repair material and restoration should be at least 8–9 MPa, and that a bond strength of 20 MPa is considered clinically sufficient (Lundvall et al. 2009; Papia et al. 2014; Kalra et al. 2015). In this study, the bond strength value of the IPS e.max CAD material was 15 MPa with GLUMA application and 13 MPa with Compo B application, which were found to be lower than the optimal values reported in previous studies (Papia et al. 2014) (Table 2). These values are consistent with the findings of Duzyol et al. (Duzyol et al. 2016). The bond strength obtained from the GC Initial material was similar to the optimal value.

The higher bond strength observed in the Compo B group compared to the Gluma group may be attributed to the application of silane containing MDP and the activation of silane immediately before application (Alsulimani et al. 2024; Cardenas et al. 2017).

Limitations

This study faced several limitations. Firstly, shear bond strength was used instead of microtensile bond strength, which enables a more uniform stress distribution across the bonding surface. Despite this limitation, shear bond strength remains the most commonly used method for evaluating ceramic bonding. Secondly, thermocycling was employed as the aging method; however, other factors, such as masticatory load and pH fluctuations, could significantly contribute to bond degradation. However, the available literature on this subject is limited, and further research is needed.
