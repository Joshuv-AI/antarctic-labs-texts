Clinical profile and long-term outcomes of pediatric-onset Takayasu’s arteritis: data from a mexican national referral center
Authors: Dilcia Paola Pagoaga-Vásquez, Gabriela Melendez-Ramírez, Emilia Josefina Patiño-Bahena, Alfonso Buendia-Hernández, Edgar Samuel Ramírez-Marroquín, Juan Eberto Calderon-Colmenero, Solange Gabriela Koretzky, Guering Eid-Lit, María Elena Soto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTakayasu arteritis (TA) is a granulomatous inflammatory disease of unknown etiology that affects the aorta and its branches, including the coronary and pulmonary arteries. Diagnosis in pediatric age is late because the initial manifestations are nonspecific.MethodsObjective: Describe demographic, epidemiological, clinical characteristics, aortic and cardiovascular damage of patients with TA in pediatric age, and status in adult life. Retrospective study between 1988 and 2023. All children met the EULAR/PRINTO/PRES criteria for pediatric TAK. The records of symptom onset, type of angiographic lesion, surgical or interventional procedures, evolution, and status were reviewed.ResultsThe median was 12 years, with Q1-Q3 (8–15). Predominant symptoms were fatigue 29%, angina in 12%, Arterial hypertension in 53%, congestive heart failure in 12%, and stroke in 12%. The most common angiographic classification of Hata was type V, associated with a pulmonary artery lesion (V + P) in 6 (12%) and a coronary artery lesion (V + C) in 4 (8%). Forty-one patients (84%) reached adulthood; 5 (10%) discontinued care in childhood, so their prognosis is unknown.ConclusionThe timely identification of TA in children is a challenge that requires clinical art. Timely medical and interventional management allows a better prognosis and long-term survival.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12969-025-01145-9.

Background

Takayasu arteritis (TA) is a rare idiopathic vasculitis that affects the aorta and its main branches [1]. Over time, this condition has been referred to as aortic arch syndrome, pulseless disease, idiopathic aortitis, stenotic aortitis, osteoarthritis, and occlusive thromboarteriopathy; its current name is attributed to Dr. Mikito Takayasu, a Japanese ophthalmologist who first described arteriovenous anomalies in the retina in a woman in 1908 [2].

The incidence is 2/1 million per year, and the prevalence is higher in countries of Central and South America, Asia, Africa, and India. The ratio in Mexico between adult women/men is 6.9:1, and in pediatric series in Mexico, it is 4:1. In Korea, it is 4.3:1; in the United States, it is 3.2:1; and in South America, it is 1.7:1. The average age of onset has been between 10 and 15 years. However, there have long been and are currently reported cases in younger children [3]. TA is the leading cause of large vessel vasculitis in children and is associated with renovascular hypertension, with a mortality rate of 35% [4].

Patients with susceptibility to TA have been identified in genes encoding human leukocyte antigens (HLA), which regulate the immune response. In the Mexican population, there is evidence of differences in the participation of HLA-B39, B44, and B5 alleles in adults who have been exposed to tuberculosis, which has not been studied in children [5]. In the initial phase of aortic damage, exudative inflammation occurs in the vasa vasorum, media, and adventitia, involving the participation of proinflammatory cytokines, complement-linked receptors, monocytes, neutrophils, natural killer cells, gamma-delta (γδ) T cells, and CD4 and CD8 lymphocytes with cytotoxic activity [6, 7].

The predominant arterial damage in TA is stenosis and occlusion, which ultimately leads to ischemic damage to the organs, and the clinical manifestations typically appear late in the most advanced stages of the disease [8]. The initial phase is rarely identified as TA, and a high index of suspicion is required to determine whether a comprehensive evaluation using imaging is necessary, which is an essential tool during this period [9–12].

Arterial damage can be documented by echocardiography, conventional angiography, magnetic resonance imaging, CT angiography, Doppler ultrasound, PET-CT, or PET-RM, these studies can help us classify the type and extent of arterial damage, which has been agreed to be of six kinds, to which damage to the coronary or pulmonary arteries can be added [13]. Once the diagnosis of TA has been established, defining inflammatory activity is the next challenge. For this purpose, laboratory constructs and acute-phase reactants have been considered. However, current guidelines suggest the use of imaging techniques, including duplex ultrasound (DUS), PET-CT with FDG⁻¹ deoxyfluoroglucose, and magnetic resonance imaging, alone or in combination, to support treatment decisions and evaluate follow-up monitoring [14–17].

In the epidemiology of series reported in the literature, some studies have reported progress in the early identification of this condition in children, attributed to a better understanding of the disease among pediatricians. However, there are still reports of patients who have required immediate invasive treatment through surgery or dialysis, because the diagnosis is not established until organ damage appears, which represents the late stage of the disease.

[18]. The objective was to analyze our experience in the pediatric population treated with a diagnosis of AT, evaluating the most frequent clinical and medical management profiles over a long time to detect which areas of opportunity exist to improve the care of this vasculitis.

Methods

This was a retrospective study at the Instituto Nacional de Cardiología Ignacio Chávez from January 1988 to March 2020.

Participants

Patients under 18 years of age who entered the pediatric age group were included. A review of the clinical records of patients who met more than three classification criteria of the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) 1990 and EULAR 2022 was carried out [19, 20]. In pediatric patients, the EULAR/PRINTO/PRES criteria for childhood Takayasu arteritis [21] were used.

Recruitment of cases with Takayasu arteritis

First, Medical records were reviewed by a rheumatologist expert who found that all patients had PRINTO PRES criteria, and demographic and clinical variables such as the age of onset of symptoms, clinical manifestations, involvement of organ damage, type of arterial injury, type of treatment, causes of death, and search for cases that are now adults.

Classification of arterial damage by HATA: Type I affects only branches of the aortic arch. Type IIa affects the ascending aorta, the aortic arch, and its branches. Type IIb affects the ascending aorta, the aortic arch with its branches, and the descending thoracic aorta. Type III involves the descending thoracic aorta, the abdominal aorta, or the renal arteries. Type IV affects only the abdominal aorta or the renal arteries. Type V combines features of Types IIb and IV. In addition, coronary or pulmonary artery involvement should be indicated as C (+) or P (+), respectively [13].

Description of arterial damage by imaging

This was defined based on the evaluation of the patients through imaging methods, including echocardiography, tomography, or magnetic resonance angiography. The results reported by tomography or angiography include the type of vascular lumen lesion, such as wall thickening (wall thickness > 3 mm) [20], significant and non-significant stenosis (< 50% and ≥ 50%), occlusion, and dilation (increase ≥ 50% of the normal diameter). When more than one type of lesion was found in a segment, the one with the greatest impact was recorded (occlusion on significant stenosis). All CMR measurements were done according to the current guidelines [22, 23].

Demographic

Age, sex, height, and weight, as well as clinical data (age at diagnosis, symptom onset, disease duration, diagnostic delay, and comorbid diseases), and current treatment-related data were recorded.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: age over 18 years at diagnosis, presence of neuropsychiatric or musculoskeletal diseases, advanced pulmonary and cardiac insufficiency due to other etiologies, a history of malignancy, and trauma in the last three months.

Inflammatory activity of the disease

Patients were defined as having inflammatory activity according to the medical report recorded, where the physician could have determined the activity through elevated acute phase reactants, as indicated by the imaging report, or by using constructs such as the NIH or Dabague Reyes (Suppl Table 1), which include clinical, laboratory, and imaging data [24]. The National Institute of Health (NIH) determine inflammatory activity, by at least two of the following four criteria that were new or worsened since the last visit: (1) presence of constitutional features such as fever and arthralgias; (2) ESR greater than 20 mm/h; (3) features suggesting vascular involvement, such as loss of pulse, vascular bruits, limb claudication, blood pressure asymmetry between the upper and lower extremities, arterial tenderness such as carotid pain; (4) angiography compatible with vascular involvement of TA. Latent disease was defined as partial resolution of symptoms or signs suggestive of active TA. Disease remission was defined as similar or identical symptoms without demonstrable vascular progression [25]. We also considered when patients had positive studies for activity through imaging, either by FDG-PET/CT or MRA [26].

Statistical analysis

The data obtained were analyzed and expressed as medians and quartiles for variables with non-normal distributions. In categorical variables. The comparison between the two groups was conducted using the Chi-square test or the Fisher test, depending on whether the observed value was less than 5 in the 2 × 2 tables. Numerical variables, when presented with non-normal distributions, were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney test or the Wilcoxon test for comparisons involving more than two variables. The p-value was considered significant in the statistical analyses if the alpha error was < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed with the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS).

Ethical issues.

The ethics committee approved this study in accordance with the ethical guidelines for retrospective data analysis in clinical research (CI-039-2024).

Results

During the study, 318 records were obtained, and 49 patients met the clinical criteria before the age of 18. Forty-one were girls (84%), and 8 were boys (16%). The median age was 12 years, with Q1-Q3 of 8–15 years. The number of children with Takayasu’s arteritis admitted to the hospital for each decade was as follows: 1980–1990 (14, 28.6%), 1991–2000 (12, 24.5%), 2001–2010 (13, 26.5%), 2011–2020 (9, 18.4%), and 2021–2024 (1, 2%).

The diagnostic delay was calculated at 24 months, with a minimum of one month and a maximum of 288 months. Follow-up

The average age of patients at admission was 11 ± 5 years, and the average age at adulthood was 39 ± 13. The average survival delta was 28 years, which was statistically significant (p = 0.001) when compared to the age at diagnosis and the age at which they reached adulthood.

Of the 49 patients included, 42 (86%) reached adulthood; 2 (4%) patients died in infancy, and the outcome of 5 (10%) is unknown as they stopped attending as children. In total, four deaths occurred during the follow-up period of this series. The two who died in childhood and two who had reached adulthood and died. The cases that died are described in Supplementary Table 2.

Treatment

The treatment with which management was initiated is known according to various periods, whose use did not vary except for severe cases refractory to management with steroids and cases in which there was a requirement for cyclophosphamide (CFM) and biologics. In the years from 1980 to 1990, there were 14 patients, of whom five were treated with Prednisone (PDN) and Methotrexate (MTX), 3 with PDN + MTX and azathioprine (AZA), 1 with AZA + PDN, and 3 (CFM) + PDN + mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) and 3 with PDN + MMF. Between 1991 and 2000, 12 patients were treated, with 8 receiving MTX + PDN, 1 CFM + MTX + PDN, 1 MTX + PDN + AZA, 1 MTX + Pentoxifylline, and 1 MTX + PDN + MMF. Discussion

The onset of Takayasu’s arteritis in children has been reported in most series to range between 10 and 15 years of age. The overall diagnostic delay is 19 months; however, it varies. In this study, 15% of the total patients treated at the Institute experienced disease onset in childhood, which may have been underdiagnosed, as the initial symptoms of TA in children during the inflammatory phase can be challenging to distinguish from those of infectious processes. Furthermore, the interpretation of symptoms in children is often influenced by family interpretation, and most children are unable to accurately express their symptoms or their chronology. Our average was a median of 2 years, with a minimum of 1 month and a maximum of 24 years. This was longer than the average found by Ye Feng in 2017, whose average delay was a maximum of 90 days.

In our country, this population is generally initially treated in pediatric centers, and their transitional care into adulthood is sometimes poorly coordinated. We present a group of patients admitted to our institution because it is a national referral center and are referred for confirmation of their TA diagnosis, continued management, or because they present with cardiovascular symptoms from the onset, which today is a critical issue for survival in these rare diseases. In certain conditions, this crucial link is addressed to improve patient adherence, deepen understanding of the disease, ensure timely care, reduce follow-up losses, and enhance quality of life, which is currently being proposed [27, 28].

Clinical presentation in children

Although TA is rare, it can be life-threatening, so it is essential to recognize the symptoms early on. While high blood pressure is more common in children, there are recurring general clinical signs that can alert the doctor. In AT, these symptoms, although nonspecific, have been shown to recur. In this study, fatigue was present in at least 29% of cases, a finding like reports from other countries [29–33]. Other systemic symptoms, such as fever, headache, and anorexia, generally do not receive the necessary attention for this disease because they are confused with other conditions, delaying early diagnosis. The incidence of fever, weight loss, and myalgia in our country was low; in different series, rates ranged from 5 to 84%, 3–65%, and 1–44%, respectively. Therefore, questioning and investigating these symptoms in more detail could lead to the early recognition of TA, as it is a diagnosis that must be differentiated from infectious, autoimmune, or cancer etiologies [34].

We had two cases that were diagnosed in the early stages, when they only presented general symptoms and had high blood pressure. The imaging study requested in these cases was definitive, as they presented thickening without arterial occlusion, which supports current recommendations and guidelines for the diagnosis and follow-up of these patients [35].

There is evidence that early clinical manifestations in the inflammatory phase could lead to a timely diagnosis, especially when the inflammation only causes a moderate sub-occlusion. There are symptoms of organic, cardiovascular, neurological, or renal damage, which would justify complete imaging studies using MRA or CT in children, as recently proposed in the new criteria in adults to classify them as TA [20]. Cardiovascular damage is reported in all series, and high blood pressure is one of the most frequent manifestations, with reported ranges of 52 to 93% [36]. In Mexico, arterial hypertension in one of the series was reported in 85%, which is relevant given that of the nine children who died, two were due to cardiorenal damage and one due to intracranial hemorrhage secondary to hypertensive crisis [33].

In this study, 26 (53%) children presented with high blood pressure; 24 presented with initial manifestations, 6 had aortic coarctation, and 12 presented with renal artery occlusion—ten of these patients presented with aortic insufficiency, and 2 with progressive renal failure. Concerning our findings, we find relevant what was reported in a meta-analysis that found that there are differences in mortality between pediatric-onset TA and those that begin at the age where clinical manifestations such as heart failure and kidney failure are more severe in patients with pediatric-onset TA compared to those that start in adulthood, so it remains to be evaluated what factors between these age groups could define the differences since they are still not clear [37].

The prevalence of heart failure in Mexico is high; in a series before ours, it was 65% [33], in this series, it was 31%, which we consider may be lower because the evaluation of these patients has improved. The damage is identified through a more targeted follow-up to identify the problem.

However, cardiovascular disease and infections were the leading causes of death in both pediatric-onset and adult-onset TA failure, which is similar to what we found in our series, since those who entered in childhood and evolved to adulthood, the death outcomes are associated with hypertensive crisis and infection, which is generally associated with therapy that conditions immunosuppression.

Coronary and renal artery involvement has been reported as highly frequent in large series of children with TA [38].

We found only three cases with coronary artery damage. However, it should be noted that not all patients diagnosed with TA undergo coronary artery evaluation, so it is unknown whether these lesions are present at the time of diagnosis. In most series, infarction and chronic cardiac ischemia were not reported, and those that did represent between 2% and 5%. In our study, the percentage was 2%, suggesting that it may be underestimated. The presence of coronary artery damage in the pediatric population is known, and some cases become evident when they present catastrophic events or in autopsies [39, 40].

It is difficult to consider coronary artery involvement in children initially, and the detection of these in an advanced phase or sudden onset is only anecdotal.

Valve damage in children in Mexico was 41%, which is understudied in this population and is considered a risk associated with cardiac and renal damage. Therefore, identifying subclinical cases could be a challenge in the future for this population.

Cardiac damage in TA is very varied, and patients with TA have also been reported with non-hypertensive dilated cardiomyopathy with a prevalence between 4 and 6% [41, 42].

Dilated cardiomyopathy can also occur in children. Few series have emphasized this clinical presentation, which, although it may be secondary to a heart attack or other causes, can be the initial manifestation. In this series, we had two cases whose initial presentation was dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), and in one of them, Chagas disease was initially suspected; however, TA was confirmed. We identified DCM in 12% of cases; however, we believe it is underdiagnosed because there is no consensus on follow-up and the type of baseline studies to be performed at the time of diagnosis in these patients. Heart failure (HF) cases may go unnoticed, and the prevalence may also be underestimated [43].

Renal damage was very high in all series, and only in the US was it low [31, 33], the high prevalence of persistence and progression of arterial hypertension in children without early diagnosis leads to changes in cardiac remodeling and function, and many patients with TA have valvular damage and ventricular dysfunction with an estimated prevalence between 15 and 50% [41, 44].

In children with TA, a systematic cardiovascular study has not been established to search for left ventricular dysfunction, which is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in patients with TA in adult life. Systolic dysfunction VI appears to be multifactorial and conditioned by hemodynamic alterations from untreated hypertension, significant vascular stenosis in the renal, aortic, or carotid arteries, or aortic regurgitation secondary to aortic root dilatation and coronary artery stenosis [45, 46]. In this series, we found cases with slightly reduced heart failure, which was reduced by 6% and 20%, respectively. However, some studies have documented that it is a risk factor for mortality when it begins in the pediatric age [47].

This suggests that serial monitoring of these patients by echocardiogram should be part of preventive treatment, given that a high frequency of renal artery damage and systemic arterial hypertension has been found in them, which leads to chronic cardiovascular damage.

[48].

In this series, one case was treated with interventional management, whose current evolution is 5 years. One of the rarest manifestations in children is the one that occurs in the pulmonary arteries due to stenosis, dilatation, or the presence of aneurysms. Dr. Lupi reported in 1975 that 50% of the patients had pulmonary artery involvement. In this series, we identified 6 (12.2%) patients, and one of them had hypoplasia of the pulmonary ring, diffuse stenosis of the pulmonary trunk and its branches, with extension to segmental and lobar branches, as shown in the case of Fig. 2. Diagnostic delay when there is already involvement in this organ is related to the risk of developing pulmonary hypertension [49].

Therefore, we assume that this is because, in the initial study of these patients, there is no search extension to identify them, as the pulmonary symptoms are varied and can imitate other diagnoses, and are of low clinical importance for the initial physician to request further imaging studies in this context [50].

With the advancement of knowledge at the pulmonary level, it is now known that pulmonary artery disease is not rare, and up to 50% of patients with occlusive lesions of the artery develop pulmonary hypertension (PH) [51, 52].

The diagnosis of PH associated with Takayasu arteritis (PH-TA) is late, and this is regrettable since, theoretically, these patients could develop postcapillary hypertension and require hemodynamic evaluation by right heart catheterization.

The little clinical information associated with PH has been documented through case series and cohort studies identified by echocardiography [51–53].

However, these are generally incidental findings that are not always guided by a high suspicion of a lung problem. A well-established epidemiological case series needs to be included.

Neurological symptoms such as syncope, severe headaches, confusional syndrome, cerebrovascular events, meningitis, encephalitis, seizures, and ophthalmological lesions caused by hypertensive retinopathy occur in up to 20%. However, there are also other symptoms not related to hypertension or those caused by intracranial aneurysms [54, 55].

Arterial damage due to stenosis in the subclavian, carotid, and renal arteries has frequently been associated with neurological manifestations [56]. Although it has been defined that cerebral ischemia in TA is caused by the occlusion of most of the supraoptic arteries, in patients with hypertensive crisis, in a good percentage of patients, the occlusion is in the renal arteries, and the prognosis is catastrophic [57]. In this series, neurological damage has multiple facets and has been reported in most pediatric series, and it has been considered that timely defining arterial revascularization in sites involved with neurological outcomes could decrease the incidence of stroke; however, the procedures also entail a generally complicated risk when the patient with TA has multiple occlusive lesions, and the inflammatory condition is active [58]. Brain damage in TA can often be the result of silent arterial hypertension [59, 60].

On the other hand, the treating physician must have an excellent understanding of this vasculitis to conduct a targeted history. The medical examination should focus on the data collected during both the inquiry and the physical examination, and the patient’s training should be specialized [50, 61–63].

The importance of establishing an early diagnosis, both in the adult and pediatric populations, is closely related to prognosis. In large series with significant numbers of patients, both populations have been evaluated, and there is a high prevalence of cardiovascular, renal, and neurological complications. These findings highlight the crucial role that adequate knowledge plays in society and the medical community in establishing immunomodulatory or immunosuppressive therapy in the early stages of TA, as these measures can limit the spread and damage of the disease, regardless of the age of onset [64]. There is no precise information on the period that might elapse between this clinical inflammatory phase and the onset of fibrosis, which leads to the occlusive state. Therefore, devastating consequences are attributed to this lack of knowledge [65].

Laboratory and image

The lack of specific laboratory markers, and the lack of awareness of the problem even when signs and symptoms appear [25], however, an elevated CRP result, elevated ESR, thrombocytosis, leukocytosis, or anemia due to unexplained causes are essential data that could support the request for an imaging study [66].

Current classification criteria, which incorporate this consideration, provide evidence for early diagnosis, prompting us to consider strategies and implement changes. Currently, while acute-phase reactants remain of great importance in the early stages of the disease, they have proven to be of little use in follow-up. Therefore, the registry shows variability, as the very treatment patients receive can modify them. It has been decided to consider imaging studies in their evaluation at the time of diagnosis. This is because, while constructs for assessing activity, which include clinical, laboratory, and imaging data, have been proposed, these have proven to be inaccurate; however, in settings where imaging technology is scarce, they can be very useful [26].

It has been proven that noninvasive imaging modalities, such as CT angiography, Doppler ultrasound, magnetic resonance angiography, and positron emission tomography with 18 F-FDG, can identify the pre-stenotic phase [67]. However, few studies have aimed at establishing whether there is a relationship between a preclinical phase and its relationship with imaging findings. The new classification criteria in TA give great importance to imaging findings [68].

Clinical alerts have been increased to improve diagnostic skills in TA [69]. Emphasis is placed on key symptoms that may alert the physician and can be obtained through a targeted history to investigate patient data related to limb claudication, angiodynia, and chest pain or angina. These symptoms become important when these patients present with persistent or recurring systemic symptoms for prolonged periods. Likewise, during the examination, one should assess for decreased or absent pulses, bruits, differences in blood pressure between arms > 10 mmHg, and arterial blood murmurs that suggest arterial injury or aortic insufficiency.

In the occlusive phase of the disease, where it is more feasible to establish and classify the diagnosis due to the deterioration with which they present according to the affected organ, there may also be cases that are no longer treatable [70]. Catastrophic cases in adults support the importance of adequate follow-up to prevent organ damage, which increases morbidity and mortality [39, 71, 72]. Therapy

Therapeutic management is generally treated according to the complexity of each individual. However, in all cases, the primary objective is the control of the inflammatory process, and interdisciplinary clinical judgment plays a crucial role in making a timely therapeutic decision regarding intervention or surgery when, at the time of admission, there is already evidence of organ damage.

In this study, due to the variability in the time that each case was collected, we can define that the therapy mainly was always under conventional standards and according to the complexity of the patient where the use of steroids, methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil was the cornerstone for management and in severe cases the use of methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide. The use of biologics is relatively recent in our institution, and this may lead to improved prognosis and decreased mortality in the future; however, this remains to be evaluated in pediatric clinical trials. The use and management of the guidelines in our institution was recently implemented, and the long-term results remain to be assessed since, as we showed, one of the cases died of septic shock and was associated with the use of biologics. The factors that could condition this outcome are multifactorial and must be well clarified in the future with a sufficient sample of cases in children.

Endovascular intervention

In this series, 14 patients received interventional treatment: 7 (50%) survived, and 6 (43%) were unaware of their current outcome because they discontinued their follow-up visit. In three cases, the procedure was hybrid with surgery and endovascular intervention. The overall survival rate, considering the time elapsed between the last visit, was 7 years. Only one patient experienced a complication due to a hemithorax at the time of stent placement. His survival after the procedure was known for three years, after which he stopped attending his follow-up medical appointments. Currently, interventional treatment is the treatment of choice for vascular lesions. In this study, 14 (28.5%) of the patients underwent an interventional procedure, a lower rate than in other series. For example, Cilliers reported 76% interventional procedures, Clemente 29.6% angioplasty, and 14.1% bypasses. Balloon angioplasty is considered an effective method for renal artery stenosis in TA and has been suggested as a safe method for the treatment of renovascular hypertension in this disease [74]. The use of angioplasty in TA with PAH has been reported to be secure in adult patients in the long term [75]. However, its follow-up in children has been different, and few series mention it [50].

Surgery

Surgical management is one of the best options for treating organic ischemia and can now be performed in a hybrid format. The decision regarding each technique depends on the affected arterial site and the severity and risks of each patient. Long-term patency and durability have been demonstrated in subjects treated with surgery. Regarding interventional management, the risks are lower, and it has been successfully combined with immunomodulatory therapy [76]. In cases with brain involvement and inflammatory activity, a lower-risk option is intervention and control of inflammatory activity [77], which can also be a successful therapeutic support when there is coronary artery involvement [78]. In pediatric series, the results with both methods are satisfactory [2], as shown in this series. Therefore, we believe that the decision to use any technique depends on the individualized consideration of each patient, given the complexity of this vasculitis. Although management recommendations have already been implemented, the timeframe in our series is relatively short in cases that have been followed up after publication.

Infectious processes constitute a whole chapter and are another critical source of complications in children. The infectious process due to the use of biologics is controversial; however, in this series, several cases were treated with biologics.

Limitations

This is a retrospective study in which it was difficult to identify patients who did not return after a period of treatment at the Institute. This possibility could be because most of them have limited resources; many may have experienced complications or died, which is unknown, as well as to events that may have occurred during the pandemic. This pediatric population may seem small; however, this is known, given that prospective studies are not feasible for any rare disease. Retrospective studies are the most used, although they cannot evaluate disease progression and response to treatment as can be assessed in a prospective cohort, where parameters are designed to be followed from the beginning, and outcome variables can be measured longitudinally in response to treatment. Survival studies were not possible due to the number of cases that failed to attend follow-up and medical consultations. These patients could not be located to determine their status.

Conclusion

Clinical symptoms in patients with TA are generally related to the site of arterial damage, although the clinical profile is heterogeneous in children. In our population, abdominal aortic and supra-aortic trunk damage is typical in pediatric patients. The early inflammatory phase of TA remains poorly recognized worldwide and requires a high level of clinical suspicion to facilitate timely diagnosis and treatment. Our series confirms that hypertension in children is the most common clinical presentation accompanied by nonspecific systemic symptoms. Therefore, both findings together should lead to suspicion of AT, since in some cases it leads to renal and cardiac failure, with serious neurological repercussions.

Patients with TA who present in childhood and survive into adulthood present with severe cardiovascular, neurological, and renal damage. Therefore, their follow-up from childhood to adulthood should be considered for complex gynecological and obstetric events. Subclinical cardiovascular presentation is currently hypothesized, so ideally, periodic cardiac monitoring would be necessary to prevent catastrophic outcomes. If Takayasu’s arteritis is suspected, comprehensive imaging studies should be performed, including studies of the cerebral, coronary, and pulmonary arteries, to identify the type of underlying arterial damage and assess inflammatory activity. It is still unknown whether organ damage progresses or is present from the onset of diagnosis.

Noninvasive endovascular interventional treatment of arterial occlusions has been successful, even though it began recently in our series, and we do not have long-term studies of its efficacy. Surgical treatment was the method of choice in the early years; however, there are currently treatment recommendations and guidelines, which we are already implementing, and hybrid treatment offers satisfactory results. Global epidemiology has revealed similar findings; however, our findings suggest that there are still areas for improvement to achieve a better prognosis and survival in Takayasu’s arteritis that occurs in childhood.
