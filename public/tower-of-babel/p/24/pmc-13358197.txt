A Randomized Controlled Trial of Dual Upper‐Extremity Training [DUET]: Combining Neurologic Music Therapy® with Noninvasive Brain Stimulation for Upper‐Limb Performance in Corticobasal Syndrome
Authors: Kyurim Kang, Isabella Sterner, Shriya Bakhshi, Chris Bedell, Alexander Pantelyat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCorticobasal syndrome (CBS) is a progressive neurodegenerative condition characterized by asymmetric limb rigidity, apraxia, dystonia, and myoclonus, with no approved symptomatic or disease‐modifying drug treatments. Neurologic music therapy (NMT)® employs evidence‐based techniques targeting sensorimotor rehabilitation, and high‐definition transcranial direct current stimulation (HD‐tDCS) modulates cortical excitability.ObjectivesThe objective was to evaluate the preliminary efficacy of active‐HD‐tDCS + NMT versus sham‐HD‐tDCS + NMT for upper‐limb motor performance in individuals with CBS.MethodsIn this single‐center, sham‐controlled, triple‐masked randomized trial, 20 CBS participants received active HD‐tDCS + NMT (n = 10) or sham HD‐tDCS + NMT (n = 10) across six 30‐minute sessions over 3 weeks. Primary outcomes included Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT), Box and Block Test (BBT), and Purdue Pegboard Test (PPT). Secondary outcomes assessed CBS‐specific function, praxis, cognition, and anxiety. Linear mixed‐effects models examined the main and interaction effects of group (active vs. sham) and time (baseline, postintervention, follow‐up), with sex and years since diagnosis as covariates.ResultsWe found no significant main effect of group but a significant main effect of timepoint, with significant improvements from T1 to T2 and T3 on the BBT, PPT, and WMFT, along with reductions in anxiety scores. The WMFT additionally showed a significant group × time interaction, reflecting differing longitudinal patterns of change across groups. Significant within‐group improvements were observed in the active HD‐tDCS group (ΔM = 6.30 at T1–T2, P = 0.0002, d = 1.35; ΔM = 7.20 at T1–T3, P = 0.0001, d = 1.54), although between‐group differences at individual timepoints were not statistically significant.ConclusionBoth NMT alone and combined NMT + HD‐tDCS were feasible in CBS and were associated with improvements in selected upper‐limb motor outcomes, although findings varied across assessments. The results further suggest a possible additive benefit of concurrent HD‐tDCS and NMT, warranting larger controlled trials.Keywords: corticobasal syndrome, motor function, neurologic music therapy, neurorehabilitation, noninvasive brain stimulation (tDCS)

Corticobasal syndrome (CBS) is a neurodegenerative condition characterized by progressive, usually asymmetric limb rigidity and apraxia, often accompanied by dystonia, myoclonus, cortical sensory loss, and alien limb phenomenon, significantly impairing daily activities. 1 Currently, no approved symptomatic or disease‐modifying drug treatments exist for CBS, which makes it essential to evaluate nonpharmacological interventions for improvement in daily function. 2 , 3

Research over recent decades highlights music's therapeutic benefits. 4 , 5 In particular, Neurologic Music Therapy (NMT)® employs evidence‐based techniques targeting sensorimotor, cognitive, and speech rehabilitation. 5 Techniques such as Patterned Sensory Enhancement (PSE)® and Therapeutic Instrumental Music Performance (TIMP)® facilitate movement timing and improve motor control. 5 , 6 , 7 PSETM incorporates rhythmic, melodic, and/or dynamic patterns into patients' performance of specific motor sequences to mirror the functional activities of everyday life; for example, reaching toward an object could be enhanced through the use of rhythmic and melodic patterns, potentially leading to an increased range of motion and improvements in gross motor function over time. 5 , 8 For TIMPTM, playing musical instruments that are carefully selected and positioned based on specific targeted movements can facilitate functional upper‐limb movements and improve both fine and gross motor performance. 5

PSETM and TIMPTM are thought to exert effects through several neural mechanisms, including auditory‐motor integration, 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 temporal prediction, 12 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 reward and motivation19, 20, 21, 22, 23 and enhancement of neuroplasticity. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 These techniques have shown effectiveness in improving upper‐limb performance in stroke 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 and Parkinson's disease (PD). 36 , 37 , 38 Importantly, individuals with CBS experience similar (albeit gradually progressive, as opposed to acute and monophasic) network disruptions and upper‐extremity impairments (including rigidity, dystonia, and apraxia) as those with stroke. 39 The potential neural targets of NMTTM in cortical and subcortical regions, and its efficacy in stroke and PD, suggest potential benefits for CBS, which share similar clinical and behavioral features.

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) involves the noninvasive use of low levels of electrical current to modulate cortical excitability in specific areas of the brain. 40 Anodal tDCS applied to the left parietal cortex has shown promise in improving ideomotor apraxia in CBS. 41 , 42 When combined with conventional rehabilitation such as physical therapy, tDCS enhances motor training outcomes in stroke and PD by modulating neuroplasticity and strengthening motor network connections. 43 , 44 , 45 Recent studies have shown that pairing tDCS with speech therapy for primary progressive aphasia and with physical therapy for stroke 44 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 and PD 42 , 43 , 51 , 52 , 53 enhances clinical effectiveness compared to using either tDCS or rehabilitation approaches alone. One significant concern with traditional tDCS is its limited focal targeting caused by the large size of electrodes. 40 , 54 However, high‐definition tDCS (HD‐tDCS) utilizes multiple smaller electrodes, thereby enhancing the focality of stimulation in specific targeted regions. 55 , 56

This study investigated whether combining NMTTM with HD‐tDCS (dual upper‐extremity training [DUET] approach) can improve upper‐limb motor function in individuals with CBS compared to NMTTM alone. We hypothesized that the combined intervention would produce synergistic benefits, yielding greater improvements in dyspraxia severity and fine and gross motor performance.

Patients and Methods

Participants

Participants with possible or probable CBS according to established criteria 1 were recruited. The participants were carefully screened for eligibility to receive tDCS stimulation to ensure safety. 57 In addition, participants who were able to understand the assessment and intervention procedures were recruited. In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the study procedures were explained to all participants, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation in the study.

Study Design

This study was a single‐center, two‐arm, sham‐controlled, triple‐masked, randomized controlled trial. The participants were randomly assigned to either the active HD‐tDCS + NMT group or the sham HD‐tDCS + NMT group using a block randomization method (block size = 4) generated by A.P. The participants, outcome assessor, and neurologic music therapist were masked to group assignment. Each participant completed a total of 8 study visits, consisting of 1 baseline assessment (within 2 weeks prior to the start of the intervention), 6 intervention sessions (30 minutes, twice weekly over 3 weeks), and 1 follow‐up assessment (1 month after the final intervention session) (Fig. 1). Considering the progressive nature of CBS, participants could continue their existing rehabilitation services (eg, physical and/or occupational therapy), provided their regimens remained consistent throughout the study. This study followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines (Supplementary Material—CONSORT checklist). 58

Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines (CONSORT) flowchart.

As part of the broader study protocol, 59 electroencephalography (EEG) and motion capture analysis were also acquired during intervention sessions to evaluate neurophysiological responses and movement trajectories. This manuscript focuses on the voluntary motor performance outcomes.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine (IRB00303400) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05073471).

Study Protocol

Neurologic Music Therapy (NMT)®

NMTTM was delivered in 30‐minute individual sessions, twice weekly for 3 weeks, by a single board‐certified music therapist (MT‐BC) with NMT‐Fellow certification (K.K.). HD‐tDCS was administered concurrently throughout the active motor training period to pair neuromodulation with task‐oriented movement. Interventions incorporated metronome‐based rhythmic cueing and live keyboard patterns to structure spatial, temporal, and force‐related aspects of movement (ie, PSETM). Participants engaged in functional exercises and instrument playing (eg, mini keyboard for finger dexterity; percussion instruments, such as hand drum, djembe, and tambourine, for shoulder and arm movements) (ie, TIMPTM) (Fig. 2). Sessions followed standard NMTTM techniques but were tailored to each participant's functional needs. Functional needs were identified prior to each session using standardized assessments of fine and gross upper limb motor performance along with therapist‐guided evaluations of movement initiation, coordination, and range of motion. Individualization was limited to predefined parameters, including task difficulty, range‐of‐motion demands, tempo within appropriate therapeutic limits, and the relative emphasis on fine versus gross motor activities. To enhance intervention fidelity and reproducibility, core components of the protocol were standardized across sessions, including externally cued motor timing, repetition‐based practice of functional upper‐limb trajectories, and continuous motor engagement during the majority of the stimulation window to support activity‐dependent plasticity. The therapist also selected specific songs and musical patterns (eg, ascending scales for upward reaching) to facilitate upper‐limb movement. Live music was used, with the therapist providing rhythmic, spatial, and dynamic cueing on the keyboard while actively playing instruments with the participant.

Exercises addressed both fine and gross motor skills of the more‐affected and less‐affected sides. The selection of instruments and the balance between fine and gross motor activities varied across sessions based on clinical judgment. No additional music‐related practice was assigned between sessions. The intervention protocol report complies with the reporting guidelines for music‐based interventions. 60 A detailed example of the NMTTM protocol is provided in Table S1.

HD‐tDCS

Using the 32‐channel StarStim EEG–HD‐tDCS system (NeuroElectrics, Inc., Barcelona, Spain), stimulation‐targeted Brodmann areas (BAs) 1–6, associated with motor execution, planning, coordination, and somatosensory function (primary motor and somatosensory cortices and their association cortices), in the hemisphere contralateral to the more‐affected arm. A 32‐electrode StarStim cap was positioned according to the 10–10 system, with the scalp prepared using cleaning and conductive gels to reduce impedance. The electrode montage was optimized to maximize current delivery to target BAs while minimizing spread to contralateral homologous regions (Fig. 3).

Stimulation locations for left and right hemispheres (adapted from Kang et al. 59 ).

During active HD‐tDCS, a total current of 2 mA was distributed across 5 electrodes (3 stimulation, 2 return; stimulation area 3.14 cm2 each). Stimulation sites were centered over the primary motor and adjacent premotor/parietal regions. Stimulation was delivered contralateral to the most clinically affected upper limb. Full electrode coordinates and current distribution details are provided in Table S2. Current was ramped up and down over 30 s at the beginning and end of each 30‐minute session, respectively. In the sham condition, only the 30‐second ramping procedure was applied, with no sustained stimulation.

Data Collection

At baseline, participants completed the Gerdner Music Preference Questionnaire 61 and the Goldsmiths Musical Sophistication Index (GMSI) 62 to document their musical background and preferences. Demographic information was collected directly from participants, and detailed information regarding medications, disease characteristics, and symptom profiles was obtained from participants' medical records.

Primary Outcomes

The primary outcome was upper‐limb motor performance assessed with the Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT), 63 a standardized measure of movement quality and functional ability. The WMFT analysis was based exclusively on performance of the more‐affected upper limb. Fine and gross manual dexterity were evaluated using the Purdue Pegboard Test (PPT) 64 and the Box and Block Test (BBT). 65 WMFT, PPT, and BBT were administered at baseline (T1), postintervention (after 6 sessions) (T2), and follow‐up (T3).

To capture proximal, session‐level changes, PPT and BBT were additionally collected immediately before and after each intervention session. For PPT and BBT, scores were recorded for the more‐affected side, less‐affected side, and total.

Secondary Outcomes

Secondary outcomes captured disease‐relevant function, cognition, and affect. The Cortico‐Basal Functional Scale (CBFS) 66 provided a CBS‐specific patient/caregiver‐reported index of everyday functional abilities. Apraxia was evaluated using the Test of Oral and Limb Apraxia (TOLA), 67 a clinician‐administered assessment of praxis that includes oral–facial and limb gesture components. Global cognition was screened using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). 68 Anxiety symptoms were measured using the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI). 69 All secondary outcomes were collected at T1, T2, and T3, and the STAI was additionally administered before and after each NMTTM session. Note that lower CBFS scores reflect better daily activity performance, and lower STAI scores denote reduced anxiety.

Data Analysis

We assessed potential covariates for all outcome measures by fitting candidate models and comparing their fit using the Akaike information criterion. The covariates considered were sex, age, baseline CBFS score, and years since diagnosis. Models that included sex and years since diagnosis had the best fit for the primary motor outcomes (BBT, PPT, and WMFT), so these covariates were included in the final analyses. A summary of model fit indices is available in Table S3.

All outcome measures were analyzed using linear mixed‐effects models (LMMs) to account for repeated measures across timepoints, with random intercepts for each participant. Fixed effects included treatment group, time, and covariates (sex and years since diagnosis).

To explore session‐level changes, additional models examined a 3‐way interaction among treatment group, timepoint (pre‐ vs. post‐session), and session number (1–6) for BBT, PPT, and STAI, aiming to evaluate immediate within‐session effects and potential cumulative changes across sessions.

Exploratory analyses were performed to contextualize intervention effects: (1) independent effects of sex and years since diagnosis were evaluated across outcome measures to verify potential moderating influences; (2) a post hoc analysis correlating baseline phenotype severity (CBFS scores) with treatment response across all outcome measures; (3) specific CBFS items related to upper‐limb function (A3: eating task; A5: dressing; A6: hygiene; A7: handwriting; A8: doing hobbies and leisure activities) were correlated with upper‐limb performance‐based outcome measures (eg, PPT, BBT, WMFT) to explore their convergent validity and potential sensitivity to change.

Effect sizes were calculated as Cohen's d with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), reported as absolute magnitudes. Direction and statistical significance of change were conveyed by the estimated mean differences (ΔM), t‐statistics, and associated P‐values.

All analyses were performed using R Studio (version 2025.05.1). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (2‐tailed). To correct for multiple comparisons, the false discovery rate (FDR) method 70 was applied.

Data Sharing

Deidentified data and analysis code are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Results

A total of 22 participants with CBS were enrolled, and 20 completed the study. Two withdrew after baseline due to wrist injury and travel burden: active tDCS + NMT (n = 10; 7 females; age 65.52 ± 6.51; years since diagnosis 1.49 ± 0.78) and sham tDCS + NMT (n = 10; 5 females; age 69.98 ± 4.10; years since diagnosis 1.79 ± 0.67). There were no significant differences between groups in age, years since diagnosis, baseline MoCA, CBFS, handedness, and GMSI scores (Table 1).

Wolf Motor Function Test

We found a significant main effect of timepoint, F (2, 36) = 10.17, P < 0.001, and a significant group × timepoint interaction, F (2, 36) = 5.07, P = 0.017. No main effect of group was observed, F (1, 16) = 1.23, P = 0.285 (Fig. 4A). Pairwise comparisons with FDR correction revealed no significant between‐group differences at T1, T2, or T3. Within‐group contrasts showed significant improvements in the active HD‐tDCS group from T1 to T2 (ΔM = 6.30, SE = 1.48, P = 0.0002, d = 1.35) and from T1 to T3 (ΔM = 7.20, SE = 1.48, P = 0.0001, d = 1.54), with no significant changes between T2 and T3 (P = 0.547). The sham group demonstrated no significant changes at any timepoint (all P > 0.31).

Box and Block Test

For BBT total scores, there was a significant main effect of timepoint, F (2, 36) = 15.63, P < 0.001, but no significant effects for group, F (1, 16) = 1.72, P = 0.308, or the group × timepoint interaction, F (2, 36) = 1.22, P = 0.308. Pairwise comparisons collapsed across groups (FDR‐adjusted) indicated significant improvements from T1 to T2 (ΔM = 10.65, SE = 2.01, P < 0.001, d = 0.55 [0.09, 1.32]) and from T1 to T3 (ΔM = 8.25, SE = 2.01, P < 0.001, d = 0.43 [0.22, 1.17]), with no significant difference between T2 and T3 (ΔM = −2.40, SE = 2.01, P = 0.240, d = 0.19 [0.14, 0.52]).

For more‐affected side scores, there was a significant main effect of timepoint, F (2, 36) = 9.26, P < 0.001. No significant effects were observed for group, F (1, 16) = 0.66, P = 0.440, or the group × timepoint interaction, F (2, 36) = 0.37, P = 0.693. Pairwise comparisons with FDR showed significant improvements from T1 to T2 (ΔM = 3.75, SE = 0.86, P < 0.001, d = 0.43 [0.21, 1.18]) and from T1 to T3 (ΔM = 2.25, SE = 0.86, P = 0.019, d = 0.26 [0.39, 0.98]), with no significant difference between T2 and T3 (ΔM = −1.50, SE = 0.86, P = 0.090, d = 0.28 [0.05, 0.61]).

For less‐affected side scores, we found a significant main effect of timepoint, F (2, 36) = 14.09, P < 0.001. No significant main effects were observed for group, F (1, 16) = 2.48, P = 0.202, or group × timepoint interaction, F (2, 36) = 0.54, P = 0.588. Pairwise comparisons collapsed across groups (FDR‐adjusted) indicated significant improvements from T1 to T2 (ΔM = 5.80, SE = 1.16, P < 0.001, d = 0.47 [0.17, 1.23]) and from T1 to T3 (ΔM = 4.90, SE = 1.16, P < 0.001, d = 0.40 [0.25, 1.14]), with no significant difference between T2 and T3 (ΔM = −0.90, SE = 1.16, P = 0.443, d = 0.12 [0.20, 0.45]).

For PPT total scores, there was a significant main effect of timepoint, F (2, 36) = 9.89, P = 0.001, with no main effect of group, F (1, 16) = 1.29, P = 0.273, or group × timepoint interaction, F (2, 36) = 1.36, P = 0.273. Pairwise comparisons collapsed across groups (FDR‐adjusted) indicated significant improvements from T1 to T2 (ΔM = 4.25, SE = 1.01, P < 0.001, d = 0.51 [0.11, 1.25]) and from T1 to T3 (ΔM = 3.20, SE = 1.01, P = 0.004, d = 0.38 [0.24, 1.10]), with no significant difference between T2 and T3 (ΔM = −1.05, SE = 1.01, P = 0.303, d = 0.17 [0.16, 0.49]).

Short‐Term Changes

Exploratory session‐level analyses indicated significant within‐treatment gains across multiple outcomes. BBT total scores improved significantly over sessions, with sessions 4–6 outperforming session 1 (P = 0.02, P = 0.03, P = 0.001, respectively). BBT (more‐affected side) showed a significant main effect of session (P < 0.001); scores improved marginally from session 1 to session 6 (P = 0.06), with a trend toward a main effect of timepoint (P = 0.06). For the less‐affected side, significant main effects of timepoint (P = 0.03) and session (P < 0.001) were observed, with sessions 3–6 significantly higher than session 1 (all P < 0.05). For PPT, the more‐affected side showed a significant main effect of timepoint (P = 0.01), whereas the less‐affected and total scores showed significant main effects of both timepoint and session (Fig. S1).

Secondary Outcomes

TOLA_Limb and CBFS total scores showed no significant group, time, or interaction effects. STAI‐B demonstrated a significant effect of time (P < 0.001), but no group or interaction effects (Fig. 4A). Pairwise comparisons collapsed across groups (FDR‐adjusted) indicated significant reductions in anxiety from T1 to T2 (ΔM = −4.95, SE = 1.18, P < 0.001, d = 0.43 [0.21, 1.18]) and from T1 to T3 (ΔM = −4.00, SE = 1.18, P = 0.002, d = 0.35 [0.30, 1.08]), with no significant difference between T2 and T3.

Exploratory Outcomes

Exploratory analyses examined potential moderators and contextual factors. In the active group, males outperformed females on MoCA scores across all timepoints (d = 1.57–2.03, P < 0.05), with no comparable effects in the sham group. Similar but nonsignificant trends were observed for motor outcomes (eg, BBT, PPT) except TOLA_Limb at active T2 (P = 0.041).

Correlations with years since diagnosis revealed that longer disease duration was associated with poorer performance on MoCA at T3 in both groups (active: r = −0.553, P < 0.001; sham: r = −0.337, P < 0.01), and with lower baseline manual dexterity (BBT) (active T1: r = −0.349, P < 0.01; sham T1: r = −0.264, P < 0.05). CBFS total scores at active T1 were negatively correlated with disease duration (r = −0.284, P < 0.05).

Exploratory analyses revealed several moderate‐to‐strong correlations between baseline CBFS measures (total and subscores) and treatment response across cognitive (MoCA) and motor outcomes (PPT, WMFT), with correlation coefficients ranging from approximately |r| = 0.65 to 0.76. These findings suggest that baseline clinical severity may influence responsiveness to intervention, with more severely affected patients having a greater or lesser likelihood of improvement from NMT or from NMT + active tDCS, depending on the clinical assessment. However, given the small sample size and multiple comparisons performed for these correlation analyses, the results should be interpreted with significant caution. Complete results of the correlation analyses are provided in Figures S2–S10 and Table S1. CBFS item‐level analyses suggested potential sensitivity of these items to treatment‐related changes, particularly for hygiene (A6) and dressing (A5); however, these effects did not survive hierarchical multiple‐comparison correction. Detailed results are presented in Figures S11–S14.

Sample size estimates for future trials were determined based on the within‐group effect sizes observed for the BBT total score change across timepoints. Power analysis (G*Power, paired t test, α = 0.05) indicated that 22 participants were needed for 80% power to detect differences at d = 0.55 (T1–T2) and 35 participants for d = 0.43 (T1–T3). Within‐group effect sizes for all outcomes can be found in Figure S15.

Discussion

This DUET for CBS study provides preliminary evidence that both NMT alone and its combination with HD‐tDCS can enhance upper‐limb motor outcomes in individuals with CBS. Across behavioral measures, significant improvements were observed from baseline (T1) to postintervention (T2) and maintained at follow‐up (T3), indicating that rhythmically structured musical patterns and instrument‐based movement exercises designed to facilitate upper‐limb motor control elicited meaningful therapeutic effects, regardless of stimulation condition. The significant WMFT group × time interaction further indicated differing longitudinal trajectories of change, characterized by greater within‐group improvements over time in the active HD‐tDCS + NMT group. Although between‐group differences were not significant at individual timepoints, WMFT gains in the active group (6.3–7.2 points) exceeded established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds from stroke populations, 71 , 72 suggesting added benefit from concurrent HD‐tDCS. However, these MCID thresholds have not been established for progressive degenerative syndromes such as CBS and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. In addition, baseline imbalance across several motor measures, particularly the WMFT, should be considered when interpreting the observed group × time interaction; the active HD‐tDCS + NMT group demonstrated lower baseline impairment severity at study entry, though baseline group comparisons were not statistically significant. Although longitudinal mixed‐effects modeling was used to account for repeated measures over time, the relatively small sample size and pilot nature of the study raise the possibility that baseline differences may have influenced the observed trajectories. Consistent main effects of time for the BBT and PPT across total, more‐affected, and less‐affected sides further underscore the therapeutic value of structured musical training that integrates rhythmic and melodic patterns with active instrument playing, whereas reductions in STAI‐B scores highlight parallel affective improvements. These behavioral gains align with previous evidence that music‐based interventions may promote interhemispheric connectivity and motor learning through auditory–motor coupling. 19 , 29 The synergistic effects observed in NMT + HD‐tDCS in WMFT may be explained by complementary mechanisms of action. No significant group × time interaction was detected for BBT or PPT; however, given the modest sample size, these findings should be interpreted as insufficient evidence to detect an additive HD‐tDCS effect, rather than definitive evidence of no benefit. Although tDCS can prime motor cortical excitability and facilitate synaptic plasticity, 73 , 74 NMTTM can provide structured, rhythmic sensorimotor engagement that may optimize the brain's receptivity to neuromodulation‐induced changes at both cortical and subcortical levels. 75

Nevertheless, because this study lacked a no‐treatment or active control condition (eg, conventional occupational therapy—a nonmusical motor control condition), the independent effect of NMTTM cannot be fully determined. Future research incorporating appropriate control arms will be essential to delineate NMTTM‐specific mechanisms and evaluate its comparative efficacy within multidisciplinary neurorehabilitation frameworks for CBS.

Including sex and years since diagnosis as covariates was crucial for interpreting intervention effects. Although sex did not show a significant main effect, exploratory analyses indicated potential differences in cognitive outcomes that merit further investigation due to emerging evidence of sex‐based neuroplasticity differences in response to neuromodulation (see Fig. S11). 76

Exploratory analyses of CBFS data suggested that hygiene and dressing tasks may be more sensitive to intervention‐related changes than total CBFS scores. This highlights the challenge of capturing subtle functional improvements in heterogeneous populations and suggests that targeted subscales may provide greater sensitivity in early‐phase trials. This observation aligns with recommendations from the International Parkinson's and Movement Disorder Society Task Force on PD (including CBS), which emphasizes the importance of using domain‐specific outcome measures for this population. 1 , 77 Although improvements were observed in task‐based motor performance (WMFT), no significant change was detected in the overall CBFS score. Global activities of daily living (ADLs)/disability measures may require longer intervention periods or larger functional gains to demonstrate measurable change, particularly in progressive neurodegenerative conditions. The CBFS was included as an exploratory outcome, and subitem analyses were conducted post hoc. These findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis‐generating. Session‐level analyses further demonstrated that performance‐based measures were responsive to repeated practice, supporting their utility as proximal indicators of treatment effects and consistent with motor learning principles emphasizing task‐specific practice and performance feedback. 78

It is important to note that other measures more directly reflecting functional performance and daily‐life motor impact, including the CBFS and TOLA‐Limb, did not demonstrate significant changes across groups or timepoints. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence related primarily to specific motor performance outcomes rather than definitive evidence of a broader functional benefit in CBS. These differences across outcome measures may reflect varying sensitivity to short‐term intervention‐related changes, particularly in progressive neurodegenerative conditions where functional disability may be less responsive over relatively short intervention periods.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. In addition to the lack of a passive control group noted above, the modest sample size limits the precision and stability of effect estimates. Although clinical heterogeneity reflects the real‐world spectrum of CBS, it may have contributed to variability in treatment response. Furthermore, the single‐center design may limit broader generalizability. Larger, multisite studies are warranted to replicate the results, identify factors that moderate treatment response, and determine whether observed motor gains translate into improved daily functioning and quality of life. Finally, the short intervention duration (6 sessions over 3 weeks) may have limited the potential for sustained structural and functional neuroplastic adaptations. Although short‐term changes in motor performance were observed, longer and higher‐dose interventions may be necessary to induce durable effects in chronic progressive neurodegenerative conditions such as CBS. Future studies are needed to evaluate dose–response effects and longer intervention durations to assess whether motor gains translate into clinically meaningful improvements in ADLs. It is important to note that masking integrity was not formally assessed using a postintervention allocation questionnaire. Although sham stimulation included ramp‐up and ramp‐down procedures designed to mimic active stimulation sensations, expectancy effects cannot be fully excluded. Future studies should incorporate formal masking assessments to evaluate potential expectancy bias.

In conclusion, our findings provide preliminary support for the feasibility and potential efficacy of NMTTM, both alone and in combination with HD‐tDCS, to improve upper‐limb motor function in individuals with CBS. The observed effect sizes appear clinically meaningful, given the progressive nature of CBS and the limited therapeutic options currently available. 3
