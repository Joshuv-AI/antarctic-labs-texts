Unicorn: U-Net for sea ice forecasting with convolutional neural ordinary differential equations
Authors: Jaesung Park, Yoonseo Cho, Jong-June Jeon, Jinku Park, Hyun-Cheol Kim, Sungchul Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sea ice at the North Pole is vital to global climate dynamics. However, accurately forecasting sea ice poses a significant challenge due to the intricate interaction among multiple variables. Leveraging the capability to integrate multiple inputs and powerful performances seamlessly, many studies have turned to neural networks for sea ice forecasting. This paper introduces a novel deep architecture named Unicorn, designed to forecast weekly sea ice. Our model integrates multiple time series images within its architecture to enhance its forecasting performance. Moreover, we incorporate a bottleneck layer within the U-Net architecture, serving as neural ordinary differential equations with convolutional operations, to capture the spatiotemporal dynamics of latent variables. Through real data analysis with datasets spanning from 1998 to 2021, our proposed model demonstrates significant improvements over state-of-the-art models in the sea ice concentration forecasting task. It achieves an average MAE improvement of 12% compared to benchmark models. Additionally, our method outperforms existing approaches in sea ice extent forecasting, achieving a classification performance improvement of approximately 18%. These experimental results show the superiority of our proposed model. A preprint version of this work is available at this url https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.03929.

Introduction

Many studies have highlighted the close relationship between global climate change and the Arctic over the years, particularly emphasizing the role of sea ice1. The phenomenon of Arctic amplification, where temperatures in the Arctic are rising at a rate four times higher than the global average, underscores the Arctic’s position as an indicator of climate change2,3. The diminishing of sea ice has led to increased surface temperatures and lower albedo, a proportion of radiation reflected by sea ice, setting off a chain of positive climate feedback mechanisms that further fuel Arctic amplification4,5.

Forecasting of sea ice loss is crucial as it affects the local Arctic ecosystem and weather conditions in the Northern Hemisphere6,7. From the late 1970s to the mid-2000s, the Arctic experienced a rapid decrease in sea ice, with summer ice declining at rates up to 13% per decade8. As shown in Fig. 1, there is a clear decrease in June sea ice concentration between 1979 and 2020. With ongoing anthropogenic influence, this trend, coupled with the sharp reduction in multi-year ice and sea ice thickness, is expected to continue, indicating both a shrinking and thinning of the Arctic sea ice cover9,10. Sea ice forecasting involves two primary forecasts: sea ice concentration (SIC) at the pixel level and overall sea ice extent (SIE). In predicting sea ice accurately, it’s crucial to capture the detailed dynamic changes in geographical morphology while retaining both spatial and temporal contexts. Temporally, through the time series of average SIC, we can effectively identify overall patterns of SIC. The average SIC time series from January to December, as shown in Fig. 2, highlights a prominent trend, with its lowest point in August and peak sea ice concentration in December. Spatially, the patterns can be identified by visualizing the sea ice edge. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the sea ice edge in July undergoes inward during a decreasing trend or in October outward shifts during an increasing trend, while the interior remains relatively stable. Sea ice forecasting has relied on physical and statistical models designed to capture the inherent complexity of the phenomenon13–15. With recent advancements in deep learning, neural networks have emerged as a powerful tool capable of making predictions based on the inherent characteristics of the data16–18. IceNet19 is a notable example of this approach, successfully using deep learning to predict sea ice edges. Additionally, Ren and Zhang20 proposed integrating various spatial attention modules with deep learning models specifically for sea ice forecasting. However, these methods still have limitations: inefficient modeling of non-stationary time series and separation of spatial and temporal dependencies (an architecture with spatial attention followed by temporal attention).

We suggest a framework of models aiming to overcome the limitations of existing sea ice forecasting models, in spatiotemporal modeling through three approaches. Firstly, we introduce neural ordinary differential equations (NODEs)21 and ConvNODE, which integrates Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) with NODE. This integration enables the efficient spatiotemporal modeling within the bottleneck of the U-Net architecture. While existing methods with ConvNODEs have primarily focused on a static task like image segmentation22–24, our model extends the structure of ConvNODE for spatiotemporal forecasting. Secondly, to address the non-stationarity, we incorporate a decomposition method into the data input layer. Forecasting models utilizing the decomposition have enhanced performance by effectively extracting trends, particularly in non-stationary time series25. Finally, our model utilizes ancillary data, similar to IceNet, further improving forecasting performance.

Building upon these changes, this study proposes a novel SIC forecasting model named Unicorn (U-Net for sea Ice forecasting using Convolutional OpeRation Node). By integrating the ConvNODE into the bottleneck of the U-Net structure, Unicorn utilizes the local spatial context within the images, all while preserving the temporal order of the data input. In addition to its distinctive model architecture, we enhance performance by incorporating ancillary image data pertaining to sea ice concentration. Related work

This section presents various approaches to forecasting SIC, encompassing physical, statistical, and deep learning models. The prior works13,14 employed the Climate Forecast System, version 2 (CFSv2), which integrates atmospheric and oceanic dynamics alongside an interactive sea ice component, to forecast SIE. Nevertheless, these models relying on physics law entail significant computational expenses and often exhibit lower performance compared to statistical-based models. Statistical models, serving as an alternative to physical-based models, such as vector autoregression (VAR)26 and linear Markov model15, are utilized for forecasting SIC. However, statistical models have limitations regarding forecasting over mid or long terms due to their reliance on simplistic modeling approaches and the challenge of incorporating exogenous features.

Deep learning-based approaches for sea ice forecasting have been proposed to address these limitations. In initial studies, simple neural network architectures such as multilayer perceptron (MLP), recurrent neural networks (RNN), and CNN have been employed27,28. However, these models encounter challenges in effectively capturing concurrent spatial and temporal features. To address the need for spatiotemporal representation learning and achieve higher accuracy, Convolutional LSTM (ConvLSTM)29 and U-Net30 architectures have been employed. ConvLSTM integrates Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and CNN layers to facilitate spatiotemporal learning31. In the context of daily SIC forecasting, ConvLSTM-based models have demonstrated superior performance compared to CNN-based models32,33. Furthermore, Kim et al.34 proposed the multi-task ConvLSTM to forecast both SIC and SIE simultaneously. This multi-task model exhibits superior performance by leveraging the relationships between the two tasks. U-Net is initially designed as an encoder-decoder architecture for semantic segmentation for biomedical images. The encoder component maps inputs to lower-dimensional feature vectors by aggregating locally and globally spatiotemporal features. Subsequently, in the decoding step, these vectors are mapped back into the original feature space by exploiting the intermediate outputs of the encoder. Due to its superior performance in pixel-wise classification, U-Net has been applied to various domains beyond biomedical imaging, including weather forecasting35,36, and traffic forecasting37. In SIC forecasting, Andersson et al.19 proposed an ensemble architecture of U-Nets, IceNet, surpassing physics-based models’ performance while maintaining computational efficiency. Additionally, Ren and Zhang20 proposed SICNet, a U-Net-based model designed for the weekly forecasting of daily SIC, featuring various modules that enhance the capacity to capture spatiotemporal dependencies. To enhance spatiotemporal representation learning, Ren et al.20 employed an attention-based framework for SIC forecasting. However, these approaches do not explicitly address the non-stationarity in sea ice forecasting, such as trends and seasonal patterns.

Ahajjam et al.38 proposed a bi-directional LSTM integrated with variational mode decomposition for sea ice volume forecasting. Their approach effectively decomposes the input time series into the stationary components, enabling the Bi-LSTM to better capture temporal dependencies. However, this decomposition requires solving an additional optimization problem. In contrast, we employ a simple yet effective time-series decomposition method39. Methods

Dataset and preprocessing

This section demonstrates the datasets and preprocessing methods used in this study. We utilized three datasets: brightness temperature (TB), sea ice concentration (SIC), and sea-ice age (SIA) datasets. Among them, TB and SIA datasets are ancillary datasets that we selected to improve forecasting performance. The brightness temperature is one of the crucial factors in forecasting SIC because the SIC estimation algorithm is based on it40. Furthermore, we utilized the SIA dataset to account for the significant differences in thickness and geographical location (spatial pattern) of sea ice depending on its age41. All datasets are characterized by image data, which have different sizes (height, width, and channels). Thus, TB and SIA images are resized to match SIC images. Figure 4 visually presents all datasets and the entire data manipulation process preceding the forecasting model’s training phase. This preprocessing method in Fig. 4 ensures that both the spatial and temporal dimensions of the TB and SIA are optimally configured for integration with the SIC forecastings.

SIC dataset

This study utilized observational sea-ice data from June 22, 1998, to June 14, 2021. The weekly SIC, North Pole, and missing data information channels are combined into one channel, while the coastline and land mask channels are used to mask out continental regions.

TB dataset

The TB image dataset is sourced from the NSIDC43. To align with our primary focus on forecasting SIC, we preprocessed the spatial and temporal resolution of TB dataset to match that of our SIC image dataset. The TB dataset is sourced from two different sensors on DMSP. The data from the Special Sensor Microwave/Imager (SSMI) sensor on the F13 platform covers the period from 1995 to 2007, while the Special Sensor Microwave Image/Sounder (SSMIS) sensor on the F17 and F18 platforms covers from 2007 to the present. After extraction, we interpolated missing pixels using the nearest neighbor method. SIA dataset

Figure 3 illustrates the decreasing patterns of SIC, particularly highlighting the high volatility at the edges. This underscores the importance of categorizing the edge and center areas in SIC forecasting. However, accurately capturing the dynamic nature of the ice edge remains a significant challenge for models. To effectively implement dynamic edge segmentation, we leverage the distinct characteristics of sea ice based on its age and incorporate this information as ancillary input data.

The SIA image dataset is also sourced from the NSIDC44. In the SIA image dataset, pixel values range from 1 to 16, with each number representing a different age of sea ice. First, we categorized sea ice into two binary groups: first-year (FYI) and multi-year sea ice (MYI). Figure 5a presents an example of SIC image (left) alongside its corresponding SIA image (right). First-year sea ice (indicated in sky blue region in Fig. 5a), which is thicker than 30 cm but has not survived a summer melt season, is predominantly found along the edges of the sea ice region. This type is highly volatile and prone to melting during the melt season. In contrast, multi-year sea ice (indicated in the white region in Fig. 5a) has thicknesses ranging from 2 to 4 meters and has survived at least one melt season. It is mainly located in the central areas of the ice mass and exhibits minimal seasonal change. Figure 5b displays the average SIC values for MYI and FYI from 1998 to 2021, illustrating the distinct characteristics in SIC between the two ice types over time.

Due to these pronounced characteristics, which significantly impact sea ice dynamics, we selected to incorporate the SIA dataset as ancillary data in our analysis. Additionally, through statistical testing such as ANOVA, we validated the categorization into two groups. Encoder

Our proposed method is designed to integrate multiple pieces of information from various datasets. At the time point t, the encoder takes three images: the component of the SIC (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{T}_t$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{R}_t$$\end{document}), the TB (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{B}_t$$\end{document}), and the SIA (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{S}_t$$\end{document}). Because the channels of SIC and ancillary images have different semantics (time points in SIC and dimensions of variables in ancillary images), the contracting path of the encoder is split into two paths: the main path and the ancillary path. The main path takes the components of the SIC, and its architecture follows the typical type of the U-Net encoder. The main path comprises recursively stacked blocks combined by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3 \times 3$$\end{document} convolutions, batch normalization, ReLU activation function, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2\times 2$$\end{document} max pooling. The ancillary path takes the concatenated ancillary images, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[\textbf{B}_t; \textbf{S}_t] \in \mathbb {R}^{3\times (H \times W)}$$\end{document} and consists of the same blocks as the main path. The output of the main path is integrated with that from the ancillary path using a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 1$$\end{document} convolution. Consequently, the encoder seamlessly fuses multiple images, and the recursive fusions via \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 1$$\end{document} convolutions provide additional information to the upsampling process by concatenating copied features. Let the output of the encoder be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{z}_{t}(0)$$\end{document} at time point t, then the process in the encoder can be written as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}&\textbf{A}_t^{(1)} = Convblock^{(1)}(cat(\textbf{B}_t, \textbf{S}_t)), ~ \textbf{T}_t^{(1)} = Convblock^{(2)}(\textbf{T}_t), \textbf{F}_t^{(1)} = Conv^{(1)}_{1 \times 1}(cat(\textbf{A}_t^{(1)}, \textbf{T}_t^{(1)}))\\&\textbf{A}_t^{(2)} = Convblock^{(3)}(\textbf{A}_t^{(1)}), ~ \textbf{F}_t^{(2)} = Conv^{(2)}_{1 \times 1}(cat(Convblock^{(4)}(\textbf{F}_t^{(1)}), \textbf{A}_t^{(2)})) \\&\textbf{A}_t^{(3)} = Convblock^{(5)}(\textbf{A}_t^{(2)}), ~ \textbf{F}_t^{(3)} = Conv^{(3)}_{1 \times 1}(cat(Convblock^{(6)}(\textbf{F}_t^{(2)}), \textbf{A}_t^{(3)})), ~ \textbf{z}_{t}(0) = Convblock^{(7)}(\textbf{F}_t^{(3)}), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Conv_{i\times i}$$\end{document} is the convolutional layer with an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i \times i$$\end{document} kernel, and Convblock is a block composed of two \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Conv_{3\times 3}$$\end{document}, batch normalization, relu] operations. Additionally, cat is a concatenation operator along the time dimension.

ConvNODE

Most existing methods based on U-Net employ CNN layers in the bottleneck to capture spatial patterns, as their primary tasks (such as segmentation) are spatial in nature. However, our task requires modeling both spatial and temporal patterns within the bottleneck layer. As a result, conventional CNNs are insufficient for capturing the spatio-temporal dependencies required in our architecture. NODEs are effective tools for modeling dynamic systems in domains such as finance and physics, as they solve differential equations in a continuous manner. In this paper, we extend this approach by employing a variant, ConvNODE, which integrates NODEs with convolutional neural networks. ConvNODE effectively captures the intricate spatiotemporal dynamics in sea ice images extracted through the encoder. It estimates the temporal evolution of the latent variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{z}_t(\kappa )$$\end{document} with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa > 0$$\end{document}, which is derived from each component \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{T}_t$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{R}_t$$\end{document}. The estimation is carried out at the output image timestamps using the NODE framework, where the rate-of-change function is represented by a convolutional operation.

The theoretical foundation enabling this structure is as follows. By representing ODE as differential equations dependent on time k and solving them, we can derive changes in the variable over time. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa$$\end{document} be the current time point and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa +1$$\end{document} be the next time point. The rate of change function for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{z}_t(\kappa )$$\end{document} is denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f(\textbf{z}_t(k))$$\end{document}. Based on these definitions, the temporal evolution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{z}_t(\kappa )$$\end{document} can be calculated using ODE as follows:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{z}_t(\kappa +1) = \textbf{z}_t(\kappa ) + \int _{\kappa }^{\kappa +1} f(\textbf{z}_t(s)) ds. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}By defining (1), we establish a strong foundation for predicting the intricate dynamics of latent variables. The effectiveness of applying the NODE framework relies on selecting the appropriate rate of change function, f, for a given purpose. Based on this understanding, Lim et al.45 defined the function f as an affine function, such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f(\textbf{z}_t(s)) = \textbf{W}\textbf{z}_t(s) + \textbf{b}$$\end{document}, for time series forecasting of tabular datasets. To proficiently model the temporal dynamics of image sequences, we employ convolutional operations instead of the linear function f. It is recognized for its ability to adeptly encapsulate both spatial and temporal information within image and video datasets46. Aligned with the ODE approach, the temporal dynamics of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{z}_t(\kappa )$$\end{document} as a function over time are defined as follows:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{z}_t(\kappa +1) = \textbf{z}_t(\kappa ) + \int _{\kappa }^{\kappa +1} Conv_{3 \times 3}(\textbf{z}_t(k)) dk. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Through ConvNODE in (2), the latent variables are computed and passed to the decoder as inputs. This structure effectively combines the advantages of NODE and convolutional operations. Therefore, it accurately reflects spatiotemporal variations in latent variables extracted from sea ice and ancillary images.

Decoder

The decoder utilizes the outputs, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{z}_{t}(0), \dots , \textbf{z}_{t}(\tau -1)$$\end{document}, to forecast the future SIC from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(t+1)$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(t+\tau )$$\end{document} time points, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{Y}_{t}$$\end{document}. Contrary to the encoder, which has only two paths (main and ancillary paths), the decoder consists of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} expansive paths to predict \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} SIC images by leveraging the corresponding latent variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{z}_{t}(\kappa ), \kappa =0, \dots , \tau -1$$\end{document}. Contextual information within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{z}_{t}(\kappa )$$\end{document} is expanded by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2 \times 2$$\end{document} upsampling convolutions in each expansive path. Subsequently, it is combined with the output of the encoder through the enhanced skip connection (copy and concatenating) with ancillary information. After three upsampling processes, the output image has the same size as the original input, as follows :\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}&\text{ For } \kappa = 0, \dots , \tau -1, \\&\textbf{z}_{t}^{(1)}(\kappa ) = Convblock^{(8)}_{\kappa }(cat(upsample^{(1)}_{\kappa }(\textbf{z}_{t}(\kappa )), \textbf{F}_t^{(3)})), ~ {\textbf {z}}_{t}^{(2)}(\kappa ) = Convblock^{(9)}_{\kappa }(cat(upsample^{(2)}_{\kappa }(\textbf{z}_{t}^{(1)}(\kappa )), \textbf{F}_t^{(2)})) \\&\textbf{z}_{t}^{(3)}(\kappa ) = Convblock^{(10)}_{\kappa }(cat(upsample^{(3)}_{\kappa }(\textbf{z}_{t}^{(2)}(\kappa )), \textbf{F}_t^{(1)})), ~ \mathbf {\tilde{T}}_t = Conv^{(1)}_{3 \times 3}(cat(\textbf{z}_{t}^{(3)}(0), \cdots , \textbf{z}_{t}^{(3)}(\tau -1))), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where upsample is an operation to increase the spatial resolution of the input, performed using transposed convolution. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the sigmoid activation function for scaling outputs. Simultaneously applying the entire process from the encoder to the decoder with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{R}_t$$\end{document}, we obtain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\textbf{R}}_t$$\end{document}, and subsequently, the forecastings are calculated as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\textbf{Y}}_t = \sigma (\mathbf {\tilde{T}}_t + \mathbf {\tilde{R}}_t)$$\end{document}.

Training

This section introduces a training process for our proposed model. Many existing approaches utilized the MSE as a loss function for the forecasting tasks47,48. Because the SIC takes values in [0, 1] like a normalized pixel intensity, the binary cross-entropy (BCE) can be used for a dissimilarity criterion between two images49. Moreover, the prior study demonstrated that models minimizing the BCE tend to outperform others that minimize different loss functions, such as a mean squared error (MSE), particularly in tasks related to image reconstruction45. Given the target \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{Y}_t$$\end{document} and output \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\textbf{Y}}_t$$\end{document}, our model is instructed to minimize the BCE loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {L}$$\end{document} as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}(\Theta ; \mathcal {T}) = \sum _{t \in \mathcal {T}} \sum _{l=1}^\tau \sum _{i,j} \textbf{Y}_{t}(l, i,j)\log \hat{\textbf{Y}}_t(l,i,j;\Theta ) + (1-\textbf{Y}_{t}(l, i,j))\log (1-\hat{\textbf{Y}}_t(l,i,j;\Theta )), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Theta$$\end{document} is the entire parameters of the model, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {T}$$\end{document} is a time point set of the training dataset, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{Y}_t(l, i,j)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\textbf{Y}}_t(l, i,j)$$\end{document} denote (i, j) element of target and predicted image for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(t+l)$$\end{document} time point, respectively. The model parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Theta$$\end{document} can be updated by stochastic gradient descent-based algorithms.

Metrics

This study evaluates sea ice forecasting models through two tasks: SIC and SIE forecasting. For SIC forecasting, mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE) are selected as evaluation metrics. RMSE is widely used for evaluating the regression model, and MAE is considered a more robust metric against outliers. All metrics are computed using pixel values within non-land ice areas to ensure a precise evaluation specifically targeting sea ice. We denote \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {N}$$\end{document} as the set of pixel coordinates representing non-land ice areas in the images. Thus, MAE and RMSE between the ground truth (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{Y}$$\end{document}) and predicted value (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\textbf{Y}}$$\end{document}) are computed as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text{ MAE } = \frac{1}{\tau N|\mathcal {T}'|} \sum _{t\in \mathcal {T}'} \sum _{l=1}^\tau \sum _{(i,j) \in \mathcal {N}}\left\| \textbf{Y}_t(l,i,j) - \hat{\textbf{Y}}_t(l,i,j) \right\| _1, ~ \text{ RMSE } = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\tau N|\mathcal {T}'|} \sum _{t\in \mathcal {T}'}\sum _{l=1}^\tau \sum _{(i,j) \in \mathcal {N}} \left\| \textbf{Y}_t(l,i,j) - \hat{\textbf{Y}}_t(l,i,j) \right\| _2^2}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {T}'$$\end{document} represents the time point set of the test dataset, and N denotes the cardinality of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {N}$$\end{document}, i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|\mathcal {N}|$$\end{document}.

For SIE forecasting, considering the critical importance of accurately predicting sea ice areas, we utilize the integrated ice-edge error (IIEE) metric. We also employ the F1-score, accuracy, and mean intersection over union (mIoU), which are widely used in computer vision tasks. To evaluate SIE forecasting performance, both ground truth and predicted SIC images are transformed into binary images using a threshold of 0.15. Compared models

For comparative analysis, we evaluate seven benchmark models, including domain-specific ones, alongside our proposed model. Baseline models

CNN has been an effective baseline model for various image-related tasks and is often used in ice concentration forecasting studies as well. In this experiment, CNN is based on a model structure from previous research dedicated to predicting ice concentrations34. ConvLSTM combines effective convolution operations for feature extraction from image data with the powerful LSTM for time-series forecasting. This model is primarily utilized for predicting changing image sequences and is frequently employed as a baseline in ice concentration forecasting research. U-Net has established itself as an excellent baseline model with its innovative structure, achieving significant success in the field of medical image segmentation. Subsequently, it has been applied across various domains, including classification, enhancement, and forecasting, demonstrating notable achievements. Particularly, as mentioned in Section 2, many ice concentration forecasting models have utilized U-Net as their baseline model. DU-Net and NU-Net are specifically designed for comparison experiments in this study. While U-Net serves as a robust baseline with its encoder-decoder structure for sea ice forecasting, concerns are raised regarding its capacity to effectively capture temporal information. To address this, we augmented U-Net with a time-series decomposition structure. We incorporated two simple time-series decomposition methods proposed in the prior work39. The trend and residual components decomposed by the decomposition block are then processed through the U-Net structure. Then, they are combined, and passed through the sigmoid function to form the final forecasting results like our proposed model.

Our ablation analysis reveals significant insights into the contributions of each component within the Unicorn model. The removal of ancillary data had the most substantial impact, causing significant declines in both SIC and SIE forecastings. Specifically, MAE increased by 4.20%, RMSE by 3.55%, IIEE by 7.73%, mIoU by 0.69%, and the F1 score by 0.38%. As discussed in Section 3.1, TB and SIA datasets play a crucial role in supporting SIC forecasting by providing additional information, such as edge segmentation based on sea ice ages. The omission of ConvNODE from Unicorn resulted in notable accuracy losses, specifically a 1.30% increase in MAE and a 2.62% increase in RMSE for SIC forecasting. This demonstrates the essential role of ConvNODE in maintaining forecasting accuracy.

In contrast, removing the DCMP block led to a 2.26% decrease in IIEE for SIE forecasting. To delve into the efficiency of the DCMP block, we made a minute comparison of performances. Figure 13 displays the monthly average performance and SIC for highlighting forecasting performances in non-stationary scenarios. Unicorn shows the overall best performance in both metrics. However, Unicorn without the DCMP block still shows a competitive performance. It is worth noting that when there are decreasing and increasing SIC patterns in order, from June to October, the Unicorn outperforms its ablated model without the DCMP block. This highlights the effectiveness of DCMP under the distribution shift scenario.

These results highlight the essential role of each component in ensuring the model’s effectiveness across different conditions. The ablation study confirms that Unicorn performs optimally when all components are fully integrated.

Discussion

Our study presents a novel approach to sea ice forecasting from two perspectives. Firstly, we developed an enhanced ConvNODE model through local time series decomposition to account for the non-stationarity of sea ice dynamics. Secondly, we propose integrating additional information from brightness temperature and sea ice age. Through real data analysis from 1998 to 2021, Unicorn shows improvements, with a 12% enhancement in the average MAE for SIC and approximately an 18% improvement in the primary SIE metric IIEE. Because of the dynamic nature of sea ice, model performance along the ice edges is particularly critical. Therefore, we conducted rigorous comparative analyses, including focused evaluations on FYI regions and the ablation study, to explain the superior performance of the proposed method.

This advancement is expected to significantly improve the precision of sea ice forecasting and stimulate further research in climate science and related fields.

Although the proposed model achieves high-quality performance, it still has some limitations. One notable limitation is its high computing cost. Our model generates latent variables and images in multiple timestamps through respective upsampling paths. This results in a relatively higher number of trainable parameters, which scale with the forecasting horizon length (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document}). For example, Unicorn contains 9.8 million parameters, compared to 1.8 million in SICNet (CBAM) and 1.9 million in SICNet (TSAM). As a result, our model requires more memory and is less suited for long-term forecasting due to its computational cost. In terms of training time under the sliding window evaluation setting, Unicorn requires 234 minutes, SICNet (TSAM) also takes 234 minutes, SICNet (CBAM) requires 79 minutes, and U-Net completes training in 37 minutes. While our model is heavier than most baselines, its training time is comparable to SICNet (TSAM). Another limitation is the absence of explainability. Due to the nature of neural networks, explaining the outputs of the proposed model remains challenging. One possible solution is to incorporate an interpretable neural network module51. Another approach is to define the sea ice forecasting problem as a multi-task problem, encompassing both SIC and SIE forecasting, as demonstrated in prior work47. In this setup, canonical correlation analysis can be employed to extract canonical information from multiple tasks52, aiding in learning shared and task-specific representations and providing explanations for forecasting results.
