Kainic acid status epilepticus - induced changes in the hippocampus and hypothalamus alter the effects of exogenous Orexin-A in the hippocampus
Authors: Tsira Kapanadze, Ekaterine Bakuradze, Gia Kutelia, Butsiko Chkhartishvili, Nanuli Doreulee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined the effects of kainic acid–induced status epilepticus (KA-SE) on hippocampal and hypothalamic function, and evaluated the consequent alterations in the modulatory role of Orexin-A in the hippocampus. Behavioral experiments revealed cognitive and emotional impairments in KA-SE rats. In vivo electrophysiological recordings demonstrated a marked reduction in electrically evoked potentials (EEPs) in the CA1 region of KA-SE rats. Application of Orexin-A enhanced EEPs amplitude in CA1 and reduced it in CA3 of control animals, but elicited no effect in KA-SE rats. Orexin-A had no significant effects on the paired-pulse facilitation of EEPs in both the control and KA-SE groups indicating postsynaptic locus of its action. Morphological analysis demonstrated KA-SE–related neuronal loss in both the hippocampus and hypothalamus, including a significant reduction of GAD-positive interneurons in the CA3 of the hippocampus and Orexin-B–positive neurons in the hypothalamus. The observed reduction in GAD-positive neurons, which represent major targets of orexinergic input within the CA3 field, likely contributes to the impaired orexinergic modulation revealed in KA-SE animals. Our study reveals divergent orexinergic modulation in normal and epileptic hippocampal circuits, providing mechanistic insight into epilepsy-induced alterations of synaptic function.Keywords: Epilepsy, Orexins, Hippocampus, Hypothalamus, Kainic Acid

Introduction

Epilepsy is the most common neurological disorder, affecting individuals across all ages, races, and social classes (Neligan et al., 2012, Fiest et al., 2017). Its risk factors include neurodegenerative diseases (Cano et al., 2021, Vossel et al., 2017), brain trauma (Campbell et al., 2014), infections (Singhi, 2011), inflammatory processes (Mukhtar, 2020), genetic abnormalities (Wang et al., 2017), and unknown origins (Sarkis et al., 2019). Epileptic neural circuits show abnormal electrical activity characterized by an imbalance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission, primarily mediated by glutamate and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), respectively (Scharfman, 2007). Excessive excitation and/or reduced inhibition enhances neuronal excitability and promotes hypersynchronization (Engel, 1996), resulting in seizures that disrupt the function of brain structures involved in epileptogenesis (Bonansco and Fuenzalida, 2016). Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), which often originates in the hippocampus, is one of the most treatment-resistant forms of epilepsy (Rizzi et al., 2002).

Beyond glutamate and GABA, other neurotransmitters such as acetylcholine, noradrenaline, and dopamine contribute to epilepsy pathogenesis (Akyuz et al., 2021). The histaminergic, serotonergic, adrenergic, and adenosinergic systems also modulate epileptic discharges, and their function depends in part on the orexinergic/hypocretinergic system (Akyuz et al., 2021, Anschel et al., 2004, Mishra and Goel, 2019, Salgado and Alkadhi, 1995, Scammell and Winrow, 2011). Orexins regulate hippocampal synaptic plasticity through interactions with glutamatergic, GABAergic, noradrenergic, and cholinergic signaling pathways (Selbach et al., 2004).

Orexins (hypocretins), produced by neurons in the lateral hypothalamus, act via two receptor types, OX1R and OX2R, distributed in multiple brain areas including the hippocampus - a region essential for physiological processes of learning and memory, but also critically involved in the pathological mechanisms underlying epileptic seizure generation. In mouse hippocampus, OX1Rs are mainly expressed by mossy cells in the dorsal dentate gyrus, whereas OX2Rs predominate in dorsal CA2 and ventral CA1 fields (Cembrowski et al., 2016, Krause et al., 2024). Orexin-A enhances learning, memory, and seizure susceptibility partly through OX1R activation, which can induce long-term changes of synaptic responses in the hippocampus (Elahdadi Salmani et al., 2022). Furthermore, the orexinergic system influences energy metabolism, cardiovascular and neuroendocrine homeostasis, and sleep-wake regulation (Sakurai, 2003, Mondal et al., 2000, Inutsuka and Yamanaka, 2013, Li et al., 2017), and its dysfunction is linked to neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, and Huntington’s diseases (Berhe et al., 2020).

Studies on orexin’s role in epilepsy have reported conflicting findings, with both antiepileptic (Doreulee et al., 2009, Doreulee et al., 2010, Zhao et al., 2014, Toplu et al., 2023) and pro-convulsant effects (Erken et al., 2012, Roundtree et al., 2016) observed depending on experimental models and conditions. These discrepancies highlight an incomplete understanding of how epileptic pathology affects orexin signaling.

Kainic acid–induced status epilepticus (KA-SE) produces structural alterations that extend beyond the hippocampus. We propose that morphological changes in regions interconnected with the hippocampus influence synaptic communication both within hippocampal circuits and between the hippocampus and associated structures. The hypothalamus, particularly its orexinergic system, is a important regulator of hippocampal functions. To investigate the effects of epilepsy on hypothalamo-hippocampal interactions, we employed a KA-induced epilepsy model where we examine morphological alterations in the hippocampus and hypothalamus, their consequences for behavioral activity, and the resulting influence on orexin-dependent modulation of hippocampal neuronal function.

Methods

Animals

The experiments were performed on Wistar rats (weight 120–150 g) in full accordance with the legal and statutory acts applicable in Georgia and the international agreements ratified by the country, such as the Law of Georgia on Health Care, Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament and European Convention for the Protection of Vertebrate Animals Used for Experimental and other Scientific Purposes. Animals were housed in a standard condition (23 ± 1°C, 50–60 % humidity) in a vivarium room and maintained under a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle (7:00 A.M.–7:00 P.M. lights on). Food and water were available ad libitum. Experiments were performed during the light phase of the cycle between 10:00 A.M. and 5:00 P.M. All efforts were made to minimize the number of animals used and their suffering.

Experimental model

A rat model of temporal lobe epilepsy was created by a single intraperitoneal injection of glutamatergic agonist kainic acid at a dose of 15 mg/kg, according to the literature (Hung et al., 2024, Lucchi et al., 2024). Administration of KA induced behavioral manifestations of seizures, evaluated using the Racine scale: stage 1 – catatonic posture followed by facial myoclonus; stage 2 – masticatory automatisms, wet-dog shakes, and head nodding; stages 3–4 – rearing accompanied by facial automatisms and forelimb clonus; stage 5 – repeated rearing and falling (Racine, 1972, Rusina et al., 2021). Status epilepticus was defined as the occurrence of continuous or recurrent generalized seizures corresponding to stage IV–V of the Racine scale. Only animals with behavioral abnormalities resembling status epilepticus (n = 32 rats in total) were selected for further behavioral, electrophysiological and morphological study. Control group animals (n = 43) received intraperitoneal injection of physiological solution. The experimental design is graphically illustrated in Fig. 1.

Schematic representation of the experimental design.

Behavioral tests

All animals were pre-handled prior to behavioral testing. Open field (OF) behavior was tested for 5 days, T-maze and passive avoidance (PA) trials lasted for 11 and 5 days, respectively.

Open field

The animals were tested within 5 days under bright light conditions in the circular (diameter 1.5 m) OF using a 5-min trial per day (Mlsna and Koh, 2013, Hasim et al., 2022). The number of lines crossed, rearing, the number and duration of grooming, the number of boluses and the time spent in central section were recorded. The arena was cleaned with ethanol 85 % between the trials, next session was started after full evaporation.

T-maze test

To evaluate spatial memory, a spontaneous alternation task was used with a T-maze apparatus that had two arms (50 cm long, 10 cm wide, and 15 cm high) and one start runway (100 cm long, 10 cm wide, and 15 cm high). The rats were subjected to one session per day, consisting of 10 trials, for 11 consecutive days. Each trial comprised a forced choice followed by a free choice, with an intertrial interval of 30–40 sec. During the forced-choice phase, only one goal arm (randomly designated as left or right) was accessible and supplied with food; upon entry, the rat was held in that arm for 10 sec. before being returned to the start compartment. After a 30–40 sec. delay, the free-choice phase commenced, in which both arms were open. The correct response required the rat to enter the arm previously associated with food during the forced-choice phase (Cimino et al., 2023, Shoji and Miyakawa, 2019). An incorrect entry was recorded as an error, and the animal was returned to the start compartment without reward. The learning criterion was set as 1 error per 10 trials.

Passive avoidance test

The equipment for PA learning consisted of two differently illuminated chambers separated by a guillotine door. The floor of dark compartment was composed of stainless-steel bars and was equipped with electrical shock for aversive stimulation whereas the brightly lit one was free of it. Each rat was placed into the brightly lit compartment and the latency to enter into the dark compartment was recorded. When entering the dark compartment light electrical foot-shock was delivered (1.0 mA for 3 sec.) and then was returned to lit compartment within the next 10 sec. The experiment was conducted over five consecutive days, with each animal undergoing a 15-minute trial per day. In the brightly lit compartment the number of boluses and urinations, the frequency and duration of grooming, the number of lines crossed and rearing were also evaluated. During five-day OF and PA tests we monitor the frequency and duration of behavioral seizure manifestations.

Electrophysiological experiments

Electrophysiological studies began in 3 weeks after the injection of either KA (KA-SE rats, n = 13) or physiological solution (control rats, n = 11) and performed in vivo under general anesthesia with Calypsol (Ketamine, Gedeon Richter) at a dose of 100 mg/kg. Novocain was applied to the wound sites for local anesthesia during preparation procedures.

For the bipolar stimulation and unipolar recording of the neuronal activity the tripolar constantan electrodes were implanted in the strata pyramidales of the CA1 and CA3 hippocampal fields at the following coordinates: AP 3.8; L 2.8, H 2.8 mm and AP 3.8; L 4.1; H 3.7 mm for the CA1 and CA3 fields, respectively, according to Paxinos and Watson’s stereotaxic atlas (The Rat Brain in Stereotaxic Coordinates, 2013). The recording electrodes were fixed to the skull bone with dental cement. Indifferent silver electrode for animal grounding was attached to the occipital region of the skull.

Bipolar stimulation of the contralateral hippocampus was used for induction of electrical evoked potentials (EEPs). The intensity of stimulus to elicit threshold and maximum electrical evoked potential was determined in each experiment. The stimulus–response relationship was determined and the stimulating pulse's intensity had been selected during the experiment to generate EEPs with an amplitude of two-thirds of the maximum. Single rectangular pulses of 5–10 V intensity and 0.2–0.3 msec duration produced by a Master-8 Pulse Stimulator (A.M.P.I., Jerusalem, Israel) were used for this purpose, the Inter Stimulus Interval (ISI) was 15 ± 5 sec, and in each 10-minute interval 6 consecutive responses were sampled and averaged. The EEPs amplitude was measured peak-to-peak. Paired-pulse electrical stimulation (ppES) with 30 msec inter-pulse intervals were applied to produce paired-pulse facilitation of EEPs. For paired-pulse stimulation ISI was 15 ± 5 sec, in each 10-minute interval 4 consecutive responses were sampled and averaged. The paired-pulse facilitation ratio was determined by dividing the amplitude of the second response by the amplitude of the first response.

The effects of Orexins (A and B) (Sigma Aldrich 06012/06262) on EEPs in both hippocampal fields were evaluated. Orexin (or saline in the control experiments) were injected with a chemitrode into the lateral ventricle through the hole made in the cranial bone according to stereotaxic coordinates AP −0,3 mm; L 1.2 mm; H 3.5 mm. Orexin-A or Orexin-B (10 µg in 10 µL) (Hagan et al., 1999, Toth et al., 2012) was administered I.C.V. in two divided doses over approximately 20 min (0.5–1 µL/min) to ensure gentle delivery, optimize diffusion, and minimize intracranial pressure fluctuations.

Data were amplified (10 ×) and filtered (1 kHz), by using an amplifier INTRACELLULAR PRE (S/N:010711, USA). Signals were passed through an analogue-to-digital interface (ADInstruments ML866 PowerLab 4/30, Australia) digitized at a sampling rate of 1 kHz and analyzed by means of software Chart5,5 with spike histogram extension; The data were statistically analyzed via GraphPad Prism and presented as the mean values and standard errors of mean, statistical analysis were done using unpaired Student’s t-test, two-way ANOVA, one-way ANOVA with appropriate post hoc test and a value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

At the end of each experiment the brain tissue around the implanted electrodes was coagulated by passing a strong current. The brain was removed, fixed in the 5 % formalin and cut into 20 µm slices. The location of the coagulated tissue in the hippocampal fields was visualized by microscopic examination of brain slices (Fig. 2).

Morphological study

Tissue fixation and preparation

For morphological analysis, brain material was examined from 20 control and 19 KA-SE animals. After 16th day of KA/saline injection, rats were decapitated under anesthesia, brains were removed and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde solution prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffered saline pH= 7.4. After fixation, the tissue sections were dehydrated by passing through in increasing concentration of alcohol (70 %–30 min, 80 %–30 min, 96 %–30 min). Afterwards, tissues were placed in acetone three times for 20 min each, acetone-benzene (1:1) for 30 min, benzene three times for 20 min each. Samples then were embedded into paraffin blocks, sectioned by Leica microtome (thickness of sections −5 µm) and stained with routine (H&E) and immunohistochemical protocols (Cardiff et al., 2014).

Immunohistochemical staining

Anti-GAD-65/67 (Sigma Aldrich G5163) were used to detect GABA-positive cells in the hippocampus. Blocking of endogenous peroxidase with 3 % hydrogen peroxide was performed on dewaxed and rehydrated slides for 10 min. Slides were incubated at 37°C for 30 min (2 % BSA (Sigma A9647) + 2.5 % normal goat serum (Sigma Aldrich G9023) + 0.5 % Triton X-100 (Sigma Aldrich T9284), then were covered with anti-GAD65/67 solution (dilution 1:1000 (sigma G5163)) and incubated at + 40C for 20–24 h. After rinsed by PBS slides were incubated in biotinylated anti-Rabbit IgG solution (dilution 1:400, (sigma B8895)) at room temperature for 2 h. Immunoreactivity was visualized by adding the extravidin-peroxidase solution (sigma E2886, dilution 1:200) for 30 min at room temperature proceeded with the application of 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (SIGMAFAST™ DAB (D4418) as chromogen.

For detection of Orexinergic cells in hypothalamus Goat anti orexin A antibodies (Santa Crus biotechnology) were used. After blocking of endogenous peroxidase with 3 % hydrogen peroxide, preincubation with 2 % BSA (2 %BSA (Sigma A9647) + 2.5 % normal goat serum (Sigma Aldrich G9023) + 0.5 % Triton X-100 (Sigma Aldrich T9284) was performed at 37°C for 30 min. Primary antibody (Goat anti orexin A, Santa Crus biotechnology) solution (dilution 1:1000) was added for 24 h at + 40C temperature, then secondary antibody solution (Swine anti goat IgG Santa Crus biotechnology) (dilution 1:400) for 2 h at room temperature. After extravidin-peroxidase solution (10 mg/ml) was added to the slides for 30 min at room temperature. For detection of stained cells 3,3’-diaminobenzidine was added as chromogen. Meyer's hematoxylin was used to contrast intracellular structures.

For evaluation of GAD positive cells in hippocampus 3 sections from each animal (N = 7 for control and N = 12 for KA-SE) were used. In each field of hippocampus (CA1, CA3, DG) 5 field of vision (Field of Vision (FOV) 156µm2 (12,5 µm x 12,5 µm) were counted. The percentages of GAD positive neurons relative to the total number of neurons were calculated for each rat. Results were grouped pre animal and the mean ± SEM were calculated for each group. The number of GAD positive cells was calculated as percentage (%) (Vadachkoria et al., 2011, Dzidziguri et al., 2018).

The neuroanatomical location of orexinergic neurons in the PeF-LH was based on the stereotaxic rat brain atlas of Paxinos and Watson at antero-posterior coordinates Bregma −3 mm −3.6 mm (The Rat Brain in Stereotaxic Coordinates, 2013).

Along with comparing our own sections with the atlas, the third ventricle and MFb-s were used as a reference as it is known that orexinergic cells are mostly located in the PeFLH area adjacent to the MFb. Sections were taken according to the Bregma coordinate, then stained, and after confirming the accuracy of the search area, the sectioning and analysis continued.

Neurons were counted in the LH bilaterally in 3 consecutive sections (20–30μm distance between consecutive sections). In each section, stained cells were counted under magnification 450X within five field of vision (Field of Vision (FOV) 156µm2 (12,5 µm x12,5 µm). The number of orexinergic neurons per 1 field were calculated for each rat. The mean ± SEM were calculated for control and KA-SE groups. The microphotographs obtained with the digital camera (Olympus c4040).

Results

Kainic acid status epilepticus -associated changes in behavior and learning

The rats with KA-SE showed significant reduction in the total number (0.6 ± 0.22, (n = 30) vs 3.24 ± 0.57, (n = 25) in the control group, P < 0.005) and duration of grooming (4.8 ± 2.58 sec, (n = 30) vs 36.3 ± 3.4 sec, (n = 25) in control, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3 A, B). In the OF test's the number of inner square crossing is raised in KA-SE animals (7.1 ± 1.7, (n = 30) vs 4.04 ± 0.53, (n = 25) in control, P < 0.05) (Fig. 3 C) without changes in a number of outer square crossing (45.8 ± 5.1, (n = 30) vs 50.05 ± 5.18, (n = 25) in control) entrance in a center (0.73 ± 0.1, (n = 30) vs 0.75 ± 0.2, (n = 25) in control) and time spent in a center (0.6 ± 0.3, (n = 30) vs 1.3 ± 0.5, (n = 25) in control).

The frequency of urination was significantly increased in KA-SE animals (1.2 ± 0.35, (n = 30) vs. 0.1 ± 0.07, (n = 25) in controls, P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A). In addition, KA-SE rats exhibited alterations in locomotor activity in the PA test, accompanied by a marked impairment of defensive behavior, as evidenced by the increased number of entries into the dark, electrified (V) compartment (1.5 ± 0.4, (n = 30) vs 0.2 ± 0.1, (n = 25) in control, P < 0.05, Fig. 4 B).

While the control rats easily reached this criterion showing considerable day-by-day progress, none of the epileptic rats was successful (Fig. 5 A.B). Thus, behavioral experiments revealed significant alterations in behavior, locomotion and cognitive abilities of the KA-SE animals.

KA-SE-associated changes in hippocampal electrophysiology

Electrophysiological experiments were performed on 11 control and 13 kainic acid–treated animals. For statistical analyses, n corresponds to the number of electrically evoked responses.

In comparison with the control animals, the amplitude of response, electrically evoked by a single intrahippocampal stimulus in the KA-SE CA1 field was characterized by significantly lower amplitudes (1.46 ± 0.1 mV, (n = 24) vs 2.31 ± 0.5 mV, (n = 24) in the control, P < 0.05) (Fig. 6 A). Different alterations were observed in the CA3 field of the KA-SE rat, where status epilepticus has no significant effect on amplitude of EEPs compare to the control animals (5.27 ± 0.8 mV, (n = 38) vs 3.78 ± 0.254 mV, (n = 35)) (Fig. 6 A).

The effects of Orexin-A on the electrical evoked potentials in the hippocampus

The effect of Orexin-A can be mediated by activation of both OX1R and OX2R. Preliminary results with Orexin-B in control animals did not show detectable effects, neither in CA1 (2.732 ± 0.054 mV, n = 8, before application vs. 2.393 ± 0.241 mV, n = 10, after application) nor in CA3 (1.394 ± 0.074 mV, n = 8, before application vs. 1.425 ± 0.066 mV, n = 10, after application); statistical differences between the groups were assessed using an unpaired t-test. In contrast, Orexin-A exerted significant effects in control animals, suggesting a predominant involvement of OX1R signaling in our model, although further studies are required to fully dissect receptor contributions.

In the control animals, Orexin-A significantly increased the amplitude of the evoked potentials in the CA1 field (from 2.31 ± 0.5 mV, (n = 38) before application, to 6.42 ± 0.67 mV, after application (n = 23), P = 0.0005) (Fig. 6 A, B) and reduced it in the CA3 field (from 5.27 ± 0.8 mV, before application (n = 38), to 1.99 ± 0.2 mV, after application (n = 33), P = 0.0006) (Fig. 6 A, D). In the KA-SE hippocampus, Orexin-A exerted no effects on the EEPs neither in the CA1 (Fig. 6 A, C;) nor in the CA3 field (Fig. 6 A, E).

The effects of Orexin-A on the paired-pulse (PP) facilitation in the CA1 and CA3 fields

Orexin-A had no significant effects on the PP facilitation of evoked responses in the CA1 and CA3 fields in both the control and KA-SE groups of animals. In the control group, the amplitude of response to the second stimulus in a pair in the CA1 field increased by 144.1 ± 25,0 % (n = 33) and 181.8 ± 18.8 % (n = 37) before and after Orexin-A application, respectively. In the CA3 field this increase before and after application constituted, respectively 186.6 ± 18.5 % (n = 38) and 153.2 ± 17.4 %, (n = 38). In the CA1 field of the KA-SE rat hippocampus the second response enhancements in the PP tests were 144.6 ± 3.1 % and 137.2 ± 3.5 % before and after application, respectively (n = 29), the corresponding values in the CA3 field constituted 168.3 ± 6.2 % (n = 26) and 158.5 ± 5.3 %, (n = 15).

Discussion

Our behavioral experiments revealed significant alteration in behavior and in cognitive abilities of KA-SE animals that can be explained by morphological changes in the hippocampal fields identified in our study. Hippocampus neuronal loss with altered emotional memory, anxiety, learning ability and locomotion was shown in KA-SE rats (Faizan et al., 2023, Khatibi et al., 2023, Maia et al., 2014). Existing literature suggests that the CA3 region of the hippocampus plays a crucial role in supporting multiple mnemonic processes essential for the formation of accurate spatial memories (Gilbert and Brushfield, 2009). For hippocampal associated memory hypothalamic orexinergic projections are also important (Mavanji et al., 2017). The reduction in the number of hypothalamic Orexin-B neurons observed in our experiments may also contribute to memory deterioration in KA-SE rats.

Several studies show increased bursting activity of neurons in the CA3 field during epileptogenesis (Goldberg and Coulter, 2013, Wang et al., 2023). Our morphological experiments revealed a reduction in GAD-positive neurons in the left CA3 region, which may contribute to excessive excitation in this area due to diminished inhibitory control. Hyperactivity in the CA3 field leads to overexcitation of the left CA1 and the right CA3 fields of the hippocampus, mediated through Schaffer collaterals and associational/commissural projections, respectively, which can be a reason of neuronal loss in corresponding fields. Excitotoxicity is considered to be a primary cause of neuronal death in chronic epilepsy (Vincent and Mulle, 2009, Yalcin and Yalcin, 2018). Excessive reactive oxygen/nitrogen production and decreased endogenous antioxidant activity have been linked to epilepsy-induced neurotoxicity (Zhu et al., 2017).

Epilepsy-induced morphological changes in the hippocampus, particularly a reduction in cell numbers, are well-documented in the literature (Postnikova et al., 2024, Li et al., 2021, Sloviter, 2008). However, there are no data about morphological alterations in the hypothalamus caused by kainic acid status epilepticus. According to our results, KA-SE decreases the number of orexin-B neurons in the hypothalamus. This can be caused by kainic acid- induced massive activation of kainate receptors (KARs) in lateral hypothalamus (Sr et al., 2007) where orexinergic neurons are present (Nambu et al., 1999). Epileptiform activity itself can also promote hypothalamic hyperexcitation via Hippocampal-Hypothalamic circuit as it was demonstrated hippocampal projections from ghrelin sensitive vCA1 neurons to the orexinergic neurons in the lateral hypothalamus (Suarez et al., 2020). Hyperactivation of these glutamatergic projections can induce death of orexinergic neurons due to massive release of glutamate which stimulate not only kainite but also other types of glutamatergic receptors including the NMDA type. Kainate-induced loss of neostriatal neurons were dramatically enhanced by quinolinate and this effect was mediated largely by activation of NMDA receptors (Galarraga et al., 1990). Selective death of hypocretin neurons, shown in pathophysiology of narcolepsy, can be caused by MHCII-mediated activation of macrophages and microglia and release of neurotoxic molecules including Fas ligand, proteases, TNF, quinolinic acid, glutamate, radical oxygen intermediates and nitric oxide (Fontana et al., 2010). Excessive NO release can also be the reason of endoplasmic reticulum stress-induced neurodegeneration of the hypothalamic orexinergic neurons via inactivation of Protein Disulfide Isomerase (PDI) and misfolding of orexin peptides (Obukuro et al., 2013).

Mesial or temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) in humans is frequently marked by hippocampal sclerosis and a notable loss of inhibitory interneurons, especially changes in hippocampal GABAergic interneuron circuits have a central role in epileptogenesis (Thom, 2014, Maglóczky and Freund, 2005). These pathological changes are believed to contribute directly to the hyperexcitability and cognitive deficits that many patients experience (Novak et al., 2022). The loss of GAD-positive neurons in CA3 that we observed in KA-SE rats is consistent with these pathological alterations and may represent a mechanistic link to the excitatory–inhibitory imbalance described in human TLE. Our morphological findings of reduced Orexin-B–positive neurons in the hypothalamus suggest a disruption of hypothalamo–hippocampal circuits in KA-SE rats. This is in line with clinical evidence demonstrating reduced orexin levels in the cerebrospinal fluid of epileptic patients following generalized tonic–clonic seizures (Rejdak et al., 2009). Moreover, sleep disturbances (Malow, 2005) and altered circadian rhythms (Fukuda et al., 2015), both processes critically dependent on orexinergic signaling, have been frequently reported in epilepsy.

Orexin-A enhances the amplitude of glutamate-evoked responses in the CA1 region, without affecting paired-pulse facilitation, indicating postsynaptic locus of its action. Orexin-A decreases the amplitude of glutamatergic responses in the CA3 region. This effect may be due to the high density of OXR2-type receptors in CA-3 field, where they are primarily located on GABAergic interneurons (Krause et al., 2024). Activation of these receptors by Orexin-A enhances inhibitory signaling through increased GABA release. That subsequently decreases the excitability of the pyramidal neurons leading to reduction of evoked responses.

In our experiments, no effects of orexin-A on evoked responses were observed in CA1 of KA-SE animals. This can be explained by extensive neuronal loss in the CA1 field that occurs during status epilepticus (Li et al., 2021). The attenuated effect of Orexin-A on CA1 responses may, at least in part, reflect epilepsy-associated alterations in the expression or regulation of OX1 receptors (Elahdadi Salmani et al., 2022). Consequently, administration of the same dose of exogenous Orexin-A produced a significantly attenuated effect in the epileptic group compared with controls, suggesting altered receptor responsiveness or downstream signaling.

The reduced effect of orexin-A on evoked responses in the CA3 field, as compared to the control group, may also be explained by a reduction in the number of orexin-B-positive cells in the hypothalamus of KA-SE animals, shown in our morphological experiments. In the control group, endogenous orexin (orexin-B) was supplemented with exogenous orexin-A, leading to a complimentary effect that enhanced activity of GABAergic neurons, thereby reducing the amplitude of the evoked responses.

The distinct effects of exogenous orexins in normal physiology and in the pathophysiology of epilepsy can be explained by morphological changes observed in KA-SE animals and the complex nature of orexinergic receptors itself (Sakurai et al., 1998). Complexity of the OXRs is manifested in the fact, that it can signal via various G-proteins and, other proteins as well (reviewed in JP Kukkonen2014) (Kukkonen and Leonard, 2014) OXRs are able to form complexes with several other G-protein-coupled receptors, and activate certain signal transducers (reviewed in JP Kukkonen2021) (Kukkonen and Turunen, 2021). While neuronal loss in CA1 and CA3 regions likely contributes to the lack of Orexin-A effects in KA-SE animals, alternative mechanisms must also be considered. Orexin receptors belong to the GPCR family, whose functional integrity critically depends on receptor density, G-protein coupling, and downstream transduction. Epilepsy has been shown to induce GPCR dysfunction in multiple models, including reduced receptor availability, uncoupling from G-proteins, and impaired intracellular signaling (Galosi et al., 2022). Similar processes could diminish orexinergic signaling in the epileptic hippocampus. Thus, the diminished modulatory effect of Orexin-A may be attributable not only to neuronal loss but also to receptor desensitization or downregulation of receptor-mediated signaling pathways. Orexins have been studied in the context of other neurodegenerative diseases (Df et al., 2020). Neuroprotective role of orexin-A against palmitic acid (PA)-induced hypothalamic dysregulation was shown: it suppresses reactive oxygen species, increases intracellular respiration and ATP, stabilizes PA-induced reduction of mitochondrial respiration (Duffy et al., 2016).

Based on above mentioned, OXR signaling is complex, and it can change depending on the physiological condition of organism. It is evident that further research in this area is highly required.

Conclusion

Kainic acid–induced status epilepticus produces profound histoarchitectural alterations in both the hippocampus and hypothalamus. These structural changes are accompanied by impairments in emotional regulation, locomotor activity, memory performance, and the electrophysiological properties of hippocampal networks. A particularly notable finding is the loss of GAD-positive interneurons in CA3, which constitute principal targets of orexinergic projections. This loss of interneurons likely contributes to the absence of orexin-mediated modulation of evoked synaptic potentials in KA-SE rats. Mechanistically, such disruption may compromise inhibitory control within hippocampal circuits, thereby facilitating hyperexcitability. Collectively, these findings highlight that orexinergic dysfunction, secondary to structural pathology, alters synaptic regulation and underscores the differential roles of orexins in hippocampal physiology under normal and pathological conditions.
