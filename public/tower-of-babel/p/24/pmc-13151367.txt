A rapid and highly sensitive CRISPR-Cas12a ortholog-assisted assay for genotyping of myostatin knockout pigs
Authors: Yuan Wang, Hui Yang, Wenhua Zhang, Dagang Tao, Suyu Shi, Sheng Li, Xiao Wu, Yunlong Ma, Jinxue Ruan, Lu Jing, Kang Ma, Xinyun Li, Xiaosong Han, Xuewen Xu, Shengsong Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCRISPR technology has profoundly transformed both genome editing and the next generation of nucleic acid detection. In this study, we characterized a Cas12a ortholog, Gs12-9 (EsoCas12a), which recognizes a broad PAM motif (NYYN, where Y denotes C or T) and maintains robust cis- and trans-cleavage activity across a wide temperature range (16–60 ℃), outperforming established Cas12a variants such as LbCas12a. Leveraging Gs12-9, we established a rapid, highly sensitive, and temperature-tolerant nucleic acid detection platform that enables precise genotyping without stringent thermal control. By integrating recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) with CRISPR/Gs12-9-mediated detection, we accurately discriminated myostatin (MSTN) knockout pigs from wild-type individuals, achieving a detection limit of 40 copies per reaction with 100% accuracy. While the MSTN knockout (KO) pig served as a proof-of-concept model in this work, the versatility of Gs12-9 supports its broader application in CRISPR-based diagnostics, including pathogen screening and genetic variant detection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-026-01559-2.Keywords: Gs12-9, Nucleic acid detection, MSTN, PAM

Introduction

CRISPR-based technologies have revolutionized genome editing and catalyzed the development of next-generation molecular diagnostics [1, 2], which are now widely applied in fields such as genotyping [3, 4] and pathogen detection [5, 6]. The Cas12a system operates through a two-step detection mechanism: cis-cleavage of target DNA induces a conformational change in the RuvC domain, thereby activating non-specific trans-cleavage of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) [7–9]. This collateral activity, when coupled with a single-stranded DNA fluorophore-quencher (ssDNA-FQ) reporter, enables specific signal generation [10]. To achieve sufficient sensitivity, target amplification is generally necessary. Isothermal amplification methods that function at constant temperatures, such as loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) [11], rolling circle amplification (RCA) [12], and recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) [13], offer a practical alternative to PCR for field-deployable diagnostic platforms. Several Cas12a-based detection systems, such as DETECTR [10], HOLMES [14], and RAVI-CRISPR [15], have been successfully established. However, despite these advances, the broader implementation of CRISPR-Cas12a for on-site nucleic acid detection remains constrained by significant limitations.

The restricted PAM compatibility and pronounced thermal sensitivity of commonly used Cas12a nucleases reduce both detection flexibility and field reliability. Consequently, engineering or discovering novel Cas12a variants that integrate broad PAM recognition [16], high cleavage activity and thermal robustness [17] is essential to advance portable, point-of-care diagnostic solutions. Currently employed nucleases, including LbCas12a [18] and AsCas12a [19], exhibit stringent PAM specificity (TTTV), which significantly restricts their targeting range across various genetic detection applications. Moreover, the activity of most existing Cas12a variants is highly temperature-sensitive, undermining their reliability in field settings where precise thermal control is unavailable, thereby hindering robust point-of-care testing. Although recent metagenomic studies have identified promising homologs with improved properties, such as CmeCas12a and YmeCas12a [20](recognizing NYY PAM) and LtCas12a [21] (recognizing TTNA PAM), there remains a clear need for novel Cas12a orthologs. To propel the development of next-generation diagnostics, these new orthologs would be expected to combine relaxed PAM specificity, high cleavage efficiency, and broad thermal stability.

To address these limitations, our previous work identified a series of Cas12a homologs from metagenomic databases [22]. Preliminary PAM determination assays suggested that one of these, Gs12-9, may recognize an NYYN motif, although this finding has not yet been thoroughly validated. More importantly, several key functional properties of this nuclease remain unexplored: its cis- and trans-cleavage efficiency, its suitability for developing highly sensitive nucleic acid detection assays, and critically, its ability to maintain stable activity across a wide temperature range to meet the practical demands of point-of-care applications.

Meanwhile, CRISPR/Cas9‑mediated gene editing has been successfully employed to precisely enhance key economic traits in pigs, including growth rate, disease resistance, and lean meat yield [23]. The role of myostatin (MSTN) as a crucial negative regulator of muscle development is well established [24], as exemplified by the natural “double‑muscling” mutation in Belgian Blue cattle [25]. To date, MSTN editing has been achieved in several Chinese local pig breeds, such as Bama [26] and Meishan pigs [27], but not yet in Enshi black pigs, which is a breed valued for its superior meat quality, efficient digestion, and balanced fat deposition [28]. It should be noted, however, that MSTN knockout (KO) can lead to adverse phenotypes such as macroglossia and reduced fertility [29, 30]. Given the rising number of gene‑edited animal models, the development of rapid, accurate, and field‑deployable genotyping methods is essential for the timely identification and management of edited individuals.

In this study, we systematically evaluated the cis- and trans-cleavage activities of Gs12-9. Given its superior trans-cleavage capability, we established a nucleic acid detection system based on this Cas12a ortholog, which is characterized by broad PAM recognition, specifically binding the relaxed NYYN motif. Subsequently, MSTN gene-edited Enshi Black pigs were generated using the CRISPR-Cas9 system. To enable early detection and rapid intervention, we developed an RPA-CRISPR-Gs12-9 assay for on-site genotyping of these CRISPR/Cas9-generated MSTN-KO pigs. Our study demonstrate that Gs12-9 holds considerable potential for widespread adoption in next-generation CRISPR diagnostic technologies.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Huazhong Agricultural University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval number: HZAUSW-2017‑007). Every effort was made to minimize pain, suffering, and distress to the animals, and to reduce the number of animals used in the study.

Plasmid construction

The hCas9 expression vector was obtained from Addgene (#41,815). The sgRNA scaffold vector (pGL3-U6-sgRNA-PGK-puromycin, Addgene #51,133) was digested with Bbs I. A pair of complementary oligonucleotides targeting the MSTN locus (MSTN-KO-F: accg TATAAGGCCAATTACTGCTC; MSTN -KO-R: aaac GAGCAGTAATTGGCCTTATA) were synthesized, annealed, and ligated into the linearized vector. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Generation of MSTN-KO porcine fetal fibroblast cells

Porcine fetal fibroblast (PFF) cells were isolated from 35-day-old Enshi black pig embryos following a previously described protocol [31]. Cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin‑streptomycin, 1% non‑essential amino acids, 2 mM GlutaMAX, and 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ in a humidified incubator.

For transfection, PFFs were seeded in 10 cm dishes and electroporated at 90% confluence using the Neon transfection system (Life Technology). Approximately 1 × 106 cells in 100 µL Buffer R were electroporated at 1350 V for 30 ms with a single pulse, together with 8 µg of Cas9 plasmid vector and 4 µg of sgRNA plasmid vector. Following electroporation, cells were plated onto 10 cm dishes. Following electroporation, cells were plated onto 10 cm dishes. After 48 h, they were trypsinized, diluted to low density, and re-seeded into fresh 10 cm plates at approximately 100 cells per plate for selection with 400 µg/mL G418 (Merck). Single colonies appeared after approximately 10 days. Colonies were isolated using cloning cylinders (Millipore, C1059-1EA) and transferred into 24‑well plates. Upon reaching sufficient density, half of the cells from each clone were harvested for genotyping, while the remaining cells were expanded in 12‑well plates. Positive clones, confirmed by sequencing, were cryopreserved for subsequent somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT).

Generation of MSTN-KO pigs using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing

To generate MSTN-KO pigs, donor PFF cells harboring a 5-bp deletion in the MSTN gene were used for SCNT, followed by embryo transfer into surrogate sows. Genomic DNA was extracted from edited PFF colonies by lysis in 10 μL NP40 buffer (0.45% NP40, 0.6% Proteinase K) at 56 °C for 60 min, followed by 95 °C for 10 min. The target region was PCR‑amplified using primers MSTN‑PCR‑F and MSTN‑PCR‑R (Supplementary Table S6). PCR products were gel‑purified and sequenced; positive colonies were cryopreserved for SCNT.

SCNT was conducted as previously described [31]. Briefly, donor nuclei were introduced into enucleated oocytes. Recipient sows (healthy multiparous Large White × Landrace hybrids) were synchronized using an oral progesterone protocol (2.2 mg/mL in edible oil, 6.8 mL/day mixed in feed for 14 days), with estrus typically occurring 6–8 days after treatment cessation. Embryos were transferred into synchronized recipients.

Pregnancy was confirmed via Brightness-mode Ultrasound on days 28 and 42 post‑transfer. Sows were housed in a dedicated gestation unit. Approximately three days before expected farrowing (gestation ~ 114 days), farrowing facilities were cleaned and sterilized, and warming equipment was prepared. Newborn piglets received colostrum promptly, followed by standard immunization and health protocols. Ear‑tagging and tissue sampling were performed three days after birth.

Conservation analysis of the Gs12-9 protein sequence

In our previous study, we utilized an AI-structure-guided pipeline based on sequence divergence and structural conservation to mine a novel Cas12a ortholog, Gs12-9 (EsoCas12a) from publicly available metagenomic databases [22]. Next, a multiple sequence alignment of five known Cas12a proteins (LbCas12a, FnCas12a, AsCas12a, MbCas12a, and PiCas12a) along with Gs12-9 was performed using Geneious Prime to assess sequence conservation patterns.

Purification of Gs12-9, LbCas12a and impLbCas12a proteins

For subsequent protein purification, the plasmids encoding Gs12‑9, LbCas12a and impLbCas12a were transformed into chemically competent E. coli BL21(λDE3) (NEB, C2527H) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Transformants were plated on LB agar containing 50 µg/mL kanamycin and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Single colonies were inoculated into liquid medium with kanamycin, cultured at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm to optical density (OD600) > 1.0, and verified by Sanger sequencing. A confirmed clone was grown in 300 mL medium to OD600 0.5–0.6, and protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 18 °C for 16 h.

Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer (300 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 5 mM HEPES, 20 mM imidazole, 1 mM DTT, pH 6.5) and sonicated (625 W, 40% power, 2 s on/3 s off, for 3 min; SCIENTZ, JY92-IIN). After centrifugation at 9000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane (biosharp, BS-PES-45) and incubated with Ni–NTA affinity resin (Huiyan Biotech, HQ060312025M) on ice for 2 h. The resin was washed with 10 column volumes (CV) each of wash buffers A (500 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris, 30 mM imidazole, pH 6.5) and B (500 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris, 50 mM imidazole, pH 6.5). Protein was eluted with elution buffer (500 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris, 300 mM imidazole, pH 6.5). Elution was monitored by Quick Start™ Bradford 1 × Dye Reagent (Bio-Rad, 5,000,205), and fractions were analyzed by SDS‑PAGE. High‑purity fractions were concentrated using a 30 kDa centrifugal filter, stored in storage buffer (500 mM NaCl, 20 mM sodium acetate, 0.1 mM EDTA, 50% glycerol, pH 6.0) at − 80 °C, and quantified with a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Cat#P0012).

Preparation of crRNAs

To investigate the optimal in vitro cleavage conditions (time, temperature) of Gs12‑9 nuclease, we synthesized the previously validated p72‑sg5 crRNA targeting the African swine fever virus (ASFV) p72 gene [15]. The crRNA sequence was: AAUUUCUAC UGUUGUAGAUAGAGCAG ACAUUAGUUUUUCA. Additionally, to assess the in vitro PAM compatibility of Gs12‑9, we designed 17 crRNAs targeting both canonical and non‑canonical PAMs (NYYN, where N = A/T/C/G and Y = C/T) at distinct sites within the p72 target sequence (Supplementary Table S3).

All crRNAs were synthesized by in vitro transcription. Each transcription template comprised a T7 promoter (TAATACGACTCACTATAGGG), a direct repeat (AATTTCTACTGTTGTAGAT), and a spacer sequence. Templates were generated by overlap PCR using a forward primer containing the T7 promoter and direct repeat, and a reverse primer containing the direct repeat and spacer. PCR products were purified and transcribed overnight at 37 °C using the HiScribe T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB, E2040S). The resulting RNA was cleaned with the Monarch RNA Cleanup Kit (NEB, T2040S). Purified crRNA concentrations were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific); samples with OD260/280 ratios of approximately 2.0–2.2 were used in subsequent experiments.

In vitro evaluation of cis- and trans-cleavage activity of Gs12-9, LbCas12a and impLbCas12a

Target DNA for cleavage assays was amplified by PCR from the ASFV‑p72 plasmid using primers listed in Supplementary Table S6. PCR products were purified with the FastPure Gel DNA Extraction Mini Kit (Vazyme, DC301‑1), and DNA concentration was measured on a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples with OD260/280 ratios of 1.8–1.9 and concentrations > 200 ng/µL were used for subsequent experiments.

Cis‑cleavage reactions contained 500 ng of Gs12‑9, LbCas12a, or impLbCas12a; 300 ng crRNA; 2 µL 10 × rCutSmart Buffer; and 200 ng p72 dsDNA in a total volume of 20 µL adjusted with ddH2O. Reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min, then terminated by adding 1 µL Proteinase K and incubating at 55 °C for 10 min. Cleavage products were resolved on 2% agarose gels, and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ. Cleavage efficiency was calculated as: Cleavage Efficiency (%) = 100 × [32], where “a” represents intact bands and “b + c” represent cleavage products.

Trans‑cleavage reactions contained 500 ng Cas12a nuclease, 300 ng crRNA, 0.75 µM ssDNA‑FQ reporter (5′‑ROX/GTATCCAGT GCG/3′‑BHQ2), 2 µL 10 × rCutSmart Buffer, and 200 ng dsDNA or ssDNA target. Reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min, heat‑inactivated at 98 °C for 2 min, and visualized under an F‑Light detector (SRB‑ES‑004, ShangRuiBio) or blue light transilluminator (EP2020, BioTeke). Images were captured with a smartphone camera. For fluorescence quantification, each reaction was diluted with 80 µL nuclease‑free water in a 96‑well black polystyrene microplate (Costar CLS3925), and fluorescence was measured on an EnSpire microplate reader (PerkinElmer) using excitation/emission wavelengths of 576 nm and 601 nm, respectively.

Nucleic acid preparation

Genomic DNA was extracted from tissues of MSTN-KO and wild-type pigs. Approximately 0.05 g of each tissue sample was homogenized and lysed in 50 µL of QuickExtract™ DNA Extraction Solution (Lucigen, QE09050) in a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube, with intermittent vortexing during sequential incubation at 65 °C for 5 min and 95 °C for 10 min, yielding PCR‑ready DNA.

Primers for PCR and recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) were designed using Primer 5.0 software (Premier Biosoft) and synthesized by TIANYI HUIYUAN (Supplementary Table S6). The MSTN and MSTN-KO gene fragments were amplified via PCR using genomic DNA from wild-type and MSTN-KO pigs, respectively, with the corresponding target sequences detailed in Supplementary Table S5. The resulting PCR products were cloned into pMD18T vectors (TaKaRa) to generate the recombinant plasmids pMD18T-MSTN and pMD18T-MSTN-KO. The porcine ZFX gene was used as an endogenous control throughout the assay.

RPA-mediated amplification of target genes

Recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) was performed using the TwistAmp™ Basic Kit (TwistDx, Cambridge, UK) in a total volume of 50 µL. The reaction mix consisted of 29.5 µL primer-free rehydration buffer, 25 pmol each of forward and reverse primers (Supplementary Table S6), and 5.1 µL nuclease-free water, combined with the lyophilized reagent pellet from the kit. After gentle mixing, the reaction solution was aliquoted into four tubes. Each aliquot was supplemented with 0.8 µL of 280 mM magnesium acetate (MgOAc) and 1 µL of template (plasmid DNA carrying the MSTN or MSTN-KO gene, or genomic DNA extracted from porcine tissues). Reactions were incubated at 39 °C for 4 min using a NaCha Multi-Temp Platform (Monad, China).

Sensitivity and sample validation of target gene detection using the CRISPR/Cas12‑9 system

The copy number of the pMD18T‑MSTN plasmid (358-bp insert) was calculated based on its molecular weight using the formula: copy number = (plasmid concentration × 6.022 × 1023) / (length × 1 × 109 × 650). To assess the detection sensitivity of the CRISPR/Gs12‑9 system, the plasmid was serially diluted from 4 × 105 to 4 × 10–1 copies/µL and subjected to RPA amplification. The detection reaction consisted of 500 ng Gs12‑9, 500 ng MSTN‑specific crRNA, 2 µL 10 × rCutSmart Buffer, 0.75 µM ssDNA‑FQ reporter, and 2 µL of RPA‑amplified product in a total volume adjusted with nuclease‑free water. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 10 min, heat‑inactivated at 98 °C for 2 min, and visualized under an F‑Light detector (SRB‑ES‑004, ShangRuiBio) or blue light transilluminator (EP2020, BioTeke) to evaluate fluorescence signal against background noise.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software. Differences between treatment and control groups were evaluated by two-tailed Student’s t-test. Significance levels are indicated as *** P < 0.001; “ns” denotes no statistical significance.

Results

Establishment of a CRISPR-Based nucleic acid detection assay using Cas12a orthologs

In our previous study, we identified the Cas12a ortholog Gs12‑9 from metagenomic databases [22]. To comprehensively characterize the cis- and trans-cleavage activities of Gs12-9, we first conducted a bioinformatic analysis of this nuclease. A multiple sequence alignment with five well-studied Cas12a proteins (LbCas12a, FnCas12a, AsCas12a, MbCas12a and PiCas12a) revealed relatively low conservation, with sequence identities of 46.57%, 40.51%, 34.75%, 45.46% and 39.87%, respectively (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Table S1). We then expressed and purified Gs12‑9, LbCas12a, and impLbCas12a, and evaluated their cis‑ and trans‑cleavage activities using an African swine fever virus (ASFV) p72 gene fragment containing the canonical “TTTV” PAM as substrate (Fig. 1B). For cis‑cleavage, all three nucleases were active between 16 and 42 °C. Gs12‑9 showed a broader temperature range (16–60 °C) than LbCas12a (16–42 °C) and impLbCas12a (16–50 °C) (Fig. 1C; Supplementary Fig. S1A). Optimal cis‑cleavage for each nuclease occurred between 37 and 42 °C, and 37 °C was selected for subsequent assays. Time course experiments at 37 °C revealed that cis‑cleavage initiated within 1 min for all three nucleases. Gs12‑9 and impLbCas12a reached high activity levels by 10 min, while LbCas12a required 15 min (Fig. 1D; Supplementary Fig. S1B).

In trans‑cleavage assays, Gs12‑9 again exhibited the broadest functional temperature window (16–55 °C), with activity peaking between 37 and 42 °C (Fig. 1E, F). At 16 °C, Gs12‑9 outperformed LbCas12a and impLbCas12a. When monitored over time at 37 °C, Gs12‑9 initiated trans‑cleavage within 1 min and approached saturation by 5 min, whereas LbCas12a required 10 min to reach comparable levels (Fig. 1G, H). Together, these data indicate that despite its low sequence similarity to canonical Cas12a homologs, Gs12‑9 retains robust and broad‑range cleavage activity, supporting its use as a candidate nuclease for CRISPR‑based nucleic acid detection platforms.

Gs12-9 exhibits broad targeting capability for classical and non-classical PAM sites

Although previous work proposed an “NYYN” PAM specificity for Gs12-9, this finding lacked experimental validation [22]. To address this gap and to systematically compare the cleavage preferences of Gs12‑9 with those of LbCas12a and impLbCas12a toward canonical (TTTV) and non-canonical PAMs, we designed 17 crRNAs targeting ASFV p72 gene fragments. Comparative analysis revealed that both Gs12-9 and impLbCas12a maintained robust cis-cleavage activity at 16 out of 17 PAM sites on dsDNA substrates, with Gs12‑9 exhibiting higher efficiency. In contrast, LbCas12a cleaved only 14 sites, three of which showed efficiencies below 10% (Fig. 2A, B). These results highlight the versatility of Gs12‑9 in recognizing diverse PAMs and its broader targeting scope for biotechnology.

We next evaluated the trans-cleavage activity of Gs12‑9 for nucleic acid detection using dsDNA (PCR‑amplified ASFV p72 fragments) and ssDNA substrates. Fluorescence detection was performed with a ROX‑labeled ssDNA‑FQ reporter under blue light (F‑light detector) and a multifunctional microplate reader. Gs12‑9 effectively mediated trans-cleavage at 16 out of 17 PAM sites with dsDNA substrate, outperforming both LbCas12a (13/17 sites) and impLbCas12a (15/17 sites) (Fig. 2C, D). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Gs12‑9 possesses broad cleavage activity across canonical and non‑canonical PAMs, underscoring its potential for versatile detection applications.

Generating MSTN-KO Enshi Black pigs via CRISPR/Cas9 system

MSTN-KO Enshi Black pigs were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 targeting the protein-coding region of the MSTN gene. PFFs were co-transfected with both the Cas9 and sgRNA vectors. (Fig. 3A, B). Following limiting-dilution cloning, candidate PFF colonies were screened for knockout efficiency (Fig. 3C). Four clonal lines (#3, #11, #18, #56) showed complete MSTN disruption (Fig. 3D). Clone #18, harboring a representative 5-bp deletion, was expanded and used as donor cells for SCNT. Three recipient sows farrowed litters of 6, 8, and 12 piglets after a gestation of 117–120 days (Fig. 3E). Sanger sequencing confirmed the intended 5-bp (TACTG) deletion in the MSTN locus of the resulting piglets (Fig. 3F). Unfortunately, 26 piglets died within five days after birth (Supplementary Table S4). Furthermore, MSTN‑edited pigs exhibited macroglossia (Fig. 3F). To enable rapid, onsite visual genotyping for timely identification and evaluation of MSTN-edited animals, we subsequently developed the CRISPR-Gs12-9 detection assay.

Establishment of an RPA-CRISPR/Gs12-9 assay for rapid on-site detection of MSTN-KO pigs

We established a CRISPR/Gs12-9 genotyping assay using a crRNA targeting the 5-bp deletion (TACTG) in the MSTN gene. This assay relies on crRNA-dependent activation of Gs12-9, which cleaves an ssDNA-FQ reporter upon target recognition to generate fluorescence. Wild-type samples produced strong signals, whereas MSTN-KO samples (carrying the deletion) showed no fluorescence (Fig. 4A). Notably, the CRISPR/Gs12-9 assay outperformed the LbCas12a-based method, providing superior genotype discrimination with a higher signal-to-background contrast (Fig. 4B, C).

To enable portable on-site detection, we integrated RPA with the CRISPR/Gs12-9 system. Using Primer5-designed primers, two RPA amplicons (268 bp and 294 bp) were generated and verified by agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. 4D). The resulting RPA-CRISPR/Gs12-9 assay clearly distinguished MSTN-KO from wild-type samples, with the former showing no fluorescence and the latter yielding a distinct red signal (Fig. 4E, F).

To determine assay sensitivity, a wild-type MSTN fragment was serially diluted (4 × 105 to 4 × 10–1 copies/reaction) and amplified by RPA. The detection limit was 4 × 101 copies per reaction (Fig. 4G–I). Collectively, these results demonstrate the successful development of a rapid, on-site RPA-CRISPR/Gs12-9 assay for detecting MSTN-KO pigs.

Evaluation of genotyping accuracy for MSTN gene-edited pigs using the RPA-CRISPR/Gs12-9 system

To assess the genotyping accuracy of the RPA-CRISPR/Gs12-9 assay for MSTN-edited pigs (Fig. 5A), we analyzed four MSTN-KO pigs, four wild-type pigs, and two control plasmids (pMD18T-MSTN and pMD18T-ZFX). The porcine ZFX gene served as an internal control to monitor DNA extraction efficiency and prevent false negatives. Agarose gel electrophoresis of RPA-amplified ZFX products showed clear bands in all sample lanes except the negative control (Fig. 5B). Fluorescence readouts from the CRISPR/Gs12-9 assay confirmed successful DNA extraction for all eight pig samples (Fig. 5C, D).

The MSTN gene was subsequently amplified by RPA, and the products were analyzed using the CRISPR/Gs12-9 assay (Fig. 5E). As expected, MSTN amplification products from MSTN-KO pigs produced no fluorescence, while those from wild-type pigs generated strong fluorescent signals (Fig. 5F, G). These results demonstrate that the RPA-CRISPR/Gs12-9 assay can accurately distinguish MSTN-KO from wild-type pigs.

Discussion

CRISPR-Cas12a-based nucleic acid detection is widely used for diagnostics. To enhance the reliability of this approach, there is a need to develop high-performance Cas12a nucleases. Here, we systematically characterized a Cas12a ortholog, Gs12-9. Our results show that Gs12-9 recognizes a broad PAM motif and maintains robust cis- and trans-cleavage activity across a wide temperature range (16–60 °C), outperforming established Cas12a variants such as LbCas12a. Leveraging these properties, we established a rapid, highly sensitive, and temperature-tolerant nucleic acid detection platform that enables precise genotyping without stringent thermal control. By integrating RPA with CRISPR/Gs12-9-mediated detection, we accurately discriminated MSTN-KO pigs from wild-type individuals. Beyond serving as a proof-of-concept in MSTN‑KO pig genotyping, the Gs12‑9 platform holds significant potential for broader CRISPR‑based diagnostic applications, such as pathogen detection and genetic variant screening.

Compared with the widely used LbCas12a [18] and the engineered broad-PAM variant impLbCas12a [16], Gs12-9 not only operated over a wider temperature window but also exhibited faster reaction kinetics, with trans-cleavage reaching near-saturation within 5 min. In cis-cleavage assays, Gs12-9 displayed robust activity at 16 of 17 tested PAM sites. In trans-cleavage tests, its performance similarly surpassed LbCas12a (active at 13 sites) and impLbCas12a (active at 15 sites). These attributes confer upon Gs12-9 clear advantages over recently reported thermostable Cas12a orthologs such as CmeCas12a, YmeCas12a [20] and engineered variants enAsCas12a [17]. Its broad PAM tolerance, coupled with stable activity across variable temperatures, makes it particularly suitable for field-ready diagnostics.

Leveraging these properties, we developed an RPA-CRISPR/Gs12-9 detection system to address the management demands of MSTN-edited pigs. Using CRISPR/Cas9, we successfully generated MSTN-KO Enshi black pigs harboring a 5-bp deletion in the MSTN gene. Most edited piglets displayed macroglossia and died during the neonatal period, underscoring the phenotypic risks linked to MSTN knockout and highlighting the need for rapid, on-site postpartum genotyping to inform care decisions [29, 30, 33]. Traditional methods such as PCR [34], qPCR [35], T7E1 [36] assays, and Sanger sequencing [31, 34] are often cumbersome, equipment-intensive, time-consuming (6 h for PCR/qPCR), costly, and poorly suited to field settings. In contrast, the RPA-CRISPR/Gs12-9 system requires only a simple heat block and portable fluorescence detector, completing nucleic acid extraction, amplification, and detection within about one hour, with 100% accuracy in distinguishing KO from wild-type pigs. The cost per sample is kept below $2 (RPA reagents $1.50, self-purified Gs12-9 $0.01, other consumables ≤ $0.20). This low-equipment, easy-to-operate approach offers clear advantages in farm or remote laboratory environments.

Beyond MSTN genotyping, the unique properties of Gs12‑9, particularly its permissive NYYN PAM recognition and broad thermostability, support its wide application in pathogen diagnostics, genetically modified organism (GMO) identification, and rapid variant profiling. For example, in ASFV detection as demonstrated here, a Cas12a nuclease with flexible PAM compatibility enhances detection adaptability and reduces the risk of false negatives caused by canonical PAM-site mutations. Furthermore, its ability to maintain robust activity across 16–55 °C without precise temperature control ensures reliability in field-deployable assays. Owing to its high cis-cleavage activity, Gs12‑9 also holds potential for genome-editing applications. Future studies should systematically evaluate its editing efficiency and specificity in mammalian cells, such as HEK293T and PK‑15. If successfully validated, Gs12‑9 could be developed into an integrated CRISPR platform that combines gene-editing and molecular detection capabilities, thereby streamlining workflows from genetic engineering to progeny genotyping.

In summary, we characterized Gs12-9 as a high-performance Cas12a ortholog with broad PAM recognition and excellent temperature tolerance. The RPA-CRISPR/Gs12-9 detection method established here enables rapid, accurate, and low-equipment-dependent genotyping of MSTN-KO pigs. This system not only addresses an urgent need for efficient management of gene-edited animals but also provides a versatile and extensible platform for diverse diagnostic applications.
