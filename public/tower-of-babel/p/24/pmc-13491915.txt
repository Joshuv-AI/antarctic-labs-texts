Feasibility and safety of oxygen-free nasal high flow therapy in the acute postoperative period: a single-center randomized study
Authors: Mari Ohnaka, Shuntaro Sato, Shinji Kurata, Mizuki Tachi, Yu Ozaki-Honda, Takuro Sanuki, Motohiro Sekino, Stanislav Tatkov, Takao Ayuse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRoutine postoperative oxygen administration in the general ward is widely practiced despite limited evidence of benefit in low-risk surgical patients and increasing concerns regarding delayed detection of hypoventilation and unnecessary hyperoxia. Nasal high flow (NHF) therapy can reduce respiratory effort even without supplemental oxygen, but its feasibility and safety in the general ward during the acute postoperative period remain uncertain.ObjectiveThis randomized feasibility study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, safety, and protocol adherence of oxygen-free NHF therapy in the general ward following oral surgery under general anesthesia, and to obtain preliminary physiological data to inform the design of a future confirmatory randomized controlled trial.MethodsAdult patients undergoing elective oral surgery under general anesthesia were randomized (1:1:1) to receive NHF with humidified room air at 40 L/min, conventional face mask oxygen at 5 L/min, or no respiratory support during the first 150 min in the general ward. Primary outcomes were feasibility-related parameters, including recruitment and protocol completion rates, tolerance of NHF therapy, need for rescue oxygen, and completeness of continuous physiological monitoring. Safety outcomes included the incidence of hypoxemia and clinically marked hypercapnia. Physiological outcomes included respiratory rate, transcutaneous oxygen (tcpO₂) and carbon dioxide (tcpCO₂) partial pressures, and oxygen saturation (SpO₂).ResultsAll 48 enrolled patients completed the study protocol, demonstrating high feasibility with excellent recruitment, protocol adherence, and data completeness. NHF therapy was well tolerated, and no serious adverse respiratory events were observed. The incidence of hypoxemia and hypercapnia was comparable across the groups. Exploratory analyses showed a lower respiratory rate in the NHF group (NHF: 11.1 ± 2.8 min−1; control: 14.7 ± 2.7 min−1; face mask O2: 13.9 ± 3.0 min−1) without evidence of carbon dioxide retention. In contrast, face mask oxygen therapy frequently resulted in supraphysiological oxygenation, while maintaining comparable tcpCO₂ levels (NHF: 55.0 ± 15.9 mmHg; control: 52.2 ± 10.8 mmHg; face mask: 55.4 ± 10.0 mmHg).ConclusionsNHF without supplemental oxygen therapy was safe, and well tolerated as postoperative respiratory management in the general ward. The study addressed key feasibility concerns and provides essential data to support the design of a future adequately powered randomized controlled trial evaluating the clinical effectiveness of oxygen-free NHF.Trial registrationTrial registration: The study was registered as jRCTs072200018 on June.23, 2020 on the site of Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCT).URL: https://jrct.mhlw.go.jp/latest-detail/jRCTs072200018The trial started on July 1, 2020 and is currently recruiting.The date of enrollment of the first research participant was March 27, 2023.

Implication statement

Nasal high flow with room air, without supplemental oxygen, may reduce the respiratory effort associated with reducing the respiratory rate while maintaining adequate gas exchange, potentially preventing hypoxemia and hypercapnia in the acute postoperative period.

Introduction

Postoperative respiratory management in the general ward commonly relies on routine supplemental oxygen administration to prevent hypoxemia following general anesthesia. This practice is largely driven by concerns regarding residual anesthetic effects, respiratory depression, and transient upper airway obstruction in the immediate postoperative period [1, 2]. However, postoperative hypoxemia does not occur uniformly across patient populations, and growing evidence suggests that routine oxygen administration may delay the recognition of hypoventilation while exposing patients to unnecessary hyperoxia [3].

Several studies have demonstrated that supplemental oxygen can mask hypoventilation by maintaining peripheral oxygen saturation despite reduced ventilation [3]. In addition, liberal oxygen therapy has been associated with adverse physiological effects, including increased oxidative stress and potential organ injury [4–8]. These concerns have prompted a shift towards more conservative, titrated oxygen strategies in acutely ill patients, which emphasizes the importance of balancing the risks of both hypoxia and hyperoxia [9, 10].

Nasal high flow (NHF) therapy delivers heated and humidified gas at high flow rates via a nasal cannula and has become an established respiratory support modality in critical care and perioperative medicine. The physiological mechanisms of NHF include clearance of anatomical dead space, generation of low-level positive airway pressure, and reduction of respiratory effort [11–13]. While NHF is most commonly used with supplemental oxygen to improve oxygenation, emerging physiological and clinical data suggests that NHF delivered with room air alone can support ventilation and reduce the respiratory workload without impairing gas exchange [14].

Despite these promising observations, the implementation of oxygen-free NHF in the general ward during the acute postoperative period remains insufficiently studied. Unlike the intensive care unit, the general ward is characterized by limited staffing and monitoring resources, and any respiratory support strategy must be not only physiologically sound but also safe, tolerable, and operationally feasible. Importantly, the question of whether oxygen-free NHF can be reliably implemented in routine postoperative care is distinct from the question of whether it improves clinical outcomes, and the former must be addressed before the latter can be meaningfully evaluated.

Feasibility studies play a critical role in bridging the gap between physiological rationale and definitive effectiveness trials. In the perioperative setting, feasibility encompasses the ability to recruit and retain patients, deliver the intervention as intended, ensure patient tolerance and safety, and obtain high-quality physiological data under real-world ward conditions. This is particularly important when exploring a potential paradigm shift in postoperative respiratory management, such as reducing routine oxygen exposure in favor of physiologically targeted support.

Patients undergoing oral and maxillofacial surgery represent a clinically relevant population in which to evaluate the respiratory support with NHF. These patients are at risk of early postoperative respiratory compromise due to residual anesthetic effects and transient upper airway obstruction, yet many procedures are associated with a relatively low baseline incidence of severe hypoxemia [2, 15]. This combination provides an appropriate context in which to assess whether oxygen-free NHF can be implemented safely under structured monitoring without increasing respiratory risk.

Therefore, the primary objective of this randomized feasibility study was to determine whether oxygen-free NHF therapy could be safely and consistently implemented in the general ward during the acute postoperative period following oral and maxillofacial surgery under general anesthesia. Specifically, the authors aimed to assess recruitment and protocol completion rates, patient tolerance of NHF, the need for rescue oxygen therapy, and the completeness of continuous physiological monitoring. As secondary, exploratory objectives, they evaluated physiological parameters including respiratory rate and gas exchange to inform endpoint selection and sample size estimation for a future confirmatory randomized controlled trial.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a single-center, randomized study to evaluate the feasibility, safety, and operational implementation of oxygen-free nasal high flow (NHF) therapy in the general ward during the acute postoperative period. The study was not designed to assess clinical effectiveness or to test superiority or non-inferiority hypotheses.

Participants

Adult patients (≥ 18 and < 85 years) scheduled for elective oral surgery under general anesthesia at Nagasaki University Hospital were screened for eligibility. Patients excluded were those who were expected to experience marked postoperative morphological changes, such as those undergoing extensive jawbone resection and those who required postoperative opioid administration for pain management. The enrolled scheduled patients were considered as having normal pulmonary status with ASA Physical Status 1–2. The patients with pre-existing lung disease were excluded and there were no patients with any pre-existing lung abnormality or abnormal oxygenation status.

The principal investigator or co-investigator registered the research subject in REDCap system after obtaining written informed consent and confirming eligibility. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in the published protocol [16] and were applied to ensure patient safety and feasibility of postoperative respiratory monitoring.

Randomization and allocation

Eligible patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to one of three postoperative respiratory management strategies: (1) NHF with humidified room air, (2) conventional face mask oxygen therapy, or (3) no respiratory support. Randomization was performed using REDCap (https://project-redcap.org/) with a computer-generated sequence. Due to the nature of the interventions, blinding was not feasible.

Interventions

Patients allocated to the NHF group received humidified room air via an NHF system (Airvo™ 2, Fisher & Paykel Healthcare, New Zealand) at a target flow rate of 40 L/min for 150 min following the arrival of a patient in the general ward. The duration of 150 min for NHF provision was determined based on the standard criteria outlined in Nagasaki University Hospital’s clinical pathway for acute postoperative respiratory care, with the aim of ensuring patient safety following surgery. To enhance tolerance, the flow rate was initially set at 20 L/min for the first 30 min and increased thereafter. Patients in the face mask group received oxygen at 5 L/min according to standard postoperative care. Patients in the control group breathed room air without respiratory support.

Anesthesia

The method of general anesthesia was standardized to maintain anesthesia with AOS (Air-Oxygen-Sevoflurane anesthesia: sevoflurane concentration 2%, FiO2 0.4) or TIVA (total intravenous anesthesia maintained with propofol 4 to 6 mg/kg/hr at a target effective site concentration of 3 µg/mL) with remifentanil. General anesthesia was induced with propofol 1.0 to 1.5 mg/kg and remifentanil 0.3 to 0.5 µg/kg/min. Analgesia was managed with fentanyl 0.5A and remifentanil (0.15 µg/kg/min). At the end of surgery (approximately 20 min before extubation) fentanyl 0.5A was administered to suppress irritation during removal of the tracheal tube. The extubation criteria for anesthetics and analgesics were set as follows: sevoflurane exhaled concentration ≤ 0.2%, propofol effective site concentration ≤ 1.5 µg/ml, and fentanyl concentration 0.6 ng/ml to 2 ng/ml. After surgery, patients were transferred from the operating room to the general ward with oxygen administered via a face mask under full consciousness.

Safety outcomes

Safety outcomes included the incidence of hypoxemia (SpO₂ < 90%) and clinically marked hypercapnia (tcpCO₂ ≥ 60 mmHg). Serious adverse respiratory events were also recorded.

Exploratory physiological outcomes

Exploratory physiological outcomes included respiratory rate, transcutaneous oxygen (tcpO₂) and carbon dioxide (tcpCO₂) partial pressures, oxygen saturation (SpO₂), and heart rate. Monitoring devices used were: TCM5 FLEX (Radiometer, Copenhagen, Denmark) – noninvasive continuous monitoring of transcutaneous O₂ (tcpO₂) and CO₂ (tcpCO₂) partial pressures using probes placed on the ear or anterior chest; and Radical-7® (Masimo, California, USA) – noninvasive continuous monitoring of SpO₂ and the respiratory rate every 2 s using a fingertip sensor and a neck-mounted respiratory sensor. Standard bedside monitoring (Nihon Kohden, Japan) was used for blood pressure, ECG and SpO₂ as part of routine postoperative care. Data from the TCM5 and Radical-7 devices were continuously recorded on an internal memory device for later analysis. These outcomes were considered hypothesis-generating and were included to inform endpoint selection and sample size estimation for future confirmatory trials.

Sample size

Since no previous studies have addressed this objective, valid endpoints for efficacy were unknown. Furthermore, there were no available data on which to base a sample size calculation. Therefore, this study was conducted as an exploratory investigation, and depending on its outcomes, a subsequent validation study will be planned.

The study is exploratory and aims to collect information for conducting verification research. Therefore, the sample size is set based on the feasibility. There are about 240 patients who undergo oral surgery within 5 h under general anesthesia at Nagasaki University Hospital in 1 year. If we estimate that about 60% of the cases in the 4-month (80 cases) are available for obtaining consent, target sample size is 48. The 48 cases of oral surgery will be divided into the 3 groups: NHF with room air, 16 cases; face mask oxygen administration, 16 cases; and no respiratory support, 16 cases.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were descriptive in nature. Continuous variables were summarized as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Point estimates and 95% confidence intervals were reported for exploratory outcomes, without formal hypothesis testing, consistent with the feasibility design.

Results

A total of 49 patients were screened and each provided their informed consent. One patient was withdrawn due to postponement of surgery, resulting in 48 patients who completed the study protocol. The recruitment and retention proportion was 97.9%. All randomized patients completed the assigned postoperative respiratory management and monitoring period, demonstrating high recruitment and protocol completion rates.

NHF therapy was generally well tolerated. No patients required permanent discontinuation of NHF due to discomfort. The patient tolerance and acceptability of NHF treatment during the acute postoperative phase was 100%. Protocol adherence was high, with no unplanned crossovers between groups. Continuous physiological monitoring data was successfully collected for the majority of the planned monitoring period in all groups, indicating good data completeness.

Respiratory function

The change of tcpCO₂ and SpO₂

No serious adverse respiratory events occurred during the study period. The incidence of hypoxemia (SpO₂ < 90%) and clinically marked hypercapnia (tcpCO₂ ≥ 60 mmHg) was comparable across the three groups, supporting the safety of oxygen-free NHF in this postoperative setting. The incidence of severe hypercapnia (tcpCO₂ ≥ 60 mmHg) was 4 (29%) in the NHF group, 5 (31)% in the no respiratory support group and 5 (31%) in the face mask oxygen group, with no differences among groups. Similarly, the incidence of moderate hypercapnia (tcpCO₂ ≥ 50 mmHg) did not differ: 8 (57%) in the NHF group, 10 (63%) in the no respiratory support group and 11 (73%) in the face mask oxygen group (Table 2). The incidence of hypoxemia (SpO₂ ≤ 90%) was 1 (7%) in the NHF group, 1 (6%) in the no respiratory support group and 0% in the face mask oxygen group. The change of respiratory rate

Exploratory analyses showed that patients receiving NHF exhibited a lower average respiratory rate compared with patients in the control and face mask oxygen groups. Importantly, this reduction in the respiratory rate was not associated with higher tcpCO₂ levels, suggesting preserved ventilation. Discussion

This randomized feasibility study demonstrates that oxygen-free NHF therapy can be implemented safely, reliably, and with high protocol adherence as postoperative respiratory management in the general ward following oral and maxillofacial surgery under general anesthesia. All key feasibility objectives – including patient recruitment, allocation, treatment delivery, tolerance of NHF, and completeness of continuous physiological monitoring – were successfully achieved. These findings establish that the study procedures and intervention are operationally viable in a general ward environment, a prerequisite for any future effectiveness trial.

Exploratory physiological observations showed that NHF was consistently associated with a lower respiratory rate and pulse rate compared with conventional postoperative management strategies. While this study was not designed to evaluate clinical effectiveness, these findings suggest a physiologically plausible reduction in respiratory workload and cardiorespiratory demand. Importantly, the respiratory rate was not used as a surrogate endpoint for hypoxemia but as an indicator of respiratory effort, and the observed reductions occurred without evidence of carbon dioxide retention, supporting the physiological safety of oxygen-free NHF.

Episodes of oxygen desaturation were infrequent across all study groups, including patients managed without supplemental oxygen. This finding supports the feasibility of withholding routine postoperative oxygen supplementation in selected low-risk patients under structured monitoring conditions. The present study does not claim that oxygen-free NHF prevents postoperative hypoxemia; rather, it demonstrates that this strategy can be applied without an apparent increase in hypoxemia or clinically marked hypercapnia during the acute postoperative period.

The rationale for exploring oxygen-free respiratory strategies is reinforced by increasing evidence that liberal oxygen administration may be harmful. In this study, to evaluate the appropriateness of oxygen therapy via face mask – the standard respiratory management protocol immediately following oral surgery (150 min post-surgery) at Nagasaki University Hospital – we compared two intervention groups (NHF and no respiratory support) with the face mask oxygen administration group. The rationale for this group (NHF with room air, without supplemental oxygen) was to evaluate the effect of NHF as a form of respiratory support independent of supplemental oxygen. Excessive oxygen exposure is known to increase reactive oxygen species production, leading to oxidative stress, cellular injury, and activation of apoptotic pathways [4–8]. Several studies have suggested that supplemental oxygen is not mandatory when oxygen saturation exceeds predefined thresholds in the immediate postoperative period [15, 17–19]. Given the low baseline incidence of hypoxemia in this surgical population, the absence of clinically relevant differences between groups in the present study is consistent with prior literature and underscores the feasibility of more conservative, physiologically guided oxygen strategies.

Current international guidelines emphasize the importance of titrating oxygen therapy to target saturation ranges to avoid both hypoxia and hyperoxia [9, 10]. In this study, a substantial proportion of patients receiving face mask oxygen exhibited supraphysiological oxygen saturation values, whereas patients managed with oxygen-free NHF spent less time in hyperoxic ranges. Although clinical outcomes were not assessed, this observation highlights a potentially important advantage of oxygen-free NHF: the ability to support ventilation while minimizing unnecessary hyperoxia. The integration of automated oxygen titration into NHF systems represents a logical extension of this approach and warrants evaluation in future trials [20].

In routine postoperative care, particularly after head and neck surgery, supplemental oxygen is often administered prophylactically for several hours due to concerns regarding residual anesthetic effects, respiratory depression, and transient upper airway obstruction. The findings of this study indicate that NHF with humidified air can be delivered safely in the general ward even in patients who remain mildly sedated. This approach maintains immediate access to supplemental oxygen that can be entrained into the air-flow of the NHF device if required, while offering a potentially more physiologically targeted form of respiratory support.

Previous physiological studies have demonstrated that high-flow nasal cannula therapy unloads the respiratory muscles and reduces the respiratory effort after extubation [21]. Consistent with these findings, the present study showed lower respiratory rates in the NHF group without evidence of impaired ventilation. The lack of observed differences in carbon dioxide levels should be interpreted cautiously, as the study was not powered to detect small differences in gas exchange. Optimization of flow rates and identification of patient subgroups most likely to benefit from oxygen-free NHF should be addressed in future research [22, 23].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a single-center feasibility study with a small sample size and was not designed to assess clinical effectiveness, robustness, or generalizability. All physiological outcomes were exploratory and hypothesis-generating. However, the study provides essential data – covering safety signals, protocol adherence, monitoring completeness, and preliminary variability estimates – that is necessary to justify and design a future adequately powered randomized controlled trial.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that room air breathing without respiratory support appears just as safe and effective as NHF or supplemental oxygen as postoperative respiratory management in the general ward following elective oral and maxillofacial surgery. By establishing operational feasibility and physiological safety, these findings provide a clinical insight and methodologically sound foundation for a confirmatory randomized controlled trial aimed at determining whether NHF can improve clinically meaningful postoperative outcomes.
