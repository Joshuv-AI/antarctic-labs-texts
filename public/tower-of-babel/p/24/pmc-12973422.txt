Anti-Diabetic Potential of Corn (Zea mays L.) Silk: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Preclinical Studies
Authors: Bohari Bohari, Rimbawan Rimbawan, Zuraidah Nasution, Ekowati Handharyani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to quantitatively evaluate the effect of corn silk on metabolic and oxidative stress parameters in preclinical diabetic models. After a comprehensive search across PubMed, Scopus, MDPI, and GARUDA databases, eight studies were included in this review and analysis. A random-effects model was employed to assess the pooled effect of corn silk intervention on the fasting blood glucose, insulin, superoxide dismutase (SOD), and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels compared to those of the pharmacological controls. The findings revealed that the administration of corn silk was not more effective than the control drugs in reducing the fasting blood glucose [standardized mean difference (SMD)=0.99, P<0.001, heterogeneity (I2)=76%] and MDA (SMD=0.86, P<0.001, I2=85%) levels or in increasing SOD activity (SMD=−0.93, P<0.001, I2=68%). However, the intervention proved significantly more effective at increasing the insulin levels (SMD=1.61, P<0.001). Subgroup analysis indicated that efficacy varied depending on factors such as dose, duration of intervention, and extraction method. In conclusion, while corn silk demonstrates a notable effect on increasing the insulin levels, this change was not accompanied by a corresponding improvement in glycemic control. Furthermore, its overall anti-diabetic effectiveness on blood glucose and oxidative stress markers remains inconsistent in animal models. The high level of heterogeneity observed across studies (I2>68%) substantially limits the generalizability of these results and highlights the crucial need for future research with more standardized protocols to substantiate its therapeutic potential.

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a considerable health problem worldwide, the prevalence of which is continuously increasing (Khan et al., 2020). Data from the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) indicates that 537 million adults live with diabetes, and this number is projected to reach 783 million by 2045. Furthermore, this disease results in 6.7 million deaths per year, with a total global expenditure of USD 966 billion in 2021 (IDF, 2021). The management of T2DM commonly includes a well-balanced diet, physical activity, and the prescription of oral hypoglycemic medications, including insulin (Bhatti et al., 2022). However, current treatment efforts are not optimal, as evidenced by the annual increase in T2DM prevalence. Using natural substances as antihyperglycemic agents offers a potential alternative, as they are generally regarded as safer, less toxic, and more economical when compared with conventional pharmacological medicines (Choudhury et al., 2018; Shahzad et al., 2024).

Corn (Zea mays L.) silk is a promising candidate for therapy, as it is used as traditional medicine in several countries, such as China, America, Thailand, and Malaysia (Akoonjee et al., 2023; Goyal et al., 2024). It contains bioactive compounds such as polyphenols, flavonoids, and antioxidants (Žilić et al., 2016; Tian et al., 2021; Singh et al., 2023). Furthermore, it has demonstrated antihyperglycemic effects in several preclinical studies. Research in rats has revealed its ability to lower blood glucose levels, reduce oxidative stress, and improve liver tissue damage in diabetic animal models (Zhao et al., 2012; Pan et al., 2017; El-Masry and Hamdy 2022; Firmanto et al., 2023; Hamzah et al., 2023).

Given the conflicting findings regarding the effects of corn silk on T2DM, where some studies have reported significant effectiveness while others show no substantial improvement in blood glucose levels and other diabetes-related parameters, a comprehensive systematic review thereof is required. Furthermore, a meta-analysis is crucial to quantitatively address this inconsistency and provide a more precise understanding of its overall effect. As a well-established factor in the pathogenesis of prediabetes, oxidative stress drives the development and subsequent complications of diabetes by exacerbating insulin resistance and causing damage to the pancreatic β-cells (Yesupatham and Saraswathy, 2025). Therefore, the evaluation of oxidative stress markers, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and malondialdehyde (MDA), is highly relevant to assess the therapeutic potential of anti-diabetic agents.

In this study, a systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted to quantitatively evaluate the effects of corn silk on key metabolic and oxidative stress parameters in animal models of diabetes mellitus. This research is expected to contribute to existing literature by synthesizing the current evidence on corn silk’s potential as an antioxidant therapy for T2DM. Moreover, it aims to refine our understanding of corn silk’s efficacy in the management of diabetes, thereby addressing an identified gap in the current scientific literature.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Protocol

This systematic review, which evaluates the effects of corn (Zea mays L.) silk on diabetes in animal studies, was performed according to the pre-specified protocol for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) (Moher et al., 2009).

Eligibility criteria

The reviewed articles were preclinical studies using diabetic rat models. The research objective was to analyze the effects of the administration of corn silk extract on the blood glucose, insulin, SOD, and MDA levels in diabetic rat models. Inclusion criteria consisted of: (1) the use of diabetic animal models, specifically rats; (2) corn silk intervention administered to the treatment group; (3) standard pharmacological treatment used in the control group; (4) at least one metabolic or oxidative stress parameter was measured (i.e., blood glucose, insulin, SOD, or MDA); (5) article type was classified as a research article; (6) articles published in Scopus-indexed journals and, for Indonesian journals, achieved an accredited rank at least Sinta 2; (7) there were no limitations on publication year or country of origin of the research; and (8) sufficient statistical data (mean, standard deviation, and sample size) for the intervention and control groups. Exclusion criteria consisted of: (1) incomplete articles (abstract only); (2) gray literature, such as research reports, unpublished undergraduate theses, master’s theses, or dissertations; and (3) review articles.

The following list summarizes the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study (PICOS) criteria applied for the study inclusion and exclusion criteria in this review:

-P (Population): diabetic rat models

-I (Intervention): corn silk extract intervention

-C (Comparison): administration of standard anti-diabetic drugs

-O (Outcome): blood glucose, insulin, SOD, and MDA

-S (Study Design): experimental

It should be noted that the selection of four specific outcomes (blood glucose, insulin, SOD, and MDA) was established as a priority in our research protocol. The primary justification for this focus is that these four markers are the most consistent and frequently reported parameters across relevant preclinical studies.

Data sources and literature search

Electronic searches for relevant publications were performed using the PubMed, Scopus, MDPI, and GARUDA databases (Table 1). No restrictions were applied regarding the publication year. Only English-language articles were included, using the following keywords: “hyperglycemia” OR “blood glucose” for diabetes mellitus and “corn silk” OR “Maydis stigma” OR “Zea mays hairs” for corn silk.

Data collection and extraction

The initial screening of articles was performed by evaluating their titles and abstracts using the Rayyan.ai platform (https://www.rayyan.ai/). Following this, the potentially relevant articles underwent full-text review to confirm their adherence to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. To ensure methodological rigor, two independent reviewers assessed the titles and abstracts during the initial selection phase. Any discrepancies between the reviewers regarding article eligibility were resolved either through discussion and consensus or by consulting a third reviewer.

A standardized data collection form was used to extract the relevant information, including the researcher’s name, corn silk source, specific data types collected, intervention duration (days), type of control/anti-diabetic drug, animal species, animal weight (g), group sizes and interventions, injection type, and extraction method. The key indicators of diabetic conditions, such as fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels, insulin, SOD, and MDA, were also recorded. For data exclusively presented in graphical format, the numerical values were extracted from the various data visualizations using WebPlotDigitizer (http://automeris.io/).

Methodological quality assessment

The methodological quality of all the included studies was assessed using the Systematic Review Centre for Laboratory Animal Experimentation (SYRCLE)’s Risk of Bias tool (Hooijmans et al., 2014).

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager (RevMan) software (version 5.4.1). The effect of intervention was quantified as the standardized mean difference (SMD), presented with a 95% confidence interval. For outcomes where a decrease is beneficial (e.g., blood glucose and MDA levels), a negative SMD benefits the intervention group. Conversely, for outcomes where an increase is beneficial (e.g., insulin and SOD levels), a positive SMD benefits the intervention group. Heterogeneity among the studies was evaluated by calculating the I2 statistic. Furthermore, subgroup analyses were performed to explore the potential sources of heterogeneity, specifically considering the corn silk dose, intervention duration, extraction method, and type of comparator drug used.

RESULTS

Literature search outcomes

Following the systematic study selection process, as depicted in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1), a total of 202 articles were initially identified from four databases. After undergoing the stages of duplicate removal, title/abstract screening, and full-text eligibility assessment based on established inclusion/exclusion criteria, eight studies ultimately met the requirements for inclusion in this systematic review and meta-analysis.

Study characteristics

Table 2 shows that considerable methodological heterogeneity was identified across the included studies. Key variations emerged in the source and type of corn silk used (e.g., experimental farms, local markets, specific cultivars such as Zheng Dan 958, or young corn), extraction methods (such as hot water, varying ethanol concentrations, or their combination), and intervention durations, which spanned from 20 to 56 days. Moreover, the animal models exhibited diversity, including different strains of male rats [Sprague Dawley, Institute of Cancer Research (ICR), and Wistar] and mice (C57BL/6J, ICR, and Kunming) with varying body weights. While most studies induced diabetes using streptozotocin, one study employed alloxan. Significant variability was also noted in the tested corn silk extract doses and types of positive control drugs used for comparison (e.g., metformin, dimethylbiguanide, rosiglitazone, or Xiaoke Pill). Although FBG was the primary measured parameter, several studies also assessed the insulin, SOD, and MDA levels.

Overall analysis

Blood glucose and insulin: Meta-analysis of the blood glucose levels revealed a significant aggregate effect for corn silk administration in diabetic rat models compared to the control group (SMD=0.99, P<0.001; Fig. 2A). The positive SMD indicates that the intervention group had higher mean aggregate glucose levels than the control group, which suggests that corn silk administration did not demonstrate superior efficacy over pharmacological control in reducing the blood glucose levels in diabetic rats.

Conversely, the meta-analysis for the insulin levels revealed a significant aggregate effect in favor of corn silk administration (SMD=1.61, P<0.002; Fig. 2B). Here, the positive SMD indicates that the mean aggregate insulin levels were higher in the intervention group compared to the control group, demonstrating that corn silk administration was significantly more effective than pharmacological control in increasing the insulin levels in diabetic rats.

SOD and MDA: Meta-analysis of the SOD levels revealed a significant aggregate effect of corn silk administration in diabetic rat models compared to the control group (SMD=−0.93, P<0.0001; Fig. 3A). This negative SMD indicates that the mean aggregate SOD levels were lower in the intervention group than in the control group, suggesting that corn silk administration was not significantly more effective than pharmacological control in increasing the SOD levels in diabetic rats.

Similarly, meta-analysis for the MDA levels showed a significant aggregate effect of corn silk administration compared to the control group (SMD=0.86, P<0.001; Fig. 3B). This positive SMD indicates that the mean aggregate MDA levels were higher in the intervention group than in the control group, thus demonstrating that corn silk administration did not demonstrate superior efficacy over pharmacological control in reducing the MDA levels in diabetic rats.

Subgroup analysis

Blood glucose: Subgroup analyses were performed to investigate the potential sources of heterogeneity in corn silk’s effect on blood glucose levels. Regarding the administered dose, the analysis indicated no significant difference in effect across the different extract doses (chi-square=4.83, df=2, P=0.09, SMD=0.89; Fig. 4A). The lower doses of <500 mg/kg (SMD=1.14, P<0.001) and 500－1,000 mg/kg (SMD=0.71, P=0.0002), corn silk extract did not demonstrate superior efficacy over pharmacological control in reducing the glucose levels in diabetic rats (Fig. 4A).

For intervention duration, no significant difference was observed in corn silk’s effect on the glucose levels (chi-square=2.11, df=2, P=0.35; Fig. 4B). No significant effect was evident when interventions lasted 20 days (SMD=0.97, P=0.14). Moreover, for longer intervention durations of 28 (SMD=0.88, P=0.002) and 56 days (SMD=1.50, P<0.001), corn silk extract was not significantly more effective than pharmacological control in lowering the glucose levels in diabetic rats (Fig. 4B).

Regarding the extraction method, a significant difference in the effect on glucose levels was observed (chi-square=11.16, df=3, P=0.01; Fig. 4C). There was no significant effect found when corn silk was extracted with hot water (SMD=0.57, P=0.22) or 40% ethanol (SMD=−0.03, P=0.94). In contrast, extracts using a combination of hot water and 95% ethanol (SMD=1.50, P<0.001) and 80% ethanol (SMD=1.37, P<0.00001) were not significantly more effective than the pharmacological control in reducing the glucose levels (Fig. 4C).

As for the type of anti-diabetic drug used for comparison, a significant difference in corn silk’s effect on the glucose levels was revealed (chi-square=23.38, df=3, P<0.001; Fig. 4D). Notably, when rosiglitazone was used as the comparator, a beneficial effect of corn silk extract on the glucose levels was observed (SMD=−0.54). This negative aggregate SMD indicates lower mean blood glucose levels in the corn silk intervention group compared with the rosiglitazone control. Conversely, against other comparator drugs such as metformin, dimethylbiguanide, and Xiaoke Pill, these pharmacological controls demonstrated superior efficacy in lowering the blood glucose levels compared to corn silk extract.

Insulin: For the insulin levels, subgroup analysis by dose revealed no overall significant difference in the effect of corn silk extract administration (chi-square=1.20, df=2, P=0.55; Fig. 5A). Specifically, at doses exceeding 1,000 mg/kg, no significant effect on the insulin levels was observed (SMD=0.53, P=0.70). However, at lower doses of <500 mg/kg (SMD=2.41, P=0.03) and 500－1,000 mg/kg (SMD=1.81, P=0.01), corn silk extract proved more effective than the pharmacological controls in increasing the insulin levels.

SOD: When analyzing the SOD levels by dose, the subgroup analysis did not identify an overall significant difference in corn silk extract’s effect (chi-square=5.30, df=2, P=0.07; Fig. 5B). While doses of 500－1,000 mg/kg (SMD=−0.27, P=0.38) and those exceeding 1,000 mg/kg (SMD=−0.65, P=0.05) exhibited no significant impact on the SOD levels, corn silk administration at doses <500 mg/kg (SMD=−1.36, P=0.0002) were not found to be significantly more effective than the pharmacological controls in enhancing the SOD levels.

MDA: Similarly, for the MDA levels, subgroup analysis by dose revealed no significant overall difference in the effect of corn silk extract administration (chi-square=1.04, df=2, P=0.59; Fig. 5C). Doses of <500 mg/kg (SMD=0.82, P=0.07) and 500－1,000 mg/kg (SMD=0.79, P=0.38) revealed no significant effect on the MDA levels. Furthermore, at doses >1,000 mg/kg (SMD=1.37, P=0.0002), corn silk administration did not demonstrate superior efficacy over the pharmacological controls in reducing the MDA levels.

Publication bias

We acknowledge that with a limited number of included studies (n=8), the visual interpretation of the funnel plot to assess asymmetry has substantial limitations. Therefore, the potential for publication bias cannot be definitively ruled out based on this method alone (Fig. 6). Therefore, further analysis using safe N analysis was necessary, which also indicated no publication bias for any of the investigated parameters. Specifically, the calculated fail-safe N values for blood glucose (622; critical threshold: 120), insulin (151; critical threshold: 45), SOD (260; critical threshold: 85), and MDA (146; critical threshold: 85) all substantially exceeded their respective critical limits. These critical thresholds were determined using the 5K+10 formula, where K represents the number of studies for each parameter (Fragkos et al., 2014). Collectively, these results confirm the robustness of our meta-analysis findings against publication bias, thereby ensuring that the results are not distorted and supporting the scientific reliability of the conclusions drawn.

Methodological quality of studies

Evaluation of bias risk for the eight included articles indicated that most methodological domains presented a low to unclear risk of bias. This was particularly true for the sequence generation and baseline characteristics. However, domains regarding performance blinding and detection blinding exhibited a higher risk (Fig. 7). Notably, one study was classified as having a high risk of bias because it employed Xiaoke Pill as a positive control, which contains corn silk, thus potentially confounding its results. This risk was further exacerbated by using “local market” corn silk without adequate standardization, thereby introducing additional variability.

DISCUSSION

Overall, the meta-analysis revealed diverse effects of corn silk extract on the analyzed metabolic and oxidative stress parameters. Notably, while corn silk extract did not exhibit superior efficacy in reducing the blood glucose levels compared to the pharmacological controls, it was substantially more effective in augmenting the insulin levels than the control group. This finding suggests that corn silk extract might positively regulate pancreatic β-cell function to enhance insulin production. However, its influence on glucose reduction did not surpass that of the standard pharmacological medications.

However, the observed increase in insulin levels necessitates cautious interpretation. Physiologically, a rise in insulin should correspond with a decrease in blood glucose levels. The absence of a significant reduction in the glucose levels in the presence of elevated insulin, as our findings indicate, raises crucial concerns about mechanistic consistency and may suggest the presence of insulin resistance, which is a hallmark of T2DM (Lee and Yoon, 2025). This paradoxical finding suggests that while pancreatic insulin secretion may be enhanced, the peripheral tissues might exhibit compromised cellular sensitivity to insulin. This observation lessens the potential therapeutic benefit of corn silk extract, as an increase in insulin without improved glycemic control may be clinically inconsequential or even indicative of a worsening metabolic state.

Regarding the oxidative stress parameters, our meta-analysis indicated that corn silk extract was not significantly more effective than the pharmacological controls in either elevating the SOD levels or reducing the MDA levels. This suggests that the antioxidant capacity of corn silk extract might not rival that of the conventional medications used as controls. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that subgroup analyses revealed variations in effects dependent on the dose and the extraction method. For instance, lower doses of corn silk extract (<500 mg/kg) exhibited greater efficacy in improving the insulin levels, while its impact on blood glucose fluctuated based on the specific extraction technique employed.

T2DM is a complex metabolic disorder that is marked by dysfunctional carbohydrate and lipid metabolism, which results from either abnormal insulin secretion or tissue resistance to the effects of insulin. Oxidative stress is a crucial factor in the pathogenesis and progression of T2DM. The excessive production of free radicals can inflict damage upon insulin-producing pancreatic cells, exacerbate insulin resistance, and contribute to T2DM complications (Bhatti et al., 2022). Fortunately, the body possesses diverse mechanisms to counteract oxidative stress by endogenously (naturally synthesized) and exogenously (acquired from external dietary sources) generating antioxidants (Jomova et al., 2024). Antioxidants function by neutralizing surplus free radicals, thereby protecting cells from their deleterious effects and aiding in disease prevention (Pham-Huy et al., 2008).

External antioxidant sources have the potential to prevent free radical-induced harm within the body (Lobo et al., 2010). Prominent natural antioxidant sources include vibrant fruits and vegetables, green and black teas, and legumes. Corn silk has a long history of use in traditional medicine and is primarily recognized for its rich antioxidant and polysaccharide content (Dongaonkar et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Specific phytochemicals, such as myricetin, kaempferol, and quercetin, present in corn silk are known for their antioxidant properties and their potential to modulate various metabolic pathways that are pertinent to the management of diabetes and its associated complications (Bacanli et al., 2019). The range of phytochemical compounds present in corn silk may synergistically modulate the pathways implicated in the pathogenesis of diabetes and regulate blood glucose levels (Chaudhary et al., 2022). For example, water-extracted corn silk demonstrated a maximal α-glucosidase inhibitory activity of 76.19±0.00% (Khushe et al., 2024). Further research has indicated that corn silk extract can reduce blood glucose levels, increase insulin, and facilitate the recovery of damaged β-cells (Rabi et al., 2022).

Based on the subgroup analysis, several recommendations emerged for optimizing corn silk extract. An optimal dose for enhancing insulin appears to fall within the <500 to 1,000 mg/kg range. Furthermore, shorter intervention duration (<28 days) might prove more effective for reducing blood glucose levels. Regarding the extraction methods, hot water or 40% ethanol extraction can be considered, as they did not demonstrate a significant difference compared to the pharmacological controls. Lastly, corn silk extract may serve as a viable alternative to rosiglitazone, although its efficacy remains lower than that of other hypoglycemic drugs. However, these findings require further validation through additional research employing varied experimental designs.

While corn silk extract has not yet exhibited efficacy comparable to that of standard pharmacological treatments in reducing blood glucose levels, its potential as an antioxidant therapy is undeniable. Optimal T2DM management relies on a combination of diet, exercise, diabetes education, and pharmacotherapy (Fujiwara et al., 2019). Appropriate dietary interventions and consistent lifestyle modifications, including regular physical activity, are essential for regulating blood glucose levels and improving insulin sensitivity (Syeda et al., 2023; Yeh et al., 2023). Beyond medication, diet, and exercise, antioxidant therapies from sources such as vitamins and natural products also possess considerable capacity to mitigate oxidative stress and enhance pancreatic β-cell proliferation and function in T2DM (Nasri et al., 2015; Shrivastav et al., 2023). Corn silk fractions have demonstrated antioxidant effects against hydrogen peroxide- or methylglyoxal-induced oxidative stress in rat β-cell lines by ameliorating the suppression of β-cell proliferation (Chang et al., 2016). Regarding safety, acute toxicity tests indicate that corn silk does not cause mortality and is non-toxic at doses up to 5 g/kg body weight. Nonetheless, the long-term use of corn silk aqueous extract at >1,000 mg/kg may exhibit hepatotoxic effects, thus suggesting that lower doses are advisable for therapeutic applications (Ikpeazu et al., 2018). In addition, other research has demonstrated that corn silk extract at doses of 100, 200, and 400 mg/kg body weight was not hepatotoxic in rats over 28 days (Saheed et al., 2015).

To optimize this combined approach, further research is essential to determine the optimal dose, treatment duration, and potential interactions among the various therapeutic components. In addition, a personalized approach, tailored to the individual patient profile, including their genetic and lifestyle factors, could substantially enhance therapeutic outcomes. Moreover, integrating pharmacological treatment, diet, physical activity, and antioxidant therapy from corn silk and other promising plant-based sources could be adopted earlier as part of a new lifestyle for individuals at risk of developing T2DM. This holistic approach offers more effective diabetes management and better prevention of severe complications. Ultimately, this aims to control the symptoms of and address the various aspects of diabetes mellitus pathophysiology, thereby offering long-term prospects for improving patients’ quality of life.

Based on these findings, the comprehensive analysis presented in this systematic review revealed that corn silk extract exhibits a significant effect on elevating insulin levels. However, this was not matched by superior efficacy in reducing blood glucose levels or improving the oxidative stress parameters compared with those of the pharmacological agents in diabetic animal models. Moreover, significant heterogeneity across the studies (I2>50%) indicates that various factors, such as study design, dose, subject populations, or extraction methods, could influence corn silk’s mechanisms against diabetes. Further research, employing highly standardized protocols for dose levels, intervention duration, and extraction methods, is imperative to resolve these inconsistencies. Furthermore, future studies must elucidate the molecular mechanisms behind the observed increase in insulin levels without the corresponding glycemic control before proceeding to human clinical trials.
