Impact of computerised visual-motor integration and visual perception training games on handwriting and visual skills in children with spastic cerebral palsy: study protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Aida kazemnazhand_Asl, Hamid Dalvand, Hooshang Dadgar, Masoud Khorrami-Nejad, Kheirollah Rahsepar Fard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionVisual perception and visual-motor integration skills are key predictors of handwriting. Children with cerebral palsy often experience difficulties in these areas, which can negatively affect handwriting. Interventions targeting these skills may improve handwriting outcomes in this population.Methods and analysisThis single-blind (data analyst blinded), three-arm randomised controlled trial will include 30 children with spastic cerebral palsy. Background

Handwriting plays a crucial role in learning and participation.1 2 It is estimated that 30%–60% of classroom time in elementary schools is devoted to fine motor and writing activities.3 Handwriting is a complex perceptual-motor task.4 It requires various abilities such as visual-motor integration, visual perception, fine motor skills and bilateral coordination.4 Other essential components include motor planning, cognitive and perceptual skills, tactile sensations and kinesthesia.4 Visual perception and visual-motor integration are essential predictors of handwriting.5 6 Difficulties in handwriting can negatively impact children’s academic achievements, writing development, emotional outcomes, social participation, self-confidence and interpersonal relationships.7 Children with cerebral palsy (CP) often experience motor impairments in the upper extremities.8 These impairments can result in academic challenges, particularly in writing and classroom participation.8

The prevalence of CP in the world is estimated at 2–3 per 1000 births and approximately 2.6 per 1000 in Iran.9 Spastic CP is the most common subtype, accounting for 66%–82% of all cases.10 Children with CP often present with comorbid visual perception disorders,11 which affect their ability to process and integrate visual information.12 These deficits can impair foundational writing skills, such as spatial orientation, letter recognition and line alignment.12 Visual perception is also essential for reading and overall academic performance.12 The components of visual perception are vital in reading and writing.12 For example, Menken et al found that children with CP exhibited significantly more problems in visual perception sub-skills compared with typically developing peers.13 Similarly, Chang et al demonstrated that training in visual perception skills improved handwriting speed and accuracy in children with handwriting impairments.14 According to previous studies, educational achievements in children with CP are low.15 Despite the use of the less affected hand for writing tasks, poor handwriting performance remains a persistent issue among many children with CP.15 Many school-aged children with this diagnosis have the ability to learn writing, and the occupational therapy team provides interventions for these children to learn handwriting or improve their abilities in this performance.16 In addition, there are differences in the abilities of visual-motor integration between normal children and children with CP, which can lead to the weakening of handwriting performance in children with CP.17 Case-Smith reported that incorporating visual-motor integration activities in school-based occupational therapy significantly improved handwriting legibility.18 Studies report that up to 81.3% of children with CP exhibit writing challenges, including unevenness and unrecognisability of words19 20 and increased writing time due to effortful motor control.3

Recent technological advances have provided new opportunities for rehabilitation.21 These include the development of intensive, meaningful, motivational and home-based programmes.21 In recent years, occupational therapists have begun to use various technologies in their interventions.22 Interventions that are based on occupation-as-means, using client-centred activities to improve clients’ specific impairments, can have better effects.23 24 Computer-based interventions fall into this category of occupations.23 24 In recent years, there has been great interest in using computerised games for patients with brain cell damage (such as CP).25

Many researchers consider computerised games a complementary tool in the rehabilitation of children with CP.26 These games can improve clinical outcomes and enhance children’s active participation by increasing motivation and extending the frequency and duration of therapeutic activities or exercises.26 27 Such factors are crucial for addressing challenges like handwriting difficulties in children with CP.28

A review of previous studies reveals that various interventions have been used to promote handwriting in children with CP. These include changes in pencil grip posture,29 modifications to desk and school furniture design29 30 and the use of computer-based technologies as writing tools.31 Other interventions involve robotic technologies,32 lower-body stabilisation techniques,33 passive assistive devices8 and intensive computer-based training programmes.34

However, prior interventions have rarely targeted the core predictors of handwriting performance (namely, visual perception and visual-motor integration) in children with CP.

A review of previous studies shows that most available software or games for children with disabilities focus on other types of disorders like autism spectrum disorder.35 In contrast, those computerised games that are designed for children with CP have focused more on the rehabilitation of motor functions.35 However, less attention has been paid to other critical areas such as handwriting, visual perception and visual-motor integration. This trial aims to examine the effects of computerised training games targeting visual-motor integration and visual perception on handwriting performance in children with spastic CP in the second and third grades of elementary school. We hypothesise that these interventions will lead to greater improvements in handwriting legibility, visual perception and visual-motor integration compared with traditional handwriting practice.

Methods and analysis

Specific aims

The primary aim of this randomised controlled trial is to examine the effects of computerised visual-motor integration and visual perception training games on handwriting, visual perception and visual-motor integration in children with spastic CP enrolled in the second and third grades of elementary school.

Study design

This study will be a single-blind (data analyst blinded), three-arm randomised controlled trial with a superiority design. A total of 30 children with spastic CP will be randomly allocated to one of three groups: (a) computerised visual-motor integration training, (b) computerised visual perception training or (c) control group. Stratified permuted block randomisation will be applied, stratifying participants by CP subtype (hemiplegia, diplegia and quadriplegia), as detailed in the randomisation section. Baseline assessments (T₀) will include: the Snellen E-chart for visual acuity, the Gross Motor Function Classification System-Expanded and Revised (GMFCS-E&R) for gross motor level, the Manual Ability Classification System (MACS) for manual ability, the SPARCLE cognition form for cognitive screening, which was derived from the impairment form used in the SPARCLE project, the Persian Handwriting Assessment Tool (PHAT) for handwriting performance, the Beery-Buktenica Developmental Test of Visual-Motor Integration (Beery VMI) for visual-motor integration and the Test of Visual Perception Skills-Revised (TVPS-R) for visual perception. Eligible participants who

meet all inclusion criteria will receive their allocated intervention. Each intervention group will receive 20 sessions of training. Postintervention assessments (T₁) will be conducted approximately 2.5 months after randomisation, using the same outcome measures (PHAT, Beery VMI and TVPS-R). Ethical approval has been obtained from the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1402.057). The trial has also been registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT ID: IRCT20230219057447N1; registration date: 25 May 2023).

Setting

This study will be conducted in selected schools and occupational therapy clinics across Tehran. Interventions will be delivered individually by the first author in quiet, designated rooms. The following centres have agreed to participate: Hami, Hasti, Zehni and Tavanyab clinics, as well as Imam Ali, Imam Reza and Haji Babaee special schools.

Participants

Inclusion criteria are as follows: children who (1) have a diagnosis of spastic CP confirmed by a neurologist;36 (2) are in the second or third grade of elementary school;37 (3) are classified at levels I, II or III of the GMFCS-E&R;36 (4) are classified at levels I, II or III of the MACS;38 (5) are able to write with a pencil;33 (6) have a cognitive score ≥70 as assessed by the SPARCLE cognition form;37 (7) do not achieve a perfect score on the PHAT;37 (8) demonstrate difficulties in visual perception skills based on the TVPS-R22 and (9) do not achieve a perfect age-based score on the Developmental Test of Visual-Motor Integration.39

Exclusion criteria include: (1) corrected near visual acuity ≤0.8 decimal (using the Snellen E-chart);33 40 41 (2) presence of any manifest eye deviation (eg, strabismus);33 (3) history of orthopaedic surgery in the upper extremities within the past 6 months or botulinum toxin injection within the last 3 months;33 (4) absence from ≥2 intervention sessions;42; and (5) withdrawal of consent or unwillingness to participate at any stage of the trial38 (see box 1).

Patient and public involvement

This study is being conducted by a rehabilitation-engineering research team. We consulted with experts in occupational therapy, speech therapy, optometry and engineering to select appropriate intervention types and delivery methods. However, participants and their families were not involved in the study design. Following parental consent, children will participate in evaluation and intervention sessions. The results of this randomised controlled trial will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications. Additionally, we plan to share the findings with educational authorities, as well as with families, caregivers and children with motor disabilities.

Interventions

The study includes two types of computerised visual training games:

Visual-motor integration game (Raya-Pooya)

This intervention is adapted from the German CogPack software43 and targets visual-motor integration skills (figure 2). The Persian version, Raya-Pooya, has been validated by cognitive science experts.44 It includes multiple stages, progressing from easy to difficult levels.

Examples of the visual-motor integration game. (A): In this section, the child must be able to lead the mouse to the cheese by finding the right path. (B): In this section, a car moves along the streets, and the child must memorise this path and then show which streets the car was moving on. (C): In this section, the child must direct the skater’s movements and catch the ball, preventing it from hitting the ground. (D): In this section, various shapes appear on the page, and the child must be able to copy them.

Visual perception game

This computerised game is based on clinically used visual perception card sets (figure 3). The digital version has demonstrated high face validity compared with the original paper-based materials45 and includes graduated stages targeting various components of visual perception.

Example of visual perception game. This game trains the seven skills of visual perception. For example, in the visual discrimination section, the child must recognise a shape that is different from the others.

Participants will be assigned to one of the following three groups:

Visual-motor integration group

Participants in this group will receive the Raya-Pooya computerised training alongside traditional handwriting practice. Each 45-min session will involve four stages of the visual-motor integration game, delivered individually two times per week over 10 weeks.

Visual perception group

Participants in this group will engage in the computerised visual perception training combined with traditional handwriting practice. Similar to the visual-motor integration group, each session will last 45 min, include four stages of gameplay and be delivered two times per week over 10 weeks.

Control group

Participants in this group will receive only traditional handwriting practice, without any computerised training.

Traditional handwriting practices

These consist of structured remedial handwriting exercises commonly delivered by occupational therapists at participating schools and clinics. The sessions include a mix of sensorimotor-based activities (eg, writing with clay, tracing in sand or using textured surfaces) and paper-and-pencil tasks targeting handwriting skills such as letter formation, copying and tracing. Therapists involved in the study will be briefed about the intervention protocol to ensure consistency across settings. Prior to study initiation, all therapists involved in delivering traditional handwriting practices will attend a briefing session to ensure protocol consistency. Each session will last approximately 45 min and will be conducted two times per week, matching the duration and frequency of the computerised interventions.6 22

Before each computerised session, children will be briefly instructed about the game stages. If errors occur during gameplay, the child will be asked to repeat the task until it is completed correctly. All computerised activities will be performed using a desktop computer and mouse.42 43 46

Outcome measures

Primary outcome measures

The Persian Handwriting Assessment Tool (PHAT)

Developed by Havaei et al, the PHAT evaluates handwriting legibility and copying speed among second- and third-grade elementary school students. The tool consists of words extracted from school textbooks, which children are asked to copy. Handwriting is rated on five legibility components: letter formation, spacing, alignment, letter size and slant. Each component is scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very poor) to 5 (very good), except for letter size, which ranges from 1 (very small) to 5 (very large). Copying speed is assessed using two indicators: (1) the total time taken to complete the task and (2) the number of letters written within 1 min.47 48 The PHAT has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity in Iranian school-aged children.1

Supplementary functional handwriting task

To enhance ecological validity, an additional functional task will be included. Children will be asked to write a short, freely chosen text in 1 min, under classroom-simulated conditions. The writing samples will be analysed based on legibility (using selected criteria from the PHAT), dictation (number of correctly written words) and writing speed (number of words or letters written in 1 min).

Secondary outcome measures

Secondary outcomes include visual perception and visual-motor integration skills, which will be assessed using two standardised tools:

Beery-Buktenica Developmental Test of Visual-Motor Integration (Beery VMI)

The long form of this test consists of 24 geometric figures arranged from simple to complex, designed for individuals aged 2–18 years. The child is asked to copy the figures, and scoring is based on a standardised acceptance/rejection rule. Items 1–10 are scored one point each, items 11–15 score two points each, items 16–21 score three points each and items 22–24 score four points each.49 The Persian version of the Beery VMI has demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties, including a significant correlation with age and a test–retest reliability coefficient of 0.72 (p<0.001).39

Although no universally accepted minimal clinically important difference (MCID) has been defined for the Beery VMI in typically developing children, previous studies in children with CP suggest that an improvement of approximately 7.31 to 11.49 points (roughly 0.5 SD) may indicate a clinically meaningful change in visual-motor integration.50 In the present study, changes of this magnitude or improvements towards normative age-based scores will be interpreted as clinically significant.

Test of Visual Perception Skills-Revised (TVPS-R)

The TVPS-R assesses seven domains of visual perception without requiring motor responses, making it suitable for children with motor impairments, such as CP. Raw scores are converted to standard scores for each subtest.51 In a normative sample of children aged 7–13 years in Tehran, the following mean standard scores were reported: visual discrimination (92.73), visual memory (94.06), spatial relationships (102.24), form constancy (92.43), sequential memory (96.25), figure-ground (102.16) and visual closure (92.88). Scores below these averages may indicate visual perceptual deficits. The Persian version of the TVPS-R has been validated for use in children aged 2–13 years and has demonstrated high reliability.52 Currently, no established MCID exists for the TVPS-R. Therefore, in this study, clinically meaningful improvement will be interpreted as progress towards normative age-based scores, particularly when children move from below-average to average ranges.

Background variables

Baseline sociodemographic variables, including age, gender, type of spastic CP and educational grade, will be recorded. In addition, to confirm eligibility, visual acuity, cognitive level, gross motor function and manual ability level will be assessed at baseline.

Sample size calculation

The sample will consist of children with spastic CP, selected based on availability. The required sample size was estimated using a one-way ANOVA (fixed effects, omnibus) and based on an effect size of 0.7, as reported by Poon et al,6 who studied improvements in handwriting speed following a computerised intervention. Although that study used the Penmanship Objective Evaluation Tool (POET) rather than PHAT, both instruments assess core aspects of handwriting performance, particularly speed and legibility. Additionally, the scoring methods and reporting formats of the two tools are similar.

Based on this effect size, the calculated minimum required sample size was 24 participants. To account for an anticipated attrition rate of 10%–25%, we plan to recruit approximately 30 children with spastic CP, with 10 children allocated to each of the three study groups.

Allocation

A stratified permuted block randomisation method will be used in this trial. Participants will first be stratified based on the type of spastic CP into three strata: hemiplegia, diplegia and quadriplegia. Within each stratum, participants will be randomly assigned to one of the three study groups: group A (visual-motor integration intervention), group B (visual perception intervention) and group C (control group). Thus, randomisation will be performed separately within each CP subtype to ensure balance across groups. To achieve allocation concealment, we will use sealed, opaque envelopes made with foil and carbon paper to prevent any visibility of group assignment. Since we are not certain about the exact number of participants in each CP subtype in advance, and to follow recommendations from similar studies (eg,https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=WOSwQSsAAAAJ&hl=en&oi=sraDoig et al), we will prepare more than the planned 30 envelopes, specifically 81 envelopes in total, consisting of 27 for each group (A, B and C). These envelopes will be distributed across three Tupperware-style containers, each labelled according to the CP subtype (hemiplegia, diplegia and quadriplegia). In each container, nine envelopes from each group (A, B and C) will be placed (totalling 27 envelopes per stratum). These envelopes will not be mixed between strata. Within each stratum, random blocks of size 3 and 6 will be created, and envelopes will be shuffled within each block. To determine the sequence of blocks, a coin-flipping method will be used: if the coin lands on heads, a block of size 3 will be selected; if tails, a block of size 6. This process will be repeated until a complete block sequence is generated for each stratum. Once the block order is finalised, it will remain fixed. Subsequently, envelopes within each stratum will be numbered sequentially: H1–H27 for hemiplegia, D1–D27 for diplegia and Q1–Q27 for quadriplegia. As each participant enters the trial, after being classified by CP subtype, the next available envelope in that stratum will be assigned to them in sequence. Importantly, this entire randomisation and allocation process will be performed by an independent researcher who is not involved in recruitment, intervention delivery or outcome assessments, in order to minimise potential bias.53

Assessment procedures

Prior to trial enrolment, baseline evaluations will be conducted as follows. Based on training provided by an optometrist, the Snellen E-chart will be used to assess best-corrected near visual acuity.54 55 The GMFCS-E&R and MACS will be administered to determine gross motor function and manual ability levels, respectively.56 57 The SPARCLE cognition form will be used to evaluate cognitive status.58 59 Handwriting performance will be assessed using the PHAT47 48 and an additional functional handwriting task. Visual perception will be assessed with the TVPS-R,51 and visual-motor integration will be evaluated using the Beery VMI.49

After completing 20 intervention sessions or approximately 2.5 months following randomisation, all children will be re-evaluated using the same instruments for visual perception, handwriting performance and visual-motor integration (see table 1).

Data management

Personal data of participants will be handled confidentially. Each participant will be assigned a unique identification code, and all data will be recorded using this code to ensure anonymity. Access to the final dataset will be restricted to the research team. Data collection will be performed by the first author, an occupational therapist with clinical experience, who will also administer the intervention. To maintain accuracy and consistency, all assessment forms and intervention records will be reviewed weekly by the corresponding author. Additionally, the computer engineer on the teams will oversee data entry, organisation and analysis using SPSS software (V.26). All digital files will be stored on a secure, password-protected device.

Statistical analysis

The statistical approach will follow the intention-to-treat principle. Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation methods to minimise bias due to dropout.

Descriptive statistics (means, SDs and frequencies) will be used to summarise baseline sociodemographic data and outcome measures.

Effectiveness analysis will be conducted in three stages:

Within-group analysis: preintervention and postintervention scores of PHAT, Beery VMI and TVPS-R (T₀ vs T₁) will be compared within each group using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, depending on the normality of data.

Within-group analysis: preintervention and postintervention scores of PHAT, Beery VMI and TVPS-R (T₀ vs T₁) will be compared within each group using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, depending on the normality of data.

Between-group comparisons at baseline (T₀): one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests will be used to compare baseline scores across groups. If significant baseline imbalances are found in outcome variables, Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) will be performed for postintervention comparisons, adjusting for the corresponding baseline scores as covariates. If parametric assumptions for ANCOVA are not met, a non-parametric alternative (eg, rank ANCOVA) will be used.

Between-group comparisons at baseline (T₀): one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests will be used to compare baseline scores across groups. If significant baseline imbalances are found in outcome variables, Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) will be performed for postintervention comparisons, adjusting for the corresponding baseline scores as covariates. If parametric assumptions for ANCOVA are not met, a non-parametric alternative (eg, rank ANCOVA) will be used.

Between-group comparisons postintervention (T₁): one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests will be used to compare postintervention scores. If significant, posthoc pairwise comparisons will be conducted using the Bonferroni correction to control for type I error. If Bonferroni is deemed overly conservative due to the number of comparisons, Holm-Bonferroni adjustments may be considered.

Between-group comparisons postintervention (T₁): one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests will be used to compare postintervention scores. If significant, posthoc pairwise comparisons will be conducted using the Bonferroni correction to control for type I error. If Bonferroni is deemed overly conservative due to the number of comparisons, Holm-Bonferroni adjustments may be considered.

Finally, exploratory subgroup analyses will be conducted to assess whether the intervention effects differ by CP subtype. Although the study is not powered for subgroup analyses, these will provide valuable preliminary insights.

Data monitoring

An internal monitoring process will be used due to the low-risk nature of the intervention. A structured session log will be completed after each session by the first author to track protocol adherence and child engagement. These logs will be reviewed weekly by the study supervisor. Interim data monitoring will be conducted every 2 weeks to check data completeness, protocol deviations and any adverse events. No formal data monitoring committee is planned.

Discussion

Current evidence regarding the effectiveness of computerised visual training games on handwriting, visual perception and visual-motor integration skills in children with spastic CP is limited and inconclusive. This study will be the first randomised controlled trial to evaluate the separate effects of computerised visual-motor integration and visual perception interventions on handwriting performance in children with CP.

Previous handwriting-related studies have largely overlooked children with CP, which represents a novel focus of this study. Moreover, in most existing studies, visual-motor integration and visual perception interventions have been delivered simultaneously to the intervention group,618 60,62 making it difficult to determine the individual contribution of each component. In contrast, our study aims to isolate and compare the effects of visual-motor and visual perception training on handwriting outcomes.

One limitation of this trial is the absence of long-term follow-up. As only a short-term (2.5 month) postintervention evaluation is planned, the durability of treatment effects remains uncertain. Future research should incorporate longer-term follow-up assessments to evaluate the sustained impact of such computerised interventions.
