Targeting and antioxidant surface-engineered mesenchymal stromal cells for enhanced erectile dysfunction therapy
Authors: Tan Ye, Xianghua Zhong, Shuting Wang, Wenwen Zhong, Ani Chi, Muyuan Chai, Peng Shi, Xuetao Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractErectile dysfunction compromises men’s quality of life and fertility. Mesenchymal stromal cell injection therapy holds clinical promise, yet its efficacy is limited by poor cell retention and survival in the corpus cavernosum due to rapid blood flow, elevated reactive oxygen species and inflammation. Here, we show that mesenchymal stromal cells surface-engineered with polydopamine nanoparticles conjugated to antibodies against vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 exhibit enhanced retention, survival, and therapeutic efficacy in erectile dysfunction. We demonstrate that the multivalent antibodies guide the cells to inflamed cavernosal endothelium, while polydopamine mitigates reactive oxygen species‑induced damage and improves the inflammatory microenvironment. We show that the underlying engineering process is rapidly achievable under mild conditions, minimizing the impact on cell viability. We find that surface-engineered mesenchymal stromal cells display improved survival under high reactive oxygen species stress and enhanced targeting capabilities in vitro. In rat models of erectile dysfunction, we demonstrate that the engineered cells achieve robust cavernosal retention, reduce local reactive oxygen species and inflammation, reverse tissue pathology, and restore erectile function. We further validate their efficacy in beagle models, confirming cross-species translational potential. Thus, this study provides a facile and translatable surface engineering strategy to overcome key bottlenecks in mesenchymal stromal cell-based erectile dysfunction therapy.Subject terms: Nanobiotechnology, Biomaterials, Nanoparticles, Mesenchymal stem cells

Introduction

Erectile dysfunction (ED), characterized by recurrent or persistent inability to achieve or maintain penile erection, affects 30–64% of men aged 40–79 years, with rising prevalence and younger onset1–3. Penile erection relies on neural stimulation of endothelial cells in the corpus cavernosum to release nitric oxide (NO), which then mediates smooth muscle relaxation, thereby inducing cavernous sinusoidal dilation and subsequent veno-occlusion4–6. Nerve or vascular impairment and tissue fibrosis disrupt the erectile cascade to cause ED, often arising from cavernous nerve trauma induced by surgical procedures or diabetic vascular/neuropathic complications7–9. Notably, extensive tissue inflammation and elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) level play a pivotal role in the early pathogenesis of ED, as they interact reciprocally to form a self-reinforcing “ROS-inflammation cycle” that inflicts irreversible damage to the corpus cavernosum10–12. Current clinical approaches, including NO-enhancing pharmacotherapies that are short-acting and carry long-term adverse effects and penile prostheses with risks of infection, inflammation and mechanical failure, fail to address underlying tissue damage and may even accelerate ED progression13–15.

Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) therapy has recently garnered attention for its robust paracrine secretion of growth factors, inflammatory regulators, and matrix metalloproteinases, and it has shown promise for ED treatment by repairing damaged corpus cavernosum tissue, with clinical application research initiated16–18. However, the high blood flow velocity and excessive ROS in the cavernosal vessels of ED patients result in poor retention and apoptosis of injected MSCs19–21. Additionally, MSCs are unable to effectively disrupt the ROS-inflammation cycle in the corpus cavernosum, which significantly impedes tissue repair processes22–24. Consequently, current MSCs-based therapies often require multiple injections, yet their overall efficacy remains limited25. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop strategies addressing ROS and poor retention issues to optimize MSC therapy for ED.

Bioengineering techniques such as preconditioning and gene editing have enhanced MSC viability and functionality. However, these approaches carry risks including short-lived efficacy and potential carcinogenesis26–28. In contrast, non-genetic cell modification, a safe and stable approach, enables the modification of cells with nanoparticles, biomacromolecules or bioactive materials to tailor stem cell functions29–31. For instance, studies have used intracellular magnetic particle loading or attachment to magnetically controlled robots to enhance MSC retention in the corpus cavernosum, but the complex procedures—including pre-treatment and post-injection magnetic field control—impair cell viability, reduce productivity, and inevitably introduce other potentially harmful components into the body32–34. In addition, the vast majority of the aforementioned cell engineering strategies fail to effectively disrupt the ROS–inflammation cycle within the corpus cavernosum, thereby hindering tissue repair27,28,33–38. We hypothesize that combining MSCs with high-density antibodies and ROS scavengers via a more facile and cell-friendly surface engineering method will endow MSCs with desirable targeting and antioxidant multifunctionality, thereby promoting ED tissue repair and facilitating clinical translation.

Here, we present a facile, cell-friendly MSC surface engineering strategy that boosts MSC enrichment and survival in the corpus cavernosum, scavenges ROS in tissues to reshape the pathological microenvironment, and thereby enhances the MSCs' therapeutic efficacy for ED. Specifically, Polydopamine (PD) nanoparticles are first prepared, onto which multiple vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM1) antibodies are covalently conjugated to form VPD particles. Prior to MSCs therapy, these VPD particles are adsorbed onto the MSC membranes via Ca2+-mediated physical bridging to obtain VPD-MSCs. PD is chosen for its efficient ROS-scavenging activity—protecting MSCs and antibodies from inactivation, disrupting the cavernosal inflammation-ROS cycle—along with safety and abundant reactive sites. High-density anti-VCAM1 antibodies target upregulated VCAM1 in inflamed cavernosal endothelial cells (ECs), enhancing MSC targeted adhesion and homing post-intravascular injection. Notably, VPD particles can be stored until MSCs are ready for use, and the surface modification process can be completed within 15 min under mild conditions at extremely low cost. In rat and beagle ED models, VPD-MSCs exhibit high in vivo targeting and viability, regulate the immune microenvironment, reduce fibrosis, promote vascular and nerve repair, and restore erectile function (Fig. 1a). This study advances MSCs-based ED therapy toward clinical application and establishes a versatile, cost-effective surface engineering paradigm to optimize stem cells for treating tissue injury and inflammatory diseases.

Results

Surface engineering of MSCs with targeting and ROS scavenging capabilities

The process of MSC surface engineering is illustrated in Fig. 1a. PD nanoparticles with a diameter of approximately 200 nm were synthesized via oxidative polymerization initially. Antibodies were then covalently conjugated to PD nanoparticles through Schiff-base and Michael addition reactions by mixing the two components (Supplementary Fig. 1a)39. For antibody selection, given that MSCs are typically administered intravascularly into the corpus cavernosum in therapeutic settings, we targeted proteins specifically expressed on cavernous ECs. In common ED models, whether neurogenic or diabetic, elevated ROS and inflammation are associated with increased expression of specific endothelial adhesion molecules such as VCAM1. These molecules mediate leukocyte adhesion and infiltration, thereby exacerbating inflammation3,9,40,41. We selected anti-VCAM1 to modify PD nanoparticles to obtain VPD particles. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed dense immunogold-labeled anti-VCAM1 antibody spots distributed on the surface of VPD (Fig. 1b). Stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (STORM) images further demonstrated overlap between fluorescently labeled anti-VCAM1 antibodies and PD nanoparticles, confirming successful conjugation of anti-VCAM1 to PD (Fig. 1c). Harsh washing with 1 M NaCl, 1% SDS, or pH 12 buffer retained ≥88% of the fluorescence, supporting that the antibodies are predominantly covalently attached via Schiff‑base/Michael addition rather than by simple physical adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 1b). FITC‑labeled VPD retained ~91% of its fluorescence after 24 h incubation in 10% FBS‑containing DMEM, indicating good stability under physiologically relevant conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Nanoparticle tracking analysis determined the PD particle concentration as 3.23 × 109 particles/mL, and ELISA measured the residual anti‑VCAM1 antibody in the supernatant after conjugation as 0.00158 mg/mL, yielding an estimated surface density of approximately 520 antibody molecules per VPD particle (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the VPD functionalization strategy achieves stable, high‑density antibody conjugation via covalent linkage, providing a robust nanoplatform for subsequent MSC surface engineering. The resulting VPD particles were stored at −20 °C until use.

VPD particles were incubated with MSCs in a Ca2+/Tris-HCl environment for 8 min. Calcium ions coordinated with catechol groups in VPD particles and simultaneously interacted electrostatically with the negatively charged MSC membrane (e.g., via phosphate groups); this Ca2+-mediated bridging adsorbed VPD particles onto the cell surface, yielding VPD-MSCs. Including centrifugation and washing steps, cell modification was completed within 15 min. Confocal bright-field imaging revealed that PD-MSCs exhibited a markedly rougher surface morphology than native MSCs (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Furthermore, both scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed the presence of PD nanoparticles attached to the cell membrane (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 2b). Fluorescence from FITC-labeled anti-VCAM1 antibodies around the cells further confirmed successful modification of VPD on MSC surfaces (Fig. 1e). The flow cytometry results further confirmed that the efficiency of VPD modification on the MSC surface reached 96.9%, indicating that each cell was decorated with approximately 3230 nano-VPD particles (Fig. 1f). Flow cytometry analysis showed that FITC‑labeled VPD bound to MSCs only in the presence of Ca2⁺, and this binding was abolished by the Ca2⁺ chelator EDTA, confirming the essential bridging role of Ca2⁺ (Supplementary Fig. 2c). The stability of VPD on the MSC surface was evaluated by incubating FITC‑VPD‑MSCs in serum‑containing medium for up to 24 h under 70 rpm shaking, which resulted in ~52% retention of initial fluorescence, demonstrating good retention under physiological conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Regarding Tris buffer safety, MSC viability remained 99.7% after 8 min exposure (the actual incubation time used) and only decreased to 89.9% after 20 min, indicating that the short 8‑min incubation does not cause significant cytotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. 2e). We next examined whether VPD modification affected the viability or functional properties of MSCs. Results from both live/dead staining and CCK-8 assays demonstrated that the survival rates of PD-MSCs and VPD-MSCs were comparable to those of unmodified MSCs, both immediately after modification and 24 h later (Supplementary Fig. 3). Considering that the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs in tissue regeneration largely depends on their paracrine activity42, we subsequently assessed cytokine secretion profiles after 3 days of culture. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, the levels of secreted paracrine factors were not significantly different among native, PD- and VPD-modified MSCs. Compared to other strategies, our Ca2⁺-mediated approach achieves rapid (15 min), high-density antibody conjugation under mild conditions without compromising cell viability or paracrine function.

PD nanoparticles possess multiple reactive surface groups that not only serve as a linker between antibodies and cells but also exhibit potent catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities, attributed to moieties such as phenolic hydroxyl and quinone groups (Supplementary Fig. 1)43,44. Their adsorption onto MSC surfaces is expected to regulate environmental ROS levels and protect MSCs via a barrier effect (Fig. 2a). Exposing MSCs subjected to different treatments to a high ROS environment to monitor their scavenging capacity for O₂•⁻, H2O2, and •OH revealed that both PD- and VPD-modified MSCs exhibited enhanced ROS scavenging capabilities. Specifically, their O2•⁻ and H2O2 scavenging capacities increased to approximately 68% and 27%, respectively, whereas native MSCs demonstrated poorer performance (Fig. 2b–d). Intracellular ROS levels, detected using the DCFH-DA assay, revealed lower fluorescence intensity in PD-MSCs and VPD-MSCs, significantly lower than that in native MSCs under H₂O₂ exposure (Fig. 2e, f). Cell viability under oxidative stress was assessed by CCK-8 and live/dead staining. After 1 h of 500 μM or 1000 μM H₂O₂, native MSC viability dropped to approximately 52.3% and 36.8%, whereas PD-MSCs and VPD-MSCs remained above 81% and 74%, respectively, with fewer dead cells at 1000 μM (Fig. 2g,h). Under prolonged exposure to 250 μM H2O2 (near the physiological ROS limit), engineered MSCs maintained >84% (12 h) and >70% (24 h) viability, markedly higher than native MSCs (~56% and ~30%)(Supplementary Fig. 5). Thus, PD modification confers ROS resistance and significantly improves MSC survival under both extreme and pathological oxidative stress conditions. To assess antibody-mediated specific adhesion of VPD-MSCs, engineered and native MSCs were injected into a flow chamber chip seeded with C166 cells (highly expressing VCAM1)45 (Fig. 2i). Evaluation of MSCs retention in the chip showed more adhesion in VPD-MSCs compared to native MSCs and PD-MSCs, with the number of adherent cells increased by approximately 117% compared to native MSCs and 129% compared to PD-MSCs (Fig. 2j, k). Thus, VPD-loaded MSCs exhibit strong ROS-scavenging ability and effective targeting toward VCAM1-highly expressing cells, holding potential to enhance MSCs' therapeutic efficacy in vascular inflammatory diseases by improving MSC targeting, retention, survival, and microenvironment regulation.

Enhanced corpus cavernosum tissue repair capacity of VPD-MSCs in ED rats

Erectile function relies upon the interaction between nerves and blood vessels6,46. Radical prostatectomy (RP) results in damage to the cavernous nerves, subsequently triggering downstream tissue hypoxia and cell apoptosis, which in turn leads to cavernous inflammation and fibrosis3. Rat cavernous neurogenic ED models were successfully constructed by squeezing the cavernous nerves (CN) on both sides of major pelvic ganglion (MPG) in the rat prostate (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 6a). Seven days after model establishment, immunofluorescence (IF) and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analyses of rat cavernosa revealed upregulated VCAM1 and Interleukin-6 (IL-6) expression, indicating that nerve injury induced downstream vascular inflammation (Supplementary Fig. 6b–d). Similarly, upregulated VCAM1 and IL-6 expression was also observed in cavernosal tissues of diabetic ED mice (Supplementary Fig. 7). The neurogenic ED model was selected for this study due to its clear etiology, reversible nerve injury, and strong clinical relevance, making it suitable for rapid evaluation of MSCs therapeutic efficacy. In contrast, the diabetic ED model is complicated by multiple pathogenic factors, long treatment cycles, and interference from other comorbidities.

Seven days after rat model establishment, native MSCs, PD-MSCs, and VPD-MSCs, all stained with the membrane dye DiD prior to surface engineering, were injected into the rat corpus cavernosum. Hematological monitoring revealed a mild increase in white blood cell counts shortly after MSCs injection across all groups, with values returning to baseline by day 7 (Supplementary Fig. 8a). In vitro hemolysis assays further confirmed that VPD-modified MSCs maintained extremely low hemolytic activity (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Following a one-month VPD-MSCs treatment cycle, no substantial inflammatory responses were observed in rat major organs (Supplementary Fig. 8c). These results indicate that VPD-MSCs exhibit sufficient in vivo safety. MSC retention was analyzed via in vivo fluorescence imaging at various time points post-injection. Results showed high retention of all three MSC types in the corpus cavernosum one day after injection, presumably due to the trapping effect of the dense microvascular network within the cavernosa. However, MSC and PD-MSC retention declined rapidly over time, potentially attributed to insufficient adhesion under blood flow impact and oxidative stress-induced apoptosis and clearance. In contrast, VPD-MSCs maintained higher fluorescence intensity, showing the best retention at the 7-, 14-, and 28-day time points compared to other groups. A partial increase in fluorescence intensity was observed in the early post-injection phase, potentially attributable to the capture of VPD-MSCs entering the circulatory system through specific targeted binding (Fig. 3b). Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of isolated organs (penis, heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) at 7 days post‑injection revealed that DiD signals were predominantly concentrated in the penile tissue, with only minimal signals detected in other organs. Additionally, PE‑labeled anti‑VCAM1 antibody signals colocalized with DiD, providing preliminary evidence for the co‑localization of antibodies and MSCs (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 9). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that VPD modification enhances MSC retention in the corpus cavernosum following a single local intracavernosal injection. Importantly, repeated VPD-MSCs administration (once weekly for four weeks) in rats did not elicit overt immune reactions in the corpus cavernosum or major organs, and white blood cell counts in routine blood tests were near or remained within normal levels after each injection. Considering that repeated dosing could potentially induce neutralizing antibodies, serum collected from rats after multiple injections was used to pre-treat VPD-MSCs; this serum treatment did not substantially impair the efficacy of VPD-mediated targeted adhesion, either in vitro or in vivo (Supplementary Fig.10). These results support the feasibility of repeated dosing in clinical applications.

Seven days post-injection, cavernosal tissues from different treatment groups were sectioned to visualize DiD-labeled MSC distribution. MSCs and PD-MSCs groups showed relatively low MSC retention, while the VPD-MSCs group exhibited stronger, uniformly distributed fluorescence signals within cavernosal vascular sinuses (Fig. 3d). Quantitative analysis of MSC fluorescence area confirmed significantly higher retention in the VPD-MSCs group compared to other groups, which was ~19-fold and ~three-fold those of the native MSCs group and PD-MSCs group, respectively (Fig. 3e). To further clarify the in vivo stability and efficacy of the surface engineering, MSCs were labeled with DiD, PD with ATTO 680 NHS, and anti-VCAM1 antibody with PE. The results showed that the VPD-MSCs group exhibited the strongest DiD signal, and the ATTO 680 and PE signals were highly colocalized with DiD, clearly demonstrating the spatial consistency of all three components in the target tissue (Fig. 3f).

To further quantify in vivo MSC retention, survival, surface modification stability, ROS scavenging, and immune modulation, cavernosal tissues were harvested and digested for flow cytometry analysis on day 7 post-treatment. To assess MSC retention and surface engineering stability, MSCs, PD and anti-VCAM1 antibody were pre-labeled with DiD, ATTO-680 and PE, respectively. Flow cytometry revealed that the VPD-MSCs group exhibited the highest proportion of DiD-positive cells (~1.87%), compared to ~0.4% in the MSCs group and ~0.67% in the PD-MSCs group (Fig. 3g, h). Among DiD-positive cells, the percentage of live cells (Calcein-AM⁺/DAPI⁻) was ~62.5% in the VPD-MSCs group, significantly higher than in the PD-MSCs (~19.7%) and MSCs (0%) groups (Fig. 3i, k, Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). Furthermore, within the DiD-positive population, ~53.56% of cells were positive for both ATTO-680 and PE, indicating that the majority of VPD remained stably associated with MSCs at day 7; the other groups showed negligible double-positive signals (Fig. 3j, k, Supplementary Fig. 11c). These results demonstrate that VPD modification enables stable retention of both the nanoparticles and the targeting antibody on MSCs in vivo, leading to enhanced MSC adhesion and survival. In contrast, PD-modified and unmodified MSCs exhibited poor tissue adhesion, and unmodified MSCs tended to lose viability and be cleared. In a separate experiment, DCFH-DA staining was used to evaluate ROS levels in cavernosal tissue. The VPD-MSCs group showed the lowest percentage of DCFH-DA-positive cells (~23.1%) and the lowest median fluorescence intensity among all groups, with a median value approximately 0.24-fold that of the saline-treated group, indicating efficient ROS scavenging enabled by sufficient PD retention (Fig. 3l, m, Supplementary Fig. 11d). Macrophage polarization was further evaluated by flow cytometry. PE-anti-CD68 antibody was used to label total macrophages, while FITC-anti-CD86 and FITC-anti-CD206 were used to mark M1 and M2 polarized subtypes, respectively. To eliminate non-specific signals, dead cells were excluded by DAPI, and the analysis of M1 and M2 populations was restricted to CD68⁺ macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 11e). The results showed that, compared with the saline group, the VPD-MSCs group exhibited a significantly reduced proportion of CD86⁺ M1 macrophages (~16.3%) and a markedly increased proportion of CD206⁺ M2 macrophages (~76.53%) within the CD68⁺ population (Fig. 3n–q). In summary, the flow cytometry results demonstrated that, at day 7 post‑injection, VPD‑MSCs retained the majority of their surface modification in vivo. This surface engineering strategy enhanced MSC adhesion and survival, reduced tissue ROS levels, and induced predominantly M2 macrophage polarization.

Four weeks after MSCs injection, functional recovery of the rat corpus cavernosum was assessed in the different treatment groups. A biosignal acquisition and processing system was used to real-time monitor carotid arterial pressure and intracavernous pressure (ICP) in rats, followed by 1 min of specific electrical stimulation of the cavernous nerve to mimic physiological erectile signals (Fig. 4a). The saline group showed a small ICP increase that declined rapidly after peaking, with a max ICP/mean arterial pressure (MAP) ratio of ~31.8%, confirming ED occurrence. In contrast, the other treatment groups exhibited varying degrees of ICP elevation; notably, the VPD-MSCs group showed a steady ICP increase to a high level sustained until the end of stimulation, with an ICP/MAP ratio of ~76% significantly higher than other treatment groups (Fig. 4b, c). These results indicate that VPD-MSCs injection during disease progression effectively restores cavernous erectile function. To further assess vascular function, rats were injected with papaverine hydrochloride to induce penile erection, and cavernous blood flow was evaluated via Doppler ultrasound. The sham group showed a marked increase in blood flow signals after erection, while the ED group had minimal detectable signals. MSCs, PD-MSCs, and VPD-MSCs groups exhibited a gradual increase in blood flow signals, demonstrating that VPD-MSCs treatment promotes recovery of cavernous vasodilation and engorgement (Fig. 4d). ED typically impairs mating behavior and thus fertility47. Post-treatment male rats were caged with two female rats each, and the number of newborn rats was monitored over 49–55 days post-treatment (Fig. 4e). ED rats rarely sired offspring, whereas treated rats—with partial erectile function recovery—could perform mating behavior to produce offspring. Importantly, all female rats caged with VPD-MSCs-treated males produced offspring, indicating substantial restoration of fertility (Fig. 4f, g, Supplementary Fig. 12).

Cavernous function is tightly linked to the integrity of vascular, neural, and stromal tissues, and tissue repair of the corpus cavernosum was further assessed in the different treatment groups. Masson’s trichrome staining of cavernous sections—with collagen fibers stained blue and smooth muscle tissue red—was performed, and the smooth muscle-to-collagen area ratio was calculated to assess cavernous fibrosis. Results showed high fibrosis levels in the saline group, while fibrosis was progressively reduced in the MSCs, PD-MSCs, and VPD-MSCs groups. The smooth muscle-to-collagen area ratio in these three groups was approximately 1.17-fold, 1.57-fold, and 1.89-fold that of the saline group, respectively, indicating that VPD-MSCs treatment was the most effective in alleviating fibrogenesis (Fig. 4h, i). IF staining for α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), cluster of differentiation 31 (CD31), neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS), endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and neurofilament (NF) was used to evaluate the status of smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, and nerve cells in cavernous tissues, respectively. The saline group exhibited the lowest expression of α-SMA, CD31, nNOS, and eNOS, confirming ED-associated apoptosis of vascular and neural cells. MSCs treatment increased the expression of these proteins, with the VPD-MSCs group showing the most significant upregulation, indicating superior tissue repair capacity. Furthermore, tissue IL6 expression levels decreased significantly following VPD-MSCs treatment, reflecting reduced tissue inflammation. However, NF expression showed no significant intergroup difference, possibly reflecting an upstream neural repair event requiring longer observation (Fig. 4j–m, Supplementary Fig. 13). Subsequent RT-qPCR analysis of gene transcription levels following VPD-MSCs treatment revealed upregulation of Bcl-2 and downregulation of Caspase-3, indicating enhanced cellular anti-apoptotic capacity. Concurrently, increased expression of nNOS and eNOS suggests a potentially stronger NO production capability, consistent with the corresponding IF results (Supplementary Fig. 14). Collectively, these results demonstrate that VPD-MSCs injection substantially reduces cavernosal fibrosis, mitigates apoptosis of vascular- and neural-related cells, repairs damaged cavernous tissues, and thereby restores erectile function in rats.

Biological mechanism analysis of VPD-MSCs therapy to promote cavernous tissue repair in ED rats

The cavernous nerve serves as a critical signaling conduit between the central nervous system and the penile corpora cavernosa, orchestrating erection initiation, maintenance, and sensory modulation. Under physiological conditions, it sustains vascular tone and cell proliferation via continuous neurotransmitter release to prevent tissue atrophy and fibrosis; during sexual arousal, neurotransmitters trigger endothelial NO synthesis, inducing smooth muscle relaxation and vasodilation48. Cavernous nerve injury disrupts tissue homeostasis, leading to a marked surge in ROS. ROS activate the RhoA/ROCK pathway to upregulate TGF-β1—subsequently driving smooth muscle apoptosis and extracellular matrix (ECM) synthesis, which promotes collagen deposition49. Concurrently, ROS enhance NF-κB-mediated VCAM1 expression, facilitating leukocyte migration and infiltration to exacerbate local inflammation; this observation aligns with our previous histological observations3.

To delineate the therapeutic mechanisms of VPD-MSCs in ED, we performed RNA sequencing on cavernous tissue post-treatment. VPD-MSCs elicited far more extensive transcriptomic reprogramming than conventional MSCs: relative to the ED model group, 1232 genes were upregulated and 1107 downregulated in the VPD-MSCs group, whereas only 197 genes were upregulated and 275 genes were downregulated in the native MSCs group—this difference likely stems from the unique targeting enrichment and microenvironment-modulating capacities of VPD-MSCs (Fig. 5a). Gene expression heatmaps revealed key pathological and therapeutic signatures: the ED model exhibited high expression of inflammatory/ROS-related genes (Ncf2, Cxcr2, Ccl24) and reduced levels of proliferation-associated (Mki67, Vegfa) and anti-apoptotic (Birc3) genes, indicating impaired cell proliferation and enhanced apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 15). After VPD-MSCs treatment, vascular repair genes (Flt1, Pdgfa, Pigf and Ednrb), nerve regeneration genes (Ngf, Nrtn and Sox6), and the M2 macrophage-associated marker Cd200r1 were significantly upregulated, while M1 macrophage-associated marker (i.e., Cd86), the fibrotic gene Col17a1 and pro-inflammatory genes (Cd68, Il12rb2) were downregulated; apoptotic markers (e.g., Casp2, Casp14) were also notably downregulated (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 16a).

We further performed enrichment analyses on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) after VPD-MSCs treatment (compared with the saline-treated group) and the specifically regulated genes (compared with those after native MSCs treatment) (Supplementary Fig. 16b, c). VPD-MSCs specifically upregulated genes were significantly enriched in KEGG pathways, including Rap1, PI3K-Akt, JAK-STAT and MAPK—key regulators of cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, and angiogenesis. The enrichment of calcium signaling pathways suggested enhanced cellular activities such as migration (Fig. 5b). GO terms highlighted enrichment in biological processes including cellular response to growth factor stimulus, extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, and vascular development, and molecular functions included regulation of nitric-oxide synthase activity and growth factor binding, all of which tightly linked to nerve repair, vascular regeneration, and tissue remodeling (Fig. 5c). VPD-MSCs specifically downregulated genes were enriched in KEGG pathways such as tight junction and glycosphingolipid biosynthesis, which potentially correlate with immune cell infiltration and inflammatory signaling (Fig. 5d). Corresponding GO terms included keratinocyte differentiation and tissue morphogenesis—processes associated with abnormal differentiation and fibrosis. These results suggest VPD-MSCs alleviate cavernous pathology via multiple routes, including immune microenvironment modulation and fibrosis suppression. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis was performed on the relevant pathway-related DEGs. MCODE analysis identified highly connected gene clusters centered on Cd40, Nfkbia, and Traf6—molecules pivotal for immune and inflammatory signaling. Hub genes identified by CytoHubba, such as Igf1, Stat3, Jak2, and Kit, are mostly core regulatory factors or interactive molecules in the PI3K-Akt and growth factor regulation signaling pathways, underscoring the central role of PI3K-Akt signaling and growth factor regulation in the tissue repair network50 (Supplementary Fig. 16d, e). GSEA further confirmed activation of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and cellular response to growth factor stimulus biological process, indicating VPD-MSCs enhance growth factor-mediated signaling to synergistically promote tissue repair (Fig. 5e).

In summary, following antibody-mediated adhesion of VPD-MSCs to cavernosal tissue, PD promotes macrophage M2 polarization through two synergistic pathways. First, PD scavenges ROS to suppress NF-κB activation, as evidenced by upregulation of Nfkbia/Nfkbid and downregulation of M1 markers Cd86 and Il12rb2. Second, this effect is synergistically reinforced by MSC-secreted paracrine factors, which, together with PD, relieve the inhibition of PI3K-Akt/JAK-STAT pathways and enhance the expression of the M2 marker (e.g., Cd200r1). This dual mechanism shifts the immune balance toward a pro-regenerative state. While VPD particles appeared stably associated with MSCs at day 7 (Fig. 3f–k), we cannot exclude partial detachment or host cell uptake during the therapeutic window; however, any such contribution would likely be minor, as VPD particles alone failed to achieve significant M2 polarization or tissue repair (Supplementary Fig. 17). Consequently, VPD-MSCs restore cavernous tissue and function by maintaining strong paracrine activity, coordinating immunomodulation (via M2 polarization), promoting neural and vascular repair, and suppressing fibrosis. This efficacy is attributed to VPD-based surface engineering, which enables MSCs to target lesions more efficiently, enhance survival, and modulate the tissue microenvironment (Fig. 5f), underscoring the critical value of surface engineering in optimizing MSCs-based therapies for ED.

Validation of VPD-MSCs therapy in restoring erectile function in beagle ED models

To evaluate the efficacy of VPD-MSCs therapy in large animal models, we selected the Beagle dog model for validation due to its penile morphology resembling that of humans. Bilateral cavernous nerve injury was induced in beagles, followed by VPD-MSCs injection (Fig. 6a). The blood routine results revealed a slight elevation in white blood cell count on the first day post-injection, with subsequent recovery to normal levels. Additionally, hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining of the testes demonstrated that VPD-MSCs treatment did not exert adverse effects on other reproductive organs of beagles (Supplementary Fig. 18). In vivo fluorescence imaging results showed strong fluorescence signals from labeled VPD-MSCs in the corpus cavernosum 28 days post-treatment, indicating sustained high cell retention within the target tissue (Fig. 6b). 28 days post-treatment, functional recovery was assessed. Beagles received intracavernous injection of papaverine hydrochloride and phentolamine mesylate to induce cavernous erection. B-mode Doppler ultrasound was used to evaluate cavernous blood flow during erection in beagles, revealing significantly higher blood flow signals in the VPD-MSCs group compared to the saline control group, reaching ~2.43 times that of the saline control group (Fig. 6c, d). Subsequently, ICP was measured under the same drug-induced erection, with MAP continuously monitored via the femoral artery. The max ICP/MAP ratio in the VPD-MSCs group was ~59.8%, approximately 2.2 times that of the saline group, approaching the level of sham beagles (Fig. 6e). These results confirm that VPD-MSCs also promote functional recovery of the cavernous tissue in beagle ED models. Masson’s trichrome and IF staining for CD31, α-SMA, eNOS, and NF of cavernous tissue revealed that beagle dogs treated with VPD-MSCs exhibited a ~50.5% reduction in fibrosis and increased expression of markers for smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, and nerve cells compared to the saline group (Fig. 6f–h, Supplementary Fig. 19). Corresponding RT-qPCR results demonstrated that after VPD-MSCs treatment, the mRNA levels of SMA, NF, and eNOS in cavernous tissue were increased to approximately 3.22-fold, 4.0-fold, and 1.5-fold those of the saline-treated group, respectively (Fig. 6i–k). These results indicate that VPD-MSCs hold therapeutic potential for ED in Beagles.

Discussion

ED arises from pathological changes in cavernous tissue, including vascular inflammation, neurogenic injury, and fibrosis51,52. MSCs transplantation has attracted substantial interest in ED treatment due to its inherent tissue regenerative potential53,54. However, loss of homing ligands during ex vivo expansion and the high-velocity blood flow environment impair their retention at lesion sites55,56. Additionally, their insufficient regulatory capacity and susceptibility to inactivation in the ROS-rich microenvironment collectively result in suboptimal therapeutic efficacy for ED. This study proposes the surface engineering of MSCs with VCAM1-modified PD particles to endow them with dual targeting and antioxidant functionalities, thereby addressing the limitations of current MSCs-based therapies for ED. PD exhibits potent antioxidant activity to preserve cell viability and break the ROS-inflammation cycle. Furthermore, it serves as a carrier for targeting antibodies, mediating high-density antibody modification on the cell surface to ensure efficient targeting, while potentially reducing leukocyte recruitment via competitive ligand binding55,57. This combinatorial therapeutic strategy enables superior therapeutic efficacy for ED with only a single injection of engineered MSCs. Unlike temporary‑relief PDE5 inhibitors (e.g., sildenafil, tadalafil), VPD-MSCs offer disease‑modifying repair for oral‑drug‑unresponsive pathological ED patients, despite injection acceptance and higher cost. Moreover, although VCAM1 targeting suggests broader potential for other vascular inflammatory diseases, ED-specific targeting may be compromised under non-in-situ administration to improve patient acceptance or concurrent inflammation elsewhere. Further investigation is needed for improved MSC engineering strategies, such as cavernosum-specific endothelial targeting or integration of stimuli-responsive systems.

Although several studies have developed engineered MSCs therapies for ED to enhance targeting, paracrine activity, or ROS resistance, these approaches are hindered by cumbersome cell processing, insufficient safety, limited functionality, and suboptimal efficacy, collectively hindering clinical translation (Supplementary Table 1)27,28,32–38. Our study addresses these limitations via an elegant, controllable surface modification strategy, wherein VPD particles are adsorbed onto MSC membranes through rapid, cell-friendly electrostatic adsorption. Noncovalent electrostatic binding minimizes interference with MSC motility, enabling migration from blood vessels into tissues58. In practical applications, VPD particles can be pre-synthesized and cryopreserved, then conjugated to MSCs within 15 min prior to therapeutic injection, with a processing cost as low as $13.8 per million cells (Supplementary Table 2). The simplicity, low cost, and scalability of this approach highlight its potential for developing multifunctional MSCs to address ED and other diseases, while the regulatory pathway as a cell-biomaterial combination product (FDA/NMPA) represents a key consideration for clinical translation.

In summary, we developed a VPD particle-based MSC surface-engineering strategy that enhances targeted homing and antioxidative capacity, addressing the poor retention, survival and therapeutic efficacy of conventional MSCs in ED. In rat and beagle ED models, VPD-MSCs promoted erectile function recovery and tissue repair via sustaining robust paracrine activity and potent immunomodulation. These findings position VPD-MSCs as a promising next-generation ED therapy, with the platform’s scalability and safety supporting its potential for clinical translation.

Methods

Cell culture

Rat bone marrow derived MSCs (catalog: s018) and C166 cells (catalog: m118), were purchased from iCell Bioscience Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Canine bone marrow-derived MSCs (catalog: CAXMX-01001) were purchased from Oricell Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). All cells were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free prior to experimental use. Canine MSCs were cultured in specific basal medium (catalog: BLDM-03011, Oricell) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and culture supplement (catalog: CAXMX-05001, Oricell), supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Other cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS (catalog: 164210, Procell) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ and 95% relative humidity (RH). Cells were passaged using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (catalog: 25200056, Gibco) when reaching 70–80% confluence. To ensure consistent biological activity, cells between passages 3 and 6 were selected for subsequent experiments.

Ethical statement

All animal surgeries and experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Shuiyuntian Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China; permit no.: SYT2024152) or Guangdong Institute of Advanced Biomaterials and Medical Devices (Guangzhou, China; permit no.: IBMD-ES-25005). Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats and beagles were purchased from and maintained at Shuiyuntian Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and Guangdong Institute of Advanced Biomaterials and Medical Devices, respectively, for experimental use. This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and the NIH Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The 3 R principles were strictly followed: sample sizes were determined based on prior data and statistical requirements (n ≥ 4 for rats, n = 3 for beagles); all surgeries were performed under deep anesthesia with vital signs monitored, and postoperative analgesia and antibiotics were administered as needed. Animals were housed in temperature-controlled environments with soft bedding and free access to food and water. Euthanasia was performed under deep anesthesia before tissue collection.

Preparation of VPD nanoparticles

50 mg of dopamine hydrochloride was dissolved in 25 mL of double-distilled water with the pH adjusted to 10. The mixture was stirred vigorously at room temperature for 24 h to initiate polymerization. The resulting PD was harvested by centrifugation at 13,500 × g for 20 min and repeatedly washed with fresh distilled water until the supernatant became clear. To achieve surface antibody conjugation, PD suspension (2 mg/mL) in 10 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.5) was mixed with 0.002 mg/mL of anti-VCAM1 antibody and allowed to react for 12 h. The resulting VPD particles were washed with double-distilled water and used for subsequent experiments.

Visualization of conjugated antibody on nanoparticles

The conjugation of anti-VCAM1 antibody onto nanoparticles was observed by TEM and STORM. For TEM observation, VPD was incubated with anti-rat IgG–conjugated gold nanoparticles at room temperature for 30 min, followed by washing. The washed samples were mounted on grids and examined using TEM. For STORM observation, PD was first labeled with Cy5 and then conjugated with Cy3-labeled anti-VCAM1 antibody. The resulting dually fluorescent-labeled VPD was deposited onto poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips and incubated for 30 min to allow attachment. Samples were mounted in STORM buffer and examined.

Surface engineering of MSCs with VPD nanoparticles

In both PD- and VPD-engineering processes, 2 mg of PD or VPD was added to 1 × 106 MSCs suspended in Tris−HCl buffer (10 mM, pH 8.5) containing 1.11 wt% CaCl₂. The mixtures were gently shaken for 8 min at 25 °C. The resulting PD- or VPD-engineered MSCs were collected and washed three times with PBS by centrifugation.

Characterization of VPD-MSCs

The morphology of unmodified MSCs and VPD-MSCs was examined directly using SEM and TEM. For SEM sample preparation, the harvested cells were sequentially fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 4 h and osmium tetroxide for 1.5 h at 4 °C, followed by graded ethanol dehydration (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%) before observation under SEM. For TEM analysis, unmodified MSCs and VPD-MSCs underwent the same fixation and dehydration procedures, after which the samples were infiltrated with and embedded in Epon resin. Ultrathin sections were then obtained using an ultramicrotome and subjected to TEM imaging. To verify the anchoring of VPD on MSC surfaces, PD was first conjugated with FITC-labeled anti-VCAM1, and the resulting VPD was applied to modify MSCs following the procedure described above. Subsequently, 1 × 106 VPD-MSCs suspended in 400 μL PBS were analyzed using a BD FACS Verse flow cytometer and visualized with a laser scanning confocal microscope (Ex/Em = 488/521 nm). Unmodified MSCs served as the control group.

Evaluation of multiple ROS scavenging activities

The scavenging capacities for O2•⁻, H₂O₂, and •OH were assessed using independent assay systems, as detailed below. All calculations were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions or established protocols. Intracellular ROS levels were evaluated using a ROS Detection Kit with DCFH-DA staining. Briefly, MSCs (1 × 106), PD-MSCs (1 × 106), and VPD-MSCs (1 × 106) were suspended in PBS containing 1 mM H2O2 and exposed for 1 h. MSCs without H2O2 exposure served as the negative control. Afterwards, the cells were incubated with DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 20 min. The intracellular ROS levels were subsequently visualized using a laser scanning confocal microscope. O₂•⁻ scavenging capacity of engineered MSCs was evaluated using a Superoxide Dismutase Activity Assay Kit (catalog: BC5165, Solarbio), which generates O₂•⁻ via the xanthine/xanthine oxidase reaction. Briefly, 20 μL of PBS (1×) containing 1 × 106 native or engineered MSCs was combined with 170 μL of working solution and 10 μL of reaction initiator, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. Absorbance at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader. The O₂•⁻ scavenging efficiency (%) was calculated as [1 – (Asample – Ablank) / (Acontrol – Ablank)] × 100%, where Asample is the absorbance of the sample group, Acontrol is the absorbance of the control (without MSCs), and Ablank is the absorbance of the blank (without reaction initiator). H2O2 scavenging activity was assessed using a Hydrogen Peroxide Content Assay Kit (catalog: BC3590, Solarbio). A mixture of 1 mL of 1 mM H₂O₂ solution containing 1 × 106 native or engineered MSCs, 300 μL of working solution, and 1 mL of reaction initiator was incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. The absorbance at 415 nm was recorded. The H2O2 scavenging efficiency (%) was calculated using the same formula: [1 – (Asample – Ablank) / (Acontrol – Ablank)] × 100%. •OH scavenging activity of PD-MSCs was assessed by detecting the fluorescence of 2-hydroxyterephthalic acid. Reaction mixtures containing 0.5 mM terephthalic acid (TA), 10 mM H₂O₂, and native or engineered MSCs at various concentrations were prepared in 25 mM PBS (pH 7.4). TA, a nonfluorescent probe, reacts with •OH to form fluorescent 2-hydroxyterephthalic acid (Ex: 320 nm, Em: 425 nm). After incubation for 12 h, the fluorescence spectra of the samples were recorded. The •OH scavenging efficiency (%) was calculated as [1 – (Fsample – Fblank) / (Fcontrol – Fblank)] × 100%, where F represents fluorescence intensity at 425 nm.

Adhesion study of VPD-MSCs on C166 cells

The adhesion assay of engineered MSCs to C166 cells under shear stress was conducted using a commercial ibidi flow chamber (μ-Slide I0.4 Luer). Prior to the experiment, the chamber was coated with 250 μg/mL collagen I, rinsed with PBS, and left to dry overnight. C166 cells were then seeded into the chamber, and experiments were initiated once the cells reached more than 90% confluence. Native MSCs, PD-MSCs, or VPD-MSCs (1 × 106 each), pre-stained with Calcein-AM in 10 mL PBS, were perfused through the C166-coated flow chamber at a flow rate of 4 mL/min, corresponding to a shear stress of 8 dyn/cm2, using a Longer Syringe Pump (LSP01-2A). After perfusion, non-adherent cells were gently removed with PBS, while adherent cells were visualized and quantified by an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Rat neurogenic ED model establishment and MSCs therapy

Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (220–250 g, 8–10 weeks old) were anesthetized via subcutaneous injection of sodium pentobarbital solution (40 mg/kg). The abdominal fur was shaved and disinfected with povidone-iodine. A midline abdominal incision was made to expose the prostate; the pelvic plexus (PP) was identified by blunt dissection of the dorsal prostate using a cotton swab, and the cavernous nerves were localized as the branches of the PP extending toward the penis. Bilateral cavernous nerves were crushed with surgical forceps for 2 min. Abdominal organs were repositioned, and the incision was sutured and disinfected with povidone-iodine. Rats were randomly assigned to four groups 7 days post-modeling: ED model group, MSCs group, PD-MSCs group, and VPD-MSCs group. Rats in the MSCs, PD-MSCs, and VPD-MSCs groups received intracavernous injection of 100 μL cell suspension (1 × 106 cells total per rat) into bilateral corpora cavernosa; the ED model group received 100 μL saline. A sham-operated control group was established by performing a laparotomy without cavernous nerve crushing. For different experimental requirements, MSCs were labeled with DiD before surface engineering, PD was labeled with ATTO-680 NHS Ester (catalog: FD-ATD501, Biolight Biotechnology, 1 mg per 50 mg PD), and a PE-conjugated anti-VCAM1 antibody (catalog: 200403, Biolegend) was used.

Beagle neurogenic ED model establishment and MSCs therapy

All beagles were anesthetized via intramuscular injection of tiletamine (2.5 mg/kg) in combination with zolazepam (2.5 mg/kg) and xylazine (1.5 mg/kg) for induction of anesthesia. After induction, anesthesia was maintained with 2% isoflurane via inhalation. Tracheal intubation was performed, and the animals were connected to a ventilator with continuous monitoring of vital signs (heart rate, respiration, blood pressure). The skin around the penis was disinfected with povidone-iodine, and a ventral penile incision was made to access the abdominal cavity. Following laparotomy, the prostate was exposed, and the cavernous nerves were visualized by dissecting the bilateral pelvic plexus and cavernous nerve branches posteriorly along the prostate. Among these 9 beagles, 6 were subjected to cavernous nerve injury for ED modeling. For the modeling procedure, bilateral cavernous nerves were clamped with surgical forceps for 2 min, followed by abdominal closure. ED-modeled beagles were then randomly divided into two groups with 3 animals per group. The first group was designated as the ED group, which received intracavernous injection of saline into the bilateral corpora cavernosa 7 days post-ED modeling. The second group was the VPD-MSCs group, which received intracavernous injection of VPD-MSCs (7.5 × 106 cells total) into bilateral corpora cavernosa 7 days post-ED modeling. Considering the significant interspecies differences, the injection dosage was referenced to the clinical treatment dosage for human ED per kilogram rather than extrapolated from the rat dose25. The remaining 3 beagles served as the sham-operated control group. This group underwent the same laparotomy and exposure of cavernous nerves as the modeled groups, but without cavernous nerve clamping, and abdominal closure was performed afterwards.

Flow cytometry analysis

One week after MSCs injection, the corpus cavernosum tissue was harvested, minced, and digested for 1.5 h in a digestion solution containing 3 wt% collagenase types I, II, and IV dissolved in basal medium. The digested tissue was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer, followed by red blood cell lysis on ice for 5 min, and terminated by PBS containing 10% FBS. After centrifugation, the cell pellet was resuspended in PBS, and dead cells were excluded by DAPI staining. For intracellular ROS measurement, the single-cell suspension was stained with DCFH-DA according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For macrophage polarization analysis, the injected MSCs (including VPD-MSCs, PD-MSCs, and unmodified MSCs) were not subjected to any fluorescent labeling. Cells were first blocked in PBS containing 5% FBS on ice for 10 min to reduce non-specific binding. Each sample was then divided into two tubes and stained sequentially with either CD68 (catalog: 201003, Biolegend, 1:100)/CD86 (catalog: 200305, Biolegend, 1:100) antibodies or CD68/CD206 (catalog: CL488-18704, Proteintech, 1:200) antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C, respectively. After each staining step, cells were washed twice by centrifugation. Flow cytometry was performed on a Thermo Fisher Bigfoot cytometer. Single‑stained and fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls were used for compensation and gating. The gating strategy included: FSC/SSC to exclude debris, FSC‑H/FSC‑A for singlets, and DAPI for live/dead discrimination. Within CD68⁺ macrophages, CD86⁺ (M1) and CD206⁺ (M2) populations were determined by comparison with isotype controls (Supplementary Fig. 11). DCFH‑DA median fluorescence intensity was used for ROS quantification. Data were analyzed with FlowJo v10 and GraphPad Prism 8.

ICP and MAP measurement

Rats were anesthetized by injecting sodium pentobarbital solution. Subsequently, the carotid arteries and cavernous nerves of the rats were exposed. The blood pressure changes in the carotid arteries and cavernous bodies of rats were collected using a biological signal acquisition device (TaiMeng, BL-420, China). Heparin sodium saline solutions with concentrations of 50 U/mL and 500 U/mL were prepared, respectively, to fill the probes for measuring the carotid artery and cavernous body blood pressure to prevent blood coagulation. The electrical stimulation probe was set with a delay of 4 ms, an amplitude of 5 V, a pulse width of 5 ms, a frequency of 25 Hz, and a sine wave with continuous single stimulation. During each test, the cavernous nerve was electrically stimulated for one minute, followed by the cessation of electrical stimulation. The mean carotid artery pressure was calculated as MAP, and the maximum ICP was recorded for subsequent calculations. For Beagles, the method employed to induce erection was identical to that used in ultrasound testing, as the process of induction via electrical stimulation proved excessively arduous and cumbersome.

Histological analysis

All animals were euthanized under anesthesia four weeks after treatment. The penile cavernous tissues were collected and placed in 4% paraformaldehyde fixative solution. Gradient ethanol was used for dehydration, and the tissues were then embedded in paraffin. Subsequently, 10 μm sections were prepared for HE and Masson staining following the standard procedures. For IF staining, the sections were first permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 and then blocked with 5% BSA solution. Next, primary antibodies at the recommended dilution ratios were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Fluorescent secondary antibody dilutions were used for incubation for 2 h. Finally, the nuclei were stained with DAPI staining solution. IF sections were photographed using a laser confocal microscope (Zeiss, LSM980, Germany).

Statistical analysis

All data were visualized and statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 software and Origin 2024. ImageJ 1.54 f software was employed for image processing and semi-quantitative analysis. For parametric tests, unless otherwise stated, all data were examined by Shapiro-Wilk normality and variance homogeneity tests. For comparisons between two groups, a two-sided unpaired Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. For RNA-seq data (Fig. 5a–d), differential expression was determined by DESeq2 with FDR < 0.05. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All representative results are from one of at least three independent experiments that gave comparable results.
