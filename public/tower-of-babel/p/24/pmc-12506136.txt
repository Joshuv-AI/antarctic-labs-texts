Development and psychometric validation of the women’s empowerment in dealing with intimate partner violence (WED-IPV) scale: a mixed-method study
Authors: Maryam Mataji-Amirroud, Ali Ramezankhani, Mohtasham Ghaffari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe present study aimed to develop and psychometrically validate a culturally sensitive instrument, the women’s empowerment in dealing with intimate partner violence (WED-IPV) scale, in Iran.DesignA mixed-methods research study to collect and analyse quantitative and qualitative data.SettingSocial emergency centres, healthcare centres and psychotherapy clinics in three Iranian metropolises.ParticipantsThe participants were 31 married women in the qualitative phase and 420 in the quantitative phase.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the validation of the WED-IPV scale, including the assessment of face, content, construct validity and the assessment of internal consistency through Cronbach’s alpha, test–retest reliability through intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) and stability of the factor structure. Secondary outcomes included the assessment of WED-IPV indicators in women using values obtained from levels specified according to the Interval of SD from the Mean formula for each indicator and total empowerment.ResultsThe scale development process refined the initial 72 items down to 28 key items through expert review and validity testing. Exploratory factor analysis identified five factors: self-esteem, attitude, self-efficacy, access to support resources and economic empowerment that explained 65.72% of the variance. The scale showed excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α=0.97; ICC=0.95) and valid associations with related measures. Analysis of completed questionnaires of 412 women revealed that a significant portion scored low or moderate in each domain: 41% in self-esteem, 35% in self-efficacy, 39% in attitude, 47% in access to support and 45% in economic empowerment.ConclusionsThe WED-IPV scale is a psychometrically robust, reliable and valid instrument for assessing women’s empowerment concerning IPV in Iranian contexts. Its brevity and user-friendly design facilitate routine application in primary and secondary care settings. Future research should explore cross-cultural validation and predictive validity in longitudinal studies to inform targeted interventions and policy formulation.

Introduction

Intimate partner violence (IPV) is a significant health and social issue with immediate and long-term consequences, including physical, sexual and emotional effects, particularly for women.1 The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention defines IPV as any form of physical, sexual or psychological aggression in an intimate relationship by a current or former partner.2 Statistics show that nearly one in three women globally will experience violence in their lifetime.3 A systematic review and meta-analysis in 2019 showed that the lifetime prevalence of IPV in women was 55%.4 In Iran, the prevalence of violence against women, particularly IPV, is also high. Studies have reported high rates of emotional, sexual and physical violence, as well as higher rates of sexual violence during pregnancy compared with global statistics.5 6 Specifically, the lifetime prevalence rates for IPV in general and its respective forms were reported in Iran as follows: 54.5% for overall IPV, 52% for mental violence, 18.2% for physical violence and 14% for sexual violence.7

In recent years, preventing violence against women and providing support for survivors has become a key priority in women’s health globally, and numerous interventions have been developed and implemented to address this issue.8 9 Evidence indicates that empowerment interventions have been effective in this field,10 and capacity building in women has played a crucial role in preventing IPV.4 Empowerment, as defined by the World Bank, involves increasing an individual or group’s capacity to make purposeful choices and achieve desired outcomes.11 Generally, empowerment is the process of helping disadvantaged individuals gain self-efficacy and control over resources,12 13 and for women, it occurs when they envision a different life and consider themselves capable and entitled to make decisions.14 Despite the conceptual clarity of empowerment, measuring women’s empowerment in IPV contexts remains challenging due to its abstract, multidimensional and context-dependent nature.15 16 The relationship between empowerment and the experience of violence is not uniform across different cultural and social settings, requiring tailored approaches to study these dynamics within specific sociocultural contexts.17 18

Several studies have contributed to measuring empowerment and its link with IPV, yet important gaps remain: Advancing IPV Programme Evaluation: MOVERS developed a community-based participatory empowerment measure focused on survivor safety, emphasising practical programme evaluation.19 However, its specificity to safety-related empowerment limits generalisability across broader empowerment dimensions or cultural contexts, such as in Middle Eastern settings.

Explaining Spousal Physical Violence through Dimensions of Women’s Empowerment in Pakistan identified some empowerment facets (mobility, property ownership, employment) as protective. Still, other dimensions (household decision-making, education relative to husbands) paradoxically increased IPV risk.20 This complex interplay reveals the nuanced, sometimes contradictory effects of empowerment dimensions that are not sufficiently captured by existing instruments.

The Personal Progress Scale-Revised and Persian-Version Empowerment Tool studies demonstrated that reliable and valid scales measuring psychological and multidimensional empowerment can be developed.21 22 Yet, these tools do not specifically address empowerment in the context of IPV or consider culturally specific factors that influence empowerment and violence dynamics.

Validation of empowerment scales in other populations, such as the Psychological Empowerment Scale for parents of children with disabilities and empowerment models related to substance use recovery,23 24 confirms construct validity and dimensionality but remains largely population-specific and context-specific without application to IPV.

Studies such as ‘Intimate Partner Violence in Relation to Husband Characteristics and Women’s Empowerment in Nepal’ and the ‘IMPACT Model of Women’s Empowerment in Tehran’ emphasise the importance of external factors (eg, husband’s behaviour, education, organisational employment) in empowerment and IPV outcomes.25 26 These findings underline empowerment as an interplay of individual capacity and societal influences, further complicating measurement.

Therefore, the development of effective tools in this area is essential, as it directly impacts the accuracy, reliability and validity of research results. Traditional research approaches often rely on either qualitative or quantitative methods, each of which provides unique insights but also has inherent limitations. Quantitative methods excel in providing measurable and statistically analysable data, facilitating generalisability and objectivity.27 In contrast, qualitative methods provide a deep understanding of contextual factors, user experiences and complex phenomena that are difficult to quantify.28 In recent years, integrating both approaches, known as mixed research, has been recognised as a useful strategy in tool development.29 This study aimed to develop and validate a reliable scale to assess women’s empowerment in dealing with IPV (WED-IPV) indicators using a mixed methodology to address this issue. The study included participants from diverse ethnicities in Iran to ensure the generalisability of the scale. This scale will be useful in assessing the effectiveness of interventions that promote women’s empowerment and reduce the risk of violence against women, and assist health researchers in evaluating the impact of empowerment on women’s health outcomes.

Methods and design

Patients and public involvement

Patients and/or members of the public did not participate in designing, conducting, reporting or sharing plans for this research.

Study design

The present study is a methodological investigation focusing on the design and psychometric evaluation of an instrument. It employs a convergent mixed-method approach,30 integrating both quantitative and qualitative techniques following a well-established and previously validated research protocol. Mixed-method research combines the strengths of qualitative insights and quantitative rigour, offering a comprehensive perspective that enhances the development, validation and assessment of measurement instruments.29 This approach enables researchers to explore user needs and contextual complexities qualitatively, while simultaneously establishing the reliability and validity of the instrument through quantitative analysis.31 Employing mixed-method approaches in instrument development results in more robust, user-centred and adaptable instruments. This methodology facilitates iterative refinement, aligns tools more closely with real-world applications and enhances their overall effectiveness.32 The study was conducted in 2022, spanning 11 months from February 2022 to January 2023, in five phases. An overview of the step-by-step process for developing and validating the WED-IPV scale is presented in figure 1. Throughout the study, adherence to the EQUATOR (Enhancing the QUAlity and Transparency Of health Research) guidelines for transparent reporting of mixed-methods research via the MMARS (Mixed Methods Article Reporting Standards) checklist was maintained.

Study participants (inclusion and exclusion criteria)

The target population for this scale comprised married women who actively participated in all stages of the research. This study specifically focused on severe cases of IPV that resulted in physical injury and significant psychological problems in women that necessitated referral to emergency medical, social or mental health services. Consequently, the primary inclusion criterion encompassed women who had experienced severe IPV directly, resulting in emotional and physical harm, as well as those with indirect exposure through witnessing IPV in their social circle within the past 5 years. Including participants with indirect experience is justified by the substantial psychological and social consequences associated with vicarious exposure to IPV. Such exposure can profoundly shape individuals’ beliefs, attitudes, coping mechanisms and help-seeking behaviours, potentially influencing their empowerment levels and responses to interventions or support services in ways comparable to direct survivors.33 34 This inclusive approach facilitates a more comprehensive understanding of the diverse IPV-related experiences and their nuanced impacts on women’s empowerment, enabling us to gain a broader and richer understanding of empowerment that extends beyond direct victimisation.

Additional inclusion criteria required participants to have been married to their current husband for at least 1 year, be aged 18 years or older, possess the cognitive capacity to provide informed consent, be willing to participate and share their experiences, can read and write, be in adequate physical and mental health to engage in the study and be proficient in the Persian language. Exclusion criteria encompassed failure to meet any of these inclusion requirements. Specifically, individuals who lacked literacy, cognitive capacity or mental and physical stability sufficient to provide informed consent and participate meaningfully were excluded. Furthermore, those experiencing acute psychological, psychiatric or physical crises at the time of the study were excluded to ensure the validity and reliability of the data collected.

Furthermore, experts from different specialised fields contributed to the scale’s content validity. These professionals must have at least a bachelor’s degree, skills in making tools, at least 2 years of professional experience working with abused women, scientific research experience in violence against women, and proficiency in the Persian language.

Given the sensitive nature of IPV, special attention was dedicated to safeguarding participants’ safety and well-being throughout all stages of data collection. To minimise potential psychological distress and risks, interviews were conducted in private, secure locations chosen by participants to ensure confidentiality and comfort. Researchers were trained in trauma-informed interviewing techniques, enabling them to recognise signs of distress and to respond supportively and empathetically.

Comprehensive safety protocols were implemented, including providing participants with information about local support services, counselling centres and crisis helplines specialised in IPV. Participants identified as being at immediate risk were offered confidential referrals to relevant social, psychological and legal support organisations. To further protect participant privacy, no identifying information was collected, and audio recordings were securely stored with restricted access limited exclusively to the research team. Data were anonymised during transcription and securely archived in encrypted digital formats in accordance with data protection regulations.

All participants were fully informed about the study’s purpose, their right to refuse or withdraw at any time without consequence, and the measures in place to protect their anonymity and confidentiality. Both written and oral informed consent were obtained prior to participation, with additional emphasis placed on ensuring comprehension of their rights and the potential emotional impact of discussing IPV. Detailed referral pathways, interviewer training and participant safety procedures were integral to our protocol, consistent with evolving standards in the field.

Scale development and psychometric validation

Phase I: item development

The first phase of the study consisted of creating a pool of items for scale development. This was achieved through a literature review and a qualitative study. The purpose of this stage was to extract the necessary codes to design the scale using inductive methods (face-to-face, in-depth, semistructured and open personal interviews) and deductive methods (codes extracted from the literature review).

Literature review

A comprehensive search was conducted in multiple databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus and Web of Science, up to January 2022. The search focused on studies related to IPV, domestic violence, empowerment, questionnaire development and psychometrics. Boolean operators such as ‘AND’ and ‘OR’ enhanced the search sensitivity and specificity.

Qualitative study

A voluntary sample of women from the general community and those referred to social emergency centres, healthcare centres and psychotherapy clinics in three Iranian metropolises participated in this research phase. Participants included women survivors and victims of violence, as well as women who indirectly witnessed IPV through relatives and friends. Purposeful and theoretical sampling with maximum variation (age, occupation, education, economic status) was used. Qualitative data were collected via semistructured face-to-face interviews. Before the interviews, participants were informed of the study’s goals and procedures, and informed written consent was obtained. The first author, a 42-year-old woman with a doctorate in health promotion, conducted the interviews in Persian. The interview guide was developed with the consensus of all members of the research team, drawing on a review of prior texts and identified information gaps. The guide comprised two sections: (1) demographic information and (2) dimensions of women’s empowerment in relation to IPV. The draft was pilot tested with two participants to assess clarity and relevance to the study objectives. Subsequently, a panel of five qualitative research experts evaluated the guide for validity and content coverage. Interviews were conducted using the final version of the guide, which comprised five core questions and ten supplementary questions (see online supplemental appendix 1, table S1). In light of participants’ responses and explanations, additional questions were posed to clarify issues and elicit further detail. The interviews were conducted individually, lasting between 30 and 90 min, and recorded with the consent of the participants. Data saturation, a pivotal methodological criterion in qualitative research ensuring completeness and depth, was reached after 31 interviews when no new insights, themes or patterns emerged, confirming that the data collection was sufficiently exhaustive to comprehensively address the research questions.35

Conventional content analysis guided the development of the conceptual framework and extract items to develop scale. Immediately after each interview, transcripts were entered into MAXQDA software (2020), following Granheim and Lundman’s five-step content analysis method: transcription, coding, categorisation, continuous comparison and researcher consensus.36 A pool of items was created based on the analysis of qualitative data, and consensus was reached among the researchers before including each item in the WED-IPV scale. They reviewed the main and subcategories repeatedly to ensure consistency between the extracted codes. The initial questionnaire was formulated by organising questions under each main structure. This questionnaire was in the Persian language to ensure its comprehension by the general population of the country.

Validation of the qualitative phase

In this stage, the validity of the data was assessed using the four criteria recommended by Guba and Lincoln.37 To enhance validity, the researcher had long-term involvement in the study, collecting and analysing data for 11 months. This allowed for continuous interaction with the participants. The female interviewer facilitated trust and encouraged participants to share their experiences more openly. Two independent reviewers verified the analysis process to ensure reliability. Participants reviewed interview transcripts and codes for validation, and all steps were documented to maintain transparency. To reduce bias, all authors collaboratively analysed and coded the data. Purposeful sampling with maximum variation, detailed descriptions of the context, methods and participant traits, supported transferability.

Phase II: initial scale modification

In the second stage, the content and face validity of the initial scale were qualitatively tested. A multidisciplinary panel of 12 experts, including 3 psychologists, 4 health education specialists, 2 epidemiologists, 2 psychiatrists and 1 sociologist, evaluated the items for grammar, wording, assignment and scaling. They also provided feedback on keywords, content gaps and suggested deletions. Additionally, 15 married women, each with at least 1 year of marriage and direct or indirect experience with IPV, assessed the scale’s relevance, clarity and understanding. Based on their feedback, the necessary revisions were made.

Phase III: validity assessment

In the third phase, the scale was evaluated for quantitative content validity, face validity and construct validity. Based on the results obtained in this phase, the questionnaire was revised, and necessary corrections were implemented.

Quantitative content validity

Quantitative content validity was assessed by the same team of 12 experts who conducted qualitative content validity. The content validity ratio (CVR) and content validity index (CVI) were used for evaluation. CVR determined the necessity of items using a 3-point Likert scale (necessary, not necessary but useful and not necessary) based on Lawshe’s table.38 Items with a CVR score of 0.56 or higher were retained. CVI was calculated by having the 12 experts individually assess each item on a 4-point scale for simplicity, relevance and clarity. The S-CVI was used to calculate the proportion of items that scored 3 or 4. Items with scores above 0.79 were retained, scores between 0.7 and 0.79 were revised, and scores below 0.7 were eliminated.39

Quantitative face validity

Following qualitative face validity with married women, a quantitative item impact analysis was conducted to refine the items and assess their importance. The same 15 women rated each item’s importance on a 5-point Likert scale (1=not important, 5=quite important). Items with a score of 1.5 or higher were retained for further analysis.40

Construct validity

Different sections of the questionnaire were subjected to exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to extract underlying latent factors and improve interpretability.4 41 Specifically, principal axis factoring with Varimax rotation was employed, as recommended by numerous researchers and commonly preferred when the objective is to obtain orthogonal and easily interpretable factors.42 43 In the context of instrument development, researchers often seek a simple, interpretable factor structure characterised by distinct dimensions. Varimax rotation maximises the variance of squared loadings to produce a ‘simple structure’, where each item loads highly on one factor and minimally on others, thereby facilitating the clear attribution of items to specific constructs.44 45 Moreover, Varimax’s orthogonal rotation tends to yield stable factor patterns that simplify subsequent analyses such as reliability assessment, validity testing and scoring procedures. This enhances transparency and reproducibility, producing results that are easier to interpret and communicate. This interpretability underpins the practical effectiveness of the instrument, particularly in applied evaluation settings where overlapping factors may lead to misclassification and ambiguity.45 46

The adequacy of data for EFA was assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity to ensure the factorability of the correlation matrix.47 The number of factors was determined via a scree plot, and eigenvalues above one and loadings exceeding 0.4 were considered acceptable criteria for construct validity.48 To ensure psychometric robustness, a sample size of 5–10 times the number of items was recommended.49 Given typical response rates of 60%–70%,50 the required sample size for construct validity was calculated accordingly. To enhance external validity and reduce sampling bias, participants were recruited from diverse regions.

Data collection involved both face-to-face and online surveys conducted via Google Forms, with questionnaires finalised in both formats. The study samples were selected using a combined method of random cluster sampling and convenience sampling. Four cities representing different regions of the country (north, northeast, northwest and central) were randomly selected. healthcare centres in each area were contacted to gather information on urban and rural healthcare centres, including the population of women covered and the level of cooperation from health liaisons. Clusters were formed based on the centres with the largest population coverage and active health liaisons. Four clusters, consisting of two urban and two rural treatment centres, were randomly chosen.

With the help of health liaisons, questionnaires in both paper and electronic forms were provided to eligible women using convenience sampling. They approached women at healthcare centres or via telephone and WhatsApp, explained the study, obtained informed consent and shared the questionnaire link. The sampling process was halted once a sufficient number of completed questionnaires had been obtained to assess construct validity. The collected data were analysed using SPSS V.22.

Phase IV: reliability assessment

The reliability of the scale was examined by examining the internal correlation and intraclass correlation. To check internal consistency, correlations between items were checked, and Cronbach’s alpha values were calculated for the total scale and its subscales. Cronbach’s alpha value greater than 0.7 was considered acceptable in this study.51 The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to assess the test–retest reliability over a 6-week interval in a sample of 30 married women aged over 18 years. The ICC was calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model, evaluating absolute agreement between the two time points. Values between 0.70 and 0.80, with p values less than 0.05, were considered indicative of adequate stability.52 53 Moreover, 95% CIs were calculated for each ICC estimate to provide measures of precision and reliability, in accordance with standard reporting guidelines.

Phase V: scale scoring and application

Following conventional procedures in health-related surveys, responses were recorded using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (completely agree).54 Items with negative semantic loading were reverse-scored to ensure consistency in scale orientation. Scores obtained from completed questionnaires were standardised using the Interval of SD from the Mean (ISDM) method for each subscale and the overall empowerment construct.5557 This method enables the context-specific normalisation of scores, taking into account the unique distribution and variability of empowerment levels within the studied population. Unlike fixed scoring systems, which apply uniform thresholds regardless of cultural or sociodemographic variations, this approach provides a more accurate and sensitive measurement of empowerment by situating individual scores relative to the group’s normative data. Such standardisation enhances the tool’s validity and interpretability, particularly in diverse populations where empowerment constructs and response patterns may differ.58 Consequently, scoring by SD intervals offers a pragmatic and scientifically robust framework for evaluating empowerment levels tailored to the population context.

This scoring method facilitated the classification of women’s empowerment into four distinct levels.

The ISDM-based classification of empowerment levels is as follows:

Poor level: A<μ–σ.

Poor level: A<μ–σ.

Average level: μ–σ≤B<μ.

Average level: μ–σ≤B<μ.

Good level: μ≤C<μ+σ.

Good level: μ≤C<μ+σ.

Excellent level: μ+σ≤D.

Excellent level: μ+σ≤D.

Where μ represents the mean score and σ the SD for the dimension of interest or the overall empowerment score.

To calculate the WED-IPV index and its dimensions, the mean and SD of the scores obtained by the participants for each dimension and for the total empowerment score were first calculated. These values were then applied to classify the empowerment levels for each domain and overall empowerment in the ISDM formula. Therefore, four levels of poor (A), average (B), good (C) and excellent (D) were determined for the total WED-IPV index and its dimensions (see online supplemental appendix 2).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS V.22 software in the quantitative section. The following methods were used: CVI and CVR for quantitative content validity, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and test–retest analysis for scale reliability, and EFA for scale factor structure analysis for construct validity. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to check for the normal distribution of the data. The ICC was measured using the Spearman correlation test. Correlations were considered significant at the p<0.05 level.

Results

Demographic information of the participants in the qualitative study

The qualitative part of the study reached thematic saturation with 31 participants, as no new themes or substantive ideas emerged in the final three interviews, based on iterative coding and constant comparison.59 17 women who participated in the study were abuse survivors selected from two social emergency centres and three psychotherapy clinics across three cities in the centre, north and northwest regions of Iran. An additional 14 women had indirect experiences of violence within their surroundings or among close relatives. These women were invited to participate from two urban healthcare centres and three rural health clinics in the regions mentioned. The average age of participants was 38.26±5.41 years. Regarding education, 41.7% had a diploma or below, while 58.3% held a university degree. Ethnically, 34.8% identified as Persian, 33.4% as Turk, 21.6% as Kurdish and 11.2% were from other ethnic groups. A majority, 58.6%, lived in urban areas, and 42.4% were from rural settings. Concerning employment, 73.7% were housewives, and 27.3% were employed. The average age at marriage was 23.84±4.54 years, and they had been married to their current spouses for an average of 12.31±6.63 years.

Results of the literature review

A comprehensive search of previous valid literature identified six studies on violence and women’s empowerment. Several tools were used in these studies to assess women’s empowerment, but these tools were not specifically designed for WED-IPV.

The areas examined in these studies and the tools used in them provided us with a comprehensive view of the dimensions of WED-IPV. Also, 17 items were extracted from these studies. However, the direct application of these items in the unique cultural and social context of Iranian women who experience IPV was unclear. Therefore, to solve this concern and design the questionnaire according to the cultural and social experiences of Iranian women, qualitative research was conducted. The questions obtained in the qualitative part of the study and the questions extracted from the review of scientific texts formed a pool of items and were carefully examined. The result showed that the items obtained from the review of scientific literature, in terms of content and concept, are similar to some questions from the qualitative study in the dimensions of attitude, self-esteem and self-efficacy and overlap with them. Therefore, they were used to improve the formulation of the initial instrument but were not directly integrated into the study instrument.

Results of the qualitative study

The units of analysis in this study were the interviews that were entered into the MAXQDA 2020 software after being implemented and transcribed. At this stage, the texts of the interviews were reviewed and analysed many times, and the semantic units were determined. 670 primary codes were obtained without considering the overlap, and 484 codes were obtained by the overlap. In the end, we reached 5 main themes that formed the final themes of the study in the qualitative phase and were in two general categories: internal empowerment indicators and external empowerment indicators. The primary scale was a 72-question scale that was developed based on the items obtained in the qualitative phase and the items obtained from the literature review, which was reduced to 50 items after removing duplicate items. Results of the validity and reliability of the scale of WED-IPV

Content validity

In quantitative content validity, considering the cut-off point of 0.7 for the CVI and the cut-off point of 0.56 for the CVR, 7 items were removed due to not having the minimum score, and the number of items in the questionnaire was reduced to 43 items. The overall CVI of the scale was 0.85 and its CVR was 0.82. The results of the qualitative content validity investigation led to the rewriting of 5 items and the removal of 14 items due to semantic similarity and overlapping with other items in the questionnaire. Therefore, the total number of questionnaire items reached 29 items after content validity.

Face validity

In quantitative formal validity, all the items obtained an item impact score higher than 1.5 and were retained, and in qualitative formal validity, one of the questions was edited in terms of writing.

Construct validity

Construct validity of the instrument was assessed using EFA, employing principal component extraction with retention of factors having eigenvalues greater than one and substantive interpretability. The sample size for EFA was carefully determined based on established psychometric guidelines to ensure robust and reliable factor solutions.

Specifically, we adhered to the frequently recommended item-to-participant ratio of 10:1, meaning 10 respondents per survey item, which is supported by several authoritative sources in the field.49 60 Given that our questionnaire consisted of 29 items, and a good sample size for factor analysis studies is considered to be at least 300,61 a target sample size of 300 participants was selected to comply with these recommendations and, as a result, increase the accuracy of factor loadings and construct validity. Therefore, considering the expected response rate of 60%–70% in social surveys,50 62 420 married women who met the inclusion criteria were recruited and completed the questionnaire. After excluding 8 questionnaires due to incompleteness, analyses were conducted on 412 valid responses. This sample size ensures that the item-to-respondent ratio remained sufficient to provide a stable factor structure, minimise sampling error and optimise the psychometric properties of the scale. Scale reliability

The internal consistency of the scale items was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. The alpha coefficients for each of the constructs score ranged from 0.91 to 0.96, with the overall scale achieving a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.97. These values indicate high internal consistency across the scales.

The test–retest reliability of the WED-IPV scale was evaluated through the ICC using a two-way mixed-effects model, assessing absolute agreement over a 6-week interval in a subsample of 30 married women from the target population. The ICC values for the scale’s five factors ranged from 0.89 to 0.95, demonstrating excellent reliability. Specifically, the self-esteem and total empowerment factors yielded the highest ICC values of 0.95, with 95% CIs of 0.91 to 0.98 and 0.89 to 0.98, respectively, indicating a high degree of temporal stability.

All ICC estimates were statistically significant (p<0.001), confirming the robustness and reproducibility of the instrument across repeated administrations. The narrow CIs further reinforce the precision of these reliability estimates, supporting the WED-IPV scale’s suitability for reliable assessment of women’s empowerment in this population. Result of scale scoring and application

The final scale comprised 28 meticulously crafted items (see online supplemental appendix 1, table S2), thoughtfully organised into five distinct dimensions: seven items assessing self-esteem, six capturing attitudinal aspects, seven evaluating self-efficacy, five exploring access to resources and three measuring economic empowerment.

Each participant’s score on each question was calculated using a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (lowest score) to 5 (highest score). Questions 2, 3, 6, 12, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 and 20 had negative connotations, so they were scored in reverse. In the next step, to assess the level of women’s empowerment based on the answers given to the questions, the mean and SD of the overall empowerment index and each of its dimensions were calculated separately (see online supplemental appendix 2, table S3), then they were inserted into the ISDM formula, and 4 levels of empowerment were obtained in the overall empowerment index and its dimensions.

The results showed that almost half of women fall into the low to moderate range (ie, the combined categories of ‘poor’ and ‘average’) for several key domains, highlighting limited empowerment and potential risks in the face of IPV. In particular, the overall picture of women’s empowerment index shows that more than a third of participants are not highly empowered. Almost 37% fall into the lower levels (poor and average), and only 9.2% fall into the high level, leaving a significant proportion of women lacking acceptable levels of empowerment to effectively deal with IPV. A look at other dimensions of empowerment further highlights these weaknesses. In the dimension of self-esteem, more than 40% of women fall into the suboptimal range of average and poor. About 40% of women display attitudes that may not strongly support empowerment and potentially limit effective action against abuse. The self-efficacy dimension is also of concern, with nearly 35% of women at average and poor levels, which leads to low confidence in their abilities to effectively deal with IPV. The most prominent weakness is in the dimension of access to support resources, with 19.8% ‘poor’ and 26.6% ‘average’, which in total indicates that approximately 47% of women lack or are only partially able to access social support resources, and only 16.6% have achieved ‘excellent’ access to support resources. In the economic empowerment dimension, which, according to the results obtained in the qualitative part of the study, is a critical determinant of independence and safety in the context of IPV, nearly 45%, or almost half of the women, lack sufficient economic empowerment and are at average and poor levels, and only 10% are at the excellent level (see online supplemental appendix 2, table S4)

Discussion

Violence against women is one of the important public health challenges that significantly impacts women’s well-being. Addressing this challenge requires empowering women to effectively cope with and prevent violence. Therefore, a comprehensive assessment of women’s empowerment indicators is necessary to take effective steps in preventing violence against women by strengthening their capacity. The main objective of this study was to develop and validate a new scale to measure WED-IPV among married women in Iran. A literature review identified a lack of reliable, standardised instruments for assessing these indicators within the Iranian context. Therefore, a mixed-methods approach, combining qualitative and quantitative techniques, was employed to design a culturally relevant scale that fills this gap.

Literature review and qualitative research generated an initial pool of items, which, after a five-step analysis, resulted in a 50-item questionnaire for psychometric assessment. In terms of face validity, only one item was modified based on women’s feedback regarding its wording, while no items were deleted. This indicates that the items were comprehensible and straightforward for the participants.

Content validity was established through expert consensus, with high agreement on CVR and CVI, reducing potential bias. Construct validity was assessed via EFA; the KMO index confirmed sample adequacy, indicating a very suitable sample size for factor analysis. The significance of Bartlett’s test of sphericity also confirmed that the correlation between items is suitable for EFA with orthogonal rotation.63 At this point, one question was removed from the questionnaire due to having a factor loading of less than 0.4. This question pertained to the attitude category, ‘Love for my children makes me tolerate my husband’s violence and not abandon them’. Women’s responses to this question exhibited significant contradictions. Many women who had a good level of empowerment chose the response option ‘agree’ or ‘completely agree’. This phenomenon can be attributed to the cultural and upbringing factors prevalent in Iran, where women exhibit a strong dependency on their offspring and put them first in life.64 They are willing to continue their married life and not abandon their children even in the worst circumstances.65

The reliability analysis showed acceptable internal consistency. The final scale consisted of 28 items across 5 domains: self-esteem, attitude, self-efficacy, access to support resources and economic empowerment.

Analysis of the final scale indicators showed that self-esteem accounted for the highest variance, consistent with prior research emphasising its role in reducing IPV and empowering women.16668 Higher self-esteem enables women to assert their rights more effectively, whereas low self-esteem often traps victims in abusive situations.

Women affected by violence frequently report feelings of inferiority, anxiety and dissatisfaction related to their self-image, reflecting weak self-esteem. This negative self-evaluation fosters unfavourable views of themselves and their social roles, leading many to remain in violent relationships without seeking constructive solutions.69 This concept is closely linked to women’s attitudes, the second indicator in this study that explains a significant amount of variance. Women’s attitudes have a profound impact on how they respond to violence. Not only do attitudes play a crucial role in reporting instances of violence, but they also influence how victims react to their experiences of abuse.70 Understanding attitudes helps us predict people’s previous experiences and subsequent behaviours in violent situations.71

The participants emphasised the significance of the emotional, informational and financial support that women can receive from their family, friends and society. They believed women who possess economic, occupational and educational power, as well as access to family support resources such as intellectual, emotional, economic and social support, are less likely to experience abuse from their husbands.72 Access to these resources is thus a critical component of WED-IPV. Evidence shows that stronger social ties with family and friends reduce IPV, as women find refuge and emotional support in close relationships, which diminishes their acceptance of violence.7274 Prior research also supports the notion that access to information, education and learning opportunities significantly reduces the frequency and severity of violence against women. It can also enhance adaptability in married life, especially through life skills training.7578

Economic empowerment is a key indicator in the development of WED-IPV, as it not only protects against economic violence but also enhances women’s confidence to leave abusive lives. Numerous studies globally have shown that women’s economic empowerment correlates with decreased emotional, sexual and physical IPV.79 80 Controlling personal income is particularly crucial, as it boosts women’s sense of empowerment and reduces vulnerability to IPV.81 Women with financial control can make safer choices and prioritise their well-being, whereas those without financial control are at increased risk. Furthermore, employment and income also act as protective factors against IPV.68 79 82 Women who have stable employment and a reliable source of income are less likely to experience violence from their partners. Economic stability enables them to set boundaries, seek support and ultimately leave abusive relationships. Economic empowerment also plays a vital role in combating economic violence, such as financial control, resource restriction and withholding support, which often sustains IPV cycles. Women, particularly housewives who lack employment and financial independence, are more vulnerable to economic violence and usually feel compelled to accept their circumstances. Therefore, providing women with financial independence and employment opportunities is essential for reducing IPV and fostering safer environments.

One of the strengths of this study is the use of a mixed method in the preparation and psychometric analysis of the questionnaire items. The systematic and participatory study process, the participation of experts with diverse expertise to validate the content of the scale, and the inclusion of a significant number of participants for construct validity and internal consistency assessment all contributed to the creation of a fully revised and valid scale. The scale designed in this study is a multidimensional scale appropriate to the sociocultural background of Iran and can accurately measure the empowerment of Iranian women in dealing with domestic violence.

Furthermore, the developed WED-IPV scale shows considerable potential for practical application across clinical, policy and community contexts. While empowerment constitutes a foundational principle of the WHO’s health-promotion framework, there currently exist no established standards, best-practice guidelines or recommendations for assessing empowerment within interventions.83 Therefore, in primary healthcare settings, this scale can serve as an effective screening tool to identify girls and women, especially in low-income and middle-income countries, who are at risk of IPV, so that they can be empowered to promote their health and prevent violence by benefiting from targeted empowerment interventions on time.84 By incorporating this assessment into routine health visits, particularly in maternal and reproductive health programmes, healthcare providers can better understand the multidimensional nature of empowerment and tailor support services accordingly, thereby facilitating early intervention and prevention.85

At the policy and administrative levels, this scale provides policy-makers with a culturally and psychometrically appropriate tool for evaluating existing IPV prevention and empowerment programmes. It can improve resource allocation as well as access to human, material and social resources for women by highlighting areas that need to be strengthened and enhancing the design and delivery of community-based interventions.86

Incorporating this tool into monitoring and evaluation frameworks enables a systematic assessment of programme effectiveness over time,83 and through gender-sensitive planning, equitable allocation of resources and strong monitoring mechanisms for the sustainable empowerment of women ensures an empirical basis for policy adjustments and innovations.87 In addition, impact evaluations, supported by specific empowerment tools, provide rigorous evidence to ensure that the voices of women and adolescent girls remain at the forefront of planning and research in vulnerable settings, and to secure the necessary funding and political investment to improve their empowerment, particularly through humanitarian settings.88

Furthermore, from a public health perspective, the WED-IPV scale, like other empowerment scales, facilitates the assessment of empowerment inequalities at the population level and allows for targeted outreach to vulnerable population groups.89 By providing a standardised metric for evaluating women’s empowerment, aligned with prior empowerment initiatives in social health domains,83 90 91 this scale supports a hybrid design that prioritises engagement of the target population and fosters multidisciplinary collaboration among health, social services and legal sectors, thereby enhancing the coherence and effectiveness of responses to IPV.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. While it confirms the scale’s psychometric properties, the generalisability of the findings is limited due to the sampling of participants from specific geographical regions. Despite efforts to ensure cultural diversity within the sample, further research with populations from different areas is needed to enhance the applicability and validation of the scale across broader sociocultural contexts. Additionally, the reliance on self-report measures and online questionnaires may introduce biases, favouring women with internet access and potentially reflecting social class differences, which may affect the representativeness of the findings. It is important to note that the scale was specifically designed for Iranian adults; therefore, future studies should assess its psychometric properties in other countries and cultural settings to explore potential variations among diverse populations. Further research is also necessary to examine the predictive capacity of the scale in assessing women’s empowerment levels over time.

Concerning the test–retest sample size, although this number is generally accepted for preliminary empirical reliability assessments, it presents limitations concerning broader generalisability. Ultimately, this scale represents a valid and culturally sensitive tool for operationalising empowerment constructs and evaluating intervention outcomes in future studies, although ongoing validation and refinement with larger and more diverse samples will be critical to extend its generalisability and utility across populations.

Conclusions

Violence against women, particularly IPV, is a significant public health issue with severe physical and mental health consequences.92 Empowering women to confront this problem can reduce health burdens and lower healthcare costs.93 Understanding the conceptual framework and indicators of WED-IPV is thus essential for designing effective interventions to prevent violence against women. In this research, a new scale was designed to measure the indicators of women’s empowerment in the face of violence. The design of this scale was done using a mixed study, and its validity and reliability were proven to a large extent. The scale developed in this research was a short and easy 28-question scale that was designed based on 5 factors: self-esteem, attitude, self-efficacy, access to support resources and economic empowerment. It offers a cost-effective tool for future research on Iranian women’s empowerment against violence and can assist researchers and policy-makers in evaluating the effectiveness of women’s empowerment programmes and policies.
