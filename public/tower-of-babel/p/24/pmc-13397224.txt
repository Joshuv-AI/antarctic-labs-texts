Repurposing Antifungal Drugs for Oral Cancer Treatment: Mechanistic Insights and Clinical Potential—A Narrative Review
Authors: Anju Mayadevi Nair, Bounika Esvanth Rao, Satish Srinivas Kondaveeti, Abhi Venkatshree Anand Kumar, Ruwan D Jayasinghe, Vijayalakshmi Ramshankar, Divyambika Catakapatri Venugopal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) continues to pose significant therapeutic challenges due to high recurrence rates and treatment-related toxicity. Drug repurposing offers an accelerated, cost-effective pathway to discover novel therapeutic indications for established medications. This narrative review evaluates the mechanistic rationale and clinical potential of repurposing antifungal agents, such as azoles and allylamines, as adjuvant therapies for OSCC.Methods:A comprehensive literature search was performed across PubMed and Scopus (up to November 14, 2025). Studies were selected based on their focus on antifungal-mediated inhibition of OSCC growth and metastasis. Methodological quality was assessed using the Scale for the Evaluation of Narrative Review Articles.Results:Antifungal agents, particularly itraconazole and terbinafine, exhibit potent antitumor activity by targeting key oncogenic pathways, including the Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B/mammalian target of rapamycin signaling axes. Preclinical evidence indicates that these agents induce apoptosis, inhibit angiogenesis, and sensitize OSCC cells to standard chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Specifically, itraconazole shows promise in treating SHH-high tumors, while terbinafine reduces tumor proliferation through antiangiogenic mechanisms.Conclusion:Antifungal drugs represent a biologically viable and resource-efficient strategy for enhancing OSCC treatment outcomes. To maximize clinical translation, future research must prioritize the development of nanoformulations and liposomal azoles to overcome existing bioavailability limitations.

INTRODUCTION

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is the eleventh most prevalent type of cancer worldwide.[1] The primary sites affected include the tongue, cheek, and floor of the mouth. The global estimates for new oral cancer cases and deaths in 2022 reached 389,485 and 188,230, respectively, indicating an increasing trend mainly affecting young people as well as certain geographic areas.[2] The global incidence of oral cancer stands at four per 100,000 individuals, with South Asia accounting for the maximum number of cases, with an incidence rate of 4.65 per 100,000 people.[3]

The major causes that increase susceptibility to oral and/or oropharyngeal cancer include tobacco usage, alcohol intake, betel nut consumption, and human papillomavirus infection.[4] The treatment planning for oral cancer primarily depends on the stage of presentation. Early-stage disease is managed by single-modality treatment such as surgery or radiotherapy, while locally advanced stages often require combinations of surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy.[5] For advanced stages of cancer, radiotherapy is used as a standalone radical therapy or as a postoperative adjuvant or in combination with chemotherapy.[6] Nearly half of the locally advanced cancers develop local recurrences in spite of adequate treatment. Local failures result as an outcome of disease resistance to concurrent chemoradiation, as well as the inability to attain clear surgical margins after resection.[7]

Due to the persistent treatment failure and high local recurrence rate, it is important to develop new strategies with minimal complications and greater cost-effectiveness. This narrative review synthesizes evidence on the mechanistic and clinical rationale for repurposing antifungal agents, particularly azoles, triazoles, and allylamines, as novel adjuvant therapies for OSCC. This article highlights the potential role of antifungal agents in targeting multiple oncogenic pathways implicated in OSCC progression and treatment resistance. The pharmacokinetic feasibility and combinatorial potential of these antifungals in combination with surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy in OSCC are also studied.

METHODS

A search was conducted using databases like PubMed and Scopus with the following terms: (“itraconazole” OR “fluconazole” OR “ketoconazole” OR “miconazole” OR “clotrimazole” OR “terbinafine” OR “azoles” OR “allylamines” OR “triazoles”) AND (“oral squamous cell carcinoma” OR “OSCC” OR “oral cancer” OR “buccal cancer” OR ‘tongue cancer’) on 14 November, 2025. A total of 678 (538 papers in Scopus and 140 articles in PubMed) were screened based on the title/abstract. The inclusion criteria of the article were limited to papers in which antifungals were used as a treatment modality for oral cancer. Articles focusing on the usage of antifungals for cancers other than OSCC and conference proceedings were excluded. Five original articles were obtained (in vitro and in vivo [n = 4] and in vitro [n = 1]). Two researchers independently searched, identified, and extracted the data into an Excel file [Table 1]. The quality assessment for this narrative review was done using the Scale for the Evaluation of Narrative Review Articles tool.[13]

MECHANISM OF ACTION OF ANTIFUNGALS

Antifungals are classified as azoles, polyenes, echinocandins, and allylamines. Itraconazole inhibits fungal lanosterol 14α‑demethylase (CYP51), blocking ergosterol synthesis and impairing fungal cell membrane integrity, leading to growth inhibition and cell death.[14] Clotrimazole interferes broadly with fungal cytochrome P450 enzymes and ergosterol synthesis and alters membrane permeability and function in fungal cells.[15] Miconazole inhibits synthesis of ergosterol and other membrane-associated fungal enzymes, compromising membrane integrity and leading to fungal cell lysis.[16] Ketoconazole is a potent inhibitor of multiple fungal cytochrome P450 enzymes, especially CYP51, leading to ergosterol depletion and impaired fungal membrane function.[17] An allylamine antifungal, terbinafine, inhibits fungal squalene epoxidase (SQLE), leading to accumulation of toxic squalene and depletion of ergosterol levels in fungal membranes, resulting in membrane dysfunction and fungal cell death.[18] The crosstalk between classical antifungal mechanisms and repurposed antifungals is mentioned below [Table 2].

MOLECULAR DRIVERS OF ORAL SQUAMOUS CELL CARCINOMA AND SUSCEPTIBILITY TO ANTIFUNGAL TARGETING

P53 LOSS AND RESISTANCE TO APOPTOSIS: A VULNERABILITY FOR REACTIVE OXYGEN SPECIES–DEPENDENT CELL DEATH

Loss-of-function mutations in the p53 tumor suppressor gene represent one of the most common genetic alterations in OSCC, occurring in approximately 65%–85% of cases.[19,20] The p53 protein normally functions as a critical regulator of cell cycle progression and apoptosis. In response to DNA damage, p53 activates transcription of the cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) inhibitor p21, which blocks G1/S cell cycle progression, allowing time for DNA repair. If the damage is irreparable, p53 activates pro-apoptotic genes (BAX, PUMA, and NOXA) that trigger mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and caspase-mediated cell death.[21] Mutation or loss of p53 function eliminates these protective mechanisms, allowing cancer cells to multiply unchecked and escape death—a defining feature of cancer.[22] This breakdown in p53-mediated cell death is a primary reason why patients with p53-mutant OSCC exhibit poorer responses to cisplatin-based chemoradiation and experience higher rates of local recurrence compared to those with p53-wild-type tumors.[23] These p53-mutant OSCC cells retain alternative death pathways independent of p53, including reactive oxygen species (ROS)–dependent cell death triggered by mitochondrial dysfunction and calcium-mediated cell death initiated through endoplasmic reticulum stress.[24] This mechanistic flexibility—the ability to activate death through pathways that do not require p53—represents a critical therapeutic opportunity. Antifungal agents such as miconazole[25] and clotrimazole[26] can exploit these alternative death mechanisms by elevating oxidative stress and disrupting calcium balance, thereby re-sensitizing p53-mutant OSCC cells to apoptosis. This approach is particularly valuable in the large proportion of OSCC patients whose tumors carry p53 mutations as it offers a way to restore cell death even when the conventional p53-dependent pathway has failed.

EPIDERMAL GROWTH FACTOR RECEPTOR OVEREXPRESSION AND PARALLEL PHOSPHATIDYLINOSITOL 3-KINASE/PROTEIN KINASE B ACTIVATION: DUAL VULNERABILITY IN ORAL SQUAMOUS CELL CARCINOMA

Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is overexpressed in 80%–100% of OSCC cases and constitutes a major driver of cell proliferation, survival, invasion, and angiogenesis.[27,28] EGFR is a receptor tyrosine kinase that, upon ligand binding, recruits and phosphorylates adapter proteins including Grb2, which initiates two primary downstream signaling cascades: (1) the MAPK/ERK pathway (RAS → RAF → MEK → ERK), that drives cell proliferation and gene expression changes supporting an invasive phenotype; and (2) the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B/mammalian target of rapamycin (PI3K/AKT/mTOR) pathway, which activates survival signals and metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis and lipogenesis.[29] The central role of EGFR in OSCC has led to clinical development of EGFR-targeted therapies, including the monoclonal antibody cetuximab and small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as erlotinib, which are now established components of standard-of-care treatment for advanced OSCC. However, EGFR-targeted monotherapy has severely limited the clinical efficacy: only 10%–30% of OSCC patients achieve durable responses to cetuximab, and nearly 70% of initially responsive tumors develop acquired resistance within 12–24 months of treatment initiation.[30] Mechanistic studies reveal that resistance to anti-EGFR therapy emerges through sustained activation of the parallel PI3K/AKT pathway, driven by genetic alterations and/or overexpression of upstream and downstream effectors that bypass EGFR dependence.[31] Approximately 21% of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma patients harbor activating mutations in PIK3CA (encoding the p110α catalytic subunit of PI3K), and these PIK3CA-mutant tumors are markedly more resistant to cetuximab than PIK3CA-wildtype tumors.[32] This dual dependence on EGFR and PI3K/AKT for survival and proliferation in OSCC creates a critical vulnerability that antifungal agents can exploit, AKT phosphorylation and survival signaling independently of the EGFR status. By targeting the PI3K/AKT axis that EGFR inhibitors alone cannot adequately suppress, antifungals offer a rationally designed complement to cetuximab, potentially re-sensitizing cetuximab-resistant OSCC tumors to apoptosis through a mechanistically complementary approach.[33]

PHOSPHATIDYLINOSITOL 3-KINASE/PROTEIN KINASE B/MAMMALIAN TARGET OF RAPAMYCIN SIGNALING AND RADIOTHERAPY RESISTANCE: A MULTI-PATHWAY SURVIVAL NETWORK TARGETABLE BY CHOLESTEROL-DEPLETING ANTIFUNGALS

The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling cascade is one of the most frequently dysregulated pathways in OSCC.[34] The pathways can be activated through multiple mechanisms, such as EGFR, insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF-1R) tyrosine kinase activity, loss-of-function mutations in the phosphatase PTEN (which negatively regulates PI3K signaling),[35] and activating mutations in PIK3CA.[36] The PI3K/AKT/mTOR cascade, once activated, controls cell survival by phosphorylation-mediated inactivation of pro-apoptotic proteins (BAD and FOXO3a)[37] and by driving metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis and lipogenesis through mTOR-dependent translation of metabolic enzymes.[38] It also promotes cell cycle progression through increased expression of cyclin D1 and suppression of CDK inhibitors.[39] Critically, activation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR is a major mechanism of intrinsic and acquired resistance to both radiotherapy and chemotherapy in OSCC.[40] Radioresistant OSCC cell lines derived from patients experiencing local recurrence after radiotherapy demonstrate significantly higher expression of phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT) and phosphorylated mTOR compared to radiosensitive parental cell lines.[41] The dual pharmacological blockade of PI3K and mTOR with the inhibitor BEZ235 combined with ionizing radiation (8 Gy) produces synergistic apoptosis induction in radioresistant lines, but not with single-agent PI3K or mTOR inhibition alone.[42] The mechanism of PI3K-/AKT-/mTOR-mediated radiotherapy resistance involves multiple layers: active AKT phosphorylates and inactivates BAD, a BH3-only pro-apoptotic protein, preventing mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and cytochrome c release; simultaneously, mTOR hyperactivity promotes translational activity of anti-apoptotic proteins (MCL-1, BCL-2, and survivin) while suppressing that of the autophagy inhibitor 4E-BP1.[43,44] Additionally, high-level AKT activation in radioresistant OSCC cells prevents p53-independent apoptosis pathways by suppressing FOXO-mediated pro-death gene transcription and promoting nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) survival signaling.[45,46] This multilayered antideath signaling network explains the clinical designation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR as a “vital therapeutic target” in treatment-resistant OSCC. Azole antifungals work by inhibiting the activity of fungal enzyme lanosterol 14α-demethylase (CYP51).[47] Depletion of membrane cholesterol by azole-mediated CYP51 inhibition physically separates PI3K from its upstream activators and prevents AKT phosphorylation, thereby suppressing PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling.[48,49] Similarly, terbinafine, an allylamine antifungal that inhibits SQLE activity in the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway, achieves PI3K/AKT suppression through identical lipid raft disruption.[10,11] This mechanism of PI3K/AKT suppression via lipid raft disruption represents a novel and mechanistically distinct approach compared to conventional PI3K inhibitors and kinase inhibitors, targeting upstream organization rather than kinase catalytic activity itself and thus may overcome resistance mechanisms that arise from kinase-domain mutations or bypass signaling. Therefore, antifungals constitute a rationally justified therapeutic complement to conventional PI3K/mTOR inhibitors in radioresistant, PI3K-hyperactive OSCC, providing both additive and potentially synergistic suppression of survival signaling when combined with radiotherapy.

VASCULAR ENDOTHELIAL GROWTH FACTOR-MEDIATED ANGIOGENESIS AND OXYGENATION: AN ANTIANGIOGENIC VULNERABILITY TARGETABLE BY MULTIPLE ANTIFUNGALS

Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is frequently overexpressed in OSCC and is the primary regulator of tumor angiogenesis, promoting the formation of new blood vessels that supply the tumor with oxygen and nutrients while facilitating metastatic dissemination.[50] VEGF is expressed by both tumor cells and stromal cells within the tumor microenvironment (cancer-associated fibroblasts, tumor-associated macrophages, and hypoxic tumor cells) and binds to VEGF receptors (VEGFR-1 and VEGFR-2) on endothelial cells and other cell types, activating PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways that promote endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and survival—the hallmarks of angiogenesis.[51,52] Elevated VEGF levels correlate with increased blood vessel formation and are linked to worse patient outcomes, including more frequent metastasis and shorter overall survival in OSCC.[53,54] Research—both in laboratory models and clinical studies—shows that blocking VEGF is an effective strategy: anti-VEGF antibodies (such as bevacizumab)[55,56] and drugs that target VEGF receptors (such as sorafenib and sunitinib)[57] shrink tumors, slow growth, and improve patient responses to treatment across multiple cancer types. A critical factor is that tumors need an adequate blood supply to be sensitive to radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Under low oxygen conditions, tumors activate survival signals through hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF-1α) that protect cancer cells. Several antifungal agents—particularly terbinafine, clotrimazole, and itraconazole—reduce VEGF production and inhibit VEGF signaling in OSCC models, leading to fewer blood vessels within tumors and limiting tumor growth that depends on new blood vessel formation.[8,9,10,11,12,25] By reducing new blood vessel formation, antifungals work synergistically with radiotherapy. Terbinafine-mediated G0/G1 cell cycle arrest and elevation of the CDK inhibitor p27 in OSCC cell lines are accompanied by reduced VEGF transcription and decreased tube formation in endothelial cell co-cultures.[10,11] Clotrimazole, acting as a calmodulin antagonist, disrupts calcium-mediated VEGF signaling in endothelial cells and reduces neovascularization in vivo.[12,25] Itraconazole disrupts fatty structures in cell membranes that blood vessel cells require to receive VEGF signals, which prevents the activation of growth and survival pathways (PI3K/AKT) in these blood vessel cells. Improved blood vessel function means tumors receive more oxygen, which makes radiation therapy more effective at damaging cancer cell DNA and triggering cell death.[8,9] Because of this, the ability of several antifungal medications to inhibit the formation of blood vessels provides a solid scientific foundation for combining antifungals with chemoradiation in the treatment of OSCC. This improves local tumor control by ensuring that chemotherapeutic drugs reach the tumor and that radiation therapy is more effective.

MATRIX METALLOPROTEINASES AND THE INVASIVE PHENOTYPE: SUPPRESSION VIA ANTIFUNGAL-MEDIATED CELL DEATH AND STAT3/NUCLEAR FACTOR KAPPA B INHIBITION

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), particularly MMP-2 and MMP-9, are frequently overexpressed in OSCC. They break down the extracellular matrix (ECM) and promote epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, which gives epithelial tumor cells mesenchymal traits like increased motility, invasiveness, and resistance to apoptosis.[58] MMPs are transcriptionally regulated by proinflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin [IL]-6, and IL-8),[59] growth factors (EGFR, VEGF, and TGF-β), and transcription factors (NF-κB, STAT3, and AP-1)[60] that are hyperactivated in OSCC and the tumor-associated inflammatory microenvironment. High expressions of MMP-2 and MMP-9 in OSCC correlate with increased depth of invasion, cervical lymph node metastasis, distant metastasis, and poor overall survival, establishing MMPs as important biomarkers of aggressive behavior.[61] Reduction in invasive potential through MMP inhibition or transcriptional suppression of MMP expression is therefore recognized as an important therapeutic goal in OSCC. Antifungal agents indirectly reduce MMP expression and activity through multiple complementary mechanisms. Terbinafine and clotrimazole induce cell cycle arrest[10,11,12,25] directly by decreasing MMP transcription through suppression of cell cycle-dependent transcription factors (NF-κB and STAT3).

In OSCC cell lines, terbinafine-mediated G0/G1 arrest correlates with reduced expression of MMP-9 mRNA and decreased gelatinase activity in conditioned media, while clotrimazole-mediated elevation of ROS and apoptosis results in decreased MMP-2 and MMP-9 in Cal-27 and SCC-131 OSCC cells.[10,11] Miconazole-mediated STAT3 suppression (via CYP2C inhibition and reduced epoxyeicosatrienoic acid synthesis) reduces MMP-9 expression in STAT3-dependent lung cancer cell lines.[61] This mechanistic link between antifungal-mediated cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and STAT3/NF-κB inhibition on one hand and reduced MMP expression and invasive potential on the other provides a compelling rationale for antifungal-based combination therapy in OSCC, particularly in tumors with high baseline MMP expression and aggressive invasive phenotype [Figure 1].

CROSSTALK AND COMMON TARGETS

Several of these antifungals converge on a limited set of central survival pathways, most notably PI3K/AKT/mTOR and Hedgehog signaling. These pathways represent shared “nodes” where multiple antifungals intersect, amplifying their therapeutic relevance.

At the membrane level, itraconazole and terbinafine both perturb cholesterol biosynthesis (via CYP51 and SQLE inhibition, respectively); azoles reduce mammalian membrane cholesterol content and disrupt cholesterol-enriched lipid raft microdomains that serve as essential signaling platforms where receptor tyrosine kinases (EGFR, IGF-1R)[10] recruit and activate PI3K via adaptor proteins. This suppresses AKT phosphorylation regardless of the initiating receptor (EGFR, IGF-1R, or VEGFR) and thereby attenuates several downstream processes such as cell survival, metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis, and translation of antiapoptotic proteins via mTOR.[10,11] In parallel, clotrimazole[25] and miconazole,[61] while classically characterized by their ROS-generating and calcium-disrupting properties, also feed into the same PI3K/AKT network by reducing NF-κB and STAT3 activity. Thus, ROS-mediated mitochondrial damage and transcriptional reprogramming converge functionally on a common PI3K/AKT survival hub.

A similar pattern of convergence is evident for Hedgehog signaling. Itraconazole is a recognized Smoothened (SMO) inhibitor at clinically achievable concentrations.[33] However, noncanonical Hedgehog activation can also be driven by PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways through lipid raft disruption, which modulate Gli transcriptional activity independent of SMO. This creates a mechanistic crosstalk in which canonical (SMO-dependent) and noncanonical (PI3K-/AKT-dependent) Hedgehog signaling are jointly attenuated by different members of the same pharmacological class.[9]

These observations indicate that antifungals do exert effects on shared signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR and Hedgehog. This convergence provides a mechanistic framework for their capacity to reverse radioresistance, overcome EGFR–bypass signaling, and impair angiogenesis and invasion in OSCC, thus supporting the rationale of repurposing antifungals as adjuvants in anticancer treatment [Figure 2].

PHARMACOKINETIC FEASIBILITY AND CLINICAL TRANSLATION POTENTIAL

Itraconazole inhibits SMO at therapeutic concentrations of 0.5–2.0 µmol/L.[8] Cmax with standard dosing (200 mg daily).[62] However, killing OSCC cells requires much higher concentrations (35–70 µmol/L), creating an unfavorable balance between what the drug can achieve (0.5–2.0 µmol/L) and what is needed (Cmax/IC50 ratio of 0.03–0.06). Increasing the dose to 600 mg daily risks serious side effects, including heart rhythm problems (QT prolongation), liver damage, and kidney dysfunction.[26] Although itraconazole accumulates 5–10 times higher in fatty tissues than in blood,[63] whether it reaches sufficient levels inside OSCC tumors remains unknown. Itraconazole works best when tailored to patients whose tumors have specific genetic features (Sonic Hedgehog [SHH]-high OSCC). It has been used at a dose of 200–400 mg daily alongside chemoradiation,[64] with regular blood tests to check drug levels and ensure the dose is working as intended.

Terbinafine blocks the activity of an enzyme (SQLE) involved in cholesterol production (IC50: 5–15 μM) and stops cancer cells from progressing through the cell cycle by increasing proteins that act as natural barriers (p21 and p27).[44,45] Standard dosing (250 mg daily) produces blood levels of 3–6 μM, which seems low compared to what is needed (Cmax/IC50 ratio: 0.3–0.6).[65] Terbinafine accumulates 10–70 times higher in fatty tissues throughout the body, and hence tumors are likely receive much higher drug concentrations. This exceptional tissue buildup allows the dose to be safely increased to 300–500 mg daily.[66] Terbinafine’s ability to prevent blood vessel formation and halt cell cycle progression makes it a promising option for systemic treatment when combined with radiation therapy at doses of 250–500 mg per day.[67]

Clotrimazole works through several mechanisms, such as disrupting calcium signaling (calmodulin antagonism) and producing harmful free radicals (ROS), and it is remarkably potent in laboratory tests (reducing tumors by 57.9%, IC50: 2–5 μM).[9] When administered systemically, clotrimazole only reaches blood levels of 0.01–0.1 μM, which is much lower than what cancer cells require (Cmax/IC50 < 0.02). As a result, systemic treatment is not feasible with current drug formulations. Therefore, to use clotrimazole effectively, it is preferred to be administered via intratumoral injection or as a 1%–2% mouth rinse solution.[25]

Miconazole interrupts a key inflammation and survival pathway in cancer cells by blocking the activity of an enzyme (CYP2C) that normally produces signaling molecules (epoxyeicosatrienoic acids, or EETs).[68] This blocks the JAK-STAT3 pathway, which promotes tumor growth. When administered intravenously (600 mg three times daily), miconazole achieves blood levels of 1–3 μM (IC50: 3–8 μM), which is marginally below that required (Cmax/IC50 ratio: 0.3–1.0), but it is sufficient when combined with chemotherapy and radiation, which provide additional cancer-killing effects.[61] Notably, blocking STAT3 also reduces inflammatory signals that tumors use to suppress the immune system, which means miconazole might work well alongside immunotherapy drugs that “release the brakes” on immune cells. Miconazole may be particularly beneficial in combination with newer immunotherapy drugs (anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 checkpoint inhibitors) that help the immune system recognize and kill cancer cells.[49] The critical pharmacokinetic limitation of miconazole is that it requires combination therapy to achieve clinical benefit.

Ketoconazole reduces exosomal biogenesis proteins (Alix, nSMase, and Rab27a) in cancer cells.[69] However, ketoconazole potently inhibits the activity of multiple CYP450 enzymes (CYP3A4, CYP2C9, and CYP2C19), creating substantial drug–drug interaction risks with chemotherapy.[70] From the current studies, it is not recommended for systemic use due to CYP450 interaction risks. Investigational approaches are limited to topical or intra-tumoral delivery in early dysplasia.

LIMITATIONS

This review is based on only five original peer-reviewed articles (four in vitro + in vivo; one in vitro only) examining antifungals in OSCC. This represents a narrow evidence base. The scarcity of published data reflects the emerging nature of this research area but also limits definitive conclusions. Most antifungal cancer research works have been conducted in other malignancies (lung cancer, breast cancer, and ovarian cancer); OSCC-specific data are sparse. To date, no Phase I or Phase II clinical trials have been completed in OSCC patients. All evidence is preclinical. While the mechanistic and preclinical evidence is compelling, clinical translation remains unproven. Pharmacokinetic assumptions are based on data obtained from fungal infections; tumor penetration is assumed but not validated. Azoles, like itraconazole, have pH-dependent absorption, which is optimal at gastric pH 1.2–2.5, which introduces significant challenges in treating oral cancer patients with altered diets, xerostomia, or reduced salivary flow due to the impairment in drug dissolution and bioavailability.

CONCLUSIONS

Oral cancer rates are increasing globally due to multiple risk factors, while current treatment options face challenges related to tumor resistance, limited access to treatment resources, and reduced treatment compliance due to its associated adverse effects. Current targeted drugs have limited efficacy, and hence there is an urgent need for new, affordable, well-tolerated adjuvant treatment options to reduce the oral cancer burden. This review emphasizes the possibility of antifungal drugs to be potentially repurposed as adjuvants in oral cancer therapy and elucidates the key pathways—PI3K/AKT/mTOR, Hedgehog/SMO, STAT3, and angiogenesis-related—targeted by these agents. Itraconazole and miconazole suppress Hedgehog/SMO and PI3K/AKT signaling, while terbinafine suppresses proliferation and angiogenesis, and miconazole disrupts STAT3-driven inflammation. Terbinafine has been shown to exhibit better tissue accumulation and a better efficacy–safety balance, while itraconazole and miconazole could be tailored for SHH-high and STAT3-high tumors, respectively. The limitations of clotrimazole for systemic use can be overcome through future research aimed at exploring new formulations (e.g., liposomal carriers and nanoparticles). Now, there is an exigency to translate these findings into clinical practice through well-designed randomized controlled trials evaluating the optimal dosing for adjuvant chemoradiation strategies. This directive approach promises to revolutionize oral cancer management in resource-limited settings like India by actively bridging the gap from bench to bedside.
