Intraprocedural Transesophageal Echocardiography Ensures Accurate Anchoring During Transcatheter Intervention for Aortic Regurgitation
Authors: Katie Wing-Sum Fan, Kevin Ka-Ho Kam, Ka Lung Chui, Ling Na Wong, Chenxu Zhao, Kent Chak-Yu So, Alex Pui-Wai Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis case report elucidates the indispensable role of structured transesophageal echocardiography imaging in guiding the implantation of the JenaValve, a transcatheter device designed for severe native aortic regurgitation via its unique locator-based anchoring system.Key StepsFirst, use the midesophageal aortic valve short-axis view to confirm capture of the left and noncoronary cusps. Second, employ a modified transgastric aortic valve short-axis 90° view to visualize the often obscured right coronary cusp. These complementary views can verify precise locator engagement on all 3 leaflets before final deployment.Potential PitfallsOverreliance on fluoroscopy can lead to valve malposition or migration due to poor cusp visualization. Excessive manipulation during positioning can dislodge locators into the left ventricular outflow tract.Take-Home MessageA systematic, multiview transesophageal echocardiography approach is critical to confirm successful leaflet capture and ensure stable JenaValve deployment in noncalcified structures, mitigating major risks of device failure.

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation has become a standard treatment for severe aortic stenosis.1 However, native aortic regurgitation (AR) poses a challenge because of insufficient valvular calcification. The JenaValve (JenaValve Technology) offers a novel solution,2 featuring a trileaflet porcine valve mounted on a low-profile, self-expanding nitinol frame connected to a sealing ring.3 The unique locator-based anchoring mechanism of the system ensures leaflet capture for security,4 making precise transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance indispensable.5 Here, we present the critical role of TEE imaging,6 particularly the midesophageal aortic valve short-axis (ME AV SAX) view and modified transgastric aortic valve short-axis (TG AV SAX) 90° view, in JenaValve deployment.Take-Home Message•A systematic, multiview TEE approach is critical to confirm successful leaflet capture and ensure stable JenaValve deployment in noncalcified structures, mitigating major risks of device failure.

Case Presentation

An 81-year-old man with severe AR (NYHA functional class II) and high surgical risk (estimated Society of Thoracic Surgeons risk score 10.2% for predicted mortality and morbidity) underwent JenaValve implantation. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed severe regurgitation (Figure 1), confirmed by an effective regurgitant orifice of 0.39 cm2 and a vena contracta of 0.62 cm, with significant left ventricular chamber dilatation (end-diastolic diameter = 6.0 cm) and borderline low ejection fraction of 53%. Procedural Details

Under general anesthesia with TEE and fluoroscopy guidance, a 25-mm JenaValve was advanced via femoral access. The right coronary cusp (RCC) could not be completely visualized with fluoroscopy with contrast injection (Figures 2B and 3B), necessitating TEE imaging. The ME AV SAX view under 3-dimensional multiplanar reconstruction (Figure 2A, Video 1) verified the leaflet capture of the left coronary cusp and noncoronary cusp (NCC). However, the RCC was obscured by valve shadowing. Thus, the modified TG AV SAX 90° view (Figure 3A, Video 2) was adopted to provide an alternative perspective, clarifying RCC alignment. Proper alignment and leaflet grasping were confirmed using these complementary views. Discussion

The unique design of the JenaValve requires a dynamic echocardiographic approach. In contrast to conventional valves that rely on radial force exerted on annular calcification, the system demands precise leaflet acquisition to prevent device embolization or migration7—major adverse outcomes of transcatheter aortic valve implantation in pure AR. Computed tomography–derived dedicated fluoroscopy guidance is often sufficient and is noninvasive. Yet, overlapping structures can introduce complexity, such as the poor visualization of RCC and the difficulty in confirming whether the position of the locators is at the cusp level or extends into the left ventricular outflow tract. The 2 views (ME AV SAX view and modified TG AV SAX 90° view) thus complement each other, as each provides a clear outline of 2 of the 3 coronary cusps. Alternating between them ensures adequate engagement of all locators before final deployment. Transthoracic echocardiography would be the last resort if TEE images are suboptimal.

This case highlights several key insights:1.Malalignment risk: Overreliance on fluoroscopy guidance increases the risk of valve migration or malposition.82.Excessive manipulation: Overadjustment of the valve during positioning may displace or even dislodge the locators into the left ventricular outflow tract, necessitating cautious handling and potentially bailout strategies.83.Anatomical limitations: The JenaValve may be challenging for specific body structures, for example, bicuspid aortic valves, where 3-leaflet capture may not be feasible.

Conclusions

This case underscores the critical role of structured TEE guidance in JenaValve implantation in addition to the computed tomography–derived dedicated fluoroscopic views. A systematic imaging approach—beginning with the ME AV SAX view in biplane or 3-dimensional multiplanar reconstruction to confirm left coronary cusp and NCC engagement, followed by the modified TG AV SAX 90° view to verify RCC and NCC alignment—should be a standard practice to minimize the risk of malposition, enhance valve stability, and ensure optimal outcomes. Familiarity with these echocardiographic techniques and multiplanar assessment is essential for operators performing JenaValve implantation.
