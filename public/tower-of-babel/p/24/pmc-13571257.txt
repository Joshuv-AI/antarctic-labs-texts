Intervening in p53-Induced Death Domain Protein 1 to Decrease Regulatory T Cell Recruitment Boosts the Effectiveness of Radiofrequency Ablation Combined With Anti–Programmed Cell Death Protein-1 Therapy in Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Xu Lu, Zhiyong Xiong, Yongsheng Tang, Yingqiu Song, Zenan Yuan, Ling Li, Manqiu Kang, Xin He, Zihan Qiao, Shuguang Zhu, Qi Zhang, Tiantian Wang, Hua Li, Changchang Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsThe efficacy of radiofrequency ablation is constrained by incomplete tumor ablation, particularly in cases of excessive tumor volume, leading to elevated recurrence rates. Elucidating the genetic and microenvironmental alterations following incomplete ablation is essential for improving therapeutic outcomes. We aimed to identify the tumor–intrinsic molecular link between residual tumor cells and the immunosuppressive microenvironment that drives recurrence after radiofrequency ablation.MethodsWe retrospectively compared recurrence in hepatocellular carcinoma patients treated with radiofrequency ablation or liver resection and established complete and incomplete radiofrequency ablation mouse models. Transcriptomic dataset analysis (differentially expressed genes and weighted gene coexpression network analysis) combined with single-cell RNA sequencing was used to identify candidate genes linking incomplete ablation to regulatory T cell recruitment. Mechanistic roles were validated by Western blotting, reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction, flow cytometry, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, immunohistochemistry/immunofluorescence, and T-cell coculture assays, and the effect of p53-induced death domain protein 1 knockdown combined with anti–programmed cell death protein-1 therapy was assessed in vivo.ResultsAnalysis of tumor recurrence post radiofrequency ablation revealed a significantly higher local recurrence rate in primary tumors exceeding 2 cm in size, indicating that incomplete ablation is a critical driver of local tumor regrowth. In preclinical animal models, incomplete radiofrequency ablation was associated with increased infiltration of regulatory T cells. Gene expression profiling identified marked upregulation of p53-induced death domain protein 1, which strongly correlated with regulatory T cell infiltration and unfavorable prognosis. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that p53-induced death domain protein 1 enhanced p53 expression via the caspase-2/murine double minute 2 axis, promoted transforming growth factor-β1 secretion, and drove T-cell differentiation into regulatory T cells. Silencing p53-induced death domain protein 1 in hepatocellular carcinoma cells reduced regulatory T cell infiltration and heightened sensitivity to immunotherapy. Strikingly, combining radiofrequency ablation with immunotherapy following p53-induced death domain protein 1 knockdown significantly enhanced treatment efficacy.ConclusionsThese findings provide a robust theoretical and practical basis for integrating radiofrequency ablation with anti–programmed cell death protein-1–targeted therapy to mitigate recurrence and optimize clinical outcomes.Keywords: Hepatocellular Carcinoma, PIDD1, Radiofrequency Ablation, Treg Cells, Tumor Recurrence

Summary

Incomplete radiofrequency ablation upregulates p53-induced death domain protein 1 in residual hepatocellular carcinoma cells, which drives regulatory T-cell recruitment through the caspase-2/MDM2/p53–TGF-β1 axis. Silencing p53-induced death domain protein 1 curbs regulatory T cell infiltration and potentiates anti–programmed cell death protein-1 immunotherapy, offering a strategy to reduce postablation recurrence.

What You Need to Know

Background

Incomplete radiofrequency ablation drives hepatocellular carcinoma recurrence via residual tumor cells remodeling the microenvironment after sublethal heat injury. Elucidating these molecular mechanisms is crucial to improve radiofrequency ablation efficacy.

Impact

In preclinical models, incomplete radiofrequency ablation reduces immunogenic cell death and increases immunosuppressive regulatory T cell infiltration. p53-induced death domain protein 1 upregulation drives regulatory T cell recruitment and T-cell exhaustion via the phosphorylated 66Shc–reactive oxygen species–transforming growth factor-β1 axis, providing a novel target for post-ablation anti–programmed cell death protein-1 therapy.

Future Directions

Future studies should develop safe, efficient p53-induced death domain protein 1–specific inhibitors or gene therapy, optimize ablation-immunotherapy combinations, and identify the optimal timing to minimize recurrence and improve survival.

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the predominant primary liver malignancy and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally.1, 2, 3 Heightened public health awareness has markedly increased early-stage HCC diagnoses, facilitating timely therapeutic interventions.2,4 Current options for early-stage HCC management include liver transplantation, surgical resection, and radiofrequency ablation (RFA).5, 6, 7 Although liver transplantation is considered the gold standard, its application is limited by donor organ shortages.8 Surgical resection remains a key alternative but is frequently precluded by underlying cirrhosis or compromised liver function.9 By contrast, RFA offers distinct advantages, including minimal invasiveness, favorable safety profile, rapid recovery, and wide applicability. In unresectable cases, RFA achieves a 90% complete response rate, with 6-year survival rates ranging from 66% to 86%.10, 11, 12 Nevertheless, its clinical efficacy is undermined by a high incidence of intrahepatic recurrence, with over 50% of patients experiencing relapse within 5 years,13,14 primarily due to incomplete tumor ablation and subsequent proliferation of residual cells. Sublethal damage to tumor cells plays a pivotal role, not only driving tumor regrowth but also remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment to accelerate recurrence.15 Despite these insights, the molecular mechanisms linking residual tumor cells to immune microenvironment alterations remain poorly understood.

Sublethal injury from incomplete RFA (iRFA) triggers a tumor stress response, altering gene and protein expression profiles. These changes likely mediate remodeling of the immune microenvironment, promoting tumor recurrence.16, 17, 18, 19 To test this hypothesis, we first confirmed that incomplete ablation with residual tumor tissue induces significant immunosuppression.20 Compared with complete ablation, incomplete ablation markedly diminished immunogenic cell death (ICD). Subsequent analysis revealed a strong association between reduced ICD and elevated levels of immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs), suggesting that incomplete ablation fosters Treg accumulation, potentially through gene regulatory shifts in residual tumor cells. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms, we analyzed publicly available transcriptomic datasets from 10 patients with HCC before and after RFA. By intersecting differentially expressed genes with Treg-related genes, p53-induced death domain protein 1 (PIDD1) emerged as a pivotal candidate linking incomplete ablation to Treg recruitment. PIDD1 is a well-established proapoptotic protein directly regulated by p53.21 Under genotoxic stress, PIDD1 activates proinflammatory signaling via the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway and triggers cell death through caspase-2 activation.22, 23, 24

In this study, we demonstrated that PIDD1 drives HCC recurrence by regulating the caspase-2/p53 pathway, increasing transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1) secretion, and promoting Treg accumulation. This immunosuppressive microenvironment accelerates tumor regrowth and relapse. Notably, Treg depletion not only prevented recurrence but also enhanced the efficacy of combined RFA and immunotherapy. These findings highlight PIDD1 as a promising therapeutic target for mitigating RFA-associated recurrence and offer a compelling rationale for integrating ablation with immunotherapy in clinical practice.

Results

Incomplete Ablation-Induced Tumor Microenvironment Changes Lead to Increased Risk of Tumor Recurrence

Routine clinical observations revealed that patients with HCC treated with RFA exhibited a significantly elevated risk of tumor recurrence. To assess this, we conducted a retrospective analysis comparing 3-year progression-free survival (PFS) rates in patients with HCC undergoing RFA or liver resection (Table 1). Overall recurrence rates showed no significant difference between the ablation and resection groups. However, subgroup analysis indicated distinct patterns: among 229 patients in the liver resection group, 60 experienced recurrence (26% rate); in the RFA1 group (91 patients, tumors <2 cm), 26 had recurrence (28%); and in the RFA2 group (77 patients, tumors >2 cm), the recurrence rate was 35% (Figure 1A and B). PFS was comparable between the resection and RFA1 groups but significantly shorter in the RFA2 group compared with the resection group (P < .05). Recurrence location analysis classified tumors within 2 cm of the original site as “local recurrence” and those beyond 2 cm as “distant recurrence.” Notably, no local recurrence occurred in the resected liver segment, whereas RFA was associated with a significantly higher local recurrence rate (Figure 1C).

RFA delivers high-frequency currents via an ablation needle, inducing ionic oscillations, friction, and heat (60 °C–72 °C) to cause coagulative necrosis. Using this principle, we developed mouse models of complete and incomplete ablation with an infrared device (Figure 1E), confirmed by gross observation and H&E staining (Figure 1F and G; Figure 2B and C). Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling staining revealed extensive apoptosis and necrosis in the complete ablation group, whereas the incomplete ablation group showed reduced apoptosis with viable tumor cells persisting (Figure 1H). Necrosis releases damage-associated molecular patterns that trigger immunogenic cell death. Calreticulin analysis indicated diminished immunogenic cell death in the incomplete ablation group compared with the complete ablation group (Figure 1I and J), reflecting impaired immune clearance of tumor cells. Flow cytometry further revealed a significant increase in Tregs in the incomplete ablation group (Figure 1K–O; Figure 2C), suggesting that reduced immunogenic cell death may be linked to Treg activation. These findings underscore Treg elevation as a key focus for future studies on incomplete ablation.

Gene Expression Changes and Regulatory T Cell Infiltration in Patients With Hepatocellular Carcinoma Before and After Radiofrequency Ablation

We hypothesized that changes in residual tumor cells after incomplete ablation could trigger an increase in Tregs. To identify tumor-intrinsic gene changes associated with immune microenvironment alterations following RFA, samples before and after ablation are difficult to obtain in clinical practice; through extensive dataset retrieval, we discovered that the GSE212604 dataset includes clinical tissue samples from 10 patients before and after RFA treatment. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the criteria of log fold change >0.5 and P < .05. This analysis revealed 382 DEGs, including 182 upregulated and 200 downregulated genes, as visualized in a volcano plot (Figure 3A) and heatmap (Figure 4A). To further identify key immune regulatory genes in the RFA context, we extended the analysis to predict immune cell distributions using the single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis method. We examined the distribution of immune cells in HCC tissues before and after RFA (Figure 4B). Using weighted gene coexpression network analysis, we grouped expressed genes into modules and assessed their correlation with immune cells. Notably, the ME pink module, comprising 451 genes, exhibited a strong correlation with Treg cells (correlation coefficient = 0.84) (Figure 4C; Figure 3B). We cross-referenced Treg-associated genes with the DEGs identified before and after RFA and pinpointed 7 core genes: ATG16L2, KIAA0895L, LINC01089, PABPN1, PIDD1, SNORA60, and STAG3L5P (Figure 4D and E). Further analysis of these core genes revealed that they were highly enriched in HCC tumors compared with adjacent normal tissues (Figure 3C and D). Mutation analysis using maftools and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) nucleotide variation data showed that these genes were mutated in 1.08% of HCC cases, with PIDD1 mutations accounting for 1% (Figure 4F). To explore protein–protein interactions among these core genes, we used the Gene MANIA online database. Among the core genes, ATG16L2, KIAA0895L, PABPN1, and PIDD1 were identified to express proteins with significant interactions. Notably, PIDD1 exhibited the highest number of interactions with other proteins (Figure 4G; Figure 3E). Based on these findings, we selected PIDD1 for further investigation to elucidate its role in modulating the immune microenvironment after RFA.

p53-Induced Death Domain Protein 1 Influences Regulatory T Cell Infiltration in the Tumor Microenvironment by Modulating the Caspase-2–p53 Pathway

To explore the role of PIDD1 in HCC progression, we initially performed a correlation analysis using the GEPIA2 database, which uncovered a significant positive correlation between PIDD1 and its downstream targets, murine double minute 2 (MDM2) and p53 (Figure 5A). To validate these findings, we established PIDD1-knockdown (KD) and overexpression (OE) models in both human and mouse HCC cell lines. Functional assays, including CCK-8 and flow cytometry, indicated that PIDD1 modulation alone did not significantly alter cell proliferation or basal apoptosis (Figure 6A–D). To mimic the clinical scenario of incomplete ablation, cells were subjected to sublethal thermal stress (42 °C for 2 hours). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis revealed a post-treatment surge in the expression of PIDD1, p53, and MDM2 (Figure 5B). Western blot analysis (preablation vs 2, 12, and 24 hours post-ablation) demonstrated a time-dependent accumulation of PIDD1, MDM2, p53, caspase-2, and phosphorylated p66Shc (p-p66Shc), a pattern further validated by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) in both human and mouse clinical specimens (Figure 5C and D; Figure 6E–H). Mechanistically, p53-mediated phosphorylation of p66Shc at Ser36 facilitated its mitochondrial translocation, thereby disrupting the electron transport chain (complexes I and III) and triggering an oxidative burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Figure 5E). To clarify the downstream branches mediating PIDD1-driven ROS production and p53 activation, we used the p53 inhibitor pifithrin-α (PFT-α), the ROS scavenger N-acetylcysteine (NAC), and the specific protein kinase C β inhibitor Hispidin to block upstream phosphorylation of SHC. Western blot results showed that both PFT-α and Hispidin significantly reduced the protein levels of p-SHC (Ser36) in both cell lines (Figure 5F), suggesting that SHC Ser36 phosphorylation is regulated by p53. Corresponding mitochondrial fractionation experiments demonstrated that p-SHC (Ser36) co-localized with the mitochondrial marker mitochondrially encoded cytochrome C oxidase II in the mitochondrial fraction, and this mitochondrial p-SHC signal was attenuated in both HepG2 and Hepa1-6 cells upon PIDD1 knockdown or treatment with PFT-α and Hispidin (Figure 5G). These findings further confirmed that PIDD1 promotes SHC phosphorylation and mitochondrial translocation in a p53-dependent manner.

Building on the establishment of PIDD1-driven mitochondrial signaling leading to ROS accumulation, we further investigated whether PFT-α and Hispidin could attenuate heat stress–induced ROS production. Dihydroethidium staining showed that these treatments significantly reduced intracellular ROS levels in heat-stressed HepG2 and Hepa1-6 cells (Figure 7A), further supporting the PIDD1-regulated p53–mitochondrial–ROS signaling axis. To further validate the role of ROS in this process, NAC treatment markedly abolished heat stress–induced dihydroethidium fluorescence in both HepG2 and Hepa1-6 cells (Figure 5H), confirming that antioxidant intervention effectively inhibits heat stress–induced ROS accumulation. We then examined whether the PIDD1/TGF-β1 axis drives immunosuppressive Treg infiltration. Naive T cells were cocultured with conditioned medium (CM) from heat-ablated HCC cells. Compared with controls, Treg differentiation was significantly enhanced, and this effect was largely abolished by PIDD1 silencing (Figure 6I and J; Figure 7B and C). To exclude potential distal confounding factors, we established a Transwell-based direct ablation model (Figure 7D). The results confirmed that ablation-induced Treg differentiation was significantly attenuated by PIDD1 KD or by using LSKL to inhibit TGF-β1 release (Figure 7E–G). Additionally, to further elucidate the upstream signals mediating heat-stressed conditioned medium (HCM)-induced immunosuppression, we treated HepG2 cells with NAC, PFT-α, or Hispidin prior to collecting HCM and then assessed its Treg-inducing capacity. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that HepG2-derived HCM induced marked Treg expansion, whereas this effect was significantly weakened when HCM was derived from cells pretreated with NAC, PFT-α, or Hispidin (Figure 8A and D). These results indicate that ROS accumulation and p53 activation are essential upstream events for TGF-β1 secretion in HCM-mediated Treg induction.

Finally, we evaluated the impact of this signaling cascade on TGF-β1 secretion. Heat stress significantly increased TGF-β1 concentration in HepG2 CM; this effect was markedly attenuated by PIDD1 KD (Figure 5I). Moreover, treatment with NAC, PFT-α, or Hispidin all significantly reduced heat stress–induced TGF-β1 secretion (Figure 5J). Subsequently, in the Hepa1-6 system, we investigated the effects of HCM-induced immunosuppression on CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocyte function and exhaustion. The results showed that HCM significantly suppressed interferon γ (IFN-γ) production by CD8+ T cells. This suppression could be significantly rescued by LSKL, NAC cotreatment, or PIDD1 knockdown (Figure 8B and E). Consistent with impaired effector function, HCM also significantly promoted CD8+ T cell exhaustion, as evidenced by a marked increase in the frequency of PD-1+T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3)+ double-positive cells. This exhaustion phenotype was slightly attenuated by LSKL or NAC treatment, whereas PIDD1 KD showed the most pronounced rescue effect (Figure 8C and F). These findings suggest that PIDD1-driven TGF-β1 and ROS are the primary mediators of heat stress–induced suppression of CD8+ T cell function. Taken together, these data establish that heat stress activates the PIDD1–p53–ROS–TGF-β1 immunosuppressive signaling axis in HCC cells, and this cascade drives Treg expansion and CD8+ T cell functional exhaustion.

Regulatory T Cell Infiltration Correlates With Immunotherapy Efficacy, but No Significant Change in Regulatory T Cell Infiltration After Combined Immunotherapy and Radiofrequency Ablation

Immune checkpoint inhibitors, particularly programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitors, are now standard treatments for HCC in clinical practice. However, immune checkpoint inhibitor monotherapy often yields limited efficacy, prompting exploration of combination therapies to enhance outcomes. The integration of RFA with immunotherapy has emerged as a promising strategy, although the mechanisms underlying RFA and anti–PD-1 combination therapy, as well as potential adverse effects, remain poorly defined. Given our prior observation of increased Treg cell infiltration post-RFA, we investigated its impact on anti–PD-1 therapy efficacy. We first analyzed the HCC immune microenvironment pre- and post anti–PD-1 therapy to identify molecular events influenced by combined RFA and anti–PD-1 treatment. Single-cell RNA sequencing in mouse models revealed significant shifts in immune cell populations following immunotherapy (Figure 9A; Figure 10A and B), with notable changes in T-cell gene expression and subpopulation dynamics (Figure 10C). Specifically, anti–PD-1 treatment reduced Treg cell infiltration while altering proportions of exhausted and cytotoxic T cells (Figure 9B; Figure 10D). This was validated clinically in patients post-liver resection and in mouse models, both showing decreased Treg infiltration after anti–PD-1 therapy (Figure 9C and D). However, the link between Treg infiltration and treatment response remained unclear. Correlation analysis revealed that higher Treg cell counts post anti–PD-1 therapy were associated with poorer responses, as evidenced by tumor size changes on imaging (Figure 9E and F). Given RFA’s induction of Treg elevation, we evaluated combined RFA and anti–PD-1 therapy in a mouse model (Figure 9G), confirmed by tumor imaging and H&E staining (Figure 9H and I). Combination therapy significantly extended overall survival (OS) (Figure 9K). Flow cytometry showed reduced Treg infiltration in the tumor microenvironment post PD-1 therapy, although RFA-induced Treg increases were not fully mitigated (Figure 9L; Figure 10E). Histologic analysis indicated enhanced tumor cell apoptosis (Figure 9J; Figure 11A) and increased lymphocyte infiltration in the combination group (Figure 9M). Although CD8+ T-cell levels remained unchanged (Figure 11B), cytotoxic T cells increased, and exhausted T cells decreased (Figure 9N and O). IF confirmed a positive correlation among PIDD1, p53, and Foxp3 expression in the iRFA group, unaffected by anti–PD-1 combination (Figure 9P). These findings suggest that RFA-induced Treg elevation may counteract PD-1 therapy efficacy, with combination benefits largely driven by anti–PD-1 enhanced T-cell cytotoxicity. However, the persistent negative regulation by Treg cells warrants further attention.

Knockdown of p53-Induced Death Domain Protein 1 Enhances the Efficacy of Radiofrequency Ablation Combined With Anti–Programmed Cell Death-1 Therapy by Reducing Regulatory T Cell Infiltration in the Tumor Microenvironment

Our prior analysis indicated that the limited efficacy of combined RFA and anti–PD-1 therapy may stem from Treg cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment. Further investigation revealed a strong association between PIDD1 expression and Foxp3+ Treg infiltration. To explore this, we utilized PIDD1 KD cell lines in a mouse orthotopic HCC transplantation model (Figure 12A–C; Figure 11C). Anti–PD-1 therapy significantly reduced tumor size and volume compared with controls, whereas PIDD1 KD alone showed no significant impact on tumor size (Figure 12D and E). Flow cytometry demonstrated that anti–PD-1 treatment lowered Treg cell counts in the tumor microenvironment, with a further reduction observed following PIDD1 KD (Figure 12F). IHC confirmed enhanced lymphocyte infiltration postimmunotherapy (Figure 12G). Although CD8+ T-cell levels remained unchanged (Figure 11D), cytotoxic T cells increased, and exhausted T cells decreased (Figure 12H and I), potentially driving therapeutic benefits. Tumor staining in mice revealed that PIDD1 KD significantly reduced p53 and Foxp3 expression, with greater reductions observed after combined anti–PD-1 therapy (Figure 12J), suggesting that targeting PIDD1 could mitigate Treg infiltration. We then established a partial ablation mouse model using PIDD1 KD cell lines (Figure 12K–M). Combined RFA and anti–PD-1 therapy increased tumor cell apoptosis (Figure 12N; Figure 11E) and significantly prolonged OS (Figure 12O). Additionally, we found that administration of LSKL, a conventional competitive inhibitor of TGF-β1, could prolong survival to a limited extent; however, this improvement did not reach statistical significance and was inferior to the therapeutic efficacy observed in the PIDD1 KD group (Figure 11F). Flow cytometry and IF showed that combination therapy reduced Treg infiltration, with an enhanced effect in the PIDD1 KD group (Figure 12P). Lymphocyte infiltration increased in the tumor microenvironment (Figure 12Q), and although CD8+ T-cell counts were stable (Figure 11G), cytotoxic T cells rose, and exhausted T cells declined (Figure 12R and S), likely contributing to efficacy. Triple staining for PIDD1, p53, and Foxp3 confirmed that PIDD1 KD, followed by combined anti–PD-1 therapy, effectively diminished Foxp3+ Treg infiltration induced by incomplete ablation (Figure 12T). These findings highlight PIDD1 as a potential therapeutic target to enhance outcomes in patients receiving combined RFA and immunotherapy.

p53-Induced Death Domain Protein 1 Affects Prognosis in Hepatocellular Carcinoma and Promotes Regulatory T Cell Accumulation in the Tumor Microenvironment Post Radiofrequency Ablation

To evaluate whether PIDD1 is a critical regulator in the progression of HCC, we examined the relationship between PIDD1 expression and patient prognosis using the TCGA database and a cohort of patients from our institution. Analysis of the TCGA database revealed that elevated PIDD1 expression in tumor tissues was significantly associated with worse OS and disease-specific survival (DSS) (Figure 13A and B; Figure 14F). Additionally, PIDD1 expression showed a strong positive correlation with alpha-fetoprotein levels (Figure 14A). In our cohort, IHC analysis classified patients into high or low PIDD1 expression groups based on tissue staining intensity (Figure 14B). Our clinical correlation analysis demonstrated that high PIDD1 expression was significantly linked to reduced OS and DSS (Figure 13C and D). Furthermore, PIDD1 expression levels progressively increased with advancing TNM stages (I–IV), aligning with the TCGA findings (Figure 13E and G). These results indicate that PIDD1 expression is closely associated with poor prognosis and suggests its role as a key regulatory gene in HCC progression. Next, we analyzed HCC tissue samples from patients undergoing hepatectomy for recurrent tumors either post-RFA or without prior RFA treatment. IHC staining showed that PIDD1 expression was significantly higher in tumor tissues from the RFA group compared with the resection-only group. Additionally, the tumor microenvironment in the RFA group exhibited significantly higher infiltration of Foxp3-positive Treg cells (Figure 13H–J). These findings were consistent in animal models (Figure 14C and D). To further quantify the proportion of Treg cells within the immune cell population, we performed flow cytometry analysis on surgically resected liver specimens from the RFA and non-RFA groups. In the control group, Treg cells constituted 0.70% of the tumor immune population, whereas in the RFA group, this proportion increased significantly to 2.44% (Figure 13K and L). Before RFA, no significant correlation was observed between PIDD1 expression and Foxp3. However, after RFA, a strong positive correlation between PIDD1 and Foxp3 expression was identified (Figure 13M), indicating that the interaction between PIDD1 and Treg cells may be a critical event during RFA therapy. Finally, to evaluate the role of TGF-β1, we measured its serum concentration using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay in paired patients undergoing hepatic resection and RFA. Patients undergoing hepatic resection demonstrated a significant reduction in postoperative TGF-β1 levels, whereas those receiving RFA showed a significant increase in TGF-β1 levels (Figure 13N). Similar trends were observed in animal experiments, where TGF-β1 concentrations increased significantly post-iRFA but were mitigated upon PIDD1 KD (Figure 14E).

Discussion

RFA is a well-established treatment for liver cancer, with National Cancer Institute database analyses indicating optimal efficacy for tumors <3 cm in diameter.25 The American Hepato-Pancreato-Biliary Association and Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer guidelines endorse 3 cm as the upper limit for RFA.13,26,27 Although prolonged ablation at a single site may reduce residual tumor cell survival and local recurrence, tumors exceeding this size or located near major blood vessels and bile ducts remain susceptible to incomplete ablation, elevating recurrence risk. This contributes to a significant PFS disparity between RFA and surgery.28, 29, 30 Reported critical tumor sizes range from 2 to 5 cm, though the maximum suitable size for RFA remains debated.31 Our center’s study, limited by few patients with tumors >3 cm receiving RFA, defined 2 cm as the critical threshold. Tumors exceeding this exhibited significantly shorter PFS with RFA compared with resection, aligning with reports that larger tumors increase incomplete ablation and recurrence risk. As a minimally invasive procedure, RFA offers reduced trauma, simplicity, and rapid recovery,32 making the management of post-RFA recurrence a key research priority. Identifying recurrence drivers or predictors holds substantial clinical value.

We developed an innovative animal model of incomplete thermal ablation, validated in vitro (Figure 1), observing reduced ICD after incomplete vs complete ablation. We hypothesize this reflects an immune killing–suppression imbalance, supported by a marked Treg cell increase in the tumor microenvironment, suggesting incomplete ablation promotes Treg elevation and impairs immune responses. This provides critical insights into postablation tumor genetic changes, guiding further research.

Residual tumor cells postablation are a known recurrence source, with incomplete ablation inducing a sublethal heat shock response that drives genetic/epigenetic changes, accelerating proliferation or invasion.33 Although in vitro studies link moderate heat stress to growth factor activation, glucose metabolism shifts,34 epithelial-mesenchymal transition, immunosuppression,35 or diminished T-cell responses,36,37 they lack in vivo genetic evidence. The GSE212604 study, sequencing ablated HCC tissues, identified significant short-term gene changes (within 10 minutes) post–heat stress, impacting long-term recurrence. Our study integrated GSE212604 data with partial/complete ablation models, focusing on Tregs and identifying PIDD1 as a Treg-associated gene influencing recurrence. We validated PIDD1-regulated pathways’ effects on Tregs in vitro and in vivo. PIDD1, a p53-induced death domain protein on chromosome 11p15.5, expresses 3 of 5 transcriptional variants.21 p53 directly regulates PIDD1 via promoter binding sites, with gamma radiation further upregulating expression.38 Under genotoxic stress, PIDD1 activates NF-κB–mediated proinflammatory signaling and caspase-2–driven apoptosis.22 Recent findings position PIDD1 as a ploidy regulator and centrosome sensor,39 functioning within the PIDDosome complex (5:5 PIDD1:RAIDD stoichiometry).40 This complex activates caspase-2, influencing downstream pathways under stress.41,42

RFA induces coagulative necrosis via thermal injury generated by high-frequency currents.30 Our in vitro studies confirmed the activation of the PIDDosome-mediated caspase-2 signaling, with PIDD1 and caspase-2 expression levels escalating in a temperature-dependent manner. Upon activation through dimerization,43 caspase-2 cleaves MDM2 to enhance p53 stability, thereby establishing a robust PIDD1–caspase-2–p53 positive feedback loop.44 Critically, we identified that elevated p53 directly or indirectly triggers the phosphorylation of p66Shc (the 66 kDa isoform of the ShcA adaptor protein) at the Ser36 residue. This phosphorylation facilitates the translocation of p66Shc from the cytosol to the mitochondria, where it interferes with the electron transport chain and inhibits the activities of complexes I and III, leading to excessive ROS generation.45,46 The resulting ROS accumulation amplifies oxidative stress and creates a pro-oxidant feedback loop. Furthermore, ROS specifically oxidizes sensitive amino acid residues on the latency-associated peptide (LAP), such as the Met253 site of the TGF-β1 LAP.47,48 This oxidative modification induces a conformational shift in LAP, causing it to lose its binding affinity for TGF-β1 and subsequently promoting the release of mature, bioactive TGF-β1.49,50 Importantly, our findings indicate that the PIDD1-driven immunosuppression is not solely dependent on TGF-β1 signaling. Excessive ROS generated by the PIDD1–p66Shc axis plays a dual role in the iRFA microenvironment. Aside from acting as an intermediate to activate latent TGF-β1, ROS also functions as a direct, TGF-β–independent metabolic toxin to CD8+ T cells. As demonstrated by our flow cytometry data, the reduction of ROS release following PIDD1 KD directly alleviated oxidative stress on T cells, thereby enhancing IFN-γ secretion and reversing the exhausted phenotype (PD-1+TIM-3+). The partial functional restoration observed in the NAC-treated group further substantiates the existence of this TGF-β1–independent, direct ROS-mediated suppressive pathway. Thus, targeting PIDD1 provides a dual effect, simultaneously neutralizing both oxidative (ROS) and cytokine-mediated (TGF-β1) immunosuppressive networks. These findings offer a novel expansion of the known thermal-responsive pathways and provide innovative insights into the mechanisms of postablation immunosuppression.

Foxp3+ Tregs, prevalent in tumors, correlate with poor prognosis.51,52 Treg depletion boosts CD8+ T-cell infiltration and anti-tumor immunity,53 but complete removal risks immune homeostasis disruption.54,55 Selective Treg targeting or understanding local generation mechanisms is critical. Normally, Tregs maintain homeostasis, but inflammatory signals like IFN-γ destabilize their suppression,56 whereas tumors stabilize Tregs for immunosuppression.57 In HCC, RFA-induced inflammation’s impact on Tregs is unclear. We show residual cells post-RFA regulate TGF-β1 via the PIDD1–p53–caspase-2 axis, promoting Treg-mediated suppression, aligning with hypoxia-driven Treg increases post-HCC treatment.58 Anti–PD-1 therapy enhances dendritic cell and T-cell infiltration,59 but RFA-induced Tregs limit its efficacy. Intriguingly, although PD-1 blockade has been reported to potentially enhance Treg proliferation and suppressive activity in certain contexts—leading to hyperprogressive disease in nonresponders,60 our study observed a significant reduction in Foxp3+ Treg infiltration following anti–PD-1 treatment. We propose that this phenomenon is driven by the unique cytokine milieu of the post-RFA microenvironment, specifically the PIDD1-mediated TGF-β1 axis. Our data demonstrate that Treg recruitment in residual tumors is strictly dependent on bioactive TGF-β1 released by thermally stressed cells via the PIDD1–caspase-2–ROS pathway. Consequently, the reduction in Tregs observed in the combination therapy group is likely a secondary effect of effective tumor control: by reinvigorating CD8+ cytotoxic T cells to eliminate residual tumor cells, anti–PD-1 therapy effectively abrogates the source of TGF-β1. Furthermore, the robust antitumor immune response typically generates high levels of IFN-γ, which has been shown to induce Treg fragility and destabilize Foxp3 expression in the tumor microenvironment.61 Therefore, in the context of iRFA, the clearance of TGF-β1-producing residual tumor cells overrides the potential direct trophic effects of PD-1 blockade on Tregs. The superior efficacy of PIDD1 KD in potentiating anti–PD-1 therapy post-RFA, compared with de novo models, highlights the context-dependent dominance of immunosuppressive pathways. In the iRFA microenvironment, acute thermal stress hyperactivates the PIDD1–caspase-2–ROS axis, establishing it as the nonredundant, master regulator of Treg recruitment via TGF-β1. Consequently, blocking PIDD1 effectively dismantles the primary immunosuppressive barrier. In contrast, de novo tumors likely rely on redundant, PIDD1-independent mechanisms for Treg recruitment. In this baseline setting, PIDD1 inhibition alone is insufficient to overcome compensatory signaling. Thus, PIDD1 functions as a stress-responsive immune modulator, with maximal therapeutic utility in postablation scenarios where the PIDD1-stress axis predominates. PIDD1 KD reduced Treg infiltration, suggesting combination therapies targeting PIDD1 could prevent recurrence, especially for tumors >2 cm. Current National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines lack post-RFA recommendations, and single-agent anti–PD-1 therapy’s limited efficacy supports combination trials. In animal models, balancing Treg reduction with immune safety enhanced RFA/PD-1 efficacy, providing a preclinical basis for HCC immunotherapy.

PIDD1’s identification offers new recurrence research avenues. We propose integrating PIDD1 expression into postoperative prediction models. Elevated preablation PIDD1 may signal higher recurrence risk, warranting aggressive intervention or targeted therapies. High PIDD1, linked to shorter PFS, suggests shorter follow-up intervals for early recurrence detection. PIDD1 knockdown reversed RFA-induced Treg infiltration, enhancing RFA/PD-1 efficacy (Figure 7), although validation in clinical samples and prospective trials is needed. Developing PIDD1 inhibitors requires safety and efficacy assessments prior to clinical use. In this study, we employed a HCC model established by orthotopic injection of Hepa1-6 cells into C57BL/6 mice. It should be noted that Hepa1-6 cells, originally derived from the C57L mouse strain, exhibit certain genetic background differences from the host C57BL/6 mice. Consequently, this model may to some extent induce a systemic host-vs-graft immune response. Nevertheless, this model has been extensively validated and widely adopted in the field of HCC immunotherapy research, demonstrating high representativeness and reproducibility. In future studies, we plan to introduce fully syngeneic models with complete genetic background matching or spontaneous HCC models62 to further eliminate potential confounding effects from systemic immune responses, thereby more precisely elucidating the functional role of PIDD1 in the natural progression of tumors and the regulation of the immune microenvironment.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Animals

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (Ethics Approval Number: 2,024,002,271). To establish an in situ HCC model, male C57BL/6J mice (5 weeks old) were injected with 1 × 106 Hepa1-6 cells into the left liver lobe. Two weeks after tumor implantation, tumor RFA was performed under anesthesia. During the procedure, a handheld infrared thermometer was used to monitor the tumor surface temperature, which was maintained above 70 °C for at least 2 minutes at a current of 2.0 A. Partial RFA was achieved by a single local heating, whereas complete RFA was simulated by multiple overlapping ablations to cover the entire tumor area. To block the PD-1 signaling pathway in vivo, mice in the experimental group were treated with an anti-mouse PD-1 monoclonal antibody (clone RMP1-14; Leinco Technologies, Inc). The anti–PD-1 antibody was administered by intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 10 mg/kg, diluted in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Dosing was performed once every 3 days for a total of 4 injections. Mice were monitored daily to assess tumor growth, treatment response, and overall health. At the end of the study, mice were euthanized using CO2 inhalation, and liver tumors were harvested for downstream analyses, including flow cytometry and histochemical evaluation.

Data Acquisition and Gene Analysis

The GSE212604 dataset, available from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds), was analyzed. This dataset includes liver tissue sequencing data from 10 patients with HCC before and after RFA. Weighted gene coexpression network analysis was used to identify genes associated with suppressor T cells. These genes were intersected with DEGs from GSE212604 to identify core DEGs. Key nucleotide variations in core genes were analyzed using the maftools package with data retrieved from TCGA. Clinical traits and protein expression levels were further evaluated using GeneMANIA (http://genemania.org) and the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org).

Single-Cell RNA Sequencing Analysis

Single-cell transcriptomic data were obtained from the mouse HCC immunotherapy dataset CRA007109, hosted by the China National Center for Bioinformation. Specifically, EGFP+ CD4+ T cells were isolated via flow cytometry and subjected to 10X Genomics single-cell RNA sequencing. Data preprocessing, integration, and visualization were performed in the R statistical environment using Seurat (v4.4.0), with additional support from the Harmony, dplyr, ggplot2, tidyverse, and patchwork packages. After initial global clustering, T cells were specifically extracted for further subclustering and high-resolution analysis. T cell identification was initially confirmed by the expression of pan-T cell markers (CD2, CD3D, and CD3E). To precisely define T cell subpopulations, we utilized a comprehensive panel of marker genes, including lineage-specific markers (CD4, CD8A), naive/memory markers (CCR7, SELL, TCF7), regulatory markers (FOXP3, IL2RA, CTLA4), cytotoxic/effector molecules (GZMB, GZMK, IFNG, PRF1, NKG7), and tissue-resident/chemokine genes (CXCR6, ITGA1, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5). Batch effects were corrected using the Harmony algorithm, and cluster identity was visualized via Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection. Statistical significance of gene expression across subsets was presented using box plots and dot plots.

Patients and Specimens

Patient cohorts and clinical data

Clinical data were retrospectively collected from 397 patients with primary HCC treated at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University between 2002 and 2019. Patients were categorized into a liver resection cohort (n = 229) and a RFA cohort (n = 168). Within the resection cohort, 13 patients received preoperative anti–PD-1 immunotherapy. For histologic analysis, tissue microarrays were constructed using samples from 124 patients in the resection cohort, which were utilized for OS and DSS analyses in Figure 13. Notably, 5 patients in the RFA cohort with tumors ≥2 cm underwent secondary surgical resection within 2 years postablation; these salvaged tissues were utilized for subsequent IHC and IF staining. Recurrence and survival data were obtained through standardized outpatient follow-up. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and baseline clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence

HCC specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dewaxed, and hydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed in EDTA buffer (microwave, 25 minutes). Sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin. For multiplex immunofluorescence, sections were stained using a TSA Plus Fluorescence Triple Staining Kit (G1236, Servicebio). Primary antibodies included anti-PIDD1 (1:400), anti-p53 (1:400), anti-Foxp3 (1:200), and anti-calreticulin (CRT; 1:400). To specifically evaluate the induction of ICD within the thermal ablation transition zone, we assessed the cell surface translocation of CRT. Following primary antibody incubation, sections were incubated with the corresponding fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (or tyramide signal amplification detection reagents) and nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole. Images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope. ICD status was defined by the characteristic punctate or circumferential accumulation of CRT signal at the plasma membrane of residual tumor cells, distinct from the cytoplasmic distribution observed in nonstressed cells. ImageJ software was used to quantify the staining intensity and positive areas.

Cell Culture, Thermal Treatment, and Gene Manipulation

Hepa1-6 and HepG2 cell lines (ATCC) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. To simulate the sublethal thermal stress observed at the tumor margin during in vivo RFA, cells were exposed to 42 °C for 2 hours (based on infrared thermography monitoring) and subsequently returned to 37 °C for recovery. Total RNA and proteins were harvested at 2, 12, and 24 hours post-treatment.

For genetic modification, PIDD1 expression was silenced using a lentiviral Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeat interference (CRISPRi) system. Cells were transduced with lentiviral particles expressing species-specific single guide RNAs (targeting human PIDD1 or mouse Pidd1) and an inactive Cas9 (dCas9) fused to a Krüppel-associated box repressor. Complementarily, to achieve stable OE of PIDD1, cells were transduced with lentiviral particles carrying the respective human or mouse complementary DNA. For the human background, the pLentiEF1a-H-PIDD1-DEST-SFB plasmid was utilized to drive the expression of human PIDD1 under a high-activity EF1a promoter. This vector also includes a C-terminal SFB tag for downstream protein detection. A similar lentiviral approach was applied for mouse Pidd1 overexpression. Following transduction, stable cell pools were established via puromycin selection (2 μg/mL for 7 days). The efficiency of PIDD1 KD or OE was rigorously validated via Western blotting to confirm substantial and consistent changes in target protein levels.

Reverse Transcription Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction Analysis

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). Complementary DNA synthesis and quantitative polymerase chain reaction were performed using the SYBR Premium Ex Taq II Kit (Takara) on an ABI PRISM 7500 system. Primer sequences are listed in Table 2. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method with 18S as the reference gene.

Western Blotting

Protein concentrations were determined using a spectrophotometer (BIO-RAD). Samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, and probed with primary antibodies against PIDD1, p53, Caspase-2, MDM2 (1:1000, Abclonal), and SHC, Phospho-SHC (Ser36) (1:1000, Affinity), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase served as a loading control. Horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies were applied, and signals were visualized via chemiluminescence.

Isolation of Mitochondrial and Cytosolic Fractions

Mitochondrial and cytosolic fractions were isolated from cultured cells using the Cell Mitochondria Isolation Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were harvested, washed with ice-cold PBS, and resuspended in the provided mitochondria isolation buffer. Following homogenization on ice, the cell lysates were centrifuged at 600 × g for 5 minutes at 4 °C to pellet the nuclei and unbroken cells. The resulting supernatant was carefully transferred to a new microcentrifuge tube and further centrifuged at 11,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C. The pellet, containing the enriched mitochondrial fraction, was resuspended and lysed in the mitochondrial lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The supernatant from the high-speed centrifugation was collected as the cytosolic fraction. Protein concentrations of both fractions were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology). To verify the purity of the subcellular fractionation and the absence of cross-contamination, equal amounts of protein were subjected to Western blot analysis. Mitochondrially encoded cytochrome C oxidase II, a mitochondrially encoded protein, was utilized as a mitochondria-specific marker, whereas glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase served as the cytosolic marker.

Isolation of Naive T Cells From Human Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells Using Magnetic Bead Separation

Peripheral blood from human donors was collected and processed using Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation to isolate peripheral blood mononuclear cells. Naive T cells were isolated using the Human Naive T Cell Isolation Kit (Stemcell Technologies, catalog: 17,555). Briefly, 50 μL of the provided cocktail was added to the isolated peripheral blood mononuclear cells and incubated at room temperature for 5 minutes. Magnetic beads (50 μL) were then added, and the mixture was incubated with a magnetic field at room temperature for an additional 5 minutes. The naive T cells were subsequently collected for downstream applications.

Splenic CD8+ T Cell Isolation

Primary murine CD8+ T cells were isolated from the spleens of C57BL/6J mice using the EasySep Mouse CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, spleens were dissociated into single-cell suspensions using a 70-μm cell strainer. After red blood cell lysis (Beyotime Biotechnology), cells were resuspended in PBS containing 2% fetal bovine serum and 1 mM EDTA and adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 108 cells/mL. The cells were sequentially incubated with rat serum and isolation cocktail, followed by RapidSpheres magnetic beads. Untouched CD8+ T cells were collected by magnetic separation.

In Vitro T Cell Interferon γ Secretion and T Cell Exhaustion Assay

Purified CD8+ T cells (effector cells) were cocultured with target cells at various effector-to-target ratios for 24 hours. To assess effector cytokine production, intracellular IFN-γ staining was performed. Prior to detection, cells were incubated for 4 hours with brefeldin A (BioLegend). Following surface staining for CD3 and CD8, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Intracellular Fixation & Permeabilization Buffer Set (BioLegend) and subsequently stained with an allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated anti-mouse IFN-γ antibody (Clone XMG1.2, BioLegend). To evaluate the exhaustion phenotype, cocultured T cells were washed and incubated with anti-mouse CD3, CD8, PD-1 (clone RMP1-30), and TIM-3 (clone RMT3-23) antibodies (all purchased from BioLegend) at 4 °C in the dark for 30 min. Data were acquired on a flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Flow Cytometry

To ascertain the proportion of T cells, murine liver tumors were harvested during an animal study; the tumor cells were sliced and digested with type IV collagenase and RNAse for 1.5 hours. The resulting digested solution was collected, separated using lymphocyte separation medium (7,211,011, Dakewe), and the proportion of T cells was evaluated. In vitro, human naive T cells were isolated from the peripheral blood of healthy donors utilizing human naive T cell sorting beads (17,555, Stemcell), and Treg cells were identified using the apparatus after the addition of the appropriate stimulant. T cells were labeled with APC.Cy7 anti-mouse CD45 antibody (103,116, Biolegend), fluorescein isothiocyanate anti-mouse CD3 antibody (100,306, Biolegend), BV421 anti-mouse CD8 antibody (100,738, Biolegend), BV785 anti-mouse CD223 (LAG3) antibody (125,219, Biolegend), and APC anti-mouse IFN-γ antibody (505,810, Biolegend). PE anti-mouse and human Foxp3 antibody (320,008, Biolegend) and fluorescein isothiocyanate anti-mouse and human CD25 antibody (102,006, Biolegend) were employed to stain Tregs. Each sample was stained with 5 × 106 cells for flow cytometry. The cells were rinsed with PBS and subsequently stained with the diluted antibodies at 4 °C for 1 hour, followed by detection using the apparatus.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay

Protein levels in serum or cell culture supernatants were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (Thermo Fisher, Catalog No. 88-8350) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 96-well plates were coated with capture antibodies overnight at 4 °C and blocked for 1 hour at room temperature. Samples were added in triplicate and incubated for 2 hours at 37 °C. After washing, biotinylated detection antibodies and streptavidin–horseradish peroxidase conjugates were sequentially applied, with incubation at 37 °C for 1 hour and 30 minutes, respectively. The reaction was developed with 3,3',5,5'-tetramethylbenzidine substrate in the dark for 15 minutes and stopped with a stop solution. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Protein concentrations were determined using standard curves, and data were reported as mean ± standard deviation from triplicate measurements. Statistical analysis was performed using 1-way analysis of variance or t tests.

Statistical Analysis

Kaplan–Meier curves were used to assess survival, and P values, along with hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals, were calculated using log-rank tests and univariate Cox proportional hazards regression. For normally distributed data, differences between 2 groups were analyzed using unpaired 2-tailed Student t tests. For comparisons among more than 2 groups, 1-way analysis of variance with the Bonferroni post hoc test was employed. Nonparametric data were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. A P value of < .05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.
