Tachycardia with left bundle branch block morphology in a patient with right bundle branch block
Authors: Ayelet Shauer, Bernard Belhassen

ABSTRACT
A 35-year-old woman with a history of surgical mitral valve repair at age 16 years, underwent Holter monitoring because of frequent episodes of palpitations. Her baseline ECG showed sinus rhythm with right bundle branch block (RBBB) and a PR interval of 120 ms, with no suspicion of ventricular preexcitation.

Discussion

Differential diagnosis of the first ECG (Fig. 3)

Probable sinus rhythm is present with progressive prolongation of the cycle length (CL) from 700 ms to 891 ms on the first seven atrial complexes. The QRS duration (110 ms) and morphology of the first five and last two QRS complexes are suggestive of RBBB, which is present on baseline ECG. The 6th P wave (∗) is not followed by an RBBB complex but by a presumed left bundle branch block (LBBB) complex following a marked PR prolongation by 220 ms (from 120 to 340 ms). The next 7th P wave is also followed by an LBBB complex preceded by a PR of 360 ms, after which sinus rhythm conducts the last two beats with an RBBB pattern.

This ECG is compatible with 3 main explanations:

First explanation: P waves #6 and #7 are not conducted to the ventricles due to paroxysmal atrioventricular (AV) block. The two wide QRS complexes associated with these blocked P waves merely represent ventricular escape beats having an LBBB pattern preceding resumption of normal AV conduction on the last two complexes. The occurrence of paroxysmal AV block following gradual sinus CL prolongation without significant PR change in a patient asleep highly suggests hyperresponsiveness of the AV node to vagotonic reflexes [1].

Second explanation: After a progressive prolongation of the sinus CL preceding QRS complexes 1 to 5, both marked PR prolongation and shift from a baseline “RBBB” complex to an “LBBB” complex occur on two successive conducted complexes. Such findings may be explained by 1 to 1 AV conduction associated with a phase 4 (bradycardia-dependent) LBBB. The marked PR prolongation preceding the LBBB complexes could result from major conduction delay in the RBB in the setting of LBBB.

Third explanation: P waves #6 and #7 are conducted to the ventricles through a right-sided accessory pathway (AP) which bypasses the normal AV conduction system. This would explain the slight initial slurring preceding the LBBB complex. The prolonged PR interval preceding the preexcited QRS complexes #6 and #7 could be due to one of the following three mechanisms: a) the AP is an AV pathway exhibiting a long conduction time; b) the AP is an atrio-fascicular pathway exhibiting a long conduction time [2]; c) the AP consists of a nodo-ventricular pathway which connects a slow AV nodal pathway directly to the ventricle muscle [3].

While P waves are well visualized throughout almost all the ECG tracing, there is no one apparent between the 7th and 8th P waves. However, a slight notch is present at the terminal part of the 7th QRS suggesting that a retrograde P′ wave is present (blue arrow). Such a P’ wave may be the result of an echo beat following the preceding marked PR prolongation.

Presumed final diagnosis of the tachycardia on the second ECG (Fig. 4)

Contrasting with the baseline sinus rhythm CLs observed on the first tracing, the baseline CLs are shorter (ranging from 763 to 779 ms for the first five QRS complexes) consistent with the fact that the patient is now awake.

An atrial extrasystole occurring on the T wave of the 5th QRS complex (red arrow) is conducted to the ventricle with a long PR interval (approximately 400 ms) and initiates a wide QRS tachycardia. The latter has an LBBB morphology and a CL ranging from 481 to 505 ms. The tachycardia complexes have a morphology similar to the LBBB complexes documented in Fig. 1. A retrograde P wave (blue arrow) is manifest at the terminal part of each of the QRS complexes of the tachycardia with progressive shortening of the RP’ interval on the first beats of the tachycardia. Considering the similarity in morphology of the LBBB complexes following a prolonged PR interval in both Fig. 1, Fig. 2, it seems likely that among the three presumed explanations suggested above for interpreting the conducted wide QRS complexes, only the last one is plausible. Both the first explanation (vagal-mediated PR prolongation) and the second explanation (phase 4 LBBB) can be ruled out.

Conclusion

In summary, we suggest that the LBBB complex in both Fig. 1, Fig. 2 represent antegrade conduction over a right sided AP (either AV or atriofascicular) having slow conducting properties or a slow nodal pathway connected to a nodoventricular AP. The tachycardia in Fig. 2 represents an antidromic tachycardia involving this pathway in the antegrade direction and likely the His-AV node system in the retrograde direction. Final diagnosis was made during electrophysiologic study. Editorial commentary (Mohammed Mhanna & Brian Olshansky)

Slowly Conducting Right-Sided Accessory Pathways – More than Meets the Eye.

We congratulate Professor Belhassen and Dr. Shauer for a thoughtful and stimulating case that highlights the diagnostic complexity of wide-QRS tachycardias with atypical conduction properties. The tracings presented are instructive, and the discussion is of high educational value. While the authors’ final diagnosis of a slow, antegrade-conducting right posteroseptal accessory pathway is plausible, several surface ECG features raise the possibility of an alternative mechanism, most notably a decremental atriofascicular or nodoventricular pathway, which merits more explicit consideration.

The patient, a young woman with baseline sinus rhythm and right bundle branch block (RBBB), develops wide QRS complexes described as having a left bundle branch block (LBBB) morphology following marked PR prolongation after an ectopic atrial beat, without manifest pre-excitation in sinus rhythm. Importantly, 12-lead ECGs are not provided, limiting the ability to determine whether the baseline and tachycardia QRS complexes represent typical bundle branch block morphologies. This distinction is relevant, as subtle features of QRS onset and axis often provide critical mechanistic clues in pre-excited rhythms. During tachycardia, each QRS complex is followed by a retrograde P wave.

The authors provide an excellent discussion of the first tracing, and, based on P-P interval behavior and subtle P-wave changes, a vagally-mediated shift in AV conduction toward a slower pathway is a reasonable explanation; pathological AV nodal block or AV dissociation are unlikely.

The second tracing is more mechanistically revealing. A wide-QRS tachycardia with purported LBBB morphology is initiated by an atrial premature beat conducting with a markedly prolonged P-R interval. The authors interpret this as antidromic tachycardia using a slow right posteroseptal AV accessory pathway. The electrophysiologic phenotype is highly compatible with a Mahaim-type pathway, particularly one with ventricular, rather than fascicular, insertion.

Several observations support this interpretation. First, the absence of pre-excitation in sinus rhythm can be consistent with slowly-conducting Mahaim pathways. Second, rate-dependent recruitment of the pathway, manifesting as wide QRS complexes only after marked P-R prolongation or during tachycardia, can be a hallmark of decrementally-conducting atriofascicular or nodoventricular pathways in contrast with the fixed conduction properties of most AV accessory pathways. The pronounced P-R prolongation (approaching 400 ms) preceding ventricular activation strongly supports decremental conduction, a defining feature of Mahaim fibers [4].

Importantly, the wide QRS complexes during tachycardia appear to demonstrate a slurred initial upstroke, rather than the sharp, abrupt-onset expected with direct fascicular activation. This finding suggests ventricular insertion of the pathway, rather than insertion into the distal right bundle branch. In atriofascicular pathways with fascicular insertion, ventricular activation is typically rapid and abrupt due to immediate engagement of the His–Purkinje system. In contrast, ventricular insertion produces a QRS with a slurred onset, reflecting myocardial rather than fascicular depolarization.

As Mahaim pathways conduct antegrade, not retrograde, during antidromic AV reentrant tachycardia, retrograde conduction is generally via the AV node–His–Purkinje system (or, rarely, a second pathway). However, in the presence of baseline RBBB, an additional consideration arises. If the pathway were truly fascicular inserting distally into the right bundle branch (as they often do), the identity of the retrograde limb becomes less straightforward. As antegrade right bundle branch conduction block already exists, retrograde conduction would be expected to traverse the same diseased bundle and not provide a complete reentrant circuit unless the pathway inserted quite proximally in the right bundle. This further supports the likelihood that the pathway inserts directly into ventricular myocardium with very proximal retrograde activation, rather than into the distal right bundle branch itself [5].

An additional anatomic consideration relates to pathway location. Most Mahaim fibers, whether fascicular or ventricular, insert along the lateral tricuspid annulus, typically between the 7 and 7:30 o'clock positions in an LAO view, rather than at the true posterior septum. While anatomic variants are well described, a posteroseptal ablation site is less typical for Mahaim physiology and, therefore, warrants cautious interpretation, particularly in the absence of confirmatory electrophysiologic maneuvers [6].

A major limitation of the report is the absence of a comprehensive electrophysiologic study designed to discriminate among slow-conducting right-sided pathways, as well as the lack of 12-lead ECG documentation of baseline and tachycardia morphologies. Several standard electrophysiological maneuvers that would have been decisive are not reported [7]. Specifically, systematic His bundle recordings defining AH and HV intervals during sinus rhythm and tachycardia are not shown, nor is the timing of retrograde His activation relative to ventricular activation clearly documented. There is no description of a Mahaim potential along the tricuspid annulus. In addition, pacing and pharmacologic maneuvers to define properties of the pathway, such as, adenosine sensitivity, progressive decremental conduction, and formal assessment of retrograde conduction are lacking.

Without these data, it remains difficult to distinguish conclusively between a slowly conducting right posteroseptal AV accessory pathway, an atriofascicular pathway, or a nodoventricular/nodofascicular variant coupled to a slow AV nodal pathway.

In summary, while the authors’ final EP diagnosis of a slow right posteroseptal accessory pathway is reasonable, the surface ECG phenotype, slurred QRS onset, baseline RBBB, and tachycardia behavior collectively favor a ventricularly inserting Mahaim-type pathway. The absence of detailed His-based electrophysiologic validation, discriminative pacing maneuvers, and complete 12-lead ECG documentation limits definitive mechanistic attribution. Explicit discussion of Mahaim physiology, particularly ventricular insertion variants and their anatomic and electrophysiologic implications, would further strengthen the educational value of this excellent case, especially in a Cornering the Fellow format aimed at nuanced ECG–EP correlation.
