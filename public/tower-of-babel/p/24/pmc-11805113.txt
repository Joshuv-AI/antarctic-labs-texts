Relationship between Kinesthesia and Motor Performance in Young Adults with Generalized Joint Hypermobility: A Systematic Review
Authors: Radhakrishnan Unnikrishnan, Jagatheesan Alagesan, Msaad Alzhrani, Ahmad Alanazi, Mohamed Sherif Sirajudeen, Mahamed Ateef, Shahnaz Hasan, Mohammad Miraj, Hariraja Muthusamy, Mazen Alqahtani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study was conducted to investigate the relationship between kinesthesia and motor performance in young adults with generalized joint hypermobility (GJH). A systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines. PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, PsycINFO, CINAHL, Scopus, and Google Scholar were scoured to select relevant papers. Ten papers were selected for the review. The study found no significant impact of GJH on the kinesthetic domains. Several studies suggested varying impacts of GJH on motor performance, with some indicating potential benefits and others indicating possible detriments. Interestingly, the impact of GJH on motor performance was found to be influenced by other factors such as fitness, body mass index, and the use of specific assessment tools. The results suggest that GJH does not significantly impact motor performance in young adults. However, the relationship between GJH and motor performance appears to be complex and may be influenced by several other factors.

INTRODUCTION

A characteristic of generalized joint hypermobility (GJH) is a greater range of movement in joints beyond what is considered normal for a given age, sex, and ethnicity.[1] In spite of its relative frequency, little is known about how GJH affects a person’s ability to move and perceive colors. Kinesthesia, or the awareness of one’s own position and movement in space, is another essential element of motor function that includes balance, coordination, and the capacity to perform precise motions.[2]

It is commonly hypothesized that individuals with GJH may experience altered kinesthetic perception because of the higher laxity and instability of their joints, which could impact their motor function.[3] “Motor performance” covers a broad spectrum of tasks, from simple ones such as standing or walking to more complex ones involving exact balance and coordination. Understanding if increased joint range of motion in the context of GJH enhances or impairs motor function in these individuals is critical.[45]

There is a lack of comprehensive reviews that consolidate these findings and explain how they connect to one another within the context of GJH, despite some research looking at the impact of GJH on kinesthesia and motor function independently. A deeper understanding of this link can only be achieved by conducting additional studies in this area to fill in the gaps in the literature. Thus, to gain important insights into the functional implications of GJH and to guide clinical practice and intervention strategies specific to this population, the current study explores and summarizes the body of knowledge regarding the relationship between kinesthesia and motor performance in young adults with GJH through a systematic review of the literature.

Impact of GJH on developmental milestones

Engelbert et al.[10] and Yardımcı and Mutlu[17] investigated the effect of GJH on developmental milestones. Engelbert et al.[10] found no significant correlation between the number of hypermobile joints and delayed motor development, suggesting that hypermobility does not necessarily lead to developmental issues. Similarly, Yardımcı and Mutlu[17] found no significant differences in cognitive, language, proprioception assessment, and motor domains between toddlers with and without GJH.

GJH and specific aspects of motor performance

Fatoye et al.[11] and Nikolajsen et al.[15] focused on specific aspects of motor performance. Fatoye et al.[11] found that children with GJH showed higher levels of passive range of motion but no significant difference in gait speed. Nikolajsen et al.[15] observed differences in walking kinetics in children with GJH, indicating a different biomechanical strategy, even if the walking pattern appeared similar to that of healthy children.

Influence of GJH on physical activity and endurance

Hanewinkel et al.,[12] Juul et al.,[13] and Moore et al.[14] examined the impact of GJH on physical activity levels and endurance. Hanewinkel et al.[12] found no significant association between GJH and the 6MWT, suggesting that GJH might not impact aerobic capacity or endurance. Both Juul et al.[13] and Moore et al.[14] found no significant difference in physical activity levels between children with and without GJH.

GJH and risk of motor coordination issues

Wright et al.[16] looked at the risk of lower motor coordination (LMC) in children with GJH. They found that developing children typically scored significantly higher on the RTSBc than those at risk of LMC and those in the LMC group.

DISCUSSION

Anieto et al.[8] and Juul et al.[13] found both benefits and detriments of GJH on motor performance. This dichotomy was evident in other studies, with GJH altering movement biomechanics[1116] without necessarily affecting overall mobility or motor performance.[911151617] Our review, like Romeo et al.’s,[18] identified overlap in symptoms and motor difficulties in children with developmental coordination disorder and joint hypermobility. Sobhani et al.[19] reported a cumulative GJH prevalence of 34.1%, with variations based on gender and age. Blajwajs et al.[20] assessed GJH broadly, noting variations in prevalence across ethnic groups, age, gender, and measurement parameters. Recent studies have acknowledged GJH’s systemic implications beyond joint health.[20] While some findings diverged from adult correlations, particularly in autonomic and gastrointestinal systems, others, such as central sensitization, gynecological implications, and urinary dysfunctions, were consistent with adult observations.[181920]

CONCLUSION

A complicated interaction between hypermobility and motor function was shown by the inquiry into the effects of GJH on children’s motor performance and kinesthesia. While some research found no such link, others identified a strong correlation between GJH and measures of motor function and fitness. Moreover, no reliable association between GJH and overall motor performance was found in any of the investigations. A better degree of motor performance was occasionally linked to manual dexterity in girls with GJH; however, this impact seemed to decrease with increasing body mass index.
