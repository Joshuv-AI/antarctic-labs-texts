Curated Cases from the TB Expert Network: Unplugged!: Navigating Uncertainty in Treatment of a Child with Multidrug-Resistant Tuberculosis
Authors: James Gaensbauer, Emily Harrison, Kristen Wendorf, Lisa Armitige, Erin Corriveau, Connie A Haley, Ethel D Weld, Lisa Chen, Vaishnavi Ramesh, Neela Goswami
Published: 2026

The following case was presented as part of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Tuberculosis Centers of Excellence TB Expert Network: Unplugged! series. In September 2024, a panel of experts was convened to discuss a challenging pediatric case. The patient is an 11-year-old girl diagnosed with pulmonary multidrug resistant-TB (MDR-TB). At the heart of the discussion was consideration of the regimen of bedaquiline, pretomanid, and linezolid with or without moxifloxacin (BPaL/M) and how to extrapolate existing data from adult studies and navigate uncertainties with a child of this age. Prominent points of discussion included the lack of data and clinical experience with pretomanid, and whether and how delamanid might be incorporated into a regimen. The findings and conclusions in this report are those of the authors and do not necessarily represent the official position of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

The case was presented by Dr. Emily Harrison, a pediatric infectious disease specialist at Children’s Minnesota. Dr. Neela Goswami, Medical Officer in the Field Services Branch of the Division of TB Elimination at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and Dr. James Gaensbauer, Medical Director for Education and Training at the Mayo Clinic Center for Tuberculosis moderated the discussion. The panel included international experts in pediatric TB including Dr. Lisa Armitage, Co-Medical Director of the Heartland National TB Center, Dr. Kristen Wendorf, Public Health Medical Officer at the TB Elimination Branch of the California Department of Public Health, Dr. Erin Corriveau, Professor in the Department of Family Medicine and Community Health at the University of Kansas, Dr. Connie A. Haley, Adjunct Professor of Adult Infectious Diseases, Vanderbilt University Medical Center, Dr. Ethel Weld, Associate Professor of Medicine, Pharmacology, and Molecular Sciences at The Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine, and Dr. Lisa Chen, Principal Investigator/Medical Director of the Curry International TB Center at the University of California San Francisco. Written informed consent from the patient’s parent and assent from the patient was provided for this publication; the work was approved by the institutional review board at Children’s Minnesota. Dr. Harrison: Our patient is an 11-year-old female who was first seen in the Infectious Disease clinic in May 2024. Her initial symptoms started 3 months prior with fatigue and a subsequent cough with right sided chest pain. She was seen in the Emergency Department, where a chest x-ray showed a right lower lobe pneumonia (Fig. 1), and she was treated for community-acquired pneumonia with amoxicillin for seven days without resolution in symptoms.

Initial radiograph.

Over the next several weeks her chest pain continued, and she developed difficulty breathing, worsening productive cough, right shoulder pain, pain with swallowing and the feeling of food getting stuck in her chest. She was noted to have a 13-pound weight loss over a few months. She then had a new fever with a repeat chest x-ray (Fig. 2) showing persistent pneumonia and “possible” hilar adenopathy. She was treated again for community acquired pneumonia with ceftriaxone followed by cefdinir for a total of 12 days. Her fever resolved, but none of her other symptoms improved, so a chest CT (Fig. 3) was obtained showing a large cystic or necrotic mass in her right lower lobe that extended to her right hilum and mediastinum. At that point she was referred to our ID clinic.

Follow-up x-ray one month later.

Initial computed tomography scan.

Dr. Gaensbauer: Radiologists in the US interpreting pediatric chest x-rays often do not comment on adenopathy, which can be a pretty important clue to the possibility of TB. The second x-ray in this patient is showing right sided adenopathy, but the original film was suggestive of that too. When you look at the lateral view, the area of the right pulmonary artery is quite full and outwardly rounded, and even in the PA view there are really two separate densities. So, having the right suspicion—clinically or radiographically—and communicating that to radiologists, can be very helpful, especially in areas where TB isn't common.

Dr. Harrison: We obtained further history at our initial ID visit. She was born in the midwestern US, and lived with her mother and multiple siblings. She is in public middle school. Her parents and grandparents were originally from East Africa and moved to the US over 25 years ago; her father continued to travel back and forth to several African countries. Our patient had no other visitors from outside the US and her only travel outside the US was a visit to Cairo, Egypt for one week three years prior, where she visited tourist sites and stayed in a hotel.

Her physical exam was notable for low weight-for-age at 39 kg, and she had crackles in her right lung base with decreased breath sounds in her right axilla. No other abnormalities were noted.

We collected three sputum samples, all of which were acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear- and Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) PCR-negative. Because of epidemiologic, clinical and radiologic concerns for TB, she was started on standard TB treatment with rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide and ethambutol (RIPE).

Dr. Gaensbauer: It’s important to note that she is a US-born individual. In the US, most cases occur among non-US-born individuals, but in pediatrics the opposite is true, with an almost exact inverse proportion. But while the majority of children with TB in the US are US-born, these children often have family members and live in communities with adults who were born in higher TB-burden countries.

Dr. Harrison: Several weeks after starting treatment, her sputum cultures became positive for MTB. PCR of the culture isolate in the Mayo Clinic laboratory suggested probable INH resistance detected based on presence of a katG mutation. The isoniazid was switched to moxifloxacin, and she continued taking rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol. Over the next several weeks she started clinically improving with less cough and better energy level and appetite. Shortly after switching to moxifloxacin she underwent repeat sputum testing. On this occasion, while she remained AFB-smear negative, her Xpert MTB/RIF was positive with detection of an rpoB mutation predicting rifampin resistance. Repeat chest x-ray (Fig. 4) at that point demonstrated some subtle improvement in the lobar opacity, though persistent adenopathy and pleural thickening persisted. At this point, her treatment with moxifloxacin, ethambutol, pyrazinamide and rifampin was held.

X-ray at time of identification of multi-drug resistant tuberculosis (3 months into illness).

During this time, a contact investigation was done with the local and state health department in collaboration with the county in the southern US where the father was living. Two siblings eventually had positive interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs) but the parents and grandparents were negative. Because MTB genotype testing at the state laboratory showed a unique genotype that did not match anyone in the US, her presumed exposure was likely outside the US.

Her TB isolate was sent to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) for molecular detection of drug resistance and showed both rifampin and isoniazid resistance, but susceptibility to ethambutol, pyrazinamide, and fluoroquinolones. Further testing at Mycobacteriology Laboratory at Wadsworth Center in New York State demonstrated susceptibility to bedaquiline, clofazimine and linezolid.

Dr. Gaensbauer: At this stage, we are faced with the task of designing a regimen for an 11-year-old girl who weighs 39 kg. I’d like to ask Dr. Wendorf to talk through some of the key considerations.

Dr. Wendorf: A lot of us are using the bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid with/without moxifloxacin (BPaL/M) regimen for most people with MDR-TB (Table 1), but if you look at the clinical trials for BPaL/M, the NixTB trial, the Ze-NIX trial and the PRACTECAL trials, they didn't include kids under age 15 years [1], [2], [3]. The current World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines recommend against the use of this regimen for youth < 14 years old, partly based on the lack of well-established pediatric dosing for pretomanid. The IMPAACT 2034 trial is currently looking into safety and pharmacokinetics of pretomanid for children from age 0 to 18 years, but currently data is unavailable for children < 14 [4].

The current American Thoracic Society/CDC/Infectious Disease Society of America joint guidelines in the US do not have short-course treatment recommendations for children with RR-TB [5]. Therefore, a common recommendation for children not eligible for BPaL/M based on age would be a longer regimen of 4–5 drugs for 15 months after culture conversion. Similarly, the WHO recommends a longer, traditional MDR treatment regimen for children < 14 years old who are not eligible for the short-course regimens below [6].

The BEAT-tuberculosis clinical trial included children as young as 6 years old through adults and studied the impact of replacing pretomanid with delamanid, as these are from the same drug class and the latter has dosing recommendations down to infancy, with the addition of clofazimine to cover for possible fluoroquinolone resistance [7]. The trial data have not been published to date, but results shared reported good safety and efficacy of the 6-month regimen compared to traditional longer MDR-treatment regimens. Thirty participants younger than 18 were enrolled, all with successful outcomes at the end of treatment. Although younger children were not enrolled in the trial, the WHO has recommended use of this regimen for children of any age, with any disease severity, and with or without fluoroquinolone-resistance, over the 9 month all-oral regimen in the table and described below [6]. However, at the time of this writing, delamanid is not routinely available in the US and can only be obtained via US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and company approval for compassionate use.

The endTB trial included adults across several arms of the study [8]. One of those arms included the use of only 4 drugs, all available in the US with safety and efficacy data available for children. This arm, BLMZ, includes 3 of the same drugs as the BEAT-TB trial, but replaces the pretomanid with pyrazinamide and extends treatment from 6 months to 9 months. Outcomes of the clinical trial showed good safety and efficacy of this regimen in adults.

The WHO’s published 9 month-all oral regimen is commonly used outside of the US but is limited to children with less severe disease. The regimen includes 7 medications, some of which take time to access in the US (clofazimine), others of which are not commonly used in the US (high-dose isoniazid), and two first line drugs to which resistance is common (ethambutol and pyrazinamide). Based on the limitations of this regimen in the US, it has not been routinely used.

Dr. Gaensbauer: If I can jump in with a question related to the fluoroquinolones, Dr. Armitage, if you could give us the brief commentary that you gave us the other day about how we interpret differences between levofloxacin and moxifloxacin, and how we might pick one or the other for pediatric patients.

Dr. Armitige: In general moxifloxacin is the more attractive option if you're doing the BPaL/M regimen because that’s the drug that was actually studied. The 400 mg moxifloxacin tablet can be cut in half and we can get dosing down to about 13 kg. And after that, you have the liquid levofloxacin which tastes nasty. So, trying to fight with a hostile two-year-old over use of that liquid is complicated. But, for most of the studies with MDR, moxifloxacin and levofloxacin have been shown to be equivalent. But we do try to lean toward using the moxifloxacin, since that's the one that's been best studied in TB, even though in children levofloxacin is a drug that has been most used.

Dr. Wendorf: We have had a few successful oral MDR treatment regimens for children in our region. We had a two-year-old with MDR-TB intrathoracic adenopathy who completed nine months of bedaquiline, levofloxacin, and linezolid with cycloserine that was dosed based on drug levels. We treated a four-year-old with MDR-TB pulmonary infiltrate that was initially starting the same regimen as the 2-year-old, but did not tolerate cycloserine, so the cycloserine was replaced with pyrazinamide and ethambutol (this was before the endTB trial was published). We considered replacing the pyrazinamide with delamanid but were worried about the time course of getting it.

This case is an 11-year-old female who is 39 kilos—it’s tricky! She's close to adult dosing. So, if we are considering BpaLM, she'd be at mostly adult dosing for both bedaquiline and moxifloxacin, although often we will use a lower dose of linezolid until kids are at least 12. But again, the biggest uncertainty is the pretomanid dose in an 11-year-old because it just hasn't been adequately studied. So, my initial reaction would be let's use our three core drugs and then choose one additional good drug. But maybe we're stuck at nine months duration now. And is that fair for this 11-year-old who's close to the size of a 15-year-old? And now we have the WHO communication shortening duration to six months if you can use delamanid in a regimen with bedaquiline, linezolid and moxifloxacin (BDLM) discussed above.

Dr. Gaensbauer: Thanks Dr. Wendorf. This is a great segue to a discussion about delamanid. For that discussion, let’s turn to Dr. Armitage and Dr. Corriveau.

Dr. Armitige: In 2021–22 we helped with an MDR-TB outbreak in Kansas and most of the adults were on BPaLM. Two prepubescent children, a 9 y/o girl with intrathoracic lymph node disease and 10 y/o boy with abdominal/pelvis lymph node disease, lived in a household with several other adults with confirmed TB disease. There was also a 13-year-old girl with culture-confirmed MDR-TB disease initially hospitalized with disseminated disease (TB vasculitis, lung and abdominal involvement) who was closely linked to the other household. The drug susceptibility profile of the Kansas MDR-TB patients showed resistance to INH, rifampin, ethambutol, rifabutin, pyrazinamide and streptomycin with susceptibility to ofloxacin. So, the first four commonly used TB drugs—rifampin, isoniazid, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide—were off the table, in addition to rifabutin and streptomycin. However, the patient’s MTB isolate was susceptible to ofloxacin, which suggested the potential to use a fluoroquinolone. So we started the 9 and the 10-year-old on bedaquiline, linezolid, moxifloxacin and then used cycloserine as the fourth drug. The 13-year-old girl was initially treated in the hospital with meropenem, amoxicillin/clavulonic acid, linezolid, moxifloxacin and cycloserine. And after discharge was placed on the same regimen as the nine- and ten-year-old, siblings, the all-oral regimen.

We never considered the BPaL/M regimen in these kids because there was zero data and we try not to use things off label when there are other viable options. There was no literature on pediatric pretomanid which was a hard stop no on using it. We did have literature suggesting that pretomanid and delamanid were similar and probably equally as effective [9]. At that time there was published literature for delamanid in children down to age three years with good pharmacokinetic data down to two [10]. Additionally, there was bedaquiline data for ages six and up [11]. The endTB data was not available back then, so we did not have that. But we did feel that if we could get the delamanid and replace pretomanid, we could do a six-month regimen that did not seem beyond the realm of possibility.

Dr. Corriveau: So, for the nine- and ten-year-olds, on the cycloserine regimen, the initial plan was to keep them on treatment for nine months and the potential to shorten that with delamanid was something that we were really thinking about. I was also a bit worried that cycloserine is a bit of a weaker drug—particularly for the 13-year-old who was much sicker. We thought if we could get delamanid on board, that delamanid and bedaquiline would be really a powerful combination—and decrease treatment time.

Delamanid requires a compassionate use/expanded access approval from the manufacturer, Otsuka, before use, and we learned a lot about that process and how long it will take (Fig. 5). This program is for drugs that are essentially promising, but not yet available in the US. The first use of this process was published in a Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report and CDC continues to help streamline the process [12]. There's an initial submission to Otsuka for eligibility which requires an approval letter from an expert consulting committee, which can be obtained from the regional TB Center of Excellence (COE). The next steps involve FDA investigational new drug (IND) approval, informed consent (there are templates available for the patient, parent or guardian) and local or regional IRB-approval for a single-patient use. The last step is drug procurement, customs and shipping from Otsuka’s site in Germany. For that there is a need for a receiving pharmacy with an assigned institutional power of attorney.

Compassionate use/expanded access process for delamanid.

It took about two and a half months from the time I did the required training to having the medicine in hand for use. Otsuka was very helpful with how to monitor the patients and what to look out for as I treated these children. For example, we monitored electrocardiographs (EKGs) every couple of weeks for the first couple of months, and then monthly out of concern for QTc prolongation, especially in combination with bedaquiline. I did submit one safety report for night terrors in the ten-year-old boy. He calmed right down with some parental reassurance, and we didn't have to stop treatment during the course for any of these three children. The three children did very well, very low side effect situation and the outcome was great [13]. We treated them for 6 months from the day we started them on the drugs, so it did actually shorten their overall treatment course to just under 9 months. I have followed them up since finishing their treatment every six months and they've been doing wonderfully with no recurrence of disease.

Dr. Lisa Chen: I will just briefly share experience from our region with a 16-year-old with multifocal skeletal TB which was isoniazid- and rifampin-resistant. She was initially managed in the hospital with moxifloxacin, linezolid, IV meropenem with clavulanic acid and IV amikacin until they could get bedaquiline. When they did, they dropped the IV medications and added cycloserine. The team worked up front to get delamanid and it took them 2 months. At that timepoint, they were able to drop the cycloserine. The teenager was having new-onset depression so it was good timing that the delamanid got there. The total treatment duration with all the combinations that she had was a year for her bone disease.

Dr. Gaensbauer: We've heard a lot of important information about delamanid and some of the challenges that still remain. When we presented options for our original case to our audience, however, in fact the most common answer that the audience endorsed was to use a regimen without delamanid, BPaL/M—and that was actually what was eventually selected for the 11 year old—so we’d now like to turn the conversation to how we navigate some of the uncertainties related to BPaL/M, particularly for children. And to start off our discussion, I’ll introduce Dr. Connie Haley, who has been a leader in evaluating our experience with BPaL/M in the US and helping introduce it into US practice.

Dr. Haley: As you all are aware, we started a BPaL implementation group (“BIG”) and began tracking a cohort of patients who have been treated with BPaL since the regimen received FDA approval in 2019 [14]. Patients being treated with BPaLM are now included as well. I’d like to share the experience we have in patients who are aged 14 years and older. Of note, not all patients included in the BIG cohort had pulmonary TB disease-- some had extrapulmonary TB, including spinal and pleural sites of disease. All patients in the cohort were managed using therapeutic drug monitoring to adjust linezolid dosing, including a serum peak concentration to optimize efficacy and a serum trough concentration to minimize potential toxicity. In contrast to many of the prior MDR treatment regimens which have been very difficult to tolerate, the BPaL regimen was quite well tolerated by patients in the BIG cohort. They experienced fewer treatment interruptions due to adverse events, such that the total median treatment duration in our real-world setting has really been very close to 6 months. One patient with spinal TB was cured using BPaL for 9 months. Indeed, one of the most compelling aspects of using the BPaL/M regimen is the shorter duration of treatment; Given that longer regimens allow more opportunity for challenges with adherence or potential toxicity, availability of a six-month treatment regimen can really make a difference to the patient and the health care team.

I want to highlight the importance of individualized linezolid dosing using data we recently published [15]. We analyzed approximately 1600 samples from about 500 adult and pediatric patients across North America that had been submitted to the University of Florida Infectious disease pharmacokinetics laboratory for clinical purposes. Of note, many patients taking a standard dose of linezolid 600 mg were found to have a serum trough concentration higher than the target of < 2 ug/mL, which is predictive of drug-induced toxicity, and had their doses lowered. This finding highlights the value of using therapeutic drug monitoring for patients taking linezolid, to avoid toxicity and enable safe treatment completion. Our data also demonstrated that the variation in linezolid dosing that is needed to reach an adequate peak serum concentration is between 12 and 24 ug/mL. Many patients taking the standard-dose linezolid at 600 mg a day didn't achieve adequate drug concentrations and required a dose change to 900 mg either daily or three times a week.

When thinking about BPaL/M for patients younger than the approved age of 18 years, it is always important to have effective communication to achieve patient and parental buy-in. Conversations about inclusion of other off-label drugs including clofazimine and delamanid are equally important. Adolescents can be particularly challenging to manage given the common existence of social, psychological, nutritional, educational, and cultural considerations. I wanted to highlight that there is one 14-year-old in our cohort who was cured in six months and tolerated BPaL very well. Despite the patient’s ultimate success, he was very challenging to manage. The child was initially identified as a close contact of a patient with known MDR-TB, but the family declined LTBI therapy. Given culture and language differences, there was a lack of understanding and acceptance of the TB diagnosis when the child’s mycobacterial cultures were negative. Additionally, the child's father had recently died, further compounding the difficulty of engaging the child and his mother in the treatment plan, particularly the recommended laboratory and clinical monitoring. Nonetheless, the clinical team did an amazing job and was ultimately able to get the patient to accept and complete treatment in six months.

Our experience highlights several key issues to consider. First, healthcare workers should think about TB, even among U.S.-born teenagers or children, if they spend significant time around adults with high-risk for TB. Second, obtaining a definitive TB diagnosis in children is critical, and samples should be sent for a rapid GeneXpert, which can detect M. tuberculosis as well as rifampin resistance in several hours; if indicated, full results for molecular drug resistance will require several additional weeks. When drug procurement delays are added to diagnostic delays, it can be quite a while before these young patients are on the appropriate therapy. Third, while medication tolerance in younger children is typically very good, adverse events can be mitigated by using serum drug levels, for at least linezolid and quinolones.

Dr. Gaensbauer: Many of the fundamental questions/uncertainties in this conversation related to the use of pretonamid in children. We are fortunate to have Dr. Ethel Weld, who is an expert in pretomanid pharmacology and leads the first pediatric clinical trial of pretomanid, which is currently ongoing.

Dr. Weld: Thank you so much for inviting me to participate today. It's been a fascinating case. I’ll present some considerations around the use of pretomanid in children, gleaned from our work doing the first-in-children study of pretomanid, IMPAACT 2034, sponsored by DAIDS and the IMPAACT Network with drug support from the TB Alliance (TBA) and pharmacometrics modeling support from Uppsala University and TBA as well [4]. The gap we're trying to address is the pharmacokinetics, safety and acceptability of pretomanid in children. We initiated a phase one, multi-site study on three different continents of a single dose of pretomanid, with the main goal of identifying the weight-banded doses of pretomanid to be evaluated for a future multiple-dose study in children. We are open to accrual and have enrolled 27 children down to age zero years of age with a target sample size of at least 36. Our interim analysis is now complete and will be presented at an upcoming meeting. The interim analysis will tell us whether we have characterized the PK of pretomanid with adequate precision and accuracy with our accrued sample size, and will enable dosing projections for the subsequent multiple-dose study. We are working under a restriction that was placed upon this study by the FDA because of a concern about testicular toxicity. So, our study is only allowing female participants to participate. But even despite that restriction, we are making good pace and, are anticipating being closed to accrual in Quarter one of 2026 with some good data to present before then. We are fortunate to have access to a child-friendly dispersible formulation that kind of tastes like orange. It's been pretty acceptable so far, and the acceptability and palatability results will be presented in an upcoming meeting as well.

I will say something about how we informed our dosing of pretomanid children, and with the caveat that this is only a single dose we're giving to children—very different from continued longitudinal dosing over months. In our model, we are using a weight-based, not an age-based, approach, attempting to achieve in children the median exposure that was seen in adults in the treatment trials that showed efficacy for pretomanid. We have two dose increments of the pediatric formulation--10 and 50 mg--and we determined our dosing based on those practical dose increments. Our cutoff for the 200 mg adult dose was 40 kg, children weighing 20–40 kg got 100 mg, those weighing 12–20 kg got 75 mg, and those weighing under 12 kg got 20–50 mg, depending on weight band. An 11-year-old girl weighing 40 kg would, in our study, be dosed with the adult 200 mg tablet dose of pretomanid, with exposures that are only a slight bit higher than the adult median exposures and still well within the observed adult interquartile range. An 11-year-old weighing 39 kg, like this patient, would be dosed with 100 mg of the dispersible pretomanid tablet in our study, but this dose would be shifted upward as soon as she gained an additional 2 lb and passed the 40 kg threshold

I’d like now to address the concern about pretomanid’s testicular toxicity in animal models. This is actually a class effect with the nitroimidazoles—seen, for example, with metronidazole, which is very widely used in human children across the globe [16]. So, testicular toxicity is something that’s not unexpected for a drug of the same class. However, this is a pre-clinical signal seen only in animals and indeed only in rodents; it was not seen in monkeys when specifically looked for. There were two studies in juvenile rats who were given up to 100 mg per kg of pretomanid per day, compared to what we were giving in our single-dose study, which was around 6 mg/kg one time only, and the adult dose in treatment trials, which was 3.2 mg/kg/day. It's a different order of magnitude of exposure to this drug. In one juvenile rat study they noted testicular degeneration in 29% of rats given 30 mg/kg of pretomanid for 6 months and in 100% of rats given 100 mg/kg/day for 6 months. In another study, rats given 100 mg/kg/day pretomanid had reduced sperm concentrations and abnormal sperm motility after 4 weeks and seminiferous tubule atrophy, germ cell degeneration affecting spermatocytes and spermatids, and decreased fertility after 8 weeks [17]. Based on this data, the level at which we know observed adverse effects were seen in these juvenile rats was 30 mg/kg per day. No adverse effects, including testicular, were seen below that exposure level, and our study exposure is much lower. When the studies were repeated in monkeys there was no testicular damage or safety signal seen [18].

When we look at the data that we have from humans on the effect of pretomanid on male reproductive hormones, hormone levels generally behave quite similarly in people who received pretomanid as compared to people who received standard TB treatment—the male hormone levels pretty much increased over time as patients got better from their TB [19]. So, as they recovered from their severe TB, their hormonal dysregulation also recovered. There is also a paternity study among male participants from four studies, which showed that of the male participants who were surveyed, 38, or 12%, reported fathering 44 total children while on pretomanid [20]. Data recently presented by Pauline Howell from Johannesburg at CROI in February 2024 looked at semen toxicity among patients treated with bedaquiline, pretomanid, moxifloxacin and pyrazinamide in South Africa and the country of Georgia [21]. The primary endpoint was a change in total sperm count (number of sperm) from baseline to week 26. In the 22 men who completed treatment, overall sperm count increased over time, perhaps with some flattening towards the end. And they also showed that there was not a correlation between the pretomanid trough and the percent change in mean sperm count, as well as showing that the median hormone values over time increased while on pretomanid, as I noted before. This would not be expected if there was a significant negative impact of pretomanid on human testicular tissue.

In terms of whether there are any reproductive risks to children who are female, no reproductive safety signal was seen in female animals taking pretomanid in any of the preclinical work and nor have any reproductive adverse events been seen in adult women taking pretomanid. It is also essential to remember that pretomanid is currently being used across the globe in children, both male and female, who are aged 14 years and above and have RR-TB, as part of the global rollout of BPaL/M, based on prevailing WHO guidance.

Dr. Gaensbauer: At this point, we’d like to see what the audience is thinking about this case. How comfortable would you be using pretomanid in an 11-year-old? Ok, here are the results: Very comfortable—looks like 37%? Not at all, No way, no how: 6%? You could be convinced: 43%. And totally indecisive, my priority would be to find something else: 15%.

Dr. Armitige: One of those “no” votes was me. Data on a single dose of pretomanid in children is not enough for me, especially with other viable options. As pediatricians, we often don’t have pediatric drug studies, so frequently have to rely on extrapolated adult data, but with a single dose, I don't know what's going to happen with metabolism over time. For a prepubescent girl, in the setting of essentially no drug data on children under age 14 years taking pretomanid, I have two other drugs—delamanid and PZA—which were used in the endTB study and have been studied in children [8]. The endTB study was an adult study, but I've used every single one of those drugs in young children, and I know them all to be efficacious, at least individually, and there is PK data and dosing data in small children. So, until I have more than data on one dose, I would not use pretomanid in prepubescent children. I am also concerned about the hormone variations and the lack of data in prepubescent children or children going through puberty.

Dr. Gaensbauer: I can briefly present a couple of counterpoints. Given our present constraints in the U.S., using delamanid adds an additional 8–10 weeks to the overall treatment regimen. In addition to the burden of longer treatment, this delay has the potential to amplify toxicity. This is true both on the front end if a more toxic bridging regimen is required—particularly if that involves IV medications—as well as at the tail end. For instance, if a drug like linezolid is used, the risk of adverse effects will certainly be greater if it is used for almost 9 months compared to 6. So, the known risks and toxicities of established regimens and drugs should be considered in parallel with navigating the uncertainties associated with novel treatments.

The fact that, historically, children just haven’t been included in early drug studies has really left children out of the benefits of many evolving treatments. If you look at pediatric MDR-TB globally--how many treatment starts, how many completions, how many successful treatments—a shortened three- or four-drug all-oral regimen could make a huge difference if it were available. And so, it's always worth kind of paying attention to the kids that are being left behind while we struggle with this concept and the associated uncertainties. We're starting to get some attention to this, for example, with more delamanid data and recommendations in a more accelerated process. So, I think the word is maybe finally getting out.

For the final word, let’s go back to Dr. Harrison—tell us: how is your patient doing now?

Dr. Harrison: This has been a great discussion and details many of the key questions and uncertainties we considered. For this patient, in discussion with her family, we elected to utilize the BPaLM regimen, using bedaquiline at 400 mg daily for 2 weeks and then 200 mg three times a week, pretomanid at 200 mg daily, linezolid at 400 mg daily, and moxifloxacin at 400 mg daily. She has been doing really well. She completed DOT with the health department here and has had no issues with any of her medications. In the end her treatment course was extended to 9 months because of persistent cavitation on CT scan at 6 months—out of an abundance of caution. Therapeutic drug monitoring was performed for all drugs and no dose changes were required based on those results, though we did end up lowering her linezolid dose for the last 3 months. We have been getting regular monitoring labs and have really seen no abnormalities. We've been getting EKGs, which have been normal. She is clinically doing well—her cough resolved completely, she's eating better, starting to gain weight, and then probably most importantly for her, she finally was able to come out of her home isolation—she was unfortunately stuck at home all summer, but she was able to attend school in person in the fall.
