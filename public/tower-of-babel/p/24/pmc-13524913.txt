End-to-end deep learning for flight trajectory reconstruction from multi-station ADS-B measurements
Authors: Yingjie Zhang, Bolin Lian, Yingxi Ding, Chenxu Yang, Xiangxiru Xiong, Yuqi Lu, Baohua Tan, Tianyu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the field of aviation safety, ADS-B is widely adopted as an active broadcast-based aviation surveillance system, enabling aircraft to broadcast their real-time position information via onboard devices. However, the open communication protocol relied upon by ADS-B has led to increasingly frequent GPS spoofing and network hijacking attacks, posing significant threats to communication security. A reliable secondary verification method is urgently needed to revalidate the position information broadcast by aircraft. To address this issue, this paper proposes a deep learning framework that does not rely on specific message content but directly calculates aircraft trajectories through tamper-proof electromagnetic signals. Based on real flight trajectories and distributed sensor signals collected from the OpenSky dataset, we trained an End-to-End neural network, innovatively introducing heterogeneous sensor encoders and trajectory decoders, and demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed model through empirical experiments. In comparison experiments, TIGER V2 achieved the lowest MDE of 38.9484 km, corresponding to a 10.21% reduction relative to the strongest non-TIGER sliding-window baseline, while also improving longitude accuracy and most latitude point-wise error metrics. Finally, through ablation experiments and visualization analysis, we demonstrate the necessity of each component of the model and the overall effectiveness of trajectory reconstruction.Keywords: ADS-B, Aircraft trajectory reconstruction, Deep learning, Encoder-decoder architecture, Neural networkSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

With the rapid expansion of the global aviation transportation network and the continuous improvement of airspace utilization efficiency, modern aviation surveillance systems are assuming increasingly important safety responsibilities. To achieve real-time perception and effective scheduling of aircraft status, the international aviation community has widely adopted the Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast (ADS-B) system as the mainstream surveillance method. As an active broadcast-based aviation surveillance system, ADS-B offers notable advantages such as low deployment costs, wide coverage, and high timeliness. This system primarily relies on global navigation satellite systems (GNSS) to obtain high-precision aircraft position information and broadcasts these data via onboard devices for real-time reception by ground stations and other aircraft, thereby achieving comprehensive flight transparency and airspace coordination1–4.

However, ADS-B, which relies on open communication protocols and clear-text broadcasting mechanisms, is susceptible to malicious attacks such as GPS spoofing, false message injection, and electromagnetic interference5–7. Research and practical cases have shown that once the ADS-B system is interfered with, it may lead to flight position drift, dispatch instruction failure, or even induce pilots to make abnormal operations due to incorrect perception8,9, posing a substantial challenge to systems that rely on broadcast messages for flight surveillance.

To address these risks, the aviation industry has gradually developed the Multi-Lateration Technology (MLAT) as a complementary ground-based monitoring mechanism. This system relies on a distributed ground-based receiver network to simultaneously receive aircraft broadcast signals from multiple sites. By leveraging the physical characteristics of signal propagation, such as time delays, energy attenuation, and spatial reception differences, the system establishes positioning constraints to infer the spatial location of targets within the airspace. Compared with surveillance methods that directly rely on message content, MLAT provides independent verification capabilities based on physical layer observations, which can assist in identifying potential tampering, deception, or abnormal behavior in ADS-B signals. Among them, traditional methods such as Kalman Filter, Extended Kalman Filter (EKF), or TOA/AOA fusion positioning10–12 can preliminarily address the generalized MLAT problem, but when faced with heterogeneous, asynchronous, and noisy sensor data in real-world scenarios, the accuracy and generalization of such models still need to be improved.

One solution is to take a data-driven approach and automatically learn the spatiotemporal correlations of signals directly from observation sequences to achieve deep learning-based trajectory reconstruction. Existing deep learning methods have demonstrated good performance in handling complex nonlinear relationships and unstructured inputs. Architectures such as CNN-LSTM13 and FlightBERT14 have been widely used for flight trajectory prediction and completion. Among them, Shi et al. proposed an LSTM-based flight trajectory prediction method that can efficiently learn long term time dependency characteristics in trajectory data15. Nacar et al. proposed the VECTOR model, which uses GRU for continuous modeling of three-dimensional trajectories and achieves good results on small sample flight records16. The popularity of End-to-End architectures has further enriched the capabilities of sequence modeling. By introducing a spatiotemporal feature-inspired encoder-decoder architecture, these models demonstrate high accuracy and robustness17. However, these methods mostly rely on preliminary positioning information (such as radar or ADS-B historical messages) as input18,19, lacking the ability to directly model and jointly localize raw sensor observation sequences. As a result, they struggle to perform effectively in extreme scenarios where ADS-B signals are interrupted, failed, or tampered with20,21.

To overcome these limitations, this paper proposes the TIGER (Trajectory Inception-GRU Enhanced Reconstruction) framework, a general-purpose End-to-End deep learning method for reconstructing 3D aircraft trajectories from multi-source heterogeneous ground sensor observation data in the absence of complete or reliable ADS-B signals. Based on the use of LSTM and Inception as encoders to model and map the observation sequences received by distributed ground sensors to a latent space22, TIGER further introduces a GRU-based recurrent decoder to reconstruct the three-dimensional trajectory point by point. Additionally, we developed a sliding window-based sample partitioning scheme for TIGER to enhance its feature perception capabilities during the training phase, and designed a sliding receptive field attention (SRFA) plugin based on this scheme to uncover higher-order spatio-temporal correlations between sequences.

Results

In this section, we provide a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed method. We begin by detailing the Parameter Settings and Evaluation Metrics to establish the experimental framework. Then, we present the Baseline and Comparison Experiment to benchmark our approach against state-of-the-art models. To further analyze the contribution of each module, a systematic Ablation Study is conducted. We also investigate the Impact of Teacher Forcing Ratio, Sliding Window Length, and Stride on Performance to identify the optimal configuration. Finally, we provide a qualitative assessment through a Visualization of representative results to demonstrate the practical effectiveness of our model.

Parameter settings and evaluation metrics

To ensure that the model has good representation ability and training stability, the encoder part of TIGER uses a bidirectional LSTM structure, with each direction’s hidden layer dimension set to 128, and two layers are stacked to enhance the modeling depth of the time series context. The decoder uses a symmetric structure of unidirectional GRU units, with the hidden dimension also set to 128 to maintain the consistency and computational efficiency of the model structure. The output layer maps the hidden state at each time step to three normalized coordinates (longitude, latitude, altitude), enabling the gradual reconstruction of trajectory points.

In terms of training strategy, the optimizer selects Adam to achieve adaptive learning rate updates, with an initial learning rate set to . An exponential decay strategy of 0.1 is introduced every 10 training epochs to prevent overfitting. Additionally, TIGER introduces a dynamic Teacher Forcing strategy to mitigate error accumulation in the decoder during training. The TF ratio is set to 0.25 during training, while the inference phase uses a fully autoregressive decoding approach. All model training is conducted in normalized space, and error calculation uses the inverse normalized label values compared to the predicted values to ensure the physical interpretation of evaluation metrics. We have attempted to compare the performance of each model under both preprocessing methods. In the MLP method, we flatten the entire source sequence as input and transform it into the target sequence. Since the space complexity of this method increases quadratically with sequence length, it is computationally infeasible for complete sequences. The trajectory-level metrics reveal a complementary pattern. TIGER V1 achieves the best FPE and FD values, 60.9409 and 114.3822, respectively, while TIGER V2 ranks second in FD and remains close to the leading group in FPE. This suggests that TIGER V1 preserves slightly better endpoint and global-shape consistency, whereas TIGER V2 provides stronger average point-wise 3D reconstruction. The difference is consistent with the role of SRFA: latent-state reweighting improves horizontal localization and MDE, but may introduce a small trade-off in altitude and terminal-position accuracy.

Notably, models trained with the Sliding-window Batch strategy reduce the test error by one order of magnitude compared with those trained using Whole-sequence Batch. Across comparable recurrent and attention-based architectures, the Sliding-window Batch setting consistently yields lower MDE than the Whole-sequence Batch setting, confirming its overall advantage for long-sequence trajectory reconstruction. This suggests that Sliding-window Batch effectively enlarges the training set and significantly strengthens the model’s ability to learn complex temporal structures, indicating its importance in this study. Under this regime, MLP reaches an MDE of 104.5937 km, while recurrent and attention-based models further reduce the MDE to approximately 40–50 km. Our insight is that, for ultra-long sequences, backpropagation struggles to effectively tune network parameters under limited training samples. Moreover, because sequence lengths must be aligned within a batch, Whole-sequence Batch training inevitably introduces a large amount of padding, resulting in sparse feature matrices and degraded performance. Sliding-window Batch helps mitigate these challenges in the present experiments.

For TIGER-V2, which incorporates SRFA-based attention in latent feature fusion, the improvement over TIGER V1 is most evident in MDE and horizontal coordinate accuracy. However, the results also show a slight degradation in altitude, FPE, and FD, indicating a trade-off between average spatial reconstruction accuracy and trajectory-level endpoint or shape consistency. This highlights the inherent complexity of three-dimensional trajectory reconstruction, which we will further investigate in the Model Architecture section.

Ablation study

To comprehensively evaluate the contribution of TIGER’s components to overall performance, we conducted ablation experiments as shown in Table 4. In this study, TIGER V1 was used as the baseline model, and the SRFA plugin was removed from V2 to create the ablation branches. The V1-Sliding_Window, V1-Encoder, and V1-Decoder variants correspond to ablations in which each of the three components introduced in the Model Architecture section is respectively removed or disabled. Specifically, the model with the Sliding-Window mechanism removed employs full-sequence batch training; the model with the Encoder ablated degenerates into a single-layer GRU neural network; the model with the Decoder eliminated utilizes a regression head to transform encoder-extracted feature vectors for direct prediction output, consequently losing the capacity for recursive decoding of the target sequence. Unlike the three complete component ablations mentioned earlier, V1-Inception only partially compromises the Encoder through removal of the Inception network, with the objective of examining the influence of multi-scale spatiotemporal convolution on the Encoder’s functionality.

In the V1-Window Batch ablation branch, model performance significantly deteriorated, with RMSE reaching 4.34 (Lon), 10.46 (Lat), and 7.73 (Alt), and MDE increasing to 1,181 km, representing an error amplification of approximately 27 times compared to TIGER V1. In the V1-Encoder case, since we directly removed the encoder and only used the GRU decoder to generate trajectory points from the original window input, the model’s RMSE and MDE both increased significantly compared to TIGER V1. Specifically, the RMSE in the latitude direction reached 2.74, and MDE exceeds 430 km, indicating that without the encoder, the model cannot extract effective temporal context, leading to a significant degradation in overall prediction capability. In contrast, in the V1-Decoder and V-Inception ablation branches, model performance was also affected, but this degradation in reconstruction capability was not decisive compared to other ablation branches, as it did not cause errors to increase by orders of magnitude and did not structurally undermine the model. Nevertheless, these components remain useful for improving trajectory reconstruction in the evaluated configuration. In summary, we recommend enabling all components and plugins to obtain the best performance observed in our experiments.

Impact of teacher forcing ratio on performance

The Dynamic Teacher Forcing (TF) Ratio mechanism allows selecting a proportion between 0 and 1 to determine the probability of using the ground truth value or the previous prediction value to continue the recursive generation of the next prediction during the training of the autoregressive model. To evaluate the impact of TF_Ratio on model training stability and trajectory recovery accuracy, we set five groups of TF_Ratio (0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0) on the TIGER V1 and V2 versions, selected MDE as the evaluation metric, and compared the results. The experimental results are shown in Fig. 2a. It is worth noting that when TF_Ratio is 0, the model generates the entire target sequence based on complete autoregression, while when TF_Ratio is 1, each next prediction value generated by the model is based on the previous true value (i.e., the previous true value is known to the model).

Impact of (a) teacher forcing ratio, (b) sliding window length, and (c) stride on MDE.

From the overall trend, a lower TF_Ratio (0.25) achieves the best reconstruction performance for both TIGER V1 and V2. Directly setting TF_Ratio to 0 or 1 does not yield optimal model performance. This is because when TF = 0, the model relies entirely on its own outputs for iterative prediction, which can lead to error accumulation; whereas when TF = 1, the model is forced to use the true trajectory as input at all times, resulting in stable training but poor adaptation to real-world inference scenarios during testing, thereby causing performance degradation. Compared to TIGER V1, TIGER V2 exhibits stronger dependence on TF ratio changes after introducing SRFA, with overall larger fluctuations in the MDE curve. This suggests that more complex models with attention mechanisms are more sensitive to changes in TF_Ratio. Additionally, the optimal TF ratio value is at a relatively low level, indicating low consistency between the test and training datasets. We will discuss further recommendations for addressing this finding in Discussion.

Impact of sliding window length on performance

The sliding window length determines the temporal receptive field of each training sample and directly affects the amount of trajectory context available to the model. To evaluate its impact, six window lengths were tested, namely L=16, 32, 64, 96, 128, and 256, with MDE used as the primary evaluation metric. The corresponding results are shown in Fig. 2b.

For TIGER V1, increasing the window length generally reduces the reconstruction error. The MDE decreases from 49.9337 km at L=16 to 37.0490 km at L=256, indicating that a longer temporal context contributes to the modeling of trajectory dependencies. However, the improvement exhibits a diminishing-return pattern as L increases. Compared with L=64, increasing the length to L=256 reduces MDE by 5.1603 km, while the training time increases from approximately 0.71 h to 1.85 h, corresponding to a 2.62-fold increase. Therefore, further enlarging the window length improves accuracy only at the cost of substantially higher computational overhead.

In contrast, TIGER V2 reaches its lowest MDE of 38.9484 km at L=64. Further increasing the window length does not improve performance; instead, the MDE rises to 41.9645 km, 42.1022 km, and 42.6436 km at L=96, 128, and 256, respectively. Meanwhile, the training time increases from approximately 0.69 h at L=64 to 6.09 h at L=256, corresponding to an 8.83-fold increase. These results indicate that TIGER V2 achieves its best accuracy with a moderate window length, whereas simply increasing L leads to higher computational cost without corresponding accuracy gains. This behavior suggests that the SRFA-based feature fusion in TIGER V2 provides a more efficient use of temporal context than relying on an enlarged sliding window alone.

Impact of stride on performance

The stride controls the overlap between adjacent sliding windows and therefore determines the density of local trajectory samples generated from each flight sequence. To evaluate its influence, six stride values were tested, namely S=1, 2, 4, 8, 16, and 32, with MDE used as the evaluation metric. The corresponding results are shown in Fig. 2c.

For both TIGER V1 and TIGER V2, the lowest MDE is obtained when S=1. As the stride increases, the reconstruction error rises consistently. For TIGER V1, MDE increases from 42.2093 km at S=1 to 66.3881 km at S=32, corresponding to an absolute increase of 24.1788 km. For TIGER V2, MDE increases from 38.9484 km to 68.6314 km over the same stride range, with an absolute increase of 29.6830 km. These results indicate that dense overlapping windows are important for maintaining reconstruction accuracy.

A larger stride reduces the overlap between adjacent windows and decreases the number of training samples derived from each trajectory. Consequently, the model observes fewer local transitions between neighboring trajectory states, which weakens its ability to learn fine-grained temporal continuity. The degradation is particularly evident when the stride increases beyond S=4, where both V1 and V2 show a marked rise in MDE. Therefore, the experimental results support the use of S=1 as the default stride setting, as it preserves the highest temporal sampling density and provides the most reliable reconstruction performance.

Discussion

In this paper, we have formulated the task of reconstructing continuous 3D aircraft trajectories from ADS-B electromagnetic physical-layer observations, without relying on any specific message content. To address this challenge, we proposed a deep learning framework named TIGER. Through comparative experiments, TIGER achieved the lowest MDE among the evaluated methods and showed competitive performance across several complementary metrics. Ablation studies further suggested that the performance improvement is associated with the two-stage End-to-End neural network architecture and the window-based sample segmentation strategy employed during training.

That said, TIGER does not achieve fully satisfactory results across all metrics. For instance, we observed that the gains in 3D trajectory reconstruction come at the cost of a slight reduction in altitude precision, potentially due to the inherent vertical sensitivity of the signal features. Moreover, the model occasionally fails on certain samples—a phenomenon we primarily attribute to sensor zero-drift effects.

These observations are also related to the data and task settings used in this study. The OpenSky competition dataset is not collected under idealized sensing conditions; according to the official benchmark description, it contains several sources of real-world observation uncertainty, including non-synchronized receivers, clock drift, inaccurate sensor localization, timestamp anomalies, and unreliable aircraft position reports. Thus, TIGER is evaluated under realistic noisy sensing environments rather than artificially cleaned observations. At the same time, the evaluation remains restricted to this dataset, which reflects a specific observation period, geographic region, and receiver-network configuration; validation on independent datasets is therefore needed to assess cross-region and long-term generalization. In addition, the proposed formulation addresses offline trajectory reconstruction rather than real-time tracking, because the complete source sequence is available during target-sequence generation. Finally, the reference trajectories used for supervision are derived from reported aircraft positions in the dataset, so residual errors or inconsistencies in these labels may influence both model training and quantitative evaluation.

These limitations point to two directions for future work. We plan to develop feature embedding algorithms tailored to different sensors, aiming to quantify the measurement characteristics of devices from various manufacturers and models and thereby reduce the impact of zero-drift on reconstruction performance. We also intend to integrate Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) into the framework to incorporate physical prior knowledge, such as aircraft dynamic constraints, with the aim of suppressing non-physical oscillations and further improving trajectory reconstruction quality.

Methods

Data collection and dataset

The dataset used in this study is sourced from real-world ADS-B29 data collected by the OpenSky network, covering tens of thousands of physical layer observation information recorded by over 5,000 receivers within a one-hour time window across a vast geographical area in Europe. Data records are based on the global discrete time axis of the receiving server, i.e., ADS-B messages received at each moment are continuously recorded starting from the server startup, including the time of arrival (ToA), signal strength (RSSI), and the corresponding reception location. Since the server records all flight messages received within each observation moment according to a “time-priority” strategy, the original data exhibits significant out-of-order issues in the flight dimension. Since all windows from the same flight remain in the same subset, temporally adjacent windows from one flight cannot appear simultaneously in both training and testing, thereby preventing overlap-induced leakage.

Problem definition

The dataset described in the previous section can be used for various purposes, such as missing trajectory coordinate interpolation, trajectory tracking, or trajectory reconstruction. In this paper, we focus on the trajectory reconstruction task. We regard as the source sequence and as the target sequence, thereby constructing a typical End-to-End trajectory reconstruction task.

Given a dataset , the goal of the trajectory reconstruction task is to recover the sequence from . Therefore, during training, we aim to optimize a solution function to establish a mapping from to , such that the predicted values approximate the true coordinates . Specifically, to handle continuous trajectory data, we partition the long-range sequences and into multiple overlapping segments using a sliding window of length W and stride S. This strategy transforms the long-range reconstruction task into a series of localized mapping problems, which helps the model capture temporal locality features. During testing, the sensor observation sequence is input, and the parameterized generates the reconstructed aircraft trajectory sequence , thereby evaluating the performance of the model (i.e., the solution function). This problem definition corresponds to a scenario where, when ADS-B message content is no longer reliable, learns the mapping relationship between multi-sensor observation signals and the true trajectory during the training phase, enabling trajectory reconstruction during the inference phase. It is important to note that since the task definition focuses on trajectory sequence reconstruction rather than real-time tracking, the complete context of the source sequence is visible to the model during target sequence generation. This task characteristic also inspired our design approach, where we first compress the source sequence into a high-dimensional vector and then progressively reconstruct the target sequence.
