Lipoprotein(a) and adverse cardiovascular outcomes in dialysis patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ioannis Bellos, Smaragdi Marinaki, Vassiliki Benetou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients receiving maintenance dialysis experience exceptionally high cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is an established cardiovascular risk factor in the general population; however, its prognostic significance in dialysis populations remains uncertain.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis of observational cohort studies evaluating circulating Lp(a) levels and adverse cardiovascular outcomes in maintenance dialysis patients were conducted. MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and CENTRAL were searched from inception through May 5, 2026. The outcomes included all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and MACEs. Random-effects meta-analysis was performed when at least two studies reported comparable adjusted hazard ratios; pooled analyses based on very few studies were considered exploratory.ResultsFifteen reports representing 14 unique cohorts and 6380 dialysis patients were included. Nine studies evaluated hemodialysis populations exclusively, three evaluated peritoneal dialysis populations, and two evaluated mixed dialysis cohorts. Exploratory meta-analyses revealed associations between elevated Lp(a) levels and all-cause mortality (HR: 1.58; 95% CI: 1.24–2.02), cardiovascular mortality (HR: 2.07; 95% CI: 1.22–3.50), and MACE (HR: 1.56; 95% CI: 1.28–1.90). Certainty was low for MACE and very low for mortality outcomes.ConclusionsAvailable evidence suggests an association between high Lp(a) concentrations and mortality and adverse cardiovascular events in maintenance dialysis patients. However, routine clinical use for cardiovascular risk stratification cannot yet be recommended because thresholds, assay standardization, incremental predictive value, and treatment implications remain unresolved.Trial registrationhttps://dx.doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.dm6gp7491gzp/v1.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12944-026-03018-w.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with kidney failure who are receiving dialysis, accounting for more than half of all deaths in this population [1, 2]. Compared with that among the general population, cardiovascular mortality is increased by approximately 10- to 20-fold among patients undergoing maintenance dialysis [1]. Traditional cardiovascular risk factors alone do not fully explain this excess risk [3]. Instead, dialysis patients exhibit a complex and distinct cardiovascular phenotype characterized by chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, vascular calcification, uremic toxicity, and profound abnormalities in lipoprotein metabolism [4].

Dyslipidemia in advanced chronic kidney disease differs substantially from that observed in the general population [5]. Although low-density lipoprotein cholesterol concentrations are often normal or only modestly elevated, patients with end-stage kidney disease display a markedly proatherogenic lipid profile characterized by hypertriglyceridemia, reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels, increased concentrations of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins, oxidized lipoproteins, small dense low-density lipoprotein particles, and elevated lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] concentrations [6]. Lp(a), an LDL-like particle containing apolipoprotein(a), has emerged as an independent and largely genetically determined cardiovascular risk factor with proatherogenic, proinflammatory, and prothrombotic properties [7]. Epidemiological and genetic studies have consistently associated elevated Lp(a) concentrations with coronary artery disease, aortic valve stenosis, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes in the general population [7, 8].

Impaired kidney function is closely linked to disturbances in Lp(a) metabolism [7]. Patients with chronic kidney disease, particularly those receiving dialysis, frequently exhibit elevated circulating Lp(a) concentrations, potentially related to impaired catabolism and apo(a) isoform-dependent mechanisms [4]. Despite the exceptionally high cardiovascular risk of dialysis populations, the prognostic significance of Lp(a) in this setting remains uncertain. Observational studies evaluating the association between Lp(a) and mortality or cardiovascular events in dialysis patients have yielded inconsistent results, likely owing to substantial heterogeneity in dialysis modality, exposure definitions, outcome ascertainment, and adjustment for confounding variables [9, 10]. Furthermore, major lipid-lowering trials in dialysis populations have largely failed to demonstrate consistent cardiovascular benefit, emphasizing the need to better characterize nontraditional lipid-related risk factors in end-stage kidney disease [4, 10]. Therefore, a systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted to evaluate the associations between circulating Lp(a) concentrations and adverse cardiovascular outcomes and mortality in patients receiving maintenance dialysis.

Materials and methods

Study design

The protocol of this systematic review has been prospectively registered and is publicly available (https://doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.dm6gp7491gzp/v1). The study was reported in accordance with the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [11]. The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in Supplementary Table S1. No ethical approval was needed because the analysis used data from previously published studies.

Eligibility criteria

The study population included adults with kidney failure receiving maintenance hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis. Studies involving predialysis chronic kidney disease populations or kidney transplant recipients were excluded. The exposure of interest was serum Lp(a) concentration, which was evaluated either as a continuous variable or according to prespecified categories or thresholds defined in the original studies; when applicable, comparisons were performed between the highest and lowest exposure categories. Because thresholds and exposure contrasts varied across studies, the term elevated Lp(a) refers to the study-specific higher category, threshold, or contrast used in the original report rather than to a uniform cutoff. The outcomes of interest included all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, coronary or peripheral revascularization, and composite cardiovascular outcomes. Studies evaluating only surrogate biomarkers or nonclinical outcomes were excluded. Eligible study designs included prospective and retrospective cohort studies. Case‒control studies, cross-sectional studies, case series, case reports, conference abstracts, animal studies, and in vitro studies were excluded.

Search strategy

The following databases were systematically searched from inception to May 5, 2026: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). In addition, the reference lists of all included studies were manually screened to identify potentially eligible articles not captured by the primary search (snowball method [12]). The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms (Medical Subject Headings [MeSH]) and free-text keywords related to Lp(a), dialysis, and cardiovascular outcomes. Study selection

Study selection was performed in three sequential stages. First, the titles and abstracts of all the retrieved records were screened for potential eligibility. Second, the full texts of the studies considered potentially eligible were independently assessed. Third, studies that did not report the outcomes of interest or met predefined exclusion criteria were excluded, and the reasons for exclusion were documented. The excluded full-text articles and the specific reasons for exclusion are listed in Supplementary Table S3. Study selection was conducted independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved through consensus.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias was independently assessed by two researchers using the risk of bias in nonrandomized studies of exposure (ROBINS-E) tool [13], which evaluates bias due to confounding, exposure measurement, participant selection, postexposure interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Particular emphasis was placed on the adequacy of adjustment for dialysis-specific confounders, including prior cardiovascular disease, dialysis modality and vintage, inflammation, nutritional status and diabetes. The exposure assessment focused on the timing and methodology of Lp(a) measurement, including assay standardization and baseline ascertainment before outcome occurrence, while participant-selection bias was evaluated according to incident versus prevalent dialysis sampling and exclusions related to biomarker availability. The outcome assessment considered the objectivity and adjudication of cardiovascular and mortality endpoints, and selective reporting was evaluated on the basis of the availability of prespecified analytical approaches. The overall risk of bias was categorized as low, moderate, high, or critical. Any possible disagreements were resolved through discussion among all the authors.

Statistical analyses

When at least two studies reported sufficiently comparable adjusted effect estimates for the same outcome, quantitative synthesis was performed using random-effects meta-analysis. The primary effect measure was the adjusted hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) for the association between higher Lp(a) concentration and each outcome. For studies reporting categorical Lp(a), the most fully adjusted HR comparing the highest versus lowest available Lp(a) category was preferentially extracted. When studies reported dichotomous exposure groups, the HR comparing elevated versus lower Lp(a) levels was used. The exact exposure contrast used for each pooled estimate is specified in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S4. Separate meta-analyses were planned for all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and composite MACE. Random effects models were used because clinical and methodological heterogeneity was anticipated across studies. Between-study variance (tau-squared) was estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method [14]. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the inconsistency index (I-squared) [15]. The 95% prediction intervals were estimated to assess the outcomes to be expected by future studies in the field [16]. Because prediction intervals are statistically unstable when based on only two or three studies, they were interpreted descriptively and were not used to make definitive inferences. Studies reporting continuous Lp(a) associations, including per log-unit or per unit increase estimates, were not pooled with categorical estimates in the primary analysis. These studies were synthesized separately when at least three comparable estimates were available; otherwise, they were summarized narratively. Publication bias was not statistically assessed because of the limited number of available studies [17].

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE approach adapted for prognostic factor research [16]. Certainty ratings were determined according to the risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Particular consideration was given to residual confounding, heterogeneity in Lp(a) measurement methods and exposure definitions, variability in cardiovascular outcome ascertainment, and limited numbers of events and studies per pooled analysis. Potential publication bias was assessed qualitatively on the basis of the overall pattern and consistency of findings across both qualitative and quantitative syntheses. Given the observational design of the included studies, heterogeneity in the definitions of MACEs, variable assay methods, and the small number of pooled studies, the certainty was not improved for any outcome. The overall certainty of evidence for each outcome was categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low.

Results

Study selection

The process of study selection is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). The database search identified 1756 records, of which 943 remained after duplicate removal and underwent title and abstract screening. Twenty-seven articles were retrieved for full-text assessment, and 12 were subsequently excluded [18–29]. The most common reasons for full-text exclusion were incorrect exposure, primarily studies not evaluating serum Lp(a) as the main prognostic factor (n = 5), followed by an incorrect study design (n = 3), apo(a) phenotype studies (n = 2), overlapping cohorts (n = 1), and noneligible outcomes (n = 1). The full list of excluded full-text articles with reasons for exclusion is provided in Supplementary Table S3. Ultimately, 15 reports [30–44] corresponding to 14 unique cohorts were included in the systematic review, comprising 6380 dialysis patients. Two reports by Cressman et al. [38, 39] originated from the same hemodialysis cohort and were counted as one unique cohort; they contributed different outcome information and were not double-counted within any quantitative synthesis.

Included studies

The methodological and patient baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Eight studies originated from Asia, four from North America, and two from Europe. The median sample size was 234 patients, ranging from 54 to 1492. Nine studies were conducted exclusively in hemodialysis populations, three in peritoneal dialysis populations, and two included mixed dialysis cohorts. Overall, 58.8% of the patients were males, and 46.6% had diabetes mellitus. The median age of the participants ranged from 47.2 to 74.0 years. Lp(a) was measured using ELISA, immunoturbidimetry, latex agglutination immunoassay, or radioimmunoassay methods. Risk of bias

The outcomes of the ROBINS-E evaluation are presented in Table 2. The risk of bias was driven primarily by confounding and participant selection. Most studies had at least some concerns for confounding because adjustment for dialysis-specific factors, including inflammation, nutritional status, dialysis adequacy, medication exposure, and residual kidney function, was incomplete. Early small prevalent dialysis cohorts were particularly susceptible to residual confounding and survivor bias and were frequently judged to have a high overall risk of bias. Larger contemporary cohorts with incident dialysis populations and more extensive multivariable adjustment were generally judged at moderate risk of bias. Exposure measurement was usually low risk because Lp(a) was measured at baseline using standardized assays before outcome occurrence. Outcome measurement was also generally low risk, particularly for mortality endpoints, whereas composite cardiovascular and cerebrovascular outcomes occasionally raised concerns regarding ascertainment. Missing data and selective reporting were commonly judged as some concerns because prespecified analysis plans and detailed handling of missing covariate data were infrequently reported.

Qualitative synthesis

Cardiovascular events

Six cohorts, including 2886 patients, were evaluated for composite cardiovascular events or atherosclerotic cardiovascular outcomes (Table 3). Five studies reported a positive association between elevated Lp(a) concentrations and cardiovascular events. MACE definitions varied across studies and included different combinations of cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, stroke, revascularization, peripheral arterial disease events, heart failure, hospitalization for coronary disease, and arteriosclerosis obliterans. In a prospective United States cohort of 129 chronic hemodialysis patients, Cressman et al. [38, 39] reported lower 48-month event-free survival among patients with elevated Lp(a) levels than among those with lower concentrations (70% vs. 89%). In a prospective CHOICE cohort including 833 incident dialysis patients, Longenecker et al. [34] reported 297 atherosclerotic cardiovascular events during a median follow-up of 27.4 months; both elevated Lp(a) and low-molecular-weight apo(a) isoforms were associated with atherosclerotic cardiovascular events, although apo(a) isoform size showed stronger prognostic discrimination.

In a multicenter Spanish cohort of 110 hemodialysis patients, Collado et al. [37] reported cardiovascular events in 29.1% of participants and identified Lp(a) levels as an independent predictor of incident cardiovascular events. In a retrospective Japanese cohort including 410 hemodialysis patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention, Hishikari et al. [41] reported MACEs in 45.6% of patients during a median follow-up of 24 months; event rates were higher among patients with Lp(a) concentrations ≥ 40 mg/dL than among those with lower concentrations (59.2% vs. 40.7%; p < 0.001). Similarly, in a retrospective Japanese cohort of 181 patients at dialysis initiation, Ambe et al. [30] reported lower five-year cardiovascular event-free survival among patients with a Lp(a) concentration ≥ 30 mg/dL (44.1% vs. 63.1%; p = 0.008). The principal negative study was the German 4D Study by Kollerits et al. [32], which included 1223 diabetic hemodialysis patients and did not demonstrate consistent associations between Lp(a) concentrations and major atherosclerotic cardiovascular endpoints.

Stroke

Three cohorts including 2188 patients were used to evaluate stroke outcomes. In a retrospective Japanese cohort of 105 patients with chronic renal failure, Ishikawa et al. [43] reported higher median Lp(a) concentrations among patients with cerebrovascular accidents than among those without cerebrovascular disease (38 vs. 23 mg/dL; p < 0.001). In a retrospective Chinese cohort of 860 incident peritoneal dialysis patients, Chen et al. [31] reported 33 hemorrhagic strokes and 12 ischemic strokes during a median follow-up of 28 months. High Lp(a) concentrations were associated with low hemorrhagic stroke risk (HR 0.30; 95% CI, 0.10–0.86; p = 0.026), whereas no association was observed for ischemic stroke. These stroke findings should be interpreted cautiously because the number of events was small, particularly for ischemic stroke, limiting the precision and robustness of the study-specific estimates. In the German 4D Study including 1223 diabetic hemodialysis patients, Kollerits et al. [32] reported an association between elevated Lp(a) concentrations and fatal stroke among patients aged < = 66 years (HR 1.54; 95% CI, 1.05–2.24; p = 0.03).

Cardiovascular mortality

Six cohorts, including 3702 patients, were evaluated for cardiovascular mortality. Five studies reported higher cardiovascular mortality among patients with elevated Lp(a) levels. In a prospective Japanese cohort of 390 hemodialysis patients, Koda et al. [44] reported higher cardiovascular mortality among patients whose Lp(a) concentration was ≥ 30 mg/dL. In a Japanese cohort of 268 hemodialysis patients followed for five years, Ohashi et al. [36] reported higher Lp(a) concentrations among patients who died from cardiovascular causes than among those who died from noncardiovascular causes. Cressman et al. [38, 39] reported an association between elevated Lp(a) levels and fatal cardiovascular events in a prospective cohort of 129 hemodialysis patients.

In the largest study, Zhong et al. [35] followed 1492 incident peritoneal dialysis patients for a median of 45.1 months and recorded 210 cardiovascular deaths; the highest Lp(a) tertile was associated with cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.80; 95% CI, 1.26–2.56; p = 0.001). In a retrospective Chinese cohort of 200 maintenance hemodialysis patients, Liang et al. [33] reported 66 cardiovascular deaths during a median follow-up of 66.5 months; cardiovascular mortality increased across Lp(a) tertiles, with the highest tertile associated with an approximately 3.5-fold greater risk than the lowest tertile. The 4D study by Kollerits et al. [32] did not demonstrate consistent associations between Lp(a) concentrations and atherosclerotic cardiovascular mortality endpoints.

All-cause mortality

All-cause mortality was evaluated in six cohorts, including 3484 patients. In the retrospective Chinese PD cohort by Zhong et al. [35], compared with the middle tertile, both the lowest and highest Lp(a) tertiles were associated with increased all-cause mortality; the highest tertile had an HR of 1.53 (95% CI, 1.18–1.98; p = 0.001). In the German 4D Study including 1223 diabetic hemodialysis patients, Kollerits et al. [32] reported an association between higher log-transformed Lp(a) concentrations and all-cause mortality, particularly among patients aged ≤ 66 years. Liang et al. [33] reported higher all-cause mortality in the highest Lp(a) tertile in 200 Chinese maintenance hemodialysis patients.

In a prospective Canadian cohort of 54 prevalent peritoneal dialysis patients, Iliescu et al. [42] observed 24 deaths during 24 months of follow-up and identified both elevated Lp(a) and smaller apo(a) isoform size as predictors of mortality. In contrast, Goldwasser et al. [40], in a prospective United States cohort of 125 hemodialysis patients, did not observe an association between Lp(a) concentration and all-cause mortality during 14 months of follow-up.

Quantitative meta-analysis

The results of the meta-analysis are presented in Fig. 2. Exploratory random-effects meta-analyses suggested that elevated Lp(a) concentrations were associated with all-cause mortality (two studies; HR: 1.58; 95% CI: 1.24–2.02). No between-study heterogeneity was observed (I-squared: 0%); consequently, the 95% prediction interval was identical to the pooled 95% CI. Similarly, a pooled analysis of two studies revealed an association between elevated Lp(a) levels and cardiovascular mortality (HR: 2.07; 95% CI: 1.22–3.50). The degree of between-study heterogeneity was moderate (I-squared: 26.8%), and the corresponding 95% prediction interval ranged from 1.02 to 4.20. Elevated Lp(a) levels were also associated with a higher risk of MACEs across the three studies (HR: 1.56; 95% CI: 1.28–1.90). Heterogeneity for this outcome was low (I-squared: 20.3%), with a 95% prediction interval ranging from 1.28 to 1.91. Because the all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality meta-analyses each included only two studies and the MACE meta-analysis included only three studies, these pooled estimates and prediction intervals should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence was judged as very low for all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality and low for MACE. Certainty was downgraded primarily because of study limitations identified by the ROBINS-E, particularly residual confounding and participant selection bias. Additional downgrading was applied because of heterogeneity in Lp(a) exposure definitions and assay methods, variability in MACE definitions, and the small number of studies contributing to each pooled endpoint. Publication bias could not be formally assessed because of the limited number of studies per outcome; however, major publication bias was not strongly suspected given the presence of both positive and neutral studies across different populations and study periods.

Discussion

In this systematic review of 6380 patients across 14 dialysis cohorts, the available evidence suggests that elevated Lp(a) concentrations may be associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and major adverse cardiovascular events. The associations were directionally consistent across most studies despite differences in dialysis modality, study period, and exposure definitions, with low statistical heterogeneity in the pooled analyses. However, the quantitative evidence base was limited, and the pooled mortality analyses were based on only two studies each. Collectively, these findings support Lp(a) as a potential marker of residual cardiovascular risk in maintenance dialysis patients but not as a biomarker ready for routine clinical implementation.

The biological rationale is compelling. Lp(a) promotes atherosclerosis and thrombosis through oxidized phospholipid transport, endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation, and impaired fibrinolysis related to the structural homology of apolipoprotein(a) with plasminogen [45, 46]. These mechanisms may be particularly relevant in kidney failure, a condition characterized by chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, vascular calcification, and profound alterations in lipoprotein metabolism [47]. Elevated circulating Lp(a) concentrations are also common in patients with advanced kidney dysfunction, potentially reflecting impaired clearance and apo(a)-isoform–dependent metabolic pathways [48, 49].

An important observation of the present analysis is that positive associations were observed across multiple cardiovascular phenotypes, including cardiovascular mortality and composite atherosclerotic outcomes. Neutral findings were largely confined to smaller early cohorts or highly selected populations. The largest negative study, derived from the 4D cohort [32] of diabetic hemodialysis patients, did not demonstrate consistent associations with composite cardiovascular endpoints, although significant associations with all-cause mortality and fatal stroke were observed in younger individuals. These discrepancies likely reflect competing nonatherosclerotic mortality risks and the marked clinical heterogeneity of dialysis populations.

The present findings are also relevant within the broader context of lipid management in dialysis patients. Large randomized trials of statin therapy in maintenance dialysis have failed to demonstrate consistent reductions in cardiovascular events, leading current KDIGO guidelines to discourage routine statin initiation in prevalent dialysis populations. This relationship between lowering LDL cholesterol levels and cardiovascular risk has reinforced the concept that nontraditional lipid-related pathways contribute importantly to cardiovascular disease in kidney failure patients. Lp(a) may represent one such pathway. Recent cardiovascular guidelines and scientific statements increasingly recognize elevated Lp(a) levels as an independent and likely causal cardiovascular risk factor, although evidence in advanced kidney disease remains limited.

Whether Lp(a) should be incorporated into routine cardiovascular risk stratification in dialysis patients remains uncertain. The included studies used heterogeneous assays and exposure thresholds, precluding the identification of clinically applicable cutoffs. In addition, no trial has evaluated whether selective Lp(a) reduction improves outcomes in dialysis patients. Routine use of Lp(a) for cardiovascular risk stratification in dialysis patients cannot be recommended on the basis of the current evidence alone because clinically applicable thresholds, assay standardization, incremental predictive value beyond established risk factors, and treatment implications remain unresolved. Nevertheless, emerging RNA-targeting therapies capable of profoundly lowering Lp(a) levels may have future relevance in kidney failure if efficacy and safety are confirmed in this context.

Several limitations merit consideration. The principal limitation of this review is the small number of studies contributing to each pooled endpoint, particularly the all-cause and cardiovascular mortality analyses, which were each based on only two studies; therefore, these pooled estimates should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. First, all included studies were observational and therefore susceptible to residual confounding despite multivariable adjustment. Second, substantial methodological heterogeneity existed regarding Lp(a) measurement methods, exposure categorization, and cardiovascular outcome definitions. Interpretation of the pooled MACE estimate is limited by heterogeneity in outcome definitions, as the included composites variably incorporated cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, stroke, revascularization, peripheral arterial disease events, heart failure, hospitalization for coronary disease, and arteriosclerosis obliterans. Third, most cohorts included prevalent dialysis populations, increasing susceptibility to survivor bias. Fourth, the included studies included hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, and mixed dialysis cohorts; therefore, the pooled estimates should not be assumed to apply equally across dialysis modalities. Fifth, assay heterogeneity is important because Lp(a) measurements were obtained using ELISA, immunoturbidimetry, latex agglutination immunoassay, radioimmunoassay, or unreported methods, and differences in calibration and apo(a) isoform sensitivity may affect comparability across studies and limit threshold development. Finally, the apo(a) isoform size and longitudinal Lp(a) trajectories have been inconsistently reported.

Conclusions

The available evidence suggests an association between high Lp(a) concentrations and adverse cardiovascular outcomes and mortality in maintenance dialysis patients. However, the certainty of evidence remains limited, particularly for mortality outcomes, and the routine clinical use of Lp(a) for risk stratification in dialysis patients is not yet supported. Further prospective studies are needed to define standardized thresholds, incremental prognostic values, and therapeutic implications.
