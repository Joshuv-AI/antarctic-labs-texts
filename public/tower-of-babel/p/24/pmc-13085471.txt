CT radiomics of adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction: machine learning-based prediction of perineural invasion status
Authors: Huida Zheng, Zhijie Chen, Zhenan Zhang, Qiming Huang, Xiaoting Ke, Jianhua Xu, Kai Ye, Jianan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreoperative diagnosis of perineural invasion (PNI), a critical prognostic factor in adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG), remains challenging. This study aimed to develop and validate a computed tomography (CT)-based radiomics model for the noninvasive prediction of PNI and to evaluate the incremental value of integrating radiomics with clinical risk factors.MethodsA retrospective cohort of 306 patients with pathologically confirmed AEG was randomized into training (n = 214) and testing (n = 92) cohorts. Radiomics features were extracted from venous-phase CT images using PyRadiomics. Following feature selection with the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) algorithm, five machine learning classifiers—logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), extra trees (ET), adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), and support vector machine (SVM)—were trained to build the radiomics model. A clinical model was constructed using independent risk factors identified by multivariate logistic regression, which were then integrated with the radiomics signature to build a combined clinical-radiomics model. Model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, calibration curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), and standard classification metrics.ResultsAmong the patients, 224 (73.2%) were PNI-positive. The radiomics model based on RF demonstrated the best performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.878 in the training cohort and 0.808 in the testing cohort. The clinical model achieved lower AUCs (0.747 and 0.636, respectively). The combined model significantly outperformed both, achieving AUCs of 0.932 and 0.861 in the training and testing cohorts (all P < 0.05, DeLong test), with good calibration and the highest net clinical benefit on DCA.ConclusionA CT-based clinical-radiomics model can effectively predict PNI status in AEG preoperatively. The integration of radiomic features with clinical parameters further enhances predictive performance and clinical utility, offering a valuable noninvasive tool to guide individualized treatment planning.Keywords: Machine learning, Radiomics, Adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction, Perineural invasion

Introduction

Adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG) is defined as a tumor centered within 5 cm proximal and distal to the anatomical cardia, and is classified into three subtypes according to Siewert: type I, type II and type III [1]. Although perineural invasion (PNI) is a well-established adverse prognostic factor in gastric cancer, its clinical significance appears particularly pronounced in AEG, where it is independently associated with worse overall survival and higher locoregional and distant recurrence rates, even after neoadjuvant therapy [2, 3]. This may be attributed to the unique anatomical location and lymphatic/neural drainage patterns at the esophagogastric junction, which differ from those in distal gastric tumors and confer a higher propensity for aggressive microscopic spread. The 5-year survival rate following radical resection remains unsatisfactory, ranging from 24 to 36%, primarily due to challenges in preoperatively identifying high-risk patients and tailoring appropriate treatment strategies [4]. Therefore, the development of reliable methods to identify key prognostic factors before surgery is crucial for implementing personalized treatment and improving patient outcomes [5].

In recent years, the management paradigm for AEG has shifted toward individualized treatment approaches. For locally advanced diseases, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy is recommended to reduce tumor burden, increase the rate of complete resection, and significantly improve overall survival [6, 7]. However, the absence of reliable preoperative biomarkers—such as indicators of PNI or lymphovascular invasion—to accurately stratify recurrence risk limits the precision of such treatment decisions [5, 8, 9]. Thus, there is an urgent need for non-invasive tools to preoperatively assess high-risk pathological features and optimize therapeutic planning.

PNI, defined histologically as tumor cell invasion involving at least one-third of the nerve circumference, represents a specific pattern of tumor progression [5]. Although PNI has been reported as an adverse pathological feature in several gastrointestinal malignancies, the evidence for its independent prognostic value in adenocarcinoma of AEG remains relatively limited and less consistent than that in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma or distal gastric cancer [10, 11]. One likely reason is that prior AEG research has predominantly focused on lymphatic spread patterns and surgical strategy, rather than biomarker-driven risk stratification [12].

Nevertheless, current diagnostic reliance on postoperative histopathology precludes its use in preoperative decision-making. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) is the primary imaging modality for preoperative staging of gastric cancer, yet it lacks sufficient sensitivity to detect PNI [13]. Radiomics, an emerging discipline, converts medical images into high-dimensional, mineable data by extracting numerous quantitative imaging features. When combined with machine learning or statistical modeling, these features enable noninvasive quantification of tumor phenotypes and prediction of pathological characteristics. To date, radiomics has been extensively applied in tumor diagnosis, staging, survival prediction, and therapeutic response assessment [14, 15].While CT-based radiomics has gained increasing traction in predicting various pathological features in gastric cancer, its application in AEG remains limited. Given the distinct anatomical location, biological behavior, and clinical characteristics of AEG compared to distal gastric adenocarcinoma, there is a compelling need to develop and validate AEG-specific radiomics models. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has developed and validated a CT-based radiomics model specifically for preoperative prediction of PNI in AEG patients. This represents a novel extension of radiomics application beyond macroscopic features (such as T/N staging or differentiation) to microscopic invasive behavior in this anatomically and prognostically unique tumor entity.

Building upon our previous work on radiological models for lymph node metastasis in AEG, this study extends the research focus to the microscopic invasive behavior of PNI [16]. By expanding the patient cohort and employing advanced machine learning algorithms, we aimed to develop and validate a CT-based radiomics model for the non-invasive prediction of PNI in AEG. Furthermore, we sought to evaluate the incremental value of integrating radiomic features with clinical risk factors, with the goal of providing a practical tool to enhance preoperative risk stratification and guide individualized treatment.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study analyzed data from 306 consecutive patients with pathologically confirmed adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG) who underwent radical surgery at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University between November 2017 and August 2025. The inclusion criteria were: (1) availability of contrast-enhanced CT performed within two weeks prior to surgery; (2) no history of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy; (3) definitive postoperative pathological assessment of PNI status; and (4) presence of a single primary tumor. Exclusion criteria included: (1) inadequate gastric distention precluding accurate assessment; (2) significant image artifacts compromising quality; and (3) incomplete clinical or pathological data. The study protocol was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee, which waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Eligible patients were randomly allocated to a training cohort (n = 214) or a testing cohort (n = 92) in a 7:3 ratio. Clinical and pathological data were retrieved from the hospital's Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) and electronic medical records. The collected variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes mellitus, tumor size, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), blood type, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 199 (CA199), tumor differentiation grade, and clinical T and N stages.

CT Scanning protocol

All patients underwent upper abdominal CT scans using either an IQon Spectral CT scanner (Philips Healthcare) or a Discovery CT 750 scanner (GE Healthcare). Scanning was performed with patients in the supine position. The standardized parameters were: slice thickness, 5 mm; tube voltage, 120 kV; tube current, 200–260 mA; and matrix size, 512 × 512. A non-ionic contrast agent (iohexol, 350 mgI/mL) was administered intravenously via the right antecubital vein at a dose of 1.5 mL/kg body weight and a flow rate of 3.0 mL/s, followed by a 30 mL saline flush. Venous-phase images were acquired with a fixed delay of 70 s after contrast injection.

Tumor segmentation and feature extraction

The radiomics workflow is summarized in Fig. 1. Volumes of interest (VOIs) were manually delineated slice-by-slice on venous-phase CT images by a junior abdominal radiologist, blinded to the clinical and pathological outcomes, using a specialized annotation platform (Neusoft Darts Medical Image, Neusoft Co., Ltd., China). Contours were carefully drawn to exclude adjacent fluid, air, and non-tumor tissues. All segmentations were reviewed and, if necessary, corrected by a senior abdominal radiologist to ensure accuracy. To assess reproducibility, inter-observer agreement was evaluated in a random subset of 30 cases using the Dice similarity coefficient (mean Dice = 0.84 ± 0.04), and intra-observer agreement was assessed by one radiologist re-segmenting the same 30 cases after a 4-week interval (mean Dice = 0.87 ± 0.03). These results indicate good reproducibility of the manual segmentation process. A total of 1834 radiomic features were subsequently extracted from each VOI using the PyRadiomics package in Python, encompassing first-order statistics, shape-based, texture, and wavelet-transformed features. All features were standardized using Z-score normalization.

Workflow for clinical-radiomics model development. Key steps include: CT segmentation and feature extraction; feature selection using Pearson's correlation analysis and LASSO; radiomics model construction with machine learning; integration of radiomic features with clinical parameters; and comprehensive model validation

Radiomics feature extraction and selection

Feature selection was performed in multiple steps. First, univariate analysis was conducted using the independent-samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test for normally and non-normally distributed features, respectively. Features with a p-value < 0.05 were retained. Subsequently, Pearson's correlation analysis was applied to address multicollinearity; for any pair of features with |r|> 0.9, the feature deemed to have greater clinical relevance was retained. Finally, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with tenfold cross-validation was applied to the training cohort to select the most predictive features and construct the final radiomic signature.

Model development and evaluation

The selected features were used to build a radiomics model using five distinct machine learning classifiers: logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), extra trees (ET), adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), and support vector machine (SVM). Each model was trained and optimized using fivefold cross-validation within the training cohort. The model demonstrating the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with balanced sensitivity and specificity in the testing cohort was selected as the final radiomics model, termed the "radiomics signature."

A clinical model was developed using independent clinical risk factors identified through univariate and subsequent multivariate logistic regression analysis. The radiomic-clinical combined model was then constructed by integrating the radiomics signature with the significant clinical predictors. The discriminative performance of all models was evaluated and compared using the AUC in both cohorts, with statistical significance assessed by DeLong's test. Model calibration was visually inspected using calibration curves and quantitatively assessed with the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. The clinical net benefit of each model was evaluated using decision curve analysis (DCA) across a range of threshold probabilities.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Python stats models package (version 0.13.2), with a two-tailed P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. For continuous variables, comparisons were conducted via independent samples t-test (for normally distributed data) or Mann–Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data). Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 306 patients with adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG) who underwent radical surgery were included in this study. The cohort consisted of 224 PNI-positive patients (73.2%) and 82 PNI-negative patients (26.8%). Patients were randomly allocated to either the training cohort (n = 214) or testing cohort (n = 92). In the training cohort, significant differences between PNI-positive and PNI-negative patients were observed in tumor size, sex, clinical T stage, clinical N stage, and tumor differentiation grade (all P < 0.05). In the testing cohort, significant differences were maintained in clinical T stage and clinical N stage (all P < 0.05). Detailed clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Clinical model development

Univariate analysis identified sex, tumor differentiation grade, clinical T stage, and clinical N stage as significant predictors of PNI (all p < 0.05). Multivariate analysis confirmed these four factors as independent predictors of PNI (all p < 0.05), with detailed odds ratios and confidence intervals presented in Table 4. These variables were subsequently used to construct the clinical model. The clinical model as shown in Table 5, relying solely on these routine clinicopathologic factors, exhibited moderate discriminative performance (AUC 0.747 in the training cohort and 0.636 in the testing cohort). This relatively modest predictive ability likely reflects the advanced disease stage and biological heterogeneity in our cohort, where conventional clinical and staging variables provide only partial risk stratification for PNI.

Combined predictive model construction and efficacy

The combined model integrated the radiomics score (Rad-score) with the four clinical predictors: sex, tumor differentiation grade, clinical T stage, and clinical N stage. In the training cohort, the combined model achieved superior performance (AUC = 0.932, 95% CI: 0.894–0.970) compared to both the clinical model (AUC = 0.747, 95% CI: 0.667–0.828) and radiomics model (AUC = 0.878, 95% CI: 0.827–0.929). This superiority was maintained in the testing cohort, with AUCs of 0.861 (95% CI: 0.782–0.940), 0.636 (95% CI: 0.511–0.761), and 0.808 (95% CI: 0.703–0.912) for the combined, clinical, and radiomics models, respectively (Fig. 5, Table 5). The combined model demonstrated statistically significant improvement over both the clinical and radiomics models across both cohorts (all P < 0.05, DeLong test), as detailed in Table 6.

Discussion

Building upon our prior research, this study represents a natural progression from assessing macroscopic lymph node metastasis to investigating microscopic PNI, underscoring our evolving understanding of AEG biology. We established a robust machine learning-based radiomics model that demonstrates preoperative CT imaging contains quantifiable information enabling PNI prediction in AEG.

The superior performance of our radiomics model over the clinical model reveals a critical paradigm: while conventional CT staging facilitates macroscopic tumor assessment, it fails to capture the microarchitectural disarray associated with aggressive biological behaviors like PNI [17]. This finding aligns with the growing consensus that radiomics serves as a bridge between medical imaging and underlying tumor pathophysiology [18]. By decoding texture heterogeneity imperceptible to the human eye, our model effectively functions as a non-invasive "digital biopsy," offering crucial advantages for preoperative risk stratification in AEG. This concept has been successfully applied in other oncologic settings, including preoperative prediction of microsatellite instability (MSI) in colorectal cancer and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutation status in lung adenocarcinoma, highlighting the translational potential of radiomics as a non-invasive alternative or complement to traditional histopathological biopsy [19, 20].

The substantial performance gap between clinical and radiomics models carries significant implications. Although multivariate analysis confirmed T stage and N stage as independent PNI risk factors, their moderate predictive power (AUC = 0.636) indicates that TNM staging alone cannot fully represent the complex biology underlying neurotropic invasion [21]. Radiomics texture features (e.g., wavelet transformations and gray-level size-zone metrics) are increasingly recognized as capturing subtle tumor heterogeneity and tumor microenvironment (TME) characteristics beyond conventional visual assessment. For example, high entropy and low uniformity may correspond to increased cellular density, desmoplastic fibrosis, and inflammatory infiltration within the TME—these changes are known to create a permissive niche for nerve-tumor interactions, thereby promoting PNI. Emerging evidence further links such radiomics signatures to key biological drivers of PNI, including hypoxia, angiogenesis, and neurotrophic signaling pathways (e.g., NGF and BDNF), in gastrointestinal cancers such as esophagogastric junction adenocarcinoma [17, 22, 23].

The integration of radiomic and clinical data generated synergistic value, with our combined nomogram achieving superior predictive accuracy (validation AUC = 0.861) [24]. This performance enables the incorporation of quantitative biological risk assessment with conventional anatomical staging to support more informed clinical decision-making. Patients with high predicted PNI probability may be considered for neoadjuvant therapy, while those with low probability could be spared overtreatment [25]. Surgically, anticipated PNI positivity may warrant more meticulous perineural dissection and comprehensive lymphadenectomy, potentially enhancing resection completeness [26]. However, these implications are still speculative. Prospective studies are needed to link the radiologically predicted PNI status with treatment response, relapse free survival and overall survival to establish the true clinical effect.

The selection of random forest as our optimal algorithm warrants discussion. Unlike traditional statistical models, ensemble methods like random forest effectively handle high-dimensional data and complex feature interactions through multiple decision tree construction and prediction aggregation [27]. This characteristic proves particularly suitable for radiomics, where imaging feature-pathology relationships are typically nonlinear and multifaceted [28]. Our model's minimal performance discrepancy between training and validation cohorts demonstrates strong generalization capability, underscoring how random forest captures complex PNI-associated imaging patterns while avoiding overfitting.

Although the present radiomics model demonstrates promising predictive performance for preoperative prediction of PNI in adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG), it must be emphasized that current evidence does not support routine modification of neoadjuvant therapy intensity or the extent of lymphadenectomy based solely on predicted PNI status. Major international guidelines for locally advanced AEG/OAC continue to recommend perioperative chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy followed by en bloc esophagectomy with standard D2/D3 lymphadenectomy primarily based on clinical TNM stage, without incorporation of PNI or other microscopic biological features into treatment stratification algorithms [29, 30]. However, accurate preoperative PNI assessment may assist in more precise risk stratification, individualized patient counseling regarding recurrence risk, and identification of high-risk patients who might benefit from intensified postoperative surveillance or enrollment in future prospective trials exploring biology-adapted treatment strategies.

Several limitations merit consideration. The retrospective, single-center design may introduce selection bias, necessitating external validation across diverse populations to confirm model robustness. Second, although standardized scanning parameters were employed, residual inter-scanner variability between different vendors may still affect radiomic feature stability. Future multi-center studies should therefore incorporate dedicated harmonization techniques, such as ComBat or image-level normalization, to further improve feature robustness across different scanners and institutions. Third, our segmentation achieved excellent reproducibility, though minor boundary variability may still affect certain shape- and texture-based features, future deep learning-based automated segmentation could further minimize this variability and enhance workflow efficiency. Additionally, while the radiomic features showed strong predictive performance, their biological underpinnings require deeper investigation through correlation with histopathological or genomic markers to better understand their relationship with PNI. Ultimately, prospective clinical trials evaluating the model's impact on treatment decisions and patient outcomes will be necessary for definitive validation.

Conclusions

This study establishes that a CT-based radiomics-clinical model can effectively predict PNI in esophagogastric junction adenocarcinoma. By integrating quantitative imaging features with clinical parameters, this non-invasive approach demonstrates significant potential for improving preoperative risk stratification and guiding personalized treatment decisions, representing an important step toward precision medicine in AEG management.
