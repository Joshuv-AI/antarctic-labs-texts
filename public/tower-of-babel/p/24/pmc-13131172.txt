Retrograde perfusion of the dorsalis pedis artery versus traditional distal perfusion catheter use in venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation
Authors: Isabelle R Lytle, Joshua G Crane, Mark S Slaughter, Steven C Koenig, Gretel Monreal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

We compared traditional versus retrograde dorsalis pedis artery DPC placement in VA-ECMO.

Central Message

Using a dynamic mock loop of femoral VA-ECMO, we demonstrated retrograde dorsalis pedis artery perfusion is an effective hemodynamic alternative to traditional distal perfusion catheter placement.

Perspective

In patients undergoing VA-ECMO in whom proximal femoral artery access is challenging or no longer viable for distal perfusion catheter (DPC) placement, a more distal location may be necessary. Using a dynamic mock flow loop of adult femoral VA-ECMO, we investigated the hemodynamic feasibility of retrograde dorsalis pedis artery DPC placement versus traditional DPC placement.

Limb ischemia is a persistent problem in femoral venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO), with clinically significant adverse events and/or poor outcomes occurring in 10% to 70% of patients.1, 2, 3, 4 To prevent limb ischemia and improve blood flow distal to the femoral artery cannula, The Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) recommends the use of a distal perfusion catheter (DPC),5,6 which is typically integrated into the ECMO circuit using the Luer port on the arterial cannula and placed distal to the femoral artery cannula; nevertheless, complications including limb ischemia and an increased risk of mortality remain.2,4,7, 8, 9, 10 In patients in whom proximal femoral artery access is challenging (eg, anatomical variation, obesity, atherosclerotic lesions) or no longer viable (eg, bleeding, infection, failed percutaneous cannulation, hematoma from previous cannulation10), a more distal DPC placement may be required.

One strategy that has been infrequently used clinically is to cannulate the dorsalis pedis artery and deliver retrograde perfusion (Figure 1).3,11, 12, 13 There are limited reports available describing the use of this approach,11,12 and although they suggest efficacy at improving limb perfusion, it is unclear how the hemodynamics with this alternative approach compare with traditional DPC placement in VA-ECMO. In this study, we used a dynamic mock flow loop model of adult femoral VA-ECMO to investigate retrograde dorsalis pedis artery DPC placement versus traditional DPC placement and the impact on distal limb hemodynamics.

Methods

This is an in vitro study (clinical trial number, institutional review board number, and informed consent are not applicable). Dynamic mock flow loop experiments were performed as described previously by our laboratory.14, 15, 16, 17 Two femoral VA-ECMO configurations were tested:

1.Traditional DPC placement: a 15-Fr arterial cannula in the right common femoral artery, contralateral 25-Fr venous cannula in the left femoral vein, and a 6-Fr pediatric arterial cannula as DPC placed anterograde in the right superficial femoral artery (RSFA).

Traditional DPC placement: a 15-Fr arterial cannula in the right common femoral artery, contralateral 25-Fr venous cannula in the left femoral vein, and a 6-Fr pediatric arterial cannula as DPC placed anterograde in the right superficial femoral artery (RSFA).

2.Retrograde dorsalis pedis artery placement: a 15-Fr arterial cannula in the right common femoral artery, contralateral 25-Fr venous cannula in the left femoral vein, and a 6-Fr pediatric arterial cannula as DPC placed retrograde in the right dorsalis pedis artery.

Retrograde dorsalis pedis artery placement: a 15-Fr arterial cannula in the right common femoral artery, contralateral 25-Fr venous cannula in the left femoral vein, and a 6-Fr pediatric arterial cannula as DPC placed retrograde in the right dorsalis pedis artery.

A dynamic mock loop was constructed to mimic adult femoral VA-ECMO (Figure 2). Tubing was selected to replicate average adult vessel diameters18,19 while working within the constraints of standard tubing and flow probe sizes (Tygon 3350; 3/4″ for the aorta and vena cava, 3/8″ for the iliac arteries, 1/4″ for the femoral arteries, and 1/8″ for the distal limb and foot arteries). A silicone ventricle (Derby City Supply) was powered by a pneumatic driver (Ventricular Assist Device Pneumatic Drive System; Thoratec [now Abbott]) that controlled ventricular filling, ejection, and beat rate. Silicone valves (Minivalve International) were placed in the aortic and mitral positions. An arterial cannula (15-Fr LS96218-015, 18 cm; Medtronic) and venous cannula (25-Fr 96600-125, 76.2 cm; Medtronic) were integrated into the loop via Tuohy connectors (Medical Murray). A centrifugal pump (Affinity; Medtronic) and driver (73-5157; Harvard Apparatus) served as the ECMO pump, and a membrane oxygenator (US5062; Eurosets) was integrated into the loop.

Illustration of the dynamic mock loop model for investigating hemodynamics during venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) support (0, 1000, 2000, 3000 rpm) with a traditional distal perfusion catheter placed anterograde in the superficial femoral artery (top) and placed retrograde in the dorsalis pedis artery (bottom).

Fluid-filled transducers (MLT0380/D; ADInstruments) were calibrated with a digital manometer (Meriam) and used to acquire pressure data at the aorta, RSFA, left superficial femoral artery, right dorsalis pedis artery, left dorsalis pedis artery, and vena cava. Transit-time flow probes (PXL with TS410 modules; Transonic Systems) were placed on the aorta, vena cava, ECMO circuit (pump outflow), right and left superficial femoral arteries, right and left profunda femoral arteries, on the DPC tubing proximal to the DPC, on the right dorsalis pedis artery proximal to the dorsalis DPC, on the right dorsalis pedis artery distal to the dorsalis DPC, on the left iliac artery, on the left dorsalis pedis artery, on the right and left deep plantar arteries, proximal to the abdominal compliance chamber, and on the right and left distal limb before the limb circuits entering the reservoirs (capillary beds) (Figure 3). The tibioperoneal trunk, posterior tibial arteries, peroneal arteries, deep plantar arteries, and profunda arteries were incorporated into our distal limb design to account for simplified collateral flow.

Arterial limb components of the dynamic mock loop and location hemodynamic instrumentation used and distal perfusion catheter (DPC) placement. L, Left; R, right; LSFA, left superficial femoral artery; RSFA, right superficial femoral artery.

The dynamic loop was primed with 6.0 L of 3.4cP glycerol-saline solution at 37 °C. Viscosity was validated using Cannon-Fenske calibrated viscometers (ACE Glass) following ASTM Standard D445-21E2. Reservoirs simulating each limb capillary bed were kept in a temperature-controlled water bath to maintain the circuit at 37 °C. The dynamic loop was tuned to heart failure conditions with a mean aortic flow of 2.5 ± 0.5 L/min, mean aortic pressure of 50 ± 5 mm Hg, central venous pressure of 20 ± 5 mm Hg, heart rate of 80 bpm, common femoral artery flow of 350 to 500 mL/min, superficial femoral artery flow of 100 to 250 mL/min, and dorsalis pedis artery flow of 5 to 30 mL/min.20,21

Experiments were performed with a DPC (6-Fr pediatric arterial cannula, 77106; Medtronic) placed (1) in the traditional position (anterograde in the RSFA) and (2) retrograde in the right dorsalis pedis artery. A 6-Fr pediatric arterial cannula was selected as the DPC on the basis of our previous study that demonstrated flow benefits of pediatric arterial cannulae over introducer sheaths on distal limb hemodynamics in VA-ECMO.16 Hemodynamic data were collected at heart failure baseline (pump off and ECMO circuit clamped) and at pump speeds of 0, 1000-, 2000-, and 3000-rpm increments and decrements (resulting in 2 sets of data). Hemodynamic waveform data were analog-to-digitally converted and recorded at a 200/s sampling rate (and low-pass filtered at 40 Hz) using a PowerLab 16/35 acquisition system with bridge amps (ADInstruments) and recorded using LabChart, version, 8.1.30 (ADInstruments). Data were plotted using Prism, version 10.5.0 (GraphPad; GraphPad). Each limb's total flow was calculated as the profunda flow plus the distal dorsalis/superficial femoral artery (SFA) flow.

Results

Using a dynamic mock flow loop model of adult femoral VA-ECMO (Figure E1), we tested the hemodynamic feasibility of retrograde dorsalis pedis artery perfusion as a distal alternative to traditional anterograde DPC placement. Systemic (aortic, caval, abdominal, and iliac) pressure and flow data were equivalent between DPC configurations (Figures 4 and 5). Distal limb hemodynamics were also equivalent between configurations. As shown in Figure 5, the retrograde dorsalis configuration resulted in slightly greater total right limb flow (the cannulated limb) than the traditional configuration (Figures 5 and 6) at 3000 rpm due to increased distal right dorsalis/SFA flow, whereas the traditional DPC configuration resulted in slightly greater right and left profunda artery flows. Mean DPC flow was nearly identical between the traditional and retrograde dorsalis DPC configurations across all pump speeds (361 mL/min vs 373 mL/min at 3000 rpm [3.46 L/min ECMO flow]). RSFA pulse pressure was slightly greater in the retrograde dorsalis DPC configuration (25 mm Hg) compared with the traditional configuration (23 mm Hg) at 3000 rpm, whereas pulse pressures remained nearly identical between configurations in the left superficial femoral artery (28 mm Hg), right dorsalis (16 mm Hg), and left dorsalis (9 mm Hg).

Flow probes were placed proximal and distal to the retrograde DPC's cannulation site and so in the traditional DPC configuration, flow through the proximal and distal right dorsalis pedis artery is equivalent (because both flow probes are on the same vessel and downstream of the traditional DPC) (Figure 7). These same flow probes show that the retrograde dorsalis artery's DPC flow bifurcated retrograde and anterograde and resulted in greater distal dorsalis flow (Figure 7) when combined with the native anterograde flow than the traditional DPC configuration (which had the right posterior tibial and right peroneal arteries as other flow path options instead of the right dorsalis pedis artery; see Figure 3).

Discussion

Femoral VA-ECMO is an important therapy for supporting patients with cardiogenic shock, but 10% to 70% experience significant vascular complications, including compartment syndrome, infection, bleeding, embolism, thrombosis, and distal-limb ischemia.1,2,4,9,10,22 Limb ischemia may result from the arterial cannula intravascularly obstructing distal femoral artery flow. To augment blood flow to this region, the ELSO recommends6 and many centers integrate a DPC into the ECMO circuit (T'd off the arterial cannula's Luer port) to deliver anterograde flow into the ipsilateral arterial system distal to the arterial cannula.2,14,16

The ipsilateral SFA is frequently used for DPC placement; however, in patients undergoing VA-ECMO in whom proximal femoral artery access is challenging (eg, anatomical variation, obesity, atherosclerotic lesions) or no longer viable (eg, bleeding, infection, failed percutaneous cannulation, hematoma from previous cannulation10,23,24) an alternative and more distal strategy may be required. One strategy is to place the DPC in the dorsalis pedis artery and deliver retrograde perfusion.3,11,12 Kimura and colleagues12 describe a patient with femoral VA-ECMO and an occlusive arterial cannula and in whom DPC placement of the superficial femoral artery was abandoned because of suspected atherosclerotic stenosis. This patient later developed limb ischemia, at which point retrograde DPC of the dorsalis pedis artery was used as a salvage procedure. Limb blood flow (Doppler) and skin temperature immediately improved and the patient was extubated and recovered without limb complications. Göbölös and colleagues11 describe using retrograde dorsalis pedis artery perfusion in 2 patients who were undergoing VA-ECMO as salvage procedures after distal-limb ischemia. The first patient (autoimmune myocarditis with severe cardiogenic shock) had constricted peripheral vasculature and proximal peripheral vascular disease that prevented traditional DPC placement. The second patient (viral pneumonia resulting in severe acute respiratory distress syndrome) had severe obesity (body mass index 88 kg/m2), which made traditional DPC placement challenging. Both patients showed improved limb perfusion/saturation and had no further complications. The dorsalis pedis artery is a potentially advantageous alternative to femoral placement, particularly for inexperienced clinicians and/or centers, because it can be easily cannulated as the result of its superficial course, the incidence of complications is low, the bones of the foot provide a firm foundation for applying pressure at time of decannulation, and its location on the body is less intrusive for patient management.25 In the event the dorsalis undergoes vasospasm or damage during cannulation, collateral flow from the plantar arteries may provide adequate support.26 Other complications may include those associated with any cannulation procedure, including infection, thrombosis, hematoma, and cannula dislodgement.26

Although these case reports suggest that retrograde DPC perfusion of the dorsalis pedis artery may be an effective alternative to traditional DPC placement, the hemodynamics of this approach had not been characterized experimentally to date. Using a dynamic mock flow loop of adult femoral VA-ECMO, we showed that retrograde DPC of the dorsalis pedis artery provided distal limb hemodynamics (pressures, flows) comparable with traditional DPC placement. DPC flow, the right-left limb flow balance ratio, and distal limb pulsatility were comparable with both approaches while maintaining equivalent ECMO flows and systemic hemodynamics (aortic, caval). In our study, we used a 6-Fr pediatric arterial cannula for both the traditional DPC and retrograde dorsalis DPC placement. Traditionally, small-caliber introducer sheaths are used as DPCs; however, we recently showed that using a pediatric arterial cannula as a DPC provides greater flow delivery and results in more favorable distal limb hemodynamics.16

There are several potential limitations to our study. Our dynamic mock loop is a lumped parameter model that simulates the vasculature of the average patient with heart failure (size, compliance, etc) and thus is not meant to be an exact replica of the distal-limb arteries and veins. We used a simplified distal limb vasculature for this early feasibility study and are unable to account for all collateral vessels that are present in animals and humans. It is possible that dorsalis pedis artery cannulation might result in vasospasm clinically, and we did not assess this in our present study. Our mock loop is also unable to replicate vascular tone, heterogenous anatomy, or complications such as obesity, atherosclerotic plaques, and/or the presence of significant peripheral artery disease. We used a glycerol-saline solution in our mock loop and are unable to assess the potential for blood trauma, thrombus, or hematoma and acknowledge that flow may be affected by blood rheology. Our mock loop provides hemodynamic data only and is unable to simulate oxygenator settings, respiratory physiology, and the impact on heart failure pathophysiology.

In summary, traditional anterograde femoral artery DPC placement may not be feasible in patients with hematoma, obesity, infection, severe peripheral vasculopathy, and/or anatomical variation. Retrograde DPC of the dorsalis pedis artery has been described with successful limb salvage in a few clinical case reports, but this is the first experimental study designed to characterize the hemodynamics of this approach. Our data suggest that retrograde DPC placement in the dorsalis pedis artery may be a viable hemodynamic alternative to traditional DPC placement in femoral VA-ECMO, as evidenced by comparable DPC flows and distal limb hemodynamics. Future consideration of DPC placement in the dorsalis pedis artery warrants further clinical investigations and consideration as a hemodynamically effective alternative strategy with the potential for valuable use in patients who undergo VA-ECMO.
