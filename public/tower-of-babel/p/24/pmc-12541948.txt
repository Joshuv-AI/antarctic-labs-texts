Comparing all-suture repair versus tension band wiring fixation in patellar fractures
Authors: Teddy Cheong, Hon Keng Pui Bryan, Lee Bing Howe, Wong Merng Koon, Eric Wei Liang Cher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionImplant-related issues necessitating removal are well reported in tension band wiring (TBW) of patellar fractures. Suture materials can minimize complications associated with TBW fixation but there is a paucity of literature on all-suture fixation outcomes. This study aims to compare outcomes between all-suture versus TBW fixation of patellar fractures.MethodsThis retrospective, single-centre study included 79 patients with patellar fractures that underwent fixation with either all-suture (n = 49) or TBW (n = 30) fixation from 2020 to 2023, regardless of demographic or fracture characteristics. Outcomes reported include intra-operative duration, length of stay, bony union rate and time, post-operative knee range of motion, complications, and re-operation rate.ResultsMean age in years was 63.5 ± 11.9 and 65.2 ± 12.9 in the all-suture and TBW groups respectively. Bony union time in months was comparable between the all-suture group (4 ± 1.58) and TBW group (4.37 ± 1.81) (p = 0.34). Union rate was comparable between all-suture (98%) and TBW groups (100%). Complication rate was lower in the all-suture group (4.1%) when compared to the TBW group (13.3%) (p = 0.19). 10% of TBW group patients underwent re-surgery due to implant-related complications compared to 0 cases of re-surgery in the all-suture group (p = 0.05). More patients in the TBW group (23.3%) experienced flexion contracture as compared to the all-suture group (2%) (p = 0.004), and with a greater degree of contracture of 2.1° ± 4.6 vs. 0.2 ± 1.4 (p = 0.002). The all-suture group had a higher prevalence of post-operative patellar baja (67.3%) (p = 0.002). However, this was found to have no significant impact on the presence of knee contracture after subgroup analysis (p = 1.00). No significant differences were seen in operative duration, length of stay and post-operative knee flexion between the two groups (p > 0.05).ConclusionAll-suture fixation of patellar fractures is a reliable surgical method with a high union rate, good union time and post-operative range of motion, while avoiding implant-related complications that may result in re-operation. It is a safe alternative surgical technique in fixation of patellar fractures.Keywords: Orthopaedic trauma, Patellar fractures, Suture fixation, Tension band

Introduction

The patellar plays a vital role in the extensor mechanism of the knee and any disruptions to its integrity can result in a significant functional disability. Intra-articular incongruity of more than 2 mm and/or the loss of extensor mechanism are indications for surgical fixation [1]. Till date, one of the most performed operations is the Arbeitsgemeinschaft fürOsteosynthesefragen (AO)-modified “11 − 8” tension band wiring (TBW) construct which uses Kirschner wires (K-wire) to reduce and ensure adequate compression at the fracture site [2]. In some cases, cerclage wiring may be placed circumferentially along the borders of the patellar. While TBW has been proven to achieve good bony union [2], implant-related issues such as implant prominence necessitating surgical removal are well reported in this fixation technique [3, 4]. Alternative methods involving non-metallic implants have been described over the years ranging from hybrid constructs to solely non-metallic fixations involving only sutures [5–7]. Current literature on all-suture fixation has shown good clinical outcomes while avoiding complications, including implant prominence and loosening [5–9]. This in turn results in a lower rate of re-operation due to implant complications which could lead to less economic burden for the patient and healthcare provider as previous literature has shown that a major contributor to cost in the treatment of patellar fractures was reoperation costs [4, 10]. Biomechanically, suture fixation has similar or higher failure load than stainless steel wire [11, 12]. Despite an increasing number of studies on this topic, the selection of all-suture fixation over TBW in the surgical treatment of patellar fractures is still a topic of controversy. All-suture fixation does not yet appear to be considered as the primary choice for fixation despite the favourable literature so far. The aim of this study is to report and compare the outcomes between all-suture versus metallic TBW fixation of surgically treated patellar fractures at our institution. Our hypothesis is that all-suture fixation has comparable outcomes with TBW fixation in the treatment of patellar fractures.

Methods

This was a retrospective, single-centre study of all patellar fractures that underwent surgical fixation with either TBW or all-suture fixation at our institution between 2020 and 2023 This study was approved by the Centralized Institutional Review Board of SingHealth (CRIB: 2024/3056). Clinical records of the patients were accessed to obtain peri-operative data relevant to the study. The inclusion criteria were 1) All isolated fractures of the patellar treated with 11-8-0 TBW fixation or all-suture constructs were included, regardless of the characteristics of the fracture, age or sex 2) Minimum of 3 months follow-up. Exclusion criteria were: 1) Patellar fractures treated with other methods such as hybrid constructs, plates or compression screws 2) History of prior surgery on the operated knee 3) Revision cases 4) Open fractures 5) Pathological fractures 6) Concomitant, additional knee fractures. Pre-operative and post-operative radiographs were taken for all cases. Fracture pattern was characterised by the location of the fracture. Inferior pole fractures were defined as fractures occurring in the distal 1/4 of the patellar, which is made up of mainly cancellous bone that has no articular surface coverage [13, 14].

Surgical technique

Patients were placed in supine position on a radiolucent table with a tourniquet applied on the operated leg. A longitudinal midline skin incision was made, followed by meticulous dissection down to the fracture site. Fracture hematoma was evacuated and the knee joint was washed thoroughly. The patellar was reduced and held with a pointed reduction clamp before fixation. Intra-operative image intensifier was used to assess the adequacy of fracture reduction.

All-suture fixation technique

High-strength non-absorbable sutures such as Dynacord® (DePuy Synthes, Raynham, MA, USA), Ultrabraid® (Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN, USA), FibreTape® (Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA), FibreWire® (Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA), or No. 5 Ethibond® (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA) were used in the fixation either individually or as a combination. As this study is a retrospective study on the all-suture technique, the choice of suture used was surgeon-dependent at the time of operation. The technique used in the all-suture fixation group was described in paper at our institution by Lee et al. [6]. In short, firstly a Krachow locking stitch method was used on the patellar tendon distal to its inferior pole. Subsequently, three trans-osseous tunnels were created using 2.7 mm beath pins, and two sutures were passed through each tunnel in the “crossed” fashion. At the proximal pole of the patellar where the suture exits, the sutures were “uncrossed” and tunnelled under the quadriceps tendon. It is important for the sutures to be under the quadriceps tendon, and as close to the proximal pole as possible to ensure that the knots were down to bone when tightened. The uncrossed sutures were then tied “like-to-like”. The side sutures (Pair 1 and Pair 3 in Fig. 1e) may continue as a figure-of-eight construct anteriorly over the patellar, with either pair tunnelled under the patellar tendon at the distal pole, and tightened to each other (Fig. 1).

Tension band wire fixation technique

Figure-of-8 tension band wiring technique described by AO is widely used in patellar fracture fixation. At our centre, TBW fixation follows the “11-8-0” configuration. After adequate reduction, two Kirschner wires were inserted longitudinally through the patellar, and an 18-gauge stainless steel wire was inserted in a figure-of-eight pattern over the patellar, thus forming the “11 − 8” construct [2]. Cerclage wiring was placed circumferentially along the borders of the patellar to form the “11-8-0” configuration (Fig. 3).

Regardless of fixation method, the articular surface was evaluated for congruency and reduction under intra-operative image intensifier. Furthermore, surfaces were manually palpated using a freer to ensure minimal step deformity. The construct was tested for stability through passive knee ranging. Torn extensor retinaculum was repaired using non absorbable sutures for added stability. Post-operative rehabilitation

Post-operative rehabilitation protocol was similar in both groups of patients. The operated knee was protected in a knee brace and kept in 20 degrees flexion for approximately four weeks to allow ease of single leg ambulation with crutches while they were kept on a non-weightbearing (WB) status. Progressive WB and increase in range of motion (ROM) was initiated at around 4 weeks after surgery. This was followed by progressive increase in ROM under the supervision of a trained physiotherapist. Patients were reviewed at the outpatient clinic at two weeks, six weeks, and four months. Subsequently, further follow up was arranged at the discretion of the surgeon.

Outcomes measured

Clinical and radiological outcomes were evaluated in this study. Clinical outcomes assessed include complications, rate of re-operation, duration of operation, length of hospital stay, and post-operative knee ROM. ROM was measured at our institution with the use of a goniometer. Passive ROM was not reported on our institution’s existing documentation. As this is a retrospective study on existing data, only active ROM was available for this analysis. Contracture was defined as the inability to achieve full extension of the knee. Complications include implant breakage, fracture, dislocations, implant infection, non-union, implant irritation/prominence causing discomfort and soft tissue infection. Radiologically, bony union rate and union time as well as the patellar index were assessed. Radiographic union was defined as the presence of cortical and trabecular bridging and disappearance of the fracture line on the anterior-posterior and lateral radiographs of the knee. Patellar index was calculated via the Insall-Salvati ratio of the most recent lateral knee radiographs, with a value of < 0.8 taken as patellar baja and > 1.2 taken as patellar alta.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables are expressed as absolute values and percentages. Parametric data was analysed using the Unpaired Student T test, while non-parametric data was analysed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test (Mann–Whitney U test). Categorical variables were assessed using the Pearson χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate. All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS version 25 statistical software. A P value ≤ 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

A total of 88 patients underwent surgical fixation of patellar fracture with either all-suture or TBW fixation between 2020 and 2023. Nine patients were lost to follow-up. This study reports on 79 patients, consisting of 49 cases of all-suture fixations and 30 cases of TBW fixations (Fig. 4). The characteristics between both groups including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), history of smoking, fracture type and follow up duration were statistically insignificant (p > 0.05). Mean age in years was 63.5 ± 11.9 and 65.2 ± 12.9 in the all-suture and TBW fixation groups respectively. The all-suture group had a mean follow-up of 8.4 ± 7.6 months, while the TBW group had a mean follow up of 9.3 ± 8.8 months (Table 1).

The clinical and radiological outcomes are reported in Table 2. The mean operative time was longer in the all-suture group but was statistically insignificant (p = 0.30). The length of stay was slightly longer in the all-suture group but failed to show a statistical significance when compared to the TBW group (p = 0.67). Post-operative knee flexion was comparable between the two groups, however more patients in the TBW group experienced post-operative knee flexion contracture when compared to the all-suture group (p = 0.004). The severity of contracture was also more in the TBW group at 2.1° ± 4.6 as opposed to 0.2° ± 1.4 in the all-suture group (p = 0.002). Bony union rate was high in both groups and the time to bony union time was slightly shorter in the all-suture group at 4 ± 1.58 months, although this was not statistically significant (p = 0.34). Regarding complications, the all-suture group had two complications (4.1%), compared to four complications (13.3%) in the TBW group (p = 0.19). Of these complications, re-surgery rate in the TBW group was significantly higher at 10% (n = 3) as compared to none in the all-suture group (p = 0.05). Complications in the TBW group consisted of three implant irritations and one implant infection. Three of the four complications underwent re-surgery and recovered well after. Within the all-suture group, there was one case of infected hematoma that resolved with antibiotics and one case of fracture non-union which did not undergo revision surgery as the patient was functionally and clinically well (Table 2). The patellar index was higher in the TBW group at 0.92 ± 0.16 as opposed to 0.77 ± 0.14 in the all-suture group (p = 0.00). The all-suture group had a higher prevalence of post-operative patellar baja (67.3%) as compared to the TBW group (30%) (p = 0.002) Only one case of patellar alta was present in the population and it occurred after TBW fixation (Table 2).

Discussion

Surgical treatment of patellar fractures commonly involves the use of the AO technique to create a figure-of-8 metallic tension band wiring construct which offers compression at the fracture site. While this has been proven to have good bony union [2], implant-related issues such as implant prominence necessitating surgical removal of implants are a well reported complication of this fixation method [3, 4]. In a study by Hoshino et al. which involved 315 patellar fractures that underwent TBW fixation, 37% of patients underwent implant removal [15]. In 2012, Lebrun et al. reported that 80% (n = 12) of their patients who were treated with TBW underwent implant removal [16]. To avoid these complications, alternative methods with non-metallic implants such as sutures have been developed and shown good outcomes [5, 9, 17–19] The main findings of this paper are that all-suture fixation can produce good clinical outcomes that is non-inferior to TBW fixation while avoiding implant-related complications that result in re-operation.

Non-union or delayed union has been seen in previous reports on TBW fixation of patellar fractures [20, 21]. However, in this study the bony union was high in both all-suture and TBW fixation group. The all-suture group had only one case of non-union, while the TBW group had no patients with non-union. This study’s findings concur with previous literature that compared metallic TBW fixation against all-suture fixation methods. In a comparative study by Shea et al., cases that underwent all-suture fixation had a 7.7% non-union rate while metallic TBW fixation cases had a rate of 22.8% non-union [7]. In 2021, Lee et al. reported comparable bony union rates between all-suture and metallic TBW fixation [22]. Thus, this study’s findings also suggest that all-suture fixation is not inferior to metallic TBW fixation with regards to patellar fracture healing.

It is not unexpected that bony union rates are high with the all-suture fixation technique. Previous biomechanical studies have demonstrated that suture materials have similar or higher failure load when compared to stainless steel wires [11, 23]. In this study, there were several suture materials utilised such as Dynacord®, FibreWire®, No. 5 Ethibond®, and Ultrabraid®. Wright et al. compared stainless steel to FiberWire® tension band construct in patellar fractures and tested to failure by three-point bending and found that FiberWire® had a significantly higher failure load than stainless steel. Furthermore, unlike stainless steel, FiberWire® maintained its initial stiffness until failure [11]. Patel et al. studied No. 5 Ethibond® sutures in patellar fractures and found that the quality of fixation was comparable with stainless steel wires and provided sufficient stability to withhold loads encountered during post-operative rehabilitation [23]. With regards to Dynacord®, there has been no biomechanical studies on this suture in patellar fractures, although it has been studied in different settings. In a study by Owens et al., Dynacord® and FiberWire® were tested and compared in infraspinatus tendons repair, and it was found that Dynacord® had less tendon cut-through and failure. Perhaps this was due to its structure and biomechanical properties [24]. There is limited literature on comparisons between suture types. Wust et al. conducted a mechanical comparison and showed that the difference in strength between Ultrabraid and FiberWire sutures was not significantly different [25]. However, there appears to be no study that has compared the types of sutures and their tensile strength in patellar fracture fixation. It is evident from our results that regardless of the type that is used, sutures for fixation in patellar fractures are a viable substitute for metal implants as there were no complications of fixation failure in the all-suture group. Moving forward, a biomechanical study comparing these suture materials in patellar fractures specifically would provide valuable insight into which suture is superior in this setting.

Time to union was slightly shorter in the all-suture group as compared to the metallic TBW group in this study, although this difference was not statistically significant. This is in keeping with existing literature which reported non-inferior union times for suture fixations [22, 26]. Ho et al., reported a faster union time in their all-suture fixation group compared to metallic TBW group. However, the difference was statistically insignificant [26]. In a study by Lee et al., the difference in union time between suture and metallic TBW fixation groups was insignificant as well [22]. In a meta-analysis by Huang et al. comparing metallic (mostly TBW fixation) against various non-metallic fixation methods, there was a statistically significant decrease in time to union in the non-metal group compared to the metal group across 11 studies [27]. Previous studies reported bony union times ranging from 6 to 11 weeks in suture fixation methods [6, 8, 18, 22]. The time to union is slightly longer in this study’s groups compared to previous literature. This could be due to this study’s outpatient follow up intervals where patients were seen at two weeks to ensure adequate wound healing, at six weeks to assess progress and up to four months to evaluate bony union and functional recovery. Subsequently, further follow up was arranged at the discretion of the surgeon. As such, this could have affected the mean time to union, which was only confirmed on radiographs taken at four months post operatively.

Complications were reported as well. Only two patients (4.1%) suffered a complication in the all-suture fixation group, one of which was an infected hematoma which resolved with a prolonged course of oral antibiotics and did not require hospitalisation or surgery. The second complication was a patient who developed non-union with a persistent fracture gap but remained well clinically with good range of motion and function. Thus, no re-operation was required. There were no cases of implant-prominence resulting in irritation that required removal. In contrast, the TBW group had four patients (13.3%) who suffered complications. This consisted of three implant irritations and one implant infection, of which only one case of implant irritation did not undergo re-operation due to a gout flare prior to the second surgery which resulted in the case being cancelled. This puts the re-operation rate at 10% in the metallic TBW group which is statistically significant (p = 0.05) when compared to the all-suture group. This study shows that possibly all-suture fixation can avoid complications that can arise from metallic TBW implants and the need for a second operation. Prior systematic reviews on various non-metallic fixation methods have yielded similar findings. In a meta-analysis by Huang et al. comparing non-metal against metal fixations, there was a statistically significant decrease in postoperative complications in the non-metallic group [27]. In a systematic review by Camarda et al. that studied 123 patients across nine studies, non-metallic fixation had a success rate of 90% and a low rate of implant removal (3.2%) [8]. This was much lower as compared to metallic TBW fixation which has a higher rate of implant removal, ranging from 10 to 52% in existing literature [2, 15, 16, 28]. It is worth noting that both the systematic reviews by Camarda et al. and Huang et al. included various methods of non-metallic fixations such as bioabsorbable screw cases [8, 27]. Future systematic reviews focussing solely on all-suture fixation would be beneficial to the overall literature.

Re-operations and re-admissions would undoubtedly result in an increased cost of treatment. Reul et al. reported in 2017 that extensive tension-band constructs were identified as the main determinants of poor outcome and increased economic burden due to higher reinterventions rates [4]. In another retrospective study by Vanderkarr et al. that involved 4365 patients, they identified that the main driver of cost for patellar fractures that underwent treatment was the re-operation cost [10]. It could be said that since all-suture fixation results in a lower re-operation rate, overall cost for the patient and hospital will be lower. However, there has been no study that has formally done a cost-analysis. Moving forward, analysing and comparing the socio-economic burden of suture fixation vs. metallic TBW fixation would be an interesting topic as there is still a paucity of literature on this area.

Functional knee ROM is an important clinical outcome after patellar fracture fixation. In this study, the all-suture group had a greater degree of post-operative knee flexion (119° ± 20.7) compared to the metallic TBW fixation group (114.8° ± 22.8), but this was statistically insignificant. More patients in the TBW group (n = 7, 23.3%) experienced flexion contracture as compared to the all-suture group (n = 1, 2%). This was found to be statistically significant in this study (p = 0.004). The severity of contracture was also higher in the TBW group at 2.1° ± 4.6 as compared to 0.2° ± 1.4 in the all-suture group which was significant (p = 0.002). The results suggests that while flexion is comparable between the two groups, metallic TBW fixation patients experience post-operative knee contracture more frequently and to a greater degree as compared to all-suture fixation. Although there is statistical significance to this point, there were no patients who had subjective complaints about their knee range of motion and function. Thus, it is unlikely there is any clinical significance to this finding in this population. Existing literature comparing knee ROM between suture and TBW fixation is limited. A comparative study between suture vs. TBW done previously by Lee et al. reported on post-operative flexion and contracture. Their study reported that patients who underwent suture fixation had a statistically significant greater degree of knee flexion compared to patients who received TBW fixation at the three months post-operation mark but no significant difference between groups with regards to contracture [22]. As there is a paucity of literature in this area, more research would be needed to make a consensus on whether all-suture fixation offers better post-operative knee range of motion.

The post-operative patellar index was analysed as well, revealing a significantly lower mean patellar index (p = 0.00) and higher number of cases with patellar baja (p = 0.002) following all-suture fixation as compared to TBW fixation. However, the development of post-operative knee contracture was not significantly affected by the patellar index (p = 0.13) or the presence of patellar baja (p = 1.00). This indicates that although there was a difference radiologically, this had no clinical significance in the patient’s knee function. Our findings concur with a study done by Shea et al. that compared suture vs. hybrid vs. metal fixation. They found a significant difference in the prevalence of patellar baja and patellar index between three fixation methods but found no significant difference amongst the groups in functional outcomes such as knee flexion and presence of extension lag [7].

Length of stay was slightly longer in the all-suture group, but the difference between the two groups was again statistically insignificant. The mean operative time was slightly longer in the all-suture group compared to TBW fixation group, but this difference was small and statistically insignificant. This concurs with the existing literature on suture vs. metallic TBW fixation operative durations [22, 26].

A valuable point to this study is the population size of the all-suture fixation group (n = 49), which represents one of the largest cohort of patients from a single institution when compared to previous literature. Previous studies on fixation methods involving sutures, whether all-suture or hybrid techniques, had relatively small numbers ranging from 13 to 30 patients [5, 7, 22, 26]. Thus, this study has a significant contribution to the limited existing literature on all-suture fixation of patellar fractures. In addition, the results of this study can be applied broadly due to the inclusion of all cases regardless of fracture location and the subgroup analysis based on fracture location yielding no significant effect on outcomes in both fixation methods.

There are several limitations to this study. First, this paper compared all-suture fixation with only metallic TBW fixation. Newer metallic fixation techniques such as the usage of cannulated screws or plating were not included. Second, this study lacks reporting on functional outcome scores which may provide valuable information on the recovery and patient satisfaction after different fixation methods. Third, several types of non-absorbable sutures were used in the all-suture group. There was no analysis between the types of sutures used due to the retrospective nature of this study as the choice of suture used was dependent on the surgeon at the time and was not always reported. However, the authors do not anticipate any differences with regards to the types of sutures used since there were no reported cases of fixation failure due to suture breakage. In addition, as this is a retrospective study, exact details pertaining to how the all-suture technique was performed as originally described by Lee et al. may be unclear, and number of cases with the anterior figure of eight suture may not always be documented in the operative notes. We recognise that there may be a handful of cases where the sutures were tied only at the proximal patellar pole [6]. However, this paper aims to focus on the comparison between all-suture vs. wiring, rather than the configuration of the all-suture construct.

In conclusion, all-suture fixation of patellar fractures is a reliable surgical method with a good union rate, union time and post-operative range of motion, while avoiding implant-related complications such as prominence and re-operation. It is safe and a good alternative surgical technique in fixation of patellar fractures.
