Dual regulation of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells modulates cytotoxic and exhaustion-associated programs during prostate cancer progression
Authors: Zhang Xingming, Jianhua Wang, Mierxiati Abudurexiti, Leilei Du, Wei Zhang, Xudong Ni, Midie Xu, Xiaoyan Zhang, Min Zhang, Zhu Yao, Xiaojie Bian, Shengming Jin, Wenfeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProstate cancer (PCa) is widely recognized as an immunologically “cold” tumor, characterized by a paucity of effector T cells and a limited response to immune checkpoint blockade therapy. Although the chemokine receptor CXCR6 has been identified as a marker of highly cytotoxic CD8+ T cells in other malignancies, its identity, regulatory mechanisms, and clinical significance in PCa remain poorly understood.MethodsWe integrated newly generated and publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing data encompassing 90 651 cells from nine patients. Multicolor immunofluorescence and flow cytometry were performed on human PCa tissue specimens stratified by Gleason Score (GS). In parallel, syngeneic mouse models, bone marrow chimeras, and in vitro T-cell functional assays were employed to investigate the role, recruitment, and transcriptional regulation of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells. Mechanistic investigations included bulk RNA-seq, chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative real-time PCR, pharmacologic modulation of FOXO1, and detailed analysis of the IL-10–STAT3 signaling pathway.ResultsThe CXCR6+CD8+ T cells represent a transcriptionally distinct subset with high expression of cytotoxic markers (GZMA, GZMB, and PRF1), and their frequency declines significantly with increasing GS. Intratumoral maintenance of these cells is sustained by CXCL16 secreted by IL1B+ M1-like macrophages; the loss of this macrophage population in advanced tumors leads to depletion of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells. Genetic ablation of CXCR6 or depletion of CD8+ T cells accelerates tumor growth, demonstrating the essential role of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells in antitumor immunity. At the molecular level, the FOXO1–KLF2 axis transcriptionally represses CXCR6 expression, and IL-10-mediated activation of STAT3 upregulates FOXO1 and KLF2, thereby suppressing CXCR6 expression and impairing cytotoxic function. Pharmacologic inhibition of FOXO1 enhances the expansion of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells and acts synergistically with anti-PD-1 therapy to inhibit tumor progression.ConclusionsThe CXCR6+CD8+ T cells are critical yet progressively diminished effectors in PCa. Their persistence within the tumor microenvironment depends on CXCL16 derived from M1-like macrophages and is counteracted by IL-10-driven FOXO1–KLF2 signaling. Targeting this regulatory axis—through inhibition of FOXO1 or IL-10, for example—represents a rational therapeutic strategy to restore CXCR6+CD8+ T-cell-mediated immunity and enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in PCa.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Prostate cancer (PCa) is widely regarded as an immunologically “cold” malignancy, as evidenced by the limited efficacy of current immunotherapies. This phenotype stems not only from a low tumor mutational burden with sparse neoantigen landscape, but also from a profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). The chemokine receptor CXCR6 plays a pivotal role in T cell biology, being preferentially expressed on specific T-cell subsets and critically governing tissue inflammation and immune-mediated damage, as demonstrated in models of non-alcoholic steatohepatitis and severe novel coronavirus (COVID-19) pneumonia. Moreover, CXCR6-expressing T cells have been shown to be essential for sustaining durable anti-tumor immunity in malignancies such as melanoma and colorectal cancer, underscoring their broad functional significance in antitumor responses.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Through single-cell RNA sequencing of human PCa tissues, we identified a previously unappreciated subset of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells whose frequency is inversely correlated with Gleason Score, implicating their role in immune surveillance. We demonstrated that the recruitment of these cells is mediated by CXCL16 secreted by M1-like macrophages, whose loss during disease progression constitutes a key mechanism of immune exclusion. Concurrently, we delineated an intrinsic exhaustion pathway: IL-10, predominantly from CD163+ macrophages in the TME, engages the IL-10R on CD8+ T cells to activate a FOXO1–KLF2 transcriptional axis, which suppresses CXCR6 expression. Therapeutically, FOXO1 inhibition synergized with anti-PD-1 treatment to reinvigorate antitumor immunity in vivo. Collectively, this study reveals novel mechanisms underlying the coordinated regulation of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells in PCa.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

This study provides the first evidence of a clinically significant association between the frequency of intratumoral CXCR6+CD8+ T cells and PCa progression. Consequently, elucidating the mechanisms responsible for the reduction or depletion of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells in high-risk PCa may uncover novel immunotherapeutic opportunities. Furthermore, our findings provide a mechanistic rationale for targeting IL-10 signaling in the treatment of PCa and support the potential of IL-10 as both a prognostic biomarker and a therapeutic target.

Background

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second most prevalent malignancy and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related deaths in men worldwide.1 In contrast to tumors such as melanoma or non-small cell lung carcinoma, which often respond favorably to immunotherapy, PCa is widely recognized as an immunologically “cold” tumor.2 3 This phenotype is driven not only by a relatively low tumor mutation burden and limited neoantigen availability for T cell recognition,46 but also by a profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) characterized by the accumulation of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs).710 These components collectively establish a hostile immune landscape that impedes cytotoxic T lymphocyte cell infiltration and function, contributing to the suboptimal efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade in PCa. Thus, a deeper understanding of the immune microenvironment, particularly the factors governing T cell activity and persistence, is urgently needed to unlock the therapeutic potential of immunotherapy in this disease.

C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 6 (CXCR6), previously known as “Bonzo” and “STRL33”,11 is encoded by the CXCR6 gene, located on chromosome 3p21.31 and belongs to the CXC chemokine receptor family of G protein-coupled receptors.12 13 CXCR6 is preferentially expressed on specific T cell populations and plays a pivotal role in regulating tissue inflammation and immune-mediated damage. For instance, in murine models of non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), hepatic CXCR6+CD8+ T cells exhibit a hybrid phenotype—co-expressing effector molecules (eg, Granzyme B and IFN-γ), exhaustion markers (eg, PD-1), and apoptotic signatures—and drive hepatocyte killing in an MHC class I-independent manner.14 Similarly, aberrant accumulation and activation of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells in the lungs have been implicated in the immunopathogenesis of severe COVID-19.15

Beyond inflammation, CXCR6 also marks a population of tissue-resident memory T (TRM) cells involved in long-term tumor surveillance. In melanoma, TRM-like CXCR6+CD8+ T cells accumulate in both primary and vitiligo-associated skin lesions and express high levels of CD69, RGS1, NR4A1, and other TRM-associated markers. These cells are crucial for sustaining durable anti-tumor immunity.16 Moreover, functional studies have demonstrated that CXCR6 expression on effector-like cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) is required for their survival, local expansion, and tumor control within the TME.17 18 CXCR6+CD8+ T cells also potentiate the efficacy of PD-1 blockade, suggesting their importance as a therapeutic ally in immunotherapy-resistant tumors.18 These findings underscore the pivotal role of CXCR6 in modulating the functional capacity of CD8+ T cells and their contribution to antitumor immunity.

Despite its known roles in immune-mediated diseases and other cancers, the abundance, function, and regulatory mechanisms of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells in the PCa microenvironment remain poorly understood. In this study, we employed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to characterize the immune landscape of human PCa tissues and identified a unique subset of intratumoral CXCR6 CD8+ T cells. Notably, the frequency of these cells was inversely correlated with the histological grade of PCa, suggesting a potential role in tumor immune surveillance.

Mechanistic investigations revealed both extrinsic and intrinsic drivers of CXCR6+CD8+ T cell depletion and dysfunction in high-grade PCa. Specifically, we identified that tumor-infiltrating M1-like macrophages secrete CXCL16, which engages CXCR6 to promote CD8+ T cell recruitment and effector function. This activation axis, however, is counteracted by immunosuppressive IL-10 signaling, which induces FOXO1 expression. FOXO1, in turn, upregulates KLF2 and represses CXCR6, forming an IL-10–FOXO1–KLF2–CXCR6 axis that impairs cytotoxic differentiation. Notably, transcriptional regulatory network inference using SCENIC19 identified KLF2 as a core transcription factor (TF) orchestrating CD8+ T cell fate transitions, further supporting its pivotal role in this suppressive cascade.

Pharmacologic inhibition of FOXO1 using AS1842856, in combination with anti-PD-1 therapy, resulted in marked tumor regression and significantly reduced tumor burden in murine models. These findings position FOXO1 as a critical intrinsic checkpoint limiting CXCR6+CD8+ T cell–mediated immunity and provide a mechanistic rationale for therapeutic strategies aimed at reinvigorating these cells within the immunosuppressive PCa microenvironment.

Collectively, our results uncover a dual-layered regulatory mechanism that governs the fate and function of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells in PCa and propose this population as a promising cellular target for enhancing responsiveness to immune checkpoint therapy.

Results

CXCR6+CD8+ T cells exhibit effector signatures and decline with tumor grade in PCa

To investigate the fate of T cells within the tumor immune microenvironment of PCa, we collected an additional patient sample at the castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) stage and integrated it with our previously published scRNA-seq dataset,20 resulting in a combined dataset comprising 90 651 cells from 9 patients.

Based on the expression of canonical marker genes, we annotated major cell types within the PCa microenvironment and quantified their absolute abundances. Consistent with prior observations, epithelial cells, T cells, and myeloid cells were identified as the predominant cellular populations (online supplemental figure S1A–C).

Given the central role of CD8+ T cells in anti-tumor immunity, we further extracted the T/NK cell compartment, with a focus on CD8+ T cells, for refined subclustering and annotation (figure 1A,B; online supplemental figure S1D,E). Using DotPlot and VlnPlot visualization combined with area under the curve (AUC) scoring, we defined five transcriptionally distinct subsets of CD8+ T cells: Memory-like (Mem-like), Effector-like (TEff-like), Quiescent-like, Transitional, and Early-activated (figure 1B–E). Notably, the TEff-like subset exhibited the highest cytotoxicity scores and expressed elevated levels of CXCR6, GZMA, NKG7, and PRF1 (figure 1C and E), supporting potent effector function.

In contrast, we identified a quiescent-like CD8+ T cell subset characterized by high expression of MT1E and MT1F, absence of cytotoxic gene expression, and consistently low exhaustion-associated scores across multiple scoring approaches (figure 1B, online supplemental figure S1F–J). Notably, a comparable metallothionein-expressing CD8+ T population has been described in the LCMV infection model,21 in which metallothionein 1 (MT1) genes ranked among the top markers of a quiescent-like T cell cluster.

To independently validate our annotation, we reanalyzed a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset.22 Consistent with our findings, CXCR6 exhibited high co-expression with GZMB in CD8+ T cells, supporting the cytotoxic phenotype of this subset (online supplemental figure S1K).

To assess the clinical relevance of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells in PCa progression, we performed multicolor immunofluorescence staining on PCa tissue samples stratified by Gleason Score (GS), a well-established prognostic indicator. We observed a distinct subset of CD8+ T cells co-expressing CXCR6 within tumor tissues, and the proportion of these cells was inversely correlated with GS (figure 1F). Quantitative analysis revealed a significant reduction of intratumoral CXCR6+CD8+ T cells in tumors with GS >7 compared with those with GS ≤7 and adjacent tissues (figure 1G). This trend was further confirmed by flow cytometric analysis, which showed a marked decline in the frequency of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells among CD45+ populations in high-grade PCa (figure 1H,I).

Collectively, through integrated single-cell and protein-level validation, we identified a transcriptionally and functionally distinct CXCR6+CD8+ T cell population with potent cytotoxic potential. Their selective depletion in high-grade tumors suggests a role in tumor immune surveillance and implicates CXCR6+CD8+ T cells as a potential target to restore anti-tumor immunity in PCa.

CXCR6 defines a highly cytotoxic CD8+ T-cell program

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed that CXCR6+CD8+ T cells exhibit a robust cytotoxic signature (figure 1C). Consistently, multicolor immunofluorescence confirmed the presence of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells in human PCa tissues, with frequent co-expression of granzyme B (GZMB) (figure 2A). Immunofluorescence staining showed a visible reduction in CXCR6+GZMB+ cell density with increasing GS (GS 3+3 ≫ GS 5+5).

Flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes revealed that, on brief ex vivo stimulation, CXCR6+CD8+ T cells produced significantly higher levels of GZMB (33.5% vs 7.1%, p<0.01) and coexpressed IFN-γ (38.7% vs 14.4%, p<0.01) compared with CXCR6⁻CD8+ T cells (figure 2B–D). These findings were recapitulated in murine spleens (online supplemental figure S2A–D), supporting CXCR6 as a conserved marker of pre-existing cytotoxic CD8+ effector T cells.

To investigate the induction and dynamics of CXCR6 expression, naïve CD8+ T cells isolated from the spleens of C57BL/6 mice were activated with anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies in the presence of IL-2. Following activation, these cells upregulated the canonical activation markers CD25 and CD44, accompanied by a progressive increase in surface CXCR6 expression (figure 2E–G). Flow cytometric time-course analysis revealed that CXCR6 expression remained low during the early phase of activation but increased substantially on prolonged stimulation (figure 2G,H). Notably, CXCR6+CD8+ T cells derived from these conditions exhibited enhanced effector function, producing markedly higher levels of GZMB, PRF1, and IFN-γ than their CXCR6⁻ counterparts (figure 2I–M).

To further dissect the molecular programs associated with the differentiation of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on in vitro-activated CD8+ T cells collected at day 2, day 4, and day 12. Differential gene expression analysis revealed that both day 2 and day 4 CD8+ T cells were characterized by pronounced effector signatures, including elevated expression of Ifng, Gzmb, Tnf, alongside metabolic regulators Hk2, Ldha, Pkm as well as cell cycle genes Cdk1, Cdk2, and Pcna, consistent with robust proliferative capacity and cytotoxic activity. Compared with day 2, day 12 CD8+ T cells exhibited upregulation of Klf2, Id2, and Runx2, whereas comparison to day 4 highlighted further enrichment of Foxo1, Lef1, and Tcf7 (online supplemental figure S2E–H), indicative of a shift toward a more stem-like or memory-like state. Notably, the expression of Prf1, Btg1, Btg2, and the tyrosine kinases Jak1 and Jak2 was also enriched at day 12, along with surface molecules Cxcr6, Cxcr3, and S1pr1 (online supplemental figure S2E–H). These findings, particularly the sustained activation of the Janus kinase pathway, suggest that the CXCR6-high-expressing subpopulation retains features of a stem-like memory compartment while preserving potent cytotoxic potential.

Together, these findings demonstrate that CXCR6 is a dynamically regulated and inducible marker of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, emerging under conditions of sustained antigenic stimulation and marking a functionally superior subset in both human tumors and murine models.

Intratumoral CXCR6+CD8+ T cells are essential for antitumor immunity

To investigate the immunoregulatory role of CXCR6 in the TME, we established a syngeneic PCa model by subcutaneously implanting TRAMP-C1 cells into Cxcr6–/– and wild-type (WT) C57BL/6J mice. CXCR6 deficiency markedly accelerated tumor progression, highlighting its functional significance in tumor control (figure 3A,B).

Comprehensive flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells in WT mice revealed that CXCR6+ cells comprised approximately 12.8%±2.1% of CD45+ leukocytes (figure 3C). Among these, CD8+ T cells represented the predominant subset (64.1%±7.4%), followed by CD4+ T cells (16.6%±3.7%), while macrophages (6.6%±1.4%) and dendritic cells (4.4%±1.4%) constituted minor populations (figure 3D,E). In contrast, Cxcr6–/– tumors exhibited a skewed immune composition, with CD4+ T cells as the dominant infiltrates, followed by CD8+ T cells, B cells, and macrophages (figure 3F–H), suggesting impaired recruitment and/or retention of CD8+ T cells.

To determine whether CXCR6-driven tumor control is mediated by CD8+ T cells, we depleted this population in both WT and Cxcr6⁻/⁻ mice. Notably, CD8+ T cell depletion abolished the differential tumor growth phenotype between the groups, indicating that CXCR6-dependent antitumor activity is predominantly mediated by CD8+ T cells (figure 3I,J).

We next employed a mixed bone marrow chimera system to dissect the cell-intrinsic function of CXCR6. Bone marrow cells from CD45.2+ WT or Cxcr6⁻/⁻ donors were mixed with CD45.1+ WT bone marrow in a 2:1 ratio and transplanted into lethally irradiated CD45.1+ recipient mice (figure 3K). Following reconstitution, the peripheral CD45.2:CD45.1 ratio remained consistent with the initial input ratio in both groups (online supplemental figure S3A–D, figure 3L, Blood and Spleen). However, the frequency of tumor-infiltrating CD45.2+ immune cells was markedly reduced in Cxcr6⁻/⁻ chimeras compared with WT controls, indicating that CXCR6 is required for efficient immune cell recruitment or retention within the TME (figure 3L, Tumor, online supplemental figure S3E). Furthermore, flow cytometric profiling of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells demonstrated significant functional impairment in Cxcr6–/– chimeric mice, as evidenced by reduced expression of the activation marker PD-1 and cytotoxic effector molecules, including GZMB and IFN-γ compared with WT controls (figure 3, online supplemental figure S3F).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that CXCR6 is essential for the accumulation, activation, and cytotoxic function of intratumoral CD8+ T cells. CXCR6+CD8+ T cells thus represent a key effector population mediating antitumor immunity in PCa.

Secretion of CXCL16 by M1-like macrophages recruits CXCR6+CD8+ T cells and wanes during PCa progression

As previously described, the proportion of intratumoral CXCR6+CD8+ T cells exhibited a significant inverse correlation with the GS of PCa. To elucidate molecular mechanisms underlying the reduction or depletion of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells in high-risk PCa, we hypothesized that their enrichment within TME is mediated by chemotaxis via CXCL16, the exclusive ligand for CXCR6.

To identify the cellular source of CXCL16, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells in human PCa. Unsupervised clustering revealed five transcriptionally distinct myeloid populations, including IL1B+, FOLR2+, CX3CR1+, and SPP1+ macrophages, as well as CD1C+ dendritic cells (figure 4A). Among these, IL1B+ macrophages expressed the highest levels of proinflammatory markers (eg, IL1B, CD86, TLR2) and CXCL16, consistent with a classically activated M1-like phenotype (figure 4A–C, online supplemental figure S4A–C). AUCell scoring further confirmed that this subset displayed the strongest M1 signature, while FOLR2+ and SPP1+ macrophages were enriched in M2-associated markers (eg, CD163, MRC1) (figure 4C,D, online supplemental figure S4B,C).

Given the chemotactic role of CXCL16, we hypothesized that IL1B+ macrophages may facilitate the recruitment of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells into the TME. CellChat23 analysis confirmed that IL1B+ macrophages exhibited strong outgoing communication toward TEff-like CD8+ T cells, with the CXCL16–CXCR6 ligand–receptor pair representing a dominant axis (figure 4E,F, online supplemental figure S4D).

To functionally validate these findings, we polarized mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) into M1- or M2-like phenotypes in vitro using IFN-γ + LPS or IL-4, respectively (figure 4G,H). Flow cytometry confirmed successful polarization based on surface markers CD80 (M1) and CD206 (M2) (figure 4H). CXCL16 protein levels were markedly upregulated in M1-polarized macrophages, as confirmed by western blot (figure 4K). Similarly, THP-1-derived macrophages polarized toward the M1 phenotype showed elevated CXCL16 expression relative to their M2-polarized counterparts (figure 4I, J, L and M).

As previously reported, macrophages (CD45+CD11b+HLA-DR+) comprise approximately 40% of CD45+ immune cells within the PCa microenvironment.24 Given the phenotypic plasticity of TAMs, we next examined dynamic shifts in macrophage polarization during PCa progression. In a syngeneic mouse model, the ratio of MHCII CD2066⁻ M1-like to MHCII⁻ CD206+ M2-like macrophages significantly decreased between day 35 and day 49 postimplantation, indicating a progressive decline of pro-inflammatory TAMs (figure 4N,O).

Consistent with these findings, multiplex immunohistochemistry analysis of human PCa tissues revealed that intermediate-risk tumors (GS=3+4) were enriched in HLA-DRA+iNOS+ M1-like macrophages, which were spatially associated with CXCR6+CD8+ T cells. In contrast, high-risk tumors (GS=5+5) showed a predominance of CD163+ M2-like macrophages and marked depletion of both M1-like macrophages and CXCR6+CD8+ T cells (figure 4P).

Together, these data identify IL1B+ M1-like macrophages as the principal source of CXCL16 and implicate them as critical regulators of CXCR6+CD8+ T cell recruitment. The progressive loss of this macrophage subset during PCa advancement results in impaired T cell infiltration and establishes a macrophage-dependent mechanism of immune exclusion with therapeutic relevance.

KLF2 restrains the CXCR6-driven cytotoxic program in intratumoral CD8+ T cells

To pinpoint transcriptional regulators governing CD8+ T-cell fate in PCa, we applied SCENIC to our scRNA-seq dataset (figure 5A). The analysis highlighted KLF2 as one of the most active regulons in both transitional and effector-like CXCR6+CD8+ T-cell subsets (figure 5B,C). Notably, violin plots revealed a mutually exclusive pattern in which KLF2 expression was high in transitional states but low in CXCR6, GZMA, and PRF1-high effector cells (figure 5D). Mapping KLF2 eRegulon activity onto the UMAP confirmed its predominant activation in non-cytotoxic clusters, whereas effector-like cells displayed a repressor mode (figure 5E,F).

Gene–regulon network modeling further predicted direct KLF2-mediated repression of CXCR6, GZMA, and PRF1 (figure 5G). Consistent with this, KLF2 regulon activity ranked among the top regulons in Mem-like subsets, further suggesting its role in maintaining a transcriptionally quiescent or stem-like state (online supplemental figure S5A,B).

Functional assays corroborated this prediction. Western blot analysis of sorted CXCR6+ versus CXCR6⁻CD8+ T cells revealed higher GZMB expression in CXCR6+ cells, consistent with greater cytotoxic potential. In contrast, CXCR6+CD8+ T cells showed lower levels of phosphorylated STAT3 and NF-κB p65, suggesting reduced activation of inflammatory signaling pathways (figure 5H). Reduced KLF2 expression further supports a tissue-resident or activated phenotype (figure 5H). Forced expression of KLF2 in PC3 cells led to a pronounced downregulation of CXCR6 at both mRNA and protein levels (figure 5I,J). Chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative real-time PCR (ChIP-qPCR) confirmed direct binding of KLF2 to the CXCR6 promoter in both PC3 and DU145 cells (figure 5K,L). A similar repressive effect was observed in transiently transfected 293 T cells (online supplemental figure S5C).

To validate these findings in situ, we performed multiplex immunohistochemistry on serial sections of PCa specimens. Low-grade (GS=4+3) tumors were enriched in CXCR6+GZMB+CD8+ T cells, while high-grade (GS=4+4) lesions predominantly harbored KLF2+CD8+ T cells with negligible CXCR6 or GZMB staining (figure 5M,N). Quantitative image analysis across 24 cases revealed a strong inverse correlation between KLF2+ and CXCR6+CD8+ T-cell densities (r=–0.78, p<0.01; figure 5O). Collectively, these data identify KLF2 as a transcriptional brake that limits the CXCR6-driven cytotoxic program in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, thereby contributing to T-cell dysfunction and immune escape in advanced PCa.

FOXO1 functions as a direct transcriptional activator of KLF2 in CD8+ T cells

The forkhead box TF FOXO1 is highly expressed in multiple lymphocyte subsets, including CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and B cells, and plays a pivotal role in maintaining T cell homeostasis. In naïve T cells, FOXO1 transcriptionally regulates key genes involved in cell trafficking and survival, such as SELL, CCR7, and KLF2.2527 Loss of FOXO1 disrupts multiple aspects of T cell biology, including homeostatic maintenance, lymphoid homing, and cell survival.25

To determine whether FOXO1 directly regulates KLF2 transcription, we first performed ChIP assays, which confirmed FOXO1 binding to the KLF2 promoter region (online supplemental figure 5D). Consistently, reanalysis of three independent FOXO1 ChIP-seq datasets2830 from human and mouse lymphocytes revealed robust FOXO1 occupancy at evolutionarily conserved motifs within the KLF2 promoter (figure 6A), supporting its role as a direct transcriptional activator. These findings are consistent with prior evidence that FOXO1-dependent expression of SELL and CCR7 is mediated, at least in part, through upregulation of KLF2.25

Although FOXO1 is a well-established upstream regulator of KLF2, it was not identified as a regulon in our SCENIC analysis (figure 5A), likely due to its intrinsically low transcript levels in T cells and predominant regulation at the post-translational level. Notably, immunohistochemical analysis of serial sections from human PCa tissues demonstrated nuclear co-localization of FOXO1 and KLF2 within CD8+ T cells. Quantitative assessment further confirmed a strong positive correlation between their expression levels (online supplemental figure S5E,F).

To assess the functional relevance of FOXO1 activity, we treated murine splenic CD8+ T cells with AS1842856, a selective FOXO1 inhibitor.31 Inhibition of FOXO1 led to a marked reduction in both KLF2 mRNA and protein expression, accompanied by a significant upregulation of CXCR6 transcript and protein levels (figure 6B,C). Notably, both total and surface-localized CXCR6 were elevated in FOXO1-inhibited CD8+ T cells (figure 6C,D). Similar results were observed in human T-cell leukemia lines (MOLT-4 and Jurkat), where FOXO1 inhibition decreased KLF2 and upregulated CXCR6 (online supplemental figure S6A–D). Conversely, overexpression of FOXO1 in PC3 cells led to robust upregulation of KLF2 and suppression of CXCR6 at both transcript and protein levels. Strikingly, KLF2 knockdown restored CXCR6 expression even in the context of sustained FOXO1 activity, functionally positioning KLF2 downstream of FOXO1 in repressing CXCR6 (online supplemental figure S6E,F). Together, these findings delineate a conserved FOXO1–KLF2–CXCR6 transcriptional cascade that operates across diverse cellular contexts, wherein FOXO1 transcriptionally activates KLF2, which in turn represses CXCR6, thereby shaping CD8+ T cell trafficking phenotypes.

Given that FOXO1 inhibition induces CXCR6 expression, we hypothesized that AS1842856 treatment could enhance intratumoral accumulation of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells and potentiate anti-PD-1 therapy. In subcutaneous PCa xenografts, AS1842856 synergized with anti-PD-1 antibody to suppress tumor growth more effectively than either monotherapy (figure 6E–G). Flow cytometric profiling of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes revealed that combination therapy significantly increased the abundance of total CXCR6+ immune cells, CXCR6+CD8+ T cells, and PD-1+ effector CD8+ T cells compared with control groups (figure 6H–M). Notably, the ratio of CD4+ to CD8+ T cells was markedly reduced following AS1842856 and anti-PD-1 combination treatment (figure 6N,O), indicating a shift toward a CD8-dominant intratumoral T cell composition, which may contribute to enhanced antitumor immunity.

Collectively, these findings identify FOXO1 as a direct upstream regulator of KLF2 in CD8+ T cells and reveal a FOXO1–KLF2–CXCR6 transcriptional axis that governs CXCR6 expression.

IL-10–STAT3–FOXO1 signaling reprograms CXCR6+CD8+ T cells toward a dysfunctional state

Immunosuppression is a hallmark of tumor progression and a key contributor to immune evasion and therapy resistance across various malignancies.32 In PCa, clinical studies have demonstrated that elevated baseline levels of circulating IL-10 are associated with adverse clinical outcomes.33

IL-10 exerts its effects through a heterodimeric receptor complex composed of IL-10Rα and IL-10Rβ chains. IL-10Rα is primarily expressed on immune cells and confers ligand specificity, whereas IL-10Rβ is broadly expressed and serves as a shared subunit for the IL-10 cytokine family.34 Although transcripts of IL-10 were not detected in our single-cell dataset—a common limitation of scRNA-seq due to the gene’s low abundance and transient expression—CXCR6+CD8+ T cells exhibited enriched expression of IL10RA (figure 7A), indicating their potential responsiveness to IL-10. Consistently, pathway activity scoring revealed a significant enrichment of IL-10–responsive gene signatures in this population, indicating activation of the IL-10–STAT3–FOXO1 axis (figure 7B, online supplemental figure S7A).

IL-10 engagement triggers the JAK1–STAT3 signaling cascade, inhibits PI3K/Akt signaling, and enhances FOXO1 activity in lymphocytes.35 36 To validate this pathway in our system, we stimulated murine splenic CD8+ T cells with recombinant IL-10 and observed a marked increase in FOXO1 protein levels (figure 7C). This effect was abrogated by Stattic, a selective STAT3 phosphorylation inhibitor,37 confirming that the upregulation of FOXO1 induced by IL-10 is dependent on STAT3 signaling (figure 7D). Concomitantly, IL-10 stimulation increased KLF2 expression while repressing CXCR6 levels (figure 7D), suggesting a coordinated transcriptional reprogramming that may impair the tumor-infiltrating capacity of CD8+ T cells. Flow cytometry analysis using either mouse spleen-derived CD8+ T cells (figure 7E,F) or those from healthy donors (figure 7G–I) further confirmed preferential IL-10R expression in CXCR6+CD8+ T cells and its upregulation on TCR stimulation, reinforcing their functional susceptibility to IL-10-mediated signaling.

To further confirm whether the level of IL-10 is elevated in the PCa microenvironment, we performed bulk RNA-seq on prostate tissues from Pb-Cre, Ptenflox/flox mice (T) and WT controls (n=3/group). Unsupervised analyses showed a clear separation by genotype: principal component analysis (PCA) resolved T and WT samples along PC1 (87.24% of the variance) with additional separation on PC2 (5.64%) (figure 7J). Consistently, the sample-to-sample distance heatmap clustered biological replicates within each group and effectively segregated T from WT (figure 7K). Differential expression (DE) analysis (cutoffs |log2FC|≥1.5, false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05) revealed widespread transcriptional reprogramming in Pten-null prostates (figure 7L). Notably, Il10 was markedly upregulated in tumors together with canonical M2‐like macrophage markers (Mrc1, Cd163) and myeloid/immune-regulatory genes (C1qa, C1qb, C1qc, C3, Ccl2). Immune-checkpoint genes (Havcr2/Tim3, Lag3) and the matrix-remodeling factor Mmp2 were concurrently elevated, underscoring an immunoregulatory, macrophage-enriched TME. Pathway enrichment (Tumor vs WT) further highlighted immune/inflammatory programs (response to bacterium, leukocyte migration, inflammatory response) and a NABA matrisome-associated signature indicative of extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling (online supplemental figure S7B,C).

To extend these findings to human disease, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence on independent human PCa specimens (n=9). IL-10 protein was readily detected in tumor regions and co-localized with CD68+/CD163+, HLA-DRA+ macrophages, whereas adjacent non-tumor areas showed low or negligible staining (figure 7M). These data converge to indicate that IL-10 is increased in the PCa microenvironment, with concordant evidence in human PCa tissues.

Together, our findings reveal that the IL-10/STAT3/FOXO1/KLF2 axis drives CD8+ T cell dysfunction in the PCa microenvironment. This pathway may underlie the reduced presence of CXCR6+CD8+ T cells observed in advanced disease and provides a potential target for overcoming immunosuppression in PCa immunotherapy.

Discussion

In China, the incidence of PCa remains significantly lower than that in Western populations. However, recent epidemiological data have indicated a consistent upward trend in both PCa incidence and mortality rates, a phenomenon primarily driven by shifting lifestyle patterns and environmental exposures. Notably, this rising trend positions PCa as the second most rapidly increasing malignancy among all cancers in men.38 Regarding immunotherapy for PCa, while immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and other immunotherapeutic strategies have not yet demonstrated significant clinical benefits, emerging evidence suggests that immunotherapy—either as monotherapy or in combination regimens—can enhance CD8+ T cell infiltration,39 sustain antitumor immunity,40 and prolong PSA doubling time.41 These findings underscore the promising therapeutic potential of immunotherapy in PCa treatment. Localized PCa or CRPC exhibits a unique immunophenotype distinct from other solid tumors, characterized by sparse intratumoral immune cell infiltration.42 43 While the clinical implications of this immunologically “cold” phenotype are well-recognized, strategies to reverse the immunosuppressive landscape and enhance the infiltration of cytotoxic effector T cells remain underexplored. Our study provides the first evidence of a clinically significant association between tumor-infiltrating CXCR6+CD8+ T cell populations and PCa progression. Consequently, elucidating the mechanisms underlying CXCR6+CD8+ T cell reduction or depletion in high-risk PCa may uncover novel immunotherapeutic opportunities.

CXCL16, the exclusive cognate ligand for CXCR6, functions as a critical regulator of antitumor immunity through its chemotactic activity. Emerging evidence highlights that tumor niche-specific CXCL16 secretion governs the spatial recruitment of CXCR6+ immune effectors, with demonstrated therapeutic implications across multiple malignancies: (1) in hepatocellular carcinoma, CXCL16 produced by hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells facilitates the accumulation of CXCR6+ natural killer T (NKT) cells, which is associated with improved tumor control44; (2) in breast cancer, radiation-induced CXCL16 overexpression enhances tumor infiltration by CXCR6+CD8+ T cells, thereby potentiating the efficacy of checkpoint immunotherapy45; and (3) in ovarian cancer, constitutive CXCL16 secretion by myeloid cells drives the accumulation of CXCR6+ T cells within the TME.46 Our study identifies M1-like immunostimulatory macrophages as the predominant CXCL16-secreting population within the PCa microenvironment. Importantly, existing studies have demonstrated that PCa cells can reprogram macrophages from a pro-inflammatory (M1-like) phenotype toward TAM phenotype.24 Therefore, elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms governing PCa-mediated macrophage reprogramming may provide therapeutic opportunities to enhance M1-like macrophage infiltration, promote CXCL16 secretion, augment CXCR6+CD8+ T cell recruitment, and ultimately suppress PCa progression.

Knolle et al. first demonstrated an inverse correlation between the levels of FOXO1 in CD8+ T cells and the percentages of intrahepatic CXCR6+CD8+ T cells in NASH murine models14. Their work revealed that IL-15 treatment promotes phosphorylation of FOXO1 for nuclear export and proteasomal degradation, which is associated with a significant upregulation in CXCR6 expression within CD8+ T cells.14 Although FOXO1 has been implicated in the regulation of CXCR6, the exact molecular mechanisms underlying this relationship remain unexplored. In the present study, we delineate a novel FOXO1/KLF2/CXCR6 transcriptional axis in CD8+ T cells derived from PCa tissues. Mechanistically, we establish that FOXO1 directly transcriptionally activates KLF2, which subsequently regulates CXCR6 expression. These findings uncover a previously unrecognized regulatory pathway that controls CXCR6-mediated functional modulation of CD8+ T cells.

ICIs as monotherapy have demonstrated limited clinical efficacy in PCa. To overcome this therapeutic challenge, recent investigations have focused on developing combinatorial approaches to enhance ICIs responsiveness. For instance, studies have shown that co-administration of ICIs with multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (eg, dasatinib or cabozantinib) synergistically reduces intratumoral MDSC infiltration while increasing the CD8+ T cells/Tregs ratio, thereby suppressing tumor burden and metastasis.47 Furthermore, scRNA-seq analysis of metastatic PCa lesions revealed that combining anti-PD-1 therapy with androgen deprivation therapy, the mainstay therapy for advanced PCa—significantly enhances CD8+ T cell infiltration in bone metastases—a factor positively correlated with anti-PD-1 response.39 48

Supporting these findings, our study demonstrates that pharmacological inhibition of FOXO1 using AS1842856 expands intratumoral CXCR6+CD8+ T cells and potentiates anti-PD-1 efficacy. The combination of AS1842856 and anti-PD-1 markedly inhibits tumor progression, suggesting that FOXO1 blockade may convert immunologically “cold” PCa tumors into “hot” tumors by enriching functionally competent CXCR6+CD8+ T cells in the TME. Thus, our findings support a dual-modality strategy in which modulation of the FOXO1/KLF2/CXCR6 axis acts synergistically with anti-PD-1 therapy to overcome primary immune resistance in PCa. Future clinical trials integrating CXCR6-targeted immunomodulation with PD-1 blockade may provide a translational path to improve response rates and long-term survival in high-risk PCa patients.

The PCa microenvironment is characterized by coexisting immunosuppressive factors that collectively promote immune evasion and tumor progression. Multiple cell types within the TME contribute to this immunosuppression through secretion of cytokine IL-10. Notably, Tregs have been shown to secrete both IL-10 and IL-15, inducing upregulation of multiple inhibitory receptors (including TIM-3, LAG-3, TIGIT, and 2B4) on tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells. This process drives an exhaustion-associated gene expression profile and synergistically promotes CD8+ T cell dysfunction.49 Clinical evidence from muscle-invasive bladder cancer demonstrates that elevated infiltration of IL-10-producing TAMs (IL-10+ TAMs) significantly correlates with poorer clinical outcomes, including reduced overall survival and recurrence-free survival. These immunosuppressive IL-10+ TAMs were associated with an immune-evasive TME signature featuring exhausted CD8+ T cells, immature NK cells, and upregulated immune checkpoint expression.50 Our study extends these findings to PCa, indicating an upregulation of IL-10 within the TME. This association highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting the IL-10/IL-10Rα axis in PCa. Supporting this, IL-10-deficient murine models and animals treated with IL-10Rα neutralizing antibodies show significantly suppressed hepatic metastasis in colorectal cancer.51

In PCa, elevated IL-10 levels directly modulate key transcriptional regulators within CD8+ T cells, critically shaping their functional evolution. The scRNA-seq of PCa tissues reveals that CXCR6+CD8+ T cell subsets characteristically express high levels of IL-10RA, rendering them particularly susceptible to IL-10-mediated regulation. Mechanistically, IL-10 engagement triggers STAT3 signaling activation, which drives FOXO1 upregulation and subsequent suppression of the KLF2/CXCR6 axis. Collectively, these findings provide a mechanistic rationale for targeting IL-10 signaling in PCa therapy and establish IL-10 as both a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target.

Several limitations of the present study warrant careful consideration. First, although our data underscore a critical role for CXCR6 in regulating the recruitment and functional state of CD8+ T cells in PCa, the absence of CD8+ T cell-specific genetic models, such as CD8-CreERT2-mediated conditional deletion of CXCR6, precludes definitive establishment of a cell-intrinsic causal role in vivo. Future studies using inducible, lineage-restricted genetic approaches will be essential to further dissect the direct contribution of CXCR6 signaling within CD8+ T cells, independent of potential confounding effects from other immune or stromal components.

Second, while the murine models used in this study recapitulate key features of prostate tumor immune remodeling, they inherently fail to fully reflect the complexity and heterogeneity of the human PCa immune microenvironment. This limitation highlights the importance of integrating experimental models with human clinical samples in future investigations.

Finally, although we identify the IL-10–FOXO1–KLF2 axis as a key regulatory pathway governing CXCR6+CD8+ T cell dysfunction, IL-10 transcripts were not robustly detected at the scRNA-seq level. This may be attributed to the transient and low-abundance nature of IL-10 expression, as well as the well-recognized dropout effects inherent to single-cell transcriptomic technologies, particularly for cytokines with short mRNA half-lives. Further validation of this signaling axis in larger, well-annotated clinical cohorts and under therapeutic conditions will be necessary to determine its prognostic and predictive relevance, particularly in relation to immunotherapy response.

Methods

Cell culture

PC-3 (RRID: CVCL_0035), DU145 (RRID: CVCL_0105), HEK293T (RRID: CVCL_0063), Jurkat (RRID: CVCL_0065), MOLT-4 (RRID: CVCL_0013), THP-1 (RRID: CVCL_0006) and TRAMP-C1 (RRID: CVCL_3614) cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA). All cells used in the study underwent regular treatment with a mycoplasma-removing agent (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, Suzhou, Jiangsu, China) to ensure they remained free from contamination; mycoplasma contamination was detected using a MycoSEQ Mycoplasma Detection Kit (cat. no. 4460623; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Cells were incubated at 37°C under a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. All basal media, including Ham’s F12K (for PC3), Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (for DU145, 293T, and TRAMP-C1), and RPMI-1640 (for Jurkat, MOLT-4, and THP-1), were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. The medium for TRAMP-C1 cells was additionally supplemented with 5 µg/mL bovine insulin and 10 nM dehydroepiandrosterone.

Preparation of single-cell suspensions from human PCa samples

Single-cell suspensions from human PCa samples were prepared using the Tumor Dissociation Kit (human, Miltenyi Biotec, 130-095-929) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, freshly resected PCa tissues were immediately rinsed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), minced into approximately 1 mm³ fragments on ice, and enzymatically digested using the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator with Heaters (program TDK-2, 37°C, 40 min). After dissociation, the resulting suspension was filtered through a 70 µm strainer, centrifuged (300×g, 5 min), and subjected to red blood cell lysis. The final cell pellet was collected for downstream analysis.

Microbead-based live cells isolation

Following the preparation of single-cell suspensions from tissues, dead cells were typically removed using a Dead Cell Removal Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-090-101) to ensure high-quality downstream analysis. Briefly, single-cell suspensions were centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min, resuspended, and incubated with 100 µL Dead Cell Removal MicroBeads per 10⁷ total cells at room temperature (RT) for 15 min. The labeled cell suspension was then applied to an LS Column placed in a MACS separator. Unlabeled live cells were collected in the flow-through, while magnetically labeled cell debris, dead cells, and dying cells were retained on the column.

Immunofluorescent staining

Frozen sections were permeabilized with a solution containing 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% NP-40, and 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS for 20 min at RT, followed by blocking with 2% bovine serum albumin for 1 hour at RT. Between each step, sections were washed three times with PBS for 10–15 min. For co-staining, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: rat anti-human CD8 (Abcam, ab60076; 1:200), rabbit anti-human CXCR6 (Abcam, ab8023; 1:100), and mouse anti-human Granzyme B (Thermo Fisher, MA1-80734; 1:100). After washing, sections were incubated for 1 hour at RT with secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated donkey anti-rat IgG (Thermo Fisher, A-21208; 1:500), Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher, A-21207; 1:500), and Alexa Fluor 633-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher, A-21052; 1:500). Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and the final fluorescence images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Flow cytometry staining and analysis

Cell viability was assessed using the trypan blue exclusion assay. Following centrifugation at 300×g for 5 min, cell pellets were resuspended in 100 µL of PBS, maintaining a cell density of no more than 2×10⁶ cells per staining reaction. For cell-surface staining, cells were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies at 4°C for 30 min in PBS supplemented with 2% FBS. For intracellular cytokine staining, CD8+ T cells were stimulated with Cell Stimulation Cocktail (plus protein transport inhibitors, 500×; Thermo Fisher, 00-4975-93) for 5 hours at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator. Cells were subsequently processed using the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher, 00-5523-00) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the provided fixation/permeabilization working solution at 4°C for 40–50 min. Following three washes with 1×permeabilization buffer, cells were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies at 4°C for 30 min. Finally, cells were resuspended in 300 µL of PBS and analyzed using a CytoFLEX Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA).

Isolation and enrichment of murine splenic CD8+ T cells for in vitro T cell receptor stimulation assays

For the isolation of murine splenic CD8+ T cells, spleens were harvested from 6–8-week-old male WT C57BL/6J mice. Single-cell suspensions were generated by mechanically dissociating the spleens through a 70 µm cell strainer (premoistened with PBS supplemented with 2% FBS) using the plunger of a 1 mL syringe, with additional PBS/2% FBS added to facilitate tissue dissociation. The single-cell suspensions were then centrifuged at 400×g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 2 mL of red blood cell lysis buffer. Following lysis, the cell pellet was collected for enrichment of murine splenic CD8+ T cells using CD8a (Ly-2) MicroBeads (mouse, Miltenyi Biotec, 130-117-044) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were resuspended in autoMACS Running Buffer (90 µL per 10⁷ cells), incubated with 10 µL MicroBeads at 4°C for 30 min, and subsequently separated using an LS Column. Unlabeled cells were collected in the flow-through, while magnetically labeled CD8+ T cells were eluted by firmly pushing the plunger after removing the column from the MACS Separator.

For T cell receptor stimulation assays, purified murine splenic CD8+ T cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 50 nM β-mercaptoethanol. Cells were seeded in 12-well plates and stimulated with functional antibodies against CD3 (5 µg/mL; Thermo Fisher, 16-0032-85) and CD28 (5 µg/mL; Thermo Fisher, 16-0281-85), along with recombinant interleukin-2 (IL-2; 10 ng/mL; ProteinTech Group, HZ-1015) for 48 hours. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 10 ng/mL IL-2, and cells were further cultured for 5–10 days to obtain activated and expanded CD8+ T cells.

Extraction and in vitro culture of murine BMDMs

For bone marrow extraction, tibiae and femora were dissected from 6-week-old to 8-week-old male WT C57BL/6J mice. The knee joints were carefully cut using a dissection scissors, and bone marrow was flushed from the medullary cavities using a 1 mL syringe prefilled with ice-cold PBS. This procedure was repeated for all bones, and the flushed bone marrow was collected in a sterile Petri dish. Visible clumps were gently disaggregated using a needle-less syringe and then filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. Subsequently, the filtered cell suspension was centrifuged and resuspended in 2 mL of red blood cell lysis buffer.

For the in vitro culture of BMDMs, bone marrow cells were cultured in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium (IMDM) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and recombinant macrophage colony-stimulating factor (10 ng/mL; ProteinTech Group, HZ-1192). The cells were maintained under these conditions for 7 days to facilitate differentiation into macrophages. Subsequently, the macrophages were stimulated for 48 hours with IMDM containing 10% FBS, lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 100 ng/mL; MedChemExpress, HY-D1056) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ; 50 ng/mL; ProteinTech Group, HZ-1301) to induce M1-like polarization, or with IMDM containing 10% FBS and interleukin-4 (IL-4; 10 ng/mL; ProteinTech Group, HZ-1004) to promote M2-like polarization.

DE analysis

Bulk RNA-seq was performed on prostate tissues from Pb-Cre; Ptenflox/flox (tumor, T) and WT mice (n=3 per group). Raw reads were quality-filtered (fastp v0.23.4) and aligned to the mm10 reference genome (GRCm38) using STAR (v2.7.11a). Gene-level counts were obtained with featureCounts (Subread v2.0.3) against GENCODE vM25 annotation (protein-coding and lncRNA; multimapping reads excluded). Alternatively, transcript quantification with Salmon (V.1.10) followed by tximport produced equivalent count matrices.

DE was conducted in R (V.4.3.0) using DESeq2 (V.1.42.1). Genes with low counts were prefiltered (eg, fewer than 10 total reads across all samples were removed). Size-factor normalization used the median-of-ratios method. A negative binomial GLM with design \~ genotype (T vs WT) was fitted, and Wald statistics were used to test coefficients. P values were adjusted by Benjamini-Hochberg to control the FDR. Unless otherwise stated, genes with |log2 fold change|≥1.5 and FDR<0.05 were considered differentially expressed. For visualization only, log2FC shrinkage (apeglm) was applied to reduce small-count inflation; statistical calls were based on the unshrunken results.

Volcano plot visualization

Volcano plots were generated in R with ggplot2 (v3.5.1) and ggrepel (v0.9.6). Axes display log2 fold change (x) and –log10(adjusted P) (y). Thresholds are indicated by dashed lines at |log2FC|=1.5 and FDR=0.05. Genes of interest (Il10, Mrc1, Cd163, Sox4, etc) were annotated; Il10, Mrc1, Cd163, and Cxcr6 were additionally highlighted in purple. To avoid visual compression caused by extremely small adjusted p values, a broken y-axis was optionally applied using ggbreak (V.0.1.4), which collapses the 100–150 interval while preserving relative ranks. Figure esthetics (point size, transparency, connectors) were kept constant across comparisons.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining was performed on 4 μm thick paraffin-embedded tumor tissue sections according to standard procedures. Briefly, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval by microwave heating at 100°C for 15 min in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Non-specific binding was blocked with 5% FBS for 30 min at RT. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against CD8 (Abcam, ab17147; 1:200), CXCR6 (Abcam, ab8023; 1:100), CXCL16 (ProteinTech Group, 60123-1-Ig; 1:100), CD68 (Abcam, ab125212; 1:200), iNOS (Abcam, ab3523; 1:200), HLA-DRA (ProteinTech Group, 17221-1-AP; 1:100), CD163 (Abcam, ab182422; 1:200), KLF2 (ABclonal, A16480; 1:200), FOXO1 (ProteinTech Group, 18592-1-AP; 1:100), Granzyme B (Thermo Fisher, MA1-80734; 1:100), α-SMA (Abcam, ab5694; 1:100) and AR (Abcam, ab133273; 1:100). After incubation with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Absin, abs996) for 30 min at RT, immunoreactivity was visualized using diaminobenzidine (DAB), followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. The tissue sections were then differentiated in 1% acid alcohol solution for 10 s and blued in 0.2% lithium carbonate solution for 30–60 s. For dehydration, the sections were sequentially immersed in 75% ethanol, 95% ethanol, absolute ethanol (two changes), and xylene (two changes), each for 5 min. Finally, the sections were mounted using a drop of Neutral Balsam mounting medium and coverslipped for microscopic examination under a light microscope.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry/immunofluorescence

Multiplex immunohistochemistry was performed on human prostate tissue sections. Paraffin sections (4 µm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval (10 mM citrate buffer, pH 6.0; 15 min). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched (3% H₂O₂, 10 min) and non-specific binding was blocked in protein blocking buffer (10 min). To accommodate multiple rabbit primaries, staining was carried out using sequential tyramide signal amplification (TSA) cycles. In each cycle, slides were incubated with one primary antibody, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary polymer and fluorophore deposition, then brief microwave retrieval to strip bound antibodies while preserving the TSA precipitate. Primary antibodies and working dilutions were: CD68 (Abcam, ab955; 1:1000), HLA-DRA (ProteinTech Group, 17221-1-AP; 1:400), CD163 (Abcam, ab182422; 1:400), and IL-10 (Abclonal, A2171; 1:1000). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI and slides mounted in antifade medium.

Generation of chimeras

For the irradiation of recipient mice, a fractionated irradiation strategy was implemented. Specifically, 5–6-week-old male CD45.1 C57BL/6J recipient mice were exposed to a total radiation dose of 9 Gy, which was delivered in two equal fractions of 4.5 Gy separated by a 4-hour interval to minimize radiation toxicity. Irradiation was performed using a biological X-ray irradiator (Rad Source Technologies, RS2000pro-225) at a dose rate of 0.3 Gy/min. On the same day, bone marrow cell suspensions were prepared from CD45.1 C57BL/6J recipient mice and age-matched congenic male CD45.2 WT or Cxcr6–/– donor mice. For competitive bone marrow transplantation, a mixture containing a total of 1×10⁷ cells resuspended in 200 µL PBS (comprising CD45.2 WT or Cxcr6–/– bone marrow cells mixed with CD45.1 C57BL/6J bone marrow cells at a 2:1 ratio) was intravenously injected into the lethally irradiated CD45.1 C57BL/6J recipient mice. To prevent potential infections, the recipient mice were individually housed and administered enrofloxacin (0.34 mg/mL) in their drinking water for the following 3 weeks. The CD45.1 C57BL/6J mice were obtained from GemPharmatech, whereas congenic CD45.2 WT and Cxcr6–/– mice on a C57BL/6J background were sourced from Saiye (Suzhou) Biological Technology.

After 6–8 weeks of hematopoietic reconstitution, approximately 50 µL of EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood was collected from the tail vein of each recipient mouse, the relative proportions of recipient-derived (CD45.1 positive) and donor-derived (CD45.2 positive) cells in peripheral blood were then quantified by flow cytometry to compare hematopoietic reconstitution efficiency between CD45.1+WT and CD45.1+Cxcr6–/– groups. Finally, the Cxcr6+/+: Cxcr6–/– chimeras were challenged with murine prostate adenocarcinoma cells (TRAMP-C1; ATCC CRL-2730) through subcutaneous injection into the right flank for subsequent analysis.

Tumor model and in vivo treatments

For tumor implantation, a total of 5×106 TRAMP-C1 cells suspended in 100 µL PBS were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of 5–6-week-old male WT C57BL/6J mice. For the CD8+ T cell deletion experiment, mice were randomly assigned to two groups. One group received an intraperitoneal injection of 200 µg of anti-mouse CD8α monoclonal antibody (clone 2.43; Bio X Cell, BE0061), whereas the control group received an isotype control antibody (diluted in 150 µL InVivoPure pH 7.0 Dilution Buffer) 2 days prior to tumor cell inoculation, followed by weekly maintenance doses. For anti-PD-1 treatment, tumor-bearing mice were randomized into two groups and received either 200 µg anti-PD-1 antibody (clone: RMP1-14; Bio X Cell, BE014) or isotype control antibody (200 µg per dose) via intraperitoneal injection every 2 days starting when the tumors became palpable. Mice that failed to develop palpable tumors within the specified observation period were excluded from the subsequent analysis. The tumor size was measured every 2 days using calipers in two dimensions to generate a tumor volume using the formula: width2×length/2. on reaching a tumor volume of 1000 mm³, mice were euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation. Single-cell suspensions were then generated from the excised tumors by enzymatic digestion, followed by flow cytometric analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cell populations. All mice were maintained in a specific-pathogen-free facility, and all related protocols were performed in compliance with the Research Ethical Committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center.

RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from PC3 or murine splenic CD8+ T cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized from the extracted RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a 20 µL reaction volume. qPCR was performed on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, California, USA) with SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Takara Biotechnology, Dalian, Japan). Each 10 µL qPCR reaction mixture consisted of 5 µL SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix, 10 µM of each primer, and 50 ng cDNA. The thermal cycling conditions were as previously described. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with ACTB used as the endogenous reference gene for normalization. Primer sequences are listed in online supplemental tables S1,S2.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

The ChIP assay was performed using the EZ-ChIP kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Cat. No. 17–371, Merck). To examine the enrichment of specific TFs at target gene promoters, the immunoprecipitated DNA was amplified by qPCR using primers spanning the potential binding regions in the KLF2 and CXCR6 promoters. The qPCR products were then analyzed via denaturing agarose gel electrophoresis. The primer sequences used were as follows: KLF2 promoter forward, 5’-CCACACCATATCCCTGCTG-3’, KLF2 promoter reverse, 5’-CCTCTATCCTTCCCTCCTTCA-3’; CXCR6 promoter forward, 5’-CCCTTATCAGTCGTTTCTAGAGG-3’; CXCR6 promoter reverse, 5’-CTTCCTTCGGAGGATGTAGC-3’.

scRNA-seq data processing and integration

Raw gene expression matrices were processed using the Seurat R package (V.4.3.0). For each dataset, low-quality cells were filtered based on the number of detected genes, UMI counts, and mitochondrial transcript percentage. Gene expression data were normalized using LogNormalize with a scaling factor of 10 000, and the top 2000 variable genes were selected for downstream analysis.

Dimensionality reduction was performed using PCA, followed by UMAP embedding. Cells were clustered using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions. For multisample or multicondition datasets, batch correction and integration were conducted using the Harmony52 package.

Cell types and immune subsets were annotated based on canonical marker gene expression, supported by unsupervised clustering and differential gene expression analysis. CD8+ T cells and myeloid compartments were extracted for downstream subclustering and gene signature scoring.

Cell–cell communication analysis

Intercellular communication among tumor-infiltrating immune populations was inferred using the CellChat23 R package (V.6.1). Cell-type-specific ligand–receptor interactions were predicted based on curated signaling databases and expression profiles of sender and receiver populations. The overall communication probability and pathway-level interactions were computed using default parameters and visualized via hierarchical circle plots, heatmaps, and chord diagrams. Differential communication patterns across conditions or clusters were assessed to identify dominant signaling axes (eg, CXCL16–CXCR6) relevant to CD8+ T cell recruitment and function.

Gene regulatory network inference

TF-centered regulatory network analysis was performed using the SCENIC19 workflow (V.1.3.1), integrating coexpression and cis-regulatory motif analysis. The pipeline was implemented using the RcisTarget, GENIE3, and AUCell components to identify active regulons across single cells. Regulon activity scores were computed using AUCell, enabling cell-level quantification of TF activity. Regulons of interest (eg, KLF2) were visualized using heatmaps and projected onto UMAP embeddings to explore their distribution across defined immune cell clusters.

Gene signature scoring

CD8+ T cell functional states

To characterize functional heterogeneity within CD8+ T cell populations, we curated gene sets representing key transcriptional programs, including cytotoxicity, exhaustion, memory, and tissue residency. The cytotoxicity signature included effector molecules involved in target cell killing and granule exocytosis (GZMB, PRF1, GNLY, NKG7, GZMH, CXCR6); the exhaustion signature consisted of transcriptional regulators and inhibitory receptors (TOX, TOX2, NR4A1, NR4A2, CTLA4, HAVCR2, TIGIT, PRDM1); the memory signature comprised genes associated with long-term persistence and recirculation (IL7R, KLF2, SELL, CCR7, FOXO1); and the tissue residency signature included markers of localized retention (CXCR6, CD69, ITGAE, ZNF683). Module scores were calculated using the “AddModuleScore” function in Seurat (V.4.3.0) and applied across all CD8+ T cells. Resulting scores were visualized using violin plots, box-and-whisker plots with individual cells overlaid, and UMAPs to facilitate interpretation of CD8+ T cell state transitions. To ensure that exhaustion-associated patterns were not dependent on a single scoring strategy, the exhaustion signature was further evaluated using multiple complementary approaches, including UCell (rank-based scoring), AUCell (area under the recovery curve–based enrichment), and single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA).

Macrophage polarization (M1/M2) scoring

To assess macrophage polarization in the TME, gene sets corresponding to classical (M1-like) and alternative (M2-like) activation states were compiled. The M1-like signature included genes associated with pro-inflammatory signaling and antigen presentation (NOS2, CD64, CD80, CD86, IL1A, IL1B, CCL5, IRF5, TNF, IL6, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11), while the M2-like signature reflected immunosuppressive and tissue-remodeling functions (ARG1, CD163, MRC1, TGFB1, IL10, CHI3L1, VEGFA, VEGFB, IL4R, CCL18, CCL20). Module scores were computed using ‘AddModuleScore’ in Seurat and assigned to myeloid-lineage cells. Score distributions were visualized by UMAP and violin plots to infer polarization states at single-cell resolution.

IL-10-responsive signature scoring

To quantify IL-10 pathway activity in CD8+ T cell subsets, an IL-10-responsive gene signature was constructed based on downstream mediators of STAT3 activation, immunoregulatory effectors, and exhaustion-associated genes. This set included FOXO1, KLF2, SOCS3, BATF, IRF4, IL1RN, CD274, STAT3, HIF1A, MCL1, MYC, PRDM1, TOX, IKZF2, BCL6, CISH, IL2RA, TNFRSF4, LGALS3, and CCL22. Signature scores were calculated using “AddModuleScore” and projected across CD8+ T cell clusters to infer differential activation of the IL-10–STAT3–FOXO1 regulatory axis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (V.8.0; GraphPad Software). Data were presented as mean±SD. For comparisons between two groups, statistical significance was determined using the Mann-Whitney U test. For multiple group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Specific statistical tests and sample sizes for each experiment are detailed in the corresponding figure legends. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility.
