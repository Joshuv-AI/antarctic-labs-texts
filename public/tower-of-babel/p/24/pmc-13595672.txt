Pituitary apoplexy following cataract surgery: a case report

Background

Pituitary apoplexy is an acute neuroendocrine emergency resulting from hemorrhage or infarction within a pituitary adenoma, most commonly a macroadenoma. Pituitary macroadenomas, which are defined as adenomas measuring ≥ 10 mm in size, often remain clinically silent until they exert mass effect on the optic chiasm or cavernous sinuses. When complicated by apoplexy, the abrupt expansion mandates urgent neuroimaging and multidisciplinary management, including endocrine stabilization, and neurosurgical evaluation. It typically presents with sudden onset, severe headache, visual disturbances, cranial nerve palsies, nausea/vomiting, and varying degrees of hypopituitarism [1].

Although spontaneous in many instances, recognized precipitants include systemic hypertension, major surgery, anticoagulation, and dynamic pituitary function testing [2]. An emerging risk factor is COVID-19, as reported in rare case reports demonstrating a speculative association, which implies that a marked inflammatory response due to severe infections can also lead to vascular dysfunction predisposing to the development of pituitary apoplexy [3]. However, apoplexy following cataract surgery remains a novel incident previously only described in a report by Shanmugan et al. [4]. Pituitary apoplexy has been reported following various surgical procedures, including coronary artery bypass grafting and mitral valvuloplasty [5]; however, there are no other known cases specifically linking cataract surgery to this condition. These instances suggest that surgical stress, even from procedures not directly involving the pituitary gland, can act as a precipitating factor for apoplexy in patients with undiagnosed pituitary adenomas.

We present a case of pituitary apoplexy, in a previously undiagnosed macroadenoma, identified following cataract surgery. This case underscoring the diagnostic nuances and therapeutic considerations inherent in this condition. This patient was managed at an academic tertiary care hospital in Pakistan.

Case presentation

A 61-year-old Pakistani male, known case of Pangastritis, presented to our emergency room (ER) with complaints of severe headache, since waking up, and projectile vomiting, following breakfast. He was in usual state of health until 4 days ago when he underwent right eye cataract surgery at a local hospital. The morning after surgery, patient started experiencing a severe headache alongside non-bloody projectile vomiting. The headache was reported as central with pain also radiating to the right. Patient went to a local hospital, where his blood pressure was found to be 175/100 mmHg. He was treated for Hypertensive Urgency with Captopril 25 mg. Thirty minutes later the blood pressure was down to 136/95 mmHg and the patient was discharged. Around 4:00 am his blood pressure went up again to 155/96 mmHg and he self-medicated with a combination drug containing Olmesartan medoxomil and Amlodipine. His blood pressure subsequently was reported to be 177/110 mmHg at 9:00 am, when he took another tablet of Captopril 25 mg, 155/97 mmHg at 10:00 am and 160/101 mmHg at 11:00 am, with continued projectile vomiting and diaphoresis. As his complaints persisted, he presented to our ER at 1:00 pm. On examination, the patient was afebrile and hypertensive with a blood pressure of 161/80 mmHg. Other vital signs were all in normal ranges, and no focal neurologic deficits were identified. The patient rated his headache between 7 and 10 on a 10-point pain scale. Rest of the examination was unremarkable. He had had right eye cataract surgery under local anesthesia 3 days ago. He was stable after the surgery and received a dexamethasone injection a day after surgery during a scheduled follow-up. The patient was admitted under Medicine and a CT Scan alongside hormonal workup (TSH, Prolactin, FSH, LH, GH, Cortisol, Testosterone, IGF-1, FT4, FT3, ALT, was ordered. Prolactin, Cortisol and Testosterone were decreased with values of 0.4 ng/ml (normal: 3–14.7 ng/ml),1.15 ug/dl (normal: 2.47–11.9 ug/dl) and 88.5 ng/dl (normal: 193–740 ng/dl), respectively. IGF-1 was increased with a value of 254.50 ng/ml (normal: 54.6–185.7 ng/ml), while rest of the hormones were all within normal ranges. CT scans showed soft tissue prominence in sella, which was unusual for his age, along with linear soft tissue density extending along the clivus focal erosion in the dorsum sella as well (Fig. 1a–c). The MRI (Fig. 2a–c) with pituitary protocol showed an abnormal signal intensity sellar lesion, measuring 16 × 23 × 13 mm in AP, TR and CC dimensions, which was returning isointense signals on both T1- and T2-weighted images with minimal peripheral post-contrast enhancement. This lesion was completely replacing the normal pituitary gland. A small T2 hyperintense area, representing a cystic component, was also identified in the right lateral aspect of this lesion. No focus of restricted diffusion was identified on DWI sequences within this lesion. This was accompanied by overlying irregularly thickened dural enhancement extending along the anterior cranial fossa and along the clivus. The pituitary stalk was also shown to be slightly thickened and enhanced. Hence, considering the patient's history, overall appearances were diagnosed to be secondary to pituitary apoplexy in a pre-existing pituitary macroadenoma without chiasmal compression. Scans also showed the absence of flow-limiting critical stenosis in the major vessels of anterior or posterior circulation or any cerebral venous sinus thrombosis. Neurology was taken onboard, and symptomatic management was started for his severe headache and vomiting using analgesics, oral anti-hypertensive, IV anti-emetics, GI prophylaxis and DVT prophylaxis along with steroids and IV fluids.

In a patient with similar presentation, the differential diagnosis includes: (1) postoperative hypertensive crisis, (2) raised intraocular pressure/acute angle closure glaucoma, (3) subarachnoid hemorrhage, (4) meningitis, and (5) pituitary apoplexy. Subarachnoid hemorrhage was excluded on the CT and the headache, while severe, was not the classic instantaneous 'thunderclap' pattern. Meningitis was excluded by the absence of fever and meningismus. Raised intraocular pressure was excluded as the operated eye was asymptomatic. Hypertensive urgency alone was treated initially, but persistent symptoms despite blood pressure correction directed attention to a central etiology. The sellar soft tissue density on CT, combined with the clinical context of a postoperative setting in a middle-aged hypertensive male with no neurologic deficits, raised strong suspicion for pituitary apoplexy, This was confirmed on MRI as the sellar mass returning isointense signals on T1 is consistent with a pituitary adenoma, while the absence of the normal T1 hyperintense 'posterior pituitary bright spot' indicates compression or replacement of normal gland tissue. Critically, the T1 isointensity combined with susceptibility artifact on SWI sequences indicates hemosiderin deposition or subacute hemorrhage within the adenoma, a hallmark of apoplexy. The minimal peripheral post-contrast enhancement without central enhancement reflects central necrosis/hemorrhage with a viable peripheral rim, another well-recognized pattern in hemorrhagic apoplexy. The T2 hyperintense cystic component in the right lateral aspect likely represents liquefied necrotic or hemorrhagic material. The irregularly thickened dural enhancement extending along the anterior cranial fossa and clivus reflects a sterile inflammatory meningeal reaction to extravasated blood, consistent with the chemical meningitis phenomenon seen in apoplexy. Absence of restricted diffusion on DWI argues against acute territorial ischemia. Taken together, these features are diagnostic of hemorrhagic pituitary apoplexy in a pre-existing macroadenoma.

Blood pressure medications were optimized, and electrolyte imbalances were corrected. Visual field assessment was normal with no signs of homonymous hemianopia. The patient was referred to endocrinology regarding correction of hormonal imbalance. He was started on Amlodipine 10 mg OD, Bisoprolol 2.5 mg OD, Hydrocortisone 10 mg OD, Hydrocortisone 5 mg OD, Testosterone enanthate 250 mg monthly, combination drug of Paracetamol 650 mg and Orphenadrine citrate 50 mg TID, Ondansetron 8 mg TID, Sodium bicarbonate 300 mg TID, Vitamin D3 125 mcg OD. It was concluded that surgical intervention is not required currently and can be considered if signs of optic chiasmal dysfunctional appear. It was also explained to the patient that surgical intervention is not required at the moment; however, he can always opt for one electively. He was thus put on an adequate regimen, including hormone replacement and subsequently discharged.

On regular 2-month follow-ups since discharge, the patient has seemed to be doing well overall. His labs are monitored for electrolyte and hormonal imbalances, which were kept within normal ranges using medications mentioned above. Patient only complained of dry mouth, fatigue and decreased appetite. A follow-up MRI revealed an interval decrease in size of known sellar lesion to 10 × 19 × 9 mm in AP x TS x CC dimension, with improvement of adjacent meningeal enhancement, slight interval increase of peripheral post-contrast enhancement, which could represent normal pituitary gland tissue and normalization of pituitary stalk diameter. No evidence of established territorial infarct, acute intracranial hematoma, or significant mass effect was seen. Next monthly follow-up resulted in similar assessment and plan and now the patient was scheduled for follow-up every 6 months. The latest follow-up was on the 17th month and an MRI was done for comparison. The MRI revealed marginal interval reduction in size of the sellar lesion to 8 × 19 × 7 mm, with persistent peripheral enhancement. Changes suggestive of chronic small vessel ischemia were again noted in the periventricular white matter with no acute infarct or intracranial hemorrhage.

Discussion

Pituitary Apoplexy is a rare life-threatening complication of an otherwise silent condition, pituitary macroadenoma. Although spontaneous apoplexy can occur, identifiable precipitating factors have been recognized too. These include angiography, cardiac surgery, head trauma, anticoagulation, and dynamic hormonal tests [1, 2]. Surgical triggers deserve particular attention as apoplexy has been reported following cardiac surgery, mitral valvuloplasty, and ophthalmologic procedures, such as cataract surgery as well [4–6].

The mechanisms by which surgical procedures precipitate apoplexy are not fully established, but two pathways are proposed. First, surgery-induced systemic hypotension may reduce perfusion to the metabolically demanding adenoma. Second, the HPA axis response to surgical stress, characterized by surges in ACTH and cortisol, may precipitate acute expansion of the adenoma through increased blood flow to an already vascularly compromised tumor [6, 8, 9]. In our patient, chronic hypertension likely caused cumulative microvascular injury within the pre-existing macroadenoma, rendering it susceptible to hemodynamic perturbations. We hypothesize that the physiologic stress of cataract surgery, even under local anesthesia, may have been sufficient to trigger apoplexy in this vulnerable tumor. However, it must be acknowledged that this remains speculative, as a direct causal role of the surgical procedure cannot be established from a single case. The temporal relationship with onset of symptoms within 24 h of surgery is the primary basis for this association. To contextualize the potential vascular vulnerability in our patient, it is worth noting that an observational cohort study by Chen et al. [7] reported an association between self-reported cataract surgery history and increased vascular mortality over 10 years. However, it must be emphasized that this is an associative finding from an epidemiological study and does not establish cataract surgery as causally responsible for vascular events. The authors themselves speculate that shared pathophysiological pathways, such as oxidative stress and crystallin protein dysfunction, may link cataract formation and systemic vascular disease as co-occurring manifestations of the same underlying process, rather than one causing the other. We cite this study not to implicate cataract surgery as a direct vascular hazard, but to suggest that patients undergoing cataract extraction may disproportionately harbor pre-existing vascular comorbidities, including, in rare cases, vulnerable pituitary adenomas, that can manifest under the physiologic stress of any surgical procedure.

Pituitary adenomas are metabolically demanding tumors that paradoxically have a poor intrinsic blood supply, characterized by low angiogenic factor expression and reduced vascular density [8]. This mismatch makes them inherently vulnerable to ischemic injury when perfusion is even transiently compromised. Ischemic infarction and hemorrhage within the adenoma represent distinct but related pathological events, infarction results from vascular insufficiency, while hemorrhage reflects rupture of fragile neovascular channels within the tumor [10, 11]. In our patient, the SWI susceptibility artifact on MRI indicates hemorrhagic transformation, classifying this as hemorrhagic apoplexy. The acute expansion of the sellar contents from hemorrhage explains the sudden-onset severe headache through stretching of pain-sensitive dural structures and raised intracranial pressure, the most common symptom of apoplexy, occurring in approximately 86% of cases [4]. The projectile vomiting similarly reflects acute increases in intracranial pressure. Notably, because the hemorrhage remained contained within the sella without significant suprasellar extension or lateral spread into the cavernous sinuses, there was no compression of the optic chiasm or cranial nerves III, IV, or VI, explaining the absence of visual field defects or ophthalmoplegia in our patient.

As pituitary adenoma is a medical emergency, another crucial step for its management is early diagnosis. Patients can present with variable symptoms, but they usually include headache (86%), visual disturbances (62%), vomiting (40%), ophthalmoplegia (25%), and altered mental status [4]. Our patient presented two positive findings, i.e., headache and projectile vomiting along with hypertension. Moreover, timely neuroimaging is also crucial for confirming diagnosis by guiding emergent neurosurgical intervention. A particularly notable feature of this case was the absence of focal neurological deficits specifically, no ophthalmoplegia, no visual field defect, and no altered consciousness. Patients with pituitary apoplexy commonly present with focal neurologic deficits at the time of diagnosis, thus indicating the severity of damage [1, 12]. The presence of these deficits typically reflects lateral extension into the cavernous sinuses or superior extension causing chiasmal compression, and their absence in our patient is consistent with the imaging finding of a sellar-confined lesion without suprasellar mass effect. From a clinical standpoint, the absence of deficits is a double-edged observation. While it reflects a favorable disease extent and likely contributed to the good outcome with conservative management, it simultaneously made the diagnosis less obvious at initial presentation. This case, therefore, reinforces that pituitary apoplexy should remain in the differential even when the neurological examination is entirely normal, particularly when symptoms are disproportionate, persistent, or arise in a patient with known or suspected vascular risk factors. Early diagnosis also correlates to the better prognosis of our patients who did not require any urgent surgical intervention and showed stabilized state of health on subsequent follow-ups. Endocrinological evaluation is also equally critical, as hypopituitarism, especially acute secondary adrenal insufficiency, can accompany apoplexy and requires immediate corticosteroid replacement [13]. Patients often also present with electrolyte imbalances that need targeted management. Among these, hyponatremia is one of the most frequently encountered conditions, typically caused by a mix of excessive antidiuretic activity and adrenal insufficiency [14].

The decision for conservative management was based on established criteria from UK pituitary apoplexy guidelines [2, 15], which support non-surgical management in the absence of: (1) severe or deteriorating visual acuity, (2) visual field defects, (3) deteriorating level of consciousness, or (4) progressive cranial nerve palsies. Our patient had none of these features as the neurologic examination was intact, visual fields were normal on formal assessment, and he remained alert and oriented throughout. Corticosteroids were administered to address acute secondary adrenal insufficiency and to reduce peri-tumoral edema. IV fluids and electrolyte correction were given supportively. The patient's clinical improvement on this regimen, combined with the follow-up MRI showing interval decrease in lesion size and resolution of meningeal enhancement, validated the conservative approach. Surgical decompression was discussed with the patient as an elective future option should chiasmal compression or hormonal failure develop, in keeping with guideline-directed care.

Long-term follow-up is needed to monitor pituitary hormone deficiencies and potential tumor regrowth, and to guide decisions about therapy. Development of permanent hypopituitarism is commonly seen in patients despite the type of treatment they have received, i.e., conservative or surgical, and it is required to be therapeutically managed throughout the lifetime. The degree of deficit may vary, but occurrence is almost inevitable. Visual defects, on the other hand, improve after treatment [16]. Recurrence of apoplexy remains a rare occurrence [17].

Conclusion

By linking cataract surgery to pituitary apoplexy, emphasis can be laid on the fact that even minor procedures may unmask significant underlying pathology in predisposed individuals. While routine preoperative screening for pituitary tumors in cataract surgery is not practical, awareness of this rare complication may aid early detection and management. Early recognition of apoplexy symptoms can significantly alter the course of management and prognosis. From a broader clinical perspective, this case contributes to the expanding spectrum of surgical triggers for pituitary apoplexy and reiterates the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in postoperative patients presenting with unexplained symptoms or neurological changes. Delayed diagnosis can result in irreversible visual impairment, permanent hypopituitarism, or even death due to adrenal crisis.
