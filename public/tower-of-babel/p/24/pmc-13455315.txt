Bacterial outer membrane vesicle-based biomimetic nitric oxide-releasing nanogenerator for boosting antitumor efficiency of enzyme dynamic therapy via augmented autophagy and immunoactivation
Authors: Xiling Song, Keyan Luo, Yangfeng Du, Linhao Xie, Yinming Chen, Yu Zhang, Tao Wu, Zhigang Zhou, Saiqi Wang, Xiaobing Chen, Jianfu Zhao, Siming Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recently, enzyme dynamic therapy (EDT) has attracted great attention in cancer therapy. Nevertheless, the limited H2O2 concentration in the tumor microenvironment (TME) and the induction of autophagy significantly impair the anticancer efficacy of EDT. Herein, a bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs)-based nitric oxide (NO)-releasing nanogenerator was synthesized to enhance the anticancer efficacy of EDT. Specifically, hollow polydopamine nanospheres (HPDA) were first prepared and used as a carrier to load lactate oxidase (LOX), the NO-releasing donor BNN6 and chloroperoxidase (CPO), followed by surface coating with OMVs to obtain the biomimetic nanogenerator HPDA@LBC@OMV. Due to the excellent tumor-targeting ability of OMVs, HPDA@LBC@OMV displayed highly efficient accumulation in 4T1 tumor tissues. In the TME, HPDA@LBC@OMV firstly catalyzed lactic acid to produce a substantial amount of H2O2, which subsequently triggered the simultaneous generation of NO and singlet oxygen (1O2) through cascade catalytic reactions, ultimately resulting in significant inhibition of 4T1 tumors. Mechanistic studies revealed that the synergistic NO/1O2 anticancer effect inhibited 4T1 tumors by inducing excessive autophagy. More importantly, it was demonstrated that the synergistic effect-induced excessive autophagy effectively triggered immunogenic cell death (ICD) in dying tumor cells, thereby further activating the body’s immune response by converting “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors to enhance anticancer immunotherapy.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04604-x.

Introduction

Recently, enzyme-dynamic therapy (EDT) by mimicking the natural neutrophil functions has emerged as a novel way for cancer therapy [1, 2–3]. In the natural killing process, neutrophil firstly engulfs the pathogens to form phagosomes, followed by fusing with cytoplasmic granules to release myeloperoxidase (MPO), which subsequently generates a substantial amount of reactive oxygen species (ROS) through cascade reactions to realize the killing functions [4]. Specifically, MPO can firstly catalyze H2O2 into hypochlorous acid (HClO) in the presence of halide ion (e.g. Cl−) and subsequently transforms to singlet oxygen (1O2) for EDT [5]. Chloroperoxidase (CPO) is an analogue enzyme of MPO with similar enzymatic catalytic functions showing better stability and biocompatibility [6]. Recent work including ours has demonstrated the promising use of EDT in treating many types of cancers such as hepatocellular carcinoma, breast carcinoma, glioblastoma and osteosarcoma [1, 2–3, 7]. Nevertheless, EDT in anticancer application still faces many challenges. The first challenge is the limited H2O2 concentration in the tumor microenvironment (TME) [8], which directly restricts the1O2 generation efficiency of EDT. Secondly, 1O2 inhibits cancer cell activity mainly through the mitochondrion-mediated apoptotic pathway, while the induction of autophagy/mitophagy greatly impairs the anticancer efficiency of 1O2 [9]. Consequently, EDT is not able to effectively activate the body’s immune system, resulting in insufficient efficacy of antitumor immunotherapy.

Currently, two delivery strategies have been developed to increase the H2O2 concentration in TME, including delivering natural enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), glucose oxidase (GOX), lactate oxidase (LOX), etc. or metal peroxide-based nanozyme with H2O2-self supply property [3, 6, 11] In the previous work of Wang’s group, SOD and CPO enzymes were co-delivered in a nanogel system for enhancing the antitumor effect of EDT on hepatocellular carcinoma [6]. In TME, SOD served as a H2O2-self supply source by converting superoxide anion (O2•−) to H2O2, thereby enhancing the [1] O2 generation level produced via the CPO-mediated enzymatic catalytic reaction. In a recent work of Zhang’s group, GOX and CPO were co-loaded in zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) by preparing the artificial “super neutrophils”, where GOX catalyzed intratumor glucose to supply H2O2 for enhancing the breast carcinoma inhibition effect of the CPO-mediated EDT [10]. In addition, lactic acid (LA) is also overexpressed in TME, and LA accumulation can result in the tumor cell proliferation, metastasis and immunosuppression [12, 13]. In the presence of LOX, LA in TME can be catalyzed and consumed with the production of pyruvate and H2O2. Because LA is a byproduct of tumor glycolysis, the LOX-based H2O2-self supplying strategy offers greater promise than those relying on SOD and GOX, owing to its non-oxygen dependent nature. As a consequence, many recent studies jointly delivered LOX and CPO to enhance the antitumor effect of EDT [2, 14. However, construction of an efficient and stable dual-enzyme delivery system still poses significant challenges. Although various metal peroxide nanozymes, such as CaO2 and CuO2, have been developed as H2O2-self supply sources, their widespread application in translational medicine is significantly limited by drawbacks including burst-like H2O2 generation and potential toxicity due to excessive release of metal ions.

Autophagy is a cell process transporting damaged organelles (e.g. mitochondria) to lysosomes for degradation, which plays a protective role for cancer cells by maintaining mitochondrial function and reducing DNA damage [15]. Autophagy has been considered as one of the main reasons for causing the failure of chemotherapy and many ROS-based cancer therapies (e.g. photodynamic therapy, radiotherapy) [16]. Normally, autophagy inhibitors like chloroquine (CQ) and 3-methyladenine (3-MA) are used for enhancing the anticancer efficacy of the aforementioned therapeutic ways [17]. However, those inhibitors display drawbacks such as rapid clearance, low delivery efficiency, obvious side effects and reduced antigen presentation [16, 18]. In this context, researchers have proposed that enhancing autophagy could be also a promising strategy to improve the anticancer efficacy of the ROS-based cancer therapies. As a proof of concept, Yin et al. prepared a multifunctional porphyrinic metal-organic framework nanoplatform (MnO2@CPCN) composed of MnO2-coated porphyrinic PCN-224 and the loaded carbonyl cyanide 3-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP, a type of mitophagy inducer) [18]. It demonstrated that the laser-induced 1O2 and CCCP release of MnO2@CPCN synergistically caused excessive autophagy/mitophagy, which subsequently resulted in complete elimination of primary tumors and inhibition of tumor recurrence and lung metastasis. Moreover, excessive autophagy/mitophagy was reported to be able to activate immunogenic death of tumor cells, thereby further enhancing the efficacy of anti-tumor immunotherapy. Nevertheless, effective strategies to induce excessive autophagy are still lacking.

Recently, gas therapy (GT) has gained enormous attraction in cancer therapy [19]. Among those active gases, nitric oxide (NO) is the most popular one that has been widely explored [20, 21–22]. Previous work has indicated that high concentrations of NO (> 1 µM) can induce cancer cell apoptosis through multiple pathways, including p53 activation, mitochondrial dysfunction, cell cycle arrest, DNA damage and inhibition of antiapoptotic proteasome expression [23]. Moreover, recent work has demonstrated that NO is also an excellent sensitizer for enhancing the anticancer effect of many other therapeutic modalities, such as chemodynamic therapy (CDT) [24–26], X-ray dynamic therapy (XDT) [27], etc. Our recent work revealed that NO can synergize with superoxide anions (O2ꞏ−) to enhance mitochondrial damage, thereby further enhancing the tumor-inhibition effect of O2ꞏ− through activation of the mitochondrion-mediated apoptotic pathway [28]. However, to the best of our knowledge, whether NO can synergize with EDT to enhance its anticancer efficacy and what the underlying mechanism might be remain completely unknown.

Herein, we hypothesized that NO may act as a mitophagy inducer to synergize the anticancer efficiency of EDT by inducing the excessive mitophagy. For this purpose, a multifunctional nanoplatform integrating both NO-based GT and EDT was designed and fabricated for enhancing anticancer therapy. Due to the hollow structure, excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, hollow polydopamine nanospheres (HPDA) were prepared and served as the carrier for loading of LOX, NO-releasing donor BNN6 and CPO to obtain nanogenerator HPDA@LBC. Recent work has proved that bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) have the preference to accumulate in tumors either via the hypoxic-targeting effect or by “hitchhiking circulating neutrophils” [29]. Subsequently, OMVs were functionalized on the surface of the nanogenerator to increase its stability and tumor-targeting ability, obtaining the biomimetic nanogenerator HPDA@LBC@OMV. Both in vitro and in vivo evaluations demonstrated that HPDA@LBC@OMV could highly efficiently target 4T1 tumors and accumulate in tumor tissues. In the TME, HPDA@LBC@OMV firstly catalyzed LA to produce a substantial amount of H2O2, which subsequently triggered the simultaneous generation of NO and 1O2 through cascade catalytic reactions, ultimately resulting in significant inhibition of 4T1 tumors due to the synergistic NO/1O2 anticancer effect. Mechanistic study revealed that the synergistic NO/1O2 anticancer effect inhibited 4T1 tumors by inducing excessive autophagy, thereby enhancing mitochondria-mediated apoptosis of tumor cells. More importantly, it was demonstrated that excessive autophagy significantly increased the immunogenicity of dying tumor cells to promote the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which subsequently triggered dendritic cells (DCs) maturation, increased cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) activation and facilitated M1-type macrophage polarization. As a result, the “cold” tumors were reversed to “hot” tumors to further enhance the antitumor immunotherapy efficacy. Taken together, HPDA@LBC@OMV nanogenerator displayed advantages of high biocompatibility, CPO loading efficiency and 1O2 generation efficiency, enhanced anticancer and immune activation efficiency, in comparison with the recently reported delivery nanosystems for EDT. The findings of our work provide a novel strategy to enhance the anticancer efficiency of EDT.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of the biomimetic HPDA@LBC@OMV

In the present work, hollow spherical polydopamine nanoparticles (HPDA) with core diameter at approximate 160 nm were synthesized (Fig. 1A) and used as the carrier for loading of H2O2 self-supplied enzyme of lactate oxidase (LOX), NO releasing donor BNN6 and 1O2-generating enzyme of chloroperoxidase (CPO), obtaining nanozyme of HPDA@LBC. To confer tumor-targeting ability to the nanosystem, OMVs were further functionalized on its surface, leading to the preparation of a biomimetic nanogenerator HPDA@LBC@OMV (Scheme 1A and Fig. 1B). Firstly, the loading capacity of LOX, CPO and BNN6 in HPDA was evaluated by UV-Vis spectrum. As it was shown, UV-Vis spectra of HPDA@LOX, HPDA@CPO and HPDA@BNN6 displayed typical absorption peak of LOX, CPO and BNN6 at 260 nm, 410 nm and 226 nm, respectively (Figure S1), suggesting LOX, CPO and BNN6 could be effectively loaded in HPDA. Then, sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) measurement was conducted for HPDA@LC characterization. As it was shown, LOX and CPO displayed typical protein band at 40 kDa and 50 kDa, respectively, while HPDA showed negligible protein band (Fig. 1C). Notably, obvious protein bands at both 40 kDa and 50 kDa were also observed for HPDA@LC (Fig. 1C), demonstrating the successful loading of LOX and CPO. It was calculated that the LOX and CPO loading efficiency in HPDA was 50% and 73.4%, respectively (Figure S2). Based on the BNN6 standard curve, the BNN6 loading efficiency was determined to be 80.6% (Figure S3). Later on, TEM, SDS-PAGE and UV-Vis measurements were performed for evaluating OMV functionalization. TEM image showed the thin layer of OMV membrane coating on the surface of the nanogenerator (Fig. 1B). SDS-PAGE result showed that all characteristic protein bands of OMV were clearly present in HPDA@OMV (Fig. 1D). UV-Vis spectrum of HPDA@OMV also displayed the characteristic peak of OMV (Figure S4). All these indicated that OMV can be effectively functionalized on the surface of HPDA. Lastly, UV-Vis and elemental mapping were conducted for HPDA@LBC@OMV characterization. In the UV-Vis spectrum of HPDA@LBC@OMV, typical bands of LOX, BNN6, CPO and OMV were all observed, although the absorbance wavelengths slightly shifted (Fig. 1F). Elemental mapping images exhibited that the key elements of C, N, O, P, S representing the presence of HPDA, LOX, BNN6, CPO and OMV were all clearly seen (Fig. 1E). All these well demonstrated the successful preparation of HPDA@LBC@OMV. Dynamic light scattering measurements exhibited that the hydrodynamic diameter of the nanosystem increased gradually from the pristine 180 nm to approximately 295 nm after substances loading and functionalization (Fig. 1G). Zeta potential of the nanosystem also gradually decreased from – 10.2 mV (HPDA) to – 22.9 mV (HPDA@LBC) and finally to – 30.2 mV (HPDA@LBC@OMV) (Fig. 1H), suggesting the high stability of the nanogenerator. Subsequently, the stability of HPDA@LBC@OMV in different biological media of water, PBS, and 10% FBS-containing RPMI-1640 medium was evaluated. Enzyme cascade catalytic NO and 1O2-releasing property of the biomimetic HPDA@LBC@OMV

As follows, the enzyme cascade catalytic activities of HPDA@LBC@OMV were investigated. The enzyme cascade catalytic reactions started with LOX, which can effectively catalyze lactic acid (LA) to produce H2O2. Firstly, the LA-depletion effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV was studied using the specific LA assay kit. As shown in Fig. 2B, a time-dependent LA-depletion curve was observed in the presence of HPDA@LOX@OMV, suggesting LA was gradually consumed by the specific enzyme catalytic activity of LOX. Similar trend was also observed for HPDA@LBC@OMV (Fig. 2B). Then, the LOX-catalyzed H2O2-generating activity of HPDA@LBC@OMV was tested by using KMnO4 reduction assay. In the presence of H2O2, KMnO4 was reduced by causing the disappearance of typical absorbance peaks ranging from 450 nm to 600 nm (Fig. 2C). The HPDA@LOX@OMV + LA and HPDA@LBC@OMV + LA treatments resulted in significant decrease of the absorbance peaks, while HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment in the absence of LA did not cause any change in those peaks, suggesting HPDA@LOX@OMV and HPDA@LBC@OMV effectively catalyzed LA in producing H2O2.

CPO is a type of peroxidase with enzyme catalytic activity by catalyzing H2O2 into 1O2. At the same time, our recent work indicated that H2O2 is an excellent inducer to trigger the cleavage of BNN6 by releasing NO [21, 30]. As a result, HPDA@LBC@OMV is expected to simultaneously generate NO and 1O2 through cascade catalytic reactions. Firstly, the NO-generating property of HPDA@LBC@OMV was evaluated using the Griess assay. As it was shown, obvious UV-Vis absorbance peak at 540 nm appeared for HPDA@LBC@OMV with H2O2 addition, while no absorbance peak was found for HPDA@LBC@OMV in the absence of H2O2 (Fig. 2D), suggesting that H2O2 effectively triggered NO release from the nanogenerator. Similarly, the HPDA@LBC@OMV + LA treatment resulted in the appearance of a typical absorbance peak at 540 nm, confirming the cascade catalytic NO-generating ability of HPDA@LBC@OMV. Moreover, it was observed that the NO releasing level of HPDA@LBC@OMV increased gradually along with time in the presence of LA (Fig. 2E), indicating the long-lasting NO generation property of the nanogenerator.

Next, the enzyme cascade catalytic 1O2-generating property of HPDA@LBC@OMV was investigated using the SOSG fluorescent probe. In the presence of H2O2, a strong fluorescent peak at 530 nm was observed for HPDA@LBC@OMV, while low fluorescent intensity was found for that without H2O2 addition (Fig. 2F), indicating H2O2 is an important catalytic substrate for 1O2 generation. Consistently, a strong fluorescent peak at 530 nm was also observed for HPDA@LBC@OMV + LA treatment, indicating 1O2 can be generated via the cascade catalytic reactions of LOX and CPO. Furthermore, electron paramagnetic resonance (ESR) measurement was also performed for 1O2 detection, where 2, 2, 6, 6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) was used as the trapping agent. In line with the above results, both the HPDA@LOX@OMV + H2O2 and HPDA@LBC@OMV + LA treatments led to the emergence of the typical triplet signals of the 1O2/TEMP adduct with a relative intensity of 1:1:1 (Fig. 2G), confirming the enzyme catalytic 1O2-generating property of HPDA@LBC@OMV. In addition, the enzymatic activity of LOX and CPO in HPDA@LBC@OMV was evaluated. Results showed that LOX and CPO in HPDA@LBC@OMV retained about 91% of the enzymatic activity of their corresponding free enzymes (Fig. 2H and I).

Taken together, we demonstrated that HPDA@LBC@OMV is a multifunctional nanogenerator capable of self-supplying H2O2 and simultaneously generating NO and 1O2 through cascade catalytic reactions (Fig. 2A), showing significant potential for synergistic NO/1O2 anticancer therapy.

Tumor-targeting ability of biomimetic HPDA@LBC@OMV

Recent work indicated that OMV possesses specific targeting ability towards hypoxic tumors [29]. Herein, the in vitro and in vivo targeting ability of HPDA@LBC@OMV toward 4T1 cells and tumors was evaluated by using 1,1′dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide (DIR) as a red fluorescent dye. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images showed that strong red fluorescence could be observed in 4T1 cells incubated with OMV-DIR and HPDA@LBC@OMV-DIR, while only weak red fluorescence was seen in 4T1 cells treated with HPDA@LBC-DIR (Fig. 3A). The results suggested that OMV functionalization could enhance the uptake efficiency of HPDA@LBC@OMV by 4T1 cells. In addition, none of HPDA@LBC-DIR, OMV-DIR and HPDA@LBC@OMV-DIR could be efficiently taken up by MCF-10 A cells (Fig. 3A), implying that OMV only specifically targets 4T1 cancer cells. Flow cytometry data displayed that HPDA@LBC@OMV-DIR treatment led to rapid increase in fluorescent intensity of 4T1 cells along with time, where 4 h incubation resulted in 95% cellular uptake efficiency (Fig. 3B and D). In contrast, only slight increase in fluorescent intensity was found for MCF-10 A cells with HPDA@LBC@OMV-DIR treatment, where the cellular uptake efficiency only reached 16% after 6 h incubation (Fig. 3C and D). The results further confirmed the specific 4T1 cancer cell-targeting ability of HPDA@LBC@OMV. Later on, the in vivo 4T1 tumor-targeting ability of HPDA@LBC@OMV was investigated by performing the in vivo fluorescence imaging assay. Results showed that intravenous injection of OMV-DIR and HPDA@LBC@OMV resulted in rapid increase in the fluorescent intensity of tumor tissues in the initial 12 h, reaching a peak value at 12 h post injection, followed by gradually decreasing in the following 24 h (Fig. 3E and F). Notably, HPDA@LBC-DIR displayed low targeting ability towards 4T1 tumors, as only slight increase in the fluorescent intensity of tumors was seen after 36 h injection (Fig. 3E and F). Subsequently, the ex vivo organs of mice after 24 h injection were collected and detected by fluorescence imaging. Obviously, OMV-DIR treatment resulted in the highest fluorescent intensity in the liver of mice, with the second-highest intensity detected in tumor tissues (Fig. 3G and I). Interestingly, HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment led to the highest material accumulation in tumor tissues, while only a small portion of the materials accumulated in the liver of mice. On the contrary, very low material accumulation was visualized in tumor tissues with HPDA@LBC treatment (Fig. 3G and I). For a better comparison, a relative tumor accumulation ratio of R[tumor/liver] calculated by dividing the fluorescent intensity of tumor by the fluorescent intensity of liver was shown in Fig. 3H. Remarkably, HPDA@LBC@OMV-DIR treated group displayed the highest R[tumor/liver] value among all the treating groups, further confirming the excellent 4T1 tumor-targeting ability of HPDA@LBC@OMV.

The in vitro synergistic NO/1O2 anticancer efficiency of the biomimetic HPDA@LBC@OMV

Prior to in vitro anticancer evaluation, the biocompatibility of HPDA@LBC@OMV was assessed using MCF-10 A cells, a well-established normal human breast epithelial cell line. Figure 4A displayed that the cell viability after HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment was higher than 85% in all the concentrations tested, suggesting the non-toxic effect of the nanogenerator on normal cells. Then, the anticancer effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV on the mouse breast cancer cell line of 4T1 cells was investigated. As it was shown, HPDA@OMV exhibited no inhibitory effect on 4T1 cells (Fig. 4B). In contrast, HPDA@LB@OMV and HPDA@LC@OMV treatment caused an obvious inhibitory effect on 4T1 cells, which was attributed to the anticancer effect of NO and 1O2, respectively. Notably, due to the synergistic NO/1O2 anticancer effect, HPDA@LBC@OMV displayed the most significant inhibitory effect on 4T1 cells by leading to the lowest cell viability. Moreover, we demonstrated that OMV functionalization could further enhance the anticancer efficacy of the nanogenerator, as a significant difference in cell viability was observed between the HPDA@LBC and HPDA@LBC@OMV-treated groups. Calcein-AM/PI dual-staining assay was also conducted to evaluate the anticancer effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV. In line with the above results, HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment yielded the highest red/green fluorescence ratio (Fig. 4D and Figure S6), further demonstrating the robust anticancer activity of the synergistic NO/1O2 effect. To verify the intracellular cascade catalytic property of HPDA@LBC@OMV for the simultaneous generation of H2O2, NO and 1O2, fluorescent probes ROS Green H2O2, DAF-FM DA and SOSG were employed. CLSM images showed that a substantial amount of H2O2 was produced in 4T1 cells upon treatment of LOX-containing materials, demonstrating their intracellular H2O2-generating ability (Fig. 4C and E). As expected, obvious NO release was observed in 4T1 cells treated with both LOX and BNN6-containing materials (Fig. 4 C and F), and obvious 1O2 generation was found in 4T1 cells treated with both LOX and CPO-containing materials (Fig. 4C and G). In summary, the results demonstrated that the cascade catalytic reactions of HPDA@LBC@OMV could occur in the cancer cell microenvironment by triggering simultaneous intracellular NO and 1O2 generation.

The synergistic NO/1O2 anticancer mechanism of biomimetic HPDA@LBC@OMV

Next, the synergistic NO/1O2 anticancer mechanism of HPDA@LBC@OMV was investigated. Previous research indicated that mitochondrion is the main target of 1O2 [31]. Herein, we hypothesized that NO was able to synergize the mitochondrial damage effect of 1O2. For this purpose, mitochondrial membrane integrity of 4T1 cells with different treatments was detected using the JC-1 fluorescent assay kit. It clearly showed that individual 1O2 treatment (HPDA@LC@OMV) obviously reduced the red fluorescence (JC-1 aggregates) ratio (Fig. 5A and Figure S7), demonstrating the mitochondrial membrane damage effect of 1O2. Similarly, individual NO treatment (HPDA@LB@OMV) also led to obvious decrease in red fluorescence ratio, suggesting NO could also induce mitochondrial membrane damage. Remarkably, HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment nearly caused the disappearance of red fluorescence by showing the least red/green fluorescence ratio (Fig. 5A and Figure S7), indicating that the synergistic NO and 1O2 effect induced severe mitochondrial membrane damage. Mitochondrial membrane damage can induce the cytochrome c leakage [31]. Then, the cytochrome c release level of 4T1 cells after treatments were detected by performing the immunofluorescence analysis. It showed that individual NO treatment (HPDA@LB@OMV) induced partial cytochrome c release, while individual 1O2 (HPDA@LC@OMV) treatment induced obvious cytochrome c release (Fig. 5B and Figure S8). Notably, the synergistic NO/1O2 effect (HPDA@LBC@OMV) resulted in the highest leakage level of cytochrome c. Mitochondrial damage can initiate the mitochondrion-mediated apoptotic pathway. Thereafter, the apoptotic effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV was evaluated using the Annexin V-PE/7AAD dual fluorescence staining assay. Flow cytometry results showed that individual NO (HPDA@LB@OMV) and 1O2 treatment (HPDA@LC@OMV) induced 28.45% and 37.99% apoptotic cells (Fig. 5C and Figure S9), respectively. Remarkably, HPDA@LBC@OMV induced the highest apoptotic cell ratio of 52.77% due to the synergistic NO/1O2 anticancer effect (Fig. 5C and Figure S9). Furthermore, the expression levels of apoptosis-related proteins were detected by performing western blot experiments. The results showed that the expression levels of pro-apoptotic proteins of Bax, Caspase-3 and Caspase-9 were all significantly upregulated by the HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment, while the expression level of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 was significantly downregulated (Fig. 5D-5G), further confirming the apoptosis-inducing effect of the nanogenerator. Since the mitochondrion is the key energy-producing organelle, mitochondrial damage can induce energy metabolism disorder by reducing ATP production and increasing intracellular ROS levels. Hence, the intracellular ATP generation level was detected using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. In line with the above results, the synergistic NO/1O2 effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV significantly suppressed intracellular ATP generation (Fig. 5H). Then, the intracellular ROS level of 4T1 cells was detected using the 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) probe. CLSM images showed that HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment induced the highest level of ROS generation (Fig. 5I and Figure S10). ROS elevation can deplete the intracellular GSH and induce DNA damage through the oxidative stress effect. Intracellular GSH levels were detected using the specific ThiolTraceTM Violet 500 fluorescent probe. As it was shown, HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment almost resulted in the disappearance of green fluorescence in 4T1 cells (Fig. 5J and Figure S11), suggesting intracellular GSH was significantly depleted by the synergistic NO/1O2 effect. Immunofluorescent images indicated that cellular DNA was severely destroyed by the synergistic NO/1O2 effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV, as the strongest green fluorescence was visualized in HPDA@LBC@OMV treated cells (Fig. 5K).

The autophagy regulation effect of biomimetic HPDA@LBC@OMV

Mitochondrial damage can trigger protective autophagy in cancer cells, where the damaged mitochondria are degraded by acidic lysosomes, thereby suppressing the subsequent apoptosis to safeguard cancer cells [32]. Previous studies have demonstrated that moderate autophagy protects cancer cells, whereas excessive autophagy inhibits cancer cell survival through a “self-killing” mechanism [33]. Therefore, the autophagy regulation effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV was investigated. Firstly, the phagocytosis of damaged mitochondria by autolysosomes was investigated by performing CLSM assay, with mitochondria and autolysosomes fluorescently labeled by MitoTracker Green and LysoTracker Red, respectively, followed by colocalization analysis. As it was shown, individual NO (HPDA@LB@OMV) and 1O2 treatment (HPDA@LC@OMV) led to partial colocalization of green and red fluorescence (Fig. 6A, B and F and Figure S12), indicating the autophagy-inducing effects of NO and 1O2. Remarkably, HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment resulted in the highest colocalization of green and red fluorescence, suggesting that NO and 1O2 synergistically promoted autophagy (Fig. 6A, B and F and Figure S12). To further confirm it, expression of autophagy-related LC3 protein in 4T1 cells after treatments was also detected by performing immunofluorescence analysis. As expected, HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment induced the highest green fluorescent intensity (Fig. 6C and G), indicating that the synergistic NO/1O2 effect significantly upregulated LC3 expression in the autophagy process. Moreover, western blot assay was also performed to investigate the expression levels of autophagy-related proteins. In the autophagy process, LC3 in the cytoplasm can be converted from the cytosolic form LC3-I to the membrane-bound form LC3-II [34]. In the meantime, p62 is another biomarker of autophagy, as it can deliver various ubiquitinated proteins bound to its ubiquitin-associated (UBA) domain to the autophagosome, ultimately leading to their degradation by the lysosome [35]. Western blot results showed that the LC3-II expression level was significantly upregulated by the HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment (Fig. 6D and H), while p62 expression was significantly downregulated (Fig. 6D and I), suggesting that excessive autophagy was induced by the synergistic NO/1O2 effect. In addition, PINK1 and Parkin proteins also play important roles in regulating mitochondrial autophagy, where PINK1 can induce recruitment of Parkin in mitochondria and activate the subsequent autophagy of the damaged mitochondria [36]. Herein, the expression levels of PINK1 and Parkin proteins were also evaluated by western blot analysis. Obviously, HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment resulted in the highest expression levels of both PINK1 and Parkin (Fig. 6E, J and K), further confirming that NO and 1O2 could synergistically promote mitochondrial autophagy. Taken together, we demonstrated that the synergistic NO/1O2 anticancer effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV inhibited 4T1 cells by inducing excessive autophagy (Scheme 1B).

The in vitro immunoactivation effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV

Immunogenic cell death (ICD) is a special type of cell death which can cause the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including calreticulin (CRT) exposure on the cell surface, high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) proteins release and ATP secretion. The released DAMPs will activate the dendritic cells (DCs) to enhance antigen presentation and stimulate the body’s immune response for anticancer immunotherapy [37]. Herein, the immunostimulatory effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV was investigated. Firstly, the ICD-inducing ability of HPDA@LBC@OMV was studied by detecting the levels of CRT exposure, HMGB1 proteins release and ATP secretion in 4T1 cells after treatments. CLSM images exhibited that HPDA@OMV treatment induced obvious CRT exposure (Fig. 7A and E), which was attributed to the ICD-inducing effect of the functionalized OMV [38, 39]. Individual treatment with NO (HPDA@LB@OMV) or 1O2 (HPDA@LC@OMV) led to increased levels of CRT exposure, while the combined NO/1O2 treatment (HPDA@LBC@OMV) produced a synergistic effect, resulting in the highest level of CRT exposure observed (Fig. 7A and E). In the meantime, HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment significantly reduced the intracellular HMGB1 levels (Fig. 7B and F), suggesting that the synergistic NO/1O2 effect effectively promoted HMGB1 release. Similarly, HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment induced the highest level of ATP secretion (Fig. 7G). Collectively, we demonstrated that HPDA@LBC@OMV was an effective ICD inducer.

Subsequently, the immunostimulatory effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV was investigated by evaluating its capability in promoting bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) maturation. In this assay, expression levels of CD80 and CD86, biomarkers of mature DCs, were detected by flow cytometry. Following the similar trends as above, HPDA@OMV induced obvious increase in CD80+CD86+ percentage, individual treatment with NO (HPDA@LB@OMV) or 1O2 (HPDA@LC@OMV) induced higher CD80+CD86+ percentages (Fig. 7C, H and I and Figure S13). Notably, HPDA@LBC@OMV induced the highest percentage of mature DCs, suggesting its excellent DC activation ability. In addition, inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6 and IL-4 expressed by DCs after treatment were detected using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. It showed that expression levels of proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IFN-γ and IL-6 were significantly increased upon HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment, while expression of anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-4 was significantly reduced (Figure S14), further confirming DCs were successfully activated by HPDA@LBC@OMV [40]. Moreover, the M1-type macrophage repolarization effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV was evaluated by flow cytometry, with LPS treatment serving as the positive control. Flow cytometry data showed that M1-type macrophage percentage was low (15.67%) in the negative PBS control group but high (52.88%) in the positive LPS-treated group (Fig. 7D and J). Upon HPDA@OMV, HPDA@LB@OMV or HPDA@LC@OMV treatment, M1-type macrophage percentage increased obviously to 42.35%, 51.06% and 57.17%, respectively (Fig. 7D and J), indicating the effective activity of OMV, NO and 1O2 in repolarizing M1-type macrophage. Remarkably, HPDA@LBC@OMV induced the highest M1-type macrophage percentage (70.62%), which was much higher than that of the LPS positive control (Fig. 7D and J), demonstrating the robust M1-type macrophage repolarization capability of HPDA@LBC@OMV.

The in vivo synergistic NO/1O2 antitumor efficiency of HPDA@LBC@OMV

Inspired by the excellent in vitro anticancer activity, the in vivo antitumor effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV on 4T1 tumor-bearing mice was investigated (Fig. 8A). Firstly, intratumoral NO and 1O2 generation was detected. Figure 8B and C showed that high levels of both NO and 1O2 were generated in mice tumor tissues with HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment, demonstrating the in vivo cascade catalytic generating NO and 1O2 property of HPDA@LBC@OMV. Then, the tumor inhibitory effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV was evaluated by monitoring changes in tumor volume, size and weight. Tumor growth curves showed that HPDA@OMV (b) slightly inhibited 4T1 tumor growth due to its moderate immunostimulatory effect (Fig. 8D and H). HPDA@LB@OMV (c) and HPDA@LC@OMV (d) exhibited obvious inhibition on 4T1 tumor growth due to the antitumor effect of NO and 1O2, respectively. Remarkably, HPDA@LBC@OMV (f) treatment exerted the most significant tumor-inhibition effect by resulting in the smallest tumor size and lightest tumor weight (Fig. 8D, E, F and H), which was attributed to the synergistic NO/1O2 antitumor effect. In addition, we found that HPDA@LBC@OMV (g) treatment by tail vein injection also exhibited significant tumor-inhibition effect, displaying the second slowest tumor growth rate and smallest tumor weight among all the treated groups (Fig. 8D, E, F and H). The result further confirmed the high tumor-targeting ability of HPDA@LBC@OMV. The excellent antitumor effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV was also confirmed by Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining histological analysis, as a significant number of apoptotic cells were observed in the H&E-stained histological images with HPDA@LBC@OMV (f, g) treatments (Fig. 8I). Furthermore, the in vivo antitumor mechanism of HPDA@LBC@OMV was investigated. Intratumoral ROS detection results showed that HPDA@LBC@OMV (f) induced substantial amount of ROS generation in tumor tissues (Fig. 8K and Figure S15). Immunohistochemical images showed that pro-apoptotic protein Caspase-3 was highly expressed in tumors after HPDA@LBC@OMV (f) treatment (Fig. 8J and Figure S16). Immunofluorescence image results showed HPDA@LBC@OMV (f) treatment induced severe DNA damage of tumor cells (Fig. 8L and Figure S17), as well as high expression of LC3 proteins (Fig. 8G and M). Taken together, these results further confirmed that the synergistic NO/1O2 antitumor effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV inhibited tumors through mitochondria-mediated apoptotic pathway, thereby inducing excessive autophagy.

The in vivo immunostimulatory effect of HPDA@LBC@OMV was also investigated. Immunofluorescence analysis data showed that CRT exposure in tumor tissue was significantly enhanced by the HPDA@LBC@OMV treatment (Fig. 9A and F), suggesting its effective in vivo ICD-inducing efficacy. The in vivo biocompatibility of HPDA@LBC@OMV

The in vivo biocompatibility of HPDA@LBC@OMV was investigated to ensure its safe biomedical applications. Firstly, the histocompatibility of HPDA@LBC@OMV was evaluated by performing immunohistochemical analysis, where mice’s main organs of heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney were collected at the end of treatments and the corresponding histological sections were stained by H&E dyes. In the H&E-stained histological images (Figure S18), no obvious pathological lesions could be observed in all the organs studied, suggesting the excellent histocompatibility of HPDA@LBC@OMV. Moreover, mice’s blood serum was also collected and analyzed for evaluating the blood compatibility of HPDA@LBC@OMV. It displayed that all the blood indicators were located in the normal range (Figure S19), suggesting the excellent blood compatibility of HPDA@LBC@OMV. Besides, mice’s weights during the whole treatment period were also monitored. The results showed that all mice maintained their body weight within a relatively stable range throughout the treatment period (Figure S20), indicating the favorable biosafety profile of HPDA@LBC@OMV. Collectively, we demonstrated that HPDA@LBC@OMV is a biocompatible material with promising potential for safe biomedical applications.

Conclusion

As a reactive gas molecule, NO plays an important role in synergistically enhancing antitumor efficacy. In the present work, we demonstrated that NO acted as an excellent sensitizing molecule that potentiated the anticancer efficiency of EDT. Specifically, NO amplified 1O2-induced autophagy, leading to excessive autophagy that subsequently enhanced mitochondria-mediated apoptosis of tumor cells. More importantly, excessive autophagy was found to effectively trigger ICD in dying tumor cells, which in turn facilitated DC maturation, increased cytotoxic T lymphocyte activation, and promoted macrophage repolarization towards M1 phenotype, thereby enhancing the efficacy of antitumor immunotherapy. As a proof of concept, we designed an OMVs-based biomimetic nanogenerator HPDA@LBC@OMV capable of simultaneously generating NO and 1O2 through cascade catalytic reactions in TME. The HPDA@LBC@OMV nanogenerator exhibited excellent anticancer efficacy both in vitro and in vivo through the synergistic NO/1O2 effect.
