“Hantavirus at sea: Why cruise ships must be ready for animal-borne disease.”
Authors: Nasro Salad Ahmed, Fardowso Hassan Mohamud, Fatima Abdijabbar Ibrahim, Anisa Hassan Elmi, Yakub Burhan Abdullahi
Published: 2026

Dear Editor

The suspected cluster of hantavirus infections on a cruise ship, widely reported in the media, should not be dismissed as a curiosity of maritime travel; it is a stark warning [1,2]. This incident lays bare a structural flaw in global maritime health preparedness: cruise ships are engineered to manage predictable viral threats, such as norovirus and COVID-19, yet remain dangerously exposed to high-fatality, environmentally transmitted zoonoses sustained by rodent reservoirs [3]. Treating this event as an outlier misses its real significance: it is an early stress test of a system unready for the next wave of rodent-borne disease at sea [1,4]. Hantaviruses are among the most pervasive rodent-borne zoonoses worldwide, with an estimated 150,000–200,000 human infections per year. The case fatality rates for hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) and hantavirus pulmonary/cardiopulmonary syndromes can reach up to 50% in severe cases. Transmission is typically through the inhalation of aerosols contaminated with rodent urine, droppings, or saliva in enclosed or poorly ventilated spaces [1,5]. Climate change, intensified rainfall, and flooding are increasing rodent population densities and expanding habitats, tightly coupling hantavirus risk to environmental and ecological disruptions [1,4]. No widely effective antiviral treatment exists, vaccines are limited in availability and uncertain in effectiveness, and management depends largely on early recognition and intensive supportive care [2,5]. In contrast, the evidence base guiding infectious disease prevention, mitigation, and management on passenger ships overwhelmingly concentrates on respiratory and gastrointestinal infections. A recent scoping review of 620 publications on passenger ships and ports found that about half concerned respiratory illnesses and 11.5% gastroenteritis, while vector-borne and other “non-routine” infections occupied only a marginal share; rodent-borne pathogens were essentially absent from this literature. Guidance, drills, and infrastructure have thus been optimized to repeat the response to COVID-19 and norovirus, not to anticipate rare but catastrophic rodent-associated threats [3]. Port and seaport studies clarify the risks of this omission for cruise operations [4]. Surveillance in African seaports has highlighted that imported rats can introduce and sustain the circulation of Seoul ortho hantavirus (SEOV), with strengthened rodent surveillance and rapid elimination in port areas explicitly recommended to prevent human outbreaks. In Hainan, China, 11.7% of rats trapped around major seaports were hantavirus-positive, and phylogenetic analysis suggested that the introduction and spread were facilitated by maritime trade and waterborne transport of infected rodents and cargo. The authors warned that convenient water transportation and high rodent abundance may accelerate the spread to other regions and even internationally, and explicitly argued that capturing and eliminating rodents on ships is essential to prevent hantavirus disease outbreaks [4]. Therefore, the public health implications of a hantavirus signal at sea are profound [1,2]. Cruise ships connect ecologically distinct regions and can act as amplifiers and disseminators of rodent-borne viruses, exposing passengers, crew, dock workers, and port communities [3,4]. Because severe hantavirus infections require advanced critical care, an outbreak at sea risks overwhelming shipboard facilities and small-port hospitals [2,5]. In an era of climate-driven rodent expansion, economic globalization, and intensifying maritime trade, the failure to integrate rodent-borne zoonoses into maritime preparedness is no longer defensible [1,4]. This event should trigger immediate policy action by maritime authorities and the World Health Organization [3]. First, rodent-borne zoonoses, including hantaviruses, must be explicitly embedded into the International Health Regulations implementation for ships and ports, including standard case definitions, outbreak investigation protocols, and requirements for environmental and rodent surveillance [3,4]. Second, mandatory and auditable rodent management plans covering structural rodent-proofing, active monitoring, and rapid control should be enforced for cruise ships and high-traffic ports, particularly in tropical and monsoon climates that favor rodent proliferation [1,4]. Third, clinical and laboratory preparedness must be upgraded through training to recognize hantavirus syndromes, pre-agreed medical evacuation pathways, and access to reference laboratories capable of hantavirus diagnostics [2,5]. The message from “hantavirus at sea” is unambiguous: a maritime health system calibrated only to frequent, low-fatality outbreaks is unfit for an age of accelerating zoonotic emergence [1,3]. Unless rodent-borne, environmentally transmitted pathogens are brought to the center of cruise and port preparedness, the next outbreak will not be a warning; it will be a failure of foresight on a global scale [1,4].
