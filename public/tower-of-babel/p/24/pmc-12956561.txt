Shaping stress: how curvature governs the mechanics of film-substrate systems undergoing volumetric expansion
Authors: Sierra J Gross, Lorenzo Valdevit, Ali Mohraz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractComposite structures with conformal coatings on porous backbones are widely employed in energy storage, flexible electronics, and biomedical devices. However, expansion-induced stresses can lead to mechanical degradation of the coatings, thereby limiting their performance. In this study, we use finite element simulations to evaluate how substrate morphology - including curvature, shape, and coating configuration - governs the mechanical response of expanding thin-film coatings, using lithiation of silicon anodes as a model case of extreme expansion. The peak stress and strain energy density of the expanding film are used as indicators of failure, and empirical relationships are introduced to predict their scaling with curvature. We find that films on shell-backbones consistently exhibit higher tensile stress but lower strain energy density than those on solid-backbones, reflecting a trade-off between cracking and delamination risks. In all studied configurations, substrates with positive Gaussian curvature amplify the in-plane stresses of the film and increase the propensity for mechanical degradation, whereas substrates with negative Gaussian curvature effectively redistribute stresses and enhance the mechanical resilience. This work highlights the advantages of shell-backbone saddle substrates for expanding thin-film systems and provides general guidelines for the design of mechanically robust architected composites and shell-based metamaterials.Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Composite structures with a conformal coating on porous backbones are commonly used in advanced energy storage systems1,2, flexible electronics3,4, and biomedical devices5,6. In these applications, the porous scaffold provides essential structural integrity and high specific surface area, while the film coating imparts additional functionality. However, volumetric expansion driven by thermal7,8, chemical6,9, or electrochemical stimuli1,2 can generate large stresses that lead to fracture and delamination of the film, thereby compromising the overall performance. Most work to ameliorate these challenges has focused on optimizing the coating thickness10,11 and tuning the material properties4,12. However, modification of these properties can come at the expense of active material loading (e.g. in energy storage applications), overall strength, and/or effectiveness of the applied coating11. Another less explored method to mitigate expansion-induced mechanical degradation is through architectural design of the substrate.

Recent advancements have enabled the synthesis of porous materials with a predominance of continuous negative Gaussian curvatures (K = k1·k2 < 0), where the two principal curvatures (k) bend in opposite directions, giving surfaces a saddle-like shape. Examples include mathematically defined triply periodic minimal surfaces, e.g., gyroids, fabricated through additive manufacturing13–15, as well as spinodal structures generated via scalable synthesis routes, such as dealloying or bijel processing (Fig. 1a)16–19. These morphologies exhibit enhanced strength and toughness per unit mass, making them highly desirable across diverse applications13–15. Furthermore, previous computational studies have highlighted minimal-surface topologies as promising candidates for reducing the stress response of coatings undergoing expansion20. However, a systematic understanding of how negative Gaussian curvatures influence the mechanical response of coatings remains limited, because the non-uniform stress distributions along oppositely curved directions cannot be captured by classical frameworks previously developed for cylindrical and spherical geometries21,22. With recent advances in the synthesis of composites with complex architectures for the applications mentioned above, the time is ripe for a systematic study of how local curvature influences the mechanics of curved films undergoing large volumetric expansion.

In this paper, we perform a computational parametric study of the mechanical response of films coated on systematically shaped backbones spanning a range of curvatures (Fig. 1b) and film-substrate configurations (Fig. 1c) representative of common 3D architected porous substrates (Fig. 1a). Specifically, we model a range of substrate geometries that collectively span the major shape classifications, including domes, ridges, saddles, ruts, and cups (Fig. 1b). These shapes reflect the basic local curvature motifs that make up the overall morphology of more complex architected systems. For each geometry, we examine single-coated solid backbones (i.e., substrates much thicker than the film), here denoted as SLBs, and double-coated shell backbones (i.e., substrates with thickness comparable to that of the film), here denoted as SHBs, as schematically shown in Fig. 1c with the substrate depicted in green and the coating in tan. While coatings on SLBs are ubiquitous (e.g., foams and strut-based lattices)23,24, SHB topologies have only recently garnered significant attention, due to their increased surface area, unique mechanical properties, and reduced relative densities when compared to SLB equivalents 19,21,25,26.

Using the lithium–silicon anode chemistry as a representative case of extreme expansion (up to 300% during lithiation)27,28, we evaluate the peak values of maximum principal stress (σmax), minimum principal stress (σmin), and strain energy density (Wmax) as proxies of the propensity for cracking, buckling, and delamination, respectively22. We find that the expanding films on SHBs develop higher σmax but lower Wmax, indicating a greater likelihood of cracking but a reduced risk of delamination relative to those coated on SLBs. For a given configuration, substrates with K > 0 amplify |σ| and Wmax of the film, while substrates with K ≤ 0 can reduce the overall |σ| and Wmax. Although demonstrated with the silicon anode chemistry, our findings offer general insights for geometry-informed substrate design to mitigate expansion-induced failure of expanding films on porous architectures, with saddle-like SHB geometries (K < 0) emerging as a particularly robust configuration.

Results

In this work, we investigate how the nickel (Ni) substrate geometry and configuration (solid vs. shell backbones) influence peak stress and strain energy density in amorphous silicon (Si) coatings undergoing 300% volumetric expansion (as explained in the Methods). We begin by analyzing SLB and SHB configurations, and conclude with a comparative analysis of mechanical behavior across backbone types and geometries.

Solid Backbones

Solid-backbone substrates are widely used in porous composite systems23,24,29, making them an important configuration to investigate. In this section, we study the stress and strain energy density distributions of coatings undergoing expansion on cup, rut, saddle, ridge, and dome substrates with characteristic radii between 2 and 10 μm.

To assess the influence of geometry in single-coated SLBs, we first compare the stress responses for models with the same characteristic R = 2 μm. In Fig. 2a, the stress tensors in the film along concave (ki < 0) and convex (ki > 0) directions are schematically summarized based on the SLB results from this study. The length of the vector indicates the relative magnitude of stress, and the color corresponds to the sign of stress (blue for σ < 0, and orange for σ > 0). The representative color contours of σmin, σmax, and Wmax for SLBs are shown in Fig.S. 1. For SLBs, the normal stress, σn, corresponds with σmax, and sign of σn matches the sign of curvature (e.g. σn < 0 for ki < 0, and σn > 0 for ki > 0). For all SLBs, constraint at the coating–substrate interface generates large in-plane compressive stresses (Fig. 2a, Fig.S. 2b) and elevated Wmax (Fig.S. 1c) within the expanding film. As a result, films on SLBs are all prone to compressive modes of failure, such as delamination, buckling, and wrinkling22,30. Furthermore, convex geometries also experience tensile normal stresses, which can accelerate delamination and promote crack formation in the film. When comparing the different shapes, as |s| increases, the geometric constraint of the SLB substrate on the expanding film increases, leading to amplified stress responses within the coating: cups and domes (s = ± 1) exhibit the largest |σ | , ruts and ridges (s = ± 0.5) exhibit reduced values of |σ| due to the presence of ki = 0, and saddles (s = 0) have the lowest |σ| due to the stress redistribution along opposing principal curvature directions.

To understand the mechanical response of films coated on SLBs with a range of substrate curvatures, we first compare σmin, σmax, and Wmax versus different curvature metrics (Fig.S. 2). We find that σmin, σmax, and Wmax follow linear trends with c, where the magnitudes and slopes appear to depend on the shape, indicating the mechanical response depends on both curvature and shape. While there are clear groupings based on K, reflecting the amplification (K > 0) or reduction (K < 0) of in-plane stress (σmin for SLBs) as the principal curvatures bend in the same or opposite directions, this metric does not fully capture the deviations in behavior between ruts and ridges. In contrast, s assigns a unique normalized value (-1 to 1) that captures the type of curvature independent of the magnitude and can be used to describe how shape amplifies the effect of curvature. As such, we introduce s·c as a combined metric for capturing the coupled effects of curvature and shape on mechanical behavior. In Fig. 2, σmin, σmax, and Wmax are plotted versus s·c, for cups (s = -1), domes (s = 1), ruts (s = -0.5), ridges (s = 0.5), and saddles (-0.5 < s < 0.5), represented by yellow, red, green, blue, and purple markers, respectively. The marker symbols indicate Gaussian curvature (circles for K > 0, squares for K = 0, and diamonds for K < 0). The consistent linear relationships shown in Fig. 2, with high-quality fits reported in Table S.2 (R² > 0.95), confirm s·c as a reliable metric to assess the mechanical response across SLB surface topologies. Importantly, the near collapse of data when plotted against s·c allows us to postulate overall design principles and guidelines across the different classes of porous substrates studied here. As seen in Fig. 2b, σmax nearly collapses onto a single master curve for all shapes because it is dominated by the normal stress component, which scales directly with the sign and magnitude of the local curvature and is less sensitive to differences in shape class. In contrast, σmin and Wmax are governed primarily by the in-plane stress components, which depend more strongly on how expansion-induced strain is distributed in each geometry, leading to different groupings in Fig. 2c, d. Negative Gaussian curvatures have a strong impact on how the in-plane stress is amplified, leading to groupings of behavior based on this metric (separation of circles, squares, and diamonds into different groups in Fig. 2c, d). This distinction provides additional insight into how shape influences the mechanical behavior of expanding films.

As s·c increases, σmax rises, while |σmin| and Wmax decrease, indicating a tradeoff between cracking and delamination risks. Concave shapes (s < 0) exhibit large compressive σ and Wmax that are concentrated near the substrate interface, thus promoting delamination, micro-crack formation, and wrinkling22,30. For convex shapes (s > 0) compressive σ and Wmax are localized near the outer surface, which can suppress longitudinal crack propagation, but the accompanying σn > 0 concentrated at the coating-substrate interface can promote unstable buckling-induced delamination22,30. Films on SLBs with cup and dome features (e.g. sintered powders, inverse opals) are especially susceptible to buckling-induced delamination because the positive Gaussian curvature amplifies the in-plane compression, leading to increases in |σmin|and Wmax. In contrast, shapes with s·c near zero provide the best balance of all metrics. Of note, saddles are particularly beneficial because they intrinsically have s (and therefore s·c) values centered around zero. Even when s ≠ 0, saddles exhibit reduced |σ| and Wmax due to the accommodation of the expansion-induced strain along opposing principal curvature directions. These characteristics make saddle geometries (e.g., gyroids, spinodals) the most mechanically resilient configuration and underscore their value as a design strategy for enhancing durability in expanding film-substrate systems. Although a planar surface also corresponds to s·c = 0, a flat film bonded to a rigid substrate remains laterally constrained and does not benefit from the strain-redistribution mechanisms unique to saddles.

For the SLB models studied here, the peak stress occurs at an early stage of lithiation ( ~ 15 vol% expansion). This lies within the small-deformation regime, which likely contributes to the linear trends observed between mechanical metrics (σmin, σmax, and Wmax) and s·c. Notably, as this peak precedes significant Si yielding, the observed linear s·c relationships may be leveraged to predict the mechanical response of other expanding thin-film systems constrained by rigid substrates, greatly expanding the applicability of this work.

Shell Backbones

Shell-backbone architectures are increasingly adopted across a range of engineering applications due to their reduced relative density, increased surface area, enhanced mechanical strength, and improved energy absorption compared to solid-backbone counterparts14,15. For electrochemical applications, double-coated SHBs enable twice as much active material loading at equivalent film thickness and reduce the substrate volume fraction, collectively enhancing gravimetric and volumetric capacities relative to SLB equivalents.

To assess the influence of geometry on the mechanics of double-coated SHBs, we first compare the stress responses of hemisphere (K > 0), cylinder (K = 0), and saddle (K < 0) models with characteristic R = 2 μm. The principal stress tensors representative of each SHB geometry are schematically summarized in Fig. 3a, based on the SHB simulation results. As before, the length of the vector indicates the relative magnitude of σ, and the color corresponds to its sign (blue for σ < 0, and orange for σ > 0). Color maps of σmax, σmin, and Wmax are shown in Fig. 3b–d, where the color bar limits are set to display tensile values for σmax (> 0) and compressive values for σmin (< 0), with values smaller and larger than the bounds of the color bar shown in black and gray, respectively. The substrate is included to visualize stress transitions across the inner and outer coatings. Unlike in the SLBs presented earlier, in SHB architectures tensile stresses develop in the substrate due to expansion of the film. This stress is significant enough to cause plastic deformation and bulk expansion of the substrate, which notably leads to different stress tensor trends in the film (Fig. 3a). Specifically, the film exhibits large in-plane tensile stresses along ki < 0 (red tones in Fig. 3a, b), and compressive stresses along ki > 0 (blue tones in Fig. 3a, c). As such, hemisphere and cylindrical geometries are simultaneously susceptible to cracking of the inner coating and buckling-induced delamination of the outer coating. In contrast, the SHB saddles exhibit more uniform distributions and lower magnitudes of σmax, σmin, and Wmax. This can be attributed to their unique curvature, where the tensile stress along one direction (ki < 0) is balanced by the compressive stress in the orthogonal direction (ki > 0).

To better understand how curvature influences the stress response, each SHB geometry was evaluated with c = 0.05–0.5 μm⁻¹. In double-coated SHBs, the stress response depends on the distribution of curvature across the shell thickness; however, classical theories are formulated for symmetric, single-layer systems, and do not account for interactions across the coatings. To map double-coated SHBs, we introduce principal curvature asymmetry, PCA=k1,inner/k1,outer, as a new geometric descriptor that quantifies the imbalance between the inner and outer surface curvatures. When combined with curvedness, the resulting metric c·PCA captures both the intensity and asymmetry of curvature in a single parameter. The σmax, σmin, and Wmax of the film are plotted versus c·PCA (Fig. 4) and ln(c·PCA) (Fig.S. 3) for hemisphere (red circles dotted line), cylinder (green squares dashed line), and saddle (purple diamonds solid line) SHB geometries.

Scaling of stress and strain energy density with curvature for shell-backbone models.

a σmax, b σmin, and c Wmax of the expanding film versus c·PCA for hemisphere (red circles dotted line), cylinder (green squares dashed line), and saddle (purple diamonds solid line) shell-backbone models.

For all models, the σmax, σmin, and Wmax demonstrate logarithmic scaling with c·PCA. Such non-linear scaling behavior is consistent with other thin-shell systems22,31. Table S.2 summarizes the coefficients for the logarithmic fits of the form y = a·ln(c·PCA) + b to the data, where a quantifies the sensitivity of the metric to curvature changes, and b represents a baseline value for comparison. Across the range of c·PCA values studied, saddle SHBs (K < 0) exhibit the lowest magnitudes of σmax, σmin, and Wmax and the weakest curvature sensitivity (a and b reduced by at least 20%), due to the redistribution of stresses along their negative Gaussian curvature. In contrast, hemisphere and cylinder SHBs (K ≥ 0) exhibit the largest σmax (Fig. 4a) and |σmin | (Fig. 4b), which correspond to the hoop stresses of the inner and outer coatings, respectively. Interestingly, the absence of curvature along k = 0 for cylinders allows the film to expand more freely along that direction, leading to a 33% reduction in Wmax and a corresponding decrease in the propensity for delamination compared to hemispheres.

Overall, SHBs with a predominance of K ≥ 0 features (e.g., spherical templated architectures or hollow fibers/struts in Fig. 1a) are the most susceptible to both cracking and delamination and should be avoided. Although these structures can be synthesized to have features with lower magnitudes of curvature (e.g., larger particle templates, or wider extrusion nozzles for 3D printed lattices and spun fibers) to reduce the propensity for mechanical degradation, these approaches also diminish the surface area-to-volume ratio. Thus, any gains in mechanical stability are offset by losses in surface area, making these strategies potentially counterproductive for some applications. Alternatively, the combined mechanical resilience of saddle SHBs across a wide range of c·PCA motivates the adoption of K < 0 substrates, such as 3D printed gyroids or scalable spinodal-templated morphologies as ideal templates for the synthesis of composite materials with conformal coatings for the applications mentioned earlier.

The subdued mechanical response of saddle SHBs suggests increased tolerance to geometric asymmetry. This, combined with moderate stress levels at elevated c, offers practical advantages for battery systems by enhancing the surface area-to-volume ratio—and thus the power and energy density. These findings motivate a deeper investigation of SHB saddles across a wider range of c·PCA values to evaluate their mechanical stability under more extreme curvature and asymmetry conditions. SHB saddles with PCA values between 0.12–17, and c values spanning 0.24–1.4 μm⁻¹ are studied in Fig. 5. This range encompasses the transition from near-cylindrical to ideal (k1 = -k2) saddle geometries and reflects practical fabrication limits, beyond which thin-film expansion is overly constrained. Figure 5c, d shows σmax and Wmax plotted as a function of c·PCA, with marker color indicating the curvedness. The relative magnitude and direction of stress tensors for saddle rut (PCA < 1), and saddle ridge (PCA > 1) shell backbones are schematically illustrated in Fig.S. 4.

For SHB saddles, σmax increases as PCA deviates from 1, with peak values localizing along the coating with the most negative k (outer for PCA < 1, inner for PCA > 1). In contrast, Wmax increases steadily with PCA and remains relatively uniform between the inner and outer coatings due to the balance of stress components when K < 0. The non-monotonic trend in σmax (Fig. 5a) paired with the consistent rise in Wmax (Fig. 5b) with PCA underscores the important role that curvature asymmetry plays in balancing cracking and delamination risks. For the full range of curvatures studied, σmax and Wmax retain the logarithmic scaling with c·PCA (Fig. 5c, d) making it a reliable metric for predicting the behavior of SHB saddles with different combinations of c and PCA. Notably, σmax and Wmax remain lower than hemispheres even when c·PCA is an order of magnitude higher. This underscores the mechanical advantages of negative Gaussian curvatures, which preserve enhanced performance with increased c and strong asymmetry. These results highlight SHB saddles as a highly robust and adaptable geometry for mitigating expansion-induced degradation and position them as leading contenders for advanced electrochemical applications where structural integrity and energy performance are both critical.

For geometries with equivalent c (i.e., same marker size), SHBs demonstrate slightly higher σmax but significantly lower Wmax compared to SLBs, indicating an increased risk of cracking but a reduced risk of buckle-delamination (Fig. 6). As c increases, SHBs exhibit a concurrent rise in both |σ| and Wmax. In contrast, the mechanical response of SLBs varies with the sign of curvature: convex SLBs (s > 0) show increasing σmax, but decreasing σmin and Wmax, whereas concave SLBs (s < 0) exhibit increases in both σmin and Wmax with rising c (described in the solid backbone subsection). Across both backbone types, shapes with K > 0 (red and yellow markers) display the highest |σ| and Wmax, suggesting greater mechanical vulnerability. Geometries with K = 0 (blue and green markers) offer intermediate values due to partial stress relaxation, an effect especially pronounced in SLBs. Notably, saddle geometries with K < 0 (purple markers), exhibit the lowest |σ| and Wmax for a given curvedness and configuration, indicating enhanced mechanical resilience uniquely enabled by their negative Gaussian curvature.

Discussion

In this study, we model the expansion of films coated on substrates with systematically varied shapes and curvatures to elucidate how substrate morphology influences the film’s mechanical response. SLBs accumulate large compressive σ and elevated Wmax that scale linearly with s·c. In contrast, SHBs exhibit more balanced tensile and compressive σ, along with lower Wmax that scales logarithmically with c·PCA. This indicates a transition in dominant failure mode from delamination for SLBs to cracking for SHBs. Across all configurations, expanding films coated on saddle geometries have demonstrated reduced sensitivity to curvature magnitude and asymmetry, making them the most mechanically resilient shape. When implemented in a SHB configuration, the combination of high surface area and low mass density enables greater energy and power density in battery systems. The combined structural reliability and functional benefits of SHB saddles position them as a robust and scalable solution for thin-film expansion systems and a strong candidate for advanced electrochemical platforms.

Although architected structures can be tessellated and consist of combinations of curvature motifs, these findings on isolated geometries suggest clear pathways for designing practical porous backbones with coatings susceptible to expansion-induced failure. Architectures dominated by dome- or cup-like features (e.g., sintered powders, inverse opals) are most vulnerable to stress-driven degradation and should be avoided where mechanical durability is critical. Systems with predominantly cylindrical or planar features (e.g., extruded lattices, spun fibers) show intermediate stability, but saddle-based morphologies (e.g., spinodals, gyroids) offer the best balance of |σ| and Wmax, and are therefore the most robust choice to delay the onset of mechanical failure during film expansion. In addition, SHB configurations can be leveraged to achieve high surface-area-to-volume ratios for improved performance in applications like energy storage, where maximizing the interface for reactions is crucial. By adopting a double-coated thin-walled structure instead of a single-coated solid one, these designs can achieve the same functional surface area with a smaller overall curvature. As such the domain size can be tuned to maximize the desired surface area while balancing the higher cracking risk associated with SHBs.

It is also worth noting that for silicon anodes, stress evolves non-linearly during expansion due to lithiation-induced softening, so the peak σmin, σmax, and Wmax were analyzed as these represent the most critical for mechanical failure. For SLBs, the peak values occur early during lithiation (15 vol.% expansion), when the film remains brittle and is more prone to cracking. In contrast, SHBs reach peak values at later stages of lithiation (60–270 vol.% expansion as c decreases from 0.5 to 0.125 μm⁻¹), when the film is more ductile, thus reducing the likelihood of brittle cracking in the first cycle despite higher tensile stress. This makes SHBs particularly advantageous for Si anodes because they can simultaneously offer enhanced surface area, and reduced failure risks due to their lower Wmax and delayed stress peak. However, ductile failure modes and performance under cycling conditions specific to battery applications will require further study.

We acknowledge the simplifications of the present analysis limit the generalizability of the findings. While the principal stress extrema and strain energy density were used here as simplified proxies for cracking and delamination to enable broad exploration of geometric trends, more sophisticated failure criteria will be needed in future work to directly evaluate damage initiation and propagation, as well as other modes of failure including wrinkling. This is especially important as material properties evolve and degradation accumulates within the first and subsequent cycles. Future work should also examine a wider range of film–substrate stiffness ratios and relative thicknesses in order to clarify the transition between bending-dominated and stretching-dominated responses observed for SHB and SLB configurations and determine how geometry-dependent trends extend beyond the Si–Li chemistry.

Overall, this work establishes a predictive framework that links substrate geometry to mechanical performance in expanding film-substrate systems and highlights substrates with K < 0 (e.g., gyroids and spinodals) as particularly durable design choices while coatings on substrates with K > 0 (e.g., sintered particles and inverse opals) are the most susceptible to mechanical failure. Though focused on Si–Ni anodes for lithium-ion batteries, this work establishes a foundation for geometry-informed design to mitigate expansion-induced degradation in other film-substrate systems, architected composites, and shell-based metamaterials 3–6.

Method

Model System

We used the commercial finite elements software package ABAQUS Standard to simulate films expanding on curved substrates of various geometries. Specifically, we simulated amorphous Si (a-Si) coatings on various Ni backbone morphologies as an example relevant to Li-ion battery anodes and particularly prone to mechanical failure from film expansion. The film thickness was fixed at 200 nm to represent a realistic scenario in these applications. During lithiation, a-Si undergoes a phase transformation to Li3.75Si, resulting in expansion up to 300 vol.% as the lithium (Li) concentration increases32–34. For slow charge and discharge of thin a-Si coatings, Li concentration can be considered uniform across the film, and the expansion is approximated as homogeneous and isotropic35,36. Following previous work27,28, the expansion was simulated using a thermal analogy, where the temperature (T) substitutes for state of charge (SoC). In this work, T is a normalized quantity such that ΔT = 1 corresponds to the full lithiation-induced expansion of 300 vol.%. Thus, values of ΔT correspond directly to proportional fractions of full volumetric expansion (e.g., ΔT = 0.5 ≈ 150 vol.% expansion). The resulting expansion- induced strain is expressed as ε=αΔT=lnVfVi1/3, and a fictitious thermal expansion coefficient (α) was set to 0.46 for the coating to simulate a 300% volumetric expansion upon a dimensionless temperature change of ΔT = 1.

Ni and Si were modeled as linear elastic - perfectly plastic solids. For Ni, the Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, and yield strength were set to 200 GPa, 0.3, and 600 MPa, respectively35. The mechanical properties for a-Si are dependent on the concentration of lithium as established in previous literature32–34, and are modeled as temperature dependent properties in the T = [0,1] range as described above and shown in Fig.S. 6. The elastic constants (E and ν) reflect the material’s small-strain response and therefore depend on the instantaneous stiffness of the local bonding environment, producing the near-linear trends observed for E and ν. In contrast, yielding in amorphous Li–Si alloys is controlled by large-strain plastic processes including Si–Si bond rupture, free-volume generation, and structural transitions between amorphous LiₓSi regimes. These mechanisms evolve nonuniformly with Li concentration, leading to an accelerated loss of load-bearing network connectivity at intermediate strain and a nonlinear dependence of σy on the state of charge as shown in Fig.S. 6. The Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, and yield strength of pure a-Si (SoC = 0) are 90 GPa, 0.28, and 6.5 GPa and they decrease to 34 GPa, 0.25, and 0.5 GPa upon full lithiation (SoC = 1), respectively as shown in Fig.S. 6. We recognize that the possible evolution of plasticity in both substrate and coating, and the temperature dependence of material properties, preclude exact extrapolation of the quantitative results of this study to other material systems of interest. Nonetheless, we surmise that most key findings on the effects of surface morphology on the mechanical behavior are widely translatable.

Model Generation

To quantitatively characterize surface geometry across the different configurations, we rely on a set of established curvature-based descriptors37,38. The curvature at a given point along a surface is defined as the inverse of the local radius, k=1/R, and the extrema are defined as the principal curvatures (k₁, k₂). Curvedness, c=0.5k12+k220.5, is a positive scalar that reflects the overall magnitude of curvature, regardless of sign. Shape index, s=0.5−1πarctank1+k2k1−k2, summarizes the local shape based on the relative orientation and symmetry of the principal curvatures; and Gaussian curvature, K=k1·k2, reflects whether the principal curvatures bend in the same direction (K > 0), opposite directions (K < 0), or if one is flat (K ≈ 0)39,40. The major shape classifications used in our study and their mapping based on principal curvatures, overall curvedness, and shape index are illustrated in Fig. 1b.

Cup, rut, saddle, ridge, and dome geometries with SLB (Fig. 7a) and SHB (Fig. 7b) configurations were created directly in the commercial finite elements software package ABAQUS CAE with a range of curvatures (c = 0.0625–0.5 μm-1) that encompasses common values used for porous architectures while providing sufficient space for the film to expand into. To create saddle-ridge and saddle-rut geometries, k2 was varied for a given k1 (0.0625–0.25 μm-1) to achieve s (-0.34–0.34) values approaching the rut and ridge limits. For SLBs, curvature was defined based on the outer surface of the Ni structure. For SHBs, a 500 nm thick Ni layer was used as the backbone, and curvature was defined along its centerline. The selected Ni thickness is experimentally attainable and provides a balance between mechanical support and reduced inactive material loading in the context of Si anode batteries. However, the optimal thickness of a SHB may vary depending on its application. At greater shell thicknesses, the mechanical behavior is expected to converge toward that of a SLB.

To ensure the results were not skewed significantly by the mesh density, convergence studies were performed. A dome geometry with c = 0.5 μm-1 was used as a representative case of sharp curvature. The maximum deviation factor within ABAQUS CAE, defined as the maximum gap between the element edge and the geometry divided by the element edge length, was used to control the density of nodes along the curved surface. The number of elements across the Si thickness were also varied to ensure the gradients along the normal direction were captured. Based on the convergence of the stress (σmax, σmin) and energy (Wmax) fields (Fig.S. 7), all models were meshed using a maximum deviation factor of 0.005 and 10 elements across the Si thickness. Each model consisted of at least 2000 linear quadrilateral CAX4R elements. For SLB models, displacement and rotational boundary conditions that prevented out-of-plane translation and rotation were placed along all edges except for the outer Si surface as shown in Fig.S. 8a. For SHB models, displacement boundary conditions were placed only along the centerline of the Ni backbone, to prevent shifting of the center point while still enabling expansion of the outer and inner Si coatings (Fig.S. 8b). The data was extracted from a region of interest centered around the point at which the characteristic radius was defined (Fig.S. 8). For each region of interest, the values at the integration points were extracted at various degrees of lithiation-induced expansion, and the 5 and 95th percentile values were used as representatives of the minimum and maximum values, respectively in order to exclude erroneous data that arise from numerical singularities. We verified that these percentile values were typically within <1% of the absolute extrema except for when the mesh was insufficiently refined (Fig.S. 8), indicating that their use reduces mesh sensitivity while preserving the trends in the stress and strain energy fields. The values reported for analysis are extracted from the state of lithiation at which the largest magnitudes of σ and Wmax occurred, enabling consistent comparison across the broad range of geometries and configurations studied.
