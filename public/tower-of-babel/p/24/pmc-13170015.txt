Impact of Moringa stenopetala dietary consumption on vitamin A deficiency and serum retinol levels among adults in Southern Ethiopia: a multicenter comparative cross-sectional study
Authors: Eskeziyaw Agedew, Direslgne Misker, Terefe Gelibo, Alemayehu Bekele, Eyasu Makonnen, Ashenif Tadele, Haregewoyin Kerebih, Solomon Worku, Yalemtsehay Mekonnen, Adamu Belay, Feyessa Challa, Temesgen Awoke, Negero Gemeda, Said Kassa, Asfaw Debella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMoringa stenopetala is a highly versatile plant that has long been utilized as food and traditional medicine by communities in the southern and southwestern regions of Ethiopia. The plant is characterized by its dark green similar to cabbage. Its leaves used to prepare by mixing with various cereal flours and incorporated into the traditional cultural diet of the community. Despite its widespread consumption and recognized nutritional value, the dietary contribution of Moringa stenopetala on vitamin A deficiency and serum retinol levels has not been sufficiently investigated at the community level. Therefore, the present study was conducted to assess the long-term effects of dietary Moringa stenopetala consumption on vitamin A deficiency and serum retinol concentration among adults residing in Moringa stenopetala-consuming and non-consuming areas of Southern Ethiopia.MethodsData were collected from study participants using a structured questionnaire designed to obtain information on socio-demographic characteristics and dietary consumption of Moringa stenopetala. The survey data were collected using digital tablets programmed with the Cispro data collection application. In addition, venous blood samples (5–8 mL) were drawn from the antecubital vein using evacuated yellow-top tubes for the analysis of serum vitamin A parameters. The collected data were exported to SPSS version 25 for statistical analysis. A one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was employed to assess the mean differences in serum retinol levels between participants who consumed Moringa stenopetala and those who did not consuming group. The F-test and corresponding P-values were used to determine the statistical significance of the observed differences between the two groups.ResultThe prevalence of vitamin A deficiency among adults who consumed Moringa stenopetala was 10.18% (95% confidence limit [CL]: 5.40–11.40), whereas the prevalence among the non-consuming group was 19.85% (95% CI: 13.2–23.0), indicating a statistically significant difference between the two groups (p < 0.005). The mean serum retinol level was significantly higher among participants who consumed Moringa stenopetala (mean ± SD: 1.62 ± 0.77; 95% CI: 1.52–1.70) compared with those who did not consume it (1.24 ± 0.65; 95% CI: 1.16–1.32). Participants who reported consuming Moringa stenopetala throughout all seven days of the week exhibited a higher mean serum retinol level (1.88; 95% CL: 1.62–2.15) compared with individuals who did not consume it (1.26; 95% CL: 1.18–1.34). Furthermore, individuals who consumed Moringa stenopetala more than four times per day demonstrated higher mean serum retinol levels than those who consumed it fewer than two times per day, suggesting a positive association between the frequency of Moringa stenopetala consumption and serum retinol concentration.ConclusionFrequent consumption of Moringa stenopetala was associated with higher serum retinol levels, highlighting its significant role in reducing vitamin A deficiency. Promoting Moringa stenopetala based diets as an effective community level dietary based intervention.Keywords: Vitamin A deficiency, Serum retinol level, Moringa stenopetala, South Ethiopia

Introduction

Moringa stenopetala (M. stenopetala) is a highly versatile plant species that has been traditionally consumed as both a food source and a medicinal plant for many years by indigenous communities in the southern and southwestern parts of Ethiopia [1]. It has multiple uses, serving as food for humans, feed for livestock, forage for bees, and as a source of traditional medicine. It is also used in the production of dye, perfume, skin lotion, lubricants, and for water purification [2, 3]. It has potential in edible oil production, poultry farms, and the pharmaceutical and food industries [4–6]. In the South Ethiopian community who resides in Konso, Derashe, Woleyeta, Gamo, and Gofa lowlands, and other growing districts of the Southern Rift Valley of Ethiopia, the leaves of M. stenopetala trees are used in the daily meal of each household as a staple diet [6, 7].

Various research conducted on M.stenopetala for its medicinal and nutritional value provides information on its importance [8]. The plant is rich in essential nutrients, including calcium, proteins, iron, and vitamins, making it a valuable dietary resource. In addition, it contains a range of bioactive compounds—such as polyphenols, flavonoids, alkaloids, and essential oils—that contribute to its therapeutic properties [9].

The leaves of M. stenopetala are rich in proteins, fats, carbohydrates, and crude fiber, as well as vitamins such as vitamin C and β-carotene, minerals including iron, calcium, potassium, and zinc, making their nutrient profile comparable to that of other leafy vegetables. With appropriate processing and incorporation into local diets, M. stenopetala has significant potential to contribute to the alleviation of malnutrition in Ethiopia [8, 10, 11].

Additional study on the nutritional profile of M. stenopetala indicated that the macronutrient nutritional composition of the leaves of M. stenopetala consists of 26.60%−30.20% crude proteins, 3.36%−4.29% fats, 38.40%−41.20% carbohydrates, and 5.87% crude fiber [12]. Regarding vitamin A composition in the form of beta-carotene, 160 µg/100 g of leaf samples [11]. It also contained significant amounts of tocopherols, vitamin A, vitamin K, and vitamin B. It is rich in essential amino acids: leucine, valine, phenylalanine, and arginine. The leaves also contain high polyphenols (3800 mg/100 g), bioflavonoid rutin (1610 mg/100 g), β-carotene, lutein, zeaxanthin, β-sitosterol, stigmasterol, and campesterol [13].

Vitamin A deficiency is a public health problem in developing countries, including Ethiopia, which affects all dimensions of the population. It is essentially required in the body to maintain the visual system, sustain normal cellular differentiation, develop resistance against infections, and uphold epithelial integrity, red blood cell production, and reproduction. Vitamin A deficiency could be attributed to prolonged deprivation of vitamin A-rich foods and is further depleted by diarrhea, measles, and respiratory infections. It also resulted among populations whose diets are lacking in animal products and green vegetables [14–16].

Although M. stenopetala has dark green leaves, the local community has traditionally consumed it as a vegetable, similar to cabbage, often mixing it with various cereal flours as part of their cultural diet for many years. However, there is currently no evidence regarding its contribution to serum retinol levels in the adult population. Therefore, this community-based study was conducted to generate evidence addressing this gap.

Material and method

Study area, target population, and design

The study was conducted in Southern Ethiopia across four randomly selected districts. Study sites were chosen from both rural and urban areas of Konso and Derashe districts, where M. stenopetala is a common staple and grows abundantly. In these communities, the leaves of M. stenopetala are consumed like cabbage and incorporated into traditional staple foods such as Kurkufa and Possissie by mixing them with cereal flour. In contrast, Chencha and Bonkey districts do not use M. stenopetala as a staple food source. All study sites share relatively similar agroecological conditions and disease burdens, with the primary difference being dietary consumption of M. stenopetala. A community-based, multi-center comparative cross-sectional study was conducted among randomly selected adult individuals, one per household. Individuals with any acute or chronic illnesses were excluded from the study.

Sample size calculation and sampling approach

The sample size was calculated by using the double population proportion formula by considering a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error and 50% prevalence to get a sufficient sample size. Then the final sample size was 844 including the non-response rate, of which 422 were for the M.stenopetala consuming group and 422 for the non-consuming group. Rural and urban study sites were selected randomly from each district, then a sampling frame was prepared from the list of households and simple random methods was applied to select one adult from each targeted household. Individuals who had any acute and chronic illness were excluded from the study.

Data collection

House-to-house data was collected by using a structured questionnaire on socio-demographic characteristics, M. stenopetala based dietary consumption and biological sample. The questioner was developed from related published articles to collect data and attached as supplementary file (Supplementary file 1).

Dietary data on M. stenopetala based dietary consumption was collected at the individual level using 24-hour dietary recall method. Consumption quantities or portion size leaf of M. stenopetala estimated using hand bunches as a local measurement unit, with one full hand bunch of Moringa stenopetala leaves considered equivalent to one kilogram and half a hand bunch equivalent to 0.5 kg. In this study, both the frequency and portion size of M. stenopetala intake were assessed to capture daily and weekly consumption habit. The data was collected by using digital tablets, which were loaded and programmed with the Cispro-software data collection application tool.

For determination of serum retinol (5–8 ml) whole blood was collected from the antecubital vein of the arm using evacuated yellow tip tubes (gel & dist activated) designated for clinical parameter tests and mixed gently.

Serum vitamin A laboratory determination

The serum was then separated from the blood after centrifugation, collected in a Nanc tube, stored in a mobile deep freezer at −20 °C, and transported to Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI) for further laboratory analysis. Then the serum specimens were transferred and stored at −80 °C until analysis for clinical markers of serum retinol level. Serum retinol level was determined by using High-performance liquid chromatography [17, 18] in the nutrition and food science laboratory at EPHI by ensuring frequent quality control and assurance based on the standard laboratory procedure.

Data analysis

The collected data was exported from the Cispro-software data collection application tool to SPSS version 25 for further analysis. Then data was sorted, cleaned, and analyzed before conducting further advanced analysis. Descriptive analysis was used to describe study population with relevant variables. Frequency tables, and graphs were employed to summarize and visualize the results. Baseline sociodemographic categorical variables were compared between the M. stenopetala-based dietary consuming group and the non-consuming group using the Chi-square test.

To assess the difference in mean serum retinol levels between M. stenopetala based dietary consuming and non-consuming groups, a one-way ANOVA test was performed. The F-test, along with its associated P-value, was used to determine whether the observed difference in mean serum retinol levels between the two groups was statistically significant.

Results

Among the study participants, nearly half of individuals in both the M. stenopetala consuming and non-consuming groups had no formal education. The proportion of males and females was almost equal in both groups. There were no significant sociodemographic differences between the M. stenopetala consuming and non-consuming participants with respect to educational level, age, sex, area of residence, and household wealth index (Table 1).

Serum vitamin A deficiency among adults in Moringa stenopetala consuming versus non-consuming group

Among adult participants from both M. stenopetala consuming and non-consuming group, the overall prevalence of vitamin A deficiency was 14.9% (95% CL: 10.4–15.8). The prevalence was 10.18% (95% CL: 5.40–11.40) among individuals in the M. stenopetala consuming group, compared with 19.85% (95% CL: 13.2–23.0) among those in the non-consuming group. This result showed a significant difference between the two groups (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1).

Effect of dietary Moringa stenopetala consumption habit on mean serum retinol level

A one-way ANOVA test showed that the mean serum retinol level was significantly higher in the M. stenopetala consuming area (1.62 ± 0.77, 95% CL: 1.52–1.70) compared with the non-consuming area (1.24 ± 0.65, 95% CL: 1.16–1.32) (P = 0.001). Participants who consumed M. stenopetala throughout all seven days had a higher mean serum retinol level (1.88, 95% CL: 1.62–2.15) than those who did not consume it (1.26, 95% CL: 1.18–1.34) (P = 0.001).

The frequency of M. stenopetala consumption was also associated with significant variation in serum retinol levels. Individuals consuming M. stenopetala four times per day had higher mean serum retinol levels than those consuming it once per day or not at all. Similarly, the quantity consumed was related to retinol levels: participants from households consuming more than one kilogram of M. stenopetala per day had higher mean serum retinol levels than those consuming less than half a kilogram.

Among sociodemographic factors, individuals in the lowest and above middle wealth quintiles had higher mean serum retinol levels than those in the second and third wealth quintiles. Participants older than 30 years had lower mean serum retinol levels compared with younger age groups. No significant differences were observed in mean serum retinol levels were observed by sex, residence, or the use of M. stenopetala for medicinal purposes (Table 2).

Discussion

The prevalence of vitamin A deficiency was 10.18% (95% CL: 5.40–11.40%) in the M. stenopetala consuming group and 19.85% (95% CL: 13.2–23.0%) in the non-consuming group. These results indicate that vitamin A deficiency was significantly higher among individuals who did not consume M. stenopetala compared with those who consumed. This finding was supported with evidence from an interventional study conducted in Burkina Faso, which reported a significant improvement in serum retinol levels after participants consumed M. stenopetala leaf powder for one year [19, 20].

The lower prevalence of vitamin A deficiency observed in M. stenopetala consuming areas may be attributed to the high nutritional content of M. stenopetala. The leaves contain substantial amounts of β-carotene, a precursor of vitamin A, reaching up to 160 µg/100 g. This makes M. stenopetala an important dietary source of provitamin A, which can contribute to improved serum vitamin A levels when consumed as part of a regular diet al.ongside staple foods such as cereals. Furthermore, β-carotene is one of the most effective precursors of vitamin A, as humans and other animals are able to convert it into active vitamin A (retinol) within the body after consumption [8, 11, 20].

In addition, M. stenopetala has dark green leaves that are commonly cooked with cereal flour. Importantly, much of its vitamin content is retained during cooking, even at relatively high temperatures. This characteristic makes M. stenopetala a valuable dietary source of vitamin A. This observation is supported by a study examining the β-carotene content of cooked M. stenopetala, which reported minimal loss of carotene during ordinary cooking processes, even at temperatures approaching 100 °C [8].

Dietary consumption of M. stenopetala for seven consecutive days, with a feeding frequency of more than three times per day and an intake of more than one bunch (approximately one kilogram) at the household level, was associated with relatively higher mean serum retinol levels compared with individuals who consumed it only once per week or in smaller amounts. This variation may be explained by the increased intake of β-carotene, the precursor of vitamin A, as both the frequency and quantity of M. stenopetala consumption increase, thereby contributing to higher serum retinol levels [21, 22].

From sociodemographic variables, individuals in the lowest and above middle wealth quintiles exhibited higher mean serum retinol levels compared to those in the second quintile. This is due to the feeding habit of lowest-wealth-quintile individuals relying on feeding of M.stenopetala based local food more frequently due to a shortage of income to purchase other food items. Additionally, individuals in the middle and above wealth quintile and older adults’ also demonstrated frequent intake of M.stenopetala based diets, often as a strategy to manage chronic non-communicable diseases.

Conclusion

Frequent consumption of M. stenopetala was associated with higher serum retinol levels, highlighting its significant role in reducing vitamin A deficiency. Promoting M. stenopetala based diets as an effective community level dietary based intervention.
