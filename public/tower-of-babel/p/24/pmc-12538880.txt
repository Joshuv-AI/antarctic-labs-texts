A novel classification and clinical evaluation of various morphologies of calcified lumbar disc herniation treated using unilateral biportal endoscopic technique
Authors: Hao Liu, Xiang Yang, Heng Wu, Shuai Zhang, Song Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo introduce a new classification of calcified lumbar disc herniation (CLDH), and to evaluate the clinical performance of the unilateral biportal endoscopic technique (UBE) in treating CLDH.MethodsBetween April 2023 and May 2025, 119 total lumbar disc herniation patients were treated with UBE at the participating hospital. The subjects were sorted into one of two groups, the CLDH group (N = 52) or uncalcified lumbar disc herniation group (ULDH, N = 67), based on the calcification of the intervertebral disc. Furthermore, the CLDH group was subdivided into three distinct categories in an attempt to conduct comparative analysis of clinical efficacies across all sub-groups. The visual analogue scale (VAS) and Oswestry disability index (ODI) were recorded at different time-points, specifically, pre-operation, 3-day post-operation, 1-month post-operation, 3-month post-operation, and at the last follow-up. The MacNab classification outcomes were recorded at the last follow-up. Any complications were recorded during follow-ups.ResultsAll patients underwent successful surgical interventions without any reports of serious postoperative complications. The ULDH patients required a shorter operative time, relative to the CLDH patients (69.7 ± 11.6 min vs., 75.9 ± 11.9 min P < 0.05). Despite this difference, all patients experienced comparable blood loss, and hospitalization duration (21.9 ± 4.8 ml vs. 21 ± 5.0 ml, P > 0.05; 4.0 ± 0.4 d vs. 3.9 ± 0.4 d, P > 0.05). Our postoperative assessments revealed remarkable improvements in both VAS and ODI scores at all evaluated time points (3-day, 1-month, 3-month post-operation, and final follow-up), relative to the preoperative baseline values (p < 0.05). More importantly, these improvements demonstrated consistent statistical significance across the entire follow-up period. Of note, we observed no marked intergroup differences in either scoring system at any postoperative evaluation time point. At the final follow-up, 94.8% of CLDH patients achieved excellent/good clinical outcomes. The ULDH patients also demonstrated comparable therapeutic efficacy with 95.5% patients exhibiting similar MacNab criteria classification (p > 0.05). Using this classification, the number of categories were as follows: 29 cases, Hill type: 17 cases, and Peak type: 6 cases. No substantial differences were evident in clinical efficacies among the three subgroups. The peak patient group experienced a longer surgical duration, relative to the stone patient group, who, in turn, required a longer operation time than the hill group, and the differences were statistically significant (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThe Song’s classification system has initially demonstrated significant value in guiding personalized surgical decision-making. Different strategic approaches of UBE provide alternative surgical options, thereby optimizing the treatment plan for patients with CLDH.

Introduction

With the aging of the population, low back and leg pain caused by lumbar disc herniation has seriously affected people’s daily lives. Lumbar disc herniation is caused by intervertebral disc degeneration; currently, many scholars have studied the molecular mechanisms of intervertebral disc degeneration [1], and various forms of cell death can exacerbate this degeneration [2]. Therefore, many researchers have applied platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) to inhibit intervertebral disc degeneration [3, 4], thereby improving the functions of nucleus pulposus and annulus fibrosus cells, and have achieved partial success [5]. Without timely intervention for intervertebral disc degeneration, the intervertebral disc will exhibit manifestations such as calcification.

Calcified lumbar disc herniation (CLDH) is a distinct pathological entity within the wide lumbar disc herniation (LDH) spectrum. It is characterized by the coexistence of intervertebral disc calcification and primary herniation pathology [6]. Currently, the influencing factors of intervertebral disc degeneration are still under investigation [7, 8]. CLDH was first described by Dr. Baron in 1924. Since then, it has emerged as a growing clinical concern largely due to the global demographic shift toward an aging population [9]. Primary CLDH manifestations include radicular leg pain and sensory deficits in the lower extremities. This is typically managed with surgical intervention, particularly, among patients exhibiting persistent or refractory symptoms following an adequate trial of conservative management strategies [10].

CLDH management predominantly employs conventional open surgical approaches over endoscopic lumbar discectomy. This preference stems from the technical challenges of endoscopic calcified fragment removal over the superiority of open techniques in achieving complete decompression under direct visualization [11]. Despite its clinical proficiency, traditional open lumbar discectomy has few perioperative limitations, such as, extensive paravertebral soft tissue dissection, prolonged surgical duration, excess intraoperative blood loss, and delayed postoperative recovery [12]. Additionally, there are growing concerns of iatrogenic muscle injury which manifests as paraspinal denervation and fat infiltration [13] Transforaminal endoscopic techniques offer a minimally invasive alternative involving local anesthesia, selective anatomical dissection, and preserved paravertebral muscle integrity. This approach accelerates functional recovery via reduced surgical trauma and optimized multimodal analgesia protocols. In fact, transforaminal endoscopic safety and therapeutic efficacy has been validated in multiple studies involving LDH [14]. Some researchers have used hydrogels to repair annulus fibrosus defects and have reduced the risk of complications [15]. But for CLDH, owing to its coaxial limitation of both operation and observation channels, along with the bone structure block, produces a blind spot on the surgical field, which increases the difficulty of microscopic operation, augments risk of residual intervertebral disc, and enhances risk of dural sac and nerve root injury conventional [6, 16]. Unilateral biportal endoscopic technique (UBE) has the unique feature of a wide operating channel, in combination with certain view and general purpose tools that are not available with conventional endoscopic techniques [17, 18]. UBE is also reported to be safe and efficacious in LDH management [18–20]. However, UBE treatment of CLDH remains relatively unexplored, and a CLDH classification for UBE has yet to be established.

In this study, we employed computed tomography (CT) imaging to categorize calcified lesions into three types, and proposed a classification system named “Song’s Classification”. Concurrently, we evaluated the safety and efficacy of UBE in managing CLDH.

Materials and methods

Patients

This single-center retrospective cohort investigation was approved by the Institutional Ethical Board of the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University (Protocol No. KY2025200). The analytic cohort comprised of 119 consecutive patients with confirmed CLDH and ULDH diagnoses who underwent UBE between April 2023 and May 2025. Prior to the study, a power analysis was conducted using G*Power. The analysis adopted an effect size of 0.8 (defined as a large effect size), a significance level of 0.05, and a target power of 0.80.

Selection criteria

Inclusion criteria: [1] Confirmed CLDH diagnosis using CT scans with multiplanar reconstruction [2]. Refractory symptoms following 12 weeks of conservative care [3] Symptoms (e.g., radicular pain, neurologic deficits) match the affected spinal level on imaging.

Exclusion criteria: patients with [1] Primary/metastatic spinal tumors, tuberculous/pyogenic spondylodiscitis, or systemic infections with spinal involvement [2]. Active local/systemic infections requiring preoperative infectious disease clearance [3]. Coagulopathies and other surgical contraindications.

Song’s classification of CLDH

Using CT scans, our team developed a unique CLDH classification system that includes three categories of CLDH (Fig. 1). All measurements were performed by assessors who were aware of the treatment groups. To minimize bias and errors, the assessments were conducted using standardized criteria. The assessors received training, and in cases of disagreement, senior orthopedic spine surgeons (with more than 15 years of experience) who had not participated in the initial assessment were invited to serve as “third-party arbitrators” to provide the final classification.

The new CLDH classification system. A: Stone type, B: Hill type, C: Peak type

The “Stone type” represents the complete absence of osseous continuity between the calcified lesion and the parent vertebral body. This presents as a radiographically dense, free-floating morphology on axial imaging, similar to an isolated stone.

The “Hill type” refers to the osseous continuity between the calcified lesion and the parent vertebral body. This manifests as a broad-based, dome-shaped osseous prominence without a conical apex on axial imaging, resembling a hillock-like morphology.

The “Peak type” exhibits an osseous continuity between the calcified lesion and the parent vertebral body. This features a narrow-based, spiculated attachment with a tapered, acuminated apex on axial imaging, and mirrors a mountain peak.

Surgical procedures

Subjects were given general anesthesia and were positioned prone on a Jackson spinal table. To minimize intra-abdominal pressure, a contoured gel pad was placed under the thoracoabdominal area on a viscoelastic mattress. The target segment was carefully identified using intraoperative C-arm fluoroscopy with biplanar (AP/lateral) imaging. We created two 0.8-cm paramedian incisions: the cranial portal (for endoscope insertion) was positioned at the inferior margin of the upper lamina and the caudal portal (for instrument access) was positioned at the superior margin of the lower lamina. Sequential dilation was achieved with tapered serial dilators, which progressively expanded the paraspinal muscle planes. Subsequently, radiofrequency ablation of soft tissues was used to expose the lamina bone. Using intraoperative C-arm fluoroscopy, we next confirmed target segment alignment. Thereafter, we located the interlaminar window, and dissected the ligamentum flavum. Lastly, after meticulous clearing of the extradural fat, the traversing nerve root was exposed within the thecal sac recess.

In patients with the stone form of CLDH (Fig. 2), the calcified lesion was exposed, then the surrounding fibrous tissue was excised using nucleus pulposus forceps or rongeur. This allowed the calcified lesion to be excised in one piece using the nucleus pulposus forceps. In case of the hill type of CLDH patients (Fig. 3), following calcified lesion exposure, the nerve root was first protected using a nerve root retractor. Then, under endoscopic visualization, a nerve dissector was used to explore the calcified lesion, confirming that the tip was round and blunt, before excising the surrounding adhesive tissue. Finally, for the peak type of CLDH patients (Fig. 4), we exercised great caution during the intraoperative calcified lesion exposure because the apex of the “peak” typically exerts substantial tension on the nerve. Therefore, blind or casual usage of a nerve root retractor to pull the nerve away from the apex can easily cause nerve damage. To circumvent this, a nerve dissector was first used to assess the tension between the nerve and “peak” apex. Following removal of the surrounding adhesive tissue, the nerve was gently retracted with a nerve root retractor, and the peak type was ground down to a hill type. Finally, a drain tube was placed before incision closure to prevent fluid buildup and reduce risk of infection.

Clinical efficacy evaluation of CLDH

The following patient information was recorded: demographic characteristics, i.e., age, sex, body mass index (BMI), surgical segment; surgical duration, intraoperative blood loss, hospitalization days and complications; and clinical outcomes, in particular, the visual analog scale (VAS) for leg and back pain, Oswestry disability index (ODI), and the modified MacNab criteria. All VAS and ODI assessments were independently evaluated by two board-certified spinal surgeons blinded to each other’s assessments, and a final score was obtained by computing the arithmetic mean both surgeons’ ratings (rounded to the nearest whole number).

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0. Normality evaluation was completed via the Shapiro-Wilk test. Parametric comparisons (Student’s t-test) were applied to normally distributed continuous variables, whereas non-parametric comparisons (Mann-Whitney U test) were reserved for non-normally distributed continuous data and ordinal categorical variables. Categorical variables were evaluated using the Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, where appropriate. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with α significance threshold set at 0.05.

Results

General characteristics of study participants

Overall, 119 patients were recruited for analysis, among which 52 suffered from CLDH and 67 from ULDH. Applying our classification, we had 29 cases of the Stone type, 17 cases of the Hill type, and 6 cases of the Peak. All patients underwent successful surgery. The CLDH and ULDH patients exhibited no marked differences in baseline characteristics, including sex, age, BMI, surgical segment (Table 1).

Intraoperative and clinical outcomes

The CLDH group experienced higher estimated blood loss and hospitalization days, relative to the ULDH group (21.9 ± 4.9 ml vs. 21.3 ± 5.0 ml, P > 0.05; 4.0 ± 0.4 d vs. 3.9 ± 0.4 d, P > 0.05), however, the difference did not reach significance. The surgical duration was longer in the CLDH versus the ULDH group (75.9 ± 11.9 min vs. 69.7 ± 11.6 min, P < 0.05). Interestingly, the three different CLDH types exhibited no marked differences in the estimated blood loss and hospitalization days. However, the Peak type required a longer operation duration than the Stone type, and the Stone type surgery took longer than the Hill type, and the differences were statistically significant (p < 0.05).

In case of clinical outcomes, our intra-group comparison revealed statistically significant reductions in the postoperative VAS scores (low back/leg pain) and ODI values relative to the preoperative scores (P < 0.05, Table 3). Meanwhile, inter-group analysis showed no marked differences in the leg pain VAS or ODI scores at any of the assessed time points (preoperative, 3-day post-operation, 1-month post-operation, 3-month post-operation, and final follow-up; all P > 0.05). The postoperative therapeutic success was measured using the modified MacNab criteria, and demonstrated excellent/good outcomes in 94.8% of the CLDH cohort and among 95.5% of the ULDH cohort. Statistical analysis revealed no obvious inter-group difference in the satisfaction rates (P > 0.05), indicating comparable patient-reported efficacy between the two surgical approaches. Finally, the three CLDH types showed no marked difference in the postoperative VAS scores (low back/leg pain) and ODI values. Figs. 2, 3 and 4 present typical cases of the three categories of CLDH treated with UBE. Finally, logistic regression analysis showed that variables including age (B = 0.000, 95% CI [−0.005, 0.006], P = 0.888), gender (B = −0.080, 95% CI [−0.223, 0.063], P = 0.268), and BMI (B = -0.008, 95% CI [−0.25, 0.010], P = 0.393) did not show a statistically significant association with clinical efficacy (Table 5).

Complications

Post-operation, we observed no nerve root injury, visceral injury, discitis, or recurrence in either patient groups. Three patients (2.5%) experienced dural tears with cerebrospinal fluid leakage (2 CLDH, 1 ULDH) secondary to fibroadhesive complexes between the calcified tissues or the uncalcified lumbar disc. These patients were provided with bed rest, lumbar drainage (2 cases), and antibiotics. All patients achieved complete symptom resolution without any neurological sequelae.

Discussion

The present study analyzed and compared the clinical outcomes of UBE treatment of CLDH and ULDH patients, with special focus on the various CLDH morphologies and their specific UBE managements. UBE treatment of CLDH and ULDH showed comparable short-term benefits. ULDH required shorter surgical duration than CLDH, likely because of the adhesion between the calcified tissue and the nerve root or dura mater in CLDH, which significantly increases surgical difficulty [21, 22]. Although CLDH required longer operative time, other variables, such as, patient blood loss, and hospitalization days remained the same as ULDH patients. UBE offers multiple advantages, namely, a wide field of view and independent operating channels, which supports precise hemostasis during procedures, and ensures reduced blood loss and tissue trauma [23, 24]. These features collectively contribute to accelerated patient recovery and expedited discharge from medical facilities.

Optimal surgical management of CLDH remains a subject of ongoing debate [6]. Traditional open surgery remains the gold standard for managing complex calcified lumbar disc herniations. However, despite its efficacy, traditional open surgery carries inherent risks including higher wound infection rates (3–8% vs. 0.5–2% in MIS), chronic axial back pain from paravertebral muscle denervation (12–22%), and segmental instability due to extensive facet joint violation (≥ 50% resection in 67%). In addition, biomechanical disruption may alter lumbar kinematics, which, in turn, increases adjacent segment degeneration risk (18.4% at 5 years) [6, 12, 13]. Meanwhile, several studies reported treating CLDH with percutaneous endoscopic discectomy [25–27]. In fact, Dabo et al. demonstrated that percutaneous endoscopic interlaminar discectomy (PEID) provides comparable pain relief outcomes as traditional open surgery while offering minimal invasiveness, reduced costs, and faster rehabilitation. However, it is important to note that the rates of early postoperative paresthesia and postoperative drug usage is significantly greater with PEID than with open surgery [28]. Cheng et al. also used PEID for CLDH management, and among the patients treated in the study, two patients developed nerve root injuries and sensory deficits post-surgery. Endoscopic Lumbar Discectomy (PELD) for CLDH is similarly minimally invasive and offers rapid recovery. Unfortunately, PELD is associated with elevated rates of postoperative complications including paresthesia and recurrence, relative to open surgery. Given these challenges, the standardized CLDH treatment protocols remain controversial in clinical practice. More recently, UBE, a relatively new form of spinal endoscopy, has become a widely used surgical procedure for LDH [29]. It features an independent working and viewing portal, which enables freer surgical maneuverability and enhanced decompression efficacy, which, in turn, successfully resolves the historical limitation of inadequate decompression in conventional endoscopic approaches. UBE utilizes instruments of open surgery [30, 31]. Therefore, it combines advantages of both endoscopic and open surgeries. Till date, there are limited reports on UBE usage in CLDH treatment.

In this study, all CLDH forms demonstrated satisfactory clinical efficacy with minimal complications, thereby establishing UBE as a viable treatment option. We attribute this favorable surgical outcome to the implementation of tailored therapeutic strategies for the various subtypes of CLDH. While previous studies proposed classification-based treatment protocols for CLDH [21, 32, 33], their clinical efficacy was suboptimal with limited documentation of prognostic accuracy and therapeutic reproducibility. One study recommended the Wu scoring system for CLDH, particularly, when calcification is close to the contained, extruded, sequestered, or low-grade migrated disc herniation [21]. The primary limitation of the Wu classification system is its highly complex nature, which limits universal applicability across all CLDH subtypes. Consequently, corresponding surgical protocols also face comparable intricacies and challenges, which impact standard implementation in clinical practice. Gu et al. employed CT data to classify CLDH into three categories based on their size and location [33]. Unfortunately, that study only reported a subset of CLDH cases, the conclusion of which may not be universally applicable to all clinical presentations of CLDH. Therefore, their proposed surgical protocols demonstrated diminished clinical guidance value.

The proposed Song’s classification system is simple, clinically practical, and encompasses all manifestations of CLDH. Hence, it demonstrates universal applicability for surgical decision-making and procedural guidance. We report zero cases of clinical recurrence during the entire follow-up period. This may be due to the tailored surgical strategies based on the proposed CLDH classification system. This customized approach enables the precise preoperative planning and optimized intraoperative decision-making necessary for optimal outcomes. This investigation revealed that the successful CLDH treatment approach must take into consideration the morphological subtype of CLDH. The Stone type, for instance, characterized by high mobility and biomechanical instability, warrants complete resection to mitigate long-term postoperative low back pain and secondary nerve compression risks due to potential migration. The Hill type calcification, which primarily compresses neural structures without significant mobility, achieves satisfactory symptom relief via selective laminectomy decompression. Notably, removal of the calcified tissue among Hill type patients can predispose them to spinal instability and elevate recurrence risk. Lastly, the Peak type calcification requires a two-step strategy involving apex resection to allow neural decompression and contouring of the residual calcified margins using specialized instruments to transform the angular morphology to preserve spinal stability.

Although our analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in clinical efficacies across various CLDH subtypes treated with UBE, the surgical duration for the Peak type of CLDH demonstrated a statistically significant prolongation. This is likely due to the complex anatomical configuration of calcified lesions in this subtype, which often necessitates meticulous dissection and prolonged manipulation owing to its rather irregular calcification morphology. As a result, surgeons must exhibit exceptional endurance and methodical precision when managing the Peak type of CLDH cases, as prolonged operative times may challenge both technical focus and procedural stamina. Optimization of surgical workflow and preoperative planning may further mitigate these challenges while maintaining equivalent therapeutic efficacy across all CLDH subtypes.

Several limitations of this study need to be taken into account when interpreting its findings. Firstly, the retrospective study design may give rise to certain inherent biases, such as selection bias. Secondly, the small sample size and single-center setup restrict the generalizability of the results. Nevertheless, based on a novel classification system, this study still provides valuable insights into the clinical outcomes of UBE in treating different morphological types of CLDH, particularly regarding UBE’s efficacy. Using the Song’s classification system, UBE can be conducted safely and efficaciously to treat patients with CLDH. In this study, UBE treated CLDH achieved good neurological recovery and pain relief with low incidence of complications. Subsequent research will focus on a prospective randomized controlled trial (RCT), aimed at validating these findings and assessing the role of this classification system in guiding surgery in terms of longer-term outcomes.

Conclusions

The Song’s classification system has initially demonstrated significant value in guiding personalized surgical decision-making. Different strategic approaches of UBE provide alternative surgical options, thereby optimizing the treatment plan for patients with CLDH. Additionally, UBE has shown favorable efficacy and safety in the treatment of CLDH. This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design, single-center sampling, and failure to control for confounding factors such as genetic factors. Additional prospective, multi-center, and long-term follow-up studies, as well as well-designed prospective randomized controlled trials (RCTs), are needed to supplement and validate our findings.
