Effect of irradiance and delivery configuration on phototherapy efficacy in preterm infants: a network meta-analysis and meta-regression of randomized trials
Authors: Muhammad Talha, Arwa Ejaz, Dawer Rizwan, Neykzad Wadeer, Fahad Amin, Kainat Wajahat, Eman Khalid, Armaghan Hanif, Aroob Farooqi, Nabeel Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo compare the effectiveness and safety of different phototherapy strategies in preterm infants using a network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, and to explore whether treatment and population-level characteristics modify the rate of bilirubin decline.MethodsWe performed a systematic review and frequentist random-effects network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials enrolling preterm infants (< 37 weeks’ gestation). Phototherapy interventions were grouped based on light source and delivery configuration. Prespecified network meta-regression analyses were conducted to explore potential effect modification by irradiance intensity, delivery configuration, gestational age, birth weight, and baseline bilirubin. The primary outcome was rate of total serum bilirubin decline within 24 h of treatment initiation. This network meta-analysis was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261307223).ResultsTwenty randomized trials were included. Double surface phototherapy led to increased TSB reduction (mean difference 0.17 mg/dl/h, 95% CI 0.11–0.22). Higher irradiance strategies demonstrated greater point estimates, although most pairwise comparisons did not reach statistical significance. Meta-regression did not identify statistically significant effect modification, but effect directions were consistent.ConclusionsIn preterm infants, phototherapy efficacy appears to be more closely related to irradiance intensity and exposed body surface area than to device category alone.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07230-6.

Introduction

Neonatal jaundice is extremely common, affecting roughly 60% of term and 80% of preterm newborns [1, 2]. Although most cases are mild, the global burden of severe hyperbilirubinemia is substantial – on the order of over a million infants per year, overwhelmingly in low- and middle-income countries [3]. Preterm infants are particularly vulnerable: immaturity of hepatic clearance, low albumin binding and an underdeveloped blood–brain barrier mean that even moderate unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia carries a high risk of bilirubin neurotoxicity and kernicterus in preterm and small-for-gestational-age neonates [1, 4]. Severe jaundice can cause acute bilirubin encephalopathy and chronic sequelae (cerebral palsy, sensorineural hearing loss, cognitive impairment) and is a recognized cause of neonatal mortality in this population. These data underscore the clinical importance of vigilant management of hyperbilirubinemia in preterm infants.

Current practice guidelines emphasize early identification and treatment of significant jaundice. For example, the 2022 AAP hyperbilirubinemia guideline reconfirms universal screening of all newborns ≥ 35 weeks’ gestation (typically with bilirubin measurement 24–48 h after birth) and provides hour-specific treatment nomograms that incorporate gestational age and other risk factors. Phototherapy is recommended only when thresholds on these nomograms are exceeded. In practice, “intensive” phototherapy is specified as blue-green light (peak ~ 460–490 nm) delivered at ≥ 30 µW/cm^2/nm measured at the infant’s surface [5]. (World Health Organization and other international guidelines similarly stress prompt phototherapy for infants with high bilirubin levels, although thresholds may vary by resource setting.) In general, phototherapy is acknowledged as the mainstay of treatment for neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, reducing the need for exchange transfusion, but it is applied judiciously in order to balance benefits and potential side effects [6].

A wide variety of phototherapy devices are in clinical use. Conventional overhead units may use broad-spectrum fluorescent, special blue fluorescent, or light-emitting diode (LED) bulbs, and newer devices can provide double-surface or wrap-around irradiation to maximize exposed skin area [7–9]. By contrast, fiber-optic “biliblanket” pads deliver light from embedded optical fibers to the infant’s torso. These modalities differ sharply in output: for example, conventional lamps typically emit on the order of 8–10 µW/cm^2/nm, whereas narrow-band LED banks can achieve 30–40 µW/cm^2/nm at a given distance (special blue lamps may exceed 30 µW/cm^2/nm) [10–12]. Fiber-optic pads, while convenient, generally irradiate a much smaller surface area and operate at lower intensity [13–15]. The efficacy of phototherapy therefore depends.

on both dose (irradiance) and coverage: closer placement of lamps and/or the use of multiple units increases the irradiance and the fraction of body surface illuminated, and greater skin exposure has been empirically associated with more rapid bilirubin decline [16]. Many units now combine overhead and under-bed lights (“double-surface” phototherapy) or multiple panels to maximize coverage. Nevertheless, output varies widely between models and facilities, and the interplay of irradiance, surface area and configuration on treatment effect is complex.

Phototherapy clears bilirubin via light-induced structural changes. Bilirubin absorbs light most strongly in the blue region (~ 460–470 nm) and is converted by photoisomerization and structural isomerization into more polar isomers (for example, lumirubin) that can be excreted in bile and urine [17]. Thus, device characteristics such as emission spectrum, intensity, and exposed surface area directly influence the photon flux available for this photochemical reaction. In practice, delivering higher irradiance up to a point accelerates bilirubin clearance though there is some evidence of a saturation effect at very high intensities. Conversely, inferior coverage or low-intensity light may slow the rate of decline. In sum, theory and limited data suggest that both device irradiance and the fraction of body surface illuminated should modulate the effectiveness of phototherapy in lowering serum bilirubin levels.

Despite many clinical trials of phototherapy, direct head-to-head comparisons between device types (e.g. LED versus fluorescent versus fiber-optic, single- versus double-surface units) are few [18]. A 2014 trial in preterm infants noted that very little data exist specifically for preterm populations [19]. Recent systematic reviews generally pool studies of term and late-preterm infants and usually find broadly similar efficacy between light sources. However, these analyses are limited by heterogeneity (different patient ages, device settings, and protocols) and often include few extremely preterm infants. In particular, there is no consensus from pairwise meta-analyses on whether any phototherapy strategy is superior in preterm neonates, and the unique physiology of prematurity may modify effects. These evidence gaps motivate a network meta-analysis of randomized trials, which can integrate direct and indirect comparisons across all device strategies and allow meta-regression to explore how factors like gestational age, initial bilirubin, or device irradiance influence outcomes particularly in preterm infants, who differ substantially from term neonates in bilirubin kinetics, skin permeability, and vulnerability to bilirubin neurotoxicity. By synthesizing the full landscape of RCT evidence focused on preterm infants, a well-conducted network meta-analysis can clarify which phototherapy modalities are most effective and under what conditions. The primary purpose of NMA was structural integration and avoidance of selective pairwise inference, not ranking.

Methods

Study design and reporting standards

This study was conducted as a systematic review with network meta-analysis (NMA) and prespecified meta-regression of randomized controlled trials (RCTs). The review was designed and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Network Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-NMA). A detailed protocol defining eligibility criteria, outcomes, and analytical strategy was finalized a priori to minimize selective reporting and data-driven analyses. This protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261307223).

Objectives

The primary objective was to compare the effectiveness and safety of different phototherapy strategies for the treatment of established unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia in preterm infants using randomized trial evidence. A secondary objective was to quantify how biologically plausible treatment- and population-level characteristics—particularly irradiance intensity, delivery configuration (single vs. double surface), gestational age, and baseline bilirubin—modify the rate of bilirubin decline.

Eligibility criteria types of studies

Only randomized controlled trials, including individual- and cluster-randomized designs, were eligible for inclusion. Non-randomized studies, observational designs, case series, crossover trials without adequate washout, and quasi-experimental studies were excluded. No cluster-randomized trials were identified among the included studies.

Participants

Eligible trials enrolled preterm infants born at < 37 weeks’ gestational age who were treated for established unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia in a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) or special care baby unit (SCBU). Trials including mixed term and preterm populations were included only if outcome data for preterm infants were extractable separately.

Interventions and comparators

Any therapeutic phototherapy strategy used for treatment (not prophylaxis) of hyperbilirubinemia was eligible. To enable coherent synthesis and avoid fragmentation.

of the network, individual devices were collapsed into mechanistic nodes based on light source, delivery geometry, and irradiance intensity:

Conventional fluorescent or white-light phototherapy (FLUOR).

Light-emitting diode phototherapy with standard irradiance (LED_std) Light-emitting diode phototherapy with high irradiance (LED_high) Fiber-optic or blanket phototherapy (FIBER).

Double-surface phototherapy was defined as simultaneous anterior and posterior illumination, regardless of light source. We acknowledge that this approach may bundle heterogeneous implementations (e.g., LED-based versus fluorescent-based systems) with differing irradiance profiles. This categorization was chosen to reflect the clinically relevant mechanism of increased exposed body surface area, but may partially violate the transitivity assumption if light source and irradiance differ systematically across double-surface configurations. Overall, the transitivity assumption was considered plausible based on the clinical similarity of all the included studies in terms of population characteristics, treatment indications, and outcome definitions.

High irradiance was prespecified as ≥ 30 µW/cm²/nm, consistent with commonly cited clinical and guideline thresholds. Sensitivity analyses were planned using alternative cutoffs (25 and 35 µW/cm²/nm). Comparators included any other eligible phototherapy strategy, differences in irradiance or delivery mode, or usual care.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the rate of total serum bilirubin (TSB) decline, expressed as mg/dL per hour, over the first 24 h of treatment. When multiple bilirubin measurements were reported within the first 24 h, the rate of decline was calculated using a prespecified hierarchy: (1) reported slope from linear regression;

(2) change between 0 and 6 h; (3) change between 0 and 12 h; or (4) change between baseline and the earliest reported post-treatment time point within 24 h. Secondary outcomes included need for exchange transfusion, treatment failure, rebound hyperbilirubinemia, hypocalcemia, temperature instability, length of hospital stay, all-cause in-hospital mortality, and neurodevelopmental outcomes when available. The rate of total serum bilirubin (TSB) decline was selected as the primary outcome because it directly reflects the photochemical efficacy of light exposure and is less influenced by unit-level practice patterns, such as local thresholds for stopping phototherapy, timing of repeat bilirubin measurements, or criteria for escalation of care. In preterm infants, where treatment thresholds and duration vary widely across centers, rate of decline provides a biologically sensitive and comparatively standardized measure of treatment response.

Prophylactic phototherapy trials (initiated before bilirubin reached treatment thresholds) and trials in which phototherapy was combined with an unbalanced co-intervention (e.g., metalloporphyrins administered in only one arm) were excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

The review protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420261307223). Electronic databases were searched from inception to February 2026. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), CINAHL, and Web of Science from inception to the most recent search date. Clinical trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform) were searched to identify unpublished or ongoing trials. Reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews were screened manually to identify additional eligible trials. Searches were limited to studies published in English.

Study selection

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility, followed by full-text review of potentially relevant articles.

Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, with arbitration by a third reviewer when required. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented and reported in the PRISMA flow diagram.

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized, piloted extraction form. Extracted variables included study identifiers, publication year, country, trial design, sample size per arm, gestational age and birth weight distributions, eligibility criteria, phototherapy device type and model, irradiance (µW/cm²/nm), distance from the infant, delivery configuration (single or double surface), treatment schedule (continuous or intermittent), baseline TSB, timing of bilirubin measurements, and outcome data.

For continuous bilirubin outcomes, mean values and standard deviations (SDs) at each reported time point were extracted. When only serial bilirubin values were available, the rate of TSB decline was calculated using either linear regression of TSB against time within the first 24 h or simple slope estimation between two predefined time points (e.g., 0–6 or 0–12 h), following a prespecified hierarchy. Medians and interquartile ranges were converted to means and SDs using validated methods when necessary. Authors were contacted to obtain missing or unclear data, particularly irradiance values.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool, evaluating bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Data synthesis and network meta-analysis

The primary purpose of the network meta-analysis framework was not to generate definitive treatment rankings, but rather to coherently structure all available randomized evidence, avoid selective pairwise comparisons, and provide an.

integrated scaffold for prespecified meta-regression analyses. Given that the network for the primary outcome was sparsely connected and largely anchored through a single common comparator (conventional fluorescent phototherapy), treatment rankings and P-scores were interpreted cautiously and not considered the primary basis for inference. Accordingly, direct pairwise estimates were prioritized for interpretation, with the network model serving a complementary role to contextualize relative effects across phototherapy strategies.

A frequentist random-effects network meta-analysis was conducted as the primary analytical approach using the netmeta package in R, consistent with the analytical framework applied in the Results section. Treatment effects for continuous outcomes (including rate of total serum bilirubin decline and time to threshold) were summarized as mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Binary outcomes (exchange transfusion, treatment failure, rebound hyperbilirubinemia, temperature instability, and mortality) were summarized as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs, reflecting the metrics most consistently reported in the included trials.

The network geometry was examined visually to ensure connectivity, with conventional fluorescent phototherapy serving as the primary reference node owing to its historical use and central position within the evidence network. Random-effects models were fitted to account for between-study heterogeneity, estimated using restricted maximum likelihood methods.

Ranking probabilities and P-scores were calculated and are reported in the Results to provide a descriptive summary of the relative ordering of interventions across outcomes. However, these rankings were interpreted cautiously and were not considered definitive measures of comparative effectiveness; primary inference was based on effect sizes, confidence intervals, and consistency of findings across direct and indirect evidence. Although ranking metrics (P-scores) are presented for descriptive completeness, the network meta-analysis was not designed to identify a definitive hierarchy of phototherapy strategies. Given the sparse network structure and reliance on a single dominant comparator, rankings were considered exploratory and were not used as the primary basis for inference. Primary interpretation focused on effect estimates, confidence intervals, and consistency of direction across comparisons.

Multi-arm trials, where present, were incorporated using a multivariate approach that accounts for the correlation between treatment arms by appropriately adjusting the variance–covariance structure within the network meta-analysis framework.

Meta-regression analyses

Prespecified network meta-regression analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity observed in the network estimates, in line with the exploratory analyses reported in the Results section. The following study-level covariates were examined: mean gestational age (weeks), mean birth weight (grams), baseline total serum bilirubin at initiation of phototherapy, irradiance intensity (µW/cm²/nm), delivery configuration (double- versus single-surface phototherapy), and treatment schedule (continuous versus intermittent).

Meta-regression models assumed a common interaction slope across treatment comparisons to preserve model parsimony and avoid overfitting, given the limited number of trials contributing to each outcome. Results of meta-regression were interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Accordingly, absence of statistical significance was not interpreted as evidence of no effect. Instead, emphasis was placed on the direction of associations, the magnitude of regression coefficients, and the proportion of between-study heterogeneity explained (R²), consistent with the presentation of findings in the Results section. Given the limited number of trials contributing to each comparison, these analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and were not intended to support causal inference. Given the aggregate-level nature of the available data, these analyses are subject to ecological bias. However, the covariates examined represent biologically grounded treatment characteristics and baseline clinical parameters, and were prespecified to explore potential effect modification rather than to support individual-level causal inference.

Assessment of heterogeneity and inconsistency

Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the between-study variance (τ²) and I² analogues adapted for network meta-analysis. Inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence was assessed using design-by-treatment interaction models and node-splitting approaches for key comparisons. When inconsistency was detected, potential sources were explored through subgroup and sensitivity analyses.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

Prespecified sensitivity analyses included exclusion of trials at high risk of bias, exclusion of trials with imputed or unknown irradiance, and alternative definitions of the primary outcome time window. Subgroup analyses were planned according to gestational age strata (< 28 weeks, 28–31 + 6 weeks, and 32–36 + 6 weeks), single versus double-surface phototherapy, and geographic or income setting.

Publication bias and small-study effects

Small-study effects and publication bias were evaluated using comparison-adjusted funnel plots for network meta-analysis and regression-based asymmetry tests for continuous outcomes, interpreted cautiously given limited power.

Certainty of Evidence

The certainty of evidence was assessed using the CINeMA framework, which evaluates within-study bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, incoherence, and reporting bias for network estimates. Given the sparse network structure and limited closed loops for several outcomes, CINeMA judgments were applied at the outcome level rather than for individual treatment contrasts.

Results

Characteristics of Included Studies

A total of 20 randomized controlled trials were included in the final analysis, enrolling preterm infants treated for established unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia in neonatal intensive care or special care settings. Sample sizes varied widely across studies, ranging from small single-center trials to large multicenter trials enrolling several hundred to nearly one thousand participants per arm.

The mean gestational age across studies generally ranged from approximately 26 to 38 weeks. Birth weight varied substantially, with reported mean values ranging from approximately 700 g in extremely low birth weight infants to over 3000 g in more mature preterm populations. Baseline total serum bilirubin levels also varied across studies, typically ranging from approximately 4 to over 16 mg/dL at initiation of phototherapy.

Interventions evaluated included conventional fluorescent phototherapy, light-emitting diode (LED) phototherapy at standard and high irradiance levels, fiber-optic phototherapy, and double-surface configurations. Reported irradiance levels ranged from approximately 8 to over 40 µW/cm²/nm, reflecting considerable variability in device output, positioning, and delivery methods.

Results were consistent when restricting analyses to studies reporting 0–12 or 24-hour slopes. Accordingly, treatment rankings and P-scores are reported descriptively and should be interpreted cautiously, with emphasis placed on magnitude and direction of effect estimates rather than relative ordering.

Overall, there was substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies in terms of population characteristics, phototherapy delivery, and outcome reporting, which were considered as potential sources of heterogeneity in subsequent analyses. Detailed study-level characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Meta-regression analyses

Meta-regression analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, including gestational age, birth weight, baseline bilirubin, irradiance, delivery configuration, and treatment schedule.

Overall, no covariate demonstrated a statistically significant association with the primary outcome of bilirubin decline. For secondary outcomes, most covariates were also not significantly associated with treatment effects; however, several patterns of heterogeneity were observed.

For rebound hyperbilirubinemia, double-surface phototherapy explained a substantial proportion of between-study heterogeneity (R² ≈ 59.7%), although this did not reach statistical significance.

For temperature instability, several covariates demonstrated statistically significant associations. Mean gestational age explained the largest proportion of heterogeneity (R² ≈ 78.5%, p = 0.002), followed by birth weight and baseline bilirubin (R² ≈ 75.7%, p = 0.015), suggesting that differences in physiological maturity and thermoregulation, rather than phototherapy modality itself, may underlie observed variability.

For mortality outcomes, gestational age explained a substantial proportion of heterogeneity (R² ≈ 74.3%), while birth weight and baseline bilirubin each explained approximately 68.5%, although these associations did not reach statistical significance.

For treatment failure, the direction of effect estimates for irradiance and double-surface phototherapy consistently favored faster bilirubin decline, supporting biological plausibility despite lack of statistical significance.

These analyses were considered exploratory given the limited number of trials contributing to each comparison and the use of study-level covariates.

Certainty of evidence

Using the CINeMA framework, confidence in evidence was judged to be moderate for the primary outcome of bilirubin decline, primarily limited by imprecision and heterogeneity. Confidence was rated low for exchange transfusion and mortality outcomes due to sparse events, wide confidence intervals, and indirectness. No outcome met criteria for high certainty. Detailed CINeMA assessments for each outcome are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Discussion

In this network meta-analysis of randomized trials in preterm infants, we synthesized evidence across phototherapy strategies to examine how technical and biological factors influence treatment efficacy. Rather than identifying a single superior device, the analysis was designed to integrate all randomized evidence coherently and to explore whether biologically plausible characteristics, particularly irradiance intensity and delivery configuration, are associated with differences in the rate of bilirubin decline.

Across analyses, phototherapy strategies characterized by higher irradiance or greater exposed body surface area were associated with higher point estimates for bilirubin decline compared with conventional single-surface fluorescent phototherapy. However, most pairwise comparisons did not demonstrate statistically significant differences, and meta-regression did not identify statistically significant effect modification by irradiance or other covariates. Importantly, the direction of effect estimates was consistent with established photobiological principles and current guideline thresholds, suggesting biological plausibility despite the absence of confirmatory statistical evidence.

These findings are broadly consistent with prior systematic reviews. Cochrane analyses have shown that LED and conventional fluorescent phototherapy achieve comparable per-hour reductions in serum bilirubin, while LED devices may reduce treatment duration and failure rates, likely reflecting their ability to deliver higher effective irradiance in routine clinical use [18, 20, 27]. Similarly, reviews comparing intermittent and continuous phototherapy have found minimal differences in bilirubin decline, aligning with our observation that any advantage of continuous delivery appears modest [15, 28]. Evidence regarding fiber-optic phototherapy is also concordant: single-surface fiber-optic devices generally demonstrate lower efficacy than overhead phototherapy in term infants, a pattern reflected in our network when fiber-optic modalities were used as the sole light source.

A key contribution of this study is its focus on effect modification rather than device ranking. Although meta-regression analyses were exploratory and conducted at the aggregate level, the consistent direction of associations for irradiance and double-surface delivery supports the hypothesis that phototherapy efficacy in preterm infants is influenced more by the amount of usable light delivered and the extent of skin exposure than by the specific light source itself [3]. These observations should be interpreted cautiously, given the risk of ecological bias, but they highlight factors that are clinically intuitive and directly relevant to bedside practice. From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that achieving adequate irradiance and maximizing exposed body surface area may be more important than the specific phototherapy device selected. In practice, this may involve ensuring appropriate lamp distance, minimizing barriers to skin exposure, and considering double surface configurations when bilirubin is rising rapidly, particularly in smaller or more immature preterm infants.

From a clinical perspective, these findings reinforce existing recommendations rather than redefine them. Current guidelines emphasize the importance of achieving adequate irradiance for intensive phototherapy, typically defined as at least 30 µW/cm²/nm in the blue spectrum. Our results suggest that strategies which reliably achieve recommended irradiance levels and maximize exposed body surface area, such as optimizing lamp distance, using high-output light sources, or employing double-surface configurations when feasible may be associated with more rapid bilirubin decline in preterm infants [26]. LED-based systems may be operationally advantageous in many neonatal units because they more consistently meet irradiance targets, rather than because of intrinsic superiority of the light source [29, 30].

Safety considerations remain central, particularly in preterm populations. Phototherapy was generally well tolerated across included trials, though differences in temperature instability were observed. Associations between LED phototherapy and hypothermia may reflect reduced radiant heat output and underscore the importance of careful thermal monitoring, especially at higher irradiance levels. Standard supportive measures including temperature surveillance, hydration, eye protection, and regular repositioning remain essential components of safe phototherapy delivery in neonatal intensive care settings.

Future research should focus on adequately powered randomized trials in preterm populations with standardized reporting of irradiance, delivery configuration, and treatment protocols. Individual patient-level data analyses may further clarify the role of gestational age, baseline bilirubin, and device characteristics in modifying treatment response. Such studies would help distinguish true biological effect modification from study-level confounding and support more precise optimization of phototherapy strategies.

Limitations

This analysis has several important limitations. First, the network for the primary outcome was sparse and largely connected through a single common comparator, limiting the formation of closed loops and reducing the stability of treatment rankings. For this reason, the network meta-analysis was not intended to identify a single ‘best’ phototherapy modality, and ranking metrics were interpreted cautiously.

Second, meta-regression was conducted at the aggregate (study) level and is therefore subject to ecological bias. However, the covariates examined—irradiance intensity, delivery configuration (single versus double surface), gestational age, and baseline bilirubin—were prespecified, limited in number, and represent biologically grounded treatment and population characteristics rather than behavioral or contextual factors. These analyses were intended to explore potential effect.

modification rather than to support individual-level causal inference. Mortality outcomes should not be interpreted as phototherapy-attributable effects and are presented solely to characterize trial safety. We did not adjust for multiple comparisons across outcomes and meta-regression analyses; therefore, findings should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory. Results of meta-regression were interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Third, irradiance reporting was inconsistent across trials, necessitating imputation or indirect estimation in some cases. Fourth, several secondary outcomes, particularly mortality and exchange transfusion, were infrequent and inadequately powered for definitive inference. Finally, variation in bilirubin measurement timing, phototherapy protocols, and supportive care practices may have contributed to residual heterogeneity. The classification of double-surface phototherapy as a single network node, irrespective of light source, represents a potential limitation. Double-surface LED and double-surface fluorescent systems likely differ in delivered irradiance and spectral output, and thus may not be strictly exchangeable. This heterogeneity could introduce residual confounding and partial violation of the transitivity assumption. However, the available trial data did not permit reliable separation of double-surface configurations by light source without fragmenting the network and further reducing power.

Accordingly, the network meta-analysis should be viewed as an integrative analytical framework rather than a ranking engine, with primary inference driven by effect estimates and biologically grounded effect modification. These findings cannot distinguish whether observed associations reflect true biological effect modification or confounding by center-level practices and disease severity.

Conclusion

This network meta-analysis of randomized trials in preterm infants underscores phototherapy strategies with high effective irradiance and greater exposed surface area were associated with a higher point estimates for bilirubin decline. Statistically significant effect modification was not identified on meta-regression. The findings suggest that adjusting skin surface exposure and irradiance intensity are more significant than phototherapy device category. In clinical practice, this highlights the necessity of appropriate distance, adequate body exposure and lamp output in the treatment of hyperbilirubinemia in preterm infants. Due to sparse and heterogenous evidence base, future trials should report irradiance configurations and standardized measurements in extremely preterm infants. Also, well-powered randomized trials based on meaningful outcomes are required for the determination of how to improve phototherapy in this susceptible population (Fig. 7).
